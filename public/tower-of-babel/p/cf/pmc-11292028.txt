Atavistic strategy for the treatment of hyperuricemia via ionizable liposomal mRNA
Authors: Mengjie Zhang, Abid Hussain, Bo Hu, Haiyin Yang, Chunhui Li, Shuai Guo, Xiaofeng Han, Bei Li, Yunlu Dai, Yuhong Cao, Hang Chi, Yuhua Weng, Cheng-Feng Qin, Yuanyu Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperuricemia is associated with an increased risk of gout, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. Most mammals maintain normal serum uric acid (SUA) via urate oxidase (Uox), an enzyme that metabolizes poorly-soluble UA to highly-soluble allantoin. In contrast, Uox became a pseudogene in humans and apes over the long course of evolution. Here we demonstrate an atavistic strategy for treating hyperuricemia based on endogenous expression of Uox in hepatocytes mediated by mRNA (mUox) loaded with an ionizable lipid nanoparticle termed iLAND. mUox@iLAND allows effective transfection and protein expression in vitro. A single dose of mUox@iLAND lowers SUA levels for several weeks in two female murine models, including a novel long-lasting model, which is also confirmed by metabolomics analysis. Together with the excellent safety profiles observed in vivo, the proposed mRNA agent demonstrates substantial potential for hyperuricemia therapy and the prevention of associated conditions.Subject terms: Molecular medicine, Drug delivery, Nucleic-acid therapeutics

Introduction

The hyperuricemia (HU) is a metabolic condition caused by excessive serum uric acid (SUA) levels due to renal under-excretion, overproduction, or gut under-excretion1. HU is a significant risk feature associated with multiple metabolic disorders, including diabetes, hypertension, atherosclerosis, renal disease, and gout. However, handling the higher levels of SUA may help to manage, prevent, and treat such clinical conditions. Uric acid (UA) became the enzymatic end product of purine metabolism in some mammals, particularly in human beings, due to the mutations in the gene encoding urate oxidase (Uox) (also termed uricase)2,3. In the sequence of enzymes involved in UA metabolism, the Uox is the first enzyme that metabolizes urate into allantoin; a water-soluble form readily excreted via the kidneys. The urate is the anionic element of UA, which dominates at biological pH4. The high SUA level leads to several pathological conditions in humans, such as urolithiasis, nephropathy, and HU5–7. The normal SUA level in humans (6 mg/dL) is elevated due to the high intake of purine-rich foods, leading to urinary retention and accumulation of UA in interstitial fluids and the joints4,8. The UA in the joints develops into crystals, recognized as foreign particles by the immune system, leading to intense joint pain, termed gout. Various conventional therapeutic modalities are commonly used to treat HU, such as probenecid (uricosuric acid) and allopurinol (xanthine oxidase inhibitor). Probenecid increases urination and thereby enhances the excretion of urate crystals, while allopurinol relieves the symptoms by decreasing UA production via inhibiting xanthine oxidase. However, increased urination enhances the excretion of urate crystals, which leads to kidney damage. At the same time, decreased production of UA may cause hypouricemia, consequently interfering with the body’s normal metabolic function. Similarly, a recombinant and pegylated mammalian uricase (pegloticase) is also extensively used to degrade UA to allantoin enzymatically. However, pegloticase has a proven drawback of immunogenicity. It also produces anti-pegloticase antibodies that increase drug clearance, efficacy loss, and infusion reactions. Moreover, clinical experience suggested that patients treated with pegloticase are more susceptible to gout flares (either worse or more frequent)9,10.

Protein and enzyme replacement therapy occurs through nucleic acid (mRNA, DNA, etc.) or direct protein delivery. However, compared to protein therapy, therapeutic protein replacement by mRNA-mediated protein expression has several potential advantages11,12. The mRNA delivery encourages transitory protein expression in the cytoplasm, circumventing nuclear entrance and curtailing genome incorporation13. On the other hand, the protein expression using endogenous machinery allows natural post-translational modifications to occur after mRNA delivery compared to protein replacement or direct protein delivery14. Therefore, the mRNA is neither the end product nor the genetic information; however, mRNA is a quick transition. Therefore, this gave great flexibility to therapeutic mRNA to broaden its applications. However, mRNA rapid degradation due to instability and deprived intracellular internalization limits the clinical applications of mRNA therapeutics15. mRNA delivery using viral vectors may regulate transgene expression due to the risk of abnormal vector integration16,17. Therefore, non-viral mRNA delivery approaches allow transient protein expression to avoid the risk of genome integration associated with viral vectors18,19. To address this, ionizable lipids or lipidoids can form different delivery platforms with mRNAs, such as lipid nanoparticles (LNP) and liposomes. Several ionizable lipids and cationic lipids are reported for nucleic acid delivery including 1, 2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), N-[1-(2, 3-dioleoyloxy) propyl]-N,N,N-trimethylammonium chloride (DOTMA), (6Z,9Z,28Z,31Z)-heptatriaconta-6, 9, 28, 31-tetraen-19-yl 4-(dimethylamino)butanoate (Dlin-MC3-DMA or MC3) and 1, 2-dioleoyloxy-3-trimethylammonium propane chloride (DOTAP)20,21. The successfully commercialized mRNA vaccines against COVID-19, SPIKEVAX (mRNA1273) and COMIRNATY (BNT162b2), also comprised ionizable LNP based on SM-102 ((1-octylnonyl 8- [(2-hydroxyethyl) [6-oxo-6- (undecyloxy)hexyl]amino]-octanoate)) and ALC-0315 ([(4-Hydroxybutyl)azanediyl]di(hexane-6, 1-diyl) bis(2-hexyldecanoate)), respectively22,23. The ionizable LNPs are neutral in biological fluids due to the presence of core components and become cationic in an endosomal acidic environment due to low pH. This shift causes proton sponge-like effects due to the presence of amine groups disrupting the membrane leading to potent intracellular mRNA endosomal/lysosomal escape compared to Lipofectamine 2000 (Lipo2000). These characteristics make ionizable LNP a more robust platform for nucleic acid delivery24.

Herein, we encapsulated the mRNA expressing Uox (mUox) in ionizable LNP, composed of thermostable ionizable lipid A1-D1-5 (N1,N4-bis(3-(bis(2-hydroxydodecyl)amino)propyl)succinamide)25,26, DOPE (1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine), CHO (cholesterol), and DMG-PEG2000 (1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxy polyethylene glycol-2000) as constitutive lipids. The optimized LNP, termed ionizable lipid-assisted nucleic acid delivery system (iLAND, also means intelligent landing technology)25. The iLAND is used to encapsulate mUox to form mUox@iLAND, which is used to transfect Hepa1-6 cells for Uox expression. The mUox@iLAND is intravenously administered to HU mice to evaluate the biodistribution, Uox expression, and anti-HU efficacy. It was proved that the mUox@iLAND effectively accumulated in the liver after administration, stayed there for a long duration, and effectively decreased the levels of UA. Lastly, we demonstrated the therapeutic potential of mUox@iLAND in two different HU mouse models, where one persistent mouse model is developed in-house. The results revealed that the mUox@iLAND successfully delivered the mUox to the liver, lowering the high levels of SUA and elevating the levels of allantoin. Consequently, an efficient formulation that can potentially be used to establish mRNA-based therapeutics for protein or enzyme expression is realized. The results revealed that the iLAND establishes a capable platform for mRNA delivery, whereas the mUox@iLAND embodies a prospective therapeutic potential for the treatment of hyperuricemia and the prevention of associated diseases.

Results

Preparation and characterization of mRNA@iLAND

Plasmid containing Uox expression sequence was firstly constructed with pcDNA3.1. After linearizing the plasmid with BstZ17I (a restriction endonuclease), mRNA expressing Uox (mUox) was prepared via in vitro transcription (IVT). Meanwhile, ionizable lipid termed A1-D1-5, together with DOPE, cholesterol and PEG-DMG2000, were used to load mRNA (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1), and the molar percentage among four lipids was 35.1%: 11.7%: 52.6%: 0.6%. The mass percentage among four lipids was 52.6%: 13.5%: 31.6%: 2.3%, and the mass ratio between the total lipids and mRNA was 15: 1. The physicochemical properties, including hydrodynamic size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential, were characterized using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS). The results suggested that the hydrodynamic size, PDI, zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency of mRNA@iLAND were ~169.1 ± 1.643 nm, 0.148 ± 0.005, -1.22 ± 0.188 mV, and 94.2 ± 0.887%, respectively (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S2). Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) was used to determine the morphology, and a uniform spherical shape was obtained with a diameter of less than 200 nm (Fig. 1C). The size and PDI of the same sample were recorded for 15 days, and it was found that the mRNA@iLAND showed good stability with size and PDI mostly around 200 nm and 0.18, respectively (Fig. 1D).

Besides various significant factors, including lipid saturation, the distance and flexibility of the charged group relative to the lipid interface, and the length of the hydrophobic chain, the pKa value of lipids or lipidoids is a significant factor in realizing the efficient nucleic acid delivery27,28. Similarly, the ionizable LNPs can robustly deliver payloads (commonly nucleic acid) to targeting cells by attaining effective endosomal escape through interacting with anionic lipids of the endosomal membrane such as phosphatidylserine in an acidic environment29. Therefore, the pKa is considered a contributing factor to the escape behavior of ionizable LNPs inside the cells. Consequently, it must meet or exceed 5.5 and should be less than 7.0; otherwise, the NPs system hardly anticipates in vivo nucleic acid carrying efficiency27. As a result, the pKa value of iLAND was 6.02 as recorded by 2-(p-toluidino)-6-napthalene sulfonic acid (TNS) assay (Fig. 1E). The pKa value less than 6.5 means that the iLAND will become protonated in endosomes causing the lipids to fuse with the endosomal membrane for efficient mRNA release into the cytosol. Generally, the pKa value ranges between 5.5–7.0 (more accurately, between 6.0 to 6.9) are the most commonly reported acceptable for in vivo delivery of nucleic acids.

Transfection, internalization mechanism of iLAND and Uox expression in vitro

For delivery efficiency and subcellular localization, the iLAND was co-encapsulated with luciferase-expressing mRNA and Cy5-labeled siRNA (Cy5 nucleic acid, Cy5-NA). The cellular internalization behavior of Cy5-NA@iLAND (iLAND) in the Hepa1-6 cell line was evaluated via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). The Cy5-NA@Lipo2000 (Lipo2000) was used as standard due to its in vitro transfection efficiency. The fluorescence imaging data revealed that the payload was successfully delivered by iLAND into the cells comparable to the Lipo2000. The Cy5-fluorescence signals from iLAND and Lipo2000 were observed in the cytoplasm, somewhere around the nuclei (Fig. 1F). These results, together with the quantitative analysis data (Fig. 1G), showed the cellular internalization of Lipo2000 is slightly higher compared to iLAND in vitro. However, the iLAND exhibited more efficient endosomal escape compared to Lipo2000 (Fig. 1H). Similarly, the same trend was observed in the data recorded by FACS (Fig. 1I, J, and Supplementary Fig. S3).

Furthermore, the cellular internalization pathway by which the iLAND enters the cell was also identified. According to reports, three main pathways are involved in the internalization of extracellular macromolecules: caveolae-mediated endocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis and caveolae/clathrin dual-independent endocytosis30,31. Therefore, we used genistein, chlorpromazine-HCl, and amiloride-HCl to inhibit the first three pathways. As the cells got enough confluence, the respective groups were added with the above three inhibitors respectively, and then iLAND was used to transfect the Hepa1-6 cells following 4 h of incubation at 37 °C. After 4 h, each sample was investigated using confocal microscopy to observe the internalization behavior. The results revealed that the cells transfected with iLAND without the inhibitors mentioned above had enhanced internalization compared to other pre-treated inhibitors. However, the internalization was greatly affected by genistein and chlorpromazine-HCl. At the same time, Amiloride-HCl showed very minimum uptake inhibition of iLAND into Hepa1-6 cell lines (Fig. 2A). Further quantitative analysis also showed the same pattern of internalization behavior of iLAND (Fig. 2B). The influences of these inhibitors on cell viability and apoptosis were also examined, which demonstrated that they have no toxicity to the tested cells (Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5). These results revealed that the iLAND followed the internalization pathway mediated by caveolae without relying on macropinocytosis. Cy5-fluorescence intensity by Lipo2000 is slightly higher than iLAND in vitro (Fig. 1I, J), which should be attributed to different cellular entry mechanisms. Lipo2000 transfects the payloads into the cells typically via classical endocytosis pathways such as clathrin, caveolae or raft-dependent pathways32. While iLAND mediated nucleic acid transfection via ligand (Apolipoprotein E, ApoE)-receptor (low-density lipoprotein receptor, LDLR) recognition, in addition to the caveolae-dependent pathway (Fig. 2A, B)25. This property, together with better endosomal escape, makes iLAND suitable for in vivo delivery33. On the other hand, Lipo2000 is a representative and widely-used cationic in vitro transfection agent but possesses disadvantages (e.g., inducing obvious toxicity) when used in vivo33.

The above results showed that the iLAND successfully delivered the cargo inside the cell. Therefore, we also examined the Uox expression by mUox@iLAND. To achieve this, we encapsulated mUox in both iLAND and Lipo2000, and used these formulations to transfect Hepa1-6 cells. The mock group also showed the expected level of Uox expressed by the cell lines; however, the Lipo2000 and iLAND groups revealed efficient overexpression of Uox (Fig. 2C). Since the Uox is always expressed by the cells34, therefore the mUox@iLAND and mUox@Lipo2000 showed elevated expression of Uox. This scenario is supposed to inhibit the accumulation of uric acid in the liver by converting UA to allantoin35. The formation of allantoin in higher levels can be excreted via the normal urination process without affecting the kidneys.

Meanwhile, the cell viability of mUox@iLAND is evaluated using Hepa1-6 cells. Lipo2000 is also used as a standard in vitro transfection control for comparative analysis. The iLAND and Lipo2000 showed comparable cell viability of more than 95% at low concentrations (1.56 ng/µL) and ~83% at higher concentrations (200 ng/µL) (Fig. 2D), further establishing the biocompatibility of iLAND in vitro.

Intracellular trafficking and endosomal escape of iLAND

The intracellular trafficking of iLAND was further explored, and CLSM was used to record the fluorescence signal of Cy5 in Hepa1-6 cells at 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, and 12 h after transfection. Lipo2000 and Mock were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. The results indicated that the increase in intracellular Cy5 signal was directly proportional to the longer transfection time. The Lipo2000 and iLAND showed higher intracellular Cy5-fluorescence signals; however, the increased pattern of Cy5 signals delivered by iLAND was constantly enhanced with a longer transfection time than Lipo2000 (Fig. 2E–I).

In addition, the co-localization of Cy5 and endosome/lysosome were also analyzed by calculating Pearson’s correlation (Fig. 2G and 2I). It was observed that the iLAND has a faster endosomal escape than the endocytosis speed after 3 h transfection time (Fig. 2I). These results can be elaborated that the iLAND exhibited maximum Pearson’s correlation at 3 h transfection time, revealing that the iLAND is primarily located in the endosome/lysosome after cell internalization. After 3 h transfection time, it is evident that the payloads successfully escaped from the endosome/lysosome because Pearson’s correlation gradually decreased at 5 h and onwards. Compared to iLAND, Lipo2000 triggered the internalization of a significant amount of Cy5 into the cells. Pearson’s correlation gradually increased as the transfection time increased after 3 h, suggesting that Cy5 may not be able to quickly escape from the endosome/lysosome. As reported, nearly 3.5% (or even less than 1%) of internalized nucleic acid can escape from the endosome and be released into the cytosol to execute specific functions36,37. The narrow endosomal escape further strengthens the need to establish advanced delivery, therefore, elaborating the lack of the development of delivery vehicles with the ability of rapid endosomal flight38,39. Hence, this experiment was conducted to explore and evaluate the endosomal escape ability of iLAND and compare it with commercially used gold standard Lipo2000 as a transfection reagent.

In vivo biodistribution

The in vivo biodistribution performance of iLAND was explored in healthy C57BL/6 J mice using in vivo imaging. Dlin-MC3-DMA and SM-102, two ionizable lipids utilized by two commercialized nucleic acid therapeutics or vaccine, were introduced in this experiment as controls. The parameters of physicochemical properties of mRNA@MC3 and mRNA@SM-102 were shown in Tab. S1. After intravenous injection, Cy5-NA@iLAND rapidly and dominantly accumulated in the liver. It is believed that the active and passive liver-targeting behavior of iLAND happened in the liver. Most types of nanoparticles may be passively entrapped by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), and 90% of the RES are located in the liver. Moreover, the ideal size and morphology of iLAND also assisted the accumulation in the liver. Notably, ApoE in blood circulation also binds to the surface of ionizable iLAND, promoting rapid and dominant active accumulation in the liver25,40.

Post administration, the Cy5-fluorescence signal was determined at each time point (1 h, 3 h, 6 h and 24 h). It was observed that the highest Cy5-fluorescence was shown by iLAND compared to MC3 and SM-102 groups (Fig. 3A). An enhanced Cy5-fluorescence was recorded at 3, 6 h post-administration. To more accurately understand and analyze the biodistribution of iLAND in each organ and various tissues, one mouse in each group was euthanized and dissected after 3, 6, 24, and 48 h post-injection (Fig. 3A). After 3 h post-injection, a large portion of iLAND accumulated in the liver, spleen, and kidney, as observed by high Cy5-fluorescence. The iLAND showed significant Cy5 signals after an initial 3 h post-injection. Moreover, peak fluorescence signals were also observed around 3–6 h post-administration (Fig. 3B, C). The data also showed that the liver is the main target organ for iLAND to accumulate in high amounts, which further established the role of mUox-loaded iLAND in the expression of Uox because the enzyme is located in the liver.

In this assay, the LNPs also co-encapsulated luciferase-expressing mRNA (mLuc). Therefore, the bioluminescence was also recorded at indicated time points. As shown in Fig. 3D, E, F, the maximum protein expression was achieved around 3−6 h post-injection. The same trend was observed when one mouse was sacrificed at each time point (3, 6, 24 and 48 h), and a high amount of protein expression was also observed at 3–6 h post-injection. The fluorescence and bioluminescence signal can be detected dominantly in the liver; however, a small amount can also be traced in the spleen. Interestingly, it was also recorded that the iLAND barely accumulated in the heart, lung, and submandibular glands. In vivo biodistribution and mRNA expression of iLAND in healthy Balb/c mice, another mouse species, were also studied (Supplementary Figs. S6, S7 and S8), which revealed similar distribution patterns in vivo. In summary, these results suggested that iLAND quickly and dominantly accumulated in the liver during the distribution phase.

Target cell types and subcellular localization of iLAND in the liver

Both FACS and liver cryosectioning were performed to clarify which cell types in the liver have internalized the iLAND nanoparticles. Two hours after injection, the liver tissue were collected and signal cells were prepared. F4/80 antibody and cytokeratin-18 antibody were used to label Kupffer macrophages and hepatocytes, respectively, and FACS was then conducted. The data indicated that 74.7% of Kupffer macrophages and 31.2% of hepatocytes internalized (Supplementary Fig. S9). This result suggests that most Kupffer macrophages uptake iLAND. While Kupffer macrophage only accounted for a small part of liver cells, and the vast majority of liver cells are hepatocytes (higher than 60%)41,42, the total amount of iLAND positive cells in hepatocytes is much greater than that of Kupffer cells.

Confocal micrographs of liver cryosections prepared from mice receiving the treatments of Cy5-NA@iLAND, Cy5-NA@MC3 or Cy5-NA@SM-102 were carefully analyzed. Here the livers were harvested 2 h after injection, followed by fixing, cryosectioning, and counterstaining with DAPI and FITC-labeled phalloidin to visualize the nuclei (blue) and rough cell outlines (green), respectively. Actually, the hepatocytes are regular in shape and large in diameter, while the nonparenchymal cells are irregular in shape and small in size. As shown in Fig. 3G, asterisks mark representative hepatocytes, and arrows indicate representative nonparenchymal cells (including Kupffer macrophages and hepatic stellate cells, etc.). We observed preferential accumulation of Cy5-NA@MC3 and Cy5-NA@iLAND in hepatocytes, with minimal association with nonparenchymal cells in liver sinusoids. Notably, a large amount of Cy5-NA@SM-102 was endocytosed by nonparenchymal cells. Furthermore, the similar distribution of Cy5-NA@iLAND and Cy5-NA@MC3 in the liver indicates the promising potential of mUox@iLAND for clinical treatment.

Safety evaluations in heathy animals

Mice were injected with MC3 and iLAND nanoformulations intravenously at dose of 0.5 mg/kg, and the serum specimens were collected at different time points (0 h, 6 h, 24 h, 48 h). Eight serum biochemistry parameters, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), glucose (GLU), urea (UREA), total cholesterol (TCHOL), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride (TRIG) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were analyzed (Supplementary Fig. S10). It was observed that AST and ALT were slightly and transiently elevated at 6 h after injection, and it quickly recovered to normal level within 48 h, in both MC3 and iLAND group. No significant difference was observed for all these parameters compared to the corresponding values in the PBS group at 48 h, suggesting that MC3 and iLAND nanoformulations were well-tolerated by the animals.

Stable hyperuricemia mouse model development and treatment with mUox@iLAND

Due to the lack of a stable hyperuricemia (HU) mouse model both in scientific research and pharmaceutical industry3, Uox deficient mice provides new insights into hyperuricemia43. In this study, we have successfully developed a stable hyperuricemia mouse model using GalNAc-siRNA. The results showed that the HU condition could be stable for several weeks with one dose of siRNA. The therapeutic efficacy of mUox@iLAND was determined by measuring the serum uric acid (SUA) levels at a pre-determined time point. The first determination of SUA levels for each group was considered normal. After using the siRNA, the SUA level was elevated, and a 3-fold increase in the SUA level was noticed after two weeks (Fig. 4A, B). After the successful induction of the HU condition, allopurinol and mUox@iLAND were used to treat the HU mice, while mice administered with PBS and normal mice were labeled as positive and negative controls. As allopurinol is a xanthine oxidase inhibitor, it blocks the production of UA and helps reduce SUA levels44. Allopurinol was added to the drinking water daily, as reported before45. While the mUox@iLAND was administered once in the entire study. After administration of the formulations, the first orbital blood was taken after 24 h, and SUA level was measured. Allopurinol showed an abrupt decrease in the SUA level; however, mUox@iLAND showed slow and steady progress by decreasing SUA levels. The PBS group was left untreated to maintain an elevated SUA levels throughout the study.

Similarly, the study continued until the mUox@iLAND showed therapeutic efficacy; therefore, a continuous treatment effect was noticed until 14 days (Fig. 4C). It can be seen that a single dose of mUox@iLAND (0.5 mg/kg) showed sustained efficacy for the entire duration of the study compared to a daily dose of allopurinol. In contrast, the PBS group has constantly shown high SUA levels. It revealed that the mouse model presented in this study can provide a stable HU condition for a more extended period, which can eventually address the shortcomings associated with various HU mouse models used before.

Moreover, the body weight of each mouse in every group was monitored during the study (Fig. 4D). Which shows no apparent weight loss compared to the control group, and therefore we continued the study until the mUox@iLAND showed its efficacy by decreasing the SUA levels. The serum biochemistry parameters, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total cholesterol (TCHOL), glucose (GLU), urea (UREA), triglyceride (TRIG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were analyzed during the study (Fig. 4E–L). No significant difference was observed for all these parameters compared to the corresponding values in the control group, further establishing that the animals well tolerated the mUox@iLAND. The weight monitoring also revealed that the induction of the HU condition did not cause weight loss; therefore, the HU mice treated with allopurinol and mUox@iLAND were found as normal as the mice in the control group.

At the end of the study, animals from each group were sacrificed, major organs were taken, and H&E-stained. The sections of each organ (heart, lung, liver, kidney, and spleen) were analyzed, and no significant change was observed in these organs (Supplementary Fig. S11). It is worth mentioning that the optical image of the isolated spleen from each group was acquired (Supplementary Fig. S12A) and the length of the spleens was recorded (Supplementary Fig. S12B), which revealed that the average length of spleen in the groups of mUox@iLAND and allopurinol was basically the same as that of normal animals, while that of PBS group was higher than that of normal mice. This scenario, to some extent, also reflects the treatment effects and health status of mice.

In addition, we established another stable hyperuricemia mouse model using GalNAc-siRNA. The metabolomics analysis of mouse serum was performed to further evaluate the systemic effects of mRNA@iLAND. The heatmap of non-targeted metabolomics showed that the serum uric acid levels in mice were significantly elevated after using siRNA (PBS vs Control), but returned to normal levels after being treated with mRNA@iLAND (mRNA@iLAND vs Control). In addition, it is worth mentioning that the level of various endogenous metabolites increased or decreased after using siRNA, but returned to normal with the treatment of mUox@iLAND, which suggests that mUox@iLAND achieve effective treatment of hyperuricemia and restore metabolism in mice (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Uric acid lowering effect of mUox@iLAND in purine-rich food intake animal model

We continued to investigate the effect of mUox@iLAND in a traditionally used purine-rich food intake mouse model. Similarly, the SUA was elevated using food intake rich in purines. In comparison, a control group was fed regular food in the study. It was noticed that the SUA level was elevated after around 2 weeks via providing purine-rich food compared to the control group (Fig. 5A, B). Subsequently, the mice were given allopurinol and mUox@iLAND, and blood was taken from orbital bleeding. As a result, a steady decrease of SUA was observed in the mUox@iLAND-treated mice. In contrast, a significant reduction of SUA was noticed in the allopurinol group (Fig. 5C). The SUA was decreased to a level not detected even in the range specified to test the SUA level in the study. Hence, allopurinol may lead to hypouricemia. However, for normal body function, a normal level of SUA must be maintained to carry out normal metabolic functions. Low blood UA level may increase the risk of neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, etc. Therefore, this significant reduction in SUA level might interfere with the body’s metabolic processes and cause undesired side effects.

On the other hand, a single dose of mUox@iLAND showed a promising reduction of UA steadily and constantly. During the entire study, the mice were fed with purine-rich food to maintain the HU condition for longer to observe the efficacy of mUox@iLAND. However, a significant reduction in the body weight of the PBS group was observed, and the physical condition of the mice in the PBS group deteriorated (Fig. 5D). Therefore, the study was terminated at day 8. Similarly, the serum biochemistry parameters including TRIG, AST, ALT, urea, TCHOL, GLU, LDL-C, and HDL-C were also analyzed during the study (Figs. 5E–L). There is a significant difference observed in the PBS group, which reveals that the continuous purine-rich food causes deterioration of the mouse. At the same time, there is no significant difference observed in the mUox@iLAND for most of parameters compared to the corresponding values in the control group, further proving that the animals well tolerate the mUox@iLAND.

At the end of the study, mice were sacrificed, and liver tissues were collected to explore its metabolome. 1900 metabolites were identified after the combination of positive and negative ion channels, among them, the number of metabolites identified by positive ion channel was 1107 and negative ion channels was 793. The classification of 1900 metabolites is shown in Supplementary Fig. S14. Based on univariate analysis, difference analysis was performed for all metabolites. The volcano plot showed upregulation and downregulation of various metabolites compared to the control group both in the positive channel (Fig. 6A–C) and negative channel (Supplementary Fig. S14). Compared to the control group (normal mice), 902 metabolites were downregulated (blue dots) and 375 metabolites were upregulated (red dots) in the PBS group (Fig. 6A), while 707 metabolites were downregulated and 254 metabolites were upregulated in the allopurinol (Fig. 6B). The difference of metabolic change between PBS group and mUox@iLAND group (G2 vs G4) was least with 12 intersection size (Supplementary Fig. S16). These results suggested that mUox@iLAND displayed comparable or even greater therapeutic effects than allopurinol in this study. The heatmap showed that the mUox@iLAND maintained the UA level precisely the same as the control group (Fig. 6D), while a high level of allantoin was observed in mUox@iLAND, suggesting the expression of Uox and consequently the conversion of UA to allantoin. This phenomenon of normal UA metabolite level and a high level of allantoin metabolite is of great importance in a way that the mice are fed with purine-rich food unlike control group, and still mUox@iLAND potentially maintain UA metabolite same as the normal food intake control group. Maintaining the normal level of UA results in a high level of allantoin metabolite.

In contrast, the UA level was significantly higher in the PBS group and lowered in the allopurinol group. The low level of UA in the allopurinol group is resulting from the inhibition of xanthine oxidase. Similarly, a deficient level of allantoin was also observed in the allopurinol group because of its xanthine oxidase inhibition capability.

Interestingly, various metabolites involved in the purine metabolism were in parallel between the control and mUox@iLAND groups, which also revealed that the mUox@iLAND maintained routine metabolic procedures inside the liver. As illustrated in Fig. 6D, the level of prostaglandins f2.beta and arachidonic acid needed for liver regeneration are well maintained in mUox@iLAND group compared to control group, while the PBS and allantoin groups showed significant alteration. This clearly signifies that the mUox@iLAND restores the metabolic processes of hyperuricemic mice to normal healthy situation.

Moreover, the various metabolites were also identified as affecting the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle directly or indirectly. It was observed that most of those metabolites were well maintained by mUox@iLAND (Supplementary Fig. S18).

Finally, three mice from each group were sacrificed. The major organs including the heart, lung, liver, kidney, and spleen were also collected and H&E-stained. The sections of each organ were analyzed. The section staining showed that the lumen of the proximal convoluted tubule in PBS group was significantly enlarged, which was presumed to be uric acid crystals (Supplementary Fig. S19). The optical images of the isolated spleens and kidneys displayed a similar pattern that was observed in the stable hyperuricemia mouse model (Supplementary Fig. S20). It was observed that spleens of mice in PBS group was significantly enlarged and the kidneys of mice in the PBS group turned yellow, suggesting the presence of uric acid crystals. Taken together, mUox@iLAND achieved significant treatment effects in the purine-rich food intake murine model, with a better safety profile than clinically-used allopurinol.

Discussion

In this study, we have successfully developed a stable and long-lasting hyperuricemic mouse model and treatment with an atavistic strategy using mUox@iLAND. The mUox@iLAND was developed to degrade UA to allantoin and thereby maintaining a normal UA levels strongly comparable with control group of mice. Our findings revealed that the developed hyperuricemic mouse model showed high stability and maintaining high UA acid levels for few weeks compared to the traditionally used purine-intake model. On the other hand, a single dose of mUox@iLAND maintained normal UA levels in two murine mouse models.

UA is the end product of purine metabolism in higher animals, such as humans and great apes, due to mutation in the Uox enzyme during evolution history. This mutation causes the inhibition of the conversion of UA into allantoin46. Since UA has a certain solubility (~420 mM), human kidneys can excrete a certain amount of UA in urination. On the other hand, allantoin is more water-soluble than UA and, therefore, is eliminated freely via the kidneys47. The accumulation of a large amount of urate leads to HU, and HU became one of the widespread metabolic diseases commonly found and categorized by elevated UA levels in the blood. This high SUA causes improper metabolic function; therefore, the body shifts the UA to muscle tissues for storage. As the body constantly takes purine-rich food, the saturated UA is finally deposited into the joints, where it forms crystals and causes gouty arthritis48. Besides gouty arthritis, HU is a risk factor for uric acid nephrolithiasis, cardiovascular and renal diseases, especially hypertension.

The prevalence of hyperuricemia (HU) among adult populations in the United States and China alone exceeds 32.5 million and 170 million, respectively49. Various medications are used to treat HU, including xanthine oxidase inhibitors (e.g., allopurinol) and uricosuric agents (e.g., benzbromarone and probenecid). However, the xanthine oxidase inhibitors are associated with severe cutaneous adverse reactions50. In addition, inhibiting the production of UA leads to hypouricemia, interfering with routine metabolic processes. Similarly, pegloticase antibodies enhance the drug-clearance, therefore interfering with the treatment. It is worth mentioning that there was a recent study that revealed a role for the gut microbiota in uric acid excretion and highlighted the potential for microbiome-targeted therapeutics in hyperuricemia51. At present, xanthine oxidase inhibitors are still the preferred drug of choice while efficacy of uricosuric agents is greatly regulated by pharmacogenetics. Due to scientific advancement and strive for next generation medications and therapies, significant efforts are underway for more effective treatment of HUA.

Recently mRNA therapeutics gained traction as a method of inducing protein or enzyme expression in vivo, with multiple studies demonstrating a long-term expression of the substrates compared to conventional replacement therapy52. However, due to its highly unstable nature, mRNA delivery is achieved via viral or non-viral vector delivery systems. The non-viral delivery system, such as lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), is considered the most effective delivery platform. Due to the recently approved three mRNA vaccines (SPIKEVAX (mRNA1273), COMIRNATY (BNT162b2), ARCoV) against COVID-19, the LNPs are gaining colossal acceptance for mRNA delivery.

Generally, the ionizable lipid comprises a hydrophilic amino head and hydrophobic alkyl chain tails. In contrast, non-ionized lipids comprised permanently charged quaternary amine moieties in their hydrophilic head group. It has been established that lipids containing ionizable amines deliver nucleic acids more effectively compared to non-ionized lipids29,53. Various ionizable lipids, such as Dlin-MC3-DMA, SM-102 and ALC-0315, have been successfully optimized and developed to deliver nucleic acids including siRNA and mRNA. Moreover, intellectual property issues are of great importance in the pharmaceutical industry. We believe that the patent claims in this field are mainly divided into three levels: (i) the chemical structures of cationic or ionizable lipids/lipidoids; (ii) the selection and molar proportion (ratio) of lipid components; (iii) what biofunctional molecules are carried and their application fields. Several patent families (e.g., PCT/CA2009/000496, including US8058069B2 and US9364435B2) protect the broad distribution ratio of LNP components that do not limit the chemical structure of lipids. In this study, the developed proprietary ionizable lipid termed A1-D1-5, constitutes a novel lipid structure with five hydrophobic tails. Then A1-D1-5, DOPE, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 were used to load mRNA at a molar percentage of 35.1%: 11.7%: 52.6%: 0.6%, which was significantly different with the claim of “a cationic lipid comprising from 50 mol % to 65 mol % of the total lipid present in the particle” in the most famous patent of US8058069B2.

In addition, another crucial molecular characteristic of ionizable lipids is their pKa values. The lipids with a pKa value range between 6.0–6.9 demonstrate enhanced in vivo nucleic acid delivery27. This short range of pKa value of iLNPs is reported to have a low charge in a neutral biological environment, therefore enhancing ApoE-mediated hepatocyte uptake via endocytosis. In the acidic endosomal compartment, the positive charge also indorses membrane intervention28. At acidic pH, suitable pKa also enables iLNPs to demonstrate an enhanced entrapment of nucleic acid and revealed neutral zeta potential at pH (7.4). These qualities reduce non-specific protein binding in circulation and improve the safety performance of iLNPs. The length and lipid chain unsaturation and proper hydrophobic tail numbers are also essential considerations for lipid design beside pKa. The iLAND showed the pKa value of 6.02, indicating that the iLAND is suitable for in vivo nucleic acid delivery vehicles.

On the other hand, over the years, various HU mouse models have been developed and used. These mouse models can be divided into two broad categories: mice with genetic modifications (genetically induced models) and mice exposed to certain environmental factors (environmentally induced models; for example, pharmaceuticals or dietary intake). However, these methods of generating hyperuricemia conditions have disadvantages, such as the genetically modified models being time-consuming and high-cost. Still, when the model is developed via deletion of a gene encoding Uox, it was noticed that at the age of 4 weeks, the Uox-deficient mice had high mortality of 65% due to severe nephropathy3,54. It is also found that the genetically modified hyperuricemia mouse models have a low birth rate of around 15% due to embryonic lethality and that a low percentage of mice also developed renal dysfunction due to the formation of UA crystals, causing glomerular or tubular lesions55. On the other hand, many reports highlighted the use of chemicals and a rich purine diet to induce hyperuricemia. The induction of hyperuricemia condition in mice is developed faster with a minimum of 7 days to a maximum of 14 days. However, the purine-rich food intake cannot be discontinued; otherwise, the HU condition cannot be maintained after 24 h. With the use of chemicals, especially oxonate, the HU condition can be maintained for a short time. Hence, it is not easy to compare such HU mouse models with HU in humans56. Therefore, we have successfully developed a stable hyperuricemia mouse model using a liver-targeted drug targeting a pivotal gene involved in HU metabolic pathway. The results showed that the HU condition could be maintained stably for at least several weeks without affecting the condition of the mouse. After stably holding high SUA, a single dose of mUox@iLAND sustainably lowered the SUA for several weeks. Due to promising results, we also validated the treatment profiles of mUox@iLAND in the traditionally used purine-rich food intake mouse model.

The metabonomics data also showed the same pattern by maintaining various metabolites involved in the purine degradation pathway. In comparison, the mUox@iLAND maintained normal levels of UA as the healthy animals, while the UA level of PBS group was elevated. On the other hand, the allantoin metabolite level in mUox@iLAND is very high compared to allopurinol, PBS, and normal mice, which shows the efficient conversion of UA into allantoin by mUox@iLAND. Although the feed of the normal mice and other groups is different, the iLAND maintained the levels of metabolites involved in the purine degradation pathway the same as the normal animals.

Taken together, this study provided a promising mRNA-based therapeutic regimen for the treatment of hyperuricemia. By employing an atavistic strategy, mRNA expressing Uox was designed and loaded with a proprietary LNP delivery system based on ionizabe lipid of A1-D1-5. Effective and durable lowering of SUA, and ideal safety profiles were observed in two rodent models, including one established in-house.

Plasmid extraction and in vitro transcription (IVT) mRNA

The coding sequence of mouse urate oxidase mRNA (mUox) was referred to as NCBI (gene ID 22262), and the sequence was shown in supporting materials, with the sequence of 5’-UTR and 3’-UTR. The whole sequence of mUox was inserted into the plasmid named pCDNA3.1, and the E. coli stably expressed UOX-pCDNA3.1 was built by AuGCT (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The engineered E. coli was cultured in an LB medium and extracted by GoldHi EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Kit. In vitro transcription (IVT) was performed using the Standard mRNA Synthesis kit (E2060s, NEB). The linearization of the plasmid was carried out using BstZ17I digestion, and the linearized plasmid was taken as an IVT template. The IVT was carried out as prescribed by the manufacturer. In brief, the resultant mRNA was capped with ARCA and further polyadenylated enzymatically. The LiCl solution was used to purify the final transcribed mRNAs, and the final mRNA concentration was determined using Nanodrop. The activity of the mUox was always checked using Western Blotting before further assay.

Preparation and characterization of mUox@iLAND

Briefly, A1-D1-5, DOPE, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000, at the molar percentage of 35.1%: 11.7%: 52.6%: 0.6%, were dissolved into a penicillin bottle using ethanol as the organic phase. Similarly, 0.05 M citrate buffer having pH 4.0 was also taken in a separate tube. The volume of citrate buffer is three times the volume of the organic phase. Then, the organic phase was injected into citrate buffer solution under stirring to form pre-LNPs. The mRNA (mLuc or mUox) was dissolved in 25% ethanol and added to LNP to create mLuc@iLAND, followed by 20 min incubation at 50 °C. The mass ratio between total lipid and mRNA is 15:1. Finally, 1 × PBS was used to dialyze the nanoparticles for at least 2 hours. The final formulation (mLuc@iLAND or mUox@iLAND) is prepared each time for every experiment. However, the formulation can be stored for 1-2 weeks at 4 °C.

RiboGreen assay was employed to determine the encapsulation efficiency (EE%). Briefly, the mLuc@iLAND was used to release mLuc by treating with 2% Triton X-100. Then, untreated mLuc@iLAND and treated with Triton X-100 mLuc@iLAND were added with RiboGreen reagent and incubated. Finally, a microplate reader was used to record the fluorescence intensity to determine the total amount of both mLuc and free mLuc. The EE% was calculated as:

The results of the hydrodynamic size, zeta potential and polydispersity index (PDI) of mLuc@iLAND were demonstrated by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) (Malvern, Zetasizer Nano ZS). While the shape and morphology were analyzed by Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi, HT7700).

Cell culture

Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) was added 10% FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Hepa1-6 cells from Cell Culture Center (Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, CAMS) were cultured in the above medium in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Urate oxidase expression in vitro

Briefly, Uox expression by Hepa1-6 cells was determined by Western Blotting. Hepa1-6 cells were platted in a 6-well plate and transfected with mUox@iLAND, mUox@Lipo2000 for 4 h. After 24 h, PLB (passive lysis buffer) and protease inhibitor were added to the cells to extract total proteins. After determining the protein concentration in each well using a BCA protein assay kit, western blotting was carried out. After loading and separation of protein by SDS-PAGE, the gel was transferred to the membrane for blotting. BSA (5%, m/v) was used 1 h at room temperature to block the membrane. Finally, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary mouse monoclonal antibody (1:1000). Following day, the membrane was washed and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibody (1: 3000). Images were taken by Chemi-luminescence imaging system.

pKa determination

Keeping in mind the importance of pKa, we used fluorescent probe 2-(p-toluidino)-6-napthalene sulfonic acid (TNS) to determine the pKa value of iLAND. In short, iLAND was prepared as described above, and 1 mL of iLAND in which the total lipid concentration is 2 μM was taken, and a series of solutions were formulated having pH range from 2.5 to 11.5. A stock solution was prepared to contain HEPES (100 Mm) buffer, MES (100 mM), ammonium acetate (100 mM), NaCl (1300 mM), and TNS (100 μM). A required quantity of the above solution was mixed with a series of iLAND, and finally, a 96-well plate was added with TNS. An excitation wavelength of 321 nm and an emission wavelength was used to detect the fluorescence. Henderson–Hasselbach equation (GraphPad Prism v.8) was used to calculate the pKa of iLAND.

Finally, the mediums were discarded, and fresh medium was added along with MTT solution. The plate was then incubated for another 4 h at 37 °C. Cellular uptake

First, the 6-well plate was added with Hepa1-6 cells and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Then, each confocal dish was added with iLAND formulation containing Cy5 (50 nM) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The plates were finally subjected to confocal microscopy for imaging.

Animals

The animals were retained in the Laboratory Animal Center of Beijing Institute of Technology. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Beijing Institute of Technology and performed in accordance with the guidelines and policies (License No: BIT-EC-SCXK(Beijing)2019-0010-M-2021010).

Biodistribution of iLAND in vivo

Healthy C57BL/6 female mice, 6–8 weeks, were fed a regular diet and water for a week to adapt to the environment. Mice were randomly divided into four groups with five animals per group. The Cy5-NA@MC3, Cy5-NA@SM-102, and Cy5-NA@iLAND were prepared and intravenously injected into the mice. To acquire fluorescence and bioluminescence imaging simultaneously, mRNA expressing firefly luciferase was co-loaded with Cy5-labeled nuclei acid (Cy5-NA) at the mass ratio of 1:1. The dose was 0.5 mg/kg for total nucleic acid. One group of mice was given PBS, which served as the negative control. After administration, each mouse was imaged at 1, 3, 6, 24, and 48 h via an IVIS Spectrum imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA) to record the fluorescence. Meanwhile, one mouse from each group was sacrificed at 3, 6, 24 and 48 h post-injection, and the major organs including the submandibular gland, thymus gland, heart, lung, liver, spleen and kidneys were isolated and fluorescently imaged. The bioluminescence was also detected at 3, 6, 24 h and 48 h post-injection.

In a separate experiment on biodistribution of iLAND, Cy5-NA@Lipo2000 were introduced as a control. Furthermore, tissues were isolated from the liver of one mouse in each group and stored at -80 °C for further use. The frozen liver tissues were sliced and observed using a Digital Slice Scanner for fluorescence detection at 6 h and 24 h post-injection.

Identification of target cell types and subcellular localization of iLAND in the liver

C57BL/6 female mice, 6-8 weeks, were randomly divided into four groups and intravenously injected with PBS, Cy5-NA@MC3, Cy5-NA@SM-102 and Cy5-NA@iLAND, respectively. The dose of Cy5-labeled nuclei acid is 1 mg/kg. For confocal experiment, the livers were harvested 2 h after injection and fixed with formaldehyde solution, then the cryosections were prepared and counterstained with DAPI and FITC-labeled phalloidin to visualize nuclei (blue) and rough cell outlines (green), respectively. For FACS experiment, livers were harvested 2 h after injection. The isolated mouse liver was ground into single cell suspension. Erythrocyte were removed using RBC Lysis Buffer. The cell suspension was divided into two parts, one of which was added with F4/80 antibody to label Kupffer macrophages, and the other was fixed and permeated, and added with cytokeratin-18 antibody to label hepatocytes. Finally, flow cytometry analysis was performed.

Efficacy study in stable hyperuricemia mouse model

The C57BL/6 female mice, 6–8 weeks, were fed a regular diet and water for a week to adapt to the environment. Then the blood was taken from each mouse via retro-orbital bleeding, and serum was collected via centrifugation and analyzed for serum uric acid (SUA) levels, which was the baseline of the SUA. The animals were randomly divided into various groups (Control, PBS, Allopurinol, mUox@iLAND). Except for the control group, the mice were given a single subcutaneous injection of GalNAc-siRNA at the dose of 9 mg/kg to inhibit the expression of a enzyam invovled UA metabolic pathway. All mice were constantly monitored for their weight, while fresh food and water were given daily for 14 days. After 14 days, blood was taken via retro-orbital bleeding, and SUA levels were analyzed. Once a stable and high serum urate level (increase 2-3 times than the baseline) was obtained, the animals were given with mUox@iLAND (0.5 mg/kg, single injection), or Allopurinol (daily dose of freshly prepared Allopurinol), or PBS. The SUA level was analyzed at pre-determined time points for 14 days. At the end of the study, the mice in each group were sacrificed. Various organs, including the heart, lung, liver, kidneys, and spleen were analyzed for histopathological screening via Digital Slice Scanner.

Efficacy study in purine-rich food induced mouse model

The efficacy of mUox@iLAND was also demonstrated in a purine-rich food intake mouse model. C57BL/6 female mice, 6–8 weeks, were taken and randomly divided into various groups (Control, PBS, Allopurinol, mUox@iLAND) and kept in standard condition for at least one week. After that, the SUA levels were analyzed as described in the above procedure. All mice were fed purine-rich food except for the control group, and regularly monitored for weight and physical conditions. The SUA levels were again analyzed on the 14th day, and once a high serum urate was obtained, the mice were treated with respective options. The control group was fed a regular diet, while the PBS group was fed a purine-rich diet. The other two groups were treated with mUox@iLAND (0.2 mg/kg) or Allopurinol (freshly prepared daily dose). The treatment and analysis continued until the condition of the mice, especially the PBS group, was stable. For that reason, weight was determined as a physical stability parameter. At the end of the study, the mice in each group were sacrificed. Various organs, including the heart, lung, liver, kidneys, and spleen were analyzed for histopathological screening via Digital Slice Scanner.

Detection of serum uric acid (SUA)

At the scheduled time point, mouse blood was obtained from the eye socket in sterile EP tube at the scheduled time point. The blood was stand at room temperature for 30-60 min to clot. After centrifugation at 1000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected as serum. The serum was staged at -20 °C until analysis. The analysis of serum biochemical indicators was performed by DIAN Diagnostics Technology Group Co., Ltd. using automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, LABOSPECT 008 AS).

Metabolomics analysis of liver

During the study explained above, three mice from each group were euthanized and sacrificed to fully understand the metabolic changes in the liver at a pre-determined time. The liver from each mouse was collected, and samples were prepared for metabolomics analysis. After slowly thawing the samples at 4 °C, an appropriate amount of sample was taken and added to a pre-cooled solution of methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:2:1, v/v). The mixture was vortexed, subjected to low-temperature sonication for 30 minutes, and then allowed to stand at −20 °C for 10 minutes. Subsequently, it was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and vacuum dried. For mass spectrometry analysis, 100 μL of acetonitrile/water solution (acetonitrile:water = 1:1, v/v) was added for reconstitution. After vortexing, the mixture was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatant was used for injection and analysis. The sample was separated using an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography (UHPLC) system with a HILIC chromatographic column. The AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer was used for the acquisition of primary and secondary spectra of the samples. The raw data was converted to.mzXML format using ProteoWizard, and then XCMS software was used for peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction. The data extracted by XCMS was first subjected to metabolite structure identification and data preprocessing, followed by experimental data quality assessment, and finally, data analysis. The analysis was performed by Hangzhou Kaitai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China).

Metabolomics analysis of serum

Two mice from each group were euthanized and sacrificed. The serum from each mouse was collected, and samples were prepared for metabolomics analysis. The Waters 2777 C UPLC (Waters, USA) coupled with the Q Exactive HF high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was utilized for the separation and detection of metabolites. A BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters, USA) was used. For positive ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of a solution of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (B). For negative ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of a solution of 10 mM ammonium formate in water (A) and 95% methanol with 10 mM ammonium formate (B). The following gradient was applied for elution: 0–1 min, 2% B; 1–9 min, 2%-98% B; 9–12 min, 98% B; 12–12.1 min, 98% B to 2% B; 12.1–15 min, 2% B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature was 45 °C, and the injection volume was 5 μL. Mass spectrometry data acquisition was performed using a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for both MS1 and MS2 data. The mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) range for the scan was 70-1050, with a resolution of 120,000 for MS1, an AGC target of 3e6, and a maximum injection time (IT) of 100 ms. Based on the intensity of parent ions, the top 3 ions were selected for fragmentation to acquire MS2 data, with a resolution of 30,000, an AGC target of 1e5, and a maximum injection time (IT) of 50 ms. The stepped normalized collision energy (NCE) settings were 20, 40, and 60 eV. The ion source (ESI) parameters were set as follows, sheath gas flow rate at 40, auxiliary gas flow rate at 10, spray voltage (|KV | ) at 3.80 for positive ion mode and 3.20 for negative ion mode, capillary temperature at 320 °C, and auxiliary gas heater temperature at 350 °C. The raw data from the mass spectrometer were imported into Compound Discoverer 3.3 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for analysis. The analysis combined data from the BGI Metabolome Database (BMDB), the mzCloud database, and the ChemSpider online database. The analysis was performed by Shenzhen BGI Co., LTD (Shenzhen, China).

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance between two groups was determined by two-tailed Student’s t-test. Multiple groups were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a Tukey post-hoc test using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).
