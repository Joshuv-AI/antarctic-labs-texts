Multiparametric profiling of HER2-enriched extracellular vesicles in breast cancer using Single Extracellular VEsicle Nanoscopy
Authors: Nan Jiang, Andras Saftics, Eugenia Romano, Ima Ghaeli, Cristal Resto, Vanessa Robles, Saumya Das, Kendall Van Keuren-Jensen, Victoria L. Seewaldt, Tijana Jovanovic-Talisman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with HER2-positive breast cancer can significantly benefit from HER2-directed therapy – such as the monoclonal antibody trastuzumab. However, some patients can develop therapy resistance or change HER2 status. Thus, we urgently need new, noninvasive strategies to monitor patients frequently. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) secreted from tumor cells are emerging as potential biomarker candidates. These membrane-delimited nanoparticles harbor molecular signatures of their origin cells; report rapidly on changes to cellular status; and can be frequently sampled from accessible biofluids.ResultsUsing Single Extracellular VEsicle Nanoscopy (SEVEN) platform that combines affinity isolation of EVs with super-resolution microscopy, here we provide multiparametric characterization of EVs with ~ 8 nm precision and molecular sensitivity. We first interrogated cell culture EVs affinity-enriched in tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81; these transmembrane proteins are commonly found on EV membranes. SEVEN robustly provided critical parameters of individual, tetraspanin-enriched EVs: concentration, size, shape, molecular cargo content, and heterogeneity. Trastuzumab-resistant cells (vs. trastuzumab-sensitive) secreted more EVs. Additionally, EVs from trastuzumab-resistant cells had lower tetraspanin density and higher HER2 density. We also evaluated EVs affinity-enriched in HER2; we found that these EVs (vs. tetraspanin-enriched) were larger and more elongated. We further optimized analytical sample processing to assess a rare population of HER2-enriched EVs from patient plasma. In breast cancer patients with elevated HER2 protein expression (vs. controls), HER2-enriched EVs had distinct characteristics including typically increased number of tetraspanin molecules and larger size. Importantly, these EVs were on average 25-fold more abundant compared to no cancer controls.ConclusionsSEVEN revealed unique characteristics of HER2-enriched EVs in cultured cells and complex biological fluid. In combination with current clinical approaches, this method is well poised to support precise therapeutic decisions.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02858-x.

Background

HER2 is a transmembrane receptor kinase expressed on epithelial cells from several organs. However, HER2 can also be overexpressed at 40- to 100-fold on the surface of cancer cells and can enhance proliferation signaling pathways [1, 2]. HER2 overexpression or amplification is observed in about 20% of breast cancers. Currently, HER2 status is determined using combined immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) [3] analysis of tissues collected from biopsies and excisions [4–6]. While IHC semi-quantitatively evaluates the level of HER2 protein expression (score ranges between 0 and 3+), FISH quantifies HER2 gene copy-number. HER2-positive breast cancers have an aggressive phenotype and increased recurrence rates [1, 7]. HER2-directed drugs, such as the monoclonal antibodies trastuzumab and pertuzumab, provide strong anti-tumor effects and have markedly improved survival for women with HER2-positive disease [8, 9]. Currently, a combination of pertuzumab, trastuzumab, and taxane is recommended as a first-line treatment for women with HER2-positive breast cancers [10, 11].

To provide additional therapeutic benefits, trastuzumab has been conjugated with small molecule therapeutics [12, 13]. One example is trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhert®): trastuzumab conjugated to a topoisomerase I inhibitor. Another example is trastuzumab emtansine (Kadcyla®): trastuzumab conjugated with a microtubule inhibitor. Trastuzumab emtansine has been extremely effective and is the recommended second-line therapy for women with HER2-positive breast cancer [5, 14]. Interestingly, these new antibody-drug conjugates can even be effective in women with HER2-low status [15] - HER2 IHC score of 1 + or 2 + without HER2 amplification. While advances in antibody-based HER2-directed therapy have greatly improved patient outcomes [16–18], many HER2-positive patients either fail to respond or develop therapy resistance [6, 8, 19–21]. To compound the problem, HER2 status/expression can change during treatment or disease progression. During neoadjuvant therapy, the rate of negative conversion is 10–40% and the rate of positive conversion is 3% [22, 23]. Additionally, during the progression of breast cancer, there is an enrichment in HER2-low tumors [24].

Given that HER2 expression can change during treatment/disease progression, we urgently need new, non-invasive strategies that provide frequent and rapid readouts of HER2 status. The need is particularly acute since antibody-based HER2-directed therapy is both costly and sometimes associated with serious side effects (e.g., cardiotoxicity [25]). For example, women who lose HER2-positive status or develop resistance to therapy could be rapidly switched to a more effective line of therapy.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are emerging as promising biomarker sources to both diagnose and monitor disease [26–28]. These nanoparticles, which are secreted by all cells, are found abundant in easily accessible biofluids. Because their release does not depend on cell death, EVs offer a rapid readout on cellular change. Moreover, EVs could report on micro-metastases [29], which may be inaccessible for biopsies. The structure of EVs entails a lipid bilayer embedded with (glyco)proteins [27]. The bilayer delineates a lumen filled with luminal proteins, nucleotides, and metabolites. The robust structure of EVs affords excellent stability in biofluids. In addition to having abundant EV protein markers, tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81 (TSPANs), EVs also contain biomarkers specific to cells of their origin [30, 31]. For example, HER2 is present on EVs derived from HER2-positive cancer cells [32].

One challenge in developing EVs as biomarkers has been the isolation of EVs from specific cell types – i.e., those that are cancerous [33]. Biofluids contain EVs from many different cell types, and only about 0.02 to 0.05% of EV proteins in plasma are believed to be derived from tumors [34]. EVs are also highly heterogeneous, and the majority have nanoscale sizes (30–500 nm). These features result in technical challenges when attempting to characterize multiple parameters of individual EVs [35]. To sensitively characterize the concentration, size, shape, and molecular content of individual EVs, we have recently developed Single Extracellular VEsicle Nanoscopy (SEVEN) [36]. This approach combines affinity isolation of EVs with quantitative single molecule localization microscopy (qSMLM) to provide detection of EVs with single molecule sensitivity and an average localization precision of approximately 7–8 nm [36]. Here, we optimized the isolation of HER2-enriched EVs from cultured cell lines (trastuzumab-sensitive and -resistant) and patient plasma. We provide an in-depth multiparametric characterization of these EVs.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

The cell lines used in the study were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). SH-SY5Y cells (HER2-negative) were grown in phenol red-free DMEM media (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 31053028) with 14% fetal bovine serum (FBS, VWR, 97068-091), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Sigma, P0781-100ML), and 4 mM GlutaMAX (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 35050061). BT-474 cells (HER2-positive, trastuzumab-sensitive), BT-474R cells (clone 5, HER2-positive, trastuzumab-resistant), and CAMA-1 cells (HER2-negative) were cultured in phenol red-free DMEM (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 31053028) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Invitrogen, 11360070), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, and 4 mM GlutaMAX. JIMT-1 cells (HER2-positive, trastuzumab-resistant) and SK-BR-3 cells (HER2-positive, trastuzumab-sensitive) were cultured in phenol red-free DMEM (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 31053028) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 4 mM GlutaMAX. HEK-293T cells (HER2-negative) were cultured in DMEM (ATCC, 30-2002) supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine (ATCC, 30-2214), and 100 U/mL Penicillin-Streptomycin. MCF-7 cells (HER2-negative) were cultured in phenol red-free DMEM (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 31053028) supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 4 mM L-glutamine, 10% FBS, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, 0.01 mg/mL human recombinant insulin (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 12585014) and 4 mM GlutaMAX. All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. In all cases, we used low passage numbers (below passage 15).

Collection of EVs from conditioned cell culture media

After plating the cells in 75 cm2 flasks, the FBS in culture media was replaced with exosome-depleted FBS (Fisher Scientific, A2720801). When the cell number reached approximately 3 × 106 per flask, the cells were split into three 150-mm cell culture dishes, each supplied with 40 mL of media. After three days of incubation, when cell confluency reached 80%, the culture media was collected. To remove cell debris, the collected cell culture media was centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min at room temperature and immediately frozen at -80 °C.

Plasma processing

The pooled human plasma (Innovative Research, IPLAWBK2E50ML) was used as a healthy control. At least two donors were pooled, but information on actual number of donors and their sex was not available. According to Innovative Research, the blood was collected in K2 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) containing dry blood collection bag and span at 5,000×g for 15 min in a refrigerated centrifuge. Plasma isolated with a plasma extractor was subsequently frozen. We applied an additional centrifugation step (2500×g for 15 min) before the experiments since platelet contamination may be largely eliminated by a second centrifugation step even in frozen samples [37]. Blood from breast cancer patients was collected under IRB 17185 and informed consent was obtained from all subjects. All subjects were female. Blood was collected at the time of surgery, post neoadjuvant treatment. At the time of blood collection, three triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients did not have residual disease, four TNBC patients had residual disease, and four patients with elevated HER2 expression had residual disease. Patient characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S6. Blood was collected in EDTA containing tubes and processed within 30 min of collection. To isolate plasma, the blood from patients (except BC45) was transferred to 15 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min at room temperature to remove red and white blood cells then transferred to a new 15 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 1200×g for 15 min at room temperature, and aliquoted and stored at -80 °C on the day of collection; an additional centrifugation at 2500×g for 15 min was performed before experiments to help remove possible platelet contaminations [37]. The blood from patient BC45 was transferred to 15 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 2100×g for 15 min at room temperature to remove red and white blood cells, and then transferred to a new 15 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 2500×g for 15 min at room temperature. All isolated plasma was aliquoted and stored in Cryogenic Tubes (Thermo Scientific, 366656) at -80 °C.

Isolation of EVs using Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC)

Using the Vivaspin 20 100 kDa concentrator, 120 mL of media from SH-SY5Y or 40 mL of media from all other cell lines was concentrated to ~ 0.4 mL as described before [38, 39]. EVs were isolated using size exclusion chromatography (SEC): 70 nm legacy qEV column (iZON; qEV original) equilibrated with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Protein content in the fractions was assessed with NanoDrop, by measuring the absorbance at 280 nm. The first five fractions, which had a high concentration of EVs and a low concentration of protein impurities, were combined.

For SEVEN, SEC isolated EVs (combined fractions 1–5) were diluted in PBS to a final volume of 80 µL, supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips. Details on dilution volumes are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Of note, when low volumes of SEC-isolated EVs were used, we employed serial dilutions.

Isolation of EVs using Capto Core resins

The spin columns and collection tubes were coated with 0.05% IgG-free BSA 0.005% Tween-20 in PBS by rotating at room temperature. Subsequently, they were washed twice with 1 mL PBS. 200 µL of Capto Core 700 (CC700, MilliporeSigma, GE17-5481-01) or Capto Core 400 (CC400, Cytiva, 17372401) resin was diluted in PBS to a final volume of 400 µL and mixed. The diluted Capto Core resin solution was then transferred to spin column (BIO-RAD, 7326008) and centrifuged at 500×g for 3 min to remove the storage buffer. After two washes with 500 µL of PBS, 100 µL of diluted plasma (50 µL of plasma diluted in PBS to a final volume of 100 µL) was placed into the capped spin column and slurry was incubated for 1 h at room temperature on a rotator. 100 µL of eluate was collected by centrifugation at 500×g for 3 min. For capturing TSPAN-enriched EVs, 6.6 µL of eluate was diluted in PBS to a final volume of 100 µL, supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips; for capturing HER2-enriched EVs, 100 µL eluate was supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips. Dilution volumes are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Isolation of EVs using Polyethylene Glycol (PEG) precipitation

400 mg/mL of polyethylene glycol (PEG, molecular weight 8000 Da, Millipore Sigma, 89510-1KG-F) solution was made in PBS. 16.7 µL of the PEG solution was added to 50 µL plasma to a final concentration of 100 mg/mL [40]. The plasma was incubated overnight at 4 °C and the precipitate was collated by centrifugation at 4 °C at 1200×g for 1 h. After removing the supernatant, the precipitate was reconstituted in 100 µL of PBS; this solution was centrifuged at 14,000×g for 5 min at room temperature to remove any insoluble components; the resulting supernatant contained isolated EVs. For capturing TSPAN-enriched EVs, 3.3 µL of resulting supernatant was diluted in PBS to a final volume of 100 µL, supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips; for capturing HER2-enriched EVs, 50 µL of resulting supernatant was diluted in PBS to a final volume of 100 µL, supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips. Of note, 50 µL was the highest volume of EV containing supernatant that reproducibly gave us good background signal with this protocol. Dilution volumes are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Isolation of EVs using thrombin treatment

Thrombin leads to plasma defibrination [41]. Protocol was based on previously published work (second spin was included to reproducibly remove debris) [42]. Briefly, thrombin at 500 U/mL (System Biosciences, TMEXO-1) was diluted twice, added to 100 µL undiluted plasma samples to a final concentration of 5 U/mL, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Plasma was centrifuged at 14,000×g for 5 min at room temperature. Supernatant was collected and centrifuged again at 14,000×g for 5 min at room temperature; resulting supernatant contained isolated EVs. For capturing TSPAN-enriched EVs, 3.3 µL of resulting supernatant was diluted in PBS to a final volume of 100 µL, supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips; for capturing HER2-enriched EVs, 50 µL of resulting supernatant was diluted in PBS to a final volume of 100 µL supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips. Starting from 100 µL of plasma, with this protocol we recovered ~ 75 µL as a supernatant. Thus, 3.3 µL of resulting supernatant corresponded to 4.4 µL of original plasma volume; 50 µL of resulting supernatant corresponded to 66.7 µL of original plasma volume. These values are reported in Supplementary Table S7 that indicates plasma dilutions.

Isolation of EVs via plasma dilution

For capturing TSPAN-enriched EVs, 1.2 µL of plasma was diluted in PBS to a final volume of 120 µL, supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips; for capturing HER2-enriched EVs, 20 µL of plasma was diluted in PBS to a final volume of 120 µL, supplemented with Tween 20 to a final concentration of 0.025%, and used for EV enrichment onto coverslips. Dilution volumes are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

TEM of negatively stained EVs was performed as described before [36, 38, 39]. Briefly, 4 µL EV samples was placed onto glow discharged 200-mesh formvar/carbon copper grids. After three washes in deionized water, EVs were stained with 1% uranyl acetate. Negative-stained samples were examined on an FEI Tecnai 12 transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at an acceleration voltage of 120 kV using LaB6 filament with a Gatan 2 k × 2 k CCD camera (Gatan; Pleasanton, CA, USA).

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

NTA was performed as described before using Nanosight nanoparticle analyzer (Malvern) [36, 38, 39].

Labeling of antibodies and measuring their photophysical properties

Clinical grade trastuzumab (Herceptin®) and commercially available tetraspanin antibodies (Abs): anti-CD9 (BioLegend, 312102), anti-CD63 (Novus Biologicals, NBP2-42225), and anti-CD81 (BioLegend, 349502) were labeled with Alexa Fluor 647 (AF647) as described before [43]. The degree of labeling ranged between 1 and 2. Photophysical properties (the average number of localizations per fluorescent probe [alpha] and maximum dark time) were determined for different Ab batches using the protocol described previously [36, 44]. Clinical grade trastuzumab was also labeled with Alexa Fluor 680 for dot blot analysis.

Dot blots

1 µL of sample was dotted onto the nitrocellulose membrane using previously described protocol [36, 38, 39]. The following primary Abs were used: anti-CD9 (BioLegend, 312102), anti-CD63 (Novus Biologicals, NBP2-42225), anti-CD81 (BioLegend, 349502) at 1:500 dilution, anti-syntenin (Abcam, ab19903) at 1:1000 dilution, anti-TSG101 (BD Biosciences, 612697) at 1:250 dilution, anti-cytochrome C (BD Biosciences, 556432) at 1:1000 dilution; anti-ApoA1 (Abcam, ab19903) at 1:1000 dilution; and appropriate secondary antibodies, either goat anti-rabbit antibody IRDye 680RD (Li-Cor Biotechnology, 926-68071) or goat anti-mouse antibody IRDye 800CW (Li-Cor Biotechnology, 926-32210)] at 1:15000 dilution, were used. To assess the expression of HER2, we used trastuzumab labeled with Alexa Fluor 680 at 1:600 dilution for 1 h. Blots were imaged with the Odyssey DLx Imaging System (Li-Cor Biotechnology).

Single Extracellular VEsicle Nanoscopy (SEVEN) imaging and analysis

EV samples were assessed with Single Extracellular VEsicle Nanoscopy (SEVEN) assay as described before [36]. Briefly, clean 25-mm diameter #1.5 H glass coverslips (Fischer Scientific, NC9560650) were coated with MCP4 polymer (Lucidant Polymers, MCP4-KIT). 0.5 µL of antibody solution containing 1% glycerol was pipetted onto the center of these coverslips. We used one of the following: (1) the mixture of anti-CD9 (BioLegend, 312102), anti-CD63 (Novus Biologicals, NBP2-42225), anti-CD81 (BioLegend, 349502) antibodies (each at 0.5 mg/mL); (2) trastuzumab (at 2 mg/mL); or (3) anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, A16112) (at 2 mg/mL). The spotted coverslips were incubated in a humidity-controlled dish for 4 h at room temperature and washed. EV samples were incubated onto antibody functionalized coverslips overnight at room temperature. Captured EVs were stained with Abs labeled with AF647 in PBS supplemented with 2% (w/v) BSA and 0.025% (v/v) Tween 20. One of the following was employed: (1) 10 nM anti-CD9, 10 nM anti-CD63, and 10 nM anti-CD81; (2) 10 nM trastuzumab; or (3) 10 nM anti-CD9, 10 nM anti-CD63, 10 nM anti-CD81, 10 nM trastuzumab. After washing, EVs were fixed and imaged as described before [36]. 41 μm x 41 μm SMLM images were analyzed using Voronoi tessellation-based clustering with Matlab R2023a (MathWorks) as previously described [36]. EVs were detected as clusters of localizations. EV size was defined as the diameter of a circle with an area equivalent with the determined cluster area of an EV. Eccentricity describes the curvature of the shape (an increase in eccentricity corresponds to a decrease in curvature; an eccentricity value of 0 represents a circle while a value between 0 and 1 represents an ellipse); circularity describes how closely a shape approaches that of a circle (1 indicates a perfect circle and 0 represents an increasingly elongated polygon). Circularity was calculated as described before [36]. Eccentricity was calculated as the ratio of the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix related to the EV localization cloud. The highest eigenvalue defines the length of the axis along which the localization distribution has the highest variance (major axis length of covariance ellipse). Likewise, the minor axis length is determined as the lowest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix. Of note, shape of EVs was previously validated by comparing SEVEN and TEM using recombinant EVs [36]. Based on published datasets, average and standard deviation for eccentricity was 0.49 ± 0.02 for SEVEN and 0.4 ± 0.1 for TEM; average and standard deviation for circularity was 0.864 ± 0.007 for SEVEN and 0.88 ± 0.02 for TEM [36].

Values obtained for the average number of localizations per fluorescent probe (alpha) were used to find correct molecular counts. For assessing cell line EVs detected with anti-TSPAN Abs, we used 4 x alpha as minimum number of localizations to define EVs; for assessing cell line EVs detected with trastuzumab, we used ~ 3 x alpha as minimum number of localizations to define EVs; for assessing TSPAN-enriched plasma EVs we used ~ 4 x alpha as minimum number of localizations to define EVs; for assessing HER2-enriched plasma EVs we used ~ 2.5 x alpha as minimum number of localizations to define EVs. Slightly different thresholds were used because HER2 was significantly less abundant compared to TSPANs; we have previously used lower threshold for rare, cancer enriched EVs [36]. EVs with minimum size of 30 nm were selected; maximal size was not limited. We excluded artifacts with extremely high number of localization (only few in all data sets were identified).

Statistics

For assessing parameters of all EVs (diameter, molecular count/EV, molecular density/EV, and circularity), logarithmic transformation was applied to approach a normal distribution and two-tailed Student t-test was used to determine p-values (in all figures, the data was plotted without logarithmic transformation). For comparing EV eccentricity, two-tailed Student t-test was used to determine p-values. For comparing average values of parameters per region of interest (ROI) and per patient (including EV counts per ROI, mean diameter per ROI, mean TSPAN molecule/EV per ROI, mean circularity, mean eccentricity, mean diameter per patient, and mean TSPAN molecule/EV per patient), Mann-Whitney test was used to determine p-values.

Results

Characterization of cultured cell line EVs

Four HER2-positive breast cancer cell lines were cultured for EV collection: (1) BT-474 (trastuzumab-sensitive, luminal B molecular subtype), (2) BT-474R (trastuzumab-resistant, luminal B molecular subtype), (3) SK-BR-3 (trastuzumab-sensitive, HER2 overexpressing molecular subtype), and (4) JIMT-1 (trastuzumab-resistant, HER2 overexpressing molecular subtype). Four HER2-negative cell lines were also cultured for EV collection: (1) SH-SY5Y (neuroblastoma), (2) HEK293T (human embryonic kidney), (3) MCF-7 (breast cancer, luminal A molecular subtype), (4) CAMA-1 (breast cancer, luminal A molecular subtype). EVs from conditioned cell culture media were isolated using size exclusion chromatography (SEC). The first five SEC fractions (F1 to F5) with low concentrations of protein impurities (Supplementary Figure S1A) were combined and used for downstream experiments. For combined fractions F1-F5, the presence of EV markers, surface markers tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81 (TSPANs) and luminal marker syntenin, as well as minimal expression of mitochondrial protein cytochrome C typically not associated with EVs were confirmed via dot blots (Fig. 1A). Dot blots in Fig. 1A also showed abundant HER2 in EVs derived from HER2-positive cell lines and minimal HER2 in EVs derived from HER2-negative cell lines. Since HER2-negative cell lines can express low levels of HER2 [45], based on dot blot results (high levels of EV markers and not detectable HER2), we selected EVs from SH-SY5Y cells as controls. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) of EVs derived from HER2-positive and control SH-SY5Y cells confirmed high particle concentration and expected size distributions (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Figure S1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed the intact morphology of EVs following SEC isolation, Supplementary Figure S1C.

EVs isolated from cell culture media were subsequently characterized with SEVEN. We affinity captured either TSPAN-enriched EVs or HER2-enriched EVs. TSPAN-enriched EVs were detected using either AF647 labeled antibodies (Abs) against TSPANs or AF647 labeled Ab against HER2 (trastuzumab); HER2-enriched EVs were detected using AF647 labeled Abs against TSPANs. Three different experimental schemes are shown on top of Fig. 2. We first evaluated the number of captured EVs per imaged region of interest (ROI); results were normalized to volume of conditioned cell culture media to enable comparison between different cell lines and different capture strategies (dilutions are detailed in Supplementary Table S1). Since most EVs contain abundant TSPANs [46], TSPAN-enriched EVs detected with fluorescent Abs against TSPANs (Fig. 2A) can be considered as general EV population. As expected, general EV population (Fig. 2A) was significantly more abundant compared to HER2-positive EV subpopulation (trastuzumab was used for either HER2 enrichment, Fig. 2B, or HER2 detection, Fig. 2C). For both luminal B and HER2 overexpressing molecular subtypes of HER2-positive breast cancer, trastuzumab-resistant cells (compared to trastuzumab-sensitive) shed more general EVs per conditioned cell culture media volume. At the same time, we found that BT-474R cells shed the most and JIMT-1 shed the least number of HER2-positive EVs. As expected, control cell line SH-SY5Y (HER2-negative [47, 48]) did not produce a significant number of HER2-positive EVs. As an additional control, EVs from HER2-positive BT-474 cells were captured on anti-rabbit IgG and stained with fluorescent trastuzumab; as expected, few EVs were detected (Supplementary Figure S2A). Descriptive statistics and p-values are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

Raw single molecule localization microscopy (SMLM) images for all conditions showed well-defined individual EVs with minimal background (Fig. 3A); enlarged individual EVs are shown in Supplementary Figure S2B. We assessed the properties of individual EVs: diameter, shape (circularity and eccentricity), detected molecular content of TSPANs or HER2, and detected density of TSPANs or HER2. For BT-474, BT-474R, SK-BR-3, and JIMT-1 cells, average diameters for general EVs (TSPAN-enriched EVs detected with Abs against TSPANs, scheme in Fig. 2A, top) were 85 nm, 84 nm, 85 nm, 100 nm, respectively (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S4). According to the coefficient of variation (CV, Supplementary Table S4), cells from HER2-overexpressing molecular subtype (compared to luminal B) shed general EVs with larger size heterogeneity.

We identified significant differences between the TSPAN- and HER2-enriched EV subpopulations (schemes in Fig. 2A, B, top, respectively); in both cases we detected TSPANs that are abundantly present on EV membranes. Thus, this approach allowed us to comprehensively interrogate the size and shape of EVs. Compared to the general EV population (TSPAN-enriched EVs), HER2-enriched EVs from four breast cancer cell lines had significantly larger diameters, larger eccentricities (more elongated shape), and lower circularities (less round shape), Fig. 3B-D and Supplementary Tables S4, S5. Additionally, HER2-enriched EVs from HER2-overexpressing molecular subtype (compared to luminal B) were significantly larger, Fig. 3B and Supplementary Tables S4, S5.

We next compared TSPAN-enriched EVs with detection of either TSPANs or HER2 (schemes in Fig. 2A, C, top, respectively); this allowed us to assess the distribution of HER2 on EVs. As expected (Supplementary Figure S2C, E, Supplementary Table S4), compared to TSPANs, HER2 molecules were less abundant. For BT-474, BT-474R, SK-BR-3, and JIMT-1 cells, the average number of detected TSPAN molecules per EVs was 22, 15, 17, 23, respectively; the average number of detected HER2 molecules per EVs was 10, 11, 12, 7, respectively. We also identified differences between EVs derived from trastuzumab-resistant and -sensitive cells. For both luminal B and HER2-overexpressing molecular subtypes, EVs from trastuzumab-resistant cells (compared to respective trastuzumab-sensitive cells) had significantly lower TSPAN density and higher HER2 density (Fig. 3E). Additionally, when HER2 was detected on TSPAN-enriched EVs, those from trastuzumab-resistant cells were significantly smaller, less elongated, and rounder (Supplementary Figure S3A-C). This suggests EVs derived from trastuzumab-resistant cells may have tightly packed HER2 clusters. Descriptive statistics and p-values are provided in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5, respectively.

Optimizing pre-analytical methods to collect EVs from human plasma

Since few EVs in plasma originate from cancer cells, we wanted to maximize loading of these rare EVs onto our coverslips. While SEC isolation has good efficiency, specificity, and integrity, it leads to ~ 4-fold loss of EVs [36]. Thus, we turned to methods that have higher EV isolation yield. Since SEVEN employs an additional affinity isolation step, EV purity was not a concern. Using plasma from one HER2-positive breast cancer patient with IHC 3+ (BC37, characteristics in Supplementary Table S6), we tested five EV isolation conditions: (1) Capto Core 700 resin (CC700); (2) Capto Core 400 resin (CC400); (3) PEG precipitation; (4) thrombin treatment; and (5) dilution of the crude plasma. The presence of EV markers TSPANs and syntenin was confirmed with dot blots (Supplementary Figure S4A). As expected, in addition to abundant EV markers, cytochrome C and Apo A were still present in most isolation protocols (Supplementary Figure S4A). SEVEN was subsequently used to assess TSPAN-enriched EVs from the 5 different isolation protocols. Raw SMLM images of TSPAN-enriched EVs show well-defined structures, Fig. 4A, top. Of note, each method required different protocol and plasma dilution to enable efficient EV capture, optimal EV surface density, and good signal-to-noise ratio for imaging (see Methods and Supplementary Table S7 for dilution details). Number of detected EVs per ROI was normalized to 1 µL of plasma. Compared to diluted crude plasma, CC700 resulted in ~ 6.7-fold EV loss, while the other three methods performed significantly better (between 1.3- and 1.9-fold loss), Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table S8. Compared to diluted plasma, CC700 isolation produced EVs that were significantly larger, less circular, and more elongated (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Figure S4B, and Supplementary Table S8 show average values for parameter per ROI; Supplementary Table S9 shows average values for all EVs; Supplementary Table S10 shows p-values). This indicates that CC700 resin may retain a fraction of smaller, largely round EVs. PEG precipitation resulted in some small but significant changes. Compared to diluted plasma, PEG-precipitated EVs had more detected TSPAN molecules per EV and lower mean eccentricity (Supplementary Figure S4B and Supplementary Tables S8-S10).

We next evaluated HER2-enriched EVs from plasma of the same HER2-positive patient. Raw SMLM images show well defined, large EVs with abundant TSPANs (red structures in Fig. 4A, bottom). When considering diluted plasma, as expected, only a small fraction (~ 0.3%) of EVs was HER2-enriched (Supplementary Table S8) further pointing to the need to optimize isolation protocol. When number of detected EVs per ROI was normalized to 1 µL of plasma, there was no significant differences between PEG precipitation, thrombin treatment, and plasma dilution; CC400 and CC700 led to slightly reduced values (Supplementary Figure S4C, and Supplementary Tables S8, S10). Since our goal was to capture as many HER2-enriched EVs as possible, we next considered the number of detected EVs per ROI (without plasma volume normalization). Thrombin treatment allowed us to use the least diluted plasma and still efficiently capture and image EVs. Compared to other approaches (Fig. 4D), thrombin treatment resulted in significantly more HER2-enriched EVs (about two-fold). No change in detected EV number with thrombin treatment was observed when EVs were stained with a combination of anti-TSPAN Abs and trastuzumab (all fluorescently labelled with AF647), suggesting TSPANs are abundant and efficiently define these EVs. As with cell line EVs, for all plasma isolation methods, HER2-enriched EVs (compared to TSPAN-enriched) were larger, more elongated, and less round, Fig. 4C, Supplementary Figure S4D, B and Supplementary Tables S8-S10. Although PEG and thrombin treatment resulted in slightly smaller HER2-enriched EVs compared to diluted crude plasma (Supplementary Figure S4D and Supplementary Tables S8-S10), other parameters were not significantly different. Of note, EV staining with a combination of trastuzumab and anti-TSPAN Abs did not change EV size or shape, but it resulted in the increase of number of detected molecules; this indicates both HER2 and TSPANs were detected on these EVs. PEG precipitation and thrombin treatment were next used to test negative controls: triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) (BC35, characteristics in Supplementary Table S6) and healthy pooled plasma. Raw SMLM images are shown in Supplementary Figure S4E. While a small number of HER2-enriched EVs from control samples was detected with both isolation methods, thrombin treatment resulted in fewer HER2-enriched EVs compared to PEG precipitation (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Figure S4C). Based on the combined data, we selected thrombin treatment for the isolation of EVs in further experiments.

Characterization of HER2-enriched EVs from breast cancer patients

Thrombin treatment was used to isolate plasma EVs collected from 3 groups of subjects: (1) HER2 group: four breast cancer patients with elevated HER2 protein expression (two patients were HER2-positive with IHC 3+; one patient was HER2-positive with IHC 1+, and one patient was HER2-low with IHC 2+), (2) TNBC group: four TNBC patients with residual disease, (3) no cancer group: three patients originally diagnosed with TNBC that had no residual disease at the time of plasma collection, and healthy pooled plasma. The patient characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table S6. The isolated EVs were captured with trastuzumab, stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies against TSPANs, and evaluated using SEVEN. Raw SMLM images (Supplementary Figure S5) show large HER2-enriched EVs for plasma of breast cancer patients with elevated HER2. We achieved excellent estimated localization precision of approximately 8 nm (Supplementary Figure S6). When data was quantified, plasma from patients with elevated HER2-yielded significantly more HER2-enriched EVs compared to TNBC patients and no cancer samples, Supplementary Figure S7A. Importantly, while there were some differences in EV properties for individual subjects (Supplementary Figure S7B, C, Supplementary Tables S11-S14), HER2-enriched EVs from patients with elevated HER2 expression were typically larger in diameter and contained more TSPAN molecules per EV; no appreciable differences were observed in circularity, Fig. 5A, Supplementary Tables S11, S12. Since size and TSPAN count appeared to show differences between groups, these two properties were used to plot 2D histogram for all detected EVs colored in groups: EV from HER2 group in red; EVs from TNBC group in blue; EVs from no cancer group in olive (Fig. 5B). Each point represents an EV and provides TSPAN content (y axis) and dimeter (x axis). As before [36, 38], we gated EVs to select for cancer-enriched population; we gated EVs that were larger in size and had higher TSPAN content, grey polygon in Fig. 5B. Patient with IHC 1+ had a lower number of gated EVs compared to patients with IHC 2+ and IHC 3+ (Fig. 5C). Importantly, significantly more gated EVs were detected from patients with elevated HER2 expression vs. 2 control groups, Fig. 5C. On average, the number of HER2-enriched EVs was 12-fold higher in HER2 group over TNBC group (compared to 6.9-fold without gating); and 25-fold higher in HER2 group over no cancer group (compared to 7.4-fold without gating).

Discussion

EVs are important biomarker candidates, and they may also have important roles in antibody-based therapy. The receptors on the EV surfaces could compete with those on target cells for available therapeutics [49]. For example, EVs with CD20 on their surface could bind to the anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody rituximab, possibly reducing the number of free antibodies available for the target lymphoma cells [50]. Similarly, EVs enriched with programmed cell death ligand 1 may contribute to resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [51]. Thus, assessment of EVs in patients on antibody therapy may be relevant for guiding treatment. Toward understanding the roles these important nanoparticles play in HER2-positive breast cancer, we used a highly sensitive approach – SEVEN – to provide multiparametric characterization of individual EVs in cultured cell lines and patient plasma.

We first assessed EVs from cell lines that are either sensitive or resistant to HER2 directed antibody therapy – trastuzumab. For both HER2-overexpressing and luminal B molecular subtypes of breast cancer, the trastuzumab-resistant cells shed a significantly larger number of general EVs (TSPAN-enriched, Fig. 2A). We detected a high number of HER2-positive EVs from BT-474R cells and low number from JIMT-1 cells (Fig. 2B, C). JIMT-1 cells also had a significantly lower number of detected HER2 molecules per EV (Supplementary Figure S2E). This is consistent with the finding that large glycoproteins on the surface of this cell line can create steric hindrance and mask trastuzumab binding site on HER2 [52, 53]. For all cell lines, HER2-enriched EVs (compared to TSPAN-enriched EVs, Fig. 3B-D) were larger, less circular, and more elongated. Identifying differences in EV shape between EV subpopulations is relevant, as it may be affected by the presence of particular membrane proteins [54] and could help identify EVs associated with cancer [55]. Interestingly, we also found that trastuzumab-resistant cells shed EVs with denser HER2, likely packed in tight, round clusters (Fig. 3E, Supplementary Figure S3). Given that HER2 is less abundant and less clustered on the membrane of these cells (compared to trastuzumab-sensitive cells) [56], EV shedding may influence HER2 distribution on cell membranes and may be mechanistically significant for HER2 therapy resistance.

All cells release soluble components, EVs, and extracellular particles; biofluids contain complex mixtures [57]. In complex samples like plasma, lipoproteins and other nanoparticles significantly outnumber EVs [58, 59]. Although cancer cells typically produce large number of EVs compared to normal cells [60, 61], the majority of EVs in the plasma originate from platelets and red blood cells [62]; only a small fraction (104-108/mL of plasma) is believed to be disease-associated [58, 59]. Thus, to characterize HER2-enriched EVs from plasma, it is essential to optimize protocols for EV isolation. Different EV isolation methods can lead to different EV yields and purity [27]. Moreover, EVs with certain properties (e.g., specific size or density range) may not be efficiently isolated with each method. Our goal was to optimize the yield of HER2-enriched EVs for SEVEN assay. Direct plasma dilution (Fig. 4) resulted in a high yield of TSPAN-enriched EVs but relatively low yield of HER2-enriched EVs. This was expected as HER2-enriched EVs are rare; plasma had to be diluted at least 6-fold for efficient EV capture due to high viscosity and we were limited by the volume we could load onto coverslip. Capto Core resins have been used successfully to isolate EVs with high yields [40, 63, 64]. Our data suggests that CC700 may retain a fraction of smaller EVs (Fig. 4B, C); this is consistent with larger pore size on CC700 resin [65] that could potentially trap small EVs. PEG precipitation and thrombin treatment produced high yield of both TSPAN- and HER2-enriched EVs (Fig. 4). However, due to centrifugation steps, these two methods could potentially remove very large EVs and oncosomes. It is also worth noting that defibration of plasma may affect certain EV populations. For example, treatment with thrombin reduced the number of EVs with major histocompatibility complex class-I [66]. Here, thrombin treatment showed the biggest yield for HER2-enriched EVs likely due to the highest overall plasma volume that was applied. Thrombin treatment also resulted in a large difference in detected EV numbers between HER2-positive patient and controls (TNBC patient and healthy pooled plasma). This protocol was also fast and straightforward, which is an important consideration for future applications. While this optimization was performed on one HER2-positive patient (with one TNBC and one healthy control), the data is consistent with prior work [42] and helped us identify methods that would not perform well with SEVEN (e.g., CC700). Importantly, different isolation approaches could be optimal for different assays and for enrichment of desired EV subpopulations.

The importance of HER2-positive EVs has been recognized in the field [67]. Compared to HER2-negative cells, HER2-positive cells release more EVs that contain abundant HER2 molecules [68, 69]. The HER2 EV levels (based on ELISA) correlated to IHC HER2 status in patients with gastric cancer [70]. Also, the number of HER2-positive EVs was elevated in patients with breast cancer and ovarian cancers as detected by antibodies conjugated with magnetic nanobeads, consistent with the expression level of HER2 on the tumor [71, 72]. In addition, proteins associated with the aberrant pathways in cancer cells, including those involved in HER2 signaling pathway, translation, and cell–cell adhesion, were found in HER2-positive EVs, reflecting the biology and proliferation ability of the tumor [67]. However, most approaches assess HER2-enriched EVs in bulk and do not provide multiparametric characterization of individual EVs. Since cancer originating EVs are rare and hard to isolate from other plasma components, sensitive techniques that can provide multiparametric characterization of HER2-enriched EVs can bring significant value for diagnostics field. SEVEN is ideally poised for this task due to high capturing efficiency, high sensitivity, and ability to provide critical parameters: size, shape, and the content of other cargo molecules [36]. SEVEN was successfully used to assess EVs from plasma of four patients with elevated HER2 (controls included TNBC and no cancer groups). Our data (Fig. 5A) suggest that HER2-enriched EVs from patients with elevated HER2 (compared to controls) had typically larger size and TSPAN contents. This could indicate increased shedding of vesicles from cell membranes. Interestingly, it has been found (digital immuno-PCR) that the number of TSPAN-positive EVs increased in patients with breast cancer [73]. Although the involvement of TSPANs in cancer development has been recognized, their roles may vary depending on the cancer type, cancer stage, and specific tetraspanin. For example, CD9 has been suggested to promote tumor growth in breast cancer and pancreatic cancer [74, 75], enhance the invasion of prostate cancer cells [76], but to suppress metastasis in lung cancer [77]. Similarly, CD81 has been found to enhance metastasis in breast cancer [78], but suppresses metastasis in bladder cancer [79]; CD63 is involved in metabolic switch and the survival of cancer cells in breast cancer [80], while it suppresses the growth and migration of hepatocellular carcinoma [81]. Furthermore, TSPANs are also associated with patient response to treatment in cancers. For example, CD9 is involved in resistance to cytotoxic drugs in TNBC [82], and CD63 is implicated in mediating resistance to docetaxel in HER2-negative breast cancer cells [83]. However, the relationship between the number of TSPAN molecules on EVs and HER2 status in breast cancer has not been investigated; the findings in this study provided a foundation for further research on this topic.

In patients with elevated HER2 expression levels, we used both the size and TSPAN content of EVs to gate cancer-associated EVs. Patients with elevated HER2 expression had on average 12-fold more gated EVs then TNBC patients and 25-fold more gated EVs than no cancer controls. The number of gated EVs was heterogeneous in the HER2 group; in contrast, two control groups had uniformly low number of gated EVs. The ability to detect HER2-enriched EVs may be associated with HER2 expression (IHC level). Patients that had HER2 amplification and IHC 1+ had lower number of detected EVs compared to patients with IHC 2+ and IHC 3+. At the same time, the patient with no HER2 amplification and IHC 2+ (HER2-low) had an appreciable number of HER2-enriched EVs. While this comprises a low number of patients, these data warrant additional experiments entailing a larger cohort.

We could efficiently detect HER2-enriched EVs using either only TSPAN or TSPAN + HER2 staining. However, it was significantly more challenging to detect them using only HER2 staining. This is likely due to the low number of EVs that have high HER2 expression combined with the experimental design: imaging many ROIs would be needed to assess an appreciable number of EVs. Since the distribution of HER2 on EVs was very informative when distinguishing trastuzumab-sensitive and -resistant cells, future advances in this area are expected to provide important information about the status of breast cancer patients.

Our approach could evaluate HER2-enriched EVs from patient plasma at many treatment time points. Serial biopsy specimens are difficult to obtain; in contrast, EVs are readily accessible and can be obtained throughout the course of therapy. During therapy, a decrease in the number of HER2 enriched EVs may indicate resistance to therapy, a change in HER2 status, or even the end of a successful treatment regimen; an increase in the number of HER2 enriched EVs may accompany changes to HER2 status or disease progression. For example, we detected a high number of HER2-enriched EVs for one patient even when the primary tumor was not clinically detectable; this patient had (micro)metastases. Thus, serial monitoring of HER2-enriched EVs may be a useful tool when combined with computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging. Importantly, timely detection of HER2-low breast cancers may be a critical opportunity for improving patient outcomes: enrichment in HER2-low tumors is observed during the progression of breast cancer [24] and trastuzumab-drug conjugates can be effective in these patients [15].

Conclusion

Trastuzumab-resistant cells secreted more general EVs. Based on EV morphology and cargo content, we could find unique characteristics of EVs from trastuzumab-resistant cells; these EVs had lower TSPAN density and higher HER2 density with tightly packed, smaller HER2 clusters. In both cell lines and patient plasma, HER2-enriched EVs (compared to TSPAN-enriched) were larger and more elongated. Thrombin pretreatment of plasma allowed us to assess abundant HER2-enriched EVs using SEVEN. For the first time, we report multiparametric characterization of individual EVs from breast cancer patients that includes size, shape, and molecular content of TSPAN molecules. Significantly more HER2-enriched EVs with increased size and TSPAN content were detected in HER2 group over controls. Thus, assessment of HER2-enriched EVs using SEVEN may have potential for diagnostics.
