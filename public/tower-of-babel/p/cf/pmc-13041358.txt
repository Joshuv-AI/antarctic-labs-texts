Risk factors for falls among older adults in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tingming Lei, Dehua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFalls among older adults are a significant public health concern. This study aims to explore and analyze the risk factors associated with falls among older adults in China, in order to develop appropriate interventions to minimize fall risk. Using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, this study analyzes the incidence of falls and employs a logistic regression model to identify the influencing factors. A total of 8,170 samples were included in the analysis. Results show that the overall incidence of falls among older adults is 18.29%. Being female, older age, poor self-rated health, depression, difficulty with activities of daily living (ADL), chronic diseases, physical pain, drinking, short sleep duration, dissatisfaction with life, and dissatisfaction with children were all significantly associated with a higher likelihood of falls. With the aging population accelerating, falls among older adults are becoming increasingly serious, severely affecting their daily functioning and quality of life. Therefore, greater attention should be paid to these risk factors, and timely, targeted prevention and intervention strategies should be implemented to effectively reduce fall incidence.Keywords: Older adults, Falls, Influencing factors, Risk factors, China

Introduction

Falls among older adults are recognized as a major public health issue [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), a fall is defined as “an event which results in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground or a lower level.” Falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury-related deaths globally [2], and finding effective strategies for fall prevention and intervention among older adults has become a public health priority.

China officially entered an aging society in the 21st century, and population aging has become one of the most pressing challenges facing the country’s social development. By the end of 2024, the number of people aged 60 and above in China had reached 310 million, with the aging rate rising from 19.8% in 2022 to 22% in 2024—an average annual increase of about 1% point [3]. Given China’s large population and rapid aging process, and based on an annual fall incidence estimate from WHO of 30% among adults over 60, more than 93 million older adults in China are projected to experience at least one fall each year. As fall risk increases, it poses a disproportionate threat to the lives of both older adults’ men and women in China [4]. On one hand, the overall incidence of fall-related injuries among Chinese older adults remains at a moderate level [5]. On the other hand, among various causes of death, most fatal injuries among China’s older adults are attributable to falls, and the trend is on the rise [6].

The incidence of falls among older adults constitutes a major public health challenge across diverse countries. A study based on data from the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe found fall incidence rates among older adults to be 7.9% in Switzerland, 9.1% in Denmark, 9.4% in Sweden, 9.5% in Austria, 11% in Italy, 11.5% in the Netherlands, 11.8% in Germany, 12.8% in Belgium, 13.9% in Estonia, 14.9% in France, and 16.2% in both Spain and the Czech Republic [7]. In Canada, data from the Canadian Community Health Survey showed that 20% of community-dwelling older adults reported experiencing a fall [8]. In South Korea, data from the National Survey of Older Koreans indicated a fall rate of over 15% [9].In China, research on falls among older adults primarily focuses on specific regions or provinces, or is limited to either urban or rural areas. For instance, the fall incidence among older adults was 20.65% in Shantou, Guangdong Province [10], 18.5% in rural Jinxi, Jiangsu Province [11], 16.88% in rural areas of County M, Anhui Province [12], and 13.1% in rural Shandong Province [13].

Although existing studies have reported the incidence of falls in the elderly in many regions of China, these evidences are from specific urban or rural samples, and the results are difficult to be directly extended to the whole country. China has a vast territory, and there are significant differences in social and economic development, public health resources and policy implementation progress between urban and rural areas and between the East and West. This difference not only affects the distribution of fall incidence, but also may lead to different directions and intensities of the same risk factor in different regions. It is impossible to describe the overall situation of the country and provide reliable intervention basis for other places only by the conclusion of local areas. Using the latest national representative survey data, this paper systematically describes the current situation and correlation strength of fall risk factors for the elderly in China under the framework of a unified method, providing an updated and comparable scientific benchmark for the follow-up mechanism research and precise intervention.

Fall-related injuries among older adults are a major public health concern in both developed and developing countries [5]. These injuries can be either fatal or non-fatal, yet most lead to negative physical and psychological consequences. The physical effects of falls among older adults commonly include soft tissue damage, dislocations, fractures, and prolonged pain [14]. Due to age-related physiological and functional decline, older adults generally require a longer recovery period following injuries caused by falls. The psychological consequences often include loss of confidence, social isolation, and reduced participation in social activities [15–17], along with fear of falling [13] and lower life satisfaction [18]. Therefore, when considering falls among older adults, it is necessary to pay attention to their physical and mental health and life satisfaction. To this end, this study selected self-rated health, mental health, activities of daily living (ADL), chronic diseases, physical pain, life satisfaction, and satisfaction with children as influencing factors.

Moreover, falls impose a substantial burden on families and society and place significant pressure on healthcare systems [8]. For example, after falling, older adults often seek assistance and emotional support [19], which can physically and emotionally strain family caregivers [20], thereby increasing the overall burden [21]. The cost of fall-related injuries among older adult’s accounts for approximately 0.85% to 1.5% of total healthcare expenditures in various countries, equating to 0.07% to 0.20% of GDP [22]. In the United States, falls among older adults result in substantial medical expenses, costing approximately USD 50 billion annually [23]. In China, the cost per fall-related injury among older adult’s ranges from USD 16 to USD 3,812 [24]. Thus, when considering falls among older adults, factors related to quality of life should not be overlooked. Accordingly, this study selects demographic factors that are closely associated with the quality of life of older adults, including educational level, marital status, place of residence, social activities, and household economic status.

Falls among older adults are typically caused by a combination of factors, including age, gender, living environment, and the use of certain medications [25, 26]. However, many older adults either fail to recognize or are unaware of their vulnerability, which increases the likelihood of such events [27].

Therefore, it is of great importance to conduct a systematic re-examination of the applicability of existing risk factors in the context of accelerating population aging and growing health risks, using more timely and nationally representative data. This will not only provide a theoretical basis for fall prevention and intervention strategies among older adults in China, but also offer useful references for prevention strategies in aging societies worldwide.

Data and methods

Data source

The data used in this study come from the fifth wave (2020) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), released in 2023 by the National School of Development at Peking University. The CHARLS survey was designed with reference to a series of international aging studies, including the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), and the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE). It adopted a multi-stage stratified probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling method. The CHARLS sample includes respondents from 450 villages in 150 counties across 28 provinces in China.

Detailed information on family structure, health, employment, retirement, and pension is collected through the CHARLS questionnaire, offering rich data on key characteristics of older adults in China [28]. This offers a broad and precise micro-level data foundation for the present study. The CHARLS survey received approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (Approval Number: IRB00001052-11015). Methods in this study were conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant local ethical guidelines and regulations. All participants in the CHARLS survey provided written informed consent before completing the interviews.

Based on the research objectives, we selected older adults aged 60 and above as the study population. After variable selection and data cleaning, a final sample of 8,170 older adults was included, consisting of 4,105 males and 4,065 females.

Variable description

The dependent variable is whether older adults have experienced a fall. This is measured based on the survey question “Have you ever fallen?” Respondents who answered “Yes” were coded as 1, and those who answered “No” were coded as 0.

The selection of influencing factors was based on existing literature and the fall risk factor classification framework. This framework posits that fall risk factors can be categorized into internal factors and external factors [29]. Internal factors generally pertain to personal attributes, including demographic characteristics, health status, and certain individual behaviors. These variables consist of age, gender, place of residence, marital status, education level, self-rated health, mental health, ADL, chronic disease status, physical pain, sleep duration, drinking, and smoking. Among these, age, gender, place of residence, marital status, and education level are considered non-modifiable, while the remaining health and behavioral variables are modifiable. External factors primarily reflect the individual’s social relationships and environmental context. These include participation in social activities, life satisfaction, satisfaction with children, and household economic status. All of these external factors are potentially modifiable and may be influenced by social support systems or policy interventions.

Demographic variables include age, gender, place of residence, marital status, and education level, which are commonly included in fall risk studies among older adults [10]. Age is grouped into three categories: 60–64, 65–69, and 70 years and above. Gender is categorized as male or female. Place of residence indicates whether older adults live in rural or urban areas. Marital status is classified into two groups: with spouse and without spouse. Education level is divided into three categories based on the highest level completed: illiterate, junior high school or below, and high school or above.

Regarding health-related factors of falls among older adults, studies have shown that various health conditions significantly affect fall risk [7, 25]. Self-rated health is measured based on the question “How would you rate your health?” and categorized into two groups: good and poor. Mental health is measured by the presence or absence of depression. The depression symptoms scale used in this study is the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D10) [30], which has been validated in Chinese older adult respondents using CHARLS data [31], with high reliability and validity. According to the scoring rules of the Chinese version of CES-D10, the options “<1 day,” “1–2 days,” “3–4 days,” and “5–7 days” are coded as 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively, with reverse scoring for the items “I feel hopeful about the future” and “I am happy.” The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depression symptoms. Based on existing research on depression criteria [30, 32], we define a CES-D10 score ≥ 10 as indicating the presence of depressive symptoms. In our study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the depression scale is 0.7802. ADL are defined based on the respondent’s difficulty in the following activities: dressing, bathing, eating, getting in and out of bed, using the toilet (e.g., squatting, standing), and controlling urination and defecation. Chronic disease status is measured by the number of chronic diseases diagnosed by a doctor for each respondent. Physical pain is measured based on the respondent’s feedback regarding pain in different parts of their body.

Some behavioral variables have also been included in studies on fall risk factors among older adults [12]. Drinking behavior is assessed based on self-reported monthly alcohol consumption, while smoking is measured by current smoking status. Sleep duration refers to the respondent’s self-reported average number of hours of actual sleep per night over the past month. In addition, several other factors are considered, including life satisfaction [18], satisfaction with children, social activities [17], and household economic status. Life satisfaction is assessed based on the question “Overall, are you satisfied with your life?” and categorized as satisfied or dissatisfied. Satisfaction with children is measured by the question “Are you satisfied with your relationship with your children?” and similarly categorized. Social activity is determined by whether the respondent engaged in any social activities in the past month, such as visiting friends, playing mahjong, chess, or cards, or participating in community group activities. Household economic status is measured by whether any household member has ever been or is currently registered as part of the government’s officially designated poor households. Statistical analysis

This study first conducted descriptive statistics to summarize the basic characteristics of the sample population. Group comparisons were then performed based on whether the older adults experienced falls, and chi-square tests were used to assess the significance of differences. Subsequently, logistic regression analysis was employed to explore the influencing factors of falls among older adults. The odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) from the logistic regression indicate the risk of falls associated with various characteristics. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Regarding health-related variables, older adults with poor self-rated health had a higher risk of fall compared to those with good self-rated health (OR = 1.376, 95% CI: 1.201–1.577). Compared to older adults without depression, those with depression had a higher fall risk (OR = 1.272, 95% CI: 1.113–1.455). Older adults with ADL difficulties had a higher fall risk compared to those without ADL difficulties (OR = 2.112, 95% CI: 1.850–2.410). Those with chronic diseases had a higher fall risk compared to those without chronic diseases (OR = 1.148, 95% CI: 1.017–1.296). Older adults experiencing physical pain had a higher fall risk than those without physical pain (OR = 1.730, 95% CI: 1.498–1.997).

In terms of behavioral factors, older adults who reported drinking had a higher fall risk compared to non-drinkers (OR = 1.346, 95% CI: 1.171–1.547). Those with shorter sleep duration had a higher fall risk than those with longer sleep duration (OR = 1.197, 95% CI: 1.051–1.363). In addition, older adults who were dissatisfied with life had a higher fall risk compared to those who were satisfied (OR = 1.255, 95% CI: 1.034–1.522), and those dissatisfied with their children had a higher fall risk than those who were satisfied (OR = 1.432, 95% CI: 1.125–1.822). Interestingly, older adults who had participated in social activities in the past month had a higher fall risk than those who had not (OR = 1.183, 95% CI: 1.049–1.334). Other variables in our analysis were not significantly associated with fall risk.

Discussion

Based on CHARLS data, this study examines the incidence and main risk factors of falls among older adults in China. Our findings reveal an overall fall rate of 18.29% among older adults, consistent with a previous meta-analysis showing a median rate of 18% [33]. Compared to the fall rates among older adults in other countries mentioned earlier, the incidence of falls among Chinese older adults is relatively high, underscoring the need for focused attention on this issue.

Based on the classification of internal and external factors within the fall risk factor classification framework, this study examined the influencing factors that were statistically significant. The results showed that significant internal factors included demographic characteristics such as gender and age, health status including self-rated health, mental health, ADL, chronic disease status, and physical pain, as well as individual behavioral variables like sleep duration and drinking. Significant external factors included life satisfaction and satisfaction with children. Among these, gender and age are considered non-modifiable, while the remaining factors related to health status, behavioral variables, and social relationships are generally modifiable and can be improved through behavioral interventions or policy measures.

Among demographic variables, we found that the risk of fall is higher among female older adults compared to their male counterparts, which is consistent with previous studies [34]. This may be related to the age-related decline in muscle mass and strength among females, which affects their balance and physical stability [35, 36]. Additionally, the higher prevalence of arthritis among females [37], as well as the tendency of females to wear inappropriate footwear during certain activities [38, 39], this renders being female associated with a higher risk of falls. Therefore, it is particularly important for female older adults to take preventive measures to avoid falls in daily life. In line with earlier findings [25], age is positively associated with the risk of falls. This could be attributed to age-related physiological decline, including impaired gait stability, deteriorating vision and hearing, and lower limb injuries, all of which reduce the ability to perform daily activities and significantly heighten the risk of fall [40, 41]. Targeted interventions such as strength and balance training should be prioritized to help older adults improve their physical coordination and stability. In addition, community-based fall prevention initiatives can help raise awareness and improve safety among older adults at high risk of falls.

In terms of personal health-related variables, consistent with previous research [42], poor self-rated health is associated with a higher risk of falls among older adults. Self-reported health status is an important indicator of overall health [43] and typically reflects their physical functioning, balance ability, and muscle strength. Depression is associated with a higher risk of fall among older adults, consistent with earlier findings [42, 44]. This may be because depressive symptoms can lead to psychomotor impairments, such as slower motor responses and reduced reaction time, which compromise balance and the ability to cope with environmental challenges [45]. It is important to pay special attention to older adults with poor self-rated health and depressive symptoms. Providing comprehensive psychological support and targeted interventions can improve their physical and mental health, thereby reducing their risk of falling.

Difficulties in ADL are associated with a higher risk of falls among older adults, consistent with previous findings [46]. This is likely because fall risk is closely associated with ADL capacity [47]. Those with ADL difficulties often lack the ability to perform daily tasks independently and tend to have poorer balance and coordination. The presence of chronic diseases has also been shown to be associated with a higher risk of falls among older adults [48], which is consistent with our findings. Recurrent symptoms associated with chronic diseases can negatively affect balance function [49]. For example, older adults with diabetes or hypertension may experience neuropathy or fluctuations in blood pressure, which impair their balance and are associated with a higher risk of falls [50, 51]. Moreover, experiencing physical pain is associated with a higher risk of falls among older adults, which is consistent with previous research [25]. Pain is a common and debilitating health stressor that often limits daily activities [52], which may in turn be associated with greater vulnerability to falls. Therefore, it is recommended that older adult care service institutions and community organizations strengthen their focus on the physical health of older adults by implementing health promotion activities aimed at chronic disease management, pain relief, and the improvement of daily living abilities to effectively reduce the risk of falls.

As for personal behavior-related factors, consistent with previous research [39], alcohol consumption is associated with a higher risk of falls among older adults. This may be because alcohol consumption affects physiological functions and significantly impairs postural control, which is detrimental to balance [53]. Older adult drinkers are more sensitive to the coordination impairments caused by alcohol [54]. Older adult with short sleep duration is at higher risk of fall, consistent with previous findings [55]. Sleep is a crucial factor for initiating or maintaining physical activity [56], and insufficient sleep can lead to poor mental state. Additionally, sleep problems may impair cognitive function and psychomotor performance, with significant associations found between sleep deprivation, slower gait speed, and dynamic instability [57, 58], which is associated with a higher risk of falls. Given these findings, communities should implement targeted health education programs and behavioral interventions to help older adults manage alcohol consumption and improve sleep quality, with the aim of reducing the risk of falls.

Regarding other associated variables, dissatisfaction with life and with one’s children is associated with a higher risk of falls among older adults. A key factor associated with the life satisfaction of older adults is family relationships [59]. Family serves as the most important bond in social life, providing individuals with a sense of belonging, security, and support. A lack of family support or dysfunctional family dynamics can lower life satisfaction, which often triggers negative emotions [60]. Negative emotions can affect the overall well-being of older adults, which is associated with a higher risk of falls. Furthermore, in China’s traditional family culture centered around filial piety, older adults often exhibit strong emotional dependence on their children and high expectations. Filial caregiving is not merely a form of intergenerational resource transfer, but also a core source of emotional value, social recognition, and late-life dignity for older adults. Strained parent–child relationships, imbalanced intergenerational reciprocity, or unmet expectations of support from children are associated with negative emotional states such as depression and anxiety in older adults, which in turn are linked to poorer postural control and gait stability [61]. Older adults who are dissatisfied with their children tend to be less likely to actively seek family support and may exhibit avoidance in intergenerational interactions. Such avoidance is also associated with fewer health maintenance behaviors [62]. Factors deeply rooted in Eastern culture and family structure differ from fall risk factors identified in Western populations, emphasizing the need to develop localized fall prevention strategies.

An interesting finding is that social activities are associated with a higher risk of falls among older adults, which contradicts previous studies [47]. This may be related to China’s unique neighborhood culture: older adults who have experienced a fall may, due to being in a recovery period or receiving more care from family members, actually increase their frequency of home-based social activities and community visits [63]. Moreover, any outdoor activity increases the duration of environmental exposure, thereby raising the absolute probability of accidental events [64]. This study did not collect data on the specific type, intensity, location, or duration of social activities. The dichotomous variable (yes/no) cannot distinguish between low-risk social interaction and high-risk exposure, which may lead to bias in the overall estimate. In summary, the positive association between social activities and falls observed in this study should be interpreted with caution. The value of this counterintuitive finding lies not in “what it proves,” but in the question it raises for further investigation: fall prevention policies should neither simply encourage nor restrict social activities, but rather focus on “what types of social activities, in what environments, and for which older adults are safe.” Future research should further deconstruct the heterogeneity of social activities and their complex relationship with fall risk.

It is important to note that large sample sizes can produce statistically significant results even when the corresponding odds ratios are relatively small. Therefore, distinguishing between statistical significance and clinical or practical relevance is essential. Such findings should be interpreted with due caution and within the appropriate context.

Conclusion

Our study indicates that the incidence of falls among older adults in China is relatively high. As China’s population continues to age, the number of older adults experiencing falls may further increase, leading to greater pressure and challenges for families and society. Identifying effective methods to prevent falls in older adults has become a public health priority. Based on the factors influencing falls in older adults, we recommend the following measures: Integrate ADL assessment (OR = 2.112) and rehabilitation interventions into the core components of community-based health management for older adults. Elevate the management of physical pain (OR = 1.730) from a treatment-oriented approach to a risk prevention strategy. Incorporate family relationship quality (OR = 1.432) into fall risk screening systems for older adults. Reconsider intervention strategies for social activities—shifting the focus from “encouraging participation” to “type differentiation and environmental suitability.” Promote the design of localized fall prevention strategies based on culture-specific risk factors.

Our study has several advantages. First, the main strength of this study lies in its use of a nationally representative sample, which provides rich data to support the reliability of our conclusions. Second, we examined a range of risk factors influencing falls among older adults, which enhanced the comprehensiveness of our findings. Finally, our results not only provide valuable evidence for fall prevention among older adults in China but also offer useful insights for other countries, particularly for developing nations, in formulating fall prevention and intervention strategies.

However, this study has certain limitations. First, due to the use of cross-sectional data, the causal relationship between falls and related factors among older adults cannot be established. Second, the 2020 CHARLS survey data was collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, and some factors related to falls (such as medication use, vision status, BMI, etc.) were not included in the survey, meaning their impact on falls among older adults was not assessed. The use of simple dichotomous measurements for several key variables in this study limits the granularity of the conclusions. Finally, the information collected from participants was self-reported, which introduces a potential risk of recall bias.
