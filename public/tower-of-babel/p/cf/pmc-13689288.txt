The impact of music listening on well-being in older adults: a systematic review
Authors: Sohail Akhtar, Alessandra Buja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapid growth of the global aging population makes it more essential to highlight the significance of well-being in later life. Artistic and cultural activities, such as passive music listening, may have a potential impact on psychological well-being, emotional well-being, and social well-being in older people. The aim of this paper is to conduct a systematic review of the evidence regarding the influence of music listening on the well-being of older adults focused primarily on emotional, psychological, and social outcomes.MethodsA systematic search of Medline (PubMed), Embase (Ovid), and PsycINFO/CINAHL (EBSCO) was undertaken until September 1, 2025, for published studies. Eligible studies were those that investigated older adults aged ≥ 60 years, from the general population, and investigated passive music listening (self-selected by participants or pre-selected and administered) on well-being outcomes. Both quantitative and qualitative designs were included.ResultsIn total, we identified 1,560 records of which 13 studies met all inclusion criteria. The studies were conducted in diverse geographical regions (Canada, China, Northern Ireland, Sweden, Australia, the United States, Thailand, and Hong Kong) and that the sample sizes ranged from 8 to 1,024 participants. Various study designs were included in this systematic review including randomized controlled trials, quasi-experiments studies, cross-sectional studies, longitudinal cohorts, and qualitative studies. Listening to music was consistently associated with improved mood, improved emotional regulation, and lowered anxiety, especially during the experiences of stress, illness and social isolation. For the additional benefit of reducing feelings of loneliness listening to music within a group or community context may have provided added effects.ConclusionThis review provides evidence that listening to music can help with emotional, psychological, and social well-being for older adults. Its diversity across different settings and cultures points to the potential to provide a cost-effective, scalable, and non-pharmacological means to support healthy ageing. Further rigorously designed higher quality studies with standardized outcomes would enhance the causal quality of the evidence base to inform practice better.PROSPERO registrationCRD420251138466Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-27955-4.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization, the number of people aged 60 years and over will reach 2.1 billion by 2050 from about 1 billion in 2020, with older adults projected to outnumber children under 5 [1]. Beyond demographic change, older adults face an increased risk of chronic disease, cognitive decline, loneliness, and lower quality of life [2–5]. Clearly, societies will continue to change, and we must take active measures to create affordable, minimally invasive, and available measures to support healthy ageing and the well-being of older adults. There is increasing focus on non-pharmacological treatments to support healthy ageing. Among these approaches, music listening has gained attention because of its potential benefits for emotional, cognitive and social well-being [6, 7]. The systematic review focuses on emotional, psychological and social well-being, aligning with the multidimensional theory proposed by Corey [8]. Within this approach, emotional well-being relates to life satisfaction, psychological well-being comprises personal growth, autonomy, meaning in life, and self-acceptance, and social well-being includes social integration, social contribution, social coherence, and social acceptance.

As a universal human behaviour, music has an innate ability to motivate emotional development, social engagement, and overall well-being (physical functioning, emotional stability, cognitive engagement, social participation, and perceived quality of life) [9]. Especially for older adults, listening to music may be helpful in obtaining positive emotions, decreasing depressive symptoms, and providing a source of comfort when feeling lonely [10, 11]. Listening to music is often considered by older adults to be a fun and enjoyable activity because it tends to foster relaxation, reduce anxiety, and offer some distraction from uncomfortable situations [12, 13]. Music may also help evoke memories with personal and autobiographical significance, stimulate cognitive engagement, and offer some element of expression, even when there are cognitive challenges [14, 15]. Numerous studies concerning the benefits of music listening have demonstrated that music can positively influence multiple dimensions of well-being [16–23]. Passive music listening may help reduce stress, lower blood pressure, promote relaxation, and support cognitive functions such as verbal fluency, recognition memory, and working memory among older adults [24–26]. The present review explicitly focuses on passive music listening, which involves listening to music without active performance. Unlike other types of musical activities, passive music listening is relatively inexpensive, readily available, and does not necessitate any particular expertise or physical involvement.

Despite growing research interest in this area, the existing literature remains fragmented and methodologically heterogeneous, with considerable variation in study designs, populations, settings, and well-being outcomes. Furthermore, there have been no prior studies conducted specifically on passive music listening and its effect on older adults’ well-being. These limitations make it difficult to draw comprehensive conclusions regarding its multidimensional effects on well-being. Therefore, it is important to conduct a systematic review in order to assess whether there are any multidimensional effects on well-being due to passive music listening among older adults.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review in accordance with PRISMA [27] guidelines (supplementary file S1) and registered the protocol in PROSPERO (CRD420251138466).

Selection process

The Embase (via Ovid), PsycINFO/CINAHL (via EBSCO) databases, and Medline (via PubMed) were searched using the search strings located in the appendix (supplementary file S2), with literature searched from inception until September 1, 2025. Two authors (S.A. and A.B.) screened the identified articles independently. Articles screening was conducted in two stages: first at the title and abstract level, followed by full-text review for eligibility. Authors resolved disagreements by discussion. The screening process occurred based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were selected based on predefined eligibility criteria developed in line with the review objectives.

Data extraction

To systematize the data collection and synthesize the information with regards to the studies incorporated, a structured data collection tool was developed. The tool included the information that relates to the following categories (i) study-related (first author, year of publication, country and study design); (ii) population-related (sample size (age range) and mean age); and (iii) intervention (including the outcome measures, key findings and the corresponding domains of well-being). Data were extracted out of the entire texts of all the included studies independently by two authors (S.A. and A.B.). The differences were solved by mutual discussion.

Risk of bias assessment

A risk of bias assessment was conducted to evaluate the methodological quality and reliability of the included studies. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the revised JBI critical appraisal tool for assessing the risk of bias in RCTs was used [28]. For observational quantitative studies (cross-sectional), the JBI critical appraisal tool for analytical cross-sectional studies was used [29]. For quasi-experimental designs, the JBI critical appraisal checklist for Quasi-Experimental studies was used [30]. For qualitative studies, the JBI appraisal tool for qualitative studies [31] was utilized. The quality assessment was conducted independently by two authors (S.A. and A.B.), and any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

Data synthesis

The synthesis process was characterised by a mixed deductive-inductive approach. In the initial stages, there was an application of a deductive approach, which was generally guided by multidimensional approaches to understand the well-being of old adults. In our data synthesis approach, we adopted an inductive thematic mapping approach in relation to the synthesis of measures, constructions, and qualitative findings from all the included studies. The synthesis approach was based on well-being-related findings that were abstracted from all the included studies, which were coded in relation to descriptive different themes. These themes were discussed among both authors (S.A. and A.B.) to reach the consensus.

Result

A total of 1560 records (Fig. 1) were identified via database searches, comprising 783 from Embase (Ovid), 260 from PsycINFO/CINAHL (via EBSCO) and 517 from Medline (PubMed). Before screening began, 308 duplicate records were removed via Zotero software, as well as one retracted article, leaving 1251 records to be screened. During screening, 1085 records were removed based on the exclusion criteria of title and abstract alone. 166 reports were requested, only 4 of which could not be retrieved. Eligibility was assessed gathering all information on the remaining 162 reports to evaluate inclusion or exclusion. In total, 70 reports were excluded for being irrelevant to the research aim, 27 based on the fact individuals were under the age of 60, 40 were excluded based on participants having specific illnesses, and 12 studies did not mention age at all. As a result, 13 studies met all the qualifying criteria and included in the final review.

Characteristics of included studies

The studies that were included [10, 32–43] were published from 2007 [10] to 2025 [32], and represented a range of countries including Canada [32], China [33], Northern Ireland [34], Sweden [10, 35, 41, 43], Australia [36, 38, 40], the United States [37], Thailand [42], and Hong Kong [39]. Thus, this review addresses music listening in later life from both Western and Asian perspectives (Table 1). All studies focused on older adults aged greater than or equal to 60 years and reported sample sizes ranging from 8 [32] to 1,024 [37] participants. The study designs varied, including qualitative inquiries (n = 5), cross-sectional surveys (n = 2), quasi-experimental interventions (n = 4), randomized controlled trials (n = 1), and longitudinal cohort studies (n = 1). Study settings included those in institutional care, community-based programs, and home-based interventions indicating some flexibility for music listening as an intervention in different living arrangements. A range of outcome measures were employed including the Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale, Geriatric Depression Scale, WHOQOL instruments, and thematic interviews capturing psychological, emotional, social, behavioral, and existential domains of well-being. Eleven studies consisted of listening to solo music, and 3 were group listening. Assessment of risk of bias indicated that 10 studies [10, 32, 34–36, 38, 40–43] had a low risk of bias, while 3 studies [33, 37, 39] had a moderate risk of bias.

Emotional and psychological well-being

Each of the studies included in the review found a consistent positive link between listening to music and emotional well-being. Nine out of thirteen studies that considered this outcome reported a positive link, while the remaining studies identified context-dependent or temporary emotional responses rather than overall negative effects. Studies in Canada [32], Northern Ireland [34], Sweden [10, 35], Australia [38, 40], and Hong Kong [39] employed a variety of methods, including an RCT, one quasi-experimental study, a time-series design, and some qualitative studies. Across these studies (Table 1), methodological diversity was evident, including randomized controlled trials (n = 1), quasi-experimental designs (n = 4), cross-sectional studies (n = 3), cohort studies (n = 1), and qualitative approaches (n = 5), with sample sizes ranging from 8 to 1,024 participants and encompassing both community-dwelling and institutionalized older adults. In spite of these varieties in methodology, the studies converged on the conclusion that listening to music can have a reliable, positive effect on mood and emotional regulation and coping skills for older adults, particularly under conditions of stress, illness, or social isolation. However, a considerable proportion of evidence was derived from small-scale or non-randomized studies conducted in institutional care settings, which may limit the generalizability of findings to broader older populations. There is quantitative evidence in support of these conclusions. Bissonnette et al. [32] conducted a time series study and found significant improvement in composite symptom scores including anxiety, pain, and depression after participants listened to pre-selected music during personalized music listening sessions. As such, it is important to note that the current study has been conducted on an institutionally sampled group of individuals (n = 8; average age 85.4). Wang and Suh [33] also documented music use as a positive coping strategy that related to better life satisfaction, happiness, and stress regulation in a cross-sectional study indicating a protective role of music during the COVID-19 pandemic. This larger survey-based study (n = 329) contrasts with smaller experimental studies, suggesting that positive associations persist across different sample sizes and study designs. Groarke et al. [34] identified that older adults could obtain emotional comfort, distraction from worry, and resilience through self-selected listening to music during a period of isolation from the pandemic. Likewise, Lindblad and de Boise [35] found that men at risk of loneliness could experience emotional expression and psychological stability through their participation in group listening to music.

Additional studies offer further support for these findings. Krause and Davidson [40] reported that for residents in residential aged care, listening to music independently on a day-to-day basis is a source of enjoyment, relaxation, and managing emotional states. They described music listening as a type of cultural resource for boosting emotional and psychological safety, and well-being. Additionally, Krause [36] reported that listening to music from radio stations was a simple, low-cost, and integrated resource for emotional security, relaxation, and companionship to the listening experience. Qualitative studies (Table 1) consistently emphasized subjective experiences such as meaning-making, identity, and emotional comfort, which are not always captured through quantitative measures. The findings also illustrate how culturally appropriate music listening is a habitual practice that can also serve as a source of psychosocial benefit for individuals. Yet, Theorell and Horwitz [41] found that while listening to music tended to increase feelings of joy and calmness, sad types of music would briefly increase feelings of sadness and anxiety, which potentially alludes to emotional valence in the music moderating a listener’s emotional state. This highlights some variability in outcomes depending on music type and individual response, indicating that effects are not uniformly positive across all contexts. Bradshaw et al. [37] showed that listening to religious music predicted lower levels of death anxiety and greater life satisfaction, self-esteem, and sense of control over three years. These findings suggest a long-term effect on psychological health outcomes. Similarly, Wattanasoei et al. [42] found that listening to warm Thai music that was culturally familiar, increased autonomy, intimacy, and happiness in older adults, which contributed to both emotional and psychological well-being. Furthermore, Travers and Bartlett [38] found that nostalgic radio music listening (Silver Memories program) increased mood and quality of life. In a randomized control trial, Lee, Chan, and Mok [39] found that 30 min of daily music listening significantly increased quality of life, covering emotional and mental health domains. Finally, Laukka [10] also found that intentional music listening for the purposes of mood regulation and identity affirmation predicted affective well-being and personal growth, indicating music has a role in eudaimonic well-being. In conclusion, while methodology and population variations exist, the strength of positive outcomes obtained through various research designs is enhanced, although different assessment instruments and length of interventions prevent comparisons among studies.

Social and psychosocial well-being

Listening to music also demonstrated a strong association with perceived social and psychosocial well-being through social connectedness, belonging, and companionship for older adults. As shown in Table 1, evidence for social outcomes was derived from a mix of qualitative, quasi-experimental, and cross-sectional studies conducted across community, home-based, and institutional settings, reflecting a broad contextual base. In heterogeneous cultural settings, music engagement enhanced perceived social support, and reduced social isolation, and connectedness in the absence of adequate physical interaction. Qualitative data supports this social function. For example, Groarke et al. [34] noted that self-selected music listening provided a sense of “social surrogate” for older adults learning to live with less social engagement during isolation, facilitating the maintenance of companionship and emotional closeness from a distance. Similarly, Lindblad [43] reported that collective music listening in a group of older men provided opportunities for engaging socially and empathetically around music and conversation about music. Such results were largely obtained from small-scale qualitative studies (n = 8–32). For instance, Krause and Davidson [40] noted that such everyday listening activities in residential aged care facilities had a positive influence on the creation of a sense of community and culture, even during solitary listening sessions. Krause [36] noted that listening to the radio as a habit had an influence on fostering feelings of comfort, companionship, and connectedness through cultural memories. Additionally, Lindblad and de Boise [35] showed that solitary listening and shared listening promoted relaxation and a sense of belonging and continuity; this proved that music is not only used in private but also in a communal setting. This implies that both solitary listening and group listening activities are linked to social benefits, indicating that social wellness can be achieved through different listening methods. Travers and Bartlett [38] found that listening to nostalgic music for three months using the “Silver Memories” radio program improved quality of life positively in community and residential settings. This quasi-experimental study (n = 113) provides stronger empirical support compared to smaller qualitative findings, though it still lacks full randomization. The “Silver Memories” program not only established emotional uplift but also established connection to the past few months of shared cultural style. Wattanasoei et al. [42] explored the role of listening to Thai music and concluded that music listening increased the life satisfaction in older adults. Lee, Chan, and Mok [39] found that daily music listening improved the social functioning domain of quality of life, indicating music may have the potential to encourage engagement with each other in a community context. Bissonnette et al. [32] found personal songs strengthens social responsiveness as an indirect measure and, thus, leads to social well-being. Laukka [10] reported that older adults used music for ingredients of identity when reminiscing, suggesting music provides for continuity of self-in-relation to others, which is critical for psychosocial well-being. Overall, while findings consistently indicate positive social and psychosocial outcomes, variability in study design, measurement tools, and population characteristics highlights the need for more standardized and longitudinal research in this domain.

Discussion

The evidence synthesized within the current systematic review has emphasized the relationship between passive music listening and different dimensions of well-being among older adults. The studies included within the current review emphasize that listening to music can have a positive impact on emotional and psychological states, especially with regards to the promotion of positive moods, stress reduction, and feelings of relaxation and comfort. These findings can especially clarify the relationship between passive music listening and well-being among older adults by focusing on music listening as a distinct form of music engagement that is separate from music participation or music therapy. This can especially clarify the potential role of music listening as a supportive factor that can improve well-being among older adults. The findings in this review are consistent with previous reviews evidencing a role for music to support mood, reduce stress, and increase social connectedness in later life [44–46]. The studies that were conducted regarding passive music listening have shown that there are positive outcomes regarding anxiety, mood, pain, and quality of life in older adults. However, the studies should be viewed with caution because of the differences in the methodologies that were utilized in the studies that were included in the review. Additionally, there are several factors that affect the effectiveness of passive music listening in older adults. These factors include music preference, environment, and support. Therefore, the effectiveness of passive music listening in older adults should be viewed as relative and variable in different settings and populations. Therefore, although the studies that were included in this review have shown that passive music listening has some value in the contributions that can be made for the public health and well-being interventions in older adults, further studies need to be conducted in order to determine the mechanisms and the ways in which passive music listening can be utilized for the public health and well-being interventions in older adults.

Looking at emotional and psychological well-being, the majority of studies observed a similar beneficial effect on mood, as measured by coping with stressors or changes due to an illness, isolation of self or others, loss, or just dealing with everyday life changes. Controlled studies report that both anxiety and positive affect decreased and increased respectfully after listening interventions [32, 39, 41]. Qualitative studies echoed participants own words to describe music as an anchor for comfort, happiness, and emotional security in stressful times such as the COVID-19 pandemic [34]. This is further support for the understanding that music can serve as an emotional resource, particularly when the music is personalized or somehow relevant to the listener. Some studies reported differences in effect due to depending on the familiarity and preference level with the music being listened to [41], suggesting tailoring may serve a valuable purpose in order to enhance emotional benefit. Cross-cultural evidence has further strengthened this idea, in that during the COVID-19 pandemic, people in many parts of the world engaged the use of music as an emotion regulation and strategy to cope with stress [47, 48]. The current study findings supported the same idea of music listening is an emotionally beneficial activity. Further findings reported that listening-based interventions also decreased anxiety symptom level, stress, and negative affect while increasing resilience and life satisfaction [32–35]. Importantly, across both institutional and community settings, the findings converged, suggesting that music listening is an effective non-pharmacological (behavioural or lifestyle-based) approach to improving the mental health of older adults. Overall, the findings would suggest that augmented music listening experience (individualized or group experience) is a pragmatic, low-cost intervention to prompt mood regulation, and satisfaction in life, and positively impacts the mental health of older adults in both community and institutional contexts [49].

In terms of social well-being, the listening to music interventions provided strong evidence for building connectedness and reducing isolation. Group listening to music was also a format that could facilitate social interaction and connection [34, 43]. Even in a restricted situation such as the COVID-19 pandemic, music was described as a “social surrogate” that could facilitate older adults’ connection to perceived social bonding and connections during a time of physical distancing. In residential care, group listening provided older adults with a structure that focused on social engagement and community building that strengthened social identity and reduced feelings of social isolation. The results support a wider context in which music is theorized as a cultural and relational resource to support social integration and emotional resilience in older adults.

Implications

This systematic review has important implications for public health and for supporting the well-being of older adults in both community and institutional care settings. The evidence from the systematic literature indicated that listening to music is a low-cost, accessible and flexible way to enhance and improve overall well-being. In terms of emotional well-being, listening to music may be an adjunct to mental health strategies as a further means to support mood and emotional regulation. The supporting evidence that listening to music is associated with decreases in anxiety, stress and depressive symptoms suggests that a music listening experience may be viewed as a non-pharmaceutical alternative and adjunct to traditional supports for older adults who may be experiencing any illness, loneliness or isolation. As it relates to psychological well-being, the review evidence identified structured and individualized methods for music listening, which support resilience and coping, and can support improvements in overall life-satisfaction. Listening to music in a meaningful, unstructured way as a casual means of music listening may be integrated into nursing home care plans, community-based approaches and mental health approaches to improve emotional stability and contribute to better psychological well-being in later life. In the context of social well-being, the findings indicated that structured music listening practices in community settings were associated with increased social interaction, reduced loneliness, and enhanced social connectedness. Community-based group listening interventions in community centers, aged-care facilities or among older adult groups may help combat social isolation and encourage supportive networks among older peoples. The results as a whole indicated that music listening is a flexible, adaptable and culturally suitable option for inclusion in preventative health policies and day-to-day care strategies for older adults.

Advantages and limitations

This systematic review has some advantages as well as a few limitations. First, to our knowledge, no systematic review of the effect of listening to music on older adults’ well-being has yet been conducted. The systematic review was also registered with PROSPERO in the interest of methodological transparency and avoiding duplication of effort. A strength was it had employed rigorous methods including broad search strategies, precise eligibility criteria, and independent dual review. The organization of outcomes into emotional, psychological, and social domains were useful for enabling more interpretative insight about the numerous means by which music can benefit well-being. Furthermore, a risk of bias assessment was conducted to evaluate the methodological quality and reliability of the included studies. There were a few limitations. Although the exploratory search could not be restricted, there was a lack of overall evidence in certain parts of the world, which reduced the generalizability of the review findings. There was a high degree of heterogeneity with design, outcomes, and the format of interventions/formats (length), which reduced the feasibility of meta-analytic approaches. In addition, the small sample sizes and short follow up time of multi-domain studies limited confidence about the longer-term effects. The study setting can also be a factor that affects the results of the music listening interventions The difference in health status, independence and social activity among institutionalized and community dwelling older adults might influence baseline well-being and music responsiveness. Thus, one should view the findings with caution. Furthermore, we only included peer-reviewed studies published in English which may create a language bias by excluding any important studies in other languages. Additionally, another limitation of this review is that it focused only on passive music listening, excluding other forms of musical engagement such as singing or playing instruments. Finally, degree of variability in the tools to measure well-being led to challenges in establishing meaningful comparisons across studies.

Conclusion

This review highlights the positive impact of passive music listening on older adults’ psychological, emotional, social, and sociological well-being. These data provide enough evidence that music listening has a positive impact on mood, reduces feelings of anxiety and loneliness, and increases social connection. Music listening is a novel, low-cost method of promoting healthy ageing due to its accessibility, ease and cultural appropriateness, and non-invasiveness of the participant. Future research should examine more robust study designs with more standardized outcomes to help support causal relationships and inform the field across varying ageing populations.
