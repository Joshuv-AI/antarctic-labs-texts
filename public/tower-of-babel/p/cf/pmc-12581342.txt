Discovery of urinary biomarkers of kiwifruit intake in a randomized intervention study
Authors: Zilin Xiao, Wanning Shang, Peiyu Li, Nan Wang, Tongtong Li, Yunan Liu, Ying Chen, Ying Wang, Hao Ma, Xuan Wang, Han Han, Geng Zong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKiwifruit is widely recognized for its nutritional value and health benefits, yet reliable and objective methods for assessing kiwifruit intake in populations remain limited.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify urinary biomarkers of kiwifruit intake and develop an optimal biomarker panel for differentiating consumers within days.MethodsA randomized, controlled, crossover dietary intervention was conducted among 17 healthy volunteers. The intervention included four phases: run-in, single‐exposure, repeat‐exposure, and follow-up. Urine samples at multiple time-point and fruit samples were prepared and analyzed using untargeted metabolomics via dual-column ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS). Candidate biomarkers were identified through a systematic statistical strategy on kinetic profiles within 24 h, and annotated for potential fruit-derived origin through spectral matching. Machine learning algorithms were employed to establish an optimal biomarker panel for assessing kiwifruit intake under habitual diet conditions.ResultsTwenty-three urinary metabolites showed significantly elevated kinetic profiles, among which 15 were matched to compounds detected in the original fruit or in vitro digestion samples. These metabolites mainly included polyphenol-related metabolites and plant-derived amino acid derivatives. The excretion of many metabolites turned to be delayed compared to those typically observed for other fruits. For example, 2-isopropylmalic acid usually peaked in urine or blood within 6 h of consuming other fruits, but in our study urinary level at 24 h was much higher compared to 6 h. Most of the selected candidates are not specific to kiwifruit based on existing literature, such as hippuric acid. In this regard, an XGBoost algorithm-based model using 7 metabolites achieved substantial discriminative performance (accuracy = 0.88) in predicting kiwifruit intake within two days.ConclusionsThis study identified potential biomarkers of kiwifruit and developed a prediction model that may differentiate consumers. Further validation is necessary to confirm the reliability and generalizability of our findings.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2100048279. Introduction

Kiwifruit (Actinidia spp.), recognized for its nutritional profile, is a rich source of vitamins, minerals, and dietary fiber, alongside diverse bioactive compounds including antioxidants, phytonutrients, and enzymes [1]. These components contribute to multiple benefits through anti-oxidant effects [2, 3], improved gastrointestinal function [4, 5], and modulation of inflammatory pathways [6]. Previous studies utilizing mouse models and in vitro digestion systems have provided valuable insights into the absorption and excretion processes of kiwifruit bioactives [7, 8], demonstrating the bioavailability of specific phenolic compounds derived from kiwifruit extracts. However, robust evidence from human intervention studies remains limited. Human trials have largely focused on the health benefits of kiwifruit compounds [6, 9, 10], especially the role of vitamin C [11, 12], without systematically characterizing their in vivo metabolic profiles. Moreover, epidemiological studies on habitual kiwifruit intake and long‐term health outcomes are scarce, largely because low per‐capita consumption of kiwifruit makes it difficult to capture using traditional dietary assessment methods [13–15]. Therefore, it is a critical need for objective and precise methods to quantify kiwifruit intake.

Recent advances in biomarkers of food intake (BFIs) studies offers a new strategy for dietary assessment by detecting specific metabolites linked to food consumption [16], based on the kinetic profiles of these compounds in urine or blood after acute exposure. In existing studies, most urinary metabolites typically reached their peak concentrations within 8 h and returned to baseline levels by 24 h [17, 18]. Such rapid turnover results in a narrow detection and sampling window, which challenges the usefulness of these BFIs for estimating habitual intake in large populations where only single time-point sampling is feasible [19]. Furthermore, many identified BFIs are of wide food origins thus lack specificity to individual food. One possible solution is developing a composite BFI panel to improve discrimination of fruit intake to some extent [17, 20].

In this study, we aimed to identify and characterize potential biomarkers of kiwifruit intake from urine samples in a crossover randomized controlled trial, and develop an optimal biomarker panel that may differentiate kiwifruit consumers over 1–2 days.

Method

Subject recruitment and ethical approval

This analysis is part of a food biomarker discovery study that aimed to identify and/or validate biomarkers of nine commonly consumed or local fruits among eastern China population. A comprehensive description of the study design and procedures is provided in the Supplementary Method. Overall, the study followed a controlled, open-label, crossover study design (Supplementary Fig. 1). Participants were recruited through posters, social media, and referrals. The inclusion criteria are healthy adults aged 18–25 with a BMI of 18.5–30.0 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria included any diagnosed health condition (chronic or infectious diseases), pregnancy or lactation, smoking or drinking, recent history of surgery or participation in other research, regular use of medications/nutritional supplements, or any allergies/intolerances to the test foods. A general questionnaire (covering demographics, medication use, and supplement use) and a food frequency questionnaire for food intake with 12 months were completed after recruitment. At the baseline and upon completion of the study, participants have height, weight and blood pressure measured.

The study was approved by the ethics committee of Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health, Chinese Academy of Sciences (No. ER-SINH-252103) and the ethic committee of Shanghai Fourth People's Hospital (No. 2021075–001), and registered on the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (ChiCTR2100048279). All participants provided their written informed consent.

Food preparation and fruit in vitro digestion samples

The green kiwifruit used in this study was sourced from Shaanxi, China, and provided by the Shanghai Fourth People's Hospital. In accordance with “The Chinese Dietary Guidelines”, which recommends a daily fruit intake of 200–350 g (fresh weight), we provided 300 g of fresh kiwifruit (corresponding to 30–45 g dry weight based on fruit water content and edible portion) on Day 3 (see Dietary intervention and sample collection below). On Day 4, the total fruit intake was doubled (600 g) and equally distributed across three meals (200 g/meal) to avoid excessive consumption at a single time point. Control meals were provided in parallel, consisting solely of plain steamed white rice and pork. Pork belly or pork chop was marinated for 1.5 h in a mixture of salt, ginger-infused water, and black pepper, then oven-roasted at 180 °C for 20 min. Both rice and pork were served ad libitum.

Food samples were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for later analysis. In vitro digestion was performed following the protocol by Brodkorb et al. [21]. Fruits were homogenized after peeling and de-seeding, and digestion was carried out in three phases: oral, gastric, and intestinal. After digestion, the samples were centrifuged, and the supernatant was stored at − 80 °C for further analysis.

Dietary intervention and sample collection

All participants completed a control round first and then the fruit-intervention rounds. Each round lasted one week and included four phases: run-in (Days 1–2), single‐exposure (Day 3), repeat‐exposure (Day 4), and follow-up (Days 5–7) (Fig. 1). On Day 1, participants avoided test fruits, alcohol, and soft drinks. On Day 2, they refrained from all plant-based foods and consumed a control meal as dinner. During the control round, only the control meals were served on Days 3 and 4, whereas in the kiwifruit round the same meals were accompanied by 300 g kiwifruit in the morning of Day 3 and 200 g kiwifruit for each meal on Day 4. Urine samples were collected at fasting (T0) and at 0–6 h post-consumption (T2, T4, T6) on Day 3, and again the next morning (T24). A cumulative urine sample over 12 h was obtained from after dinner on Day 4 until the morning of Day 5. On Days 6–7, participants resumed their usual diet (excluding test fruits), and fasting urine samples were collected in the morning. Fasting blood samples were collected pre-breakfast on Day 3 and Day 5. Two fecal samples were obtained before (Days 1–3) and after (Days 5–7) food consumption. All samples were stored at − 80 °C.

Study design of the randomized, controlled, crossover dietary intervention. Each intervention arm consisted of a 7-day phase: 2-day run-in, 1-day single-exposure intervention with controlled meals, 1-day repeat-exposure intervention with controlled meals and 3-day follow-up. Participants consumed either control meals or fruit with control meals (Dose A fruit followed by three Dose B fruit) with a 7-day wash-out between arms. Metabolome profiling

The original food sample was taken and ground in liquid nitrogen. A total of 500 mg of the powdered sample was mixed with MeOH:ACN:H2O (2:2:1), vortexed, sonicated in an ice-water bath, and centrifuged at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, dried under vacuum, and reconstituted with 100 μL of ACN:H2O (1:1). The sample was then vortexed and centrifuged again, and transferred to the autosampler for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis. For in vitro digestion sample, a 100 μL aliquot was mixed with 400 μL of MeOH:ACN (1:1), vortexed and then processed in parallel with original food samples. For urine samples, a 100 μL aliquot was mixed with 400 μL of MeOH:ACN (1:1) containing 10 isotopic internal standards (Shanghai Applied Protein Technology). The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 4 °C, and then supernatant was dried under nitrogen, reconstituted with MeOH:ACN (1:1), vortexed, transferred to the autosampler. Equal volumes of all samples were mixed and processed using the same preparation method as quality control (QC).

To minimize the impact of signal fluctuations, a randomized sequence was employed for sample analysis. QC samples were interspersed within the sample sequence to monitor system stability and assess the reliability of the experimental data. Metabolomic analysis was performed as described by Luo et al. [22]. Samples were separated using the Vanquish ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (UHPLC) and then subjected to MS with a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo). Metabolites were separated using HILIC (ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide Column, 130 Å, 1.7 µm, 2.1 mm X 100 mm, 1/pk) and T3 (ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 Column, 100 Å, 1.8 µm, 2.1 mm X 100 mm, 1/pk) columns. MS detection was performed in both positive and negative ion modes with electrospray ionization. The detection mode was set to full scan/data-dependent acquisition, allowing the top 10 MS1 ions to obtain MS/MS spectra, with collision energies applied at stepped normalized levels of 20, 40, and 60 eV. The UHPLC-MS operating conditions for metabolite analysis are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Data preprocessing

The raw data were converted to.mzXML format using ProteoWizard. Peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction were performed using the XCMS package (version 4.4.0) in R. Metabolite identification was performed using an in-house database (Shanghai Applied Protein Technology) established with authentic standards. Structural annotations of metabolites in biological samples were achieved by matching retention times (ΔRT < 0.2 min), molecular masses (mass error < 10 ppm), MS/MS fragmentation spectra (Score > 0.9), and collision energies [22, 23]. The identification confidence levels according to the metabolomics standards initiative were considered as Level 2 or higher.

To ensure data quality, preliminary filtering was conducted according to the following criteria: (1) peaks with retention times less than 0.5 min or within the final 2 min of the chromatographic analysis were removed; (2) peaks with more than 50% missing values across all sample groups were removed. Batch correction was subsequently conducted using the NormAE model. The corrected data were further filtered to remove peaks with low average response intensity or with a relative standard deviation exceeding 40%.

Data analysis

Metabolite intensities were initially normalized and standardized using constant sum method [24]. Data from four modes were analyzed separately. To identify features elevated after test food intake, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated comparing feature intensities at each post-intake time point on Day 3 (T2, T4, T6 and T24) with baseline (T0) in the intervention arm. Features showing d > 0.30 at any time point were retained for subsequent analyses. The area under the curve (AUC) of the kinetic profiles was calculated and standardized for quantitative assessment of metabolic dynamics over the 0–24 h period (AUC0–24 h). Partial least square discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) models [25] were constructed using the “ropls” package to compare the AUC0–24 h of features between the intervention and control groups, with metabolites selected based on a variable importance in projection (VIP) score greater than 1.5. Model performance was assessed by sevenfold cross-validation based on R-squared values. Subsequently, a two-tailed paired t-test was applied to exclude features with between-group differences prior to food intake. One-tailed paired t-tests were adopted to identify features with significantly higher levels in the intervention group at T24 compared to the control group at T24 (between group) and the intervention group at T0 (within group). The focus on metabolites significantly elevated after 24 h of intake was intended to capture metabolites with longer postprandial detection windows so that they could be possibly used in large observational studies that typically collected single morning or random urine samples. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to identify metabolites with early postprandial peaks (0–6 h, Supplementary Analysis). P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate (FDR) correction, with significance set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.2.1).

Selected metabolites were further annotated by matching to metabolites detected in either original kiwifruit samples or kiwifruit in vitro digestion samples according to mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and MS2 spectra. Consistent m/z values and fragment patterns were used to infer potential correspondence, providing supportive evidence of fruit origin.

Testing the performance of candidate biomarkers

To evaluate the discriminative performance of selected biomarkers for kiwifruit intake, we applied the XGBoost algorithm-based model to urine samples from the repeat‐exposure phase and follow-up phase. It is a gradient-boosting framework known for its efficiency in handling high-dimensional data and for providing robust predictions by iteratively building decision trees [26]. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using GridSearchCV to identify the optimal model parameters, and a fivefold cross-validation was applied to ensure model reliability and minimize overfitting. Model performance was assessed by accuracy to evaluate its classification capability using metabolites detected in either the original kiwifruit or its in vitro digested form. Within this restricted pool, discriminative performance was assessed at both the single-metabolite level and by exhaustively evaluating all possible metabolite subsets to identify an optimal predictive panel. The machine learning models were implemented in Python using the XGBoost package (version 1.4.2).

Result

Participants

Of the 20 volunteers initially enrolled in the study, 17 volunteers who completed both the control and kiwifruit intervention phases were included in the analysis. Detailed characteristics of the study population are presented in SupplementaryTable 2.

Detection and identification of metabolites

A total of 30,065 features were obtained from urine samples including 5,629 features in Hilic(+) mode, 5,031 features in Hilic(-) mode, 8710 features in T3(+) mode, and 10,695 features in T3(-) mode. Approximately 20% of features were annotated with identification information of level 2 (Supplementary Table 3). The identified metabolites categorized based on their chemical taxonomy are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Analysis of original kiwifruit samples yielded 11,315 features. Rich profiles of organic acids and their derivatives were detected across all four analytical modes, with the top five identified metabolites of highest intensities including quinic acid, citric acid, malic acid, and ascorbic acid. In parallel, 31,336 features were detected in vitro digestion samples. Cholic acid and its derivatives were widely identified across multiple modes, which may also come from the bile salts added during the intestinal digestion [21]. Quinate showed the highest intensity in Hilic(-) mode, consistent with the deprotonation of quinic acid under neutral-to-alkaline intestinal conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To focus on dietary signals and minimize the influence of endogenous metabolic regulation, we prioritized features that showed post-intervention increase compared to controls. Effect-size profiling with Cohen’s d showed that over 30% of the detected features exhibited increasing trends after kiwifruit intake. PLS-DA analysis further narrowed these to 495 features across four analytical modes with VIP scores greater than 1.5, showing their discriminatory ability in cumulative postprandial metabolite excretion within 24 h. Subsequently, paired t-tests were conducted on these features, resulting in 27 selected features in Hilic(+), 45 in Hilic(-), 31 in T3(+), 58 in T3(-) that demonstrated significant differences both between and within groups at 24 h post-intervention (T24).

In total, 24 metabolites with identification confidence levels higher than Level 2 were considered as candidate biomarkers, including six in Hilic(+), six in Hilic(-), five in T3(+) and seven in T3(-). Hippuric acid was detected in both Hilic(+) and T3(-). To further annotate the presence of these discriminatory metabolites in test fruit, the metabolites were matched against metabolomic profiles of both original kiwifruit samples and kiwifruit in vitro digestion samples. Sixteen metabolites (15 unique metabolites) were matched: six were aligned with the original kiwifruit sample, two with the in vitro digestion sample, and eight metabolites with both (Table 1, Supplementary Table 4).

Prediction models at the repeat‐exposure phase and follow-up phase

During the repeat‐exposure phase, the individual metabolite in cumulative urine showed robust predictive performance in differentiating kiwifruit consumers as simulating frequent consuming, with accuracies exceeding 0.85 (Table 2). However, these individual metabolites performed poorly to predict kiwifruit intake in free-diet follow-up phase, with accuracies less than 0.80 (Table 2). To identify an effective panel of candidate biomarkers for assessing kiwifruit intake, we initially constructed 32,767 XGBoost models to evaluate all possible combinations of 16 metabolites (15 unique metabolites) detected in either kiwifruit or in vitro digestion samples. The highest accuracy was achieved with a panel of seven metabolites. The optimal panel comprised hippuric acid, o-methoxyhippuric acid, p-toluric acid, 2-isopropylmalic acid, 3-methyladipic acid, p-coumaric acid, and N-isobutyloxamic acid, with a classification accuracy of 0.88. Moreover, an analysis using the full 24-metabolite set (23 unique metabolites) with identical model parameters showed no improvement in classification performance (Δ Accuracy = −0.04).

Discussion

This intervention study examined urinary excretion of metabolites after a standardized kiwifruit dose and identified 23 candidate biomarkers as potential BFIs of kiwifruits with elevated postprandial levels at 24 h. Fifteen of them were matched to compounds detected in original kiwifruit or its in vitro digested samples. Although these metabolites may not be specific to kiwifruit, a XGBoost algorithm-based model using 7 metabolites achieved substantial discriminative performance (accuracy = 0.88) in predicting kiwifruit intake within two days.

Our strategy prioritized fruit-originated metabolites that remained significantly elevated in next morning urine, which enables the capture of metabolites with detection windows exceeding a day and the prediction of food intake from at least 1–2 days prior to sampling. This strategy offers a practical solution, balancing feasibility with reliability in dietary biomarker research compared to the collection of 24-h urine samples. As a result, potential biomarkers of acute food exposure within a few hours were not kept in our main analysis. The sensitivity analysis targeting the 0–6 h postprandial window identified 17 early-responsive metabolites (see Supplementary Analysis), 13 of which were not selected by the 24 h-based screening strategy. Owing to their transient nature, these metabolites were not incorporated into subsequent intake prediction analyses. Notably,. our finding aligns with previous pharmacokinetic evidence showing that urinary excretion of vitamin C peaks within 3 h after intake and returns to near-baseline levels by 8 h [27] (Supplementary Table 5, Supplementary Fig. 4O). Current research recommends such nutrient/component be used as a biomarker for frequent consumers or food compliance [19].

Interestingly, many identified metabolites exhibited delayed excretion in urine. For example, 2-IPMA typically peaked in urine or blood within 6 h of consuming other fruits [17, 28, 29], but in our study urinary level at 24 h was much higher compared to 6 h. As one possible reason, kiwifruit contains relatively higher fiber (3%) [30–32] that is predominantly digested in the colon and encapsulates other nutrients hindering their immediate absorption[31, 33]. Consistent with this, diets containing cellulose or kiwifruit fibers have been shown to reduce ileal organic matter digestibility compared to fiber-free diets in an in vivo digestion study among swines [34]. Another possible explanation is that some kiwifruit-originated compounds may also be secondary metabolites of other food components by gut microbiota fermentation and hepatic metabolism in humans. According to our analysis, hippuric acid exists in kiwifruit samples with low intensity on MS, therefore urinary hippuric acid is more likely to be metabolites of chlorogenic acid as the most abundant polyphenol in kiwifruit [35, 36]. It may be produced through microbial fermentation of quinic acid moiety in the gut or hepatic metabolism of benzoic acids – both are downstream products of chlorogenic acid degradation [37]. A study on in vitro digestion of phenolic compounds in kiwifruit juice demonstrated that chlorogenic acid undergoes low degradation during the gastric digestion phase, and decreased by approximately 30% during intestinal digestion [8]. Collectively, our findings further supported potential impacts of food matrix on absorption and metabolism that warrant systematic investigation for more foods in relation to their potential health impacts.

Similar to existing intervention studies on BFI discovery, most of the selected candidates are not specific to kiwifruit based on existing literature. For example, hippuric acid is widely recognized to reflect dietary flavonoid intake from fruits and vegetables [38], while the consumption of other polyphenol-rich foods such as coffee, tea, and cocoa also correlates with elevated urinary hippuric acid [39]. P-coumaric acid is found in green kiwifruit juice and involved in polyphenol metabolic pathways, which is synthetized in plants from phenylalanine via cinnamic acid or directly from tyrosine by tyrosine ammonia-lyase, and can be further synthetized into caffeic, ferulic and sinapic acids [40]. Several metabolites are linked to amino acid primary metabolism in plants. 3-methyladipic acid, an organic acid previously detected in pears [41], is likely derived from the metabolism of leucine or isoleucine in kiwifruit. 2-isopropylmalic acid, a key intermediate in the biosynthesis of essential branched-chain amino acids like leucine, is presumed to originate from secondary metabolic pathways involving malic acid in plants and has been widely reported in studies on bananas, peas, and wine [17, 28, 42]. Given the limited number of test foods in a highly controlled intervention study, the specificity of these identified biomarkers warrant confirmation in studies with more complicated diet background.

In this regard, we developed a multi-biomarker prediction model using urine samples during follow-up phase with free diet background, to test whether these unspecific markers could collectively differentiate kiwifruit consumers after 1–2 days. As a result, a panel including 7 polyphenol metabolites and secondary metabolites of amino acid reached the highest accuracy of 0.88 on Day 6 (Table 2). Panel-based strategies have been increasingly utilized in BFI research to increase the accuracy of food intake prediction while addressing the low specificity of identified biomarkers [19]. For example, a study on banana intake biomarkers used a PLS-DA model to evaluate all possible combinations of identified metabolites, demonstrating that the combination of two certain metabolites yielded the highest predictive accuracy [17]. Our study adopted the XGBoost algorithm, which effectively captures non-linear relationships and feature interactions, thereby enhancing model performance. The most optimal panel comprised seven metabolites, and additional features may introduce noise or lead to overfitting. Future studies are needed to further validate whether such study design combined with machine-learning model may enhance the performance of selected biomarkers in other settings.

Key advantages of our research include the following: First, we employed a multi‐phase, blank‐controlled design – comprising a single‐exposure phase, a repeat‐exposure phase and a follow‐up phase – to enable a systematic evaluation of urinary metabolites as recent food intake biomarkers. Second, by using an untargeted metabolomics workflow with dual-column separation, we achieved broad signal coverage and substantial identification of over 4,000 known metabolites, ensuring intensive statistically analysis for biomarker screening. Third, we implemented a rigorous biomarker statistical pipeline that integrates both cumulative postprandial metabolite excretion and single time-point measurements to identify the most discriminative compounds. The emphasize of 24-h detection window may ensure the potential applicability of these food biomarkers in cohort studies, while the incorporation of machine-learning algorithms further enhanced the predictive performance of our biomarker panels.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, external validation in independent populations was not performed. We partially mitigated this by leveraging follow-up samples within our own study as an internal validation. Second, the in vitro digestion process employed in this study did not include microbial fermentation in the colon and hepatic metabolism, both of which are critical for simulating the complete metabolic profile of food-derived compounds. Therefore, all 23 discriminatory metabolites that passed statistical filtering are listed, with annotations indicating whether they were observed in the original kiwifruit or in the in vitro digestion samples for further investigation. Future studies should consider incorporating these physiological processes for a more comprehensive assessment. Third, the study investigated exclusively green kiwifruit (Actinidia deliciosa) from single source. Further research is required to explore variations in biomarkers across different kiwifruit cultivars to enhance the generalizability of the findings [43].

Conclusions

In conclusion, we identified 23 urinary metabolites with clear time response and substantial half-lives as potential biomarkers of kiwifruit intake, among which 15 were suggested to be of fruit-derived origin. Although these metabolites were not food-specific, multi-biomarker prediction models showed high accuracy in differentiating recent kiwifruit consumers. Clearly, further validation is necessary to confirm the reliability and generalizability of our findings.
