Dental students’ self-confidence toward endodontics according to educational stages: a longitudinal study
Authors: Oğuz Tavşan, Seden Kara Ongun, İlkim Aktaş, Berk Çelikkol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe self-confidence levels of undergraduate dental students regarding the steps of endodontic treatment and the types of teeth treated were evaluated to longitudinally assess changes across different stages of their education within the same student cohort.MethodsThis longitudinal cohort study included 98 undergraduate students from the Faculty of Dentistry at Uşak University, who were prospectively followed across three consecutive educational stages: preclinical training, an endodontic observation internship, and an endodontic practice internship, from 2023 to 2025. At each stage, students rated their self-confidence regarding specific endodontic procedures and tooth types using a Likert scale. Data were analyzed using the Friedman test, with a significance level of p < 0.05.ResultsOverall, students’ self-confidence demonstrated a procedure-dependent pattern across educational stages. Significant increases were observed in preparatory procedures, including anamnesis, diagnosis, and dental anesthesia, as well as in several clinical procedures, such as working length determination, canal shaping, irrigation, root canal filling, and permanent restoration. In contrast, decreased confidence was noted in rubber dam application, flare-up management, and bleaching procedures. Regarding tooth type, students showed increased confidence in maxillary anterior, mandibular anterior, and premolar teeth, whereas confidence decreased in maxillary and mandibular molars.ConclusionDental students’ self-confidence in performing endodontic procedures changes gradually from the preclinical stage to clinical practice. While educational progression enhances confidence in many procedures and tooth types, confidence tends to decline in more complex clinical situations, particularly in molar endodontics and complication management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09186-8.

Introduction

The primary objective of dental education programs is to prepare students for the profession through knowledge- and education-based training. The European Society of Endodontology (ESE) has emphasized that “Undergraduate dental education should aim to train graduates who possess the skills necessary to provide high-quality patient care, are aware of their level of competence and limitations, and are able to appropriately refer patients whose clinical needs exceed their knowledge and experience” [1]. In this context, self-confidence is increasingly recognized as a measurable educational outcome reflecting students’ perceived readiness to translate theoretical knowledge into clinical practice and, therefore, should be regarded not merely as an individual perception but also as an indicator of the effectiveness and success of the educational curriculum [2]. In this regard, students who perform endodontic treatments are expected to possess scientific and technical competency across all stages of dental procedures [3]. This requirement has become increasingly critical due to rising life expectancy and the growing demand of individuals to preserve their natural dentition. Current trends indicate that this demand will continue to increase [4]. As a result, it is essential for dental students to acquire adequate theoretical knowledge and clinical experience in endodontic procedures before graduation [5].

Preclinical training provided prior to clinical practice constitutes a fundamental and critical stage that supports the development of students’ competence and self-confidence [6]. During this phase, students apply the theoretical knowledge they have acquired in lectures to predesigned scenarios using manikins and anatomical models, thereby gaining essential preclinical skills [6, 7]. Preclinical education contributes not only to the development of motor skills but also to the reinforcement of conceptual understanding, the enhancement of competence levels, and the overall effectiveness of the learning process. This laboratory-based training enables students to gain experience with rare cases, helps compensate for the limited number of real patients, and allows early detection and correction of procedural errors, thereby improving patient safety. The use of various educational techniques and simulations during the preclinical stage facilitates a more confident, better-equipped, and well-prepared transition into real clinical settings [7, 8].

The primary objective of clinical education in dentistry is to develop students’ psychomotor skills and enhance their clinical competencies. Clinical training is carried out under the supervision of faculty members in university clinics, where students perform procedures in various fields, such as endodontics, prosthodontics, periodontics, and restorative dentistry [6, 8]. The transition from preclinical to clinical training presents various challenges for students and often leads to a certain degree of stress and anxiety, particularly in endodontic procedures, which are considered technically complex [3, 9, 10]. Individual, anatomical, biological, and psychological variations in patients, as well as differing approaches among instructors during the clinical phase, may further complicate students’ adaptation process. Identifying these transitional challenges is crucial for improving the quality of dental education programs and guiding the development of more effective curricula [11].

The literature contains various studies that assess dental students’ self-confidence and stress levels related to endodontic procedures at different academic levels [3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11–13]. Dental students have been reported to be particularly susceptible to stress during the transition from preclinical education to clinical practice [14]. Students’ perceptions of procedural complications such as endodontic file separation have also been investigated in the literature [13, 15]. In addition, recent studies have evaluated students’ learning experiences and perceived confidence when different obturation techniques are introduced during preclinical endodontic training [16]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that, during this transition period, the level of stress experienced in endodontics is higher than that observed in other disciplines [17, 18]. Additionally, studies have reported significant differences in confidence levels between academic year groups at each stage of root canal treatment [19, 20].

Most existing studies are cross-sectional and therefore fail to capture longitudinal changes in self-confidence within the same cohort of students. To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first to prospectively and longitudinally evaluate self-confidence levels related to endodontic procedures and treated tooth types by following the same student cohort across consecutive educational stages, from preclinical training through endodontic observation and clinical practice. Thus, the aim of the present study was to analyze and compare the self-confidence levels of Uşak University Faculty of Dentistry students regarding endodontic treatment following preclinical training, clinical observation, and endodontic practice internships. Furthermore, by identifying the areas in which students feel inadequate or experience stress during the endodontic treatment process, our objective was to contribute to improvements in related educational programs. The hypothesis of the study was that students’ self-confidence in endodontic procedures will increase as their training progresses.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved in two phases by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Uşak University: the first phase (transition from preclinical training to clinical observation) on January 04, 2024 (decision no. 291-291-23), and the second phase (transition from clinical observation to clinical practice training) on April 15, 2025 (decision no. 634-634-03).

Sampling method and size

In the power analysis conducted, the values from a previously published study (16) were used as a reference. With a 95% confidence level (1 - α), 95% test power (1 - β), and an effect size of w = 0.389, the required sample size was determined through a chi-square test to be a total of 87 students.

Educational curriculum

Undergraduate dental education is delivered through a five-year standardized curriculum regulated by higher education authorities. Endodontic education is an integral component of this curriculum and is formally included in the second, third, and fourth academic years.

Endodontic training begins in the second year of the undergraduate program. During the second semester of the second year, students commence preclinical endodontic training, which continues through the first semester of the third year, resulting in a total preclinical training period of approximately one academic year. This phase combines theoretical instruction with extensive hands-on laboratory practice.

Throughout the preclinical period, students receive comprehensive theoretical education on endodontic principles while simultaneously performing root canal treatments on extracted human teeth representing all tooth groups, using the step-back technique for root canal shaping and the cold lateral compaction technique for obturation. These teeth are mounted in phantom jaws and phantom head simulators in order to replicate the clinical working environment and simulate real patient conditions as closely as possible. This approach aims to facilitate the acquisition of both psychomotor skills and clinical procedural sequencing under controlled conditions.

Students who successfully complete the preclinical endodontic training phase proceed to the endodontic clinic observation stage during the second semester of their third academic year, which lasts six months. During this period, undergraduate students attend the endodontic clinic as observers, where they closely follow postgraduate endodontic residents managing real patients and performing endodontic treatments. This observational phase is designed to familiarize students with real clinical workflows, patient management, and decision-making processes prior to independent clinical practice.

In the fourth academic year, students transition to active clinical practice, which lasts one academic year. Building upon the skills and knowledge acquired during preclinical training and clinical observation, students perform endodontic treatments on real patients under faculty supervision, applying the step-back technique for root canal shaping and the cold lateral compaction technique for obturation. Endodontic procedures are carried out on teeth for which endodontic treatment is clinically indicated, encompassing different tooth groups and varying levels of case complexity.

Students who successfully complete the endodontic clinical requirements in the fourth year continue their education in the fifth year within an integrated multidisciplinary clinical setting, where all dental specialties are practiced concurrently, for one academic year. This final stage emphasizes comprehensive patient care and interdisciplinary collaboration, consolidating the students’ clinical competence prior to graduation.

Study design

This study was designed as a longitudinal observational cohort study conducted with undergraduate dental students enrolled in the Faculty of Dentistry at Uşak University. The study population consisted of a single cohort of students who progressed sequentially through predefined stages of the undergraduate endodontic curriculum. Data were collected at multiple time points corresponding to key educational stages rather than from independent or unmatched student groups.

The same cohort of students completed preclinical endodontic training during the first semester of the 2023–2024 academic year, participated in the endodontic observation internship during the second semester of the same academic year, and subsequently undertook the endodontic clinical practice internship during the 2024–2025 academic year.

The first data collection (Time Point 1) was performed after the completion of preclinical endodontic training, corresponding to the end of the first semester of the third academic year in our curriculum, when students had already received theoretical instruction on several clinical aspects of endodontic treatment. Data were collected at each educational stage using anonymous, self-administered paper-based forms related to endodontic procedures and treated tooth types on a voluntary basis. To minimize potential response bias, students completed the forms individually in an environment where no faculty members were present and without interaction with other students. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki.

A total of 107 students were initially eligible for inclusion. Because the study aimed to follow the entire class cohort across consecutive stages of endodontic education, all eligible students were invited to participate, representing a census sampling approach. However, 2 students failed the endodontics course, and 7 students failed other compulsory courses and, in accordance with curriculum progression requirements, were not permitted to advance to the subsequent academic year. As these students were unable to complete all predefined educational stages, they were excluded from the longitudinal analysis. Consequently, the final study sample consisted of 98 students who completed all stages of the curriculum and all assessment time points (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Study design and longitudinal flow of the student cohort across the three stages of undergraduate endodontic education

Prior to the study, students were informed that participation was voluntary and that their responses would not affect their academic standing or individual performance. Participants were asked to rate their self-confidence levels regarding the steps of endodontic treatment procedures and the treatment of different tooth groups. The questions used in this study (Supplementary Material 1) were directly adopted from the study by Tanalp et al. [9]., which evaluated dental students’ perceptions and self-confidence levels regarding endodontic treatment. Participants evaluated their self-confidence levels using a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5. The scale was structured as follows: 1 = “Not confident,” 2 = “Slightly confident,” 3 = “Neutral,” 4 = “Confident,” and 5 = “Highly confident.” The primary outcome variable of the study was students’ self-confidence level, evaluated across different endodontic procedural steps, and tooth groups at three predefined educational time points.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using the R programming language. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. Normality testing indicated that none of the dependent variables measured repeatedly over time followed a normal distribution. Therefore, the Friedman test, a non-parametric alternative for repeated-measures comparisons, was applied for all longitudinal analyses. Effect sizes for the Friedman test results were calculated using Kendall’s W together with 95% confidence intervals. When statistically significant differences were detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction to control for multiple testing. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum–maximum). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reliability and factor structure analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v23) and IBM SPSS AMOS (v24). The reliability of the evaluated dimensions was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the internal consistency of the subdimensions at each measurement time point (T1, T2, and T3), and the corresponding coefficients were reported in tabular form. To examine the construct validity of the evaluated dimensions, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using the data obtained at the first measurement time point (T1) in the AMOS program, and the theoretically defined two-factor model was tested. The assumption of multivariate normality was evaluated using Mardia’s critical ratio. Since the critical ratio was below 20 (10.029), the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation method was used for parameter estimation. Model fit was evaluated using the chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (CMIN/DF) together with the fit indices GFI, IFI, TLI, CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR. Standardized (β₁) and unstandardized (β₂) factor loadings, standard errors, test statistics, p values, and explained variances (R²) were reported for the evaluated items. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

A total of 98 undergraduate students from the Faculty of Dentistry at Uşak University who successfully completed endodontic training from the second semester of the second academic year through the end of the fourth academic year were included in this study. Statistical analyses revealed significant differences in students’ self-confidence levels depending on their stage of education. According to the results of the Friedman test, significant differences were observed in the following procedures: patient history taking (p < 0.001), making a diagnosis (p < 0.001), administering anesthesia (p < 0.001), applying a rubber dam (p < 0.001), determining working length (p < 0.001), shaping root canals (p = 0.006), irrigating canals (p = 0.010), root canal filling (p = 0.047), flare-up management (p = 0.005), completing permanent restoration (p = 0.001), and performing non-vital bleaching (p < 0.001) (Table 1). In particular, the most pronounced improvement in self-confidence was observed in patient history taking, which increased from 3.78 ± 0.80 in the preclinical stage to 4.35 ± 0.65 in the clinical internship; in diagnosis, which rose from 3.33 ± 0.82 to 4.00 ± 0.68; in anesthesia administration, which improved from 3.44 ± 0.93 to 4.08 ± 0.79; in root canal shaping, which increased from 3.60 ± 0.89 to 4.04 ± 0.74; in root canal irrigation, which rose from 3.94 ± 0.85 to 4.30 ± 0.61; and in permanent restoration, which improved from 3.77 ± 0.90 to 4.34 ± 0.59. These consistent increases across educational stages indicate a steady gain in clinical competence and procedural confidence. Conversely, procedures such as rubber dam application, flare-up management, and non-vital bleaching showed a decline in self-confidence levels among students during the clinical internship compared with the preclinical or observation stages. Self-confidence in rubber dam placement decreased from 3.41 ± 1.10 in the preclinical stage to 2.46 ± 1.21 in the clinical internship. Similarly, confidence in performing non-vital bleaching dropped from 3.46 ± 0.98 to 2.70 ± 1.19. With respect to flare-up management, self-confidence increased from 3.35 ± 0.94 in the preclinical stage to 3.66 ± 0.87 during the observation stage but subsequently decreased to 3.20 ± 0.88 in the clinical internship. Procedures related to radiographic evaluation, post-treatment follow-up, and patient communication did not show significant differences between educational stages (p > 0.05).

When changes in self-confidence levels were evaluated based on the type of tooth treated, significant differences were also found between the educational stages. According to the Friedman test, significant differences were observed in the following tooth groups: maxillary anterior teeth (p = 0.008), maxillary molars (p = 0.002), mandibular anterior teeth (p = 0.028), mandibular premolars (p = 0.003), and mandibular molars (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Self-confidence for maxillary anterior teeth increased from 4.00 ± 0.90 in the preclinical stage to 4.23 ± 0.78 in the clinical internship. For mandibular anterior teeth, the mean score increased from 4.00 ± 0.88 to 4.32 ± 0.74, and for mandibular premolars from 3.68 ± 0.98 to 4.10 ± 0.77. In contrast, self-confidence for maxillary molars decreased from 3.08 ± 1.03 in the preclinical stage to 2.65 ± 0.96 in the clinical internship, and for mandibular molars from 3.29 ± 1.08 to 2.81 ± 1.08. No significant difference was found for maxillary premolars (p < 0.05).

Supplementary Table 1 presents the internal consistency coefficients (Cronbach’s alpha) of the two dimensions evaluating students’ self-confidence levels regarding different endodontic procedures at three measurement time points: preclinical training (T1), observation clinic (T2), and clinical internship (T3). For the dimension “self-confidence regarding various endodontic procedures,” the Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.879 at T1, 0.904 at T2, and 0.857 at T3, with the highest internal consistency observed at T2. For the dimension “self-confidence regarding the endodontic treatment of different tooth types,” the alpha coefficients were 0.845 at T1, 0.828 at T2, and 0.806 at T3. Overall, Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.80 across all measurement time points in both dimensions indicate a high level of internal consistency throughout the measurements.

Supplementary Table 2 presents the results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) conducted to examine the two-factor structure of the scale. In the multivariate normality assessment, the critical ratio was found to be 10.029. Since this value was below 20, the Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation method was used for parameter estimation. The model fit indices were calculated as CMIN/DF (347.25/183) = 1.898, GFI = 0.763, IFI = 0.840, TLI = 0.811, CFI = 0.836, RMSEA = 0.096, and SRMR = 0.082 (Supplementary Figs. 1–2). For the first factor (students’ self-confidence regarding various endodontic procedures), the standardized factor loadings of the items ranged between 0.392 and 0.663, and all loadings were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.001). The explained variance (R²) values for the items within this factor ranged from 0.208 to 0.825. For the second factor (self-confidence regarding the endodontic treatment of different tooth types), the standardized factor loadings ranged between 0.456 and 0.908, and all item loadings were statistically significant (p < 0.001). In this factor, the R² values ranged from 0.154 to 0.429. In both factors, the unstandardized coefficient of the reference item was fixed to 1.000 for scaling purposes. The unstandardized coefficients (β₂), standard errors, and test statistics for the remaining items are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Additionally, the construct validity of the scale was examined using the data from the first measurement time point.

Discussion

This study longitudinally evaluated perceptions of self-confidence and competence among the same cohort of undergraduate dental students across consecutive educational stages, following preclinical training, an endodontic observation internship, and an endodontic clinical practice internship, with respect to the steps of root canal treatment and their application to different tooth groups. In addition, its objective was to contribute to the enhancement of educational quality in the field of endodontics, based on the findings obtained. The hypothesis that students’ self-confidence in performing endodontic procedures would increase as their education progressed was partially confirmed. While improvements in self-confidence were observed in certain procedural steps and tooth groups, a decline in confidence was also noted in other areas.

The primary objective of undergraduate endodontic education is to enable students to make more accurate and effective clinical decisions during the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up phases [21]. Previous studies have emphasized that students’ perspectives on educational practices and their perceived self-efficacy constitute important indicators for evaluating and improving dental curricula [22, 23]. Consistent with these findings, the present study demonstrated a progressive increase in students’ self-confidence as they advanced through successive stages of education, particularly during the diagnostic and preparatory phases of endodontic treatment. This observation supports the effectiveness of a structured, stepwise educational model.

Endodontic treatment is among the most demanding dental procedures due to complex root canal anatomies and technical challenges, with its success relying on the accurate execution of multiple interdependent steps [12]. Although recent advancements and techniques in root canal treatment have been introduced to simplify procedures, they can still be perceived as overwhelming by both dental students and practitioners [16]. Consequently, diminished self-confidence affects dentists’ ability to perform endodontic treatments. In addition, many students report feeling inadequately prepared when performing endodontic procedures, which are often perceived as complex and technically demanding components of dental training [3, 12, 24]. Another study reported a strong association between students’ lack of experience in endodontic treatment and their levels of anxiety and self-confidence [25].

A significant increase in students’ self-confidence was observed during the educational process in the following stages: obtaining a patient’s history, establishing a diagnosis based on dental anamnesis, and administering local anesthesia prior to treatment. These findings are consistent with the study by Tanalp et al., which reported high levels of self-confidence at similar stages [9]. The observed increase in self-confidence is thought to be related to the reduction in initial stress experienced by students who, in the preclinical phase, focus solely on technical skills without direct interaction with patients. As they transition into clinical settings, interacting with real patients, making accurate diagnoses, and successfully initiating treatment likely contribute to this positive shift. Studies indicating that students who practice on models exhibit greater self-confidence and calmness during local anesthesia procedures [26, 27] support our findings. These findings are consistent with the recommendations of the ESE undergraduate curriculum guidelines, which emphasize the importance of structured simulation-based training prior to clinical exposure [1]. Within our faculty’s instructional protocol, extensive preclinical model-based training in dental anesthesia precedes clinical education. It appears to enhance students’ confidence and performance during clinical internships, with a notable increase in self-confidence observed when students begin administering anesthesia independently.

No significant change in students’ self-confidence levels was observed throughout the educational process regarding the preparation of the access cavity and the identification of root canals. This step is often reported as the most challenging and stress-inducing step for students, and increasing demonstrations on simulated or extracted teeth during preclinical training has been suggested as a strategy to enhance early clinical exposure and reduce stress when treating patients [12, 28, 29]. Statistically significant increases in students’ self-confidence were observed in the following stages: determination of working length, shaping of root canals, irrigation, root canal filling, and final restoration. These elevated confidence levels in endodontic treatment steps are consistent with findings from similar studies [8, 28, 30]. The observed increase in confidence regarding working length determination was likely due to the contrast between its instruction via radiographic methods in preclinical training and the easier and faster application of this step using electronic apex locators in clinical practice. Additionally, the literature suggests that difficulties encountered in endodontic procedures can be overcome through increased practice [28, 31]. Consistent with this, increased confidence in other procedural stages appears to be associated with the positive impact of intensive hands-on practice during the preclinical phase on clinical performance. In fact, one study reported that students experienced a “practice shock” during the transition from preclinical to clinical training, which heightened their anxiety levels [11]. Studies by De Souza and Frese also emphasized the need for a more gradual transition from the preclinical to clinical phases [32, 33]. In our study, the general increase in students’ self-confidence regarding endodontic procedures throughout their education can be attributed to the structured endodontic training approach at our faculty, which consists of preclinical, observation, and practical clinical stages. This structured approach appears to align with the gradual transition model recommended in the literature.

A statistically significant decrease in students’ self-confidence was observed regarding the clinical management of flare-up cases. This low level of confidence is consistent with findings reported in similar studies in the literature [9, 34]. Flare-ups are undesirable occurrences that can arise during the endodontic treatment process and may require unscheduled appointments in some cases [35]. Because these situations often involve patients experiencing pain or anxiety, it has been suggested that dental curricula should incorporate communication training and simulation-based exercises addressing such clinical scenarios [36–38]. However, limitations in training alone may not fully account for the observed decline in self-confidence. Flare-up management is recognized as a clinically challenging and stress-inducing situation not only for undergraduate students but also for experienced dental practitioners [39]. Beyond educational factors, flare-up management represents a clinically complex and multifactorial challenge influenced by microbial status, host inflammatory response, and procedural variables [40]. This inherent clinical unpredictability may further increase students’ perceived difficulty in managing these situations, particularly when they are encountered in real patient settings. Therefore, the decline in students’ self-confidence may be related not only to curricular factors but also to the intrinsic complexity and clinical demands of flare-up cases. The limited exposure to flare-up scenarios during preclinical training, combined with the requirement for students to interact directly with patients experiencing pain or distress between treatment sessions during clinical internships, may further explain the challenges faced and the associated decrease in self-confidence.

The procedures involving bleaching endodontically treated teeth and rubber dam application were reported by students as the areas in which they felt the least confident. This decrease in self-confidence is consistent with previous findings in the literature [9]. At the institution where this study was conducted, bleaching procedures are not a mandatory component of the undergraduate curriculum and are generally performed by postgraduate students; therefore, lower confidence levels among undergraduate students are expected. In contrast, the use of a rubber dam is an essential part of modern endodontic treatment, and it is not possible to complete treatment successfully without it. At our institution, rubber dam application is mandatory during undergraduate endodontic clinical training. Nevertheless, students may encounter practical challenges related to case complexity, patient cooperation, and time constraints, which could negatively affect their perceived confidence despite formal curricular requirements. However, widespread resistance to the application of rubber dams has been reported among both dental students and professional practitioners [41, 42]. This resistance is mainly attributed to the technical difficulty of the procedure and the potential dissatisfaction from patients. Nonetheless, rubber dam usage is mandatory not only from a clinical success standpoint but also in terms of ethical and legal responsibilities [9]. Therefore, students must be equipped with the necessary skills to perform rubber dam application effectively in all clinical cases. Similarly, the ESE undergraduate curriculum guidelines highlight the importance of targeted educational support and progressive clinical training for technically demanding procedures and complication management during undergraduate endodontic education [1]. This should be supported by increasing the focus on this skill during preclinical training and should be prioritized by dental faculties.

When self-confidence levels were assessed regarding different tooth groups treated with endodontic procedures, significant increases were observed throughout the educational process in the maxillary anterior, mandibular anterior, and premolar teeth. These findings are in line with similar studies reported in the literature [9, 30]. These increases were most likely the result of less complex root canal systems and the more accessible positions of these tooth groups compared with molars. On the other hand, a significant decrease in self-confidence was noted for procedures involving maxillary and mandibular molars during the educational process. Other studies in the literature have also indicated that students feel inadequately prepared for endodontic treatment of molars and report lower confidence levels with these teeth [5, 9, 12, 30]. In addition, a meta-analysis conducted by Brochado Martins et al. revealed variability in treatment outcomes depending on tooth type and procedural complexity [43]. The complex and variable root canal anatomies of molar teeth, their posterior location in the oral cavity, direct clinical interactions with patients, and students’ limited clinical experience in this area contribute to the challenges of treating these teeth and the decline in self-confidence. Although preclinical training is intensive and comprehensive, difficulties in access and patient-related variability in the clinical setting are considered major factors explaining this drop in confidence when treating molars [12]. These findings may also have important pedagogical implications. Lower confidence levels in complex clinical scenarios, particularly during molar treatments and the management of flare-ups, highlight the need for targeted educational strategies such as increased simulation-based training, case-based learning, and closer clinical supervision during the transition from preclinical to clinical practice.

While a previous global survey study [23] has primarily focused on the structural and curricular characteristics of undergraduate endodontic education, the present study adopts a complementary approach by providing student-centered evidence through a longitudinal assessment of self-confidence levels across successive educational stages. Despite differences in scope and content among these studies, it is noteworthy that the areas in which undergraduate endodontic students experience the greatest difficulty, as well as the procedural stages associated with lower levels of self-confidence, show substantial consistency across the literature [12, 24, 30]. Our study makes a unique contribution to the literature by progressively evaluating dental undergraduate students’ self-confidence levels regarding endodontic procedures and the tooth groups involved, from preclinical training through clinical internships. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Self-confidence levels were assessed only within a single cohort of students, without comparisons with students from other institutions. Moreover, the study was conducted at a single institution and did not account for individual demographic variables, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Because progression to subsequent academic years is strictly regulated by the institutional curriculum, students who failed prerequisite courses were unable to advance to the next stage and therefore could not be included in the longitudinal follow-up. Also, since the data were collected through self-reported confidence ratings, subjective factors, such as social desirability bias leading to a possible overestimation of confidence, particularly during early educational stages, or students’ individual interest in endodontics, may have influenced the results [44]. Furthermore, it should be emphasized that self-confidence does not necessarily correspond to objective clinical competence. Since the present study evaluated students’ perceived confidence rather than their actual clinical performance, the findings should be interpreted within the context of self-perception rather than as a direct indicator of clinical proficiency. Another methodological consideration is that some model fit indices obtained from the confirmatory factor analysis were below the commonly recommended threshold values (≥ 0.90). This may reflect the fact that the evaluated questions were originally intended to rate students’ self-confidence in specific endodontic procedures and the treatment of different tooth types rather than to function as a formal psychometric scale. Finally, recall bias between different educational stages cannot be entirely excluded, as participants were asked to reflect on their perceived confidence across varying clinical experiences. Future research is recommended to include larger samples and comparisons across students from multiple universities.

Targeted educational interventions addressing specific areas in which students report decreased self-confidence may be more effective than a general increase in overall clinical practice time. Advanced simulation models that replicate complex root canal anatomies, particularly in molar teeth, acute pain–related flare-up scenarios, or isolation challenges such as rubber dam application may allow students to encounter high-difficulty clinical situations in a structured manner before transitioning to real patient care. Such focused approaches may contribute to reducing the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice in future research and in the development of undergraduate endodontic curricula.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study evaluated changes in dental undergraduate students’ self-confidence regarding endodontic procedures across consecutive educational stages, from preclinical training to clinical internships. The findings indicated that self-confidence increased in several procedural steps as training progressed; however, lower confidence levels persisted in more complex clinical areas, particularly molar endodontic treatment and complication management. These results highlight that, beyond theoretical knowledge and technical skills, adequate clinical exposure and case diversity play an important role in the development of students’ self-confidence. Expanding hands-on training opportunities and simulation-based practice may further support students in managing complex endodontic procedures.
