Persistent postoperative opioid use in patients treated with total hip arthroplasty and hemiarthroplasty for femoral neck fracture: a propensity-score-matched analysis
Authors: Mina Tohidi, Jensen Murphy, Stephen M Mann, Patti A Groome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Pain management after hip fracture surgery is particularly challenging because clinicians must simultaneously minimize prolonged opioid exposure while ensuring effective recovery in this vulnerable patient population. We sought to compare persistent postoperative opioid use across a matched group of patients with femoral neck fracture treated with either total hip arthroplasty (THA) or hemiarthroplasty and to describe the association between patient characteristics and opioid use.Methods:Routinely collected health care databases were linked at ICES to create a population-based cohort of patients aged 66 years or older in Ontario, Canada, who underwent THA or hemiarthroplasty for a femoral neck fracture between 2002 and 2016. We used propensity-score matching to balance potential confounders. We quantified and compared persistent postoperative opioid use between treatment groups using survival analysis.Results:The full cohort consisted of 45 119 patients, of whom 41 247 (91.4%) were treated with hemiarthroplasty and 3872 (8.6%) were treated with THA. More than half (n = 25 168, 55.8%) of patients had not filled an opioid prescription in the year before hip fracture and were considered naive to opioids. Overall, 5958 (23.7%) opioid-naive patients filled at least 1 opioid prescription between 90 days and 1 year after hip fracture. All eligible patients who underwent THA were matched 1:2 to hemiarthroplasty patients (matched cohort n = 6492). There was no statistically significant difference in persistent postoperative opioid use between treatment groups (relative risk 1.06, 95% confidence interval 0.96 to 1.17). Patients living in long-term care or requiring home care services and patients with a high comorbidity burden were more likely to use opioid medication, before and after hip fracture.Conclusion:Persistent opioid use is common after both THA and hemiarthroplasty for hip fracture. Patients with higher health care needs before fracture and higher comorbidity burden were more likely to persistently use postoperative opioids.

Most older patients with displaced femoral neck fractures are treated with hemiarthroplasty or total hip arthroplasty (THA).1 Hemiarthroplasty is technically easier and has a shorter operative time, lower risk of dislocation, and less intraoperative blood loss.2 However, in younger, more active patients, THA is sometimes favoured, given its lower risk of acetabular erosion and the potential for better pain control and improved function.2 A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 randomized controlled trials demonstrated a small, possibly unimportant, difference in health-related quality of life favouring THA.3 Functional outcome — which was measured using a composite of pain, function, deformity, and range of motion — was similar between groups.

Postoperative pain after hip arthroplasty is problematic for patients and for the health care system.4 Persistent postoperative opioid use can lead to dependency and contributes to the overall opioid epidemic.5,6 Few studies have looked specifically at postoperative pain control among patients treated with hemiarthroplasty or THA, and those few studies have shown mixed results. Several studies have shown that patients treated with hemiarthroplasty had more long-term postoperative pain than those treated with THA.1,8–10 Leonardsson and colleagues found that patients younger than 70 years who were treated with THA reported less pain and more satisfaction than those treated with hemiarthroplasty. However, there was no statistically significant difference by treatment approach among patients aged 70 years and older.11 Li and colleagues found that postoperative pain was higher among patients treated with THA, compared with those treated with hemiarthroplasty, immediately after surgery. However, at 6 months and 12 months postoperatively, patients treated with THA had improved pain control, range of motion, and joint function.2 Viswanath and colleagues showed that patients treated with THA had higher likelihood of hospital readmission because of pain than those treated with hemiarthroplasty. 12

The purpose of our study was to compare persistent postoperative opioid use among patients treated with hemiarthroplasty or THA for hip fracture. Our hypothesis was that there would be no difference in opioid use between treatment groups between 90 days and 1 year after hip fracture. A secondary aim was to describe the association between baseline patient characteristics and preoperative and postoperative opioid use in this population.

Methods

Study design, setting, and study population

This population-based retrospective cohort study used provincial-level health care data from Ontario, Canada. Ontario is the most populous province in Canada, with 15.4 million residents in 2023. The Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) provides residents with coverage for medically necessary health care. Individual-level data on health care encounters and patient demographics were linked from multiple administrative databases at ICES. The databases and relevant data are described in Appendix 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.020425/tab-related-content.

The eligible study population consisted of patients aged 66 years or older who underwent THA or hemiarthroplasty for a femoral neck fracture in Ontario between Apr. 1, 2002, and Dec. 31, 2016. We restricted the cohort to these dates to allow for at least 1 year follow-up after treatment. We identified hip fractures using codes from the Canadian version of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (S720.010, S72.011, S72.080, S72.081, S72.090, S72.091). We obtained treatment information for the index procedure from OHIP procedure codes billed by the primary surgeon.

We excluded nonresidents of Ontario, patients who were not eligible for OHIP at time of hip fracture, those missing ICES key numbers (IKN) for linkage, patients younger than 66 years at diagnosis, those with pathologic hip fracture, those with more than 1 procedure code on the same day, and those whose surgery was performed more than 7 days after hospital admission.

We used opioid prescription data from the Ontario Drug Benefit (ODB) Claims database, which contains claims for prescription drugs received under the ODB program. To be eligible for the ODB program, residents require a valid OHIP card and must meet at least 1 of the following criteria: age 65 years or older, residents of long-term care facilities, receiving services under the Home Care Program, receiving coverage from the Trillium Drug Program, on social assistance, or eligible for the Special Drugs Program. Patients aged 66 years and older at time of hip fracture were included in this study so that we would have complete data for the 1-year lookback window for opioid use before hip fracture. The other exclusion criteria were for analytic purposes to limit the risk of misclassification of exposure. The complete list of opioid medications used in this study is included in Appendix 1.

Baseline characteristics

We measured age, sex, rurality, marginalization, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, frailty, dementia, residence in long-term care, preadmission home care services, congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD), diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and treatment year. We determined marginalization using ON-Marg, a multifaceted, area-based measurement that considers economic, ethnoracial, age-based, and social marginalization. 13 Baseline comorbidity burden was measured using the Johns Hopkins Aggregated Diagnosis Group (ADG) Index, including health care encounter data for 2 years before hip fracture. Frailty was measured using the Johns Hopkins Adjusted Clinical Groups (ACG) indicator for frailty-defining diagnoses.14 Details regarding race and ethnicity were not captured in available data sets and therefore omitted in this study. Additional details regarding variable definitions, including validation studies, are in Appendix 1. Patients accrued follow-up data until the end of OHIP eligibility, death, or Dec. 31, 2017, whichever came first.

Outcome measures

Patients were considered naive to opioids if they had not filled any opioid prescriptions in the year before hip fracture.15

The primary outcome was persistent postoperative opioid use. This was defined as filling at least 1 opioid prescription between 90 days and 1 year after hip fracture. 16 The focus of this study was on medium-term opioid use as a proxy for ongoing pain control, rather than opioid use in the early postoperative period to deal with acute, surgery-related pain. We used the 1-year cut-off to decrease the incidence of opioid use for other, non-hip-related causes.

Statistical analysis

We compared baseline demographics of patients by perioperative opioid use status using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the Student t test for continuous variables.

Patients who underwent THA were matched 1:2 with those who underwent hemiarthroplasty using greedy nearest-neighbour propensity-score matching, with a caliper distance of 0.2 standardized deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Patients with missing data (n = 18 from THA group, n = 417 from hemiarthroplasty group) were excluded from the match. Balance diagnostics were performed to ensure that the process created a comparable matched cohort in which potential confounders and predictors were balanced between treatment groups.

The following variables were included in the propensity score model: age, sex, marginalization, rurality, ASA physical status class, comorbidity burden (sum of all ADGs, sum of all major ADGs, ADG7 [the ADG “likely to recur: discrete” category, used to classify medical conditions that occur episodically]), dementia, frailty, long-term care residence, preadmission home care services, diabetes, COPD, CHF, and hypertension. In sensitivity analyses, we created several matched cohorts with varying matching ratios (1:1, 1:3) and baseline variables (all baseline variables, only variables that were predictors of outcome).

We compared persistent postoperative opioid use between treatment groups using relative risk (RR). We used the Kaplan–Meier failure function (the complement of the survival function) to estimate the probability of postoperative opioid use within each treatment group at specific time points, while accounting for risk of death and censoring. We used a stratified log-rank test to compare equality of curves in matched samples.

We used post hoc, multivariable logistic regression models to determine which factors were independently associated with opioid use before hip fracture and persistent postoperative opioid use after surgery.

A data set with the eligible study population was created by a statistician at ICES and provided to the authors. The primary author cleaned the database, applied further exclusion criteria, and performed the analyses.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.15 (SAS Institute). A 2-sided p value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the University Health Sciences and Affiliated Teaching Hospitals Research Ethics Board (no. 6024833).

Results

Full and opioid-naive cohort

During the study period, 46 080 patients aged 66 years and older were admitted to a hospital with a nonpathological femoral neck fracture and underwent hemiarthroplasty or THA. After application of exclusion criteria, the full cohort consisted of 45 119 patients, of whom 41 247 (91.4%) were treated with hemiarthroplasty and 3872 (8.6%) treated with THA. Of the full cohort, 25 168 patients (55.8%) had not filled any opioid prescriptions in the year before hip fracture. These patients were considered naive to opioids. Matched cohort

Two appropriate matches in the hemiarthroplasty were found for each of the 2014 eligible patients in the THA group, resulting in a matched cohort of 6042 patients (Table 2). The covariate balance in the matched cohort was considerably improved, with all baseline characteristics having standardized differences less than 0.05.

Persistent postoperative opioid use by treatment group

Overall, 5958 (23.7%) opioid-naive patients filled at least 1 opioid prescription between 90 days and 1 year after hip fracture. Among all opioid-naive patients, those treated with hemiarthroplasty were slightly more likely to use opioids during this postoperative period, than those treated with THA (23.8% v. 22.0%; RR 1.09, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.18).

In the matched cohort, there was no statistically significant difference in risk of persistent postoperative opioid use between treatment groups (23.2% v. 22.0% for hemiarthroplasty and THA groups, respectively; RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.17). The risk of postoperative opioid use remained similar between treatment groups after accounting for risk of death (1-yr cumulative incidence of 25.7% [95% CI 24.2% to 27.1%] v. 24.0% [95% CI 22.1% to 26.0%] in the hemiarthroplasty and THA groups, respectively; stratified log-rank test p = 0.08) (Figure 2). Results remained unchanged in sensitivity analyses that accounted for different strategies of propensity-score matching, including number of matches for each patient who underwent THA and exact matching for year of surgery between patients treated with hemiarthroplasty and THA.

Association between patient characteristics and postoperative opioid use

Patient characteristics associated with persistent postoperative opioid use among opioid-naive patients included long-term care residence before hip fracture (23.4% v. 13.9% for patients with persistent postoperative opioid use v. nonusers, respectively, p < 0.001), dementia (38.8% v. 34.0% for patients with persistent postoperative opioid use v. nonusers, respectively, p < 0.001), and higher comorbidity burden (Table 3).

Residence in long-term care before hip fracture remained strongly associated with persistent postoperative opioid use after controlling for patient baseline characteristics including comorbidity burden (adjusted OR 2.09, 95% CI 1.92 to 2.28) (Table 4). Higher comorbidity burden (measured as the sum of all ADGs), use of home care services before hip fracture, and younger age were associated with persistent postoperative opioid use (Table 4). Lastly, female patients were slightly more likely to use persistent postoperative opioids than male patients (adjusted OR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.17).

Discussion

Our study has several important findings regarding opioid use among patients with hip fracture. Almost half of patients aged 66 years and older with hip fracture used opioid medication in the year before hip fracture. Of patients who were naive to opioids before hip fracture, approximately 1 in 4 filled at least 1 opioid prescription between 90 days and 1 year after hip fracture. These findings highlight the high prevalence of opioid use in this patient population in general, both before and after hip fracture. Patients living in long-term care or requiring home care services and patients with a high comorbidity burden were more likely to use opioid medication before and after hip fracture. This finding is likely multifactorial; these patients are likely to have chronic conditions requiring analgesia and they may have easier access to health care providers who would prescribe opioid medication than healthier patients and those living at home. The particularly pronounced prevalence of opioid use among the most vulnerable patients has been shown in other population-based studies. Jensen-Dahm and colleagues showed that opioid use rose steadily with age and was particularly high among patients with dementia (32%) and patients residing in nursing homes (41%).17

Previous studies that have recommended THA over hemiarthroplasty for treatment of displaced femoral neck fractures comment on improved pain control and function with THA at 1 year after the procedure and beyond.1,7–10 In contrast, our study measured persistent opioid use during the subacute postoperative period, between 90 days and 1 year after hip fracture. Our findings, which contrast with earlier studies, may be attributable to 2 differences in study methodology. First, our outcome measure of opioid use was different than prior studies that measured pain and function using various scoring systems, including the Harris hip score, the 36-Item Short-Form Survey, the Western Ontario and McMaster University Osteoarthritis Index, and the Timed Up and Go Test. Second, the specific period of interest in our study is one that has been generally understudied. A difference in pain scores and function may exist, but according to results of our study, this may not equate to differences in opioid use.

The high prevalence of persistent postoperative opioid use after hip fracture is documented in several studies. Hereford and colleagues showed that 23% of opioid-naive patients with hip fracture become chronic users after surgery. 18 Using a Danish nationwide registry, Edwards and colleagues found that 13% of previous opioid nonusers filled at least 2 opioid prescriptions 3 to 12 months after femoral neck fracture. 19 They identified several risk factors associated with opioid use during this time, including younger age, obesity, preoperative use of anti-inflammatory and antidepressant agents, and presence of cardiovascular comorbidities, diabetes, gastrointestinal diseases, dementia, COPD, or renal disease.19 Similarly, Okike and colleagues used data from the Hip Fracture Registry in the United States to study opioid use in the first year after surgery.20 Younger age, female sex, current or former smoker status, anxiety, chronic musculoskeletal pain, and general pain were associated with opioid use. They compared treatment types and showed that patients who underwent internal fixation had higher likelihood of prolonged opioid use than those who underwent hemiarthroplasty (incidence rate ratio (IRR) 1.41 95% CI 1.11 to 1.78). Like our study, they did not find a statistically significant difference in opioid use between patients treated with hemiarthroplasty and THA (IRR 0.84, 95% CI 0.09 to 7.84). Unlike our analyses, Okike and colleagues did not control for specific comorbidities. This may explain, at least in part, some of differences between our results.

Strengths of our study include the large, population-based cohort and use of rigorous propensity-score methods to balance baseline variables and limit the potential for confounding.

Limitations

The use of routinely collected health care administrative data limited our ability to capture certain patient characteristics that are associated with increased opioid consumption, such as race or ethnicity, education level, alcohol and tobacco use, mental health symptoms, and size of initial opioid prescription.21 If these variables are also associated with treatment choice between hemiarthroplasty and THA, our results would be limited by unmeasured or residual confounding. We did not have access to details on race or ethnicity in available data sets. Absence of these data may also affect study generalizability; this limitation is partially mitigated by our use of a population-based cohort.

We restricted our cohort to patients aged 66 years and older to ensure that we had complete and accurate records of opioid prescriptions. However, it would also be relevant to investigate for potential difference in postoperative opioid use among younger patients, given that persistent postoperative pain, functional limitations, and long-term opioid dependence may have different implications than in an older cohort.

We accounted for potential changes in opioid management practices over time in a sensitivity analysis that enforced exact matching for treatment year between patients treated with hemiarthroplasty and THA. Still, it would be valuable to examine whether opioid prescriptions patterns have changed over time in this patient population.

Lastly, future study of the association between persistent postoperative opioid use and functional status is warranted.

Conclusion

In summary, we showed that opioid use before and after treatment for hip fracture is common among patients aged 66 years and older. There was no statistically significant difference in persistent postoperative opioid use between patients treated with hemiarthroplasty and THA. We identified several patient factors associated with increased odds of postoperative opioid use after hip fracture in patients who were previously naive to opioids. These include higher pre-existing health care needs, higher comorbidity burden, and younger patient age. Targeted preventive intervention strategies and appropriate counselling may be beneficial for this vulnerable population. Further exploration of these interventions is required.
