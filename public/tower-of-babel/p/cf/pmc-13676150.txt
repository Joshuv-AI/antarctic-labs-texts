Use of an AI metabolomics score to predict cardiovascular risk among patients with Psoriasis in the MGB and UK Biobanks✰
Authors: Christos P Kotanidis, Art Schuermans, Priyam Choksi, Jessica Lasky-Su, Pradeep Natarajan, Linke Li, Anand Rohatgi, Elizabeth Karlson, Katherine Liao, Joseph F Merola, Michael Garshick, Charalambos Antoniades, Ron Blankstein, Michael Honigberg, Leo Buckley, Brittany N Weber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic inflammation in psoriasis accelerates atherosclerosis and confers excess cardiovascular risk that is not fully captured by traditional clinical predictors. Metabolomic profiling may identify disease-specific metabolic signatures associated with cardiovascular events and enhance risk prediction beyond conventional factors.ObjectivesTo determine whether plasma metabolomic profiling improves cardiovascular risk prediction in patients with psoriasis and to develop and externally validate an artificial intelligence–driven metabolomic risk score (MetaPsoAI) for incident cardiovascular events.MethodsWe analyzed 1086 individuals with psoriasis and no prior coronary artery disease from the Mass General Brigham (MGB) Biobank who underwent nuclear magnetic resonance–based quantification of 168 circulating metabolites. Associations between metabolites and incident all-cause death or myocardial infarction were assessed. An extreme gradient boosting algorithm was used to develop MetaPsoAI for prediction of myocardial infarction or stroke. External validation was performed in the UK Biobank (n = 274,000), stratified by psoriasis status.ResultsMultiple metabolites, particularly HDL subfractions and amino acid metabolites, were associated with cardiovascular outcomes. MetaPsoAI stratified risk across quartiles, with individuals in the highest quartile demonstrating a 3.6-fold higher risk of myocardial infarction or stroke in the MGB cohort. In the UK Biobank, the highest quartile was associated with a 1.9-fold increased risk among psoriasis patients, with stronger effect sizes compared with non-psoriasis controls.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the potential of AI-driven metabolomics to refine cardiovascular risk prediction in immune-mediated diseases and enable precision prevention strategies for patients with psoriasis.Keywords: Psoriasis, Cardiovascular risk, Prevention, Special populations, AI, Scores

Introduction

Autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases are increasingly recognized as major contributors to excess cardiovascular (CV) risk, independent of traditional risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia [1]. Systemic immune activation accelerates atherosclerosis through persistent activation of innate and adaptive immune cells, upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and endothelial dysfunction, thereby amplifying vascular inflammation and promoting plaque vulnerability [2]. As a result, individuals with autoimmune diseases experience higher rates of myocardial infarction, stroke, and premature cardiovascular mortality compared with the general population [3,4].

Among these conditions, psoriasis has emerged as a prototypical systemic inflammatory skin disease with significant cardiovascular implications [5]. Psoriasis is driven by activation of T cells and myeloid cells, with cytokine networks centered on TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23, and IL-6, which overlap with key mediators of atherogenesis [6]. Epidemiologic studies demonstrate that patients with psoriasis are up to 50% more likely to develop cardiovascular disease, with risk rising in proportion to disease severity [7]. Severe psoriasis is independently associated with a higher incidence of myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death, even after adjustment for traditional risk factors. Patients with psoriasis exhibit greater vascular inflammation and a higher burden of high-risk coronary plaque, with patterns similar to those observed in type 2 diabetes and familial hypercholesterolemia [8]. Despite these associations, residual cardiovascular risk remains incompletely explained by traditional risk prediction models [9].

Metabolomics offers a promising avenue to bridge this gap. By capturing small-molecule intermediates of cellular metabolism, metabolomic profiling provides an integrated readout of genetic, environmental, and inflammatory influences on disease biology [10]. In cardiovascular medicine, metabolomic signatures have been associated with atherosclerosis, coronary plaque progression, and adverse outcomes, but their utility for individualized risk prediction in autoimmune diseases such as psoriasis remains largely unexplored [11]. Importantly, integrating big data such as transcriptomics and metabolomics with artificial intelligence (AI) approaches enables the construction of multidimensional risk scores that may capture novel disease biology beyond conventional clinical and biochemical markers [12].

Here, we sought to determine whether lipid metabolomics can improve cardiovascular risk prediction in patients with psoriasis. Using large-scale biobank resources, we developed and validated an AI-driven metabolomic risk score (MetaPsoAI) for long-term prediction of hard cardiovascular clinical outcomes. Our findings provide insight into the metabolic underpinnings of cardiovascular risk in psoriasis and highlight the potential of metabolomics-informed precision medicine strategies for inflammatory diseases.

Methods

Study cohort

We utilized data from the participants in the Mass General Brigham (MGB) Biobank [13]. MGB Biobank participants were included in the analysis if they had plasma metabolomics data available. Participants were excluded if they had a history of coronary artery disease, were taking omega-3 fatty acids [14] or hormonal contraceptives [15] in the prior 3 months or were pregnant [16] at the time of blood draw. Psoriasis status was defined using a machine learning–derived computed phenotype based on structured and unstructured EHR data, developed and implemented within the Biobank, as previously described [17].

Exposures and outcomes in the MGB biobank

Metabolite levels were quantified using a targeted commercial H1 nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) platform (Nightingale Health, Helsinki, Finland). The primary exposures of interest were a core panel of measured metabolites (used here as the primary features) and additional derived ratios and composite measures. Unless otherwise specified, metabolites in the main text refers to the primary measured panel quantified on the same platform across cohorts. Analysis of raw biomarker distributions across batches and spectrometers suggested minimal batch- and spectrometer-related effects (Extended Data Fig. 1). The levels of clinically available metabolites measured by the NMR platform correlated significantly with levels available in the electronic health record (Extended Data Fig. 2). A standardized quality control pipeline was applied to the metabolomics data prior to analysis. Zero values and missing values were imputed using half the minimum observed value for each metabolite. Extreme outliers were excluded using a threshold of greater than four interquartile ranges from the median. Metabolite values were subsequently log-transformed (natural logarithm), and linear regression models were used to adjust for spectrometer-related effects, with residuals extracted for downstream analyses. These residuals were then standardized to ensure comparability across metabolites. The main outcome was first a composite of all-cause death or acute myocardial infarction (MACE) and second a composite of adverse cardiovascular events, defined as incident acute myocardial infarction and/or stroke. Cases of the outcomes were defined using two ICD codes (Extended Data Table 1).

Covariates

Demographic covariates were derived from patient-reported date of birth, patient-selected sex at birth (male, female or X), patient-selected race (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, White and Other, Unknown or Declined) and patient-selected ethnicity (Hispanic, Non-Hispanic and Unknown or Declined). Medical history was abstracted from ICD codes in the electronic health record data. The presence of one or more ICD codes in the 12 months before the date of the metabolomics blood draw was considered to indicate a positive history for that condition. Medication history was abstracted from electronic health record prescription orders. MGB Biobank participants were considered to be taking a medication if they had one or more prescription orders for that medication in the 90 days before the date of metabolomics blood draw. The lone exception to the medication use definition was for steroids, which required at least two prescription orders to capture chronic, rather than one-time, users. The years in which the blood sample used for metabolomics analysis was drawn were grouped into quartiles to account for temporal confounding. Fasting status was collected at the time of blood draw for all participants.

Matched controls analysis

To compare metabolomics associations with non-psoriasis matched individuals, MGB Biobank participants with psoriasis were matched using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement at a 1:2 ratio with MGB Biobank participants without psoriasis or rheumatoid arthritis based on the estimated propensity scores. Propensity scores were estimated using age, legal sex, race, ethnicity, creatinine, latest BMI, blood draw year (quarter), fasting status, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension.

Building metapsoai

To build MetaPsoAI an AI driven metabolomics score of cardiovascular risk in patients with psoriasis we turned to the MGB cohort again. To avoid confounding and direct pharmacologic modification of lipid metabolites at the time of sampling, we excluded patients receiving statins at the time of blood sampling. We acknowledge this restriction may reduce representativeness and may introduce selection bias; this is discussed explicitly in the limitations. Extreme gradient boosting with method “gbtree” was the method with the best performance as we have seen previously [12]. We therefore decided to choose extreme gradient boosting, also because this is the de facto standard algorithm for getting accurate results from predictive modelling with machine learning. It’s the fastest gradient-boosting library with very high accuracy. It is understood to perform well in many applications with a fair amount of data and can detect and learn from non-linear data patterns [18]. The final product of the XGBoost algorithm was log transformed and the value 10 added (using the formula: [log(MetaPsoAI) + 10] to avoid negative or very low values and normalize its distribution) and defined as MetaPsoAI. To avoid overfitting issue, we used 5-fold cross validation and tuned the hyperparameters by optimising step size shrinkage, L2 regularization parameter and learning rate. We used early stopping technique that works by monitoring the performance of the model that is being trained on a separate test dataset and stopping the training procedure once the performance on the test dataset has not improved after a fixed number of training iterations. It avoids overfitting by attempting to automatically select the inflection point where performance on the test dataset starts to decrease while performance on the training dataset continues to improve as the model starts to overfit. Although modest number of samples were used during training step of model development, the accuracy of the model was not degraded on external validation data suggesting that model was not overfitted.

Validation in the UK biobank

External validation analyses were performed in the UK Biobank. The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort of approximately 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years recruited across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010 [19]. At study baseline (i.e., enrollment), all participants underwent physical examination and provided information on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history, and medication use. Data on healthcare utilization was extracted from linked hospital records, enabling the ascertainment of prevalent diseases (onset before enrolment) as well as incident events (onset after enrolment). As detailed earlier [20], participants’ records were linked to data on secondary care admissions (up until 31/10/2022) and death certificates (up until 31/05/2024), and were used to identify prevalent conditions (with onset before enrolment) and incident events (onset after enrolment). Time-to-event data from death certificates were also censored to match the follow-up period available for secondary care data (up until 31/10/2022).

The present analysis used the subset with baseline EDTA-plasma metabolomics profiled on the Nightingale Health high-throughput nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) platform. Details on metabolomic profiling within the UK Biobank have been described previously [21]. We excluded individuals with any prevalent cardiovascular disease at baseline, resulting in a final cohort including 1858 individuals with and 230,003 without psoriasis at baseline.

The metabolomic risk score (i.e., MetaPsoAI) ascertained from the NMR metabolite data was log-transformed and offset (i.e., added to 10) prior to analyses. We analyzed MetaPsoAI as (i) a standardized continuous variable (per SD) and (ii) categorical bins (quartiles with the lowest category as reference). Consistent with the outcome MetaPsoAI was trained on, we tested the associations of MetaPsoAI with a composite of incident myocardial infarction and stroke, stratified by psoriasis status at baseline. Additional analyses tested the associations of MetaPsoAI with (i) incident stroke alone; (ii) a composite of cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke; and (iii) and all-cause mortality. Follow-up began at assessment and ended at the first event, death, or end of linkage, whichever occurred first. Definitions of cardiovascular conditions and psoriasis have been published earlier [20].

Statistical analysis

Participant demographics are summarized as percentages or mean (standard deviation) for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Between-group comparisons were performed using Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon paired samples test for numeric variables.

In the MGB Biobank matching was performed with propensity scores that were estimated using logistic regression models including all covariates. The association of each metabolite with outcomes was assessed using Cox regression models adjusted for with false discovery rate correction for multiple comparisons. Missing values within the MetaPsoAI development dataset were imputed with predictive mean matching using the “mice” package. Probabilities for outcome events were plotted using Kaplan–Meier failure curves.

In the UK Biobank we fit Cox proportional-hazards models separately in psoriasis and non-psoriasis groups for each endpoint, reporting hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for the top vs bottom exposure category and per-SD increase. Multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, ever-smoking, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive therapy, lipid-lowering therapy, type 2 diabetes, and psoriasis medications. Body mass index and systolic blood pressure were imputed using predictive mean matching (using the mice() function of the mice package in R) with age, sex, smoking, antihypertensive therapy, lipid-lowering therapy, diabetes, and psoriasis-drug use in the imputation model. Cumulative incidence was visualized with Kaplan-Meier curves.

All tests were two-sided and α was set at 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed in the R environment (R version 3.6.0 and R Studio version 1.2.1335) using the survival, survminer, mice, and rms packages.

Results

Metabolite associations with cardiovascular outcomes in the MGB biobank

We leveraged the Mass General Brigham (MGB) Biobank, which includes over 40,000 participants, to examine the relationship between circulating metabolites and cardiovascular risk. A detailed flowchart of the study population and analytic pipeline is shown in Fig. 1. Plasma metabolites were quantified using a high-throughput nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy platform (Nightingale Health), which provided comprehensive coverage of 168 metabolites across lipid, amino acid, and energy pathways (Extended Data Fig. 1).

From this, we identified 1086 individuals with psoriasis and no prior coronary artery disease. The mean age was 62 years, 42% were male, and 13% were taking statins at baseline; full demographic characteristics are reported in Extended Data Table 2.

A total of 21 metabolites were significantly associated with the primary endpoint of MACE after FDR correction (Fig. 2a). Higher levels of cholesterol and cholesterol esters in small HDL particles emerged as the strongest protective factors, whereas free cholesterol and cholesterol esters carried in very large HDL particles were among the top adverse predictors. A complete list of metabolite associations with the primary endpoint and a composite of acute myocardial infarction and/or stroke in patients with psoriasis is provided in Extended Data Table 3.

Comparison with matched non-psoriasis controls

To compare the metabolite associations with cardiovascular outcomes between patients with and without psoriasis, we constructed a 2:1 matched control cohort from the MGB Biobank. Controls were matched to psoriasis cases on demographic and cardiovascular risk factors using the methodology detailed in the Methods. This resulted in a cohort of approximately 2000 individuals without psoriasis.

When we compared metabolite associations across psoriasis cases and matched controls, 19 metabolites were predictive in both groups (Fig. 2b). Importantly, two metabolites emerged as uniquely predictive in psoriasis compared to the control group: cholesterol esters in extra-large HDL particles and omega-3 fatty acids, neither of which reached significance in the control population (Fig. 2b). In the control population, HDL related metabolites and albumin as the most protective, while large HDL particles were associated with adverse outcomes (Fig. 2c). A complete list of metabolite associations with the primary endpoint in controls is provided in Extended Data Table 4.

To visualize these cross-cohort differences in effect magnitude and direction, we employed mirror bar plots depicting the hazard ratios of corresponding metabolites in psoriasis and control groups. Although the directionality of associations was largely concordant between groups, the magnitude of effect was consistently amplified among patients with psoriasis, suggesting the possibility of accentuated metabolic perturbation in the context of psoriatic disease (Fig. 2d). These mirror bar plots are intended to provide a descriptive visualization of relative effect size magnitude and direction across groups. They do not represent formal statistical interaction testing, and differences between psoriasis and control groups should be interpreted cautiously given potential sampling variability.

Development of the metapsoai score

Given the extensive range of metabolites identified in our discovery analysis, each with varying effect sizes and directions of association, we next sought to derive an integrative metric capable of summarizing the metabolomic signature of cardiovascular risk in psoriasis. While individual metabolites provide valuable biological insights, their collective predictive capacity may improve clinical risk stratification. Thus, we developed an artificial intelligence–driven composite score designed to capture multidimensional metabolic information in a single, interpretable cardiovascular risk estimate (MetaPsoAI).

To minimize confounding, we excluded individuals receiving statin therapy at the time of blood sampling and restricted the outcome definition to acute myocardial infarction and/or stroke, omitting all-cause mortality to isolate cardiovascular-specific risk. Model development followed the analytic framework described in the Methods, employing an extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) algorithm trained on adjudicated cardiovascular events either myocardial infarction or stroke (n = 29). Missing metabolomic values were imputed using predictive mean matching, and 5-fold cross-validation with early stopping was implemented to prevent overfitting.

The XGBoost model identified key metabolites across diverse biochemical pathways as top contributors to the MetaPsoAI score (Fig. 3a). The MetaPsoAI score included metabolites from lipid subclasses, amino acid metabolism, glycolytic intermediates, and osmolyte pathways, highlighting the broad metabolic landscape underpinning cardiovascular risk in psoriasis. The full list of metabolites contributing to MetaPsoAI is given in Extended Data Table 5. The distribution of MetaPsoAI scores exhibited a right-skewed profile, with most individuals clustering at lower predicted risk and a distinct subgroup demonstrating elevated values (Fig. 3b).

Metabolites span major biochemical categories including lipids, amino acids, glycolytic intermediates, and osmolytes. b, Distribution of the MetaPsoAI score in the MGB Biobank psoriasis cohort (n = 1086). The right-skewed distribution reflects a broad range of predicted risk, with a distinct high-risk tail. c, Kaplan–Meier curves depicting incidence of acute myocardial infarction or stroke across quartiles of the MetaPsoAI score. Patients in the highest quartile (Q4) demonstrated a 3.6-fold higher risk compared to those in Q1–Q3. d, Kaplan–Meier curves for the secondary composite endpoint of all-cause death or acute myocardial infarction, showing stepwise risk stratification by increasing MetaPsoAI score.

Within the MGB Biobank training population, patients were stratified by quartiles of the MetaPsoAI score. Those in the highest quartile exhibited a 3.6-fold (HR 3.63, 95% CI: 2.79 to 4.72, p < 0.001) higher risk of myocardial infarction or stroke compared to individuals in the lower three quartiles (Fig. 3c). Importantly, the score also retained predictive value for the broader composite of all-cause death or myocardial infarction, with higher MetaPsoAI values associated with significantly increased risk of this secondary endpoint (Fig. 3d). Collectively, these findings indicate that the MetaPsoAI score may capture a metabolomics-derived cardiovascular signal among individuals with psoriasis.

External validation of MetaPsoAI in the UK Biobank

To evaluate the generalizability of MetaPsoAI, we next validated the model in the UK Biobank, which includes over 274,000 participants with plasma metabolomics data quantified using the same Nightingale Health NMR platform [21]. This cohort comprised 1858 individuals with psoriasis and without known cardiovascular disease and approximately 230,003 participants without psoriasis.

When applied to the endpoint on which it was originally trained -acute myocardial infarction and/or stroke- MetaPsoAI was validated, with patients in the highest quartile having a 1.9-fold higher risk (HR 1.89, 95% CI: 1.09 to 3.29) compared to patients in the lowest quartile (Fig. 4a). In the non-psoriasis population, the hazard ratio for the highest quartile was 1.35 (95% CI: 1.28 to 1.44), Fig. 4a). When examining endpoint components, stroke was the strongest driver of the differential effect (Fig. 4b). Psoriasis patients in the highest quartile had an HR of 3.72 (95% CI: 1.52 to 9.14, p = 0.004), whereas non-psoriasis participants in the same quartile had an HR of 1.30 (95% CI: 1.2 to 1.41, p < 0.001).

Kaplan–Meier curves for the prognostic value of the MetaPsoAI score for acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and/or stroke (a), stroke alone (b), composite endpoint of cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke (c) and all-cause mortality (d) across MetaPsoAI quartiles in individuals with psoriasis (left, n = 1858) and non-psoriasis (right, n = 230,003). Unadjusted HR (95% CI) are represented in the images. See appendix for similar results with the HRs after adjustment for risk factors.

For the composite endpoint of cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke, MetaPsoAI again validated in both groups (Fig. 4c). In psoriasis, the adjusted hazard ratio for the top versus bottom quartile was 1.85 (95% CI, 1.15–2.98), compared with 1.36 in the non-psoriasis population, with higher cumulative incidence across the 16-year UK Biobank follow-up period. Finally, when tested against all-cause mortality, MetaPsoAI did not predict overall death in psoriasis patients (Fig. 4d). indicating that the score reflects cardiovascular rather than generalized health risk. Effect sizes and directions remained largely unchanged after adjusting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors (Extended Data Fig. 4). Although MetaPsoAI remained associated with cardiovascular outcomes after adjustment for traditional risk factors, formal assessment of incremental discrimination was not the primary objective of this proof-of-concept study. Future work should evaluate the added predictive value of metabolomic risk integration beyond established clinical risk scores in psoriasis-specific populations.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that plasma metabolomic profiling reveals cardiovascular risk signatures among individuals with psoriasis and that integration of these signals through artificial intelligence enables risk stratification (Central Illustration). Using two large biobanks, we identified metabolites linking psoriasis to cardiovascular outcomes and developed MetaPsoAI, an AI-driven metabolomic score that stratifies long-term risk of cardiovascular events. In parallel, we identified distinct patterns of HDL subfractions, and amino acid metabolites associated with cardiovascular outcomes, providing biological context for the observed predictive signal. Together, these findings offer translational insight into how systemic inflammation alters cardiovascular metabolism in psoriasis and how AI-based integration of these features can refine cardiovascular risk assessment in inflammatory disease.

Central Illustration.

Psoriasis is now firmly established as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease, driven by chronic systemic inflammation that accelerates atherosclerosis and plaque vulnerability [22]. Imaging studies using coronary CT angiography and PET/CT have shown that psoriasis severity correlates with noncalcified plaque burden and aortic vascular inflammation, both of which regress following improvement in skin disease [23]. Mechanistically, inflammasome signaling and cytokine pathways involving IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α contribute to endothelial dysfunction and vascular immune activation [24], supporting the view that psoriasis represents a human model of inflammation-driven atherosclerosis. These data have prompted consensus guidelines advocating recognition of psoriasis as a condition conferring enhanced cardiovascular risk [5,25].

Our metabolomic analyses extend these findings by identifying circulating metabolites that track with cardiovascular outcomes in psoriasis, suggesting that metabolic remodelling may underlie the observed excess risk. Consistent with prior population-based metabolomics studies, pathways involving lipids, amino acids, and glycolysis were the strongest determinants of risk [26,27]. HDL subfractions and amino acid metabolism, particularly involving branched-chain and aromatic amino acids, emerged as central features, mirroring previous associations with atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction [28,29]. In a large pooled cohorts analysis, larger HDL particles and higher cholesterol content in HDL was associated with increased risk of AMI and ischemic stroke [30,31]. These pathways are closely linked to oxidative stress, nitric oxide bioavailability, and immune activation [32], processes known to be dysregulated in psoriasis [5]. The amplification of these metabolic signals among psoriasis patients compared to matched controls suggests that systemic inflammation intensifies existing cardiometabolic perturbations.

Development of MetaPsoAI addresses a translational gap. While individual metabolites are biologically informative, their combined predictive power is limited by complex intercorrelations and nonlinear interactions [33]. By using artificial intelligence modelling, we integrated multiple metabolic features into a single composite score at the individual-patient level [34]. External validation in the UK Biobank confirmed the generalizability of MetaPsoAI and underscored its cardiovascular specificity. However, differences in apparent performance between a disease-enriched subgroup and the general population can also arise from covariate distributions, treatment patterns, and sample-size imbalance. Thus, stronger associations in psoriasis should not be viewed as definitive evidence of disease specificity. MetaPsoAI was developed in the MGB Biobank due to its comprehensive EHR data, enabling granular medication ascertainment and robust disease phenotyping, including validated algorithms for psoriasis identification, and subsequently validated in the larger UK Biobank to ensure external generalizability.

Several limitations merit consideration. This was a retrospective observational study, and although results were validated across large biobanks, prospective evaluation in multi-ethnic and treatment-diverse populations will be required to confirm generalizability and clinical utility. Residual confounding by unmeasured inflammatory or treatment variables cannot be excluded, and the extent of disease severity was not accounted for. The modest number of cardiovascular events in the psoriasis subset may limit model stability, though cross-validation and external replication mitigate this concern. To minimize confounding from direct pharmacologic effects on lipid metabolites, individuals receiving statin therapy at the time of blood sampling were excluded from development of MetaPsoAI. Although external replication is a necessary first step, formal evaluation of incremental performance, calibration, and utility versus standard clinical risk models will be required before clinical implementation. Metabolomic profiling captures systemic metabolic state at a single time point; longitudinal studies assessing dynamic metabolomic shifts with disease activity or therapy will be crucial to establish causality. Our study focused on psoriasis as a model of inflammation-driven cardiovascular risk; looking ahead, future studies evaluating the performance of this approach in broader immune-mediated populations will be important for improved cardiovascular risk stratification. Finally, the derivation cohort was drawn from a hospital-based biobank with a mean age in the early 60 s. Biobank participation may preferentially capture individuals engaged in healthcare systems and those who survive long enough to undergo biospecimen collection, introducing potential selection and survivor bias.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that metabolomic signatures carry prognostic value for cardiovascular risk in psoriasis, with HDL subfractions and amino acid pathways emerging as key contributors to disease-associated metabolic remodelling. The development of MetaPsoAI serves as a proof-of-concept, with an integrative metabolomic score intended to capture multi-metabolite patterns of CV risk in psoriasis. By integrating these multidimensional signals into an AI-driven score, MetaPsoAI could serve as a new tool for identifying psoriasis patients at the highest cardiovascular risk. These findings highlight the potential of combining metabolomics with artificial intelligence to advance precision prevention in immune-mediated diseases and set the stage for future studies integrating metabolic, inflammatory, and genomic data to refine personalized cardiovascular risk prediction.
