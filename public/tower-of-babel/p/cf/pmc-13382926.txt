Cerebral toxoplasmosis: Parasitology, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention - a narrative review
Authors: Verner N Orish, Susuana A Anadjoe, Josee C Orish, David Adzah, Kenneth Ablordey, Donatus W Adongo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral toxoplasmosis is a major opportunistic infection caused by Toxoplasma gondii, particularly in individuals with advanced HIV/AIDS and other forms of immunosuppression. This narrative review synthesizes current knowledge on the parasitology, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of cerebral toxoplasmosis, with emphasis on clinical relevance and public health implications. We outline the parasite’s life cycle and mechanisms of central nervous system invasion, distinguishing latent CNS infection in immunocompetent hosts from reactivated disease in immunocompromised patients. Diagnostic challenges are highlighted, including nonspecific clinical features, overlapping radiological findings, and variable sensitivity of serological and molecular assays. Therapeutic strategies are reviewed, focusing on standard regimens that suppress tachyzoite replication but fail to eradicate tissue cysts, necessitating prolonged maintenance therapy until immune recovery. Preventive measures such as food hygiene, avoidance of cat exposure, prophylaxis in HIV patients, and antiretroviral therapy are discussed as key interventions to reduce disease burden. Despite advances, significant gaps remain in diagnostics, definitive treatment, and preventive approaches. This review underscores the need for integrated clinical, molecular, and epidemiological research to improve outcomes and reduce the global impact of cerebral toxoplasmosis.Keywords: Cerebral toxoplasmosis, Toxoplasma gondii, HIV/AIDS, Diagnosis, Treatment, Prevention

Introduction

Toxoplasmosis is a ubiquitous parasitic infection caused by the apicomplexan protozoan Toxoplasma gondii (Khairullah et al., 2024). It is a zoonotic disease sustained by felines, which serve as the definitive hosts, while humans and other warm-blooded animals act as intermediate hosts (Khairullah et al., 2024). Globally, toxoplasmosis affects nearly one-third of the population, with prevalence ranging from approximately 11–15% in the USA, 20–50% across Europe, over 30% in the Middle East, and 30–60% in parts of Africa (Krings et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2023). Most infections remain asymptomatic in immunocompetent individuals, where the parasite persists in latent tissue cysts (Hökelek, 2019; Sowmya and Mangat, 2024). However, in immunosuppressed people, toxoplasmosis manifests with severe complications, notably involving the eyes (ocular toxoplasmosis), the developing fetus (congenital toxoplasmosis), and the central nervous system (cerebral toxoplasmosis) (Sowmya and Mangat, 2024). The latter represents a critical clinical entity, characterized by parasite invasion of neural tissue, cyst persistence, and reactivation leading to life-threatening encephalitis (Wohlfert et al., 2017; De Keersmaecker et al., 2024).

Cerebral toxoplasmosis is made possible by the migratory nature of tachyzoites and their remarkable ability to exploit host immune cells to gain access to the brain (Wohlfert et al., 2017; De Keersmaecker et al., 2024). After initial dissemination from the site of infection, tachyzoites circulate in the bloodstream and can cross the blood-brain barrier through several mechanisms. One of the most significant is the so-called “Trojan horse” effect, in which tachyzoites infect leukocytes such as monocytes and dendritic cells (Wohlfert et al., 2017; De Keersmaecker et al., 2024). These parasitized immune cells then traffic across endothelial barriers, effectively “smuggling” the parasite into the central nervous system (CNS). Once inside, tachyzoites invade neural tissue and differentiate into bradyzoites, forming intracellular cysts that persist in neurons and glial cells. The dual strategy, direct migration and immune cell hitchhiking, underpins the parasite’s neurotropism and explains its capacity to establish latent infection in the brain, with devastating consequences when reactivation occurs in immunocompromised hosts (Wohlfert et al., 2017; De Keersmaecker et al., 2024).

The knowledge of cerebral toxoplasmosis is punctuated by several unclear areas and ongoing controversies. These include the precise routes of parasite entry into the brain beyond the “Trojan horse” mechanism and direct tachyzoite motility (Schlüter and Barragan, 2019). These uncertainties underscore the need for a focused review of the parasitology of cerebral toxoplasmosis, aimed at clarifying current knowledge, highlighting gaps, and guiding future research toward improved diagnostic, therapeutic, and preventive strategies. To ensure transparency and minimize bias, the following section outlines the literature search strategy and methodological framework applied in this narrative review.

Literature search strategy

This review was conducted as a narrative synthesis with explicit methodological steps to enhance transparency. A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases, covering publications from January 2000 to March 2025. The following keywords and Boolean combinations were applied: “Toxoplasma gondii” AND “cerebral toxoplasmosis”, “neuropsychiatric manifestations”, “HIV/AIDS”, “immunocompromised host”, “CNS infection”, “diagnostic imaging”, and “therapeutic regimen”.

Inclusion criteria comprised peer-reviewed articles, clinical studies, molecular and immunological reports, epidemiological investigations, therapeutic trials, and diagnostic studies relevant to cerebral toxoplasmosis. Exclusion criteria comprised non-English publications, conference abstracts lacking complete primary data, case reports lacking CNS pathology, and studies not directly addressing cerebral toxoplasmosis or its neuropsychiatric associations.

Reference lists of key articles were also screened to identify additional relevant studies. The final selection was based on relevance to the objectives of this review, with deliberate integration of molecular findings, clinical case series, epidemiological data, therapeutic regimens, and diagnostic differentials. This approach ensured a comprehensive synthesis that spans basic science, clinical practice, and public health perspectives.

Latent CNS versus cerebral toxoplasmosis

The parasite’s ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and gain access to neural tissue occurs even in immunocompetent individuals (Wohlfert et al., 2017). In this context, the host mounts a robust cell-mediated immune response, primarily driven by CD8+ T lymphocytes, natural killer cells, interferon-γ, and interleukin-12, which forces tachyzoites to convert into slow-growing bradyzoites (Fig. 1) (Elsheikha et al., 2020). These bradyzoites establish latent tissue cysts within neurons and glial cells (Fig. 1), thereby limiting further injury or inflammation to the brain. This state is referred to as latent CNS toxoplasmosis, or subclinical CNS infection, and does not constitute clinical disease (Table 1) (Rostami et al., 2020; Latifi and Flegr, 2025). By contrast, cerebral toxoplasmosis arises only when immune surveillance is lost, as in HIV/AIDS, malignancy, or immunosuppressive therapy (Fig. 1), Under such conditions, bradyzoites reactivate into tachyzoites, leading to uncontrolled replication, necrosis, and symptomatic encephalitis (Table 1) (Elsheikha et al., 2020). This distinction between latent infection in immunocompetent hosts and reactivated disease in immunocompromised hosts is central to understanding the parasitology of T. gondii in the brain.

Distinction between Latent CNS toxoplasmosis and cerebral toxoplasmosis.

Latent CNS infection versus cerebral toxoplasmosis remains a central area of debate, particularly given emerging links between latent infection and neuropsychiatric disorders and subtle neurocognitive impairment in domains such as memory, processing speed, and executive function (Andreou et al., 2025; Cavalari et al., 2025). Further debates center on whether T. gondii is truly neurotropic or whether latent CNS infection is the rule rather than the exception (Schlüter and Barragan, 2019; Anthony, 2025). To illustrate these distinctions, the key differences between latent CNS toxoplasmosis and overt cerebral toxoplasmosis are summarized in Table 1.

Evidence linking latent CNS infection to neuropsychiatric impairment remains inconsistent. While epidemiological studies report associations with cognitive decline and mood disorders (Andreou et al., 2025; Cavalari et al., 2025), neuropathological investigations often fail to demonstrate overt tissue damage in immunocompetent hosts (Latifi and Flegr, 2025). These discrepancies may reflect differences in study design and geographical strain variation (Schlüter and Barragan, 2019). A balanced interpretation is that latent infection is generally subclinical but may exert subtle immunomodulatory effects in certain populations, warranting longitudinal studies to resolve this controversy. Reported associations between latent toxoplasmosis and neuropsychiatric disorders are largely based on cross-sectional epidemiological studies (Andreou et al., 2025; Latifi and Flegr, 2025), as highlighted in recent reviews (Cavalari et al., 2025), limiting causal inference and underscoring the need for longitudinal designs.

Taxonomy of Toxoplasma gondii

With the traditional classification, T. gondii is a protozoan parasite within the domain Eukaryota. It belongs to the kingdom Protista, phylum Protozoa, and subphylum Apicomplexa, a diverse group of obligate intracellular parasites characterized by the presence of an apical complex used for host cell invasion (Burki et al., 2007). Within Apicomplexa, T. gondii is placed in class Conoidasida, order Eucoccidiorida, and family Sarcocystidae. The genus Toxoplasma currently contains a single recognized species, T. gondii (Vilela and Ferreira, 2024). In contrast, recent phylogenomic revisions have reorganized eukaryotic taxonomy into supergroups. According to the International Society of Protozoology T. gondii is now placed within the TSAR supergroup (Telonemia, Stramenopiles, Alveolata, Rhizaria) (Adl et al., 2019; Burki et al., 2007, 2020). Within TSAR, the first rank is Alveolata and the second rank Apicomplexa, alongside related genera such as Eimeria, Isospora, and Cryptosporidium. This dual perspective highlights both the historical taxonomy and the modern supergroup classification, ensuring alignment with current consensus while preserving context from earlier frameworks.

Historically, T. gondii strains were classified into three major clonal lineages (Types I, II, and III) (Tharaka et al., 2025). However, this framework is now considered obsolete. Recent population genetic studies have demonstrated that the parasite population is organized into 15 haplogroups, collectively forming six principal clades, with at least 189 distinct genotypes identified to date (Su et al., 2012). In the Northern hemisphere, clonal strains predominate, characterized by a limited number of highly clonal and widespread lineages. In contrast, in South America, there is a diverse array of less common genotypes, which demonstrate a higher degree of recombination and are associated with more severe or atypical clinical manifestations. This updated classification underscores the global genetic diversity of T. gondii and its implications for cerebral toxoplasmosis.

The molecular classification of T. gondii strains has revealed that cerebral toxoplasmosis is not uniformly caused by a single lineage but reflects the interplay of strain virulence, host immunity, and geographical distribution (Hosseini et al., 2018). While Type II strains dominate clinical cases in Europe and North America (Calero-Bernal et al., 2022; Fadel et al., 2024), atypical and recombinant lineages in South America and Africa highlight the parasite’s genetic diversity and its potential to cause severe CNS disease (Hosseini et al., 2018; Galal et al., 2025a, 2025b). This diversity underscores the importance of integrating strain taxonomy into our understanding of cerebral toxoplasmosis, as molecular epidemiology continues to shape diagnostic, therapeutic, and preventive strategies. Current strain classifications rely heavily on molecular typing from limited geographical cohorts, which restricts generalizability and calls for broader, multicenter sampling strategies (Hosseini et al., 2018; Galal et al., 2025a, 2025b). Future research must integrate molecular epidemiology with clinical outcomes across diverse regions to ensure models of neuropathogenesis reflect global strain diversity.

History of cerebral toxoplasmosis

Toxoplasmosis was first recognized as a human disease in 1939, when T. gondii was identified in the neural tissue of a congenitally infected newborn who presented with blindness, deafness, and other severe congenital anomalies and died shortly after birth (Dubey et al., 2021). This landmark case established the parasite’s role in congenital infections, highlighting that cerebral toxoplasmosis was not the first observed clinical manifestation of the disease.

The parasite itself had been discovered earlier, in 1908, in a North African rodent (Ctenodactylus gundi), though its medical importance remained unclear until human cases were documented (Al-hajj and Kekillioğlu, 2023). By the 1950s, congenital toxoplasmosis was firmly associated with neurological manifestations such as hydrocephalus, cerebral calcifications, and chorioretinitis (Bollani et al., 2022).

Cerebral toxoplasmosis as a distinct clinical entity gained prominence during the HIV/AIDS epidemic of the 1980s and 1990s, when it emerged as one of the most common opportunistic central nervous system infections in patients with advanced immunodeficiency (Vidal, 2019). It was characterized by focal neurological deficits, seizures, and encephalopathy, often confirmed by neuroimaging and histopathology (Vidal, 2019). In recent decades, advances in molecular typing have expanded knowledge of T. gondii strain diversity and its influence on disease severity. Type II strains are most consistently implicated in cerebral toxoplasmosis in Europe and North America, while atypical and recombinant strains, particularly in South America and Africa, have been linked to more severe or unusual neurological manifestations (Calero-Bernal et al., 2022; Fadel et al., 2024).

Burden of cerebral toxoplasmosis

Globally, T. gondii infection affects approximately 30% of the world’s population (Sengupta et al., 2025). Among HIV-infected individuals, pooled seroprevalence is 35–45%, and cerebral toxoplasmosis accounts for up to one-third of CNS opportunistic infections in endemic regions (Safarpour et al., 2020). Mortality remains high if untreated, but antiretroviral therapy (ART) and prophylaxis significantly reduce risk (Fatemeh et al., 2025; Moutombi Ditombi et al., 2026).

Since cerebral toxoplasmosis became prominent as the most common opportunistic infection during the HIV/AIDS epidemic of the 1980s-1990s incidence has declined from the early 2000s, especially in high-income countries due to ART scale-up (Safarpour et al., 2020; Moutombi Ditombi et al., 2026). However, the disease continues to pose a major burden in resource-limited regions, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America. The burden is greatest in sub-Saharan Africa, where HIV prevalence is highest and cerebral toxoplasmosis accounts for up to 30–40% of CNS opportunistic infections in AIDS patients (Feleke et al., 2019). In Latin America, especially Brazil and Colombia, exposure to atypical and recombinant T. gondii strains contributes to severe neurological disease, with prevalence among HIV/AIDS patients reported at 20–30% (Xiao and Yolken, 2015; Gatti et al., 2025). In Europe and North America, prevalence is lower, typically 10–15% of untreated HIV/AIDS patients, reflecting widespread ART and prophylaxis (Xiao and Yolken, 2015). Global prevalence estimates often derive from heterogeneous study designs and variable diagnostic criteria, making direct comparisons across regions problematic and weakening the precision of burden assessments (Feleke et al., 2019; Safarpour et al., 2020).

Transmission, life cycle and migration of Toxoplasma gondii to the brain

Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular parasite with felids as definitive hosts and a wide range of warm-blooded animals, including humans, as intermediate hosts (Fig. 2) (Almashhadany et al., 2024). In the gastrointestinal tract of the cat, sexual division occurs, leading to gametogony and sporogony, which culminates in the shedding of environmentally resistant oocysts (Robertson, 2016; Mishra and Tiwari, 2024). Transmission occurs primarily through ingestion of oocysts from contaminated food or water, consumption of tissue cysts in undercooked meat, and congenital infection (Robert-Gangneux and Dardé, 2012). Following ingestion, tachyzoites disseminate via the bloodstream and establish latent tissue cysts, particularly in neural and muscular tissues (Montoya and Liesenfeld, 2004).

Cerebral migration occurs through two main mechanisms: direct motility across endothelial barriers and leukocyte-mediated “Trojan horse” trafficking (Fig. 2) (Wohlfert et al., 2017; Ross et al., 2022; De Keersmaecker et al., 2024). Pathogenesis and clinical features of cerebral toxoplasmosis

The pathogenesis of cerebral toxoplasmosis is driven by the parasite’s ability to breach the blood-brain barrier (BBB) (Wohlfert et al., 2017; Ross et al., 2022; De Keersmaecker et al., 2024). Beyond the well-established “Trojan horse” mechanism, in which parasitized leukocytes traffic T. gondii into neural tissue, free tachyzoites can also traverse the barrier directly (Wohlfert et al., 2017; De Keersmaecker et al., 2024). These complementary strategies highlight the parasite’s versatility in CNS invasion and explain its capacity to establish persistent infection within neurons and glial cells. More recent findings have expanded this model by highlighting molecular adhesion mechanisms, strain variability, and the immunomodulatory capacity of T. gondii. Adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1/CD18 facilitate the sequestration of parasitized phagocytes in cortical capillaries, thereby enhancing localized neuronal colonization (Rodriguez et al., 2025). Genotyping studies have revealed that different T. gondii lineages vary in their efficiency of blood-brain barrier (BBB) traversal, suggesting strain-specific neuropathogenic potential (Fernández-Escobar et al., 2022). Further evidence shows that tachyzoites actively modulate host immune signaling, including suppression of IFN-γ-mediated pathways, enabling them to evade clearance and sustain replication within neural tissue (Pan et al., 2022). While both the “Trojan horse” mechanism and direct tachyzoite motility are well-documented, the relative contribution of each pathway remains debated. Some studies emphasize leukocyte-mediated trafficking as the dominant route (Wohlfert et al., 2017; De Keersmaecker et al., 2024), whereas others highlight strain-specific differences in endothelial penetration (Fernández-Escobar et al., 2022; Ross et al., 2022). Reconciling these findings suggests that T. gondii likely employs multiple complementary strategies, with host immune status and parasite genotype determining which pathway predominates. This underscores the need for comparative in vivo studies to clarify the hierarchy of invasion mechanisms. Again, most evidence for “Trojan horse” trafficking derives from murine models, which may not fully capture human CNS invasion, highlighting the need for validation in well-powered human studies (Wohlfert et al., 2017).

Once inside the brain parenchyma, tachyzoites replicate within neurons, astrocytes, and microglia, leading to host cell lysis, necrosis, and inflammatory lesions (Rostami et al., 2020; Latifi and Flegr, 2025). In immunocompetent hosts, immune pressure forces conversion into bradyzoites, resulting in the formation of latent tissue cysts. In immunocompromised individuals, however, unchecked tachyzoite replication causes widespread tissue destruction, cerebral edema, and the clinical syndrome cerebral toxoplasmosis or toxoplasmic encephalitis (Rostami et al., 2020; Latifi and Flegr, 2025). Clinically, this condition manifests with seizures, focal neurological deficits, and encephalopathy, but additional features are frequently observed. These include headache, fever, confusion, altered mental status, hemiparesis, ataxia, cranial nerve palsies, visual disturbances, and speech difficulties. In severe cases, patients may present with coma, psychiatric symptoms, or rapid neurological deterioration (Wohlfert et al., 2017). Neuroimaging typically reveals multiple ring-enhancing lesions with surrounding edema, most often in the basal ganglia and corticomedullary junction, correlating with the destructive pathology seen histologically (De Keersmaecker et al., 2024).

Although latent CNS toxoplasmosis has traditionally been regarded as a benign, quiescent stage distinct from cerebral toxoplasmosis, recent evidence has challenged this view (Bruschi and Fabiani, 2025). Several studies suggest that latent infection may not be entirely silent, but instead associated with subtle neuropathological changes, including low-grade neuroinflammation and dysregulated host immune signaling cascades, and potential links to neuropsychiatric disturbances (Andreou et al., 2025; Cavalari et al., 2025). Molecular findings indicate that bradyzoite cysts can intermittently release antigens and influence cytokine pathways, while epidemiological data point to associations with cognitive impairment and mood disorders (Gulshan et al., 2024; Bruschi and Fabiani, 2025). A recent forensic study further strengthens this link: T. gondii brain infection was identified in 30% of decedents, with a notable tropism and increased parasite load in the hippocampus. Elevated CD45RO expression was specifically correlated with hippocampal infection, indicating a localized neuroinflammatory response that may underlie neuropsychiatric associations (Nessim-Salazar et al., 2026). These observations have raised debate over whether latent toxoplasmosis should be considered merely a reservoir for reactivation in immunocompromised hosts, or whether it represents an early, subclinical phase of cerebral involvement (Dalimi and Abdoli, 2025). While consensus still holds that overt cerebral toxoplasmosis arises primarily from cyst reactivation under immunosuppression, the possibility of latent infection contributing to early neuropathogenesis remains an area of active investigation and controversy.

Diagnosis of cerebral toxoplasmosis

In resource-limited settings, clinical manifestations often serve as the primary tool for raising suspicion for cerebral toxoplasmosis (Ortiz and Norris, 2024). Non-specific systemic symptoms such as headache, fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy, and myalgia are common, while neurological features, including confusion, poor coordination, seizures, and focal deficits, are particularly suggestive in patients with advanced HIV/AIDS (Dunphy et al., 2021; Ortiz and Norris, 2024). However, reliance on clinical features alone is inherently flawed, as these findings overlap with a wide range of central nervous system (CNS) pathologies.

Laboratory investigations

Serological testing remains a cornerstone in establishing exposure to T. gondii. The presence of IgG antibodies indicates prior infection, which is common in patients with cerebral toxoplasmosis and highly prevalent in endemic regions, while IgM antibodies suggest acute infection but are less useful in cases of reactivation (Sołowińska and Holec-Gąsior, 2025). Molecular assays, particularly polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for T. gondii DNA in cerebrospinal fluid, provide more direct evidence of active infection, though sensitivity may vary (Bondarenko et al., 2020). However, reported PCR sensitivities vary widely, reflecting methodological differences and small sample sizes in many studies, which reduces confidence in their reproducibility across clinical settings (Bondarenko et al., 2020; Fadel et al., 2024)

Neuroimaging

Contrast-enhanced CT and MRI are indispensable in diagnosis. The hallmark finding is multiple ring-enhancing lesions typically located in the basal ganglia or corticomedullary junction, often accompanied by surrounding edema and mass effect. MRI offers superior sensitivity compared to CT (Bhiraju et al., 2025). These imaging features, while highly suggestive, are not pathognomonic and must be interpreted within the clinical context (Bhiraju et al., 2025).

Differential diagnosis

Several conditions can mimic cerebral toxoplasmosis both radiologically and clinically (Alves et al., 2025). Primary CNS lymphoma often presents with solitary or few lesions, frequently located periventricularly, and may enhance homogeneously rather than in a ring-like fashion (Marcus et al., 2021). Tuberculomas, which are common in endemic regions, produce ring-enhancing lesions that may calcify; systemic evidence of tuberculosis can assist in differentiation (Priyadarshi et al., 2021). Fungal abscesses, such as those caused by cryptococcosis or aspergillosis, may generate similar lesions, particularly in immunocompromised hosts (Yang et al., 2022). Pyogenic brain abscesses also present with ring-enhancing lesions, but they are typically associated with fever, leukocytosis, and an identifiable source of infection, and the lesions tend to be fewer and larger (Rosso et al., 2021). Others include neurocysticercosis, which produces multiple cystic or calcified lesions with the characteristic “hole-with-dot” sign in endemic regions (Lee et al., 2026); progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), which manifests as non-enhancing lesions in advanced HIV/AIDS (Villanueva et al., 2022); metastatic brain lesions, often multiple and located at the gray-white junction in patients with systemic malignancy (Bhiraju et al., 2025); neurosarcoidosis, which can produce enhancing parenchymal or meningeal lesions in the context of systemic sarcoidosis (Westendorp and Brouwer, 2025); and viral encephalitis, particularly HSV or CMV, which may cause diffuse or focal signal changes rather than classic ring lesions (Jehangir et al., 2014) (Table 2). Finally, tertiary syphilis with neurosyphilis can present with progressive dementia, psychiatric symptoms, and gummatous lesions, making it an important consideration in the differential diagnosis of CNS opportunistic infections (Table 2) (Elsheikha et al., 2020; Vidal, 2019).

Integrated approach

A reliable diagnosis of cerebral toxoplasmosis requires integration of clinical suspicion, serological evidence of exposure, molecular confirmation where available, and neuroimaging findings. In resource-constrained environments, clinicians often rely on the combination of HIV/AIDS status, IgG seropositivity, and characteristic imaging features to make a presumptive diagnosis, while maintaining vigilance for alternative causes of ring-enhancing lesions (Milburn et al., 2024). Taken together, a combination of clinical suspicion, serology, molecular assays, and imaging provides the most reliable framework for diagnosing cerebral toxoplasmosis, even in settings where resources are constrained.

Treatment of cerebral toxoplasmosis

Treatment of cerebral toxoplasmosis is indicated in patients who present with compatible neurological symptoms such as headache, seizures, focal deficits, or altered mental status, together with radiographic evidence of multiple ring-enhancing brain lesions (Dian et al., 2022). The indication for this empiric therapy is strongest in immunocompromised individuals, with compatible symptoms and signs, particularly those with advanced HIV/AIDS and CD4 counts below 100 cells/μl and among those who are T. gondii IgG-positive. IgG positivity reflects prior exposure and latent infection, which in the setting of immunosuppression can reactivate and cause cerebral disease (Sreenivas et al., 2011). Thus, in HIV/AIDS patients with typical imaging findings, IgG positivity strongly supports empiric therapy. In contrast, IgM positivity indicates acute or recent infection, which is uncommon in cerebral toxoplasmosis but highly relevant in pregnancy, where acute maternal infection carries a risk of congenital transmission (Sołowińska and Holec-Gąsior, 2025). Immunocompetent individuals rarely require therapy unless severe systemic, ocular, or central nervous system involvement occurs.

The standard regimen is the combination of pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine, and folinic acid (leucovorin) (Table 3). Pyrimethamine is the most effective anti-toxoplasma drug; sulfadiazine provides synergistic activity, and leucovorin prevents pyrimethamine-induced bone marrow suppression (Spach, 2026). Therapy is typically continued for at least four to six weeks, with longer courses required in severely immunocompromised patients. Alternatives include pyrimethamine combined with clindamycin and leucovorin in cases of sulfa allergy, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, which has demonstrated comparable efficacy and is widely used in resource-limited settings (Table 3) (Spach, 2026).

Therapeutic regimens for cerebral toxoplasmosis.

Importantly, maintenance therapy is essential in HIV/AIDS patients after the acute episode has been controlled (Spach, 2026). Current drugs do not eradicate tissue cysts; thus, relapse is common if therapy is stopped while the immune system remains suppressed. For this reason, patients are kept on secondary prophylaxis, usually lower-dose pyrimethamine plus sulfadiazine or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole with leucovorin, until immune reconstitution is achieved with effective antiretroviral therapy (Table 3) (Spach, 2026). Once the CD4 count rises above 200 cells/μl and remains stable for at least six months, the risk of relapse drops significantly, and maintenance therapy can be safely discontinued.

Recent treatment modalities have expanded the therapeutic options. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole has gained traction as both treatment and prophylaxis, offering cost-effective coverage. Atovaquone alone or in combination have been reported to have good result in refractory cases (Goudarzi et al., 2023), and clindamycin have been reported to successfully treat cerebral toxoplasmosis (Madi et al., 2012). Novel agents aimed at eradicating tissue cysts are under investigation, since current therapies act only on tachyzoites and fail to eliminate latent stages, leaving patients vulnerable to relapse. Among these, nanoparticle-based therapies have emerged as innovative approaches, offering improved drug delivery, reduced toxicity, and potential activity against tissue cysts compared with conventional regimens (Jafarpour Azami et al., 2021).

A major controversy in management concerns the balance between empiric therapy and early biopsy. In high-income settings, brain biopsy of lesions can help distinguish toxoplasmosis from primary central nervous system lymphoma, which has a very different treatment pathway (Zoubi et al., 2017). Advocates of early biopsy argue that it avoids delays in diagnosing lymphoma. However, in resource-limited settings, biopsy is often impractical due to a lack of neurosurgical expertise, advanced imaging, and pathology support. In these contexts, empiric therapy is the only realistic option, with diagnosis inferred from clinical and radiographic improvement within 10–14 days (Pantoja Ruiz et al., 2021). This pragmatic approach reflects the realities of healthcare infrastructure, though it carries the risk of missing alternative diagnoses.

Other challenges include uncertainty about the optimal duration of therapy. Some patients require longer courses than the conventional six weeks, and the significant toxicity of current regimens, which can cause bone marrow suppression, hypersensitivity, and hepatotoxicity (Dunay et al., 2018).

Control and prevention of cerebral toxoplasmosis

Active primary prevention

Primary prevention of toxoplasmosis aimed at reducing exposure to T. gondii in the general population can directly lower the incidence of cerebral toxoplasmosis (Wehbe et al., 2022). The most effective strategies are public health measures that prevent new infections, such as safe food handling practices, avoidance of undercooked meat, thorough washing of vegetables and fruits, and minimizing contact with cat feces or contaminated water (Smith et al., 2021; Wehbe et al., 2022; CDC, 2024). Thus, by reducing exposure in the general population, primary prevention not only lowers the rate of new infections but also decreases the burden of latent infection, which has the potential to be reactivated in immunosuppressed individuals in the population, thereby reducing the overall incidence of cerebral toxoplasmosis (Bruschi and Fabiani, 2025).

However, among immunocompromised individuals, particularly those with HIV/AIDS, primary prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is recommended for opportunistic infections including toxoplasmosis, as this prevents initial reactivation (Smith et al., 2021). Effective antiretroviral therapy remains the cornerstone of long-term prevention, since immune reconstitution dramatically reduces the risk of cerebral toxoplasmosis. Thus, while community-level interventions lower the overall burden of latent cases, clinical prophylaxis and ART directly protect those most vulnerable to reactivation, ensuring a comprehensive approach to preventing cerebral toxoplasmosis (Coleman et al., 2023).

Secondary prevention

Early empiric therapy initiated promptly in immunocompromised patients with compatible neurological symptoms and radiographic findings, as well as T. gondii IgG-positive result, is regarded as secondary prevention. The focus is on patients who already have cerebral toxoplasmosis (Dian et al., 2022; Spach, 2026). To prevent relapse, maintenance therapy is required because current drugs suppress the tachyzoite stage but do not eradicate latent tissue cysts. As a result, patients remain at risk of reactivation until immune recovery is achieved through effective antiretroviral therapy, defined as a sustained CD4 count above 200 cells/μl for at least six months.

Challenges in the control of cerebral toxoplasmosis

Diagnostic challenges

The true burden of cerebral toxoplasmosis, like overall toxoplasmosis, remains uncertain primarily due to diagnostic limitations (Khairullah et al., 2024; Valencia Uribe et al., 2025). Accurate diagnosis is a major obstacle, as clinical manifestations such as headache, seizures, or focal neurological deficits are non-specific and overlap with other opportunistic infections and central nervous system malignancies (Liu et al., 2015). Neuroimaging with CT or MRI often reveals ring-enhancing lesions, but these findings are not pathognomonic and may mimic conditions such as primary CNS lymphoma or tuberculomas (Bhiraju et al., 2025). Serological testing adds limited value, since IgG positivity reflects prior exposure rather than active disease. Moreover, IgM assays are often unreliable due to prolonged persistence or false positives, making it difficult to distinguish recent infection from past exposure (Sołowińska and Holec-Gąsior, 2025). As a result, serology alone cannot confirm active cerebral toxoplasmosis and must be interpreted cautiously alongside clinical and radiographic findings (Bondarenko et al., 2020). Definitive diagnosis requires brain biopsy, a procedure rarely feasible in resource-limited settings due to its invasiveness and technical demands (Shakir et al., 2024). Consequently, clinicians frequently rely on empiric therapy, a practice that carries the dual risks of overtreatment and delayed recognition of alternative diagnoses (Zibly et al., 2014). Moreso, advanced imaging or molecular assays are often unavailable in endemic regions (Hilabi et al., 2023). Taken together, these limitations highlight the need for multimodal diagnostic algorithms that combine imaging, molecular assays, and clinical context rather than reliance on a single modality.

Therapeutic limitations

Current anti-toxoplasma drugs suppress tachyzoites but do not eradicate tissue cysts, leaving patients vulnerable to relapse. Long-term maintenance therapy is therefore required until immune recovery is achieved, but adherence is frequently compromised by drug toxicity, pill burden, and limited availability of pyrimethamine or sulfadiazine in many regions (Dunay et al., 2018; Montazeri et al., 2018). Furthermore, resistance patterns and drug interactions with antiretroviral therapy complicate management (Apetroaei et al., 2024). Experimental therapies, including nanoparticle-based drug delivery, remain in early stages.

Preventive barriers

Public health measures such as safe food handling, avoidance of undercooked meat, thorough washing of produce, and minimizing contact with cat feces or contaminated water are effective but difficult to enforce consistently (Alghafari, 2025; Fernicola et al., 2025). In high-resource settings, CD4-guided prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is standard, whereas in sub-Saharan Africa universal prophylaxis is used due to limited laboratory monitoring (National AIDS Control Programme, 2022). Both approaches face challenges of adherence, drug supply interruptions, and patient education. No licensed human vaccine exists, and the only available live-attenuated vaccine (Toxovax®, MSD Animal Health (Intervet Ireland Ltd.), Dublin, Ireland) is restricted to veterinary use (Toxovax® Datasheet, 2024). These gaps, combined with constrained healthcare infrastructure, hinder comprehensive control strategies.

Future research perspectives

Despite progress in understanding T. gondii, important gaps remain in the study of cerebral toxoplasmosis. Future investigations should prioritize clinical validation of cerebral invasion pathways, as current evidence for direct motility and “Trojan horse” trafficking is largely derived from murine and in vitro models. Longitudinal cohort studies are needed to clarify the potential cognitive and neuropsychiatric consequences of latent CNS infection. Improved diagnostic tools, including standardized PCR protocols and advanced imaging modalities, would enhance early detection and differentiation from other CNS pathologies. Research should also focus on novel therapeutic strategies that balance efficacy with reduced toxicity, particularly in immunocompromised populations. Finally, integrated epidemiological studies are required to refine burden estimates and guide preventive interventions in high-risk groups.

Conclusions

Cerebral toxoplasmosis remains a formidable clinical and public health challenge, particularly in regions with high HIV prevalence and limited healthcare infrastructure. Diagnostic uncertainty, therapeutic limitations, and barriers to effective prevention contribute to ongoing morbidity and mortality. Current drugs suppress but do not cure, leaving patients vulnerable to relapse until immune reconstitution is achieved. Preventive measures, while effective in principle, are inconsistently applied and difficult to sustain in endemic settings. The absence of a human vaccine and the limited availability of advanced diagnostics further compound the problem. Addressing these challenges requires a multifaceted strategy: strengthening public health interventions, ensuring reliable access to ART and prophylaxis, improving diagnostic capacity, and investing in research on novel therapies capable of eradicating tissue cysts. Only through such integrated efforts can the burden of cerebral toxoplasmosis be reduced, transforming it from a persistent opportunistic threat into a preventable and controllable disease. The global burden of cerebral toxoplasmosis reflects both epidemiological and socioeconomic disparities. While incidence has declined in high-income countries due to ART scale-up, resource-limited regions continue to face high morbidity because of restricted access to prophylaxis and advanced diagnostics. Strain diversity in Latin America and Africa further complicates prevention strategies, as atypical genotypes may evade standard therapeutic regimens. Addressing these inequities requires integrated approaches that combine ART expansion with region-specific molecular surveillance.
