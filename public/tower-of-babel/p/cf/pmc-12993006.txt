Targeting the osteoporotic bone microenvironment: Mechanistic insight and therapeutic biomaterials for accelerating bone regeneration
Authors: Xinya Lu, Anbiao Zhang, Chengyun Zhang, Dong Guo, Zhong Alan Li, Denghui Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoporotic bone defects (OBD) present a major clinical challenge, largely due to a pathological bone microenvironment that severely compromises regenerative capacity. Moving beyond conventional classification schemes that often fail to capture this pathological complexity, our review establishes a conceptual framework that categorizes emerging biomaterials by mirroring the intrinsic functional components of the bone microenvironment itself—namely, ion-releasing, metabolite-based, extracellular matrix (ECM)-mimetic, and cytokine-loaded systems. The component-centric taxonomy not only systematizes a disparate field but also directly links material design to underlying pathological mechanisms. Accordingly, these materials are engineered not merely as structural replacements but as active modulators designed to reprogram the pathological niche by concurrently targeting key mechanisms such as cellular dysfunction and immune-metabolic dysregulation. Furthermore, we explore the emerging roles of bone organoids and artificial intelligence (AI) in refining preclinical models. By integrating a deep biological understanding of the niche with engineering innovation, our work aims to provide a cohesive framework and forward-looking perspective to guide the development of effective, microenvironment-targeted regenerative strategies.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic metabolic bone disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture [1], which often leads to bone defects related to the disease. These defects frequently present clinically as fragility fractures, such as vertebral compression fractures and hip fractures [[2], [3], [4]], which are difficult to heal due to insufficient bone regenerative capacity [5]. Globally, individuals over 50 face a significant risk of osteoporotic fractures, with the International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF) reporting incidence rates of one-third for women and one-fifth for men [6]. Among hip fracture patients, 10–20% die within one year after the fracture [7]. This considerable clinical burden translates into a staggering economic toll, with osteoporosis and related fracture costs projected to reach approximately 76.7 billion euros in Europe and 50 billion US dollars in the USA by 2050 [8]. Similarly, China is also projected to see a substantial economic burden, with the annual cost of osteoporosis-related fractures set to exceed 25 billion US dollars by 2050 [9].

The core pathophysiological process of osteoporosis involves a disturbance in bone remodeling, wherein bone resorption exceeds bone formation. This disturbance has distinct initiating factors in postmenopausal osteoporosis and senile osteoporosis. In postmenopausal women, the drastic decline in estrogen levels leads to a significant increase in osteoclasts (OCs) activity [[10], [11], [12]]. In contrast, elderly individuals experience age-related cellular senescence, reduced number and function of osteoblasts (OBs) that cannot adequately replace resorbed bone [13,14]. In conventional pharmacological treatment, bisphosphonates primarily attenuate bone loss by inhibiting bone resorption [15], but they are associated with upper gastrointestinal adverse effects and potential renal impairment [16,17]. Estrogen replacement therapy helps restore bone metabolic balance by supplementing estrogen, which not only inhibits excessive activation of OCs but also helps maintain OBs function [18]. However, it is limited by an increased risk of breast cancer, endometrial cancer, and cardiovascular diseases [19]. Both conventional treatment strategies have significant side effects and exhibit limited efficacy in promoting the repair of osteoporosis-related bone defects. Increasing evidence suggests that bone loss and compromised bone defect repair fundamentally arise from the homeostatic disturbance of the bone microenvironment, which is primarily characterized by a dysregulation between osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis, coupled with immune dysregulation.

The bone microenvironment is a complex and dynamic network that includes core cellular components such as bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), OBs, and OCs along with the ECM, a vascular network, sophisticated innervation, various bioactive substances, such as metabolites and cytokines, alongside local physicochemical factors, including pH and ion concentration [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Cellular components regulate bone formation and resorption through signal crosstalk. Osteogenesis is closely coupled with angiogenesis. The presence of a vascular network supplies cells with oxygen, nutrients, calcium, and phosphates for mineralization, thereby facilitating waste removal from the site [24]. Innervation directly regulates the activity and function of OBs and OCs through the release of neurotransmitters such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and neuropeptide Y (NPY) [25]. The ECM provides physical support and anchoring sites for signals. Bioactive substances and physicochemical factors maintain cellular functions via metabolic regulation and signal transduction. Disruption of this synergistic relationship disturbs the bone microenvironment, ultimately resulting in osteoporosis and impaired bone defect repair.

The pathological bone microenvironment in osteoporosis—characterized by cellular dysfunction, dysregulated signaling pathways, metabolic disturbances, and chronic inflammation—severely impairs bone regenerative capacity. Conventional mono-therapeutic strategies are often inadequate to restore such a dysregulated milieu. Accordingly, a new generation of engineered biomaterials has been developed that can actively intervene in the pathological niche and exert multi-target regulation to promote bone regeneration. While previous reviews have systematically summarized biomaterials for osteoporotic bone regeneration [26,27], few have described the diverse types of engineered biomaterials designed based on the pathological mechanisms of osteoporosis and the core components of the dysregulated bone microenvironment.

Distinct from conventional engineered materials that solely focus on the single target of “inhibiting osteoclastogenesis” or “promoting osteogenesis,” this review establishes a comprehensive framework structured around the pathological dysregulation of four core components in the osteoporotic bone microenvironment: ions, metabolites, cytokines, and ECM. The primary objective of this review is to categorize and evaluate emerging biomaterial strategies—specifically ion-releasing, metabolite-based, ECM-mimetic, and cytokine-loaded systems—that act as active modulators to supplement deficient components, correct aberrant pathways, and restore immune homeostasis (Scheme 1). We further aim to elucidate how these component-centric strategies synergistically promote vascularization and neurogenesis to advance neuro-vascular-bone coupled repair. The maintenance of bone microenvironment homeostasis

This chapter delves into the intricate regulatory mechanisms underlying bone remodeling, with a focus on the roles of BMSCs, OBs, OCs, and the ECM. Furthermore, this chapter explores the effects of key metabolites, cytokines, and ions on bone metabolism and immune regulation. Understanding these multi-level interactions is crucial for elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms of osteoporosis and its associated bone defects, as well as for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

Cellular regulation of bone homeostasis

BMSCs

BMSCs play a central role in maintaining bone homeostasis through their capacities for self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation. Their osteogenic differentiation is primarily regulated by two canonical signaling pathways, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and the BMP/SMAD pathway. Ligands such as Wnt3a and Wnt10 b bind to the receptor complex consisting of Frizzled receptors and low-density lipoprotein receptor-related proteins 5/6 (LRP5/6) on the cell membrane. This binding event inhibits the activity of glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β), which in turn leads to the cytoplasmic accumulation and subsequent nuclear translocation of β-catenin [[28], [29], [30]]. In the nucleus, β-catenin forms a complex with TCF/LEF transcription factors, which not only activates osteogenic key genes including runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and Osterix but also upregulates proliferation-related genes such as CCND1 and c-Myc, thereby promoting BMSCs proliferation [[31], [32], [33]]. This dual mechanism ensures a sufficient cell population prior to differentiation, laying the foundation for osteogenic differentiation. The BMP/SMAD signaling pathway also serves as a crucial regulator of BMSCs osteogenic differentiation. Ligands like BMP-2/4/7 bind to type II receptors (BMPRII) on the cell membrane, recruiting and phosphorylating type I receptors (BMPRI), leading to the activation of downstream SMAD1/5/8 proteins. Upon activation, SMAD1/5/8 partners with SMAD4 to form a complex that translocates into the nucleus. There, it directly upregulates the expression of the key transcription factors, Runx2 and Osterix [[34], [35], [36]] (Fig. 1). Additionally, other signaling pathways such as Notch, Hedgehog, PI3K/AKT, and HIF-1α also play regulatory roles in the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]].Fig. 1Schematic representation of BMP/SMAD and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways in orchestrating osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. OBs

Bone formation is a multi-stage process involving cellular differentiation, bone matrix synthesis, mineralization, and bone remodeling. OBs, the key cells responsible for bone formation, are derived from BMSCs. Runx2, a critical regulatory factor, activates genes such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP), osteocalcin (OCN), and type I collagen α1 (COL1A1) to initiate OBs maturation and bone matrix formation [43,44]. Osterix, acting downstream of Runx2, further promotes OBs maturation [45]. During the process of bone matrix mineralization, ALP secreted by OBs generates inorganic phosphate (PO43-) through hydrolyzing pyrophosphate (PPi) [[46], [47], [48]]. Notably, as an endogenous mineralization inhibitor [48], the decomposition of PPi not only eliminates the inhibition of mineralization but also serves as a source of PO43-. Meanwhile, calcium ions (Ca2+) are transported to specific regions via active transport. When the concentrations of Ca2+ and PO43- in these regions reach supersaturation, hydroxyapatite (HA) crystals begin to form, endowing bones with hardness and strength [49,50].

OBs also regulate intercellular communication through the secretion of cytokines. The secreted osteoprotegerin (OPG) competitively binds to the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL), inhibiting the differentiation of OCs precursor cells into mature OCs and thereby slowing bone resorption [[51], [52], [53]]. OBs also secrete macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) to indirectly regulate OCs activity [54,55]. Sustained release of growth factors such as transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), insulin-like growth factors (IGFs), and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) by OBs not only promotes the differentiation of BMSCs into the osteogenic lineage but also stimulates their own proliferation and matrix synthesis, forming a positive feedback loop [[56], [57], [58], [59]].

OBs actively participate in osteoimmune crosstalk. They support all stages of B lymphopoiesis via secretion of interleukin-7 (IL-7) and CXCL12 (SDF-1), whereas DLL4 secreted by OBs is indispensable for the development of mature T cells [60]. Conversely, factors such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), secreted by activated T cells, macrophages, and B cells, modulate OBs differentiation and function in a concentration- and context-dependent manner [61]. These bidirectional interactions constitute a sophisticated regulatory network that collectively maintains homeostasis between the skeletal and immune systems.

This multi-dimensional regulatory mechanism makes OBs a central hub in the bone microenvironment for coordinating cellular behaviors, maintaining bone homeostasis, and responding to internal and external stimuli. Dysfunction of OBs directly leads to various skeletal diseases such as osteoporosis and bone developmental abnormalities.

OCs

OCs originate from monocyte-macrophage precursors in the hematopoietic lineage, and their differentiation is strictly regulated by the RANKL/RANK/OPG signaling pathway. M-CSF promotes the proliferation of OCs precursors and induces the expression of RANK receptors by activating its receptor c-Fms (CSF-1R) [62]. RANKL is primarily secreted by OBs and osteocytes. It binds to the RANK receptor on the surface of OCs precursors, recruiting TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) to activate multiple critical signaling pathways, including NF-κB, MAPK (ERK, JNK, and p38), which further upregulate the expression of the key transcription factor nuclear factor of activated T cells cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1) and c-Fos [63,64]. As a major transcriptional regulator, NFATc1, in collaboration with transcription factors such as AP-1 (Activator Protein 1, primarily composed of members of the Jun and Fos protein families) and MITF, upregulates the expression of bone resorption-related genes such as cathepsin K (CTSK), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP), and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), while promoting the fusion of precursors to form multinucleated mature OCs [[65], [66], [67]]. Inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-6, interleukin-17 (IL-17), and TNF-α can promote the binding of RANKL to RANK, thereby indirectly facilitating OCs generation [68]. During bone resorption, OCs tightly attach to the bone surface through their unique podosomal structures, creating a relatively enclosed acidic microenvironment. Intracellular proton pumps continuously transport hydrogen ions (H+) into this microenvironment, significantly lowering the local pH (pH ≈ 4.5) to dissolve calcium and phosphorus minerals in HA [70,71]. Meanwhile, OCs secrete various proteases such as CTSK and MMP-9 to efficiently degrade organic components in the bone matrix.

ECM

ECM plays a crucial role in the bone microenvironment, and its functions are mainly reflected in providing physical support, regulating cellular behavior, and maintaining the stability of the microenvironment. The ECM forms a fibrous network through cross-linking of collagen as the scaffold, filled with glycosaminoglycans and fibrin to construct a stable three-dimensional structure, which offers attachment sites and mechanical support for cells and maintains bone tissue integrity [72,73]. The elastic modulus of the matrix significantly influences osteogenic differentiation. Matrices with a stiffness higher than 5 kPa can activate the nuclear translocation of YAP/TAZ transcription factors [74], which thereby drives the expression of Runx2 and promotes OBs maturation and mineralized nodule formation [75]. In contrast, soft matrices with a stiffness lower than 5 kPa inhibit YAP nuclear translocation, resulting in its predominant localization in the cytoplasm [74]. In addition, changes in ECM stiffness can regulate the inflammatory response of macrophages through the YAP/TAZ signaling axis, thereby affecting bone metabolic balance [76]. Such functional integration provides design principles for developing biomaterials that combine structural support with biochemical signaling [77].

In the maintenance of bone microenvironment homeostasis, the ECM achieves precise regulation of bone metabolism through multiple mechanisms. On one hand, fibronectin in the ECM can bind to the α5β1 integrin on the surface of OBs via its core cell adhesion domain, the arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) sequence. This binding significantly enhances the adhesion efficiency, metabolic activity, and ALP expression of OBs [78]. The αvβ3 integrin on the surface of OCs exhibits high affinity for the RGD sequence. This binding enables OCs to adhere to the bone matrix. Additionally, αvβ3 integrin is involved in the assembly and reorganization of OCs podosomes, thereby forming a “sealing zone” surrounding the resorption area, which serves as the functional basis for OCs to achieve bone resorption [79,80]. On the other hand, the ECM acts as a dynamic reservoir for cytokines. It can immobilize BMPs to regulate their spatial localization and release rate, thereby enabling precise regulation of processes such as OBs differentiation and bone regeneration [81]. Meanwhile, the ECM also plays an important role in maintaining the balance of ions and minerals in bone tissue. HA crystals, the main component of bone minerals, are closely related to the ECM in terms of their formation and deposition [82]. Acidic glycoproteins in the ECM, such as OPN and bone sialoprotein (BSP), can bind to Ca2+ and provide anchoring sites for the nucleation of HA, thereby promoting bone mineralization [83].

Bioactive soluble substances

Metabolites

As a metabolically active tissue, the bone microenvironment maintains its homeostasis through glucose, amino acid, and lipid metabolism, processes in which numerous metabolites play indispensable roles. The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, a central process in glucose metabolism, has been increasingly recognized in recent years for the close association of its key intermediates, including citrate and α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), with the regulation of bone metabolism [[84], [85], [86], [87], [88]]. Notably, more than 90% of the body's citrate is sequestered within the skeletal system [89], where its concentration is 5–25 times higher than in other tissues [90]. This specific distribution underpins its multiple regulatory functions: citrate not only facilitates HA nucleation by binding Ca2+ [[91], [92], [93]], but also fuels OBs and generates acetyl-CoA for histone acetylation, thereby epigenetically regulating osteogenic gene expression [94]. In addition, citrate exhibits significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, which can effectively inhibit the release of inflammatory cytokines [95,96]. These unique properties of citrate provide the rationale for developing citrate-incorporated biomaterials that mimic bone's natural composition and enhance bone regeneration [[97], [98], [99], [100], [101]]. For instance, Ma et al. demonstrated the excellent biocompatibility of citrate [102], showing minimal adverse effects on the viability and function of osteogenic cells, and no unwanted immune activation [103], while Xu et al. summarized citrate-based biomaterials' roles in orthopedic applications [104]. α-KG, another important intermediate of the TCA cycle, upregulates the expression of osteogenic differentiation markers, such as ALP and OPN, in OBs lineages [105]. Furthermore, α-KG was shown to boost both the proliferation and the osteogenic potential of BMSCs in aged mice. It ameliorates cellular aging-related phenotypes, prolongs cellular lifespan, downregulates the expression of senescence-associated genes (e.g., p16, p21, p53), reduces intracellular ROS levels, and alleviates age-related bone loss in murine models [106]. Moreover, metabolites from glycolysis, such as pyruvate and lactate, regulate local pH through redox reactions, collaborating with TCA cycle metabolites to form a bone metabolic regulatory network.

In the bone microenvironment, various amino acid metabolites orchestrate bone metabolic balance through complex regulatory networks. Glutamine, a key amino acid in cellular metabolism, is catalyzed by glutaminase (GLS) to form glutamate, which subsequently enters the TCA cycle to generate α-KG [107,108]. This process not only enhances the TCA cycle activity but also supplies essential metabolic intermediates for OBs. Furthermore, glutamine modulates ROS homeostasis to suppress the release of inflammatory cytokines, thereby indirectly inhibiting OCs activation [109]. Notably, a recent study by Xu et al. revealed that the synergistic action of glutamine, citrate, and magnesium enhances energy metabolism and co-activates both AMPK and mTORC1 signaling, thereby effectively driving BMSCs toward osteogenic differentiation [110]. Given the specific coupling between osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis, this finding provides a crucial theoretical foundation for metabolic interventions aimed at restoring bone homeostasis in osteoporosis. Additionally, tryptophan metabolites regulate bone remodeling through the intestinal AhR-mediated Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [111]. Arginine metabolism-derived nitric oxide (NO) exerts dual regulatory effects on bone metabolism. Specifically, physiological levels of NO promote OBs proliferation, whereas excessive levels induce apoptosis [112].

Cytokines

Cytokines are core mediators of immune homeostasis and bone metabolic balance in the bone microenvironment. Notably, macrophages, as key effector cells, dominate this process through their polarization states and secretion of inflammatory cytokines. M1 macrophages release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and IL-6, which activate the NF-κB and RANKL/RANK pathways to promote osteoclastogenesis [113,114]. In contrast, the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-4 (IL-4), and TGF-β by M2 macrophages inhibits OCs activity and activates the BMP/SMAD pathway to promote osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs [115,116]. Furthermore, M2 macrophages promote angiogenesis through the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). These actions collectively support bone repair [117]. In addition to macrophages, T helper 17 (Th17) cells and regulatory T (Treg) cells are crucial participants in the immune homeostasis of the bone microenvironment. The pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-17 and TNF-α, secreted by Th17 cells, act to amplify the inflammatory response and accelerate bone resorption. In contrast, Treg cells maintain immune tolerance by releasing IL-10 and IL-4, while simultaneously promoting osteogenesis through activation of the BMP pathway [[118], [119], [120]]. B cells contribute to the maintenance of a normal bone microenvironment by inhibiting OCs differentiation through the secretion of IL-10 and OPG [120,121].

Ions

In the bone microenvironment, ions such as Ca2+, magnesium ion (Mg2+), zinc ion (Zn2+), copper ion (Cu2+), and manganese ion (Mn2+) play critical roles in maintaining bone homeostasis and immune balance through multiple mechanisms. Each ion has unique characteristics, and Ca2+ and Mg2+ are predominantly distributed in bone tissue. Over 99% of the body's calcium is stored in bones, mainly in the form of HA crystals, which constitute the inorganic matrix of bone [122]. In adults, the total body magnesium is approximately 24 g, with over half stored in bones [123,124]. Beyond serving as the primary mineral component of bone, Ca2+ also functions as a crucial intracellular signaling molecule. Through the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR), Ca2+ induces the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype to participate in the regulation of the osteoimmune microenvironment, while promoting OBs differentiation and inhibiting OCs activity [125,126]. Mg2+ incorporates into HA crystals by partially substituting for Ca2+, which suppresses excessive growth and maturation of HA [127], consistent with its role in regulating bone apatite structure and density [128]. Additionally, Mg2+ acts as an enzymatic cofactor involved in the synthesis of matrix proteins, thereby promoting bone formation [129]. Deficiency in Mg2+ not only increases bone fragility but also disrupts immune homeostasis and stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β [130].

Although Zn2+, Cu2+, and Mn2+ are relatively low in bone tissue, they also exert key regulatory effects. Zn2+ interferes with the NF-κB pathway by modulating CATB protein adsorption. This action promotes M2 macrophage polarization to alleviate inflammation and concurrently upregulates key osteogenic markers, including ALP and RUNX2, in OBs [131]. Cu2+ serves as an essential cofactor for lysyl oxidase (LOX), enhancing its enzymatic activity, which in turn promotes the cross-linking of collagen fibers and ultimately improves the mechanical strength of the bone matrix [[132], [133]]. Mn2+ serves as an essential cofactor for enzymes such as manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD) [134]. By facilitating the scavenging of ROS and reducing oxidative stress, Mn2+ reduces damage to OBs and inhibits osteoclastogenesis [135].

Key changes in the osteoporotic bone microenvironment

During the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, the bone microenvironment undergoes a profound and self-reinforcing disturbance. This disturbance manifests primarily at three interconnected tiers: cellular dysfunction (including compromised osteogenic capacity and metabolic disorders), dysregulated signaling pathways that disrupt the delicate balance between bone formation and resorption, and sustained immune dysregulation that fuels a chronic inflammatory state. These intertwined pathological layers collectively create a vicious cycle that drives disease progression.

Dysregulated signaling pathways

Dysregulation of signaling pathways serves as the key molecular basis for bone microenvironmental disturbance. The dynamic balance between bone formation and bone resorption relies on the precise regulation of core pathways, including Wnt/β-catenin, BMP/SMAD, and RANKL/RANK/OPG. Under pathological conditions, these pathways demonstrate aberrant activity. Osteogenesis-related pathways may exhibit reduced activity due to inhibition by inflammatory cytokines or increased degradation of key molecules [136], whereas osteoclast-related pathways may become hyperactivated as a result of hormonal deficiency or inflammatory stimulation [137]. Furthermore, the abnormal regulation of signal transduction by ions collectively impairs the role of signaling pathways in maintaining bone homeostasis, ultimately disrupting the balance between bone formation and resorption [138,139].

Core pathological alterations in the bone microenvironment

Cellular metabolic and functional disorders are the core link in the homeostatic disturbance of the bone microenvironment, and they are closely associated with abnormalities in cellular components, metabolites, and the ECM within the microenvironment.

Cellular dysfunction

BMSCs

BMSCs exhibit a decline in self-renewal capacity under aging or pathological conditions, which is accompanied by reduced telomerase activity and diminished proliferative potential [140]. Epigenetic abnormalities also emerge, characterized by decreased global DNA methylation and reduced repressive histone modifications (such as H3K9me3 and H4K27me3), which contribute to genomic instability and impaired chromatin structural stability, thereby exacerbating BMSCs’ senescence and functional impairment [141]. In terms of lineage commitment, osteogenesis-related signaling pathways in BMSCs, including Wnt/β-catenin and BMP/SMAD, are suppressed [142], while the expression of key adipogenic transcription factors such as PPARγ and C/EBPα is upregulated [142,143]. Additionally, the non-coding RNA miR-188 is significantly elevated in BMSCs, driving their adipogenic differentiation. This trend further promotes the expansion of bone marrow adipose tissue and compromises bone formation [144].

OBs

With increasing age, both the number and osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs decline, leading to a reduction in OBs production. Concurrently, the intracellular accumulation of ROS promotes OBs apoptosis, which further exacerbates the decrease in bone formation [145,146]. In addition, the function of OBs declines significantly. The expression of Type I collagen and OCN, which are closely associated with bone matrix synthesis, as well as ALP, which regulates mineralization, is notably downregulated. This leads to insufficient deposition and mineralization of the bone matrix [145,147].

OCs

In osteoporosis, the number and activity of OCs are abnormally elevated, leading to excessive bone resorption [148]. Morphologically, activated OCs are typically characterized by an increased number of nuclei, cytoskeletal rearrangement, and the formation of sealing zones [149]. These hyperactive OCs ultimately exacerbate bone loss. In terms of differentiation, key driving factors in the osteoporotic microenvironment include the accumulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, ROS, and a dysregulation in the RANKL/RANK/OPG signaling pathway [[150], [151], [152]]. Persistent inflammatory responses directly stimulate OCs differentiation and synergize with other cellular immune components, forming a positive feedback loop that further amplifies bone resorption [153]. Functionally, OCs secrete degrading enzymes such as CTSK and MMP-9, and acidify the microenvironment via proton pumps, accelerating the dissolution of bone matrix and minerals [154,155].

Metabolic dysregulation

Meanwhile, metabolite dysregulation further impairs cellular functions. In recent years, metabolomics techniques have advanced significantly in osteoporosis research. Through a comparative analysis of metabolite profiles between osteoporotic patients and healthy controls, the critical roles of metabolites in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis have been elucidated, specifically regarding cellular metabolic regulation, bone microenvironmental homeostasis, and inflammatory responses. These findings elucidate disease mechanisms and highlight novel therapeutic targets.

The pathogenesis of senile and postmenopausal osteoporosis is closely linked to disturbed TCA cycle intermediates within the bone microenvironment. During aging, the TCA cycle becomes functionally impaired. Chen et al. demonstrated markedly reduced citrate levels in the bone tissue and plasma of patients with senile osteoporosis [156]. Wakili et al. found that in the postmenopausal bone microenvironment, elevated ROS induce MMP-9 expression in BMSCs, promoting citrate synthase (CS) degradation and impairing mitochondrial citrate synthesis. The resulting citrate deficiency impairs bone remodeling via two pathways: attenuating BMSC osteogenic differentiation through reduced histone acetylation, and diminishing citrate deposition required for bone mineralization [23]. Studies by Wang et al. revealed that circulating levels of α-KG are significantly lower in aged mice/rats compared to young ones [106]. This reduction weakens the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs, suppresses collagen synthesis, and impairs antioxidant defenses, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress via elevated ROS [42].

In amino acid metabolism, the association between tryptophan metabolites and osteoporosis is notable. Recently, Chen et al. observed significantly reduced levels of tryptophan-derived metabolites such as indoleacetic acid (IAA) and indolepropionic acid (IPA) in the feces and serum of postmenopausal osteoporotic women and ovariectomized (OVX) mice. These levels correlate positively with bone mineral density (BMD) and microstructural parameters [111]. Additionally, as abnormal glutamine metabolism is linked to bone degeneration, supplementing glutamine or its metabolites may restore skeletal homeostasis [109,157].

Disruption of ECM

Under osteoporotic conditions, disordered arrangement of collagen fibers and abnormal geometric structure of minerals in the ECM disrupt the microstructural stability of bone tissue and reduce the hardness of the bone matrix [136,158]. Meanwhile, abnormal degradation of the ECM is accompanied by the release of inflammatory cytokines, which exacerbates bone resorption [159].

Abnormal expression of inflammatory cytokines

Immune dysregulation leads to disturbances of the bone microenvironment [160]. Meanwhile, ROS accumulate substantially in the microenvironment and interact with inflammatory factors [161], collectively disrupting bone metabolism and ultimately compromising microenvironmental homeostasis [26].

Both senile and postmenopausal osteoporosis exhibit a significant state of chronic inflammation, which is closely associated with the abnormal expression of inflammatory mediators and cytokines. The decline in estrogen levels following menopause exacerbates age-related inflammatory dysregulation, resulting in elevated systemic and local IL-6 secretion. IL-6 acts synergistically with IL-1 and TNF-α to amplify the inflammatory response and promotes osteoclastogenesis through the induction of RANKL expression [162,163]. A recent study found that estrogen deficiency drives a subset of B cells into a hyperproliferative state. This aberrant proliferation further increases the RANKL/OPG ratio, ultimately potentiating the activation of OCs and bone resorption [10]. In senile osteoporosis, the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) exhibits aberrant hyperactivity, releasing a multitude of inflammatory cytokines that not only amplify the inflammatory response but also inhibit the production of OPG [[164], [165], [166]]. Both types of osteoporosis ultimately result in an elevated RANKL/OPG ratio and enhanced OCs activity, leading to reduced bone mass.

Therapeutic strategies targeting the osteoporotic bone microenvironment

The treatment of OBD remains a significant clinical challenge, primarily attributed to its intricate pathological microenvironment, which encompasses insufficient OBs activity, excessive OCs activation, impaired angiogenesis, compromised innervation, and immune dysregulation. To address these multifaceted pathological features, a variety of engineered biomaterials have been developed to actively intervene in the osteoporotic bone microenvironment. These biomaterials function not merely as structural substitutes but as sophisticated modulators capable of reprogramming the pathological niche through multi-targeted regulation. Ion-releasing biomaterials

In recent years, ion-releasing biomaterials have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. Although metal ions show significant potential for modulating bone metabolism and promoting regeneration, their administration in free form poses risks due to systemic toxicity. To address this limitation, these ions are often encapsulated within biomaterials to optimize their bioavailability and mitigate adverse systemic impacts. Ions including Ca2+, Mg2+, Zn2+, Cu2+, and Mn2+ have been extensively studied and applied in clinical practice. Osteogenic, ion-releasing biomaterials

In the field of osteoporotic bone regeneration, some studies have focused on developing materials capable of directly promoting bone healing. Among these, nanomaterials show great potential for facilitating these processes due to their high specific surface area, excellent biocompatibility, and tunable surface properties. Cheng et al. developed energetic calcium phosphate nanominerals (ECPN) that utilize polyphosphate as an energy source. By mimicking the nanostructure of natural bone, ECPN generates ATP under physiological conditions, promoting autophagy and the osteogenic differentiation of rBMSCs while inhibiting OCs activity [167] (Fig. 4A). Beyond nanomaterials, hydrogels with loose, highly porous structures and high hydrophilicity are also ideal candidates for bone regeneration. Zhao et al. constructed a bisphosphonate-functionalized injectable hydrogel microsphere (GelMA-BP-Mg). Utilizing the coordination reaction between bisphosphonate (BP) and Mg2+, they developed a microsphere system capable of actively capturing and sustainably releasing Mg2+. This system significantly upregulates the expression of osteogenic genes Runx2 and ALP in BMSCs, as well as pro-angiogenic factors VEGF and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), while inhibiting OCs activity through synergistic effects with BP [170] (Fig. 4B). In contrast to the localized sustained release offered by injectable hydrogels, bone cement materials prioritize mechanical reinforcement. In this regard, Zhao et al. proposed an innovative bone cement formulation, developing an injectable dual-crosslinked PEGylated poly(glycerol sebacate) (PEGS)/calcium phosphate cement (CPC) composite material. By loading alendronate (ALN), this material not only enhances bone adhesion but also exhibits superior mechanical properties. Additionally, the controlled release of ALN effectively inhibits excessive OCs activity, thereby promoting bone regeneration [172] (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, Lu et al. engineered a pH-responsive magnesium-gelatin microsphere system (GelMa/Mg/DMF MS). The acidic microenvironment of osteoporotic defects accelerates the degradation of internal magnesium, triggering the co-release of Mg2+ and hydrogen gas. Mg2+ not only promotes osteogenic differentiation via Runx2, OPN, and OCN upregulation but also neutralizes local acidity to suppress osteoclast-mediated resorption. In addition, Zn2+, Mn2+, and Cu2+ are essential trace elements, and in recent years, advancing insights into their roles in bone metabolism have driven a surge in related research. For example, Chen et al. developed a sialylation-based multifunctional hydrogel (SF-ZIF@NA), which prevents precursor osteoclasts (preOCs) from fusing into mature osteoclasts (mOCs) and reduces excessive bone resorption by enzymatically removing sialic acid on the surface of OCs. Moreover, the hydrogel can release Zn2+ in response to acidic microenvironments. This Zn2+ further promotes OBs differentiation and angiogenesis by synergizing with platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) secreted by the preserved preOCs [171] (Fig. 5A). Fracture is the most common complication of osteoporosis, seriously affecting patients' quality of life. In response to this clinical challenge, Qian et al. developed a zinc-based degradable intramedullary nail (IMN) with a specialized coating. Composed of zoledronic acid (ZA) and calcium-zinc silicate (Ca(Zn)Si), this coating not only improved the IMN’s corrosion resistance and reduced its degradation rate, but also enabled the sustained release of Zn2+, Ca2+, silicon ions (Si4+) and ZA, which significantly enhanced fracture healing, and improved BMD and bone quality in a rat model of osteoporotic femoral fracture [173] (Fig. 5B). Beyond Zn2+, Mn2+ also exhibits unique potential. For instance, the manganese-containing β-tricalcium phosphate (Mn-TCP) bioceramics developed by Li et al. can effectively scavenge ROS in OCs precursor cells, inhibit OCs formation and function, promote OBs differentiation, and ultimately accelerate the regeneration of OBD through the release of Mn2+ ions [135] (Fig. 5C). In the field of osteoporosis treatment, apart from research focusing on regulating the activity of OCs and OBs to promote bone regeneration, the role of innervation in bone healing has gradually attracted attention. Song et al. proposed the “ultrasound-driven innervated bone regeneration” strategy, in which Zn-Cu alloy scaffolds were implanted into the skull defect sites of osteoporotic rats and combined with low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS). Immune-regulating, ion-releasing biomaterials

With the deepening understanding of the importance of the immune microenvironment in osteoporosis, an increasing number of recent studies have focused on regulating this microenvironment through biomaterials to ameliorate osteoporosis. For example, the metal-polyDNA nanoparticles (Ca-polyCpG MDNs) prepared by Liu et al. are composed of Ca2+ and ultralong single-stranded CpG sequences. These nanoparticles not only neutralize hydrogen ions secreted by OCs and supplement Ca2+ to promote bone remineralization, but their polyCpG sequences also can activate immune cells and induce the secretion of interleukin-12 (IL-12), thereby inhibiting the differentiation and function of OCs and reducing bone resorption [168] (Fig. 6A). In contrast to Liu's calcium-based nanomaterials, Fu et al. designed calcium-aluminum layered double hydroxide (CALC) nanosheets with a unique two-dimensional layered structure. This structure offers a larger specific surface area and higher ion transport efficiency, potentially providing an advantage over traditional nanoparticles. The material not only neutralizes the acidic environment to inhibit OCs activity but also utilizes released Ca2+ to react with PO43-, forming calcium phosphate nanoparticles (CAPs). For osteoporotic fractures, improving the immune microenvironment can effectively promote bone regeneration. For instance, Ma et al. established a mouse model of osteoporotic femoral shaft fracture and implanted high-purity magnesium implants into the mice. They observed that compared to clinically common Ti6Al4V implants, magnesium alloy implants exhibited stronger osteogenic activity, more effectively promoted the formation of new bone trabeculae and blood vessels, and regulated immune cells. Specifically, they increased immature neutrophils in the early stage and shifted lymphocytes and macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby playing a positive role in facilitating fracture healing [178] (Fig. 6C). Building on this, Cao et al. further explored the synergistic interaction between magnesium and other mechanisms, and developed a multicore functional microsphere scaffold (GMA MSs). It uses GelMA as the matrix to encapsulate magnesium spheres and ALN. Within the acidic inflammatory microenvironment, the magnesium spheres hydrolyze to produce H2, which inhibits the polarization of macrophages toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. The generated Mg2+ promotes osteogenesis, while the alkaline environment from hydrolysis facilitates the release of ALN to inhibit osteoclastic activity. This multimechanism synergistic effect not only achieves local bone repair but also alleviates systemic osteoporosis. Its comprehensive therapeutic efficacy significantly surpasses that of single-material strategies, providing a novel approach for the synergistic treatment of OBD [177] (Fig. 6D).

Biomaterials containing zinc, manganese, and copper also play important roles in improving the immune microenvironment of osteoporosis. For example, the Zn-2Cu-0.5Zr alloy developed by Ji et al. inhibits the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype of macrophages by releasing Zn2+, reduces TNF-α, IL-6, and ROS, while activating the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, increasing the expression of IL-10 and CD206. This remodels the immune microenvironment to be conducive to bone repair, significantly upregulates the expression of key osteogenic genes such as ALP and Runx2, and enhances the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs. As a key alloying element, Cu synergistically improves the overall performance of the material by forming the CuZn5 phase, achieving a yield strength of 198.7 MPa and an ultimate tensile strength of 217.6 MPa. It also endows potential antibacterial properties, reduces inflammatory interference, and accelerates the healing of tibial fractures in elderly osteoporotic rats [179] (Fig. 7A). Advances in metabolite-based biomaterials for osteoporosis treatment.

Glucose metabolite-based biomaterials

Citrate and α-KG, key products of glucose metabolism, affect bone homeostasis through complex metabolic mechanisms. The development of their related biomaterials can be clearly divided into two categories: targeted delivery and metabolic regulation. These two categories drive therapeutic innovation from the perspectives of precise drug administration and metabolic regulation, respectively.

Targeted delivery biomaterials focus on precise enrichment in bone tissue, achieving efficient accumulation and controlled release of drugs at bone lesions through chemical modification or carrier design. For instance, Chen et al. developed a citrate-modified, pH-sensitive bone-targeted delivery system for estrogen. This system exploits the high affinity between citrate and HA to achieve precise skeletal targeting. Upon reaching the bone tissue, the acidic microenvironment—created by H+ secretion from OCs—triggers a structural rearrangement of the polymer, inducing the rapid release of estrogen. This mechanism significantly mitigates systemic drug leakage and adverse uterine effects while facilitating the localized, estrogen-mediated inhibition of osteoclastogenesis, thereby enhancing both therapeutic efficacy and safety in postmenopausal osteoporosis management [181] (Fig. 8A). α-KG also shows promising prospects in the treatment of osteoporosis. As a dietary supplement with high safety and few side effects, α-KG is regarded as a promising therapeutic agent. Filip et al. conducted a 6-month randomized double-blind study on 76 postmenopausal women with osteopenia. Daily administration of α-KG calcium salt (containing 6 g α-KG and 1.68 g calcium) led to a 1.6% increase in lumbar spine BMD compared with baseline [186]. However, α-KG exhibits poor bioavailability and limited bone-targeting efficiency. To address these issues, Cheng et al. developed a near-infrared luminescent lanthanide nanoparticle carrier (LMEK) for bone-targeted delivery of α-KG to enhance its efficacy in osteoporosis treatment. The nanocarrier achieves bone-targeting through an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-modified mesoporous silica layer, significantly increasing the accumulation of α-KG in bone tissue. It improves osteoporosis by inhibiting the accumulation of HIF-1α to reduce the formation and activity of OCs, while promoting the differentiation and mineralization of OBs. Metabolic regulation biomaterials focus on the intervention and remodeling of cellular metabolic pathways, influencing cell functions by regulating metabolite concentrations or signaling pathways in the microenvironment. For example, Wu et al. constructed a citrate-functionalized scaffold to investigate the metabolic regulatory mechanism of citrate in macrophage polarization and its therapeutic potential for osteoporosis. High concentrations of citrate significantly inhibit glycolysis in M1 macrophages, reprogramming their metabolism toward oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and suppressing the pro-inflammatory phenotype, thereby indirectly reducing OCs formation and bone resorption. While high concentrations of citrate moderately inhibit metabolic activity in M2 macrophages, this effect can be alleviated by citrate consumption to maintain the anti-inflammatory phenotype. In contrast, low concentrations of citrate weakly inhibit M1 macrophages without significantly affecting their glycolytic pathways, thus avoiding excessive suppression of inflammatory responses. Meanwhile, low concentrations of citrate have little impact on M2 macrophage metabolism and may even enhance osteogenic activity to maintain bone homeostasis by promoting their anti-inflammatory phenotype [182] (Fig. 8C). Therefore, developing biomaterials that precisely modulate citrate levels represents a promising strategy to restore bone homeostasis in osteoporosis. To address OBD, Kang et al. fabricated a calcium phosphate cement (CPC) incorporated with α-KG polyester microspheres. The incorporation of α-KG polyester microspheres enhances the bioactivity, biodegradability, and porosity of the CPC, accelerating its degradation and forming interconnected pores that facilitate bone regeneration. This system improves the osteogenic microenvironment by inhibiting inflammatory responses via the downregulation of IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α and reducing oxidative stress via ROS scavenging and superoxide dismutase (SOD) upregulation. Furthermore, it stimulates the differentiation of OBs, a process primarily orchestrated by the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. These effects collectively enhance the ability to repair OBD [42] (Fig. 8D).

Amino acid metabolite-based biomaterials

Amino acids, as the basic building blocks of proteins, play a key role in biological processes. Amino acids such as arginine and their derivatives have become important functional components for the surface modification of bone biomaterials by participating in processes including cell adhesion, metabolic reprogramming, and inflammation regulation. Lysine can serve as a core component of bone repair scaffold substrates, and by acting as a structural and functional carrier, it supports the regulation of the osteogenic microenvironment during the repair of OBD.

Arginine and its derivatives are widely used in the surface modification of bone biomaterials to enhance osteogenic performance. For example, RGD peptides—composed of arginine, glycine, and aspartic acid—can enhance cell adhesion, promote OBs migration, and facilitate bone regeneration. Rammelt et al. coated the surface of Ti implants with RGD peptides alone or in combination with collagen and chondroitin sulfate, which markedly increased the rate of new bone formation after 4 weeks [187]. Park et al. immobilized RGD sequences on the surface of Ti implants via electrodeposited polyethylene glycol (PEG), forming an RGD/PEG/Ti interface. This enhanced the early adhesion and differentiation of OBs, notably increasing the bone-implant contact rate in rabbit cancellous bone and improving bone repair capacity [188]. Although this method substantially improves bone repair efficiency by immobilizing RGD peptides, its efficacy in the complex pathological microenvironment of OBD remains unvalidated. Its effectiveness may be limited under such complex conditions accompanied by bone loss, an inflammatory microenvironment, and OBs dysfunction.

Recently, various material-based strategies have emerged to address these challenges. For example, inspired by the extracellular skeleton structure of the liver, Gu et al. prepared a bionic liver skeleton microstructure on the surface of polyetherketoneketone (PEKK) scaffolds via femtosecond laser etching and sulfonation. They found that this structure can activate the MET signaling pathway, induce the retrograde transport of arginase-2 (Arg2) from mitochondria to the cytoplasm, and generate ornithine and urea via arginine hydrolysis. This process drives the polarization of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages, and enhances OXPHOS by regulating the activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex II to provide energy for bone regeneration. Meanwhile, spermine, a metabolite of arginine, activates mitochondrial calcium channels, synergistically improves energy metabolism, inhibits inflammatory cytokines, and indirectly upregulates osteogenic factors [184] (Fig. 9A). Collectively, these effects significantly promote new bone formation and osseointegration in osteoporotic rat models, highlighting the key role of arginine metabolism and its derivatives in regulating macrophage function and improving the osteoporotic microenvironment. Gao et al. developed an electrostatically encapsulated 3D-printed polyelectrolyte scaffold (APS@P) for the repair of OBD. The scaffold was composed of alginate/ε-polylysine (Alg/ε-PL) and incorporated a bone-targeting liposomal formulation of salvianolic acid B (SAB-BTL) to achieve sustained and targeted drug delivery. ECM-mimetic biomaterials

In clinical applications and bone tissue engineering, ECM-mimetic biomaterials exhibit significant advantages in bone defect repair and fracture healing by simulating the microenvironment of natural bone ECM. Through their three-dimensional structures, they support OBs’ adhesion, differentiation, and bone mass augmentation [189]. Currently, ECM-based scaffolds are mainly divided into decellularized ECM (dECM) scaffolds and ECM-modified biomaterial scaffolds. dECM scaffolds directly utilize bioactive components such as collagen networks and functional proteins retained after the decellularization of natural tissues to promote bone regeneration [190,191]. ECM-modified biomaterial scaffolds further enhance therapeutic efficacy through functional modifications, including mineralization and the loading of growth factors such as BMP-2-related peptides [192]. With the development of bone regenerative medicine, the osteoinductive and osteogenic potential of ECM-mimetic biomaterials is attracting increasing attention.

Accordingly, Sun et al. developed heparinized mineralized SIS loaded with the BMP2-related peptide P28 (mSIS/P28), using decellularized small intestinal submucosa (SIS) as the core. They first deposited HA on SIS via mineralization to mimic the natural bone ECM, forming mSIS, and then conjugated P28 to heparin-modified mSIS. Via the synergistic effect of SIS nanofibers, HA physical induction, and heparin-P28 chemical signals, mSIS/P28 significantly promoted the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs from ovariectomized rats (OVX-rBMSCs) in vitro, without the requirement for extra osteogenic components. In vivo, in OVX rat cranial critical-sized defects, the scaffold achieved 40 days of sustained P28 release. New bone formation was over 7 times that of pure SIS [192] (Fig. 10A). Further refining this strategy of compositional mimicry, Tang et al. developed an in situ mineralized ECM composite that replicates the organic-inorganic hierarchy of natural bone, thereby significantly enhancing the osteoconductivity of the base scaffold [193]. Distinctively, Liu et al. developed a biomimetic cryogel (GC-Normal dECM) based on dECM derived from normal rats. It was fabricated by combining dECM with methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) through unidirectional freezing, photo-crosslinking, and genipin crosslinking, possessing an oriented porous structure and controllable degradability. This cryogel can promote the proliferation, migration, and osteogenic differentiation of OVX-rBMSCs. Mechanistically, it reduces the accumulation of ROS in OVX-rBMSCs by downregulating Romo1, activates antioxidant pathways, improves the cellular microenvironment, and upregulates ALP activity as well as the expression of osteogenic genes such as Runx2, Col1A1, and OCN. However, the native ECM acts not only as a biochemical reservoir but also as a dynamic mechanical instructor. While current osteoporotic research prioritizes biochemical cues, reconstructing mechanical attributes like viscoelasticity and strain-stiffening is crucial to restore the compromised mechanosensitivity of the osteoporotic microenvironment. Although these advanced systems were primarily established for general bone tissue engineering, they exhibit dynamic mechanical properties that are essential for rectifying the pathological mechanotransduction observed in osteoporotic bone. For instance, Liu et al. engineered a “viscoelastic liquid-crystalline” scaffold by infusing a chitin whisker/chitosan hydrogel into a 3D-printed PLLA framework. This system mimics the rapid stress relaxation of native bone, significantly enhancing cell spreading and differentiation compared to elastic controls [195]. Similarly, Mavlankar et al. developed mineralized peptide amphiphiles that exhibit collagen-like strain-stiffening. By stiffening under deformation, these self-healing composites provide a mechanically dynamic environment that effectively supports OBs proliferation [196]. Integrating such mechanical biomimicry with biochemical strategies represents the next frontier in treating OBD.

Advances in cytokine-loaded biomaterials for osteoporosis treatment.

In the field of osteoporotic bone repair, research exploring the functions of single cytokines and their adaptation to biomaterials has deepened progressively. In studies on immune regulation and osteogenesis coupling, Casarrubios et al. fabricated two types of scaffolds, namely nanocrystalline silicon-substituted hydroxyapatite (NanoSiHA) and crystalline silicon-substituted hydroxyapatite (SiHA), both of which were functionalized with VEGF via non-covalent adsorption. SiHA/VEGF scaffolds promoted endothelial cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, leading to enhanced bone regeneration, increased trabeculae, and improved vascularization in osteoporotic sheep. In contrast, NanoSiHA scaffolds exhibited limited bone regeneration due to poor pre-osteoblast proliferation and higher inflammatory responses [198] (Fig. 11A). As a key osteogenic cytokine, the delivery and mechanism of BMP-2 under osteoporotic conditions have long been a research hotspot. Elisabet et al. encapsulated BMP-2 and 17β-estradiol in PLGA and PLA-S microspheres, respectively, and delivered them using a P-T-CD supramolecular gelatin in osteoporotic rat calvarial defects. Although the combination therapy enhanced collagen type I expression, the new bone exhibited immature and insufficient mineralization compared to that in non-osteoporotic rats. Nevertheless, it still outperformed BMP-2 monotherapy in terms of overall bone repair and mineralization in the calvarial defects [199] (Fig. 11B). The clinical application of BMP-2 is limited by its short half-life and side effects caused by high doses. Echave et al. developed a gelatin/bioceramic composite scaffold for loading BMP-2 to promote the repair of OBD. They introduced calcium ceramic compounds into the gelatin matrix, creating 3D scaffolds with high porosity and good biocompatibility. The scaffold significantly reduces the required dose of BMP-2, needing only 600 ng per scaffold, which is lower than the traditional dose of 2–15 μg, and significantly enhances the mineralization of new bone. The scaffold showed robust bone regeneration capabilities in an osteoporotic mouse calvarial defect model, with a significantly higher repair rate than the blank scaffold group and a higher degree of maturation of the new bone [200] (Fig. 11C). To more comprehensively address the multifaceted challenges of OBD repair, Lee et al. developed a multifunctional scaffold system. This system integrates zinc oxide (ZO), ALN, and BMP-2 nanoparticles, thereby endowing it with multiple functionalities, including anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, anti-osteoclastogenic, and osteogenic properties. Particularly, ZO continuously releases NO, which activates the cGMP and protein kinase G signaling pathways, thereby promoting angiogenesis and bone formation. Additionally, ZO downregulates the RANKL/OPG ratio by inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, further enhancing bone defect repair. Within this component-centric taxonomy, extracellular vesicles (EVs) are systematically classified under the cytokine-loaded framework, a categorization based on their functional role as integrated signaling vectors. Despite the intrinsic complexity of EVs, which include lipids, metabolites, and nucleic acids, their regenerative potential within the osteoporotic niche is predominantly attributed to the targeted delivery of encapsulated or surface-displayed protein signals, such as BMP-2 and VEGF [205,206]. This classification underscores a paradigm shift wherein EVs are engineered as bioactive packages designed to restore paracrine homeostasis and rectify the dysregulated cytokine networks characteristic of the pathological bone microenvironment. For instance, Li et al. demonstrated that genetically modifying MSCs to overexpress BMP-2 yields engineered exosomes that leverage the synergistic effect of the natural secretome and upregulated growth factors to drive osteogenesis [205]. Addressing the vascular deficit, Lin et al. developed “gene-activated” exosomes encapsulating VEGF plasmids to effectively direct vascularized osteogenesis through intracellular gene delivery [206], while Sun et al. engineered biodegradable hydrogel depots to ensure the sustained, localized release of functional exosomes, preventing rapid in vivo clearance [207].

Building on these engineering foundations, Tang et al. applied a transcriptomics-guided strategy to develop a precision therapy specifically for the osteoporotic niche. By analyzing gene expression profiles in ovariectomized mice, they identified the overactivated immune-metabolic PI3K/AKT axis as a critical therapeutic target. To modulate this pathway, they engineered exosomes that co-encapsulate the osteoinductive cytokine BMP-2 and bifunctional cobalt-aluminum layered double hydroxide nanosheets. This system not only effectively scavenges ROS but also utilizes the encapsulated BMP-2 to synergistically regulate the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, successfully restoring trabecular microarchitecture and rebalancing bone metabolism [202] (Fig. 12A). Distinct from mammalian sources, bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) have emerged as a highly scalable and engineerable alternative. Zhou et al. utilized this platform to develop a sequential release system using BEVs carrying the pBMP-2-VEGF shuttle plasmid (BEVs-BP). This approach employs the vesicle lumen for gene delivery to induce sustained, endogenous factor expression, thereby circumventing the immunogenicity of exogenous proteins. By encapsulating these vesicles within an IL-4-loaded GelMA hydrogel, the system mimics the natural healing cascade: rapid IL-4 release first induces M2 macrophage polarization to resolve inflammation, followed by the sustained release of BEVs-BP to upregulate BMP-2 and VEGF during the repair phase. This stage-specific “immuno-osteogenic” strategy significantly accelerates fracture healing in osteoporotic models [203] (Fig. 12B). Integrated biomaterial systems

The conceptual framework laid out in prior sections defines the core therapeutic modules rooted in the intrinsic components of the bone microenvironment. Ion-releasing biomaterials regulate cellular behavior through the release of key bioactive ions and can further function as delivery platforms for the spatiotemporally controlled release of VEGF and nerve growth factor (NGF). This approach synergistically promotes vascularization and neurogenesis, thereby advancing neuro-vascular-bone coupled repair [208,175]. The strategy of using metabolite-based and cytokine-loaded biomaterials promotes osteogenic differentiation by supplementing deficient metabolites or delivering key cytokines within the bone microenvironment [110,201]. Based on the structural and bioactive characteristics of natural bone ECM, ECM-mimetic biomaterials can construct a supportive structure similar to the natural bone microenvironment [194]. Relying on their own components such as collagen, glycosaminoglycans, and growth factors, these biomaterials activate osteogenesis-related pathways, thereby providing favorable support for cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation [209]. By integrating the strategies of supplementing deficient components, correcting aberrant pathways, and modulating the immune microenvironment, these materials proactively drive the dysregulated microenvironment back to homeostasis. Building upon these foundational modalities, contemporary engineering strategies are moving toward their strategic superposition to further optimize therapeutic outcomes. By integrating components from these distinct classes into unified hybrid platforms, synchronized, multidimensional repair cascades can be orchestrated.

This combinatorial approach matches the intricate pathology of osteoporosis, thereby ensuring that the advantages of each functional module are synergistically amplified to achieve comprehensive tissue regeneration. For example, Zhao et al. proposed an innovative “hot spring-mimetic” strategy, developing a photothermal calcium magnesium phosphate cement (MCPC) combined with manganese-substituted Fe3O4 (MnxFe3-xO4) and infused with a PLGA hydrogel loaded with VEGF and NGF. This material not only takes advantage of the mechanical support and osteogenic capacity of the bone cement but also enables the sustained release of VEGF and NGF via hydrogel degradation. Meanwhile, Mn2+ and Mg2+ act as signaling drivers for angiogenesis and neuroregeneration, while manganese-based components scavenge excessive ROS under photothermal stimulation, thereby facilitating the reconstruction of neurovascular networks and bone regeneration [175] (Fig. 13A). Similarly, Zheng et al. developed a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) material with the surface modified via N2 plasma immersion ion implantation (PIII) and IL-4 solution treatment to immobilize the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-4. This design exploits the strengths of both categories in a temporal relay. IL-4 acts first to polarize macrophages toward the reparative M2 phenotype, mitigating the early acute inflammatory response, while the synergistic release of a small amount of ALN and Ca2+ in this early phase further contributes to constructing a favorable osteoimmunomodulatory microenvironment. Subsequently, the sustained release of ALN and Ca2+ enhances osteogenesis while suppressing RANKL-mediated osteoclastogenesis, thereby restoring bone homeostasis [197] (Fig. 13B). Challenges and future perspectives

While engineered biomaterials targeting the osteoporotic bone microenvironment hold significant promise, their clinical translation is impeded by a series of formidable challenges, which also delineate the priorities for future research. This section discusses the key limitations and future directions for the development of ion-releasing, metabolite-based, ECM-mimetic, and cytokine-loaded biomaterials.

Distinct challenges across biomaterial categories

Each class of biomaterials faces its own unique set of obstacles. For ion-releasing biomaterials, the precise spatiotemporal control over ion release remains a primary hurdle, as uncontrolled burst release can lead to cytotoxicity. The complex and often unpredictable synergistic or antagonistic interactions between multiple ions in composite systems are poorly understood. The long-term biosafety of these materials, particularly within the unique pathophysiology of osteoporosis, requires thorough validation. Additionally, the impact of individual patient differences—such as age, sex, and specific osteoporotic etiology—on ion metabolism and therapeutic efficacy adds another layer of complexity.

Metabolite-based biomaterials are constrained by the inherent instability of metabolites in vivo and the vast complexity of individual metabolic pathways, which complicates the prediction of uniform therapeutic outcomes. The dose-dependent effects of key metabolites like citrate and α-KG on OBs, OCs, and immune cells are not fully elucidated. Crucially, the low local delivery efficiency and poor targeting specificity of these metabolites significantly hamper their therapeutic potential and clinical applicability.

ECM-mimetic biomaterials struggle to fully recapitulate the intricate biochemical complexity and nanoscale architecture of the native bone extracellular matrix. The immunogenic risk associated with dECM from xenogeneic sources also limits their clinical adoption. Beyond biocomposition, a significant gap often exists between the mechanical properties (such as stiffness, toughness, and viscoelasticity) of current synthetic scaffolds and those of natural bone tissue, which can adversely affect their integration and ultimate repair performance in the defective host environment.

Cytokine-loaded biomaterials face the classic dilemmas of achieving precise control over cytokine release kinetics, maintaining their bioactivity in vivo, and minimizing off-target effects. The situation is further complicated by the complex synergistic and antagonistic relationships between different cytokines, making the engineering of sophisticated, multifactor delivery systems with temporally sequential release profiles a significant technical hurdle.

Converging challenges and integrated solutions

Underlying these category-specific hurdles are broader, systemic bottlenecks that define the current frontier of the field. Firstly, there is a critical need for physiologically relevant and predictive preclinical models. Traditional models often fail to replicate the chronic, multifactorial nature of the osteoporotic niche, leading to promising in vitro results that do not translate in vivo. Secondly, the field must address patient heterogeneity. Factors such as age, sex, genetic background, and microbiome composition create a spectrum of osteoporotic endotypes, demanding a shift from one-size-fits-all solutions toward personalized therapeutic strategies.

Bone organoid technology offers a transformative approach to these converging challenges. By differentiating from stem cells and self-assembling into 3D structures, bone organoids closely emulate the intricate cellular makeup, spatial organization, and functional properties of natural bone tissue [210,211]. The construction of these high-fidelity models begins with the precise replication of the physical microenvironment. Recent breakthroughs have focused on recreating native structural cues. Gai et al. engineered a dECM-based bionic scaffold to provide essential architectural support for vascularization [212]. Wang et al. established a 3D bioprinting strategy using hydroxyapatite-based hybrid bioinks to achieve the self-mineralization of bone organoids [213]. Similarly, Zhu et al. utilized dynamic GelMA/DNA hydrogels to recreate the native viscoelasticity of the bone matrix, thereby facilitating the self-organization of woven bone organoids [214]. While structural mimicry provides the physical framework, recapitulating the dynamic signaling and developmental landscape is equally critical for functional fidelity. Moving beyond static scaffolds, recent studies have integrated bioactive cues to mimic complex physiological processes. Zhang et al. developed a “developmental” osteo-organoid (ossification center-like organoid, OCO) by integrating BMP-2 into its inner-core hydrogel to achieve osteogenic functionality [215].

To further capture the systemic complexity of the skeletal environment, the field is now advancing from single-tissue models to integrated multi-tissue systems. Bai et al. proposed the concept of “assembloids” which transcends the limitations of single-organoid systems by integrating multiple tissue units—such as bone, cartilage, and vasculature—to recapitulate the complex multicellular microenvironment in vivo [216]. This inspires the recognition that osteoporosis, as a systemic disorder involving the dysregulation of bone, bone marrow, vasculature, and other key components, urgently requires advanced in vitro models capable of incorporating these elements and simulating their dynamic interactions. Assembloid technology represents a pivotal and promising pathway toward achieving this goal, thereby advancing osteoporosis mechanistic research and drug development into a new, more integrated dimension. Expanding this systemic perspective to distant-organ crosstalk, Wang et al. utilized intestinal organoids to generate therapeutic EVs. These organoid-derived EVs effectively modulated the gut-bone axis to ameliorate osteoporosis secondary to inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), demonstrating the unique capability of organoid technology to unravel and treat complex, multi-system pathologies [217].

Collectively, when such physiologically relevant 3D systems are generated from patient-specific cells—such as BMSCs, adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)—they effectively mirror the pathological heterogeneity of osteoporosis, providing a high-fidelity human model for disease modeling and therapeutic screening [211,218]. Beyond serving as high-fidelity disease models, these patient-specific organoids offer an ideal biomimetic platform for the functional evaluation of engineered biomaterials [219]. This approach enables researchers to move beyond simplistic biocompatibility assays to assess complex therapeutic outcomes—such as the restoration of osteogenic-osteoclastic coupling, the modulation of immune microenvironments, and the reconstruction of mineralized bone matrix—all within a controllable system that retains the patient's unique biological context.

Integrating technologies for future therapies

The path forward lies not in incremental improvements within isolated material categories, but in a synergistic integration of biology and engineering. The development of next-generation biomaterials for osteoporotic bone regeneration can be accelerated by integrating bone organoids, organ-on-a-chip systems, and AI into a closed-loop feedback system (Fig. 14). However, the high-fidelity data generated by patient-specific organoids, while rich in pathophysiological insights, creates a subsequent bottleneck—the translation of complex phenotypic readouts into optimized biomaterial design parameters. AI is positioned to address this critical gap, offering the computational power necessary to infer predictive design rules from multidimensional datasets [221]. Specifically, machine learning (ML) algorithms can analyze high-throughput data generated from bone organoid experiments to identify nonlinear and synergistic relationships between material parameters (e.g., composition, structure, release profile) and functional outcomes (e.g., osteogenic restoration, immunomodulation). For instance, Yakavets et al. recently utilized machine learning to analyze microfluidic organoid arrays, successfully identifying optimal multidrug regimens from a vast combinatorial space [222].

Building on such proofs of concept, AI is poised to significantly accelerate the development and optimization of biomaterials for osteoporosis treatment. Through iterative prediction and validation, AI transforms the multidimensional data from patient-specific organoids into targeted design strategies, ultimately establishing a closed-loop discovery platform that systematically addresses both physiological relevance and individual heterogeneity. Within this integrated framework, AI deciphers high-fidelity data from organoid assays and dynamic organ-on-a-chip cultures to refine biomaterial design ex vivo, establishing a bi-directional data flow that enables the preclinical assessment of safety and therapeutic efficacy in patient-specific osteoporotic bone organoids. This bioengineering strategy mitigates the unpredictability of in vivo performance and thus facilitates the development of more reliable and personalized osteoporotic bone regeneration therapies.

Conclusions

This review proposes a novel conceptual framework categorizing biomaterials according to the core functional components of the bone microenvironment—ions, metabolites, ECM, and cytokines—which represents a paradigm shift from single-target therapies to systemic microenvironmental reprogramming. These biomaterials collectively reverse the osteoporotic niche by simultaneously promoting osteogenesis, inhibiting osteoclastogenesis, and restoring immune-metabolic balance, thereby enabling vascularized and innervated bone regeneration. Looking forward, the integration of patient-derived bone organoids and organs-on-chips for pathologically relevant testing and AI-assisted design is poised to overcome translational barriers and usher in an era of personalized bone regenerative medicine.
