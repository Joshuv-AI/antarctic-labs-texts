The effect of mobilization with movement on pain, joint distance, effusion area, and inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis: a double-blind randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Ömer Faruk Özçelep, Nur Tunali, Atahan Turhan, Esat Polat, Ceren Demır, Doğuş Gümüşay, Figen Tuncay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective management of established rheumatoid arthritis emphasizes on pain relief, disease control, joint preservation, and improved function and quality of life. This trial examines the effects of mobilization with movement on joint distance, effusion area, and inflammation in patients with established rheumatoid arthritis, with interventions applied to both hands.MethodsA total of 76 participants were screened, and 21 met the inclusion criteria. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: the mobilization group (MG) (n = 7), sham mobilization group (SMG) (n = 7), or control group (CG) (n = 7). The groups underwent a two-week traditional physiotherapy program. In addition, the MG received mobilization with movement (5 sessions per week, 15 min per session), while the SMG received squeeze-release application during the same period. The primary outcomes were the pre- and post-treatment comparison of pain, joint space, effusion area, and inflammation. The secondary outcomes included comparisons of hand function, dexterity, grip strength, disease activity, and quality of life. Participants were assessed at both baseline and post-treatment.ResultsImprovement in joint distance, effusion area and inflamation were greater in the MG. Joint space increased by 0.3 mm at the wrist and 0.2 mm at the MCP and PIP joints. Effusion at the PIP joint decreased by 0.04 mm², and inflammation was reduced by 50.59% at the wrist and 53.33% at the PIP joint.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that mobilization with movement effectively increases joint space and reduces inflammation in the wrist and PIP joints, improving the quality of life. Therefore, adding mobilizations to the conventional physiotherapy program might be beneficial to improve joint distance in RA patients.Trial registrationClinical Trial Registry-NCT05660629.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent inflammation, joint swelling, tenderness, and progressive synovial damage [1]. RA is categorized into early and established stages. Early RA refers to the first few years after symptom onset, while established RA includes all patients who have had the disease for more than 1 to 2 years, even even in the absence ofvisible joint damage [2]. The prevalence of RA in Western populations is estimated to be between 0.5% and 1% [3], with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 2:1 [4].The main clinical features of the disease include pain, stiffness, fatigue, functional impairment, and work disability [5].

Established RA management aims to control pain and disease activity, prevent joint damage, and improve functioning and QoL [6]. RA disease activity reduction remains elusive despite significant advances in management [7]. A large percentage of patients respond well to DMARDs [8]. The prevalence of chronic pain indicates that patients require proactive RA treatments [9]. Therapeutic patient education, exercise therapy, physical modalities, orthotics, and mobilizations are nonpharmacological treatments [10].

Joint mobilization techniques, such as Kaltenborn, Mulligan, and Maitland, are used to stretch tendons, ligaments, and joint capsules, as well as to enhance normal joint movement [11]. The Mulligan concepts spesifically emphasizes restoring the gliding component of joint movement, which helps achieve a complete, pain-free range of motion [12]. Additionally, Mulligan proposes that mobilization with movement (MWM) corrects joint position errors caused by injury or strain. Moreover, evidence suggests that MWM may exert hypoalgesic effects and could potentially induce sympathoexcitation [13]. MWM is a unique manual therapy technique involving the application of a sustained accessory glide at a peripheral joint while performing a movement that would normally provoke pain, either actively or passively [14]. In a study on RA patients’ hands, the Kaltenborn mobilization reduced pain, improved blood flow, altered joint fluid composition, and influenced MCP joint space [15]. Another study using the same method found that the Kaltenborn technique reduced pain in the CMC joint and scaphoid regions in older women [16]. Studies have demonstrated that MWM can reduce pain, improve knee function, and enhance mobility in patients with knee and hip osteoarthritis [17, 18].

To our knowledge, no prior studies have investigated the effects of MWM on patients with established RAThis study aimed to investigate the effects of MWM on the wrist, metacarpophalangeal (MCP), and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints, focusing on joint space, effusion area, and inflammation levels in patients with established RA.

Methods

Study design

This double-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry on November 30, 2022 (NCT05660629). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the University Non-Interventional Research Ethics Committee (2023/64). All participants provided written informed consent. The study adhered to the CONSORT criteria.

Participants

The study icluded 21 participants with established RA recruited from a physical therapy and rehabilitation outpatient clinic.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of RA per ACR/EULAR criteria; (2) physician-supervised care for ≥ 1 year; (3) age 18–65 years; (4) low-to-moderate disease activity (DAS-28 ≤ 5.1); (5) Steinbrocker Functional Classification I–III; (6) stable medication use for ≥ 3 months prior to baseline assessment; (7) no upper extremity surgery within the past 6 months; (8) cognitive ability to follow instructions; and (9) no concurrent participation in other rehabilitation programs or studies.Exclusion criteria were: (1) soft tissue injury or hand fracture; (2) acute joint inflammation within the past week; (3) recent upper extremity surgery; and (4) pregnancy within the last 3 months.

Randomization

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence by a secretary not involved in the study.Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: mobilization, sham mobilization, or control.

Blinding

Blinding the physiotherapist to the interventions was not possible due to the study’s nature. The patients were informed that the study compared different physiotherapy approaches. Post-study, a blinded assessor determined whether each patient belonged to the experimental or control group. Additionally, a statistician blinded to group allocation performed the analysis without access to group labels (e.g., Group A).

Mobilization with movement methods

A certified Mulligan practitioner (10 + years of experience) administered the 2-week MWM program (10 sessions; 5 days/week; 45 min/session).This intervention involved applying two oscillations per second to the wrists, MCP, and PIP joints of both hands. Participants performed three sets of six repetition during the first week, increasing to seven repetitions per set in the second week, with a one-minute rest between sets. Sham mobilization methods

A physiotherapist (10 years of experience) performed sham mobilization (Table 2), using gentle squeezing/releasing movements to mimic the active intervention’s tactile feedback without therapeutic effects.

Outcome measures

Participants’ demographic information were recorded. Our primary outcomes included joint distance, the degree of inflammation, and the area of effusion, while our secondary outcomes encompassed pain, hand grip strength, dexterity, hand function, quality of life, and disease activity. We used a GE Healthcare Versana R2 VS ultrasound machine to check for inflammation in the hand joints, following previously defined settings. The main ultrasonographic assessments included measuring the distances of the wrist, MCP, and PIP joints (in mm), the size of fluid buildup (in mm²), and the level of inflammation using color Doppler pixels [20]. Inflammation was graded as follows:

Pain levels were evaluatedpre- and post-treatment using the McGill-Melzack Pain Questionnaire (MPQ) [21]. The Duruoz Hand Index (DHI) was utilized to evaluate hand function and has demonstrated validity and reliability across a broad spectrum of upper extremity conditions [22]. The hand grip strength was measured using a Jamar Hand Dynamometer (Takei Scientific Instruments Co. Ltd., Japan) [23]. Dexterity was evaluated using the Nine Hole Peg Test (NHPT) [24]. Quality of life was evaluated using the Rheumatoid Arthritis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RAQoL) [25]. Disease activity was assessed using the disease activity score (DAS-28), which involved based on evaluations of 15 joints in each hand or wrist while excluding the knees, elbows, and shoulders.

Sample size

Power analysis was conducted by using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.4; Universitat Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the required sample size. As no prior studies had investigated the effects of the MWM on joint distance, inflammation, effusion, pain, quality of life, or grip strength in patients with RA, a reference study was used. Pain scores from a pilot study by Chandran and Goel [26], which looked at the efficacy of curcumin alone or with diclofenac sodium in RA patients, were used to calculate the needed sample size. With an effect size of f = 2.307, power of 80% (β = 0.20), and significance level of α = 0.05, the required sample size was 18. After adjusting for a 20% dropout rate, the final required sample size was determined to be 21 participants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM® SPSS® Statistics (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations (mean ± SD), and categorical variables as percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA for parametric data and the Kruskal-Wallis H test for non-parametric data. Within-group (pre- and post-treatment) comparisons were analyzedusing the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. When significant differences were identified, Bonferroni -adjusted post hoc tests were applied. Since both upper limbs from each participant were included, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were used to account for the correlation between repeated measurements within individuals. This approach, as recommended by Zeger and Liang [27], ensures more accurate estimation of treatment effects by adjusting for within-subject dependency. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (ηp²) and interpreted as small (ηp² = 0.01), medium (ηp² = 0.06), or large (ηp² = 0.14). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

However, nine participants-three from the groups-dropped out of the study. As a result, 21 participants, with seven equally distributed across the three groups, were included in the final analysis. In the mobilization group, joint distanceincreased by 0.3 mm at the wrist (14.29%, ηp²=0.33) and 0.2 mm at the MCP and PIP joints (16.67%, ηp²=0.34), reesulting in an overall increase of 13.67% (ηp²=0.32) from pre-treatment to post-treatment. Although reductions were observed in other joints, these changes were not significant (p > 0.05); Fig. 3; Table 4).

Discussion

This study, we believe is the first of its kind for RA, invesitgates whether MWM can be safely applied in patients with established RA and examines its effects on symptoms and ultrasound findings in the affected joints. MWM applied to the hand and wrist appears to improve quality of life by increasing joint space and reducing inflammation in this patient population. Specifically, MWM increases the joint space in the MCP and PIP joints while also reducing inflammation in the PIP joint, leading to overall improvements in joint function and pain relief. Mobilization techniques may be used in a clinical setting, and the results suggest that these methods are a relatively safe treatment option for individuals with established RA.

Common joint mobilization methods used in research includepassive joint mobilization techniques and named approaches such asKaltenborn, Maitland, and Mulligan. However, current evidence remains insufficient to conclusively support their effectiveness [28, 29]. Research has demonstrated that incorporating manual mobilization of the hands in patients with RA into specialized healthcare services is practical, safe, and effective [15]. Mulligan proposed that the effectiveness of MWM in relieving pain and improving mobility derives from its ability to correct skeletal misalignments [30]. Additionally, studies on Mulligan mobilization for carpal tunnel syndrome have shown that this technique improves nerve function and joint mobility by positioning the joint in a pain-free manner [31].

According to a systematic review and meta-analysis covering the years 2008–2017, MWMs were found to be effective in reducing pain and disability in peripheral joints when compared to sham treatments. However, when compared to other mobilization methods, MWM showed a significant advantage only in pain reduction and was not superior in reducing disability [32]. In addition, as stated in the literature, mobilizations may lead to a general reduction in deep tissue pressure sensitivity by enhancing pain-inhibitory mechanisms [33]. In the present study, the reduction in pain scores following mobilization treatment was found to be smaller than the SEM. This findings suggests that the observed changes may not be of clinically significant. One possible explanation for this outcome is the relatively small number of participants in each group.

The key radiographic signs of RA include bilateral joint involvement, periarticular osteopenia, joint space narrowing, and bony deformities [34]. MRI is considered the most appropriate imaging modality for detecting early changes in RA patients, except in cases of joint space widening, for which computed tomography (CT) is more suitable [35]. Ultrasound demonstrates a sensitivity of 79% and a specificity of 97% in detecting changes at the metatarsophalangeal joints in RA compared to 32% sensitivity and 98% specificity for conventional radiography [36]. In the study by Levitsky et al. [15], researchers examined 320 hand joints using ultrasound, including 12 participants with RA and 8 with hand osteoarthritis to enable fair comparison. One hand from each participant was randomly selected to receive weekly gentle Kaltenborn manual mobilization, which involved passive stretching of the MCP joints performed by manual therapists. The results showed reductions in joint pain, swelling, and inflammation, as well as an increase in MCP joint space.In that study, an average increase of 0.3 mm in joint space was reported in the MCP joint, while no such increase was observed in the PIP and DIP joints. In contrast, the present study documented an increase of 0.3 mm in the wrist and 0.2 mm in both MCP and PIP joints. Similar to the previous findings, a reduction in inflammation was observed in the wrist and PIP. Additionally, a decrease in the effusion area of the PIP joint was identified.This improvement is thought to be associated with soft tissue relaxation and pressure applied around the joint during movements, which may facilitate joint mobility by correcting joint positioning.

Patients with deformities exhibited a significant increase in disease activity during the first five years [37]. Furthermore, it has been reported that functional capacity is primarily associated withdisease activity in the early stages of RA, whereas it is predominantly associated with joint damage in the later stages of the condition [38]. The disease duration of patients in our study ranged from 3 to 25 years, indicating that all individuals had established RA. A reduction of 0.79 units in disease activity was observed in the mobilization group, which had a mean disease duration exceeding 20 years. These findings suggest that exercise-based mobilizationmay help alleviate disease activity in patients with long-standing RA. However, the reduction observed in the sham mobilization group warrants further investigation to determine whether this effect is attributable to the mobilization itself or to the patient–therapist interaction.

Researchers have found that patients with RA exhibit reduced hand strength and significantly impaired hand function test resultscompared to healthy individuals [39]. In our study, consistentwith the literature, handgrip strength was found to be decreased in both hands in participants with RA relative to healthy controls. We believe this reduction is attributable to pain and pain-related functional decline. However, as demonstrated in the study conducted by Özçelep et al., an increase in handgrip strength did not lead to functional improvement [40]. Previous literature also indicates that improvements in grip strength is associated with a reduction in the number of active joints, and that a high DAS-28 is consistently linked to impaired pinch and grip strength [41]. Contradictory findings in the literature may be due to limited sample size and the inclusion of uncontrolled variables.

The main goal in the treatment of RA is to minimize the impact of the disease on daily life by improving quality of life and reducing disability [42]. Dogu et al. demonstrated that 6-week isotonic and isometric hand exercises improved hand function, dexterity, and quality of life while reducing pain and disease activity in patients with RA [43]. Furthermore, manual therapy can be applied to joints, muscles, or nerves, with treatment aims including pain reduction, improved joint movement range and quality of life, increasing nerve mobility, muscle lengthening, and restoration of normal function [44]. A minimally significant impairment in physical function, as measured by HAQ (0.25 increase in HAQ) corresponds to a 2.0 increase in RAQoL score [45]. In our study, we observed a 6.15 decrease in the RAQoL score in the mobilization group. This clinically significant improvement may be attributed to the reduction in pain and correction of positional errors, which in turn lead to improved function.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. Although the sample size calculation was conducted appropriately, the inclusion of multiple outcome measures with only seven participants per group may limit the robustness and generalizability of the results. The small sample size increases the risk of type II errors and may not provide adequate statistical power to detect meaningful differences across all variables. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies with larger sample sizes are strongly recommended to confirm these preliminary results and ensure the reliability of the observed effects.

Additionally, ultrasound imaging—while useful for assessing joint inflammation—relies heavily on the operator’s skill, may perform poorly in individuals with obesity, and presents challenges in evaluating small joints, which can result in missed findings and underestimation of inflammation. Even with the use of Power Doppler, distinguishing between active inflammation and fibrotic tissue remains difficult. Furthermore, although our results suggest that mobilization treatment led to greater improvement than sham therapy in patients with chronic RA (DAS-28 < 5.1), the extent to which these improvements are sustained over the long term remains unclear. Future longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods are recommended to verify the persistence of the observed beneficial effects and to better understand the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

MWM treatments for the hand and wrist may improve quality of life in individuals withRA. MWM appears to improve joint function and reduce pain by increasing joint space, particularly in the MCP and PIP joints, and by decreasing PIP joint inflammation. These results demonstrate the non-invasive effectiveness of MWM in managing RA-related symptoms, offering patients an opportunity to mprove daily function. However, further research is warranted to evaluate the long-term benefits and broader clinical applicability of this approach.
