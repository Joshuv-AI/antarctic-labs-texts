Using leaf and stomatal traits to predict biomass production and water use efficiency in Populus
Authors: Jiaxin Wang, Heidi J Renninger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYClimate change is reshaping ecosystems, driving plants to adapt through leaf‐trait plasticity that reflects strategies for growth and water use. Predicting biomass production and intrinsic water use efficiency (iWUE) remains challenging because of genetic, taxonomic, and environmental variability. Here, we used eastern cottonwood and Populus hybrids as a model system to test whether easily measurable leaf traits can serve as reliable predictors of performance, and whether adding stomatal and biochemical traits improves predictive power. Across two field sites in Mississippi, leaf mass per area (LMA), biomass production, iWUE, leaf area, and foliar nitrogen (N%) differed significantly among taxa and sites, while other traits were conserved. Factorial analysis of mixed data (FAMD) revealed distinct clustering of taxa and sites, indicating coordinated variation among leaf and stomatal traits. Pairwise correlations highlighted fundamental trade‐offs, with biomass positively related to LMA and petiole length but negatively associated with iWUE, N%, and carbon isotopic ratios (δ13C). Leaf temperature and leaf angle varied among taxa and were significantly correlated with LMA and petiole length, suggesting mechanisms of heat dissipation and leaf movability that link simple traits to gas exchange and productivity. Weighted multiple linear regression models explained 80%–91% of variation in biomass production and iWUE. Models using only LMA, petiole length, and stomatal metrics performed nearly as well as those incorporating N%, and δ13C, with complex traits adding approximately 10% explanatory power. These results demonstrate that simple morphological traits capture integrated functional trade‐offs, while complex traits refine predictions. This tiered approach provides an efficient framework for selecting high‐yielding, water‐efficient genotypes of Populus and other hardwood species, offering practical pathways to enhance carbon uptake and iWUE under climate change.Keywords: biomass production, climate change, heat dissipation, leaf boundary layer conductance, leaf functional traits, phenotyping, photosynthesis, stomata arrangement

INTRODUCTION

In response to the pervasive impacts of global climate change, plants are dynamically adjusting their geographical distribution and their temporal patterns of growth and reproduction (Hoffmann & Sgrò, 2011). This adaptive response involves plants modifying their leaf traits through various mechanisms, such as morphological, physiological, behavioral, and ecological adaptations to exhibit plant or leaf‐trait plasticity (Atkin et al., 2015; Keenan & Niinemets, 2016; Reich & Oleksyn, 2004). Variation in leaf traits across different species and taxa in response to environmental changes represents fundamental trade‐offs in resource allocation and biomass investment as described by the leaf economics spectrum (LES) (Pierce et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2004). As a result, leaf traits serve as indicators of plant functional strategies, influencing vegetation properties and offering a means to estimate plant growth and functional performance (Hikosaka et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2023). Leaf functional traits regulate carbon and water cycles through photosynthesis, a process directly influenced by stomatal metrics, plant health, and other leaf characteristics (Harrison et al., 2020; Hassiotou et al., 2010; Poorter & Bongers, 2006). However, predicting plant growth and functional outcomes based on leaf traits is challenging, given the substantial variation observed among different genotypes, species, and environmental conditions (Cui et al., 2020). This challenge becomes more pronounced with climate change, as the past climate has strongly shaped established patterns and associations in leaf traits (Doughty et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2019).

The most studied leaf functional traits include anatomical, physiological, and biochemical characteristics, such as leaf petiole length, leaf size, leaf mass per area (LMA, the inverse of specific leaf area, SLA), leaf lifespan, leaf area, leaf nitrogen content, leaf thickness, leaf dry matter content, and leaf photosynthesis (Nadal et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2017). They play crucial roles in regulating carbon and water balance, aiding plants in acclimating and adapting to the changing climate. For instance, leaf size and leaf area can vary by up to a millionfold across the plant kingdom, and there is a trend of larger leaves in equatorial areas and smaller leaves toward the poles (Díaz et al., 2016; Wright et al., 2017). Larger and thicker leaves, for example, tend to have higher photosynthetic capacity than smaller and thinner leaves, potentially contributing to greater productivity (Niinemets, 2001). However, larger leaves may be less efficient regarding water use and heat dissipation due to their larger boundary layer compared with smaller leaves (Wright et al., 2017). Petioles, the primary conduit for supplying water to leaf veins, play substantial roles in various aspects of plant life, including water transport, leaf temperature regulation, responses to stress, and overall productivity (Blackman et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2021). Depending on the tree species, petiole length can play a large role in leaf and canopy architectural traits, such as leaf angle or inclination, which regulates light interception and carbon gain (Falster & Westoby, 2003). However, these potential underlying mechanisms that explain the relationships between commonly determined leaf traits and biomass production remain relatively underexplored.

Although stomatal density, stomatal size, length, area, leaf vein density, stable carbon isotope (δ13C) and nitrogen isotope (δ15N) ratios are also considered leaf traits, they are studied relatively less frequently. Specifically, stomatal metrics are rarely used to predict plant growth and physiology compared with other leaf anatomical and biochemical traits (Bertolino et al., 2019; Dow et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2021). Stomata regulate gas exchange by controlling the movement of ions (such as K+, Cl−, and malate2−) and water into and out of guard cells to determine their turgor and in turn stomatal aperture (Blatt et al., 2022). Consequently, this intricate process plays a pivotal role in regulating plant gas exchange and water use efficiency (Jezek et al., 2021). Leaf C and N content reflect plant metabolic processes and nutrient status, while δ13C and δ15N isotopes provide insights into water use efficiency (WUE), photosynthetic pathways, and nitrogen cycling within ecosystems (Wang et al., 2021). Traditional stomatal metrics, such as stomatal density and area, are species dependent, influenced by environmental conditions (Casson & Gray, 2008; Cowan, 1978) and have been used to estimate physiological functioning as well as paleoclimate conditions (Poole et al., 1996; Royer, 2001). In turn, incorporating additional stomatal metrics including the ratio of stomatal aperture area to guard cell area, guard cell area, width, and length with a suite of leaf traits could expand the possibilities for estimation of plant growth and physiological performance.

The Populus genus, often referred to as the Arabidopsis of forestry (Taylor, 2002), was the first woody plant to have its genome sequenced, and its relatively modest genome size, extensive molecular genetic maps, and simplicity of genetic transformation methods make it a crucial species for research in this domain (Taylor, 2002). Poplars and cottonwoods, known for their rapid growth, carry significant commercial and ecological value, particularly in terms of providing biomass for bioenergy and climate change mitigation (Deckmyn et al., 2004; Henner et al., 2020). Therefore, predicting the physiological performance and growth of poplar and cottonwood trees using leaf and stomatal traits has the potential to serve multiple purposes, including understanding the role of leaf traits and stomatal metrics in plant biomass production and physiological functioning. Previous studies have shown that leaf traits and stomatal metrics including leaf area and SLA (Marron et al., 2005), maximal individual leaf area (Monclus et al., 2009) and stomatal density (Al Afas et al., 2006) were correlated with biomass production metrics.

Here, we use Populus as a model to investigate how integrating leaf traits and stomatal metrics can predict biomass production and water use efficiency (iWUE). We hypothesize that combining multiple traits, rather than considering them in isolation, explains substantial variation in these outcomes. We focus on how differences among sites and hybrids are reflected in key traits such as LMA, leaf area, petiole length, stomatal metrics, and carbon isotopes, and how these traits contribute to predictive models. By examining trait correlations, site and taxonomic differences, and regression models of varying complexity, we aim to identify both easily measurable traits that serve as reliable proxies and more detailed metrics that add explanatory power. This approach not only clarifies the potential mechanisms linking traits to productivity and WUE in Populus, but also provides a framework for evaluating the trade‐offs between simple and complex trait measurements in predictive models, which has practical implications for rapid phenotyping, sustainable forestry, and crop improvement.

RESULTS

Descriptive results of measured traits

Significant differences in measured traits were observed between Monroe and Pontotoc sites for selected taxa (Table 1). For LMA, D × M exhibited higher values in Monroe (1.10 ± 0.06 g cm−2) than in Pontotoc (0.84 ± 0.04 g cm−2, P < 0.01), while other taxa showed no site effect. Biomass production was greater in Monroe for D × D (17.53 ± 2.11 versus 8.89 ± 1.08 kg, P < 0.01) and D × M genotypes (15.36 ± 1.32 versus 7.58 ± 1.13 kg, P < 0.001). For iWUE, D × D plants had higher values in Pontotoc (34.46 ± 2.48 μmol mol−1) relative to Monroe (21.75 ± 2.63 μmol mol−1, P < 0.01). Leaf area was also larger in Pontotoc for D × M genotypes (209.17 ± 17.47 versus 135.45 ± 13.32 cm2, P < 0.01). Foliar nitrogen concentration (expressed as N%) differed for D × D genotypes, with higher values in Monroe (2.53 ± 0.08%) than in Pontotoc (2.13 ± 0.06%, P < 0.01). All other traits, including petiole length, dry weight, δ13C, and selected stomatal or guard cell anatomical measures, showed no significant differences between sites.

Clustering and correlations in leaf traits and stomatal metrics

FAMD, incorporating leaf morphophysiological and stomatal traits, revealed distinct clustering patterns among taxonomic groups, highlighting variations in leaf and stomatal traits (Figure 1). For example, D × M, T × M, and D × N × M clustered together, while T × D and D × T, as well as D × D and D × N, formed separate groups. Overall, D × D, D × N, D × M, T × M, and D × N × M comprised one broader group, and T × D and D × T formed another. These clusters exhibited significant intra‐ and inter‐group variations. FAMD incorporating biomass production, iWUE, genotype, and site data further categorized genotypes into two groups based on geographic sites (Figure S1). Adding taxonomy as a categorical variable revealed more distinct clusters: T × D, T × M, and D × N × M emerged as separate groups, while D × D, D × N, and D × T formed a larger cluster (Figure S2).

PCA of leaf traits and stomatal metrics showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 45% and 15% of the total variance, respectively (Figure 2). Traits related to stomatal dimensions, including guard cell width, guard cell area, stomatal pore area, stomatal aggregation index, and ratio of stomatal pore to image area, contributed strongly to PC1. Stomatal density and leaf structural traits such as leaf area, petiole length, and dry weight were more closely associated with PC2, together with biomass production. LMA was aligned with stomatal traits and iWUE, while leaf area and petiole length were aligned with biomass production.

Correlations among measured traits

Pairwise correlations among traits showed that biomass production was positively related to LMA (r = 0.48, P < 0.001) and petiole length (r = 0.24, P < 0.01), but negatively with iWUE (r = −0.55, P < 0.001), foliar N% (r = −0.25, P < 0.01), and δ13C (r = −0.19, P < 0.05) (Table 2). LMA correlated positively with biomass but negatively with iWUE (r = −0.52, P < 0.001) and N% (r = −0.37, P < 0.001). Structural traits were strongly associated, with petiole length positively related to leaf area (r = 0.56, P < 0.001) and leaf dry weight (r = 0.55, P < 0.001), while leaf area and dry weight were tightly correlated (r = 0.84, P < 0.001). The iWUE was positively related to δ13C (r = 0.33, P < 0.001) and N% (r = 0.30, P < 0.001). Stomatal and guard cell traits showed weaker associations, with guard cell width negatively related to the ratio of the stomatal pore area to stomatal guard cells (r = −0.28, P < 0.01) and the ratio of the sum of all whole stomatal area to the measured image area strongly negatively correlated with the ratio of stomatal pore area to guard cell area (r = −0.53, P < 0.001).

Leaf temperature exhibited significant variability, ranging from 22°C to 43°C, both within and across different taxonomic groups (Figure 3a). In the morning, the average leaf temperature across all taxonomic groups was between 25°C and 30°C and was 35%–38% lower than temperatures observed at noon and in the afternoon. Among taxonomic groups, D × D had the lowest mean leaf temperature, while T × D displayed the highest mean leaf temperature, of approximately 37°C during the midday and afternoon. Notably, substantial variation in leaf temperature was observed within different taxonomic groups. For instance, the leaf temperature of D × M genotypes varied from around 22 to 40°C in the morning, 26 to 44°C at noon, and 26 to 42°C in the afternoon.

Moreover, we found significant correlations among leaf temperature, leaf petiole length, and LMA (P < 0.001; Figure 3b–d). Specifically, measured petiole length of Populus genotypes ranged from approximately 2–10 cm and clustered into four distinct groups: 2.0–3.0, 3.0–5.3, 5.3–7.5, and 7.5–10.0 cm. Leaf petiole length exhibited a significant and positive correlation with leaf temperature for petioles shorter than 3.0 cm (r = 0.68 and P < 0.001) and longer than 8.0 cm (r = 0.37 and P = 0.001). Furthermore, LMA displayed a significant positive correlation with leaf temperature (r = 0.21, P < 0.001). Notably, significant correlations were observed between leaf petiole length and LMA. Leaf petiole length exhibited a significant negative correlation with LMA (r = −0.45, P < 0.001) when petioles were shorter than 6.0 cm, and a significant positive correlation with LMA (r = 0.58, P < 0.001) when petiole length exceeded 6.0 cm. Finally, leaf angle displayed considerable variation across different taxonomic groups as well as within them and exhibited a significant positive correlation with leaf petiole length (r = 0.56, P < 0.001) (Figure 4).

Modeling iWUE and biomass production

Our model selection outcomes indicate that the most suitable models for predicting biomass production and iWUE in Populus are WMLR models. Among the various leaf traits and stomatal metrics, LMA, petiole length, ratio of stomal pore area to guard cell area, and guard cell minimum width are found in the best fit models (Tables 3 and 4). These models account for a substantial portion of the variability, explaining approximately 80%–91% variability in biomass production and iWUE of Populus. Models are associated with wRMSE ranging from 6% to 21% for biomass production and 8% to 13% for iWUE, respectively.

For estimating biomass production, LMA holds a predominant role, with a relative importance of 37%, and the ratio of stomal pore area to guard cell area demonstrates a significant positive correlation with biomass production, bearing a relative importance of 26%. For the estimation of iWUE, the best fit models included petiole length, LMA, the ratio of the sum of all whole stomata area to the measured image area (the ratio of the sum of all whole_stomata area to the image area), and leaf area. Here, petiole length emerges as a key parameter with a relative importance of 43%, closely followed by LMA with a relative importance of 39%. Furthermore, models that incorporate leaf biochemical traits such as foliar N% and δ13C provide an additional explanatory power of approximately 10% in the variation of biomass production and iWUE in Populus. These models also exhibit a reduction of around 5%–10% in wRMSE when compared with models that solely rely on easily measurable leaf traits and stomatal metrics.

DISCUSSION

Variation in leaf traits across sites and taxa

Our study revealed significant differences in leaf traits among Populus taxa and between two Mississippi field sites, underlining the robustness of our predictive models. Traits such as LMA, biomass production, iWUE, leaf area, and foliar N% varied significantly between sites, whereas other traits—such as petiole length, dry weight, δ13C, and stomatal dimensions, remained stable. This pattern underscores the combined influence of genotype and local environment on trait expression, even under relatively similar climates. A recent study by Renninger et al. (2024) similarly documented low plasticity in LMA and δ13C, while physiological traits and foliar N exhibited greater site‐specific variation across Populus hybrids in Mississippi. Such genotype‐by‐environment interactions have also been reported in broader tree trait research, where site conditions modulated trait correlations and productivity (Blumstein, 2024). These findings enhance the generality of model predictions by confirming that key relationships between traits, biomass, and iWUE hold across different genetic backgrounds and environmental contexts.

The PCA results reinforce the view that leaf structural traits and stomatal metrics form distinct but complementary axes of functional variation. The strong contribution of stomatal density to biomass production and stomatal aggregation index to iWUE indicates the adaptive significance of stomatal distribution patterns as key modulators of plant function. In particular, the alignment of stomatal traits with PC1 (iWUE) implies that Populus hybrids with higher stomatal density or more aggregated stomata may optimize WUE under limiting conditions, potentially through tighter control of transpiration or enhanced responsiveness to environmental cues. Meanwhile, their influence on PC2 (biomass production) points to a dual role in supporting carbon gain when water is abundant. The alignment of LMA and stomatal dimensions with iWUE suggests that thicker or denser leaves with larger leaf stomatal complexes promote WUE, but at the expense of growth. Conversely, the clustering of leaf area, petiole length, and biomass production highlights the importance of canopy architecture and light capture in maximizing productivity. Together with FAMD clustering across taxa and sites, these PCA findings demonstrate that Populus hybrids can be grouped into functional strategies defined by either resource conservation (high iWUE, stomatal investment) or resource acquisition (large leaves, high biomass). This integrated framework strengthens the predictive value of easily measurable traits by linking them mechanistically to gas exchange, carbon gain, and water regulation (Klein et al., 2025; Lawson & Blatt, 2014).

Trait correlations and functional trade‐offs

The correlation matrix (Table 2) revealed consistent relationships among morphological, physiological, and anatomical traits. Biomass production was positively related to LMA and petiole length but negatively related to iWUE, N%, and δ13C, reflecting fundamental trade‐offs between productivity and WUE. Our data showed that leaves with higher LMA tend to have greater leaf temperature (Figure 3). The strong correlation between petiole length, leaf area, and dry weight underscores the functional role of canopy architecture in enhancing light capture and productivity (Buckley et al., 2015); however, more exposed leaves can elevate transpiration, explaining the negative relationship with iWUE. Physiologically, iWUE's positive association with δ13C solidifies the use of isotopic signatures as long‐term indicators of WUE, and its positive correlation with N% reflects enhanced carbon assimilation potential at the cost of increased water loss (Lawson & Blatt, 2014). Recent work also shows that stomatal morphology—like guard cell size and pore dimensions—can predict iWUE and δ13C variation in trees, with larger stomatal features supporting photosynthesis but reducing water efficiency (Petrík et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Together, these correlations underscore why simple morphological traits serve as proxies for complex functional trade‐offs between growth and resource use.

Mechanistic basis of key predictive traits

The observed variation in leaf temperature (22–43°C) and its correlation with petiole length and LMA provide direct evidence of mechanisms underlying trait–function relationships. Shorter petioles were associated with higher midday temperatures, likely due to reduced convective cooling, while longer petioles allow for more leaf movement and fluttering, enhancing leaf separation and airflow. Similarly, LMA was positively correlated with leaf temperature, suggesting that thicker or denser leaves dissipate heat less effectively. Leaf angle also varied across taxa and correlated positively with petiole length, with more vertical leaves reducing self‐shading and allowing thermal regulation. These findings support the idea that leaf movability and heat dissipation are emergent functions of simple morphological traits, helping to explain why petiole length and LMA are strong predictors of both biomass and iWUE. Drake et al. (2013) Lawson and Blatt (2014) demonstrated that stomatal regulation and leaf structural traits interact to balance cooling and carbon assimilation under heat stress, and our results confirm that these mechanisms operate in Populus under field conditions.

Comparison with other predictive approaches

WMLR models explained 80%–91% of variation in biomass and 79%–83% of variation in iWUE. Models based only on easily measured traits (LMA, petiole length) performed nearly as well as those including stomatal pore‐to‐guard cell ratios, guard cell width, N%, and δ13C, with complex traits adding ~10% explanatory power and reducing error by 5%–10%. These results demonstrate that simple morphological traits integrate multiple functional processes, while complex traits refine predictions by capturing finer scale regulation of gas exchange and biochemical allocation. Dillen et al. (2008) found that specific leaf area, leaf nitrogen concentration, and stomatal density together explained only approximately 50%–60% of variability in biomass yield under short‐rotation coppice conditions, with predictive power varying by site and taxon. Similarly, Al Afas et al. (2007) reported that anatomical and morphological traits such as stomatal density, nitrogen concentration, and mesophyll thickness were positively correlated with productivity, but their PCA models accounted for approximately 60% of inter‐genotype variability, emphasizing canopy position and taxonomic grouping rather than direct prediction of yield. Compared with these earlier efforts, our models integrate both simple morphological traits (LMA, petiole length) and finer scale stomatal metrics, enabling stronger predictions across sites and taxa. This suggests that combining easily measurable traits with select anatomical variables provides a more scalable and robust framework for predicting Populus productivity than using either type of trait alone.

Limitations and future directions

Our study was conducted at two sites with contrasting soils and management but broadly similar climates, limiting our ability to test predictive capacity under extreme drought or heat. Populus can grow under much lower precipitation than observed here, and models may not transfer directly to such conditions. Future studies should validate these trait–function relationships under stronger environmental gradients. Additionally, while correlative approaches highlight robust associations, manipulative experiments (e.g., controlled water stress) would be needed to test causal mechanisms.

By showing that simple morphological traits predict biomass and iWUE nearly as well as complex anatomical and biochemical traits, our study provides a tiered framework for Populus or other hardwood species breeding and screening. Easily measurable traits like LMA and petiole length can serve as primary indicators, while stomatal metrics and δ13C can refine predictions where resources allow. This dual‐level approach balances efficiency with accuracy, enabling the identification of genotypes optimized for both productivity and WUE. In the context of increasing water scarcity and climate variability, such predictive frameworks can accelerate the development of resilient, high‐yielding varieties and contribute to sustainable forest and bioenergy management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study sites

Field trials were established at two locations in northeastern Mississippi, USA: Pontotoc County (34°8′18″ N, 88°59′40″ W) and Monroe County (33°51′16″ N, 88°17′28″ W) (Figure 5a). The sites differ in elevation, soils, fertility, and hydrology, providing contrasting environments for evaluating trait–productivity relationships in Populus. “Pontotoc” (110 m a.s.l.) has silt loam soils, pH 5.6–6.5, higher carbon (8.3 mg/g) and nitrogen (1.1 mg/g) contents, and a lower C:N ratio (8.4). “Monroe” (67.7 m a.s.l.) has fine sandy loam soils, pH 4.5–5.5, lower C (5.4 mg/g) and N (0.5 mg/g), and a higher C:N ratio (10.8). Bulk density was similar (1.44 versus 1.46 g/cm3) between sites. Climates were comparable, with mean annual temperatures of 22.3°C at Pontotoc and 22.7°C at Monroe, but precipitation was greater at Pontotoc (1545 mm versus 1378 mm, 30 years average) (Figure 5b). Hydrology also differed, with lower surface discharge at Pontotoc (393 ft3/s) compared with Monroe (1337 ft3/s). It should be noted that Monroe is an alluvial terrace site located about 500 m from the Buttahatchee River whereas Pontotoc is an upland site. These environmental contrasts provide the basis for testing genotype responses (Table 5).

Poplar establishment

The field trials included 168 genotypes representing seven Populus taxa: P. deltoides × P. deltoides (D × D), P. deltoides × P. maximowiczii (D × M), P. deltoides × P. nigra (D × N), P. deltoides × P. trichocarpa (D × T), P. trichocarpa × P. deltoides (T × D), P. trichocarpa × P. maximowiczii (T × M), and P. deltoides × P. nigra × P. maximowiczii (D × N × M) (Table S3 from Wang et al. (2025)). In 2018, 100 genotypes were planted at the Monroe site, and in 2019, 108 genotypes were planted at Pontotoc, with 40 genotypes shared across both sites.

Dormant, rootless cuttings (~23 cm) were procured from GreenWood Resources (Portland, OR, USA) and stored frozen until planting. Before planting, cuttings were soaked in insecticide (Admire Pro, Bayer, Whippany, NJ, USA) for 4 days. Eastern cottonwood (P. deltoides × P. deltoides) cuttings, approximately 41 cm in length due to rooting requirements, were sourced from the Mississippi State University Forestry Department orchard. These were soaked in water and insecticide for 18 h before storage.

Prior to planting, rows at each site were tilled and subsoiled to approximately 46 cm depth (Figure 6). Cuttings were spaced 1.8 m within rows and 2.7 m between rows. Pre‐emergent herbicides (Goal 2XL, Dow AgroSciences LLC; Pendulum 3.3EC, BASF) were applied following planting to control competing vegetation. During the first growing season, both sites were managed under “free‐to‐grow” conditions, which included tilling, manual weeding, and selective herbicide applications (Select, Valent USA LLC, Walnut Creek CA, USA) for bermudagrass (Cynodon dactylon (L.) Pers.).

Coppice management

Beginning after the second year of growth, half of the blocks at each site were managed under a coppice system with a 2‐year rotation. The remaining blocks were maintained as non‐coppiced controls. It is important to note that, in this study, coppiced trees were not included in the regression models used to predict biomass production and iWUE, which were based only on non‐coppiced trees. Instead, coppiced plots provided a practical setting for measuring traits such as leaf angle and leaf temperature, which are easily accessible and serve as proxies for canopy architecture and heat dissipation. These measurements offered complementary insight into trait variation but were not directly linked to biomass or iWUE predictions.

Leaf functional traits

Leaf morphological, biochemical, and physiological traits, such as leaf area, leaf weight, leaf petiole length, leaf C and N content, and carbon and nitrogen isotopic ratios (δ13C and δ15N), were measured during the middle of the growing seasons (July and August) from 2020 to 2022 at the two field sites (from non‐coppiced blocks) using leaf biometric measurements, gas exchange, and mass spectrometry (see below). LMA was calculated using leaf dry weight divided by leaf area (g cm−2). We adopted a random sampling approach, selecting 30 genotypes at Pontotoc and 32 at Monroe site, ultimately yielding a total of 56 distinct genotypes (there are overlaps across two sites). Specifically, the sample comprised 10 genotypes each from the D × M and D × D taxa, five genotypes from each of the D × T and D × N taxa at the Monroe site, and seven genotypes each from D × M and D × D, six genotypes from D × N and T × M, three from D × T, and one from D × N × M at the Pontotoc site (Table S1).

Gas exchange measurements were conducted using a Li‐COR 6400 XTP photosynthesis system (Li‐COR Biosciences Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA) equipped with a 2 × 3 cm leaf chamber and a red–blue LED light source. We selected non‐shaded, fully developed mature leaves from the top canopy for measurement, cut the sub‐branches from trees, and recut them under water before measurement. Stomatal conductance (g s) was continuously monitored during measurements, and data collection was terminated if g s fell below 0.05 μmol m−2 s−1. Measurements were taken between 9:00 am and 1:00 pm local time, with reference humidity maintained at 45%–55%, leaf temperature controlled at approximately 25°C, and chamber light intensity set to 1500 μmol m−2 s−1. Net photosynthetic rate at ambient CO2 concentration (400 ppm) was extracted from the initial measurement and used to calculate iWUE as the ratio of photosynthesis to stomatal conductance, and instantaneous WUE as the ratio of photosynthesis to transpiration. Leaves were collected and kept in a cooler and brought to the lab for further stomatal morphological and biochemical measurements.

To process leaves in the lab, we applied transparent nail polish to 4–6 locations on the abaxial epidermis of the leaves avoiding large leaf veins. After about 5–8 min, dried nail polish was removed from the leaves, placed on pre‐cleaned microscope slides, and covered with one or two coverslips. After collecting nail polish peels, leaves were measured for petiole length, scanned for individual leaf area with petiole using a Li‐COR 3100 leaf area scanner (Li‐COR Biosciences Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA), then oven dried at 65°C for 1 week and weighed. Dried leaves were ground using a mortar and pestle with large leaf veins removed. We sieved the ground leaf powder using a 250 μm mesh, weighed about 4.0 mg of ground leaf powder on an Ion BM‐5 Microbalance (A&D weighting Co., San Jose, CA, USA), and encapsulated it in tin capsules for further elemental analysis. Encapsulated samples were shipped to UC Davis Stable Isotope Facility for total C, N, stable isotope δ13C, and δ15N measurements. Detailed procedures and instruments can be found at the UC Davis Stable Isotope Facility (https://stableisotopefacility.ucdavis.edu/carbon‐and‐nitrogen‐solids, accessed on April 15, 2025).

Nail polish peels were used for leaf stomatal image collection using a compound light microscope (202 281, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital camera (MU300, AmScope, USA) with a 5‐mm lens and a fixed microscope adapter (FMA050, AmScope). Ten unique images were taken at ×200 magnification for each leaf. After collecting stomatal images, we imported the images to StoManager1, an automated and high‐throughput software integrated with multiple deep learning and geometrical and mathematical algorithms, for stomatal metrics measurement (Wang et al., 2024). Specifically, we measured the area, width, and length of the leaf stomatal complex, stomatal pores (inferred from measuring the length and space between inner guard cell walls), and stomatal guard cells. We also measured the ratio of the stomatal pore area to stomatal guard cells (ratio_area_st_gc_mean), the ratio of the sum of all whole_stomata area to the image area (ratio_area_to_img) and leaf stomatal arrangement indices such as stomatal density, stomatal evenness, divergence, and aggregation (Liu et al., 2021). All metrics used for further analysis are the averages measured from at least 10 images of the individual genotype. More details about these stomatal metrics can be found in Wang et al. (2024).

Tree biomass production

Tree height and diameter at breast height (DBH) were measured annually to estimate aboveground biomass using an allometric approach. We harvested all taxa and clones from half of the blocks after the second growing season (coppice management) and measured fresh and dry biomass to construct a universal allometric equation for eastern cottonwood and hybrid Populus. However, because these equations were derived from 2‐year‐old trees, they tended to underestimate the biomass of older trees. To improve accuracy, we therefore adopted the broader allometric equation developed by Dahal et al. (2022), which is based on a destructive sampling method in Mississippi and encompasses multiple year measurements. Although originally developed for eastern cottonwood, their equation performed well on our Populus hybrids (Figure 7).

Leaf temperature and angle

We measured leaf temperature in August 2023 on coppiced blocks (age 5, 1 year old after recent coppice) using a non‐contact digital infrared thermometer (FLUKE‐62 MAX, Fluke Corp, Washington, USA) to evaluate if there were any differences in leaf temperature across different genotypes measured for morphology and anatomy. Coppiced blocks, during the second growing season, were measured since intact leaves were easier to access and measure without destructive sampling. We measured at least 10 leaves including both shaded and sunlit leaves from the bottom to the top of the canopy for each genotype at the Pontotoc site. We bent big stems if the top canopy could not be reached. We measured temperature on a sunny day in the morning (9–10 am), noon (12–1 pm), and afternoon (3–4 pm).

To determine leaf angles, pictures were taken from the side of the target tree on a sunny and non‐windy day, and the camera was held level and as close to perpendicular from the ground as possible. We used ImageJ to measure the angle of leaves in photos of coppiced trees taken after measuring petiole length in situ for at least 10 leaves for genotypes that were measured for leaf temperature (Schindelin et al., 2015). Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.3.1) (R Core Team, 2022). Trait differences among sites and taxa were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis tests, followed by Dunn's post hoc tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons; taxa represented at both sites were additionally compared with t‐tests. To evaluate functional coordination, pairwise Pearson correlations were calculated among all traits, and significance was determined with adjusted P‐values. Since we have both continuous and categorical data, we employed the factorial analysis of mixed data (FAMD) for data description and visualization using FactoMineR (Lê et al., 2008), vcd (Meyer et al., 2020), and factoextra (Kassambara, 2016) packages in R. FAMD is a principal component method used to analyze a data set containing both quantitative and qualitative variables (Pagès, 2004). Package factoextra was used to perform principal component analysis (PCA) to pre‐select leaf and stomatal functional traits that were potentially correlated with Populus dry biomass production and iWUE.

To train the predictive models, we first constructed correlation matrices to identify relationships among traits. Variables significantly correlated with biomass or iWUE were retained as predictors in multiple regression models, with biomass or iWUE as the dependent variable and leaf/stomatal traits—including stable isotopes δ13C and δ15N—as independent variables. Model assumptions of linearity, independence, and homoscedasticity were evaluated by plotting residuals against fitted values, and residual normality was assessed using Q–Q plots. Multicollinearity was checked using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with VIF >6 were removed. Influential observations were identified and handled accordingly.

We employed weighted multiple linear regression (WMLR) because trait data across genotypes and sites exhibited heteroscedasticity, with some taxa contributing greater variance than others; weighting by the inverse of residual variance reduced this imbalance and improved model robustness. Multiple regression was also appropriate because productivity and iWUE are emergent properties shaped by interacting leaf, stomatal, and isotopic traits, and this framework captures trade‐offs and synergies among them. Unlike more complex models, WMLR provides interpretable coefficients that offer direct biological insight into the direction and magnitude of trait effects, while serving as a transparent, parsimonious baseline against which more complex approaches can be compared. We used Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), R 2, and weighted root mean square error percentages (wRMSE) to evaluate and select the best fitted models.
