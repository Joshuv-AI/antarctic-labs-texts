Heritage cracks dataset: Superficial damage
Authors: Maybelin C Garcia-Chiquito, Elia M Alonso-Guzmán, José A Guzmán-Torres, Wilfrido Martínez-Molina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Heritage Cracks dataset contains a total of 1,716 high-resolution images documenting superficial cracking in ignimbrite masonry and lime-based plaster surfaces from heritage buildings in the historic center of Morelia, Mexico, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. A total of 686 images were acquired under real field conditions using smartphone cameras along representative streets and architectural elements with visible deterioration, while 1,030 images were generated through data augmentation. The dataset was designed to support artificial intelligence applications for cultural heritage inspection, particularly object detection and computer vision-based damage assessment. All images were organized, preprocessed, automatically oriented, resized to 640 × 640 pixels, and manually annotated with bounding boxes by specialists in construction pathology. Crack selection considered visible damage associated with weathering, material degradation, structural adjustment, environmental exposure, erosion, overload effects, and biological activity. The dataset is divided into training, validation, and testing subsets; includes image, annotation files, configuration metadata, and documentation to facilitate reproducibility. Its structure allows direct use in deep learning workflows, including YOLO-based models and other object-detection architectures. By providing field-acquired images from real heritage construction systems, this dataset contributes to the development, evaluation, and benchmarking of non-invasive tools for crack detection, preventive conservation, and structural health monitoring. It is useful for researchers, engineers, conservation specialists, and developers working on AI-assisted inspection of vulnerable historic infrastructure in Latin American urban heritage contexts.Keywords: Heritage buildings, Pathology construction, Artificial intelligence, Benchmark dataset

Background

Artificial intelligence has become an increasingly relevant tool for automating inspection [1], monitoring, and damage detection in applications involving civil infrastructure and cultural heritage conservation [2]. Computer vision techniques show strong potential for identifying visible pathologies, including cracks, surface deterioration, material loss, staining, and other degradation [3,4].

Heritage buildings require special attention [5], as their structural and architectural value limit the use of invasive inspection methods [6]. Image-based artificial intelligence offers a non-invasive alternative for periodic monitoring and preventive conservation. The development of reliable computer vision modes, however, depends on the availability of high-quality, well-organized, and properly annotated image datasets [7].

The Heritage Cracks dataset was created to address this need. It focuses on superficial cracking in the masonry of ignimbrite buildings and in lime-based plaster surfaces of heritage buildings in Morelia, Mexico. The dataset provides high-resolution images, standardized preprocessing, and manual annotations for object detection workflows, supporting reproducible research and the development of artificial intelligence for crack detection in heritage construction systems.

Data Description

Image data description

The dataset consists of a total of 1,716 images, of which 686 were captured in the field and 1,030 images were generated through data augmentation performed on the Roboflow platform. This dataset depicts surface cracks in heritage buildings located in Morelia, Michoacán, Mexico. The images were captured under actual inspection conditions and document cracks in ignimbrite masonry and lime-based plaster surfaces.

The workflow implemented in Roboflow can be divided into two stages: preprocessing and data augmentation. The preprocessing stage is applied to all images to standardize image dimensions and remove camera rotation metadata. The purpose of this step is to prevent unintended image rotations during model training and inference. As shown in Fig. 1, these operations correspond to the Resize and Auto-Orient processes.

Workflow diagram illustrating the preprocessing and data augmentation procedures applied to the dataset.

During the augmentation stage, a predefined 3× expansion factor available in the platform was applied to the original training dataset. A random combination of transformations was then applied to each image. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this stage can be divided into two components: the generation of cloned images and the application of augmentation multipliers.

Roboflow performs synthetic image augmentation exclusively on the training subset and subsequently applies a stochastic data augmentation process to the generated images. This approach prevents contamination of the validation and test subsets with artificially generated data. The platform randomly selects images and applies transformations according to the predefined probability associated with each augmentation technique. As a result, each duplicated image receives a unique combination of effects determined by the probability distributions of the applied filters. Consequently, the exact percentage or number of images modified by any specific augmentation technique cannot be determined.

After the application of this workflow, the final dataset consisted of 1,716 images.

All images underwent preprocessing to ensure consistency, including orientation correction using EXIF metadata, metadata removal, and resizing to 640 × 640 pixels, which is a standard dimension for efficient object detection models.

To enhance variability, several augmentations were applied to the images using the Roboflow platform. These included horizontal and vertical flips with a 50% probability to introduce orientation diversity, 90° rotations distributed equally among four possible angles, and random cropping to simulate zoom variations of up to 20%.

Minor rotations ranging from -9° to +9° were used to mimic camera tilts. Random saturation adjustments between −25% and +25% simulated variations in lighting conditions and image appearance. Salt-and-pepper noise, affecting 0.1% of the pixels, was introduced to model image-quality fluctuations. Collectively, these augmentations increase dataset diversity and improve its suitability for robust computer vision models.

Each subset contains the corresponding images and annotation files required for object-detection workflows. Additional files include configuration metadata and documentation to support reproducibility. The folder organization is shown in Fig. 2.

The folder CRACKS_PHONE.v2 contains all images, organized into test (10%), training (75%), and validation (15%) folders. Each folder contains the corresponding image and bounding box annotation files. The “labels” folder contains annotation files in YOLO format. Each image has a corresponding text file (.txt) with the same filename. Each row in a label file represents one annotated crack and follows the structure: class_id x_center y_center width height, where class_id identifies the object category (0 = crack), x_center and y_center correspond to the normalized coordinates of the bounding box center, and width and height represent the normalized dimensions of the bounding box. All values are normalized relative to the image size and range from 0 to 1. An example of the annotation format is shown in Fig. 3. The number of labels assigned to each image depends directly on the number of cracks identified within that image.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Study area and image acquisition route

Image acquisition took place in the historic center of Morelia, Michoacán, Mexico. It began at the Morelia Aqueduct and followed its route toward the Las Tarascas Fountain. The process continued along streets parallel to Avenida Madero—La Corregidora to the south, and Santiago Tapia, Aquiles Serdán, and Melchor Ocampo to the north—as well as along perpendicular streets, including Galeana and Rayón.

These locations were chosen for their high concentration of heritage buildings with visible deterioration, such as surface cracking, weathering, and material degradation. The Santa Rosa de Lima church on Santiago Tapia Street was also included for its architectural and heritage value. In Fig. 6, red lines indicate the streets where images were collected.

Image acquisition area in the historic center of Morelia, Mexico. Red lines indicate the streets where the photographic survey was conducted.

Image acquisition teams

The data collection was carried out by two field teams. Before the image acquisition process, both teams standardized the visual criteria used to identify, classify, and photograph cracks. This ensured consistency in the type of damage recorded and reduced subjectivity during the image acquisition stage.

Criteria for crack selection

A visible crack was included in the dataset if its width was at least 1 mm and it appeared on a heritage building on the selected study area streets, where ignimbrite masonry was the primary construction material. Lime-based coatings over ignimbrite walls were also considered.

Cracks in joints with cement-based mortar were excluded since they did not represent the target material condition. Cracks were included if visual inspection indicated that they were associated with overload, structural adjustment, erosion, weathering, environmental exposure, or the growth of flora or microflora.

Requirements for taking photographs

Images were captured with each smartphone’s rear camera, avoiding flash to prevent artificial reflections, shadows, or overexposure. Brightness settings remained unadjusted to maintain realistic field conditions. Optical zoom was generally avoided, except in fewer than 10% of cases when photographing elevated architectural elements, such as arches or temple walls, where the height limited accessibility.

Image acquisition and processing

High-resolution digital photographs were taken on different days with two smartphones: an OPPO Reno 11 5G, featuring a MediaTek Dimensity processor and a 50 MP + 32 MP + 8 MP camera system, and an iPhone 12 Pro, which has a triple 12 MP camera system and a 3D TOF LiDAR sensor.

Camera angles were adjusted based on the location, height, orientation, and accessibility of each crack, thereby increasing dataset realism by simulating field inspections of heritage buildings. Representative camera parameters were f/1.8 aperture, ISO 80, and a focal length of ∼5 mm. All images were saved in JPG format. The varied acquisition conditions—different surfaces, lighting, crack geometries, angles, and distances—enhance the dataset's suitability for training and evaluating artificial intelligence models.

Data labeling process and classification

All images were manually reviewed and annotated by the authors, who have expertise in construction pathology, damage assessment, and heritage buildings inspection. The labeling process was carried out using the Roboflow platform described in section 3.1 Image Data Description.

The labeling process identified visible surface cracks appropriate for object-detection tasks. Bounding boxes encompassed the full visible extent of each crack, from one visible end to the other, ensuring the crack remained entirely within the box. For branching cracks, the main segment was labeled up to the branching point, and a new bounding box was generated for each branch, providing a precise representation of complex geometries. These annotations are suitable for training object-detection models such as YOLO and other deep learning architectures for visual damage detection.

The number of annotations per image ranges from a single detected crack to as many as nineteen, depending on the characteristics and condition of the image. In Fig. 8, it can be observed that the bounding boxes overlap due to their proximity and the multiple divisions of the cracks. All bounding boxes were assigned to the single class label "cracks", representing the detected crack regions within each image. Limitations

The Heritage Cracks dataset contains images of surface cracks in the masonry and lime-based plaster of heritage buildings in Morelia, Mexico. Although highly representative of the materials and architectural characteristics of this region, it may not fully reflect the variability in other stone, masonry systems, coatings, or geographic environments. Though the dataset includes variations in lighting, distance, camera angle, surface texture, and crack morphology, some photographic parameters are limited by the smartphone cameras used. Consequently, variables such as aperture, ISO, shutter speed, sensor type, and focal length do not cover all conditions found in other devices.

The dataset is intended for visual crack detection and does not contain structural measurements, laboratory test results, crack-depth information, or mechanical characterization. It is best suited for image-based damage detection, computer vision benchmarking, and non-invasive monitoring applications.
