Cortical gradient compression links cognition and transcriptomic signatures in primary angle-closure glaucoma
Authors: Yuanyuan Wang, Xiao Liang, Chen Yang, Chan Xiong, Jie Liu, Lu Sun, Yixin Liu, Wenchao Xiong, Xianjun Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The cerebral cortex is organized along hierarchical gradients that integrate sensory and cognitive functions, yet whether this organization is disrupted in primary angle-closure glaucoma and contributes to cognitive impairment remains unclear. Here we examine cortical functional gradients in individuals with primary angle-closure glaucoma using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging and transcriptomic analyses. We show that patients exhibit compression and reorganization of cortical gradients, particularly within visual and default mode networks, and that these alterations are associated with visual performance and cognitive decline. Genes related to gradient disruption are enriched in synaptic signaling, neurodevelopmental pathways, and astrocyte-associated processes. These findings identify cortical hierarchical remodeling and its molecular correlates in primary angle-closure glaucoma, providing insight into the neural basis of cognitive impairment in this disease.

Introduction

Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG) is one of the most severe forms of chronic glaucoma, characterized by anterior chamber angle closure and impaired aqueous humor outflow, ultimately leading to elevated intraocular pressure, optic nerve damage, and visual field loss1. PACG has a higher prevalence among Asian populations and carries a substantially greater risk of blindness than other types of glaucoma, making it an increasingly significant global public health concern2,3. Beyond ocular manifestations, such as elevated intraocular pressure, visual field loss, and blurred vision, accumulating clinical and neuroimaging evidence suggests that PACG is associated with cognitive decline and neurodegenerative features resembling those observed in Alzheimer’s disease (AD)4,5. Our previous studies have likewise revealed widespread structural and functional brain abnormalities in individuals with PACG6,7. However, the neural mechanisms underlying these complex visual-cognitive impairments remain largely unclear.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has emerged as a key tool for investigating the neural mechanisms of PACG due to its high spatiotemporal resolution and noninvasiveness8,9. Previous studies have reported that PACG patients retain global small-world properties, but show decreased local efficiency, characterized by reduced visual network connectivity and compensatory increases in cognitive-affective regions10,11. Longitudinal findings have shown that network centrality within the visual cortex can partially recover after surgical treatment, suggesting reversibility of visual network damage12. Moreover, disrupted cross-network connectivity13,14and impaired dynamic integration have been observed15,16, reflecting decreased inter-network coordination. However, most previous studies examined large-scale networks at the same analytical level, overlooking the hierarchical organization underlying cortical integration, thereby limiting a deeper understanding of the neural mechanisms of cognitive impairment in PACG.

The hierarchical organization of the cerebral cortex is considered a fundamental principle of human information processing, enabling sensory information to be progressively integrated into higher-order cognitive regions17,18. Resting-state fMRI-based gradient analysis provides a macroscopic framework for characterizing this hierarchical structure. The principal gradient typically reflects a functional transition from unimodal sensorimotor regions to transmodal default mode network (DMN) areas, serving as a core axis of cognitive hierarchy19,20. Gradient abnormalities have been reported in various neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease21,22, dementia23, schizophrenia24, and major depressive disorder25, often manifesting as gradient compression or reorganization associated with cognitive deficits. Moreover, functional gradients are tightly coupled with spatial patterns of gene expression, and transcriptomic features can largely account for cortical functional differentiation26,27. Genetic factors also play a critical role in the pathogenesis of PACG, with family history being a well-established risk factor28,29. In addition, recent transcriptomic neuroimaging studies have identified genes related to ion channel regulation and extracellular matrix composition that are associated with spontaneous brain activity in PACG30. Therefore, integrating functional gradient mapping with transcriptomic analysis may provide novel insights into how microscale molecular processes shape macroscale cortical reorganization.

In summary, this study employed resting-state MRI to investigate cortical functional gradient alterations in patients with PACG and integrated cognitive assessments with whole-brain gene expression analyses to explore the neurobiological mechanisms underlying cognitive impairment in PACG. We found significant cortical gradient compression and reorganization, primarily involving the visual and DMNs, which were closely associated with visual and cognitive performance. Transcriptomic analyses further revealed enrichment of gradient-related genes in synaptic signaling and neurodevelopmental pathways. These findings suggest that disrupted cortical gradients are associated with altered hierarchical functional organization in PACG and provide preliminary molecular annotations that may be relevant to cognitive impairment.

Global functional gradient features

To enhance interpretability, we focused on the first two functional gradients, as they explained the largest proportion of variance in the functional connectivity matrix (Supplementary Fig. S1). Functionally, the principal gradient (gradient 1) typically reflects the dominant hierarchical axis of cortical organization, extending from primary sensory and motor regions to higher-order transmodal areas, such as the DMN. In contrast, the secondary gradient (gradient 2) captures a complementary axis of functional differentiation, often separating visual and sensorimotor systems from limbic and associative networks. At the group level, patients with PACG exhibited spatial displacement in both principal and secondary gradients relative to HCs (Fig. 2a). Quantitative analyses further revealed spatial compression of the principal and secondary gradients in the PACG group, reflected by a narrower distribution of gradient values in the histograms and a contracted point cloud in the joint gradient space. (Fig. 2b, c). Moreover, PACG patients exhibited significantly reduced gradient range (T = −2.281, P = 0.024, Cohen’s d = −0.393) and standard deviation (T = −2.697, P = 0.008, Cohen’s d = −0.464) in the principal gradient, as well as reduced gradient range (T = −3.358, P = 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.577) and standard deviation (T = −2.470, P = 0.015, Cohen’s d = −0.425) in the secondary gradient compared with HCs (Fig. 2e). Cortical regions-level and network-level functional gradient

We performed ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analysis, including age, sex, and education as covariates, to assess cortical region-level differences in gradient values between the PACG and HC groups. The OLS results revealed significant alterations across 78 cortical regions in both the principal and secondary gradients, spanning seven large-scale brain networks: visual network (VIS), somatomotor network (SMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), salience network (SAL), limbic network (LIM), frontoparietal control network (FPC), and DMN. All comparisons were corrected for multiple testing using the BH-FDR method (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

The network-level gradient alterations showed high spatial consistency with the cortical regional findings. Compared with HCs, patients with PACG exhibited significantly increased gradient 1 values in the VIS (t = 4.270, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.739) and decreased values in the SAL (t = −2.406, P = 0.041, Cohen’s d = −0.416) and FPC (t = −2.661, P = 0.031, Cohen’s d = −0.460). More pronounced differences were observed in gradient 2, where PACG patients showed significantly reduced gradient values in the VIS (t = −2.718, P = 0.010, Cohen’s d = −0.471)and DOR (t = −3.101, P = 0.008, Cohen’s d = −0.536) and increased gradient values in the SMN (t = 2.814, P = 0.010, Cohen’s d = 0.487), LIM (t = 4.023, P = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.696), and DMN (t = 2.88, P = 0.010, Cohen’s d = 0.499). All comparisons were corrected for multiple testing using the BH-FDR method (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

Effects of clinical factors

Partial Spearman correlation analyses revealed significant associations between gradient metrics and clinical measures in the PACG group. The principal gradient value of the VIS was negatively correlated with VA (r = −0.337, Pcorr = 0.032), whereas the gradient range, standard deviation, and the secondary gradient value of the VIS were positively correlated with VA (r = 0.366, Pcorr = 0.015; r = 0.378, Pcorr = 0.012; r = 0.452, Pcorr = 0.001). In addition, the secondary gradient value of the DMN was negatively correlated with MoCA scores (r = −0.457, Pcorr = 0.001). Cortical gene expression related to regional changes in the gradient

In the PACG cohort, the first PLS component (PLS1) explained the largest proportion of variance in the principal gradient and captured the dominant spatial pattern of gene expression across cortical regions. We therefore focused on PLS1 as the primary transcriptomic signature for downstream pathway and cell-type annotation, whereas results for the secondary gradient are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2. PLS1 revealed a gene expression pattern highly expressed in the occipital cortex and closely aligned with the first functional gradient (Fig. 4a, b). A significant spatial correlation was observed between the PLS1-weighted gene expression map and the t-value map of the principal gradient (r = 0.598, P = 0.001), which remained robust after controlling for spatial autocorrelation (Fig. 4c). Bootstrap resampling identified 3993 genes contributing to the regional variability associated with the principal gradient (Fig. 4d). Among them, 1852 genes, most prominently LRRC49, were positively correlated with the principal gradient values (PLS1⁺; Z > 4.66, all P < 0.05, Bonferroni corrected) (Fig. 4e), whereas 2141 genes, led by SSTR1, were negatively correlated (PLS1−; Z < −4.66, all P < 0.05, Bonferroni corrected) (Fig. 4f). Assigning PACG-related genes to cell types

In the principal gradient of the PACG cohort, cell-type enrichment analysis revealed distinct expression profiles for PLS⁺ and PLS− genes. PLS⁺ genes were specifically enriched in granule neurons of the cerebellum and cholinergic motor neurons of the spinal cord (Fig. 5c). In contrast, PLS⁻ genes showed broad enrichment across multiple neuronal and glial populations, including cortical deep-layer (L5–L6) neurons, striatal D1⁺/D2⁺ spiny projection neurons, cholinergic neurons in the habenula, basal forebrain, and striatum, serotonergic neurons in the brainstem, and astrocytes in both the cortex and cerebellum (Fig. 5d).

Validation analyses

The results of the validation analyses supported the robustness of our findings. First, covariate-adjusted analyses controlling for global cortical thickness and mean global functional connectivity yielded global-, network-, and regional-level gradient patterns that were highly consistent with those of the primary analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 8–12). Second, network-level analyses based on the Schaefer-400 parcellation with Yeo-17 network assignment demonstrated gradient alterations largely concordant with the main results (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Tables 13, 14). Finally, analyses repeated using the Schaefer-200 parcellation with Yeo-7 networks showed directionally consistent global and network-level gradient effects (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Tables 15–19).

Discussion

This study provides evidence of large-scale cortical functional gradient alterations observed at the cross-sectional level in patients with PACG, characterized by hierarchical compression of the principal and secondary gradients and topographic displacement across several key functional networks, including the VIS, FPC, and DMN. These gradient metrics were closely associated with visual impairment and cognitive dysfunction, reflecting alterations in cortical hierarchical organization. Furthermore, integrative analyses combining gene expression and transcriptomic data revealed that this hierarchical reorganization pattern is supported by specific molecular pathways and cell-type mechanisms, providing a potential biological basis for the observed cortical hierarchy alterations in PACG.

In this study, patients with PACG demonstrated marked spatial compression of global cortical functional gradients, reflecting disrupted hierarchical organization between lower-level sensory processing and higher-order cognitive integration31,32. Within the gradient framework, the degree of differentiation between functional regions is closely related to their spatial distance on the cortical surface, and long-range connections between unimodal and transmodal areas form the basis of efficient information processing33,34. The significantly reduced gradient range and standard deviation of both the principal and secondary gradients in PACG indicate a more compact functional distribution along both hierarchical axes, with diminished inter-regional differentiation that may impair the brain’s capacity for integration and segregation, thereby disrupting cognitive function35,36. Moreover, the reduced range and standard deviation of the secondary gradient were positively correlated with VA, indicating that greater gradient compression was associated with poorer visual function. These findings point to a preliminary association between visual impairment and global hierarchical compression in PACG. However, given the cross-sectional design and the modest sample size, it remains unclear whether this relationship reflects downstream consequences of chronic visual pathway dysfunction or other disease-related factors. Longitudinal and adequately powered studies are warranted to clarify directionality and temporal dynamics.

At both the regional and network levels, patients with PACG exhibited widespread gradient alterations, indicating that the disease involves large-scale disruption of hierarchical brain organization rather than localized abnormalities. Given the strong functional heterogeneity across cortical regions, regional results may show local fluctuations that hinder systematic interpretation. Therefore, we summarized the mean gradient characteristics across seven large-scale functional networks to capture system-level alterations. Along the principal gradient, PACG showed higher mean gradient values in the VIS and lower values in the SAL and FPC networks, indicating altered relative hierarchical positioning among functional systems37,38. Moreover, the principal gradient value of the VIS was negatively correlated with VA, possibly because this upward shift reduced the region’s sensitivity to basic visual input and impaired visual information processing efficiency. However, this association should be interpreted cautiously and requires replication in larger cohorts. Such hierarchical misalignment may also disrupt the normal cascade of information processing, weakening effective interactions between perceptual and regulatory systems39. Consistently, Li et al. reported that Parkinson’s disease patients exhibited greater susceptibility to attentional deficits, and perceptual disturbances when visual system activity was abnormally enhanced, and prefrontal activity was reduced40. Similarly, Xie et al. found in end-stage renal disease that dominant activity within the perceptual system may indicate decreased efficiency in processing external information41.

Along the secondary gradient, patients with PACG exhibited a more pronounced pattern of network-level alterations, characterized by significantly reduced gradient values in the VIS and DOR, accompanied by increased values in the SOM, LIM, and DMN. The decreased gradient values in VIS and DOR suggest a diminished hierarchical role of external visual and attentional processing pathways, potentially reflecting their reduced relative positioning within the global information-processing architecture42. This interpretation is supported by correlation analyses showing that reduced gradient values in VIS were associated with poorer VA. In contrast, the elevated gradient values in SOM and LIM may indicate redistribution toward somatosensory and emotional systems in the context of visual impairment43. The DMN, a core network highly active during resting states, has been implicated in cognitive dysfunction across neurodegenerative conditions44,45, and plays a central role in self-referential cognition46. Prior work in PACG has reported reduced within-DMN functional connectivity associated with cognitive impairment47. In the present study, DMN secondary-gradient values were significantly elevated, suggesting a relatively more transmodal hierarchical profile. Prior studies indicate that excessive predominance or coupling of transmodal hubs, such as the DMN may constrain network flexibility and cross-network integration48,49. Consistently, higher DMN secondary-gradient values were associated with lower MoCA scores, linking DMN hierarchical profile to global cognitive performance in PACG.

Functional gradient alterations in PACG may reflect multiple contributing factors, including molecular and cellular processes. Using imaging-transcriptomic approaches, researchers have begun to provide molecular context for large-scale brain connectivity organization by linking spatial neuroimaging patterns to regional gene-expression profiles30. We found that the weighted gene-expression pattern of the first PLS component showed spatial correspondence with the principal-gradient alteration map, with higher weights in the occipital cortex. GO/KEGG analyses suggested that PLS− genes were enriched in synaptic signaling, ion transport, and lipid metabolism50,51, whereas PLS+ genes were enriched in metal ion transport and ABC transporter pathways. Notably, these enriched processes broadly relate to neuronal communication and metabolic/transport functions, offering a plausible biological context for large-scale hierarchical organization. Cell-type enrichment analyses further provided convergent, hypothesis-generating support for this transcriptomic pattern. Together, these findings offer preliminary molecular annotations for macroscale hierarchical differences in PACG. More detailed mechanistic interpretations and pathway-level considerations are provided in the Supplementary discussion.

This study has several limitations. First, it employed a cross-sectional design with a relatively limited sample size. Future large-scale longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings and further investigate the dynamic evolution of the principal gradient during the progression of PACG. Second, given the sample size, brain–behavior correlations should be interpreted conservatively, as reproducible brain-wide association effects often require substantially larger samples52. Third, cognitive function was assessed solely using the MoCA, which reflects overall cognitive performance but has limited sensitivity to subtle or domain-specific impairments. Finally, the imaging-transcriptomic analysis relied on publicly available AHBA data from healthy post-mortem donors rather than the PACG cohort; because transcriptomic and imaging data were not obtained from the same individuals, interindividual variability in gene expression may limit direct inference regarding PACG-specific molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (no. IIT2024-283). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Participants

Patients with PACG were recruited between 2017 and 2024 from the Department of Ophthalmology, First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, based on the diagnostic criteria of the European Glaucoma Society guidelines53. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) gonioscopic evidence of angle closure (Shaffer grade 0–2); (b) intraocular pressure (IOP) greater than 21 mmHg; (c) visual field defects consistent with glaucoma; and (d) no prior glaucoma treatment. Exclusion criteria comprised: (a) secondary glaucoma (caused by endophthalmitis, ocular trauma, or corticosteroid exposure); (b) other ocular diseases; (c) systemic conditions, such as hypertension or diabetes; (d) neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease or Parkinson’s disease; (e) incomplete MRI or clinical data; and (f) contraindications to MRI. Ultimately, 66 patients with PACG were included (25 males, 41 females). In addition, 69 healthy controls (HCs) matched for age, sex, and education (25 males, 44 females), all right-handed and free from ocular, systemic, or neurological disorders, were enrolled as the control group.

Clinical data acquisition

All subjects received a detailed ophthalmologic examination, which included: (a) slit-lamp gonioscopy to evaluate the anterior chamber angle; (b) intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement with Goldmann applanation tonometry; (c) spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) for structural assessment of retinal nerve fiber layer thickness (RNFLT), average and vertical cup-to-disc ratios (A–C/D and V–C/D), and anterior chamber depth (ACD); and (d) functional evaluation, consisting of mean visual acuity (VA) and visual field mean deviation (MD) obtained using standard automated perimetry (Humphrey Field Analyzer, 24-2 protocol). Cognitive performance was assessed using the MoCA. Disease duration was defined as the interval from the first confirmed diagnosis of PACG to the time of clinical evaluation.

MRI data acquisition

Resting-state fMRI data were acquired at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Jiangxi, China) using a 3.0T Siemens scanner (Erlangen, Germany) equipped with an 8-channel phased-array head coil. During scanning, participants were instructed to remain still with their eyes closed, to stay awake, and to avoid specific thoughts or head movements. Functional images were collected over approximately 8 min, yielding 240 volumes, with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view (FOV) = 200 × 200 mm², matrix = 64 × 64, slice thickness = 4 mm, and interslice gap = 1.2 mm. 3D T1-weighted images were acquired using a three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.26 ms, flip angle = 9°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm², matrix = 256 × 256, 176 sagittal slices, and slice thickness = 1 mm.

Data preprocessing

The dicm2nii toolbox implemented in microGL was used to convert raw DICOM files into the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) format. Preprocessing of rs-fMRI and T1-weighted images was carried out using fMRIPrep 24.1.0rc254. For the structural data, T1-weighted images were first corrected for intensity inhomogeneity and subjected to skull stripping. Tissue segmentation was then performed on the brain-extracted images to delineate cerebrospinal fluid, white matter, and gray matter, followed by cortical surface reconstruction using the FreeSurfer recon-all pipeline. Spatial normalization to the ICBM 152 Nonlinear Asymmetrical template (2009c) was achieved through nonlinear registration of brain-extracted T1 images to the template. For functional data, head motion estimates were obtained relative to the BOLD reference, yielding transformation matrices and six parameters of translation and rotation. Functional images were co-registered to the corresponding T1-weighted images via boundary-based registration with six degrees of freedom, and time series were projected onto the fsaverage5 surface. Confound regression incorporated 36 nuisance regressors, including head motion parameters, mean global signal, mean signals from white matter and cerebrospinal fluid, their temporal derivatives, and quadratic terms. Finally, a band-pass filter (0.01–0.1 Hz) and surface-based spatial smoothing (6 mm FWHM) were applied to the functional data.

Connectome gradient construction

We generated individual connectome gradients for each participant using the BrainSpace toolbox (https://github.com/MICA-MNI/BrainSpace)55. For each participant, the mean time series of all brain parcels was extracted according to the Schaefer 400 atlas, and a 400 × 400 functional connectivity matrix was constructed using Pearson correlations. Each brain region’s connectivity profile was expressed as a vector, and cosine similarity was computed between pairs of regional vectors to produce a 400 × 400 similarity matrix. These similarity values were subsequently transformed into angular distances and normalized. Individual gradients were then derived using the diffusion embedding approach19,56, a nonlinear dimensionality reduction method that projects high-dimensional connectivity data into a low-dimensional space. Subject-specific gradients were computed separately for each participant from their own connectivity data. In this framework, each parcel’s gradient value represents its relative position along a continuous axis of similarity in whole-brain connectivity profiles, rather than an activation magnitude. The resulting functional gradients were ordered by explained variance, with higher components accounting for progressively less variance. To obtain a stable and unbiased reference space for gradient alignment, a group-level gradient template was computed from an independent functional connectivity dataset derived from healthy participants (i.e., the Schaefer 400 “holdout” group-level connectivity matrix provided by the BrainSpace toolbox). Individual gradients were subsequently aligned to this reference template using iterative Procrustes alignment (100 iterations).

Functional gradients estimation

In this study, three global gradient metrics were calculated: gradient range, standard deviation, and explained ratio. The gradient range represents the difference between the maximum and minimum values, reflecting the overall dispersion of the gradient distribution. The standard deviation quantifies the variability of gradient values relative to the mean, indicating the spread of the gradient distribution. Spatial compression was quantified as a reduction in the dispersion of gradient values, indexed by decreased gradient range and/or standard deviation. The explained ratio denotes the proportion of total variance in the connectivity structure accounted for by each gradient component, with higher values indicating greater explanatory power in characterizing brain functional organization.

The first two functional gradient components were retained for subsequent analyses. Functional gradients were computed at the parcel level using the Schaefer 400 cortical parcellation. According to the Yeo 7-network template57, the 400 cortical parcels were subsequently assigned to seven canonical brain networks. These network labels were used for network-level summarization and interpretation of the parcel-wise gradient results, allowing comparisons at both the regional and network levels. For each participant and each gradient component, network-level mean gradient values were computed by averaging parcel-wise gradient scores within each network, reflecting the network’s relative position along the gradient axis.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses of clinical variables and assessment scales (including age, sex, and education) were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median with interquartile range. Group differences were assessed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables, and the chi-square test for categorical data.

Group differences in gradient patterns between PACG patients and HCs were assessed using OLS regression, with group coded as PACG = 1 and HCs = 0, and age and sex as covariates. Positive t-values indicate higher gradient values in PACG compared with HCs, whereas negative t-values indicate lower values in PACG. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, and multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR-BH) method.

To further investigate the associations between cortical gradient values and clinical features in PACG, Spearman correlation analyses were performed, with multiple testing controlled using the FDR-BH method. Clinical variables included disease duration, ophthalmic measures (VA, IOP, RNFL, A/C–D, V/C–D, ACD and MD), as well as MoCA scores.

To evaluate the robustness of the primary gradient findings, we conducted three validation analyses. First, a covariate-adjusted validation was performed by additionally controlling for age, sex, education, global cortical thickness, and mean global functional connectivity; connectome gradients were re-estimated after adjustment and group comparisons were repeated. Details of cortical thickness and functional connectivity estimation are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Second, we examined the stability of network-level results under an alternative network definition by assigning the Schaefer-400 parcels to the Yeo-17 network framework and recomputing network-level gradient metrics. Third, we repeated the analyses using the Schaefer-200 parcellation with Yeo-7 network assignment to assess robustness to parcellation resolution. Across all validation analyses, gradient estimation, statistical testing, multiple-comparison correction, and effect size calculation followed the same procedures and thresholds as in the primary analysis.

Estimation of regional gene expressions

The Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA; http://human.brain-map.org) provides a resource for investigating the spatial correspondence between cortical gradients and transcriptomic patterns. In this study, we used gene expression data obtained from six adult postmortem brains (mean age 42.50 ± 13.38 years; 5 males and 1 female), covering 3702 anatomical samples (Supplementary Table 1). Data preprocessing followed the standardized workflow proposed by Arnatkevičiūtė et al.58 and included the following procedures: (1) re-annotating probes to genes with the Re-annotator tool; (2) discarding probes whose signal intensity did not exceed background levels in at least 50% of samples; (3) retaining probes most strongly correlated with RNA-seq data; (4) assigning samples to the nearest Schaefer-400 parcel centroid within a 2 mm distance; (5) normalizing expression values for each donor using a scaled robust sigmoid transformation; and (6) selecting genes based on differential stability across donors. Because of limited sampling in the right hemisphere (only two donors), all analyses were restricted to the left hemisphere. Regional expression values were then averaged across samples within each parcel, yielding a final expression matrix comprising 200 regions and 15,631 genes.

Partial least squares analysis

Partial least squares (PLS) regression was applied to examine the association between the spatial pattern of between-group cortical gradient alterations (PACG-HCs) and regional gene expression59,60. The resulting PLS components were interpreted as transcriptomic patterns that spatially covary with the between-group gradient alteration map. After preprocessing of the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) data, a regional expression matrix of 200 parcels × 15,631 genes was obtained. Parcels with missing gene expression values were excluded, resulting in 195 cortical regions used for analysis. The gene expression matrix served as the predictor (X), and the parcel-wise t-statistic map derived from the PACG-HCs contrast for each gradient component served as the response variable (y). PLS models with up to five components were estimated using the R pls package, and both cumulative and incremental R² values were calculated to quantify explained variance. To account for spatial autocorrelation, spin-based spherical permutations (5000 iterations) were used to generate null distributions of incremental R² for component-wise significance testing61. The first significant component (α = 0.05) was selected; if no component reached significance, the one with the lowest permutation p-value was reported. For the target component, gene weights were extracted and their stability assessed via bootstrap resampling of regions (1000 iterations), yielding standard errors and Z-scores. Gene-level significance was determined using Bonferroni correction, and genes were categorized into PLS+ (positively weighted) or PLS− (negatively weighted) groups according to the sign of their Z-scores for downstream enrichment analyses.

Enrichment analysis

Enrichment analyses were used to translate PLS-derived gene sets from the PACG-HCs contrast into biologically interpretable pathways and processes. Functional enrichment analysis of PLS1+ and PLS1− gene sets was performed using Metascape (https://metascape.org), which integrates data from over 40 biological knowledge bases. Significance was determined using the platform’s default thresholds (P < 0.01, overlap ≥ 3, enrichment factor > 1.5), with multiple comparisons corrected using the FDR-BH method. Enriched terms were clustered based on similarity, and the most representative term from each cluster was reported.

Assigning PACG-related genes to cell types

Cell-type assignment was performed to characterize which major cortical cell classes were preferentially implicated by the gradient-associated gene signature. This analysis was conducted using the online cell-type- specific expression analysis tool (http://doughertytools.wustl.edu/CSEAtool.html), which provides reference cell-type gene sets derived from multiple published single-cell transcriptomic datasets of human cortical postmortem tissue62. The integrative reference framework helps mitigate potential biases introduced by heterogeneity across sequencing platforms and analytic pipelines, and enables systematic evaluation of enrichment patterns across a broad set of annotated cortical cell populations.

Conclusion

In summary, PACG is characterized by widespread cortical functional gradient alterations, manifested as hierarchical compression and shifts in the relative positioning across networks, which is closely associated with visual and cognitive impairments. Transcriptomic analyses further provided preliminary molecular annotations for the spatial pattern of these gradient alterations. Together, these results offer new clues toward the neural basis of PACG-related cognitive impairment and motivate future studies integrating longitudinal imaging with patient-level molecular measures.
