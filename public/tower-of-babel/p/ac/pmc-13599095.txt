Principles for medical students’ responsible use of generative AI: a student-partnered Delphi study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to the widespread use of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) in undergraduate medical education (UGME), this study aimed to develop a set of consensus-based principles for the responsible and ethical use of GenAI, established by medical students and faculty and further validated by international medical and artificial intelligence (AI) experts.MethodsA four-round modified Delphi process, an in-person idea-generation round, two structured rating rounds, and an external expert validation round were conducted between May 2025 and February 2026. Background

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), particularly large language models (LLMs), is rapidly changing education [1, 2] and healthcare [3]. In medical education, GenAI tools are used for tasks such as explaining complex concepts, creating practice questions, and summarizing notes [4, 5]. The potential benefits of artificial intelligence (AI) include personalized tutoring, feedback, and learning support, must be weighed against limitations that are particularly important in medical training, such as hallucinations, bias, and risks to professional standards [4, 6, 7].

Survey and qualitative studies report that many students are willing to use LLMs for learning support and perceive them as having educational value [4, 8, 9]. However, they also recognize ethical and practical risks, including inaccurate outputs, privacy and data security concerns, and uncertainty about proper academic and clinical use [4, 10, 11]. This combination of high acceptance with acknowledged risks highlights the need for clear guidance that promotes beneficial AI use while safeguarding learners, patients, and medical institutions [12, 13].

Evidence suggests that policies and training programs at medical schools have not kept pace with advances in GenAI [14, 15], often shifting responsibility for ethical and appropriate use to individuals rather than institutions [16]. For example, Ichikawa and colleagues’ survey of GenAI policies and training at U.S. osteopathic medical schools highlights limited preparedness and variability, reinforcing the need for clearer, systemic guidance for students and faculty [17]. Consequently, there is a growing consensus in the literature that medical schools should explicitly define principles and institutional policies for the responsible use of GenAI, supported by governance and curricula [17–19]. Triola and Rodman, for instance, recommend that institutions (1) establish clear policies for appropriate GenAI use, including protecting institutional and patient data, (2) create a local governance body that includes students to define safe and ethical use and oversee evolving tools, and (3) specify learner competencies and develop a curriculum to teach responsible use [20].

Overall, the rapid uptake of GenAI among medical students, the acknowledgment of risks to professional development, and the lag between technological progress and institutional policy highlight the need to develop and validate principles for the use of GenAI in medical education. Ideally, this process should involve learners as partners and include expert reviewers to ensure relevance, feasibility, and alignment with professional standards [17, 20].

Our research aimed to establish consensus-driven principles for the ethical and responsible use of GenAI, written specifically by medical students for their peers and validated by international experts in medical education and AI. This study is part of a larger effort to incorporate AI into our university’s curriculum [21, 22], employing a co-design approach that involves collaboration between students and faculty members [23].

Methods

Study context and student-faculty partnership

Given the key role of medical students as stakeholders in this domain, we used a co-design model that included medical students alongside educators and experts [21]. This approach addresses the gap in education policymaking by prioritizing the ‘user-as-expert’ perspective, supported by the Students as Partners (SaP) model [24, 25]. By centering the initial drafting on students’ perspectives, we aimed to mitigate power asymmetries often found in traditional top-down guideline development, ensuring the resulting principles are not only pedagogically sound but also practically resonant with the digital-native learner population [26].

From the initial cohort of first-year medical students (2023–2024 academic year), 20 volunteers, ranging from non-users of AI to early adopters, were purposively recruited. These students were central to the initiative’s design, receiving longitudinal coaching from two experienced medical educators (JS and JP) and collaborating within specialized research subgroups to execute the four interrelated studies in the project, including the current Delphi study.

Beyond the primary research objectives, this initiative provided a structured platform to cultivate student scholarship. This overarching project encompassed a scoping review of the landscape of AI in UGME [18], a qualitative exploration of students’ perceptions of AI [4], and two distinct Delphi studies; one focused on the identification of AI-related competencies and their associated learning objectives (Simoni et al., submitted for publication DOI: https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-7905807/v1) and the other, this study, on identifying GenAI principles of use.

To ensure informed participation from the beginning of the project, a foundational phase was implemented to establish a baseline of AI knowledge across the student-faculty cohort. This preparatory process included expert-led seminars and a structured journal club focused on critical appraisals of key literature in medical education and AI. These orientation activities, plus the scoping review [18], informed the development of the ethical principles described here. Furthermore, just prior to starting this Delphi study, the team attended a presentation by an AI ethics expert and a workshop in which several frameworks identified through previous activities were reviewed and critically appraised. These included the UNESCO guidance for GenAI in education [27], the WHO guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health [28], the Government of Canada Guide on the use of GenAI [29], the European Union’s Ethical guidelines on the use of AI in data in teaching and learning for educators [30], UNESCO ChatGPT and AI in Higher Education [31], World Economic Forum AI Guidance responsible use in education [32], University of Navarra Guide on GenAI [33], Association of American Medical Colleges Principles for the Responsible Use of AI in and for Medical Education [34], and two papers on the Ethical Use of AI in Health Professions Education: AMEE Guide No 158 [15] and Preparing for Artificial General Intelligence in Health Professions Education: AMEE Guide No.172 [35].

Study design and overview

The research team employed a modified, four-round Delphi study. Round 1 took place in May 2025, followed by three subsequent rounds conducted between June 2025 and February 2026 (Fig. 1). Distinctively, this study utilized a co-creation model, where medical students served as the primary authors of the principles, which were subsequently refined and validated by an international panel of experts in AI medical education, AI ethics, AI technology education, or senior medical educators involved with AI curricula.

Delphi study overview

A Delphi method was chosen because it uniquely gathers expert judgment and identifies agreement and disagreement. The four-round structure was chosen deliberately: a qualitative idea-generation round (Round 1) to ground the principles in students’ own language and experience, two structured rating rounds (Rounds 2 and 3) to test and stabilize consensus under predefined decision rules, and a final external validation round (Round 4) in which a separate, multinational expert panel appraised the near-final framework. This arrangement combines the conventional two-survey Delphi core with qualitative generation and includes an additional external validation phase. The objective of each round is stated at the start of the corresponding subsections below. The conduct and reporting of this study follow the DELPHISTAR recommendations [36], and the complete checklist of which is provided (Supplement 1).

Participants

The participants in the first three rounds were a 15-member investigator team comprising 13 students from the larger initial 20-student research group (who had, in the interim, progressed to the second year of studies) and two medical educators (JS and JP). The latter led the overarching project to incorporate AI into the medical curriculum discussed above. No separate cohort of outside educators or students was recruited for Rounds 1 to 3. The student researchers were a combination of Spanish and international students enrolled in the university’s English-language international track. Together, the 15-member investigator team represents eight countries: the United States (n = 2), Canada (n = 1), Mexico (n = 1), Poland (n = 1), Spain (n = 6), Switzerland (n = 1), Austria (n = 2), and China (n = 1). The deliberate 13:2 student-to-faculty ratio operationalizes the Students-as-Partners co-design model [24, 26], in which students served as the primary architects of the principles given their stakeholder role. As described by Niederberger et al., students can be considered “experts” or “participants” [36].

The final 4th round comprised an expert panel. They included medical educators at our university (n = 2) who were not part of Rounds 1–3, international medical educators (n = 2), AI business or ethics educators (n = 4), and AI technology educators (n = 1) from Hungary, Argentina, the Philippines, Indonesia, Kenya, the United States, and Mexico, providing at least one expert from each of the five United Nations (UN) regions outlined in the Governing AI for Humanity [37]. ‘Expert’ was defined a priori through a structured, eligibility framework under which each panelist had to satisfy at least one of four verifiable qualifying routes: (A) AI scholarship - peer-reviewed AI or applied-AI publications, formal AI roles, or AI-focused teaching, including AI ethics and governance; (B) applied AI-in-education practice - designing or delivering AI-related courses, faculty development, or curricular initiatives; (C) current public scholarship on AI, education, and ethics; or (D) senior clinical or medical-education stakeholder such as a program director or a curricular leader. Candidates were identified through the investigators’ international academic networks. The specific routes and domains of expertise of our panel of experts in Round 4 are provided in Supplement 2.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The University of Navarra Research Ethics Committee reviewed and approved the study protocol (2024.084 mod1). All participants provided informed consent, and participation was entirely voluntary. To ensure the integrity of the consensus process and mitigate “groupthink” or social desirability bias, participation responses remained anonymous to the rest of the panel throughout rounds [38, 39]. The primary researcher maintained a master list of identities to monitor longitudinal response rates against a predefined minimum threshold of 70% per round, following Sumsion’s recommendation that a Delphi should obtain at least a 70% response rate in each round [38, 40]. This standard requires respondents and non-respondent participants to be known to the primary researcher. This quasi-anonymous design means that participant identities are known to the primary researcher, while all participants’ responses are anonymous to the panel. All data were de-identified prior to analysis and panel-wide feedback.

Data collection procedures

Round 1

The objective of Round 1 was to generate, in the participants’ own language, the initial set of principles. In May 2025, an in-person session was convened with a core panel (n = 13 students; n = 2 professors) to address a central open-ended inquiry: ‘What are the ethical and responsible GenAI principles of use for medical students?’ Each participant had been asked to bring at least 10 potential Gen AI principles. During the session, the panel consolidated all written statements, grouped identical or closely similar statements, and collaboratively agreed in real time on a single central statement for each group of related statements. All 15 participants took part in this stage of the qualitative inductive thematic analysis [41]. Following the session, the two medical educators (JS and JP) conducted a secondary thematic synthesis to refine wording and merge overlapping concepts by consensus, resulting in a streamlined set of preliminary principles for subsequent validation rounds (Fig. 1).

Round 2

The objective of Round 2 was to subject the initial principles to structured rating. In June 2025, the principles generated in the previous round were transitioned into a structured online questionnaire via Google Forms (see Supplement 3). Participants evaluated each statement using a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 7 (“Strongly agree”), with a free-text field for comments on every item. Ratings and comments were analyzed as described under ‘Data analysis and consensus criteria’ below. The anonymized statistical summaries were subsequently presented to the panel in an online forum to facilitate reflexive feedback (Fig. 1). Following this discussion, the lead researchers (JS and JP) conducted a thematic review of the qualitative feedback to reword or consolidate principles, and items that failed to meet the predefined decision rules were removed.

Round 3

The objective of Round 3 was to test whether consensus remained stable when the panel re-rated the principles set after the Round 2 rewordings and consolidations, and to resolve the items that had shown insufficient agreement. Between August and October 2025, a second structured online questionnaire featuring the updated set of principles was distributed to the panel (see Supplement 4). Following the protocol established in Round 2, participants re-evaluated the refined statements, and the same decision rules were applied unchanged (see ‘Data analysis and consensus criteria’ below) to inform further thematic modifications in selecting the principles for the final round (Fig. 1). The anonymized statistical summaries were subsequently presented to the panel in an online forum to facilitate reflexive feedback. This iterative process ensured that the resulting guidelines were both statistically stable and conceptually robust.

Round 4

The objective of Round 4 was to externally validate the near-final framework by a separate, multinational expert panel. In the final phase (November 2025 to February 2026), the remaining principles were transitioned to the panel of nine experts for formal validation. Utilizing a structured online questionnaire, the nine experts evaluated each principle using the following scale: ‘agree,’ ‘disagree,’ or ‘agree with suggestions’ (see Supplement 5). Qualitative data were also collected through open-ended text fields, enabling nuanced feedback and the proposal of additional principles. All invited experts (n = 9) provided feedback. The final stage of the study was a consensus meeting with the entire primary research team of 15 (February 2026). Each principle was reviewed in turn, with every expert’s suggestion reviewed collaboratively. The final phrasing was decided by consensus, which in this case involved open discussion and voting. The same consensus process was used to adjudicate the two principles that the experts had newly proposed (see Fig. 1).

Data analysis and consensus criteria

For Rounds 2 and 3, the mean, median, and interquartile range (IQR) were calculated for every item. The median was the primary consensus measure because responses on a 7-point Likert scale are ordinal, and the median is the appropriate measure for central tendency for ordinal data [38, 42]. In addition, with a 15-member panel, the mean is highly sensitive to a single outlying rating, whereas the median is robust to such outliers, a desirable property in a Delphi study. The IQR was the paired measure of dispersion [43], because a median rating alone cannot distinguish genuine agreement from disagreement. Means were computed for descriptive purposes only and did not inform consensus decisions.

No universally accepted consensus thresholds exist in the Delphi literature [43, 44]. Since the principles created were intended to govern conduct in medical training, we required a group median of “agree” or above on the 7-point Likert scale. A principle was retained when its median rating was ≥ 6 (which, on a 7-point integer scale, corresponds to “agree” or above) and its IQR was ≤ 1.5. Items with an IQR > 1.5 were classified as showing insufficient agreement and underwent a structured qualitative review of participants’ written comments, in which the principles were retained (only where comments supported the item as worded), reworded, consolidated with analogous items, or excluded. Items with a median below 6 were excluded. High consensus was defined as a median ≥ 6, with an IQR of 0–1. The analytical framework of the median as the primary consensus measure, the IQR as the paired dispersion criterion, and the use of participants’ qualitative comments to guide rewording and consolidation was specified a priori, before Round 2 data collection. The precise numeric cut-offs were operationalized at the Round 2 analysis stage and applied unchanged in Round 3.

Participants’ written comments were analyzed thematically after each rating round. For items meeting the retention criteria, comments were screened for wording refinements. For items showing insufficient agreement, the structured qualitative review examined whether the dispersion reflected disagreement with the item’s substance (leading to exclusion), its wording (leading to rewording), or its overlap with other items (leading to consolidation). Every decision, along with the comments that motivated it, is documented in Supplement 6.

Results

In Round 1, all 15 invited panelists participated (15/15) and identified 181 potential principles. Real-time collaborative coding during the research team meeting, followed by the two lead investigators’ secondary thematic synthesis, merged conceptually redundant items, producing a consolidated list of 37 foundational principles which served as the basis for subsequent rounds.

In Round 2, all 15 invited panelists responded to the 37 items (15/15; 100% response rate). Principles were retained for subsequent rounds only if they met the predefined consensus threshold: a median score exceeding 5.5 and an IQR 1.5 (see ‘Data analysis and consensus criteria’). Twenty-eight items showed high consensus (median ≥ 6; IQR 0–1). Applying the decision rules together with the structured qualitative review of comments: 4 items were excluded for insufficient agreement (IQR 1.5–2), 1 item was excluded for a median below 6 (median 5), 6 items were reclassified as duplicates or as statements that were not principles of use (e.g., curricular aims or learning objectives), 10 overlapping items were merged into 4 composite principles on the panel’s suggestion, and 16 items were retained, two of them with panel-guided rewording. This yielded the 20 principles carried into Round 3 (Supplement 6).

Round 3 maintained a 100% response rate (15/15) for the 20 items. Quantitative analysis identified 15 principles with high consensus (median ≥ 6; IQR 0–1). Five items showed insufficient agreement, prompting the structured qualitative review of participants’ comments: two were reworded and retained (the principles on GenAI limitations and on continuous, proactive learning about new tools), two were consolidated into related principles (into the prompting and the reasoning-and-creativity principles), and one was excluded (understanding the algorithms of the GenAI used, on feasibility grounds). In parallel, four high-consensus items whose content overlapped with broader principles were incorporated into them during conceptual consolidation, and the items were regrouped into four domains (ethical use, effective use, responsible use, and promoting critical thinking). This streamlined the list to 13 principles for external validation (Supplement 6).

In Round 4, all nine invited experts participated (9/9; 100% response rate) and achieved unanimous agreement on the appropriateness of the 13 principles. Specifically, three principles were accepted in their original forms, while 10 received constructive suggestions for linguistic refinement, expansion, or consolidation. Additionally, the external experts proposed two novel principles for consideration. At the final consensus meeting of the primary research team (February 2026), each principle and every suggestion pertaining to it were reviewed in turn under the unanimity rule described in the Methods: six principles remained unchanged (the three accepted as written and three for which suggestions were considered but the original wording was preferred), and seven were reworded to improve clarity. Of the two newly proposed principles, one — ‘Practice sustainable use of GenAI’ — was endorsed unanimously and integrated as principle 11. Notably, an environmental-stewardship item had been generated by the original panel in Round 1 and excluded in Round 2 for insufficient agreement (IQR = 2) before being independently re-proposed by the external experts. The second proposed principle was judged, under the same unanimity rule, to be encompassed by an existing principle, and its content about “Cognitive offloading” was incorporated into principle 12’s wording rather than added as a separate item (Supplement 6). This iterative synthesis resulted in a final, formalized set of 14 GenAI principles of use (Table 1).

Discussion

This study established 14 consensus-based principles to guide the ethical and responsible use of GenAI among medical students. These guidelines were derived from a multi-stage research project that integrated previous evidence from both an extensive scoping review of AI in UGME (through April 2024) [18] and a qualitative study of students’ AI use [4]. Building on this foundation, the project utilized two distinct yet complementary Delphi studies: one to define AI learning objectives and competencies in UGME (Simoni et al., submitted for publication –DOI: https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-7905807/v1) and the current study to establish responsible use of GenAI by students. By centering students as primary stakeholders, this methodology ensured that the resulting principles are not only pedagogically sound but also contextually tailored for meaningful adoption by digital-native learners. The final validation by an international expert panel confirmed that these student-partnered principles align with international guidance and provide a scalable model for the responsible adoption of AI in medical curricula.

Comparison of the present principles with other frameworks

These 14 principles address a notable gap in the UGME literature: the lack of peer-reviewed, medical student-focused guidance on GenAI beyond institutional policies. To our knowledge, this is the first such framework in which students are co-researchers. While global bodies such as the United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) [27] and the World Health Organization (WHO) [45] have defined AI risks and established broad ethical frameworks for GenAI in education, there is a lack of specific application to medical training and education. Furthermore, compared with humanities-based AI guidelines such as those of the American Historical Association [46], which prioritize original authorship and historical inquiry, our principles are uniquely centered on patient safety and clinical reasoning.

A benchmark for our findings is the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) Principles for the Responsible Use of AI in and for Medical Education, which offers a high-level framework for institutional accountability, equity, and human-centeredness [34]. While our principles align with the seven AAMC’s core pillars, there are notable differences in detail and perspective.

Both the AAMC and our principles are rooted in a human-centered philosophy that promotes HITL oversight and asserts that AI should serve as a cognitive enhancer rather than a replacement for clinical judgment. Additionally, there is widespread agreement on the importance of equity and transparency: both sets of guidelines highlight the need to mitigate algorithmic bias and ensure that AI tools do not worsen existing disparities in healthcare and education. Finally, both frameworks acknowledge that technology adoption is ineffective without literacy and emphasize the need for training programs to equip faculty and students with the skills to use these tools responsibly.

Despite these shared values, there are distinctions in detail and perspective between the two frameworks. The AAMC principles are a strategic roadmap designed to be scalable and flexible to each institution’s mission, culture, and curriculum. Their perspective is primarily institutional and visionary, focusing on broad objectives such as interdisciplinary collaboration, national parity in access, and the long-term evolution of medical curricula. In contrast, our principles offer a more operational framework tailored for immediate use in educational and clinical training settings. Furthermore, our principles emphasize cognitive and technical rigor by addressing the practical mechanics of prompt engineering and the psychological risks of cognitive offloading. This ensures that users critically verify outputs rather than passively rely on automated answers. While the AAMC references general ethical standards, our framework provides ethical anchoring by explicitly binding the use of AI to the four pillars of bioethics (nonmaleficence, beneficence, autonomy, and justice), thereby establishing and connecting a standard taught in medical school. In addition, the specificity to data security, where we implement strict guardrails prohibiting the entry of PHI and requiring formal institutional vetting for software, moves beyond the AAMC’s broader policy-level privacy concerns. Lastly, principle nº 11, Practice sustainable use of GenAI, is currently absent from the AAMC’s focus, which highlights the significant energy consumption of LLMs and fosters sustainability awareness [27, 47].

Ultimately, the two frameworks are complementary rather than contradictory. The AAMC defines the professional vision for the future of medicine, while our principles provide the precise behavioral guardrails, tailored to medical students, necessary for safe, ethical, and effective daily practice.

In addition, a recent preprint – not yet peer-reviewed – has provided a set of guidelines for the responsible use of AI, representing one of the first attempts to articulate student-facing rather than institution-facing rules of conduct for GenAI in higher education [48]. Our present framework shares several foundational principles with Alsammani’s ten-principle student guidelines for higher education [48]. Both frameworks converge on several core themes that reflect an emerging scholarly consensus on responsible GenAI use in academic settings: transparency in disclosure, privacy protection, critical appraisal of AI outputs for accuracy and bias, independent verification, learner accountability, the use of GenAI as a cognitive aid rather than a substitute for genuine learning, and the need for continuous engagement with evolving tools and policies.

However, where Alsammani’s guidelines are intended to be universal in scope for higher education and designed for portability across disciplines, the present 14 principles are explicitly grounded in the demands of UGME. The anchoring of GenAI use in the four bioethical principles and the explicit prohibition on inputting PHI reflect obligations unique to the medical training context that general higher education frameworks do not need to address. Similarly, the principle of the HITL addresses the high-stakes nature of clinical educational decisions, a feature that is absent from Alsammani’s framework. Lastly, the explicit attention to cognitive offloading – distinguishing tasks that may be appropriately delegated to GenAI (e.g., note summarization) from those that must remain with the learner (e.g., clinical reasoning) – addresses a concern specific to professional formation that goes beyond the preservation of general academic skills. Notably, unlike Alsammani’s framework, which was developed without direct student involvement, the present principles were developed with medical students as architects. Taken together, these distinctions suggest that while Alsammani’s framework offers a valuable and adaptable foundation for higher education broadly, the present guidelines address a more specialized and currently underserved gap in UGME literature.

Our principles also intersect with the entrustment-based framework of Gin and colleagues, who propose extending the logic of Entrustable Professional Activity (EPAs) to the supervision of AI itself. AI participation in education-related tasks should be calibrated to the tool’s demonstrated trustworthiness, under graded human supervision [49]. Their framework converges with ours from the learner’s side of the relationship. Risk-stratified verification (Principle 8) operationalizes graded supervision of GenAI output, scaling scrutiny to the task’s stakes. Human oversight (principle 4) preserves the entrustment decision as a human act, and accountability (principle 9) locates final responsibility with the learner regardless of the tool’s contribution. Read together, the two frameworks describe a bidirectional entrustment relationship in which supervisors entrust learners with progressively more independent activity, and learners, in turn, extend or withhold trust in AI outputs based on explicit criteria. This reciprocity situates responsible AI use within the established architecture of medical education rather than alongside it [49].

Ethical consideration

Beyond their convergence with existing frameworks, the principles engage with several debates in the ethics of AI in medical education. Principles 8–10 on verification, accountability, and transparency are exercises in epistemic responsibility, in that they keep learners answerable for knowledge claims made with machine assistance and align with D’Souza et al. Twelve Tips for addressing ethical concerns in the implementation of AI in medical education [50]. The concept of cognitive offloading in principle 12 takes a position in the debate over which capacities are constitutive of professional formation and that clinical reasoning, critical thinking, and empathy are necessary and essential pillars in medical education. Human oversight and bioethics anchoring principles 1 and 4 connect to the trustworthiness literature, which holds that trust in AI must be earned and calibrated rather than presumed, and that it remains subordinate to nonmaleficence and justice [51]. Learner autonomy is protected in a double sense: autonomy from the tool, through a deliberately preserved AI-free learning space (principle 13), and autonomy through the tool, by developing the skilled, reflective use that prevents dependence (principles 7 and 12). Finally, the development process itself models participatory governance of emerging technologies. Our work aligns with the call by D’Souza et al. to have students as co-designers and co-owners of these principles, and, by doing so, students are more likely to internalize them as part of their professional identity than if they were externally imposed [50].

These debates also expose what a framework can raise but not by itself resolve, since principles alone cannot guarantee ethical practice and must be embedded in everyday habit [52]. Cultivating ethically sound behavior and protecting the erosion of critical thinking from on-demand GenAI use, which invites cognitive offloading and quiet deskilling of clinical judgment, depends less on the principles themselves than on the conditions that enact them. These include AI-literate faculty, formative rather than punitive responses to misuse, assessments that reward demonstrable reasoning, deliberately preserved GenAI-free learning spaces (Principles 12–13) and the peer-led modeling described below [50]. Academic integrity is also a moving target because the standards of professionalism against which AI use is judged are contingent and evolve with the tools of practice, such as informed consent and the displaced paternalism of the doctor “knows best” [51]. As GenAI is normalized, that evolution can be read as a slippery slope that hollows out the competencies that define the physician, or as a legitimate reallocation of effort, in which a physician can offload “routine work” and redirect the freed-up effort toward more distinctly human endeavors. AI could be either deskilling in disguise or a sensible division of labor, so anchoring the principles in foundational bioethics helps distinguish these apart [52, 53]. This framework should be treated as a living document that is periodically re-deliberated through the same student-partnered process that produced it.

There is an urgent need to incorporate a global perspective and consensus on the professional and ethical use of AI in medical education. The UN report highlights that many parts of the world are excluded from AI governance efforts [37]. As of 2024, 7 countries were involved in all AI international governance initiatives, while 118 countries, mainly from the Global South, were not included [37]. This is concerning because technology is global and has a worldwide impact.

Relevance of the ‘by student-for-student’ design of the study

A unique feature of this study is that, unlike other AI guidelines, which are primarily ‘top-down’ and written from an institutional vantage point, our 14 principles offer a ‘bottom-up’ operational approach that translates into specific, actionable behaviors. Centering students as architects and decision-makers in drafting these guidelines guaranteed that the resulting framework aligns with end-user needs, as recommended by Triola and Rodmand [20]. The co-design development process facilitates a significant shift in perspective, as students are uniquely positioned to identify the practical points of AI integration in their medical education [54, 55]. In addition, because this process included students – hence stakeholders – they are more likely to internalize it as self-endorsement rather than as externally imposed rules, which supports buy-in, uptake, and enactment by students [56].

The ‘by student, for student’ nature of this framework allows for a peer-led mentorship model for integration. A possible example is the ‘train-the-trainer’ model, in which students serve as peer stewards for the responsible and professional use of GenAI [57, 58]. By teaching their peers how to use GenAI through the lens of these principles, students transform high-level goals into actionable, daily behaviors. In addition, this secures the ‘bottom-up’ buy-in necessary for successful curricular integration [50]. Student leaders in AI excellence could lead workshops on effective prompt engineering and data security, helping maintain the framework’s bottom-up authenticity.

Limitations of the study

Although the process was informed by several activities, including a comprehensive scoping review and up-front orientation, the principles reflect those of a relatively small group of students, educators, and experts. Because regional representation in the expert panel, in several cases, rested on a single expert, the framework is best described as internationally informed rather than as reflecting an international or global consensus. The results may not necessarily reflect the contexts and realities across all institutions or the socio-economic realities of every medical school globally. In addition, deep longitudinal expertise specific to large language models does not yet exist on any panel, given that the technology has been publicly available only since late 2022. Furthermore, the rapid evolution of GenAI technology presents a challenge to the long-term stability of these guidelines.

Conclusions

Considering the rapid integration of GenAI into medical education, it is essential that students receive guidance regarding its ethical and responsible use. Through a student-partnered Delphi method, consensus was reached within a multinational student-faculty panel, and the resulting framework was validated by international experts in AI medical education, AI ethics, AI technology education, or senior medical educators, yielding a set of internationally informed, expert-validated principles governing medical students’ use of GenAI. While numerous medical institutions are formulating guidelines for deploying GenAI, they may build on the framework we have established for adopting GenAI usage principles for their students and institutions.
