A Truncated WRKY Protein Enhances Drought Resistance in Wild Tomatoes Through the SlWRKY16‐CIP2b‐SlSYP121 Module
Authors: Yin Ding, Xuting Chen, Ke Wu, Huifang Hou, Yaling Wang, Bentao Yan, Muhammad Hafeez Ul Rehman Khursheed, Chunyu Shang, Xingguo Zhang, Yu Pan, Lang Wu, Jinhua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDrought stress is a major abiotic factor that severely affects plant growth and food production. Identifying drought‐resistant genes and their regulatory mechanisms is essential for mitigating the negative impacts of drought on plants. In this study, we identified a natural single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) mutation in SlWRKY16 that is closely linked to drought tolerance in tomato. This SNP leads to the expression of a truncated SlWRKY16 protein. The CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of SlWRKY16, which produces this truncated SlWRKY16 protein, exhibits enhanced drought tolerance, whereas the overexpression lines demonstrate the opposite effect. Yeast two‐hybrid screening demonstrated that SlWRKY16 physically interacted with CONSTANS Interacting Protein 2b (CIP2b). The CIP2b knockout mutants displayed increased sensitivity to drought stress. Importantly, this drought‐sensitive phenotype was rescued in double mutants (cip2b/slwrky16). RNA‐seq analysis revealed that a syntaxin gene (SlSYP121) co‐expressed with both SlWRKY16 and CIP2b. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays confirmed that SlWRKY16 directly binds to the promoter of SlSYP121 and represses its expression, while the truncated SlWRKY16 protein failed to bind. Moreover, SlSYP121 acts as a positive regulator of drought tolerance. Our findings further demonstrate that the interaction between CIP2b and SlWRKY16 reduces the binding affinity of SlWRKY16 to the SlSYP121 promoter. This study identified a key SNP associated with differences in drought tolerance between wild and cultivated tomato, elucidated the regulatory function of the SlWRKY16‐CIP2b‐SlSYP121 module in the tomato drought response, and enhanced our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying plant drought resistance.

Introduction

With the intensification of global climate change, extreme weather events such as droughts are becoming more frequent (Stott et al. 2016). Drought stress severely compromises crop growth and development, leading to reduced productivity and posing a significant threat to food security while impeding the sustainable development of modern agriculture (Barnabás et al. 2008; Ahluwalia et al. 2021). Consequently, addressing the challenge of drought is critical for ensuring global food security. Drought stress imposes irreversible negative effects on both vegetative and reproductive growth in plants (Gao et al. 2023; X. Liu 2023; Aun et al. 2024; Barman et al. 2024). Under drought conditions, plants enhance water uptake by promoting the elongation of primary roots (Dinneny 2019; Karlova et al. 2021). Simultaneously, drought stress suppresses the growth of above‐ground plant parts and induces stomatal closure to minimise water and energy loss (Abdalla et al. 2021; Seleiman et al. 2021; Verbraeken et al. 2021). At the cellular level, drought stress triggers excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), disrupting the ROS balance within plants. This disruption results in lipid peroxidation of cell membranes, chlorophyll degradation, membrane instability, ion leakage, and negatively impacts photosynthesis and the antioxidant system (Shawon et al. 2020; Karavolias et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2024). To survive, plants have evolved strategies to mitigate ROS through an antioxidant system composed of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD) (Mittler 2002). Additionally, drought stress promotes the accumulation of specific metabolites, such as ascorbic acid, proline, flavonoids, and carotenoids, which protect plant cells and membrane systems from ROS induced damage (Mittler et al. 2022).

Transcription of genes involved in the regulation of antioxidant pathways is frequently activated by WRKY transcription factors, which play a crucial role in modulating transcriptional responses within plant defence mechanisms against environmental stressors, including ROS (Javed and Gao 2023). WRKY transcription factors are widely distributed across animals, plants, and microorganisms. These factors are defined by the highly conserved WRKYGQK motif, which gives them their name (Rushton et al. 1996). Extensive studies have shown that members of the WRKY gene family play critical roles in mediating responses to abiotic stresses. In Nicotiana tabacum (tobacco), heterologous expression of chrysanthemum WRKY genes DgWRKY1 and DgWRKY3 significantly enhanced salt tolerance in tobacco plants (Liu et al. 2013). In Oryza sativa (rice), the overexpression of OsWRKY45 and OsWRKY87 enhanced drought tolerance (Tao et al. 2011; Yan et al. 2021). The inactivation of OsWRKY5 increased drought tolerance via the abscisic acid (ABA) signalling pathway (Lim et al. 2022). In Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis), the wrky63 mutant exhibited heightened sensitivity to drought stress (Ren et al. 2010). Additionally, AtWRKY8 is highly expressed in root structures under salt stress, and the wrky8 mutant exhibits high sensitivity to salt, inhibiting seed germination and growth (Hu et al. 2013). AtWRKY28 and the bHLH family member AtHBH17 function synergistically to enhance Arabidopsis resistance to high salt and oxidative stress (Babitha et al. 2013). AtWRKY1 binds to the W‐box region in the promoters of MYB2, ABCG40, DREB1A, and ABI5, thereby modulating gene expression to enhance drought tolerance in plants (Qiao et al. 2016). In Solanum lycopersicum (tomato), overexpression of SlWRKY8 enhanced the tolerance of transgenic plants to both drought and salt stress (Gao et al. 2020), while overexpression of SlWRKY6 improved drought tolerance by enhancing ROS scavenging ability (Chen et al. 2024).

CONSTANS Interacting Protein 2b (CIP2b) belongs to the NF‐Y family of transcription factors, which is ubiquitously present in eukaryotes and comprises three subunits: NF‐YA, NF‐YB, and NF‐YC. Initially, the NF‐YB and NF‐YC subunits form a heterodimer, which subsequently associates with NF‐YA to assemble into a trimeric complex that specifically binds to the CCAAT box element, thereby regulating the transcription of target genes (Nardone et al. 2017). In plants, numerous studies have reported the role of NF‐Y family members in regulating drought responses. For instance, overexpression of ZmNF‐YB2 in Zea mays (maize) significantly increased yield under water‐limited conditions (Nelson et al. 2007). Additionally, the StNF‐YC9 gene in Solanum tuberosum (potato) showed substantial upregulation in response to dehydration and drought treatments. Overexpression of StNF‐YC9 in potato resulted in notably longer roots and enhanced stomatal closure under polyethylene glycol‐induced stress (Li et al. 2021). In Glycine max (soybean), overexpression and suppression of GmNFYA5 correspondingly increased and decreased drought tolerance (Ma et al. 2020). In Triticum aestivum (wheat), TaNF‐YA7‐5B, along with NF‐YB and NF‐YC subunits, forms a heterotrimer. Overexpression of TaNF‐YA7‐5B enhances drought tolerance by modulating stomatal closure, leaf water retention, osmotic stress biosynthesis, and cellular ROS homeostasis (Zhao et al. 2022).

Tomato is one of the most widely cultivated vegetables and fruit crops globally, possessing significant economic value. However, the majority of cultivated tomatoes exhibit susceptibility to drought stress. Under drought conditions, the growth of tomato plants is markedly inhibited, resulting in substantial reductions in both fruit yield and quality (Obadi et al. 2023, 2024). Therefore, developing drought‐resistant tomato germplasm resources is essential. Currently, strategies to enhance drought tolerance through genetic engineering have been successfully applied in tomatoes (Alshareef et al. 2019; W. Liu 2024; Li, Ai, et al. 2024). In this study, we identified and characterised the drought regulatory module comprised of SlWRKY16‐CIP2b‐SlSYP121 (SYNTAXIN OF PLANTS121), elucidating its pivotal role in the drought response mechanism of tomato plants. Through genetic, molecular and physiological analyses, we found that SlWRKY16 acted as a negative regulator under drought stress. Specifically, SlWRKY16 interacts with CIP2b, a positive regulator of drought tolerance. Furthermore, RNA sequencing (RNA‐Seq) and electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) revealed that SlWRKY16 directly binds to the SlSYP121 promoter and represses its expression. However, the interaction between SlWRKY16 and CIP2b alleviates this repression. SYP gene are essential for plant responses to abiotic stresses and for maintaining ROS homeostasis. They mitigate oxidative damage by modulating antioxidant enzyme activities, thereby preserving cellular homeostasis under drought conditions (Zhu et al. 2002; Wang, Zhang, et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). Here, we also found that SlSYP121 enhances plant drought tolerance by facilitating the elimination of ROS. The SlWRKY16‐CIP2b‐SlSYP121 regulatory module enriches the drought tolerance regulatory network in tomatoes, providing a molecular foundation for breeding programs aimed at improving drought resistance.

Results

A Truncated WRKY Protein Resulting From a SNP Mutation Is Significantly Associated With Drought Tolerance Phenotypes in Natural Populations

To comprehensively investigate the key genes associated with drought tolerance in natural variation, we conducted a systematic analysis of genome‐wide SNP data from 455 tomato germplasm materials (Lin et al. 2014). The results identified 29 SNPs in the SlWRKY16 gene region (Table S1 and Figure S1). Among these variants, SNP670 (G‐to‐T) stands as the sole mutation causing a large‐scale change in the amino acid sequence, as it introduces a termination codon (TAA), resulting in a truncated SlWRKY16 protein (Figure 1a). Owing to its unique protein‐altering effect, SNP670 was selected as the primary candidate mutation for further investigation. Furthermore, we extended our SNP analysis to an expanded set of 455 tomato germplasm samples, including 53 tomato wild species (S. pimpinellifolium, PIM), 135 cherry tomatoes (S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme, CER), and 267 big‐fruited tomatoes (S. lycopersicum, BIG) germplasm (Figure 1b) (Lin et al. 2014). CER represents the lineage domesticated from PIM, and BIG represents the lineage improved from CER. The analysis showed that about 97% of cultivated tomato varieties and 79.26% of early domesticated cherry tomatoes exhibited SlWRKY16 GG allele purity, whereas in wild tomatoes, only 13.21% exhibited SlWRKY16 GG purity and 73.58% exhibited SlWRKY16 TT allele purity (Figure 1b). This robust genotype–phenotype association suggested that SNP670 might be a crucial natural variant responsible for drought adaptation. In addition, selection pressure analysis on SlWRKY16 showed that the SlWRKY16 locus was not significantly selected during domestication in tomato (Figure 1c).

Based on this SNP mutation, we categorised tomato germplasm resources into two haplotypes, G and T, and conducted correlation analyses in combination with data on the drought stress survival of these materials. The results demonstrated that the SlWRKY16 GG genotype material exhibited significantly higher survival under drought stress, whereas the SlWRKY16 TT genotype material was predominantly drought‐sensitive (Figure 1d). These findings suggest that this SNP induces a loss‐of‐function mutation in SlWRKY16, resulting in a truncated SlWRKY16 protein that may be associated with drought tolerance in tomatoes.

The Features of SlWRKY16

SlWRKY16 belongs to subgroup II‐b of the WRKY transcription factor family (Figure S2). Phylogenetic analysis across rice, Arabidopsis, maize, potato, and tomato identified StWRKY6a as its closest homologue (Figure S3). To determine subcellular localisation, we constructed an SlWRKY16‐GFP fusion construct and transiently expressed it in tobacco leaves. While GFP from the empty vector was distributed in both cytoplasm and nucleus, SlWRKY16‐GFP localised exclusively to the nucleus (Figure 1e). As a transcription factor, apart from its nuclear localisation, another characteristic is its transcriptional activation or repression activity. To assess whether SlWRKY16 has intrinsic transcriptional activation activity, the full‐length and the truncated SlWRKY16 (SlWRKY16Δ670) were fused to the GAL4 DNA–binding domain in pGBKT7 (SlWRKY16‐BD and SlWRKY16Δ670‐BD) and transformed into the AH109 yeast strain. All constructs demonstrated normal growth on SD/−Trp medium. However, only the positive control grew on SD/−Trp/−His/−Ade medium, while neither SlWRKY16‐BD nor SlWRKY16Δ670‐BD activated the HIS3/ADE2 reporter. These results suggest that neither the full‐length nor the truncated SlWRKY16 shows transcriptional activation activity in yeast. To further assess transcriptional regulatory activity in planta, SlWRKY16 and SlWRKY16Δ670 were fused with the GAL4 DNA‐binding domain, resulting in GAL4‐BD‐SlWRKY16 and GAL4‐BD‐SlWRKY16Δ670 constructs. Given that SlWRKY16 was predicted to act as a repressor, we also generated GAL4‐BD‐VP16 fusions, namely GAL4‐BD‐VP16‐SlWRKY16 and GAL4‐BD‐VP16‐SlWRKY16Δ670. These constructs were co‐expressed along with a GAL4‐UAS::LUC reporter in Arabidopsis protoplasts. Compared with GAL4‐BD alone, both GAL4‐BD‐SlWRKY16 and GAL4‐BD‐SlWRKY16Δ670 exhibited a reduction in LUC activity. Furthermore, GAL4‐BD‐VP16‐SlWRKY16 and GAL4‐BD‐VP16‐SlWRKY16Δ670 significantly diminished the activation induced by VP16, thereby confirming that SlWRKY16 acts as a transcriptional repressor in planta (Figure S4).

To investigate the tissue‐specific expression of SlWRKY16 under normal growth conditions, we conducted quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) analysis to quantify its abundance across various tomato tissues. With root expression serving as a reference point, we found that SlWRKY16 is expressed in multiple tissues, exhibiting the highest levels in flowers, followed by leaves, and the lowest levels in stems and fruits (Figure 1f). To further investigate the role of SlWRKY16 in abiotic stress responses, we analysed its expression dynamics under drought and salt stress conditions. Under abiotic stresses, SlWRKY16 expression was dynamically regulated: salt stress (NaCl) caused initial suppression followed by induction peaking at 6 h (Figure 1g). Similarly, under polyethylene glycol (PEG) induced drought and abscisic acid (ABA) treatment also repressed expression early, but upregulation occurred after 12 h (Figure 1h,i). These findings indicate that SlWRKY16 exhibits a dynamic response pattern to abiotic stresses and initially responds negatively to drought stress.

SlWRKY16 Exerts a Negative Regulatory Effect on Drought Tolerance in Tomato

To investigate the truncated SlWRKY16 protein caused by SNP670 and its role in drought tolerance, we generated two loss‐of‐function mutants (SlWRKY16‐CR2 and SlWRKY16‐CR3) using CRISPR/Cas9 technology and confirmed editing at the second target site, resulting in a two‐base‐pair deletion or a one‐base‐pair insertion (Figure 2a,b). The mutation at the second target site results in the deletion of the WRKY domain, which is analogous to the variation induced by SNP670. We also developed two overexpression lines (SlWRKY16‐OE1 and SlWRKY16‐OE2) under the CaMV 35S promoter, showing 5–10‐fold higher transcript levels than wild type (WT) (Figure 2c).

Mannitol is widely employed to induce osmotic stress in plants and to simulate drought conditions (Verslues et al. 2006; Claeys et al. 2014). To evaluate the drought resistance of SlWRKY16, we exposed SlWRKY16‐CR, SlWRKY16‐OE, and WT to simulated drought treatment on Murashige and Skoog medium (MS) supplemented with 200 mM mannitol. Drought tolerance was assessed by measuring the growth of primary roots and hypocotyls. As shown in Figure 2d, there was no significant difference in the growth of WT, SlWRKY16‐CR, and SlWRKY16‐OE plants under control conditions (MS medium without mannitol). However, under mannitol‐induced drought stress, the primary root growth of SlWRKY16‐OE lines was significantly inhibited compared to WT, indicating reduced drought tolerance in SlWRKY16‐OE plants. No significant difference was observed in hypocotyl growth between SlWRKY16‐OE and WT plants. In contrast, SlWRKY16‐CR lines exhibited significantly enhanced growth of both primary roots and hypocotyls compared to WT and SlWRKY16‐OE lines (Figure 2e,f). These results collectively suggest that SlWRKY16 negatively regulates drought tolerance in tomato, as evidenced by the superior drought resistance of SlWRKY16‐CR plants compared to WT and SlWRKY16‐OE lines.

To further verify the drought resistance of SlWRKY16, we cultivated both WT and SlWRKY16 transgenic lines under normal watering conditions in soil until they reached the five‐leaf stage. Subsequently, all plants were subjected to drought stress. As shown in Figure 2g, after 4 days of drought treatment, both WT and SlWRKY16‐OE lines exhibited wilting, whereas SlWRKY16‐CR lines showed only slight wilting. By the 8th day of drought treatment, wilting was significantly more severe in WT and SlWRKY16‐OE lines compared to SlWRKY16‐CR lines. During the subsequent 3‐day water recovery period, 38% of WT plants survived, while 70% of SlWRKY16‐CR plants recovered, indicating a significantly higher survival rate for SlWRKY16‐CR lines compared to WT (Figure 2h). In contrast, only 20% of SlWRKY16‐OE plants survived. These results suggest that loss‐of‐function of SlWRKY16 enhances drought resistance in tomato plants, whereas overexpression of SlWRKY16 increases drought sensitivity.

Inhibition of SlWRKY16 Expression Alleviates ROS‐Induced Damage in Tomato Plants Under Drought Stress

Abiotic stress can cause oxidative damage in plant cells, where superoxide anion (O2·−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) are major ROS that often accumulate under such conditions (Dunand et al. 2007). To evaluate the oxidative damage induced by drought stress, we conducted nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT) and diaminobenzidine (DAB) histochemical staining assays to detect O2·− and H2O2 accumulation in SlWRKY16‐OE, SlWRKY16‐CR, and WT lines. As shown in Figure 2i, under drought conditions, SlWRKY16‐CR plants exhibited lighter staining, indicating reduced O2·− and H2O2 accumulation and suggesting enhanced ROS scavenging ability. In contrast, SlWRKY16‐OE plants showed darker staining, reflecting higher levels of O2·− and H2O2 accumulation and greater sensitivity to drought stress.

ROS are generated under abiotic stress conditions, causing oxidative damage to plant cells. To mitigate the adverse effects of ROS, plants have evolved oxygen scavenging enzymes such as POD and SOD, which function to detoxify ROS (Mittler et al. 2022). In this study, we examined the changes in ROS levels by measuring the activities of SOD and POD in both WT and SlWRKY16 transgenic plants before and after exposure to drought stress. The results demonstrated that, following drought stress, the activities of SOD and POD were significantly higher in slwrky16‐CR lines compared to WT lines, while no significant differences were observed in enzyme activity between SlWRKY16‐OE lines and WT lines (Figure 2j,k).

Malondialdehyde (MDA), a byproduct of lipid peroxidation, is widely recognised as an indicator of oxidative stress in plants (Morales and Munné‐Bosch 2019). To evaluate the extent of lipid peroxidation, we quantified MDA levels in both WT and SlWRKY16 transgenic plants under normal and drought conditions. As illustrated in Figure 2l, under normal conditions, no significant differences in MDA levels were observed between the transgenic lines and WT lines. In contrast, under drought conditions, MDA levels were significantly lower in SlWRKY16‐CR lines compared to WT lines, while SlWRKY16‐OE lines exhibited significantly higher MDA levels than WT lines. Collectively, these findings suggest that suppression of SlWRKY16 expression enhances tomato drought resistance by increasing the activity of ROS‐scavenging enzymes, thereby mitigating ROS‐induced oxidative damage.

SlWRKY16 Interacts With CIP2b Both In Vivo and In Vitro

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying drought resistance mediated by SlWRKY16, the yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) system was employed to screen for interacting proteins. The full‐length sequence of SlWRKY16 was cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate the bait plasmid pGBKT7‐SlWRKY16, which was subsequently used to screen a tomato cDNA yeast library. This screening identified multiple proteins potentially interacting with SlWRKY16 (Table S2). Among these, CIP2b (Solyc01g079870), a member of the NF‐Y family of transcription factors, stood out as particularly noteworthy. Extensive studies have demonstrated that NF‐Y‐like transcription factors play a critical role in abiotic stress responses, especially in regulating drought tolerance and ROS homeostasis (Nelson et al. 2007; Chen et al. 2015; Zhou et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2021). Given their importance, we focused on elucidating the potential function of CIP2b in drought stress. Y2H assays confirmed that yeast cells co‐transformed with both pGBKT7‐SlWRKY16 and pGADT7‐CIP2b plasmids grew on SD/−Leu/−Trp/−His/−Ade medium, whereas negative controls did not (Figure 3a). These results suggest that SlWRKY16 and CIP2b interact within yeast cells.

We further validated the interaction between SlWRKY16 and CIP2b through a BiFC assay. Yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) signals were clearly detected in tobacco mesophyll cells co‐expressing SlWRKY16‐cYFP (CE‐WRKY16) and CIP2b‐nYFP (NE‐CIP2b), whereas no fluorescence was observed in control cells co‐expressing pUC‐SPYNE (NE173) with CE‐WRKY16 or pUC‐SPYCE (CE155) with NE‐CIP2b (Figure 3b). To further confirm this interaction, we conducted a Co‐IP assay. FLAG‐SlWRKY16 and HA‐CIP2b fusion proteins were constructed, and the results demonstrated the formation of a SlWRKY16/CIP2b protein complex in tobacco leaves co‐expressing both fusion proteins (Figure 3c). Consistent with these findings, LCI assays also revealed fluorescence exclusively in areas co‐expressing SlWRKY16 and CIP2b (Figure 3d,e). Collectively, these results provide compelling evidence for the direct physical interaction between SlWRKY16 and CIP2b.

The CIP2b Gene Positively Modulates Drought Resistance in Tomatoes

SlWRKY16 plays a crucial role in conferring drought tolerance. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms involved, we investigated whether its interacting protein, CIP2b, also participates in the drought response. To explore the function of CIP2b, we constructed a knockout vector for CIP2b and obtained two gene‐edited tomato lines (designated as CIP2b‐CR10 and CIP2b‐CR21) via genetic transformation. We identified the knockout sites and confirmed that gene editing occurred in the CIP2b gene sequence, leading to either a one‐base deletion at target site 1 or a four‐base deletion at target site 2 (Figure 4a). WT and CIP2b‐CR plants were grown to the five‐leaf stage and then subjected to drought treatment. As illustrated in Figure 4c, after 5 days of drought exposure, CIP2b‐CR plants began to show signs of wilting, whereas WT plants exhibited no significant changes. Following 10 days of drought treatment, WT plants remained largely unaffected, while CIP2b‐CR plants displayed severe wilting. After a 3‐day rehydration period, WT plants fully recovered, whereas nearly all CIP2b‐CR plants had perished. Survival rate analysis indicated that WT plants maintained a survival rate of 44%, whereas CIP2b‐CR plants showed a survival rate of < 20% (Figure 4b). These results demonstrate that tomato plants lacking the CIP2b gene exhibit heightened sensitivity to drought conditions, suggesting that the CIP2b gene positively regulates drought tolerance in tomatoes.

When plants are subjected to stress, the proline (Pro) content in the plant increases. This is because proline plays a critical role in regulating the osmotic environment within cells, thereby mitigating stress‐induced damage. Drought stress is frequently associated with oxidative stress, which leads to lipid peroxidation and the production of MDA as a byproduct. Oxidative stress causes cellular damage, resulting in cytoplasmic leakage and increased conductivity (Claeys et al. 2014). Therefore, we measured the levels of Pro, MDA, and relative electrolyte conductivity to evaluate stress responses. The experimental results demonstrated that in the control group, the Pro, MDA content, and relative electrolyte conductivity were consistently low across all plant lines, with no significant differences observed among them. In contrast, under drought treatment, the CIP2b‐CR lines exhibited significantly higher Pro, MDA content, and relative electrolyte conductivity compared to the WT (Figure 4g–i).

Additionally, we assessed the drought tolerance of the plants by measuring the activities of antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, CAT, and POD. Under normal conditions, no significant differences were observed in CAT, POD, and SOD activities between WT and CIP2b‐CR lines (Figure 4j–l). However, after drought treatment, the activities of CAT, POD, and SOD in the CIP2b‐CR lines were significantly lower than those in WT lines. Furthermore, NBT and DAB staining was used to assess oxidative damage in tomato leaves before and after drought treatment. The results indicated that CIP2b‐CR plants experienced more severe oxidative stress under drought conditions compared to WT plants, suggesting increased sensitivity to drought stress (Figure 4m,n). These findings suggest that the knockout of the CIP2b gene led to a reduction in the activity of these antioxidant enzymes, thereby weakening the plant's antioxidant defence system.

Comparative Analysis of Drought Resistance Phenotypes Between cip2b/wrky16 Double Mutants and Their Corresponding Single Mutants

Both SlWRKY16 and CIP2b are involved in drought tolerance and form a protein complex. To examine their combined roles, we generated cip2b/wrky16 double mutants by crossing single mutants and evaluated them alongside CIP2b‐CR, SlWRKY16‐CR, and WT plants under drought conditions. All lines grew similarly before stress (Figure 5a). After 4 days of drought, CIP2b‐CR lines wilted noticeably, while others remained healthy. By day 8, CIP2b‐CR plants showed severe wilting; in contrast, WT and double mutants showed mild symptoms, and SlWRKY16‐CR plants remained vigorous. Following 3 days of rehydration, all lines except CIP2b‐CR recovered.

The double mutants were similar to WT (Figure 5b), indicating restored drought tolerance in the progeny. Survival rates post‐drought showed that double mutants survived at over 35%, not significantly different from WT. CIP2b‐CR lines had < 20% survival, significantly lower than WT and double mutants, while SlWRKY16‐CR lines exceeded 60%, outperforming both (Figure 5c). Under drought, levels of CAT, POD, and SOD in double mutants were comparable to WT (Figure 5d,f), while CIP2b‐CR lines had significantly reduced enzyme activity and SlWRKY16‐CR lines had increased levels. The Drought Resistance of the SlWRKY16 Loss‐Of‐Function Mutant Was Enhanced Through the Activation of ROS‐Related Genes

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of SlWRKY16 and CIP2b genes during drought stress, RNA‐seq was employed to analyse the genes and pathways associated with these two regulators. A total of 12 cDNA libraries were generated from drought‐treated (DT) samples, including WT, SlWRKY16‐CR lines, CIP2b‐CR lines, and cip2b/wrky16. Each cDNA library generated approximately 39.47 to 53.91 million raw reads. After alignment to the tomato genome (ITAG3.0), approximately 38.17 to 52.68 million reads were successfully mapped, with over 91% of the clean reads being uniquely aligned. These results confirm the high reliability of the transcriptome data (Table S3).

Next, genes with significantly altered expression levels were identified and statistically analysed using a threshold of |log2(FoldChange)| > 1 and padj ≤ 0.05. As shown in Figure S5a, there were fewer differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between SlWRKY16‐CR, CIP2b‐CR, and cip2b/wrky16 lines under DT conditions. The extent of DEG expression changes is highlighted in Figure S5b. The heatmap of gene expression for WT, SlWRKY16‐CR, CIP2b‐CR, and double mutant cip2b/wrky16 showed distinct colour patterns under DT conditions, indicating that the expression of many genes was regulated by SlWRKY16 and CIP2b. These findings indicate that a substantial number of genes are responsive to drought stress, and the expression of both SlWRKY16 and CIP2b is critical for regulating these genes under such conditions.

We further identified a substantial number of DEGs associated with SlWRKY16 and CIP2b. Phenotypic analysis revealed that SlWRKY16‐CR plants exhibited enhanced drought tolerance, whereas CIP2b‐CR plants displayed reduced drought tolerance. No significant difference in drought tolerance was observed between slwrky16/cip2b and WT. The expression differences among these DEGs were the primary factor contributing to the variations in drought tolerance. Consequently, the screening results indicated that five DEGs showed significant expression changes across genotypes, and their expression patterns were strongly correlated with the drought tolerance trends observed in the respective genotypes (Figure 5h). These genes were identified through Venn diagram analysis (Figure S5c) and are likely to play crucial roles in regulating drought tolerance mediated by SlWRKY16 and CIP2b.

To further validate the reliability of the transcriptome analysis, we performed qRT‐PCR on the DEGs identified in our study. Specifically, five co‐expressed genes from the previously described screening were selected to examine their expression levels in WT, SlWRKY16‐CR, CIP2b‐CR, and cip2b/wrky16 double mutant plants. The qRT‐PCR results showed a high degree of consistency with the RNA‐seq data (Figure 5i–m), thereby confirming the robustness and accuracy of the RNA‐seq findings.

SlWRKY16 Directly Negatively Regulates the Expression of SlSYP121

Based on the transcriptome data analysis, we further focused on five DEGs co‐regulated by SlWRKY16 and CIP2b (Table S4), and analysed their promoter regions. The results revealed that the promoter regions of Solyc01g006950 (SlSYP121), Solyc06g064550, and Solyc07g062490 all contained typical W‐box binding motifs, suggesting that they might serve as direct target genes of SlWRKY16 (Table S5). Since NF‐Y family members typically bind DNA via CCAAT elements, which were not identified in the promoters (−2000 bp) of these five genes, and given that CIP2b belongs to the NF‐YC subfamily, which requires cooperation with NF‐YA and NF‐YB subfamilies for DNA binding, it is likely that CIP2b does not directly interact with DNA but instead regulates downstream gene expression through its interaction with SlWRKY16. Considering the potential stress‐responsive functions of these genes, we conducted further experiments to verify whether they are indeed direct targets of SlWRKY16.

In order to verify whether SlWRKY16 can directly bind to the promoter regions of target genes, we utilised 34‐bp sequences (containing W‐box elements) upstream of the promoters of three genes as probes and conducted EMSA analysis with the SlWRKY16 protein. The results demonstrated that the W‐box probe at the SlSYP121 promoter exhibited a distinct binding band with SlWRKY16, whereas no specific binding was observed for the W‐box probes at the Solyc06g064550 and Solyc07g062490 promoters (Figure S6). These findings indicate that SlSYP121 may serve as a direct target gene of SlWRKY16.

To further confirm the direct regulation of SlSYP121 by SlWRKY16 and SlWRKY16△670, we conducted Y1H, LUC and EMSA assays. In Y1H assays, yeast expressing pGADT7‐SlWRKY16 with the SlSYP121 promoter grew on SD/−Leu medium with 150 ng/mL AbA, whereas the negative controls did not (Figure 6a), indicating SlWRKY16 binds to the SlSYP121 promoter. In N. benthamiana, co‐expression of SlWRKY16 or SlWRKY16△670 with a LUC reporter under the SlSYP121 promoter revealed that SlWRKY16, but SlWRKY16△670 did not, significantly repressed luminescence (Figure 6d), suggesting only SlWRKY16 suppresses SlSYP121 transcription via direct binding. Protoplast transient expression assays using the pGreen system showed consistent results through reduced LUC/REN ratios when SlWRKY16 was present (Figure 6e). EMSA further verified this interaction: a biotin‐labelled 34 bp SlSYP121 promoter fragment containing the W‐box showed a gel shift upon incubation with SlWRKY16 protein. The binding was abolished by an unlabelled competitor and absent when using a mutated W‐box probe (Figure 6i). No shift was observed with SlWRKY16△670, confirming its inability to bind the W‐box. These findings demonstrate that SlWRKY16 directly binds to the W‐box in the SlSYP121 promoter to repress its transcription, whereas SlWRKY16△670 lacks this regulatory function.

Together, these results suggest that CIP2b reduces both the binding affinity and transcriptional repression activity of SlWRKY16 on the SlSYP121 promoter, consistent with gene expression patterns observed in WT, single mutants, and the cip2b/wrky16 double mutant.

Analysis of Drought Tolerance in SlSYP121 Gene Silenced Plants Induced by VIGS

Given that SlSYP121 encodes a syntaxin protein, which has been extensively documented to play a role in regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) in plants (Wang, Zhang, et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023), we further investigated its function under drought stress conditions. Using virus‐induced gene silencing (VIGS) technology, we silenced the SlSYP121 gene and subjected both silenced plants (TRV‐SlSYP121) and empty vector control plants (TRV) to drought stress. The results demonstrated that the expression level of SlSYP121 was significantly reduced in TRV‐SlSYP121 plants (Figure 7a). On day 10 of drought stress, TRV‐SlSYP121 plants exhibited significant chlorosis and curling, whereas TRV plants showed relatively mild symptoms. By day 14, all plants displayed severe chlorosis and water deficit phenotypes. However, upon rehydration on the third day after drought cessation, only TRV plants resumed normal growth (Figure 7c). Additionally, NBT and DAB staining revealed that the accumulation of O2·− and H2O2 in TRV plants was significantly lower than in the TRV‐SlSYP121 group, indicating reduced oxidative damage under drought stress (Figure 7b). Statistically, the survival rate of TRV‐SlSYP121 plants was 33.3%, significantly lower than that of TRV plants at 77.8% (Figure 7d). Further physiological analysis revealed that the activities of SOD and POD were significantly reduced in TRV‐SlSYP121 plants, indicating that SlSYP121 regulates plant drought resistance by modulating ROS metabolism (Figure 7e,f).

Discussion

During the course of plant evolution, single SNP mutations can significantly influence plant physiological traits and adaptations. For instance, mutations in the CDK8 gene in Arabidopsis have been shown to decrease plant tolerance to salinity (Guo et al. 2025). In maize, screening 11.5 million SNPs with a minimum allele frequency of 0.05 identified the COOL1 gene as a negative regulator of cold tolerance in maize seedlings. Loss‐of‐function mutations in COOL1 significantly enhanced cold tolerance in maize (Zeng et al. 2025). Similarly, a significant SNP associated with drought tolerance was identified in tomato, leading to a premature stop codon in the B‐box zinc finger transcription factor BBX18, which affects drought tolerance in tomato (Li, Ai, et al. 2024). Analogous to these findings, this study identified an SNP mutation (G‐to‐T variant) in the WRKY16 gene that is significantly associated with drought tolerance in tomato by analysing genome‐wide SNP data from 360 tomato germplasm materials. This SNP introduced a termination codon (TAA), resulting in the premature termination of the SlWRKY16 protein during translation (Figure 1a). By correlating drought survival rates across different materials, we observed that tomato materials harbouring the premature termination mutation exhibited significantly higher survival rates under drought stress. In contrast, materials lacking this mutation were predominantly drought‐sensitive (Figure 1d). This suggests that the SNP associated with drought resistance in tomato leads to premature termination of SlWRKY16 transcription, thereby producing a truncated WRKY protein in wild species. Wild tomato species, which originated in desert regions, are generally characterised by high drought tolerance. This indicates that the SNP mutation in the SlWRKY16 gene may serve as a critical factor in regulating drought tolerance in tomato. Despite the absence of significant selection pressure on the WRKY16 gene during tomato domestication (Figure 1c), further investigation into the natural variation of this gene revealed a strong association between the loss‐of‐function mutation in SlWRKY16 and the drought‐adapted phenotype.

WRKY transcription factor family members are closely associated with stress responses in many plants, especially under drought stress. For instance, in rice, OsWRKY114 negatively regulates drought tolerance by inhibiting stomatal closure and accelerating water loss (Song et al. 2022). In maize, ZmWRKY79 actively enhances drought tolerance by promoting ABA biosynthesis (Gulzar et al. 2021). In tobacco, overexpression of the cotton gene GhWRKY6 suppresses ROS scavenging and reduces the expression of ABA‐responsive genes, thereby increasing salt stress sensitivity in transgenic plants (Li, Li, et al. 2019). TaWRKY1‐2D in wheat improves drought tolerance by enhancing proline and chlorophyll accumulation, as well as elevating antioxidant enzyme activity (Yu et al. 2023). In tomato, overexpression of SlWRKY8 not only strengthens immunity against pathogen infection but also increases drought resistance (Gao et al. 2020).

Although extensive research has been conducted on WRKY transcription factors in various crops, the functions of specific WRKY genes, particularly in tomato, remain incompletely understood. This study reveals that SlWRKY16 acts as a negative regulator of drought tolerance in tomato. It influences drought tolerance by interacting with other proteins and modulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes. It is important to note that despite drought responses involving systemic regulation at the whole‐plant level, tissue‐specific expression analysis indicated that under drought stress conditions, both SlWRKY16 and CIP2b were predominantly expressed in leaves rather than roots (Figure S8). This expression pattern aligns with their regulatory roles in modulating antioxidant enzyme activities and ROS homeostasis in aerial tissues. Therefore, in this study, leaf physiological parameters were employed as primary indicators to evaluate drought tolerance, which is also in line with previous drought‐related studies in tomato and other crops (Cao et al. 2024; Ling et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025). Meanwhile, drought stress poses a severe threat to plant growth, significantly influencing crop yield and quality and resulting in substantial economic losses. Consequently, enhancing drought tolerance in crops has long been a crucial objective for breeders. In this study, the loss‐of‐function mutation of SlWRKY16 under drought stress markedly enhanced drought tolerance in tomato. The SlWRKY16‐CR lines exhibited increased activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD and POD) and reduced levels of membrane lipid peroxidation products (MDA) (Figure 2j,k). These results indicate that SlWRKY16 may negatively regulate drought tolerance. It is well‐documented that antioxidant enzymes play a critical role in plants' defence against oxidative stress by scavenging ROS and reducing oxidative damage to cell membranes and biomolecules (Alscher et al. 2002; Foyer and Noctor 2011). In our study, SlWRKY16‐CR more effectively scavenged ROS, thereby alleviating cell damage under drought stress and enhancing drought tolerance. Conversely, the overexpression of SlWRKY16 significantly compromised drought tolerance in plants. The SlWRKY16‐OE lines accumulated higher levels of ROS under drought stress (Figure 2j,k), exhibited greater membrane damage, and displayed more pronounced wilting and growth inhibition. Despite no significant changes in the activity of antioxidant enzymes, the elevated ROS accumulation implies that excessive SlWRKY16 may impair the plant's capacity to cope with drought stress, possibly by interfering with other antioxidant pathways or interacting antagonistically with regulatory factors.

Transcription factor interactions play crucial roles in the fine‐tuning of plant stress responses. For instance, in Salvia miltiorrhiza, the SmbHLH60 forms a heterodimer with SmMYC2 to co‐regulate secondary metabolism (Liu et al. 2022), while tomato STM3/J2 interactions are involved in inflorescence development (Wang, Liu, et al. 2023). In this study, we demonstrated that SlWRKY16 directly interacts with the NF‐Y family member CIP2b (Figure 3a,e). The NF‐Y family is extensively implicated in plant growth, development, and environmental adaptation, with several members reported to function significantly in abiotic stress responses. For example, soybean GmNF‐YC14 enhances drought tolerance by increasing ABA sensitivity (Yu et al. 2021), and rice Cdt‐NF‐YC1 regulates salt tolerance via both ABA‐dependent and ABA‐independent pathways (Chen et al. 2015). Additionally, the transcription factor OsNF‐YA7 has been shown to confer drought resistance in rice (Lee et al. 2015). Functional analyses revealed that CIP2b and SlWRKY16 play opposing roles in drought regulation. Plants overexpressing SlWRKY16 exhibited increased sensitivity to drought stress, while loss‐of‐function mutants of SlWRKY16 demonstrated enhanced drought tolerance (Figure 2g). Conversely, CIP2b‐CR lines displayed heightened sensitivity under drought conditions (Figure 4c), indicating that CIP2b functions as a positive regulator of drought tolerance. In this study, when we crossed CIP2b‐CR lines with SlWRKY16‐CR lines, the resulting cip2b/wrky16 double mutant showed drought tolerance levels comparable to those of WT under drought stress (Figure 5a). The WRKY16 gene negatively regulates drought resistance, whereas the CIP2b gene positively regulates it. Consequently, in the double mutant cip2b/wrky16, the drought resistance reverted to the WT state, and the opposing resistance effects were neutralised (Figure 5a). This observation was further supported by our transcriptome analysis, which revealed that under drought conditions, WRKY16 and CIP2b regulate distinct sets of genes. Specifically, 762 genes are co‐regulated by both genes, 1009 genes are specifically regulated by SlWRKY16, and 879 genes are specifically regulated by CIP2b. Therefore, these two genes regulate different genes involved in different pathways, and they also co‐regulate the same genes involved in the same pathway (Figure S7). As a result, the drought‐tolerance phenotype returns to a level similar to that of the WT. These findings underscore the multifaceted role of CIP2b in drought regulation and suggest additional downstream targets worthy of future investigation. Moreover, this discovery clarifies the synergistic regulation between WRKY and NF‐Y family members in response to abiotic stresses and provides novel insights into their functional interactions.

ROS are essential in plant stress response, and their oxidative reactions produce O2·−, HO−, and H2O2, which can function as signalling molecules. However, excessive accumulation of ROS may cause oxidative damage. Plants rely on antioxidant systems such as SOD, CAT, POD, and GSH to maintain the dynamic balance of ROS, and WRKY and NF‐Y transcription factors play key roles in this process (Javed and Gao 2023). For example, CsWRKY25 enhances disease resistance by maintaining ROS homeostasis through RbohB and RbohD (Li, Xian, et al. 2024). Whereas TaWRKY19 disrupts ROS homeostasis by binding to the NOX10 promoter and increases the susceptibility of wheat to stripe rust (Wang et al. 2022). In addition, OsWRKY45 enhances antioxidant capacity in rice by regulating CAT and SOD (Tao et al. 2011), and GhWRKY17 overexpression promotes H2O2 accumulation and enhances drought tolerance in cotton (Yan et al. 2014). the NF‐Y family is also involved in ROS regulation, such as GmNF‐YB1 regulates ROS signalling through the ABA pathway, which enhances drought tolerance in soybean (Mallano et al. 2021). AtNF‐YC1 mediates antioxidant capacity through SOD and POD in Arabidopsis thaliana (Yu et al. 2023). These results indicate that WRKY and NF‐Y transcription factors synergistically regulate the ROS scavenging system, thereby playing a crucial role in enhancing plant stress tolerance.

Interaction among transcription factors not only modulates their own activities but also influences plant adaptation to stress through the regulation of downstream gene expression. In Arabidopsis, DREB2A interacts with HSFs to upregulate the expression of heat stress protein genes, thus improving heat tolerance (Huang et al. 2024). In another example, the AtWRKY8 mutant in Arabidopsis thaliana exhibits increased sensitivity to salt stress, whereas a mutation in the VQ9 gene enhances salt tolerance. Furthermore, WRKY8 and VQ9 interact, and their interaction reduces the DNA‐binding activity of WRKY8 (Hu et al. 2013). In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of transcriptome data and drought tolerance in CIP2b‐CR lines, SlWRKY16‐CR lines, cip2b/wrky16, and WT plants. We screened for SlSYP121 genes co‐expressed with SlWRKY16 and CIP2b. Y1H, LUC, and EMSA assays demonstrated that SlWRKY16 directly binds to the promoter of SlSYP121 and represses its expression. However, in the presence of CIP2b, the interaction between SlWRKY16 and CIP2b diminishes the repressive effect of SlWRKY16 on SlSYP121 expression. These findings suggest that CIP2b may indirectly promote SlSYP121 expression by modulating the DNA‐binding ability of SlWRKY16.

SYP, as a membrane‐associated protein, plays a critical role in the plant's response to abiotic stress. In Arabidopsis thaliana, AtSYT1 enhances osmotic stress tolerance by stabilising the membrane structure (Zhang et al. 2023). In rice, OsSYT5 is involved in regulating signal transduction under drought stress (Zhu et al. 2002). VIGS‐mediated silencing of SlSYP121 substantially decreased the drought tolerance of tomato plants by reducing the activity of antioxidant‐related enzymes, leading to an accumulation of ROS. Furthermore, SlSYP121 may play a role in ROS accumulation, and its upstream regulator, SlWRKY16, may influence the dynamic balance of ROS via SlSYP121, thereby modulating the drought resistance of tomato plants.

In this study, we established a model that elucidates the molecular mechanism underlying SlWRKY16‐CIP2b‐SlSYP121‐mediated drought resistance in tomato. SlWRKY16 negatively regulates drought tolerance by directly binding to and suppressing the transcription of SlSYP121, which leads to reduced ROS scavenging capacity and increased plant susceptibility to drought stress. In contrast, CIP2b positively modulates drought resistance through its interaction with SlWRKY16, thereby decreasing SlWRKY16's binding affinity to the SlSYP121 promoter and relieving the suppression of SlSYP121 expression. This regulatory process enhances the plant's antioxidant defence system, alleviates oxidative damage, and ultimately improves drought tolerance (Figure 8). This regulatory network not only clarifies the functional roles of SlWRKY16, CIP2b, and SlSYP121 in tomato drought tolerance but also provides novel molecular targets for future breeding strategies. Importantly, our findings uncover a previously unknown regulatory module SlWRKY16‐CIP2b‐SlSYP121 that plays a key role in the tomato drought response. The identification of SlWRKY16 broadens our understanding of WRKY transcription factors in the regulation of drought tolerance and offers valuable genetic resources for molecular breeding aimed at improving drought resistance in tomato.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Tomato (S. lycopersicum) cv. Ailsa Craig was used as the WT in this study. Both WT and transgenic tomato plants were cultivated in a greenhouse under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at approximately 25°C. For nucleotide diversity (π) analyses, the tomato materials included 53 accessions of currant tomato (PIM), 135 accessions of cherry tomato (CER), and 267 accessions of large‐fruited tomato (BIG), with SNP data retrieved from the SGN database (https://solgenomics.net/) (Lin et al. 2014). To investigate whether the SlWRKY16 gene experienced selection during domestication and improvement, SNP loci within the chromosomal region harbouring SlWRKY16 (Chr2: 19.0–21.5 Mb, SL2.40) were identified. Nucleotide diversity (π) levels were calculated for each window using a 50 kb sliding window with a 5 kb step size across the PIM, CER, and BIG subpopulations. Additionally, the nucleotide diversity ratios between PIM and CER (π_PIM/π_CER) and between CER and BIG (π_CER/π_BIG) were computed to elucidate selection signals in this region during domestication and breeding improvement.

Gene Expression Analysis and Real‐Time PCR

As previously described, tissue and plant hormone treatments were established (Li, Chen, et al. 2019). Specifically, 5–6 leaf stage WT seedlings were selected for treatment. For plant growth regulator treatments, leaves were sprayed with ABA (10 μM). For drought and salt stress treatments, the soil was irrigated with PEG (100 mM) and NaCl (100 mM), respectively, while distilled water served as the control. Following each treatment, tomato leaves were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis. To analyse tissue‐specific expression patterns under normal conditions, roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits were harvested from WT tomato plants for RNA extraction. To evaluate drought‐responsive expression, samples (roots, stems, and leaves) were obtained from five‐leaf stage WT seedlings after 4 days of drought treatment, a time point when distinct wilting symptoms were observable. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Takara, China). Reverse transcription of mRNA was carried out using the HiFiScript gDNA Removal RT Master Mix kit (CWBIO, China). Transcript levels were quantified by qRT‐PCR. qRT‐PCR was performed on the Q3000 real‐time PCR system using SYBR Green qRT‐PCR Master Mix (Tiangen, China). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method based on CT values.

Plasmid Construction and Plant Transformation

The full‐length coding sequence of SlWRKY16 (Solyc02g032950) was amplified from tomato (S. lycopersicum cv. Ailsa Craig) via PCR and subsequently cloned into a CaMV35S‐driven pMV2 vector (a modified version of the pBI121 vector) for overexpression analysis. Two guide RNAs (gRNAs, designated as target 1 and target 2) were specifically designed for SlWRKY16 and CIP2b (Solyc01g079870), respectively, using the CRISPR‐p v2.0 tool (http://cbi.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/) and inserted into the CRISPR‐Cas9 vector pTX through homologous recombination. The resulting recombinant plasmids were then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 and used to generate transgenic tomato plants (S. lycopersicum cv. Ailsa Craig) via Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation. All transgenic materials utilised in this study were homozygous T2 or T3 generation plants. The slwrky16/cip2b double mutant was obtained by crossing slwrky16 and cip2b plants and further validated by PCR analysis.

Drought Stress Treatment

The tomato seeds were placed on moist filter paper in an incubator at 28°C for germination. After germination, the seedlings were transplanted into 5 × 10 seeding trays. At the three‐leaf stage, uniform seedlings were selected and transplanted into nutrient bowls containing soil of uniform weight. When the plants reached the five‐leaf stage, WT and transgenic tomato plants with uniform growth were selected for drought treatment. For the drought‐treated group, plants were fully watered initially, excess water was drained, and no further watering was provided thereafter. In contrast, the control group was watered normally throughout the experiment. The first day of drought treatment was defined as the day when the topsoil of the drought‐treated group became dry. Leaf samples were collected from both groups for further physiological measurements. Simulated drought conditions were established using mannitol treatment. After sterilisation, seeds were germinated, and those with uniform germination were selected and sown onto MS medium (control group) and MS medium supplemented with 200 mM mannitol. The plants were then grown in a greenhouse at 25°C, and their growth was monitored.

Measurement of Physiological Indexes

The lipid peroxidation was quantified following the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method. Approximately 0.1 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenised in 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). After centrifugation, the supernatant was mixed with 0.67% TBA and heated at 95°C for 30 min. Absorbance was recorded at 450, 532, and 600 nm, and MDA content was calculated accordingly. Proline was extracted from 0.1 g leaf samples using 3% sulfosalicylic acid. The extract was reacted with acid‐ninhydrin reagent at 100°C for 30 min, and the chromophore was extracted with toluene. Absorbance was measured at 520 nm, and proline concentration was calculated using a standard curve. The CAT, SOD and POD activities were measured in accordance with our previous work in the WT and transgenic plants in the control and drought‐treated groups, respectively, as described previously (Li, Ai, et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025). For analysis of leaves in response to drought stress, the third fully expanded leaves from the top of tomato plants after 9 days of drought treatment were detached and incubated with DAB or NBT for the detection of H2O2 or superoxide radicals as described previously (Li, Ai, et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025).

Yeast Two‐Hybrid (Y2H)

The full length of the coding sequence (CDS) of SlWRKY16 was amplified via PCR and subsequently cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate the bait plasmid pGBKT7‐SlWRKY16. Yeast transformation was performed using the PEG/LiAc method as detailed in the yeast protocol manual PT3024‐1 (TaKaRa Bio). The bait plasmid pGBKT7‐SlWRKY16 was co‐transformed with a Y2H cDNA library into the AH109 yeast strain to identify potential interacting proteins. Co‐transformed yeast cells were plated on SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade selective medium and incubated at 30°C for 2–3 days. Positive clones were further verified using X‐α‐GAL assays, and their nucleotide sequences were amplified using SMARTIII and CDS III primers before being sequenced. To validate the Y2H screening results, the full‐length CDS sequences of identified interacting proteins were cloned into the pGADKT7 vector. These constructs were then co‐transformed with pGBKT7‐SlWRKY16 into AH109 yeast for point‐to‐point validation. Co‐transformed yeasts were cultured on SD/−Leu/−Trp medium for 2–3 days, followed by activation through shaking. The activated yeast culture was adjusted to OD600 = 0.2 with 0.9% NaCl and serially diluted to three concentrations (1×, 10×, and 100×) for spotting on SD/−Leu/−Trp medium and SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade/X‐α‐GAL medium. As controls, yeast harbouring the pGBKT7‐p53 + pGADT7‐T recombinant plasmids served as positive controls, while yeast containing pGBKT7‐SlWRKY16 + pGADT7 recombinant plasmids acted as negative controls.

Yeast One‐Hybrid (Y1H)

The full‐length sequence of SlWRKY16 was amplified from tomato cDNA by PCR. Subsequently, the SlWRKY16 sequence was inserted into the pADT7 plasmid via homologous recombination to generate the recombinant plasmid pADT7‐SlWRKY16. A 2000 bp fragment upstream of the SlSYP121 promoter was amplified using tomato genomic DNA as a template. This promoter sequence was then integrated into the pAbAi vector through homologous recombination, resulting in the construction of the recombinant plasmid pAbAi‐SlSYP121. The pAbAi‐SlSYP121 plasmid was linearised and subsequently transformed into the yeast strain Y1H Gold. Yeast transformants were screened by PCR, followed by co‐transformation of pADT7‐SlWRKY16 and pADT7 into yeast cells harbouring the pAbAi‐SlSYP121 sequence. The transformed yeast cells were cultured on SD/−Leu medium at 30°C for 2–3 days. Positive clones were further selected and incubated on SD/−Leu medium supplemented with varying concentrations of Aureobasidin A (AbA) to determine the minimal inhibitory concentration of AbA that suppressed the growth of yeast cells containing pGADT7 + pAbAi‐SlSYP121. Resuspensions of yeast cells (pGADT7 + pAbAi‐SlSYP121 and pGADT7‐SlWRKY16 + pAbAi‐SlSYP121) were adjusted to OD600 = 0.2 with 0.9% NaCl, serially diluted (1×, 10×, and 100×), and cultured on SD/−Leu medium containing an appropriate concentration of AbA at 30°C for 2–3 days.

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation Assay (BiFC) and Luciferase Complementation Imaging Assays (LCI)

BiFC detection was performed as described previously (Li et al. 2016). Briefly, the full‐length sequences of CIP2b and SlWRKY16 were amplified and subsequently cloned into either the pUC‐SPYNE (NE173) or pUC‐SPYCE (CE155) vectors. The resulting recombinant plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 for subsequent transformation. Tobacco leaves were infiltrated with GV3101 suspensions containing a 1:1 mixture of CIP2b fused to YFPNE and SlWRKY16 fused to YFPCE. Negative controls included NE173 + CE‐WRKY16 and NE‐CIP2b + CE155. Samples were analysed under a laser confocal microscope (Zeiss, LSM780, Germany) 48 h post‐infiltration.

In luciferase complementation assays, luciferase proteins were split into two functional fragments corresponding to the N‐terminal (nLuc, residues 2–416) and C‐terminal (cLuc, residues 398–550) regions. Full‐length CIP2b and SlWRKY16 were amplified and inserted into either nLuc or cLuc vectors. The resultant recombinant constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens, and tobacco leaves were co‐infiltrated with combinations of SlWRKY16‐cLuc + CIP2b‐nLuc, cLuc + CIP2b‐nLuc, and SlWRKY16‐cLuc + nLuc. After 24–48 h, the substrate fluorescein was applied to the abaxial side of tobacco leaves, and imaging was performed using Optical in vivo Imaging.

Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay (LUC Assays)

The CDS sequences of SlWRKY16 and CIP2b were amplified by PCR. The pGreen II 62‐SK vector was linearised by digestion with Sac I and Hind III, and the amplified CDS fragments were inserted separately into the vector using homologous recombination to generate pGreen II 62SK‐SlWRKY16 and pGreen II 62SK‐CIP2b constructs. All constructs, along with the empty pGreen II 62SK vector as a control, were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. For transient expression assays in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves, the following combinations were prepared: (1) pGreen II 62SK‐SlWRKY16 + pGreenII 0800‐SYP121pro, (2) pGreen II 62SK‐SlWRKY16 + pGreenII 0800, (3) pGreen II 62‐SK + pGreenII 0800‐SYP121pro, and (4) pGreen II 62SK‐SlWRKY16 + pGreen II 62SK‐CIP2b + pGreenII 0800‐SYP121pro. The Agrobacterium cultures were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.8, mixed in equal volumes according to each treatment group, and infiltrated into the abaxial side of 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves. Plants were kept in the dark for 2 days at 22°C–24°C. LUC and REN activities were subsequently measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System Kit (Promega, USA).

For protoplast‐based dual‐luciferase assays, mesophyll protoplasts were isolated from four‐week‐old Arabidopsis thaliana Col‐0 rosette leaves following the protocol described by Yoo et al. (2007). Equal combinations of the above plasmids were transfected into protoplasts using PEG‐mediated transformation. After incubation in the dark at room temperature for 16 h, LUC and REN signals were measured. The LUC/REN ratio is used to evaluate the transcriptional activity of transcription factors and their regulatory effects on downstream genes.

EMSA

SlWRKY16 full‐length CDS were amplified (terminator codon removed) and inserted into the pET32a vector. The recombinant plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3) and expression of SlWRKY16‐His recombinant protein was induced with 0.1 mM concentration of IPTG. The cells were incubated on a horizontal shaker at 16°C and 100 r/min for 12–16 h. The E. coli bacterial precipitate was collected by high‐speed centrifugation (13 000 r/min) for 10 min and resuspended in 1× PBS buffer containing a final concentration of 1 mM PMSF and 1 mM DTT. The E. coli cells in the resuspension solution were broken using an ultrasonic cell crusher (SCIENTZ, China) (ultrasonic crusher used parameters: 300w working for 5 s, stopping for 5 s, total working time 60 min). Protein purification was performed using His‐tag Protein Purification Kit (Beyotime, China). The probes were synthesised and labelled with biotin‐labelled probes 5′ terminal (Tsingke, China). EMSA was followed by Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Beyotime, China), and the operation was carried out according to the instruction manual. The resulting images were captured using Amersham Imager 600.

RNA‐Seq

The leaves of WT, CIP2b‐CR, SlWRKY16‐CR, and cip2b/slwrky16 lines were exposed to drought conditions for 8 days, after which leaves were collected for RNA extraction. RNA samples from drought‐treated (DT) WT, CIP2b‐CR, SlWRKY16‐CR, and cip2b/slwrky16 lines were used to generate separate cDNA libraries, yielding the DT_WT, DT_CIP2b, DT_WRKY, and DT CxW libraries (three biological replicates per treatment). These libraries were sequenced using the Illumina platform (Tsingke, China), and the filtered reads were aligned to the tomato genome (ITAG3.0) using HISAT2 software (Kim et al. 2015), Gene expression levels were quantified using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values, and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014), Genes with |log2 (fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted (p < 0.05) were considered differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using GO and KEGG databases to identify pathways associated with drought stress response. The raw data has been uploaded to NCBI (PRJNA1266390).

Co‐Immunoprecipitation Assay (Co‐IP)

The full‐length CDS fragments of SlWRKY16 and CIP2b were cloned into pH7LIC vector. The resulting recombinant vectors were used to express Flag‐tag or HA‐tag proteins in tobacco leaves. Tobacco leaves were injected with a combination of SlWRKY16‐Flag + CIP2b‐HA, Flag + CIP2b‐HA, and SlWRKY16‐Flag + CIP2b, and then cultured at 25°C for 24–48 h (16 h light/8 h dark cycle). Subsequently, tobacco leaves were collected and ground and broken, then total protein (INPUT) was extracted from the samples using protein extraction buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20, 1 mM PMSF, and 1 × Protease Inhibitor Cocktail], and the clear supernatant was collected and incubated with magnetic Flag capture beads at 4°C for 2 h. After washing three times with protein extraction buffer, the samples were resuspended with an appropriate amount of protein upload buffer and heat denatured for subsequent WB experiments. For Western blot analysis, the levels of SlWRKY16 or SlCIP2b proteins were detected using anti‐Flag (1/1000) and anti‐HA (1/1000) antibodies, respectively.

Virus‐Induced Gene Silencing (VIGS)

In VIGS experiments, silent interfering fragments of SlSYP121 were designed using the SGN VIGS Tool (https://vigs.solgenomics.net/). The interference fragment was amplified from tomato genomic DNA and cloned into the pTRV2 vector to generate the recombinant plasmid pTRV2‐SlSYP121. Subsequently, the recombinant plasmid pTRV2‐SlSYP121, along with pTRV1, pTRV2, and pTRV2‐PDS, was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101. A mixture of pTRV1 + pTRV2‐SlSYP121 (experimental group), pTRV1 + pTRV2‐PDS (positive control), and pTRV1 + pTRV2 (negative control) was infiltrated into tomato cotyledons. Drought treatment was initiated once the positive control exhibited a distinct PDS gene silencing phenotype. The silencing efficiency was further validated by qRT‐PCR analysis.
