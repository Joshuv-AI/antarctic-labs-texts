Toxicity and mechanistic analysis of di(2-ethylhexyl)phthalate in renal cell carcinoma progression: a systematic study with network toxicology and molecular docking strategies
Authors: Biao Ran, Xinyi Wang, Bohan Liu, Junjiang Ye, Liangren Liu, Zhaofa Yin, Zhongli Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMillions of tons of unrecycled plastic pollute landfills and oceans, and di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), a plasticizer classified as a possible carcinogen, is found in many environments, and poses health risks. Renal cell carcinoma (RCC), one of the most prevalent malignancies, accounted for approximately 434,419 new cases and 155,702 deaths in 2022. DEHP can cause developmental dysregulation, reproductive impairments, tumorigenesis, and transgenerational diseases; however, the mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the toxic targets and molecular mechanisms associated with DEHP exposure and RCC occurrence by integrating network toxicology and molecular docking, utilizing multilevel bioinformatics data. By systematically utilizing diverse databases, 82 targets associated with both DEHP and RCC were identified. Subsequent screening with STRING and Cytoscape revealed 25 key targets, including CASP3, BCL2, MMP9, BCL2L1, CTSS, and APP. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these targets are involved in apoptosis, abnormal hormone activity, cancer-related signaling cascades, ligand-receptor interactions, and endocrine system signaling pathways. Molecular docking simulations via CB-Dock confirmed the high-affinity binding interactions between DEHP and these key targets. These results suggest that DEHP exposure may promote the development of RCC by regulating apoptosis and proliferation through pathways such as neuroactive ligand–receptor interactions, pathways related to cancer, and apoptosis. This study provides a theoretical basis for understanding the molecular mechanisms of DEHP-induced renal carcinogenesis and serves as a foundation for future experimental studies to validate these computational predictions regarding DEHP-associated tumor risk from plastic products and environmental exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03543-7.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma (RCC), Di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), Environmental pollutants, Network toxicology, Molecular docking

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the extensive application of industrial chemicals has resulted in the proliferation of numerous environmental pollutants, many of which have largely unknown toxicological profiles. Di-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (DEHP), also known as bis(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate or dioctyl phthalate (DOP), is one of the most widely used phthalate derivatives, primarily serving as plasticizers to impart flexibility to polymer products [1]. Owing to its cost-effectiveness and efficiency, DEHP has become a crucial component in the plastic industry, with over 2 million tons produced annually worldwide [2]. As a lipophilic, colorless, viscous liquid with minimal odor, DEHP is more soluble in organic solvents such as paint removers, gasoline, and oils than in water [3]. Human exposure to DEHP occurs mainly through dermal contact and ingestion, and it is frequently detected in blood and urine samples [4]. While DEHP offers considerable industrial benefits, exposure to it has raised significant health concerns because of its potential adverse effects [5]. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) reported that ingestion of 50 µg/kg/day DEHP may cause testicular damage [6]. Moreover, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified DEHP as a Group 2B carcinogen on the base of evidence from rodent studies [7]. Given its pervasive presence in daily life, elucidating the toxicological mechanisms of DEHP is imperative.

Urinary system malignancies, including prostate cancer (PCa), bladder cancer (BCa), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and testicular cancer, represent significant global health challenges. Previous studies have suggested that DEHP can promote prostate cancer cell proliferation in vitro, and that its primary metabolite, mono(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (MEHP), can accelerate the progression of prostate cancer, with effects intensifying over prolonged exposure [8, 9]. In vivo studies have indicated that male Sprague-Dawley rat offspring exposed to DEHP in utero and during lactation exhibit increased susceptibility to prostate carcinogenesis [10]. Voss et al. reported that lifelong exposure (159 weeks) to DEHP induced testicular tumors in rats, with an increase in tumor multiplicity over time [11]. Additionally, MEHHP, another metabolite of DEHP, is associated with an increased risk of urothelial cancer in patients with chronic kidney disease [12]. These findings suggest that DEHP may accelerate the progression of various urinary malignancies.

However, studies specifically investigating the relationship between DEHP and RCC are relatively rare. Renal cell carcinoma, accounting for approximately 90% of kidney cancer cases, is the 14th most commonly diagnosed carcinoma worldwide and caused an estimated 155,702 deaths in 2022 [13, 14]. While earlier studies suggested a possible association between DEHP and kidney tumors [15, 16], the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, and research methods are limited. Given the potential role of plastic derivatives in RCC development and the lack of contemporary data, further research is urgently needed to explore the mechanisms of DEHP-induced carcinogenesis in the kidney.

Innovative approaches, such as network toxicology and molecular docking, offer promising tools for investigating these complex interactions. Network toxicology is an emerging research approach that integrates bioinformatics, big data analysis, genomics, and related technologies to study the toxic pathways of compounds and the molecular mechanisms of diseases [17]. Molecular docking predicts and verifies interactions between small molecules and protein receptors at the molecular level, simulating the spatial orientation of small molecules within biological macromolecules [18]. These methods have been successfully employed to examine prostate toxicity induced by environmental pollutants such as bisphenol F [19] and to investigate the molecular pathways of prostatic carcinogenesis associated with DEHP [20]. This technology is essential for understanding potential therapeutic effects and side effects.

In this study, we utilized network toxicology, molecular docking, and multilevel bioinformatics data analysis to comprehensively elucidate the complex interactions and molecular pathways between DEHP exposure and renal carcinogenicity. By identifying their targets and exploring their mechanisms of action, we aim to provide a scientific basis for developing preventive measures and seeking safer alternatives to DEHP. Furthermore, understanding the carcinogenic potential of DEHP and its urogenital toxicity will improve our ability to assess its safety in daily use and medical applications, providing a foundation for establishing relevant safety standards and protective measures.

Methods

The data in this study were obtained from various online database platforms and software, including PubChem, Swiss Target Prediction, DAVID, and FUMA. The detailed databases and tools used and the corresponding URLs are listed in Appendix A.

Preliminary network analysis of DEHP toxicity

Integrating network search algorithms with biotoxicity prediction methods in relevant software tools has enabled the utilization of structural models to predict the toxicity associated with DEHP compounds. We employed three software tools—namely, ProTox 3.0(https://tox.charite.de/protox3/), AD-METlab 2.0(https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/service/evaluation/cal), and Vnn-ADMET (https://vnnadmet.bhsai.org/vnnadmet/), as tentative screening tools to obtain preliminary yet accurate information regarding DEHP-induced toxicity.

Collection of DEHP targets

The standard structure and SMILE representation of DEHP were identified via a search for “di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate” in the PubChem database(https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [21]. Using this structural information, potential DEHP targets were extracted from the ChEMBL database, with a focus on “Homo sapiens”. The SMILE string of the DEHP was subsequently uploaded to the Swiss target prediction platform(http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) to uncover additional targets. The resulting structures were examined rigorously for consistency. The refined and unified targets were used to create a comprehensive target library for DEHP.

Selection of the kidney toxicity-related target network

We conducted a comprehensive search of domestic and foreign literature, as well as the GeneCards databases (https://www.genecards.org/) and TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/), using the keywords “Renal Cell Carcinoma” and “Carcinoma, Renal Cell” to identify relevant targets. To ensure that the genes obtained were highly related to RCC and toxic effects, we set the “score” threshold to the median value and selected genes with a score above the median value to establish a kidney toxicity target library. Additionally, we used a Venn diagram to identify common potential targets between DEHP targets and renal cell carcinoma targets, considering the intersecting portion as potential targets for DEHP-induced RCC toxicity.

Construction of the protein‒protein network and screening of core targets

The intersecting genes identified as potential targets of DEHP-induced RCC toxicity were analyzed via the STRING database (http://string-db.org/). The analysis parameters were set to restrict the species to “Homo sapiens” and use a “Minimum required interaction score” of “High Confidence > 0.7”. These settings ensured a comprehensive analysis of the active target proteins corresponding to the target genes.

The STRING results were subsequently imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1, https://cytoscape.org/) to facilitate network biology visualization and analysis. Cytoscape was employed to calculate the molecular connections of network nodes and edges, enabling the construction of a protein‒protein interaction (PPI) network diagram. The core targets of DEHP-linked RCC were identified via criteria such as betweenness centrality and closeness centrality greater than the median, average shortest path length greater than the median, and degree value greater than twice the median.

Gene function and pathway enrichment analysis of target proteins

To investigate the biological functions of potential targets involved in DEHP-induced RCC toxicity, we used the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) combined with RStudio software to perform gene ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. GO analysis was used to assess biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), clarifying the main biological functions. Additionally, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant pathways associated with potential targets of DEHP-induced RCC toxicity. Furthermore, we conducted a focused KEGG enrichment analysis on the core targets via the same tools to delve deeper into the pathways implicated in renal cell carcinoma. This analysis aimed to elucidate the key signaling pathways involved in biological processes. Finally, visual analysis was performed to interpret and present the results of the GO and KEGG analyses effectively, ensuring a comprehensive understanding of our findings.

Molecular docking of DEHP and key targets

The molecular docking approach was utilized to further analyze the intermolecular interactions between DEHP and the core target proteins identified in our study, predicting binding modes and affinities.

We retrieved the DEHP molecular structure from the PubChem database. The small molecules were subsequently input into the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/), with filters set to “Reviewed” entries and species limited to “Homo sapiens”. To ensure comprehensive protein coverage, we selected the UniProt ID corresponding to the longest amino acid sequence. The obtained UniProt ID was then used to search the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/), where the crystal structure with the highest binding score was downloaded as the docking receptor. Prior to docking, water molecules and original ligands were removed from the target protein structure via PyMOL software (https://pymol.org/). The DEHP ligand structure, sourced from PubChem, underwent energy minimization via molecular mechanics optimization to refine its conformation.

Next, the ligand and protein were uploaded, and molecular docking was performed using CB-DOCK2 [22]. We uploaded the protein PDB file and the DEHP SDF file to CB-DOCK2, selecting the “auto blind docking” mode to obtain the docking results. CB-DOCK2 conducts fully automatic protein‒ligand blind docking calculations in four steps: (1) input and quality check of protein and ligand structural data; (2) preprocessing of structural data, including the repair of missing atoms and hydrogen atoms, as well as the removal of water molecules and other heteroatoms; (3) protein pocket detection, spatial parameter estimation, and molecular docking on the basis of Vina scoring and template matching with conformational transfer; and (4) comprehensive processing and visualization of the calculation results. The results were then visualized in two dimensions via Discovery Studio 2019 (https://www.3ds.com/products/) and Maestro 12.8 software (https://www.schrodinger.com/platform/products/maestro/). To further validate the docking results, we also used AutoDock Tools 1.5.7 and AutoDock Vina [23, 24] for additional analysis.

Results

Initial network assessment of DEHP toxicity

Upon integrating the results from various software tools, we established the toxicity profile of DEHP, revealing that its primary toxicity endpoint was associated with carcinogenicity, encompassing tumors of the reproductive system and liver cancer. The potential carcinogenic mechanisms of DEHP involve the activation of nuclear receptors and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα), interference with estrogen receptor α and aryl hydrocarbon receptors, and the induction of oxidative stress.

ProTox-II toxicity class

DEHP is classified into Toxicity Class III, indicating that it has moderate toxicity potential. LD50 value: The predicted median lethal dose (LD50) for DEHP is approximately 1500 mg/kg, suggesting a moderate level of acute toxicity.

ADMETlab 2.0 ADMET properties

DEHP exhibited moderate absorption and distribution. It showed potential for bioaccumulation, with a logP value of 7.5, suggesting significant lipophilicity. Toxicity prediction: This tool indicated possible hepatotoxicity and reproductive toxicity, which is consistent with the known effects of DEHP.

Vnn-ADMET toxicity profile

DEHP is predicted to be associated with a high risk of endocrine disruption, specifically affecting estrogen receptor pathways. Metabolic Stability: The compound demonstrated moderate metabolic stability, indicating that it is likely to persist in biological systems for a considerable duration.

Discussion

Urological malignancies impose a significant socioeconomic burden and profoundly affect patients’ quality of life. Despite advancements in diagnosis and treatment, the underlying mechanisms driving these cancers remain incompletely understood [13]. In China, kidney cancer is the third most common genitourinary tumor after bladder and prostate cancers, accounting for 2–3% of adult malignant tumors, with its incidence increasing annually [14]. This study reports the potential carcinogenicity and nephrotoxicity of DEHP through toxicology database analysis and literature search. Di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate is a persistent environmental pollutant known for its endocrine-disrupting properties, leading to substantial health implications. Exposure to DEHP has been associated with hepatotoxicity, reproductive impairments, and an increased risk of various cancers [33]. Understanding its toxicological effects will help us better prevent and avoid harmful effects. Our study revealed multiple molecular intersections between the toxicity of DEHP and the development of kidney cancer, involving key molecules such as BCL2, CASP3, MMP9, BCL2L1, and CTSS, among others. Enrichment analysis of these molecules revealed several potential pathogenic pathways, including the neuroactive ligand‒receptor interaction pathway, apoptosis, pathways in cancer, and calcium signaling pathways. Molecular docking of these core targets revealed that the binding affinity of DEHP to these targets may lead to adverse reactions. Overall, we employed an integrative approach that combines network toxicology, molecular docking, and bioinformatics to explore the mechanisms underlying DEHP-induced carcinogenesis in the kidney.

Previous studies have established a correlation between DEHP exposure and the development and progression of various tumors via diverse pathogenic mechanisms and signaling pathways. For instance, Kim et al. demonstrated that DEHP promotes the progression of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by enhancing cell proliferation, inflammation, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [34]. An evaluation of 111 cases revealed that DEHP exposure increased the risk of papillary thyroid carcinoma fivefold [35]. In breast cancer research, cumulative exposure to more than 10,000 mg of DEHP is associated with a nearly twofold increase in estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer rates [36]. Furthermore, DEHP exposure at 10⁻⁹ M significantly promotes tumor sphere formation in breast cancer stem cells, with increased levels of stem cell marker expression, Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) pathway activation, and increased expression of the oncogene ΔNp63α [37]. With respect to urological malignancies, MEHHP, a primary metabolite of DEHP, has been associated with an increased risk of urothelial cancer in patients with chronic kidney disease [12]. Urinary concentrations of DEHP metabolites are linked to an elevated risk of prostate cancer. Phthalates may activate the MAPK/AP-1 pathway, potentially promoting cell proliferation in prostate cancer [9]. DEHP has been detected in numerous urine samples, and previous literature reviews have confirmed its significant impact on the occurrence and development of various tumors. Therefore, further studies on the nephrotoxicity and carcinogenicity of DEHP, particularly with respect to renal cell carcinoma, are extremely important.

Core targets analysis of DEHP with RCC

BCL2 is a well-documented antiapoptotic protein that inhibits cell death, thereby promoting cell survival [38]. Previous research has demonstrated that increased BCL2 expression is correlated with reduced sensitivity of RCC cell lines to the anti-Fas antibody CH11 [39]. Specifically, RCC cells with elevated Fas expression were more responsive to CH11-induced apoptosis, whereas downregulation of BCL2 enhanced this apoptotic response, highlighting the protective role of BCL2 in Fas-mediated cell death. Additionally, venetoclax, a BCL2 inhibitor, has been shown to increase RCC sensitivity to sunitinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, suggesting a potential synergistic therapeutic strategy [40]. Similarly, BCL2L1 (Bcl-xl), another member of the Bcl-2 family, inhibits apoptosis by binding to proapoptotic proteins such as Bax and Bak, preventing mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and subsequent cell death [41, 42]. Inhibitors targeting Bcl-xl, including ABT-737 and its derivatives, have demonstrated efficacy in selectively inducing apoptosis in cancer cells, thereby increasing the effectiveness of existing cancer treatments [43].

Caspase 3 (CASP3) is essential for executing apoptosis and necrosis, significantly impacting tumorigenesis and cancer progression [44]. Activated by initiator proteases, CASP3 cleaves substrates, leading to cell death [45]. In renal cell carcinoma, CASP3 plays diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic roles; its downregulation increases RCC sensitivity to sunitinib, suggesting new treatment avenues [46]. Furthermore, machine learning models incorporating apoptosis regulators such as CASP3 have shown significant prognostic predictive power in clear cell RCC. Concurrently, MMP-9 promotes extracellular matrix degradation, facilitating tissue remodeling, inflammation, and tumor progression. DEHP-induced MMP-9 overexpression is linked to breast and bladder cancers [28] and, in RCC, correlates with increased invasiveness, metastasis, and poor prognosis, making MMP-9 a potential therapeutic target [47, 48]. Together, DEHP modulates CASP3 and MMP-9, enhancing RCC aggressiveness by inhibiting apoptosis and promoting metastasis, thereby identifying critical pathways for targeted interventions. The CTSS, which is involved in lysosomal protein degradation, plays roles in antigen presentation, inflammation, and tissue remodeling. In RCC, elevated CTSS expression is associated with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis [49, 50], positioning the CTSS as a biomarker and therapeutic target for RCC progression. APP, located on chromosome 21, is crucial for amyloid-beta (Aβ) peptide production and is primarily linked to Alzheimer’s disease [51]. In addition to neurodegeneration, APP influences cancer-related cellular processes [52]. While its direct role in RCC is indirect, APP-mediated signaling pathways may impact RCC development and progression, warranting further investigation. DEHP exposure likely exacerbates RCC through multiple pathways by modulating proteins such as BCL2, CASP3, MMP-9, BCL2L1, and CTSS. This modulation affects apoptosis, extracellular matrix dynamics, and cellular signaling, promoting tumor survival, invasiveness, and metastasis. Understanding these interactions will aid in the development of targeted therapies and highlight the need to reduce DEHP exposure for RCC prevention and management.

The development of therapies that restore CASP3 activity or inhibit MMP-9 expression may reduce RCC progression and improve survival rates. Additionally, public health initiatives aimed at reducing DEHP exposure could serve as preventive measures to lower the incidence of RCC.

Pathways enrichment analysis of DEHP with RCC

Our pathway enrichment analysis identified neuroactive ligand‒receptor interactions as a significant pathway involved in DEHP-induced renal cell carcinoma carcinogenicity. Nerves are essential components within the RCC tumor microenvironment, with peripheral nerve invasion closely correlated with tumor progression and poorer prognosis [53]. Neurogenically mediated metabolic changes in vascular endothelial cells, such as the suppression of endothelial oxidative phosphorylation, have been shown to facilitate RCC growth via angiogenesis [54, 55]. In the context of our findings, key targets identified in the KEGG pathways, including CASP3, play regulatory roles in RCC cell dynamics, suggesting that DEHP exposure may significantly contribute to both the incidence and metastatic potential of RCC. Moreover, the “Pathways in Cancer” pathway is critically involved in the mechanisms underlying DEHP-induced RCC toxicity. This pathway is integral to the development and progression of various malignancies [56] and influences RCC through several mechanisms, including signal transduction (e.g., the PI3K/AKT, mTOR, Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK pathways), angiogenesis (VEGF signaling), and gene mutations (e.g., VHL, BAP1 mutations) [57, 58]. Additionally, given the crucial regulatory functions of targets such as BCL2, BCL2L1, and MMP9 in tumor biology [59–61], we speculate that DEHP may cause renal tumor cells to resist apoptosis through this pathway and promote tumor progression and invasion.

Our enrichment analysis also highlighted a possible relationship between DEHP toxicity in RCC and the dysregulation of apoptosis pathways. Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is notably compromised in RCC, particularly in clear cell renal carcinoma. The apoptosis repressor with a caspase recruitment domain (ARC) significantly disrupts apoptotic signaling by inhibiting both extrinsic and intrinsic pathways, which are vital for RCC cell survival and proliferation. The suppression of mitochondrial apoptosis by ARCs enhances the antiapoptotic functions of the Bcl-2 family, positioning it as a potential therapeutic target [62]. Wang et al. reported that DEHP exposure activates the p53 signaling pathway, thereby promoting apoptosis [63]. These data, combined with the roles of key KEGG pathway targets (e.g., BCL2 and CASP3), suggest that DEHP may impact RCC incidence and metastatic capabilities by modulating apoptotic processes.

Furthermore, our analysis revealed an association between DEHP-induced nephrotoxicity and calcium signaling pathways. Dysregulated calcium signaling is a fundamental regulator of tumor progression and is implicated in processes such as epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and resistance to therapies [64]. This finding aligns with preclinical and clinical evidence indicating that calcium signaling dysregulation is central to the molecular reprogramming involved in RCC onset and progression [65]. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesize that DEHP exposure may contribute to aberrant calcium signaling in RCC cells, thereby promoting tumorigenesis, disease progression, and the development of invasive and metastatic phenotypes. Additionally, DEHP exposure is correlated with elevated creatinine levels and the onset of chronic kidney disease (CKD), with severe cases progressing to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), necessitating dialysis [66, 67]. Chou et al. reported a potential link between urinary levels of phthalate metabolites in CKD patients and an increased risk of urothelial carcinoma [12]. Moreover, an increased incidence of cancers, including RCC, has been observed in dialysis patients [68]. These findings suggest that DEHP exposure may be associated with an increased risk of RCC in patients undergoing dialysis, potentially due to cumulative toxic exposure and compromised renal function.

These insights highlight potential therapeutic targets within these pathways. Interventions aimed at modulating neuroactive ligand‒receptor interactions or inhibiting specific signaling pathways could offer new avenues for RCC treatment. Future studies should investigate the efficacy of drugs targeting these pathways in preclinical and clinical settings. Targeting ARC or restoring apoptotic pathways could increase the effectiveness of existing RCC therapies. Clinical trials assessing agents that modulate apoptotic regulators may provide novel treatment options for RCC patients. Therapeutic strategies that normalize calcium signaling may mitigate RCC progression in patients with DEHP exposure. Furthermore, monitoring DEHP levels in CKD and dialysis patients could be crucial for the early detection and prevention of RCC. Future research should focus on developing interventions that reduce DEHP accumulation in these high-risk populations.

Innovation and limitations

Our study employs an innovative methodology that utilizes network toxicology and molecular docking analyses to predict the potential carcinogenic risk of DEHP exposure in relation to RCC. By integrating vast amounts of bioinformatics data and big data analysis technologies, network toxicology allows for a comprehensive and systematic analysis of complex interactions between toxins and biological systems. This approach goes beyond traditional toxicology methods that focus on single targets and often rely on animal or cell tests involving significant costs and ethical concerns. By considering multiple targets and their interactions, our method enhances the understanding of the multifaceted biological effects triggered by toxins and aids in toxicity assessment and risk management strategies. Ultimately, this approach not only addresses limitations related to time, cost, and ethical issues but also lays a solid foundation for future research and potential clinical translation strategies, which may improve outcomes for patients with urological malignancies.

However, our study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. We did not provide direct experimental evidence demonstrating that DEHP toxicity is specific to RCC. The proposed interactions between DEHP and key genes such as BCL2, CASP3, and MMP9 remained theoretical until they were confirmed by laboratory studies. Moreover, our reliance on bioinformatics tools may introduce biases that could impact our findings. Computational predictions are subject to uncertainties due to database biases and limitations inherent in prediction algorithms. Acknowledging these uncertainties and the lack of empirical validation helps readers understand the constraints of our study. To address these limitations, future research should focus on experimental validation through in vitro and in vivo studies to substantiate the predicted interactions and clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying DEHP-induced RCC toxicity. Techniques such as gene knockdown or overexpression could be employed to investigate the roles of BCL2, CASP3, and MMP9 in DEHP-exposed renal cells. Additionally, long-term exposure models that mimic environmental DEHP levels would provide more accurate assessments of its chronic effects. Enhancing bioinformatics analyses by incorporating multiple databases and cross-validation methods could also reduce biases and improve the reliability of computational predictions. Such comprehensive investigations are warranted, as controlling exposure to environmental pollutants such as DEHP could influence cancer incidence, leading to improved prevention and management strategies for RCC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our research offers an examination of the probable renal carcinogenesis induced by DEHP through network toxicology and molecular docking analysis. We identified 82 putative targets associated with DEHP-induced carcinogenicity and further extracted 25 core targets, including BCL2, CASP3, and MMP9, which may play important roles in mediating the toxic effects of DEHP on RCC. Our research explored the potential of DEHP to initiate renal cell carcinogenesis through network toxicology and molecular docking analysis. By scrutinizing pivotal signaling networks, specifically focal adhesion and cancer pathways, DEHP exposure may facilitate renal cell carcinogenesis by specifically interacting with the BCL2, CASP3, MMP9, BCL2L1, CTSS, and APP genes. This study not only elucidates the molecular mechanisms of DEHP-induced RCC toxicity and identifies potential therapeutic targets but also demonstrates the utility of network toxicology and molecular docking in analyzing the toxicity and molecular biology of environmental pollutants. New research methods, including network toxicology and molecular docking, can foster greater collaboration among urologists, oncologists, environmental biologists, and industry professionals. These methods will enable a more thorough understanding of how environmental pollutants, such as plastics, affect the risk and progression of diseases, such as tumors, and will aid in the development of multidisciplinary strategies to address these issues.
