Deresuscitation Informed by Ultrasound in Patients With Septic Shock Trial
Authors: Brendan Innes, Christopher Allison, Nicholas Seidler, Burton Shen, Andrew Fried, Gary Phillips, Andrew Levinson, Adeel Abbasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) can be used at the point‐of‐care to identify venous overload and end‐organ congestion in an effort to individually tailor resuscitation and deresuscitation in patients with septic shock.MethodsWe conducted a pilot randomized controlled feasibility trial of VExUS‐guided fluid management in adult patients with septic shock. Participants were randomized to VExUS‐guided fluid management or usual care. Daily ultrasounds were performed on all participants; in the VExUS‐guided study arm, the clinical care team was informed of the VExUS results and given a fluid management recommendation (the intervention).ResultsWe enrolled 19 participants; 12 (63%) were randomized to the VExUS arm. The intervention period fluid balance was −65 mL (interquartile range [IQR] −1733 to 2016) in the VExUS arm compared to 2608 mL (IQR −435 to 4460) in the usual care arm (p = .21). The secondary outcomes of acute kidney injury, respiratory failure, hospital, and 30‐day mortality did not differ significantly between the 2 arms; although the rates were lower in the VExUS arm.ConclusionsWe demonstrate that VExUS‐guided fluid management in patients with septic shock is feasible, was not associated with an increased risk of adverse events, and may reduce the volume of fluids administered.

Intravenous (IV) fluid resuscitation is a cornerstone of sepsis management. 1 In patients with septic shock, isotonic IV fluid is administered to restore intravascular volume, augment cardiac output and improve organ perfusion. IV fluids are also given to correct electrolyte abnormalities and replete fluid losses leading to excess fluid administration and contributing to positive fluid balances frequently observed in patients with septic shock. Over‐resuscitation may harm patients as a positive fluid balance is associated with fewer ventilator free days and increased mortality. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Patient‐tailored fluid resuscitation in patients with sepsis and septic shock is one potential strategy to limit over‐resuscitation and was listed as a research priority by the Surviving Sepsis Campaign in 2023. 8

Interventions aimed at lowering positive fluid balances in critically ill patients include adopting a conservative fluid management strategy of limiting IV fluid resuscitation and maintenance fluids, concentrating medications, and deresuscitation—the active removal of fluid from patients using diuretics or ultrafiltration. Compared to IV fluid resuscitation, deresuscitation has been understudied in patients with septic shock, but more robustly studied in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 In the 2006 “Comparison of Two Fluid‐Management Strategies in Acute Lung Injury (FACTT)” trial, ARDS patients deresuscitated with diuretics experienced a higher number of ventilator‐free days compared to patients managed with a liberal fluid strategy. 12 A 2022 study of heterogenous ventilated intensive care unit (ICU) patients found that a conservative resuscitation strategy coupled with a deresuscitation protocol resulted in a 970 mL lower fluid balance at day 3 compared to usual care (p < .001). 13 A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis of critically ill septic patients found that patients who received active deresuscitation experienced an increase in ventilatorfree days and fewer ICU days, but the practice had no effect on mortality and was limited by the size and quality of the studies included in the analysis. 14

Non‐invasive point‐of‐care ultrasound (POCUS) is commonly used to guide IV fluid resuscitation in patients with shock. 15 A 2023 meta‐analysis of 12 randomized controlled trials found that POCUS‐directed resuscitation of patients with septic shock reduced mortality (relative risk = 0.78; [0.65–0.94]) and the volume of IV fluid administered (mean difference: −1.02 L [−1.28 to −0.75]) in the first 24 hours of care. The venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) was introduced in 2020 as a systematic approach to identify end‐organ congestion, venous overload, and fluid tolerance at the bedside. 16 The VExUS score correlates with mean systemic filling pressure and pulmonary artery catheter measurements of volume overload and has the potential to direct deresuscitation. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 We designed the DeResuscitation Informed by Ultrasound for patients with septic shock (DRIUS) pilot randomized controlled trial to determine the feasibility of VExUS‐guided deresuscitation in patients with septic shock.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted our trial in a combined medical and surgical ICU at a tertiary‐care academic medical center between April 2021 and February 2023 (IRB# 202121). The trial was registered prior to commencement and there were no deviations from the protocol (NCT04921319). Informed consent was obtained from the patient or their surrogate medical decision maker prior to study enrollment. Patients were recruited for enrollment if they were at least 18 years old and admitted to the ICU with a suspected primary diagnosis of septic shock. At the time of screening, patients were required to have a known or suspected infection, be in shock requiring vasopressor use to maintain a systolic blood pressure >90 mmHg or mean arterial pressure >65 mmHg, and or have a persistent serum lactate >2 mmol/L following resuscitation with 30 mL/kg of IV fluids. 21 Patients were not eligible for enrollment if they were pregnant, incarcerated, had a primary admitting diagnosis other than septic shock (including hemorrhagic shock, acute coronary syndrome, acute pulmonary edema, status asthmaticus, a drug overdose, an acute cerebral vascular accident, a primary burn or trauma injury), had received cardiopulmonary resuscitation within the previous 24 hours, or required immediate surgery, had a condition that might interfere with the VExUS (active atrial fibrillation or flutter, cirrhosis, portal venous thrombosis, severe chronic kidney disease with estimated glomerular filtration rate <15 mL/min or were on renal replacement therapy), or had been admitted to the ICU for >24 hours. Enrolled participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to VExUS‐guided fluid management or usual care by a computer‐generated random number sequence. There were no post‐randomization exclusions or contamination. This assignment was concealed until after randomization to prevent selection bias. Study investigators remained blinded to the results until the conclusion of the study.

Ultrasound Protocol and Intervention‐Non‐binding Fluid Balance Target

Participants in both arms had a VExUS performed once daily after study enrollment on days 1 through 5 of their ICU stay or until ICU discharge, whichever came first. This was defined as the intervention period. The ultrasounds were performed by 1 of 3 trained physicians sonologists as originally described. 16 The physician sonologists had a foundation of formal POCUS training during residency and or fellowship and were trained to perform VExUS assessments through didactic video instruction, hands‐on ultrasound sessions, and testing prior to the start of the study. Using a phased array or curvilinear array transducer (GE Venue, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), the sonologists first assessed for venous overload by measuring the size of the inferior vena cava (IVC) and evaluating for respirophasic variation. They next assessed end‐organ congestion by analyzing electrocardiography‐augmented Doppler waveforms of the hepatic, portal and intrarenal veins, classifying them as normal, mildly or severely congested (Figure 1). A participant was categorized as having a VExUS score of “0—no venous congestion” if the maximum IVC diameter was <2 cm and normal flow patterns were observed in the hepatic, portal and intrarenal veins; a score of “1—mild venous congestion” if the IVC maximum diameter was ≥2 cm with normal or mild abnormalities of flow in one of either the hepatic, portal or intrarenal veins; a score of “2—moderate venous congestion” if the IVC maximum diameter was ≥2 cm with a severe venous congestion flow pattern in 1 venous system; and a score of “3—severe venous congestion” if the IVC maximum diameter was ≥2 cm with a severe venous congestion flow pattern in multiple venous systems. 16 The sonologists also performed a POCUS lung examination to quantify extravascular lung water following the VExUS. 22 An external and blinded ultrasound fellowship‐trained reviewer with extensive experience in VExUS clinically and as a researcher independently scored the study ultrasounds to evaluate the interrater reliability of the VExUS scores.

For participants in the intervention arm, the clinical care teams were immediately informed of the VExUS scores and given a suggested and predefined but non‐binding fluid balance target for the ensuing 24 hours (intervention): VExUS score of 0—“The patient will likely tolerate more IV fluid if clinically needed,” VExUS score of 1—“The patient has evidence of mild venous congestion. We recommend targeting a net neutral or negative fluid balance with diuresis,” VExUS scores of 2–3—“The patient has evidence of moderate to severe venous congestion. We recommend targeting a fluid balance of negative 1‐2 liters with diuresis.” The clinical care team was blinded to the VExUS results for participants in the control arm and the results of the POCUS lung examination for all participants.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was the intervention period fluid balance, defined as the sum of all recorded fluid intake in the ICU (including IV fluid boluses, maintenance IV fluid, and medication carrier fluids; excluding tube feeds and enteral free water) minus all recorded output in the ICU (including urine, enteral tubes, and drains) at the end of the intervention period. Secondary outcomes included the intervention period fluid balance plus pre‐randomization fluids, fluid balance at ICU discharge, acute kidney injury defined by the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria; respiratory failure defined as oxygen supplementation via high‐flow nasal cannula, non‐invasive ventilation, or mechanical ventilation; multisystem organ dysfunction as defined by an increase in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, ICU and hospital length of stay, hospital, and 30‐day mortality.

Statistical Analysis

Participant demographics were summarized with median and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables, categorical variables were described using counts and percentages. A box plot depicted the net fluid change while the Kruskal‐Wallis method was used to compare the median change in fluid volume during the intervention period between the 2 study arms. Fisher's exact test was used to determine statistical significance for categorical variables and Kruskal‐Wallis rank test was used for continuous variables (p < .05). A Cohen's Kappa (κ) statistic of interrater agreement was used to compare the categorical VExUS scores between the study sonologists and the external reviewer. All analyses were done using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

We screened 95 participants and enrolled 19 participants (Figure 2). Twelve participants (63%) were randomized to the VExUS arm. The median age of participants in the VExUS arm was 67 years (IQR 54–77) compared to 73 years (IQR 70–82) in the usual care arm (Table 1). Six participants (50%) were male in the VExUS arm and 2 participants (29%) were male in the usual care arm. The median serum lactate was similar in both arms, 3 (IQR 2–4) in the VExUS arm and 3 (IQR 2–5) in the usual care arm. The median Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II scores were 15 (IQR 12–20) and 23 (IQR 19–33) in the VExUS and usual care arms, respectively. The most common site of infection in participants in both arms was the urinary tract (58% in the VExUS arm and 43% in the usual care arm). All participants were included in our intention to treat analysis including one participant in the VExUS arm who dropped out on day 2.

Participants received a median volume of 5378 mL (IQR 3975–8200) and 5100 mL (IQR 1850–7900) prior to randomization to the VExUS and usual care arms, respectively (p = .18) (Table 1). There was no significant difference in our primary outcome between the 2 arms; −65 mL (IQR −1733 to 2016) in the VExUS arm and 2608 mL (IQR −435 to 4460) in the usual care arm (p = .205; Table 2). We also observed no significant differences in the secondary outcomes between the study arms. The intervention period fluid balance plus pre‐randomization fluids was 5998 mL (IQR 3821–7764) in the VExUS arm and 7465 mL (IQR 4244–9205) in the usual care arm (p = .27). Figure 3 shows the intervention period fluid balance plus pre‐randomization fluids throughout the intervention period. Organ dysfunction, defined by an increase in the SOFA score during the intervention period, occurred in 2 (16.7%) participants in the VExUS arm compared to one (14.3%) participant in the control arm. Fewer participants in the VExUS arm developed acute kidney injury and respiratory failure. VExUS participants had a lower hospital and 30‐day mortality. However, none of these differences were significant. One participant died in each study arm prior to ICU discharge.

Deresuscitation and Ultrasound Assessments

The median ICU length of stay in the VExUS arm was 2.8 days (IQR 1.7–4.3) and 2.3 days (IQR 1.8–6.3) in the control arm; 5 (26%) study participants had an ICU length of stay ≥ 5 days (Table 2). A total of 42 VExUS and POCUS lung examinations were performed. The distribution of VExUS scores summed over the intervention period is displayed in Table S1. The interrater agreement between the study sonologists and the external reviewer on the VExUS scores had a κ value of 0.8, 0.8 in the VExUS arm and 0.9 in the control arm (Table S2).

For the individual components of the VExUS, the agreements were varied: a κ value of 0.9 for the IVC score, 0.7 for the hepatic vein score, 0.5 for the portal vein score, and a κ value of 0.6 for the intrarenal vein score. Table S3 lists the VExUS scores and the results of the lung ultrasound assessment of extravascular lung water. Venous excess ultrasound scores of 0 and 1 had similar degrees of extravascular lung water. The VExUS assessment of mild venous congestion or a VExUS score of 2–3 concurred with mild extravascular lung water on the POCUS lung examination, with a κ value of 0.8.

Discussion

We demonstrate that the VExUS can be used to identify fluid overload and safely guide resuscitation and deresuscitation in patients with septic shock. Despite a fluid balance difference of −2736 mL at ICU discharge between the 2 study arms, we did not observe higher rates of organ injury, adverse events, or mortality in the VExUS‐guided fluid management arm.

The median ICU length of stay in our study cohort was under 3 days, longer ICU lengths of stay may have resulted in a larger difference in the net fluid balance at ICU discharge. Our study findings suggest that the multi‐end‐organ VExUS may provide a more sensitive assessment of systemic fluid tolerance compared to lung ultrasound. The VExUS also identifies venous overload and end‐organ congestion reliably. In our study, the interrater agreement in the VExUS scores between our study sonologists and an independent expert reviewer was strong. However, the agreements for each component of the VExUS varied. This is consistent with a recent study that found a similarly strong agreement between VExUS scores by attending physician sonologists across 4 different specialties and lower agreements for the components of the VExUS. 23

Our study adds to our understanding of the utility of VExUS to target resuscitations in several ways. First, fluid management informed by VExUS offers the potential to individualize care by identifying critically ill patients most likely to benefit from deresuscitation. The VExUS includes a Doppler assessment of venous flow within target organs proximal to the site of tissue injury, potentially providing a more reliable assessment of venous congestion than other commonly utilized clinical markers. Changes in venous flow in the hepatic, portal, and intrarenal veins relate to biomarkers of volume overload. 16 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 In our study, on the initial VExUS performed on day 1, mild venous congestion was observed in 9 participants (47%) and severe venous congestion in 2 participants (11%). Second, our study identified a high prevalence of patients with end‐organ congestion due at least in part to the volume of IV fluids administered prior to randomization, a median of 5100 mL (IQR 3350–7900) over a median time from triage to randomization of 19 hours (IQR 11–41). This included IV fluid boluses and medication carrier fluids administered in the emergency department, and for 2 participants IV fluids they received prior to arrival in the emergency department. Excluding medication carrier fluids and fluids administered prior to arrival in the emergency department, participants received a median of 2500 mL (IQR 1000–4372) bolus IV fluids prior to randomization or a median of 27 mL/kg (IQR 11–46), approximately the fixed volume recommended by the Surviving Sepsis Guidelines of at least 30 mL/kg. 33 This suggests that for a subset of patients with septic shock the recommended 30 mL/kg may be potentially harmful and a conservative or restrictive fluid strategy alone may be insufficient at preventing the complications associated with volume overload, further suggesting the utility of targeting treatment with VExUS.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a single‐center study, our study lacks external validity potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, as a pilot trial our sample size resulted in a randomization imbalance and random imbalance in baseline characteristics between the 2 arms, and was underpowered to detect a significant difference in our primary outcome.

Patient enrollment was significantly impeded by the severe restrictions placed on hospital visiting hours during the COVID‐19 pandemic, limiting our ability to obtain written consent from the legally authorized representatives of patients who met criteria for enrollment. Fourth, adherence to the recommended fluid balance target was only 72%, potentially limiting the effect size of our intervention. Given the design of our pilot randomized controlled feasibility trial, the recommendation was non‐binding, allowing the clinical care team to consider standard of care clinical factors when making resuscitation and deresuscitation decisions. Finally, a technical limitation of the VExUS is that it cannot be performed reliably in all patients, further limiting trial enrollment and excluding a potentially significant subset of patients with septic shock, including those with atrial fibrillation and chronic liver or kidney disease.

Conclusion

The use of VExUS to guide fluid management in patients with septic shock is feasible, was not associated with an increased risk of adverse events, and resulted in a lower but non‐significant net fluid balance at ICU discharge.
