Preoperative C-reactive protein as a predictor of conversion and complications in laparoscopic cholecystectomy for acute cholecystitis
Authors: Parham Khoshdani Farahani, Shakiba Khosravi Nejat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLaparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) is the gold standard for gallbladder surgery but may require conversion to open surgery in complicated cases. While preoperative C-reactive protein (CRP) is known to reflect inflammation, its predictive role in surgical outcomes remains debated. This study evaluated CRP as a predictor of surgical outcomes in acute cholecystitis and uncomplicated gallbladder stones, explicitly excluding choledocholithiasis to isolate gallbladder-specific inflammatory predictors.Materials and methodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 180 patients undergoing LC for acute cholecystitis (n = 76), chronic cholecystitis (n = 14), or uncomplicated cholelithiasis (n = 90). Preoperative CRP levels were measured, and ROC analysis determined optimal thresholds for predicting conversion and complications (hydrops, necrosis, phlegmon, abscess, bleeding). Due to incomplete albumin data (17.8% missing), the CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR) could not be robustly analyzed; thus, CRP was the primary focus. Patients with common bile duct (CBD) stones (n = 32) were excluded to eliminate confounding biliary obstruction. Demographic and clinical data of the patients were recorded, and statistical analysis was performed using R software.ResultsAcute cholecystitis patients had higher CRP (25.4 vs. 7.1 mg/L, p < 0.001), longer hospital stays (7.4 vs. 2.2 days, p = 0.001), and more complications (e.g., necrosis: 16.7% vs. 1.1%, p < 0.001). CRP predicted conversion to open surgery with Area Under the Curve (AUC) 0.964 (optimal threshold: 7.5 mg/L, sensitivity 100%) and complications (AUC 0.899–0.983). Chronic cholecystitis patients had lower CRP (4.2 mg/L) and no conversions.ConclusionsCRP demonstrated high accuracy (AUC 0.964) in predicting conversion and complications in acute cholecystitis, with thresholds actionable for surgical planning. Its utility in uncomplicated cholelithiasis is limited.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03212-0.

Introduction

Managing patients with acute cholecystitis or gallstones via laparoscopic cholecystectomy poses challenges in predicting surgical complexity [1]. The Tokyo Guidelines 2018 highlight a knowledge gap regarding the role of CRP as a marker for surgical difficulty [2, 3]. A reliable predictive tool could improve outcomes, reduce complications, and optimize preoperative planning [4]. CRP’s predictive value is confined to acute inflammatory states (e.g., acute cholecystitis), as uncomplicated gallstones lack systemic inflammation.

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the standard treatment for these conditions, offers advantages like shorter hospital stays and fewer complications [5]. However, the risk of conversion to open surgery remains, impacting outcomes and hospitalization duration [6]. Identifying predictors for such conversions is critical for effective treatment planning [7, 8].

This study aims to investigate the predictive value of the preoperative CAR in identifying the risk of conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy in patients with acute cholecystitis, as defined by the Tokyo Guidelines 2018. While CAR reflects both inflammatory and nutritional status, limitations in the availability of albumin data necessitated a focus on CRP alone as a standalone marker. Prior evidence supports CRP’s utility in acute cholecystitis, justifying its selection for this analysis [9].

The primary objective is to assess the relationship between preoperative CRP levels and the complexity of laparoscopic cholecystectomy, particularly in predicting intraoperative challenges and conversion to open surgery [10]. Although patients with both acute cholecystitis and uncomplicated gallbladder stones were considered, CRP’s predictive relevance is primarily confined to acute inflammatory cases [11].

Identifying CRP as a reliable preoperative indicator has the potential to significantly enhance risk stratification protocols, support clinical decision-making, and reduce conversion rates, ultimately improving surgical outcomes [12]. The findings may contribute to the development of standardized preoperative assessment guidelines, aiding surgeons in evaluating case complexity and determining the appropriate level of surgical expertise required [13].

This study evaluates CRP as a predictor of surgical outcomes in acute cholecystitis, explicitly excluding choledocholithiasis and uncomplicated cholelithiasis to isolate gallbladder-specific inflammation. While CRP is known to reflect acute inflammation, its role in uncomplicated gallstones is physiologically implausible and thus not examined.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study analyzed 180 patients who underwent LC for acute cholecystitis between 2023 and 2024. Data were extracted from electronic medical records, including preoperative CRP levels, operative notes, and postoperative outcomes. Due to the retrospective design, informed consent was waived with an opt-out option; all data were anonymized.

After obtaining the ethics code (IR.ABZUMS.REC.1403.091), analyzed anonymized data with waived consent under institutional review board approval, adhering to STROCSS 2024 guidelines [9].

Patients meeting the inclusion criteria are invited to participate, and informed consent is obtained. (CRP levels, demographic/clinical variables) were collected from existing medical records, aligning with the retrospective design.

Patients with choledocholithiasis (CBD stones confirmed by imaging/cholangiography) were excluded to isolate predictors specific to gallbladder pathology. CBD stones alter biliary dynamics and systemic inflammation independently, confounding CRP’s role in gallbladder-related outcomes. Concurrent biliary obstruction, cholangitis, or prior ERCP were exclusion criteria, as these conditions elevate CRP independently of gallbladder inflammation and necessitate distinct management pathways.

Demographic data, laboratory results (specifically CRP levels), and surgical conditions were recorded in a checklist designed by the researcher (checklist 1).

This study aimed to investigate the predictive role of CRP in surgical difficulty (Surgical difficulty was prospectively assessed intraoperatively using a standardized 10-point scale adapted from the Nassar and Tokyo Guidelines classifications) [9]. during laparoscopic cholecystectomy in patients with acute cholecystitis or uncomplicated gallbladder stones.

The primary analysis focused on acute cholecystitis patients (n = 76), in alignment with the Tokyo Guidelines 2018. Patients with chronic cholecystitis (n = 14) and uncomplicated cholelithiasis (n = 90) were included for comparison but not used in AUC calculations for conversion and complication prediction.

Study limitations

Given the high number of exclusion criteria and the high prevalence of some of them, it was very difficult to secure an adequate sample size for this study.

Albumin levels were unavailable for 17.8% of patients, precluding robust analysis of CAR; thus, CRP was selected as the primary biomarker.

Data analysis

A sample size of 180 was determined sufficient to detect significant associations (power 80%, alpha 0.05) based on prior studies reporting CRP’s predictive ability (AUC ~0.9).

Missing albumin data (17.8%) precluded CAR analysis per protocol; this limitation is offset by CRP's standalone predictive performance (AUC >0.9).

Missing data patterns were assessed using R’s mice package. No significant differences existed in CRP distributions between patients with/without albumin data (p=0.21, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test), supporting the decision to retain all cases for CRP analysis.

Results

After excluding choledocholithiasis (n = 32), outcomes reflect purely gallbladder-related inflammation (acute/chronic cholecystitis) or uncomplicated stones.

Our final cohort of 180 patients comprised three distinct groups: uncomplicated gallbladder stones (50%, n = 90), acute cholecystitis (42.2%, n = 76), and chronic cholecystitis (7.8%, n = 14). This stratification ensured our outcomes reflected purely gallbladder-related conditions without confounding biliary tract involvement.

Notably, Patients with choledocholithiasis (common bile duct stones) or complicated cholelithiasis (e.g., biliary pancreatitis, Mirizzi syndrome) were excluded to isolate inflammatory predictors specific to acute cholecystitis.

Gender frequency percentage in the two study patient groups

In the cholecystitis group, 84.4% of patients are female and 15.6% are male; in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group, 91.1% are female and 8.9% are male. Statistical analysis (P = 0.17) shows no significant difference in gender distribution between the groups, despite females being predominant in both.

The average age and weight in the two study patient groups

In the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group, the average weight is 76.66 kg (SD = 11.49), compared to 81.96 kg (SD = 13.73) in the cholecystitis group, with a significant difference (P = 0.006).

The average age is 45.14 years (SD = 16.10) in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group and 50.46 years (SD = 32.61) in the cholecystitis group, with no significant difference (P = 0.16) (Table 1).

Average treatment times in the two study patient groups

The average surgery duration was 24.89 min (SD = 9.569) in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group and 28.28 min (SD = 14.614) in the cholecystitis group, with no significant difference (P = 0.067).

Hospitalization duration was significantly longer in the cholecystitis group (7.39 days, SD = 15.124) compared to the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group (2.18 days, SD = 0.464) (P = 0.001).

The average duration of acute symptoms was 4.20 days (SD = 2.051) in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group and 3.87 days (SD = 2.661) in the cholecystitis group, with no significant difference (P = 0.34) (Table 1).

The average laboratory parameters before and after surgery in the two study patient groups

The average preoperative CRP is 7.11 (SD = 9.934) in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group and 25.43 (SD = 32.961) in the cholecystitis group, with a significant difference (P = 0.001). The average leukocyte count is 8122.22 (SD = 2507.641) in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group and 9572.22 (SD = 4056.872) in the cholecystitis group, with a significant difference (P = 0.004). The average postoperative serum amylase is 37.41 (SD = 34.098) in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group and 53.16 (SD = 78.354) in the cholecystitis group, with no significant difference (P = 0.082) (Table 2).

The frequency percentage of postoperative complications in the two study patient groups

AUC values reported for conversion to open surgery and complications (e.g., hydrops, necrosis, abscess, bleeding) were derived exclusively from the acute cholecystitis subgroup (n = 76), ensuring the analyses reflect inflammatory processes specific to acute disease.

In the cholecystitis group, 4.4% required open surgery (P = 0.043), while no cases were reported in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group. No patients in either group had adjacent organ or bile duct injuries. Hydrops occurred in 27.8% of cholecystitis patients vs. 3.3% in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group (P = 0.001), and bile necrosis occurred in 16.7% of cholecystitis patients vs. 1.1% in the uncomplicated gallbladder stones group (P = 0.001). Phlegmon was found in 30% of cholecystitis patients vs. 4.4% in uncomplicated gallbladder stones (P = 0.001), and abscess in 25.6% vs. 4.4% (P = 0.001). Bleeding occurred in 22.2% of cholecystitis patients vs. 6.7% in uncomplicated gallbladder stones (P = 0.003). All differences were statistically significant (Table 2).

Postoperative serum amylase levels were routinely measured to exclude pancreatic complications but did not differ between groups (Table 2) and were not analyzed as predictors of surgical outcomes.

Discussion

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the standard treatment for gallbladder issues, particularly acute cholecystitis, due to its benefits like shorter hospital stays and fewer complications compared to open surgery [10–12]. This cross-sectional study involved 180 patients with acute cholecystitis. Pre-surgery, serum CRP levels and the CRP to CAR were measured, and demographic and clinical data were analyzed using R software [13].

This study confirms that preoperative CRP is a highly accurate predictor of surgical outcomes in acute cholecystitis, with stage-specific thresholds (e.g., 7.5 mg/L for conversion risk, 17.5 mg/L for necrosis/phlegmon) offering actionable clinical insights. Our findings align with prior evidence but advance the field by refining CRP’s utility for acute inflammatory complications while explicitly excluding choledocholithiasis and uncomplicated cholelithiasis, which lack comparable inflammatory drivers.

The Tokyo 2018 guidelines recommend this method for treating severe acute cholecystitis. However, one major challenge is that in some cases, laparoscopic surgery may be converted to open surgery, which can significantly impact complication rates and length of hospital stay [14].

This study examines the role of the CRP to CAR as a predictor for conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery to improve surgical planning and outcomes [15].

The study found that uncomplicated cholelithiasis (50%) was more common than acute cholecystitis (42.2%) and chronic cholecystitis (7.8%). The proportion of women was high in both groups. The cholecystitis group had significantly higher weight (81.96 kg vs. 76.66 kg), longer hospital stays (7.39 vs. 2.18 days), higher CRP and leukocytosis levels, and more postoperative complications, including open surgery (4.4%), hydrops, necrosis, phlegmon, abscesses, and bleeding (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in operative time or age, and postoperative amylase levels were similar.

The 7.5 mg/L threshold aligns with Díaz-Flores et al.’s cutoff (11 mg/dL) for difficult LC [12], while our higher thresholds (e.g., 17.5 mg/L for necrosis) refine risk stratification for advanced pathology. This supports CRP’s integration into preoperative algorithms, such as prioritizing early surgery for high-CRP patients.

While CRP’s link to acute cholecystitis is established, our study refines its clinical utility by identifying stage-specific thresholds. For instance, a CRP > 7.5 mg/L in early presentations (symptom duration < 72 h) signaled conversion risk (100% sensitivity), while levels > 17.5 mg/L predicted necrosis/phlegmon. This granularity aids tailored decision-making, such as prioritizing surgery or allocating senior surgeons a practical advance beyond prior studies.

CRP can help improve surgical planning and predict complications in acute cholecystitis [16]. Further research is needed to confirm and refine these results.

The study by Kaboul Gorboulak et al. (2015) highlighted CRP as a reliable marker for grading acute cholecystitis severity based on the Tokyo guidelines.

Similarly, the present study found that CRP, with an AUC of 0.964, effectively predicts the conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery, aligning with Kaboul Gorboulak’s findings. Additionally, this study demonstrated CRP’s ability to predict postoperative complications, further supporting its role as a reliable predictor in acute cholecystitis management [16, 17].

Beliaev et al. (2015) found that CRP had higher diagnostic power than WBC in identifying acute cholecystitis, especially in mild to moderate cases. The present study also showed significantly higher CRP levels and leukocytosis in the cholecystitis group compared to the uncomplicated cholelithiasis group. These findings confirm CRP’s superior diagnostic value over WBC, highlighting its effectiveness in predicting complications [3, 18].

The study by Díaz-Flores et al. (2017) identified CRP as a predictor for difficult laparoscopic cholecystectomy (DLC) with an ideal cutoff of 11 mg/dL. The present study showed a strong predictive performance for CRP with an AUC of 0.964 in predicting conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery. This aligns with Díaz-Flores’ findings and further emphasizes CRP’s accuracy in predicting postoperative complications such as hydrops and necrosis [19].

The study by Wevers et al. (2013) found that age and CRP levels are independent predictors for laparoscopic cholecystectomy conversion. Similarly, the present study confirms that CRP levels are an excellent independent predictor for conversion to open surgery, emphasizing CRP’s role in predicting complications and guiding surgical decisions [20].

Sutcliffe et al. (2016) developed a risk score for predicting conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery, emphasizing factors like age, gender, surgical indication, and gallbladder characteristics. The current study complements this by focusing on CRP as a key predictor, demonstrating strong predictive value for complications, and aiding in optimal surgical planning and management [21].

Both previous and current studies highlight CRP as an effective marker for predicting the severity and complications of acute cholecystitis [22]. The current study shows CRP’s strong performance in predicting laparoscopic-to-open surgery conversion and postoperative complications [23]. These findings align with previous research, emphasizing CRP as a key tool in managing acute cholecystitis.

While the CRP to CAR was initially considered for its combined assessment of inflammation and nutrition, our findings demonstrate that CRP alone serves as an effective, low-cost, and widely accessible biomarker, particularly valuable in resource-limited settings where albumin testing may be inconsistent [24]. However, further research is needed to optimize predictive models by potentially combining CRP with other markers and validating these findings in larger cohorts [25–28].

This study has several important limitations: its cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences, and the sample size of 180 patients - while adequate to detect significant associations (AUC 0.899–0.983) - may limit generalizability across diverse populations. These constraints emphasize the need for multicenter validation to confirm CRP’s predictive thresholds for surgical outcomes.

Stringent exclusion criteria, such as excluding patients with common bile duct stones or prior ERCP, further constrained the sample size due to the prevalence of these conditions. Additionally, the single-center design limits applicability to other regions or ethnic groups, and the lack of analysis of other inflammatory markers (e.g., procalcitonin, IL-6) may have missed opportunities to enhance prediction accuracy.

While excluding CBD stones strengthened internal validity, it reduced sample size and may limit applicability to settings where choledocholithiasis co-occurs with cholecystitis. However, this aligns with our aim to evaluate CRP’s role in isolated gallbladder inflammation.

Despite albumin data limitations, CRP’s standalone performance suggests it is a pragmatic, low-cost marker for resource-limited settings where CAR may be impractical.

CRP’s predictive utility is inherently limited to acute cholecystitis, as uncomplicated gallstones lack inflammatory drivers. This study did not assess other markers (e.g., procalcitonin) that may enhance prediction in non-inflammatory scenarios.

Conclusion

CRP is a strong predictor for converting laparoscopic to open surgery and complications in acute cholecystitis, including hydrops, necrosis, phlegmon, abscesses, and bleeding. It is a valuable tool for patient evaluation and management. Further research is needed to validate and improve predictive models by combining CRP with other markers.

Suggestions include conducting longitudinal studies to better understand the causal relationship between CRP, CAR, and surgical complications. A multicenter analysis across diverse populations can enhance the applicability of results. Combining CRP and CAR with other markers could improve predictive models for managing acute cholecystitis. Additionally, further research into treatment strategies based on these biomarkers could help refine surgical planning and improve patient outcomes.
