Association of Ambroxol Hydrochloride and Clenbuterol Hydrochloride Oral Solution with Respiratory Symptom Improvement and Safety in Chinese Children with Pneumonia: A Real-World Propensity Score–Matched Study
Authors: Ying Liang, Yesheng Ling, Bing Hu, Yingxue Zou, Enmei Liu, Jinhai Ma, Changshan Liu, Quan Lu, Xiaoyan Dong, Huifen Zi, Chuangli Hao, Rongjun Lin, Xiangrong Zheng, Bingfei Li, Lingping Zhu, Mei Fang, Weimin Tian, Zhiqiang Zhuo, Deyu Zhao, Zhimin Chen, Yuejie Zheng, Jingyang Zheng, Yong Yin, Qiuyu Tang, Liqun Wu, Li Gu, Jinzhun Wu, Liyi He, Tao Ai, Ning Wang, Minjun Zhang, Hailin Zhang, Hanmin Liu, Youxiang Zhang, Jianguo Hong, Zhiying Han, Yunbo Mo, Hongmei Qiao, Zhiliang Tian, Lixia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the association of ambroxol hydrochloride and clenbuterol hydrochloride oral solution (AHCHOS) with respiratory symptom improvement and safety in Chinese children with pneumonia using real-world data.MethodsA propensity score-matched (PSM) cohort study was conducted. Children patients (≤14 years) with a diagnosis of pneumonia between May, 2018 and July, 2019 were considered as the study population. The main outcome of interest was the overall rate of improvement of respiratory symptoms in treatment groups with or without AHCHOS at day 7 using respiratory symptom scores (QS) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score of severity of respiratory signs. Secondary end points include medication adherence assessment and safety assessment.ResultsA total of 3103 children with a diagnosis of pneumonia were included. After propensity score matching, a sample of 1428 patients was analyzed. AHCHOS use was associated with greater improvement in cough score (p=0.01) and day 7 clinical sign improvement rate (p=0.03) compared with no AHCHOS use. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested larger differences in children aged 3–6 years and in selected severity strata. Medication adherence assessmentdid not differ significantly between groups at days 4, 7, 14, and 28. Adverse events were similar between groups.ConclusionIn this real-world propensity score-matched analysis, AHCHOS use was associated with improved respiratory symptom outcomes in children with pneumonia and appeared to have an acceptable safety profile. These findings should be interpreted as observational and hypothesis-generating.Trial Registration NumberThe study was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/, ChiCTR1800015818).

Introduction

Pneumonia remains one of the most common respiratory infections in childhood and continues to be a major cause of outpatient visits and hospitalizations worldwide.1 In young children, the disease burden is driven not only by the infection itself, but also by symptoms such as cough, sputum retention, wheezing, and dyspnea, which can prolong recovery and reduce quality of life.2 China has one of the highest burdens of childhood pneumonia globally, accounting for approximately 12% of the global total of invasive pneumonia cases in children under 5 years.3 Although mortality from childhood pneumonia has declined in many settings, symptom control and recovery in routine practice remain important clinical challenges.4 Similar to Western countries, childhood pneumonia is known to be a mixed viral-bacterial infection in approximately 23% to 33% of cases.5 Among viral causes, respiratory syncytial virus is the most common, while Streptococcus pneumonia is the predominant bacterial cause of pneumonia in children. Administering appropriate antibiotics early in the course of pneumonia is crucial for improving outcomes, particularly when the infection is bacterial in nature. However, determining the specific pathogen responsible for pneumonia in children is challenging, leading to empirical antibiotic treatment in most cases.6 In China, even though the majority of hospitalized children with pneumonia receive antibiotic therapy, the decision to administer antibiotics remains difficult, especially due to the increasing prevalence of viral and bacterial coinfections.7 Mucus retention in the airways is a significant contributor to lung infections, and the use of expectorant and cough suppressant agents plays a vital role in the prevention and treatment of pneumonia.8

Ambroxol hydrochloride and clenbuterol hydrochloride (AHCH) are two drugs commonly used in the treatment of respiratory conditions.9 Ambroxol acts as a mucolytic agent, helping to thin and loosen mucus in the respiratory tract, while clenbuterol is a β2 receptor agonist that can help dilate the bronchi.10 In combination, these agents are intended to facilitate airway clearance and relieve respiratory discomfort. However, the role of ambroxol and β2-agonist combinations in pediatric pneumonia remains uncertain. Available evidence is limited, and much of it comes from small studies, single-center experiences, or heterogeneous respiratory conditions rather than large pneumonia-specific cohorts. In addition, the extent to which this combination provides meaningful benefit in routine clinical practice, particularly in children with different ages and disease severities, has not been fully clarified.

The data for the present study were derived from a multicenter registry of lower respiratory tract infections in Chinese children that was previously established and described by our tea.11 That earlier publication focused on the construction of the registry and provided a descriptive overview of the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of the enrolled population. In contrast, the present study uses that same database to address a distinct research question by applying a propensity score–matched design to evaluate the association of AHCH oral solution (AHCHOS) with respiratory symptom improvement, medication compliance, and safety outcomes in Chinese children with pneumonia. This analytical extension moves beyond the initial descriptive report and provides more targeted evidence on treatment-related outcomes in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

The study was conducted in 36 research centers nationwide, and the target population was children aged ≤ 14 years with pneumonia who attended the respiratory department (inpatient) of a tertiary general hospital, a maternal and child health center, or a specialized children’s hospital.11

Data were collected from the clinical visits of the enrolled children, including the characteristics of the children, the use of AHCHOS to improve respiratory symptoms (cough, volume of sputum, difficulty in coughing up sputum, and rales, etc). After baseline registration of the child, an on-site visit was conducted on day 7 after treatment, and the appropriate clinic information was collected. In addition, at days 4, 14, and 28 after treatment, information on child Patient-Reported Outcome (PRO) was collected. From May 23, 2018, to July, 2019, the recruitment, data collection, organization, and analysis of all eligible children were completed. Overall study design is shown in Figure 1. This study was approved by the ethics approval unit (No. 2018-2). All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Guardians of all of the children included in this study provided written informed consent. The study was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/, ChiCTR1800015818).

Key eligibility criteria included children aged less than or equal to 14 years and children diagnosed with pneumonia. Those children who were unable to obtain signed informed consent were not included.

Study Endpoints

The primary study endpoint of this study was the overall rate of improvement of respiratory symptoms in treatment groups with or without AHCHOS at day 7 determined by respiratory symptom scores (QS) score of respiratory symptoms,12 VAS score of severity of respiratory signs and VAS score of quality of life.13,14 According to the respiratory symptom scoring criteria (QS), the same pediatric respiratory specialist recorded the corresponding respiratory symptoms of each child at the time of enrollment and on the 7th day of treatment. The criteria for evaluating the efficacy of respiratory symptoms were as follows: clinical control: it was defined as the lack of mildness or disappearance of the signs; significant effect: it was defined as the change of the signs from 3 to 1, or 2 to 0; improvement: it was defined as the change of the signs from 3 to 2, or 2 to 1, or 1 to 0; and ineffective: it was defined as the lack of improvement or even aggravation of the signs. Rate of improvement in respiratory symptoms = (sum of each individual symptom score before treatment - sum of each individual symptom score after treatment)/sum of each individual sign score before treatment × 100%. Clinical efficacy criteria: clinical control: > 75% improvement in respiratory symptoms after treatment; significant effect: 50% ~ 75% improvement in respiratory symptoms after treatment; improvement: 25% ~ 49% improvement in respiratory symptoms after treatment; ineffective: < 25% improvement in respiratory symptoms after treatment. The total effective rate was calculated according to the number of clinically controlled, apparently effective and improved cases, and the total effective rate (%) = (number of clinically controlled cases + number of apparently effective cases + number of improved cases)/total number of cases × 100%. Secondary end points include assessment of medication adherence according to whether the medication was taken as prescribed on days 4, 7, 14, and 28, and adverse events (AEs).

Procedure

AHCHOS was manufactured by Beijing Hanmi Pharm. Co., Ltd., China. The dosages administered were in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations specified in the product information. Briefly, for pediatric patients under 12 years old, the recommended dosage was oral administration of 2.5 to 15 milliliters twice a day. For children over 12 years old, the recommended dosage was oral administration of 20 milliliters twice a day. After significant improvement of symptoms, the dosage could be reduced to 10 milliliters once, 2–3 times a day. For patients experiencing severe respiratory distress, the initial dosage for the first 2–3 days was oral administration of 20 milliliters once, 3 times a day. Concomitant treatments for pneumonia, including antibiotics and supportive therapy, were recorded when available and were considered in the interpretation of outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using SAS 9.4 statistical software. The variables included in the propensity score model (PSM)15 were selected based on clinical relevance and potential association with treatment allocation and disease severity. These variables included respiratory symptoms (cough, sputum volume, difficulty of sputum coughing, and rales), age, disease severity, exposure to second-hand smoke, whether or not the baby was born prematurely, whether or not there was a history of allergy, and C reactive protein (CRP) level, the propensity score of each patient was calculated by multivariate logistic regression analysis, and a combination of nearest-neighbor matching and caliper matching was adopted. The closest individuals in the propensity scores of children in two groups were matched at a 1:1 ratio, and the caliper value of the matching was 0.25. After matching, covariate balance between groups was assessed to confirm comparability of the two cohorts before outcome analysis. Measurement data were expressed as (x ± s), and t-tests or rank-sum tests were used according to the distribution characteristics of the data. Count data are expressed as rate (%), and the chi-square test or Fisher’s test was used. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subgroup analyses were considered exploratory and were interpreted descriptively.

Safety

In the control group, the total number of adverse events (AEs) was 4 (0.56%); of these, the number of cases of AEs related to the study drug was 4 (0.56%). In the experiment group, there were five AEs related to the study drug (0.7%). One serious AE (drug allergy) occurred in the experiment group and the control group had none. The differences were not statistically significant between all these comparisons (Supplemental Table 5).

Discussion

Pneumonia is the most common infectious disease in childhood and one of the most common causes of hospitalization in children. Treating pneumonia can be challenging, especially if the causative agent is resistant to antibiotics or if the infection is complicated by other factors such as comorbidities or drug allergies.1,16 Expectorants, medications that facilitate mucus clearance from the airways, have been a subject of ongoing research in the management of respiratory infections, including pneumonia.17,18 While some studies suggest that expectorants may alleviate symptoms and improve outcomes by enhancing mucus clearance,19 their efficacy in pneumonia treatment remains controversial, with limited evidence supporting their use as standalone therapies.20

This study found that AHCHOS use was associated with greater short-term improvement in selected symptom measures in children with pneumonia, particularly cough and total QS scores, with more notable differences observed in certain age and severity subgroups. In particular, children aged 3–6 years appeared to derive greater benefit in several symptom-based outcomes, and improvement in cough symptoms was also observed in children with more severe presentations. The medication was generally well tolerated, with no clear safety signal identified in this cohort. These findings are broadly consistent with previous studies suggesting that ambroxol-based therapy may help relieve respiratory symptoms in pediatric pneumonia.21 A small prior study also reported better pulmonary function and clinical efficacy with ambroxol hydrochloride combined with procaterol hydrochloride in children with pneumonia.22 However, because the present study is observational and relies on symptom-based outcomes, the results should be interpreted cautiously as associative rather than causal. The apparent age-specific effect deserves further investigation in larger prospective studies.

This study has limitations. Although AHCHOS was associated with greater short-term improvement in some symptom measures, the magnitude of benefit was modest, and the observational design precludes causal inference; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. In addition, the symptom-based outcomes used in this study are clinically relevant but partly subjective, and the potential influence of unmeasured confounding and concurrent treatments cannot be excluded. As a real-world data analysis, PSM was used to reduce confounding and improve comparability between groups; however, this approach led to the exclusion of unmatched participants, reducing the sample size from 3103 to 1428 and potentially decreasing statistical power. Therefore, although real-world evidence can provide useful insights into routine clinical practice, randomized controlled trials are still needed to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

AHCHOS use was associated with modest short-term improvement in respiratory symptom scores in Chinese children with pneumonia and appeared to have an acceptable safety profile. However, the observational design, the symptom-based nature of the outcomes, and the absence of hard clinical endpoints preclude causal inference. These findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and further prospective studies are needed to confirm these observations and define their clinical relevance.
