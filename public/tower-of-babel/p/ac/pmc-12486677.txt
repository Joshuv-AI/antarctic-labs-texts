Pelvic floor support changes in postpartum pelvic organ prolapse with involvement of single and multiple compartments
Authors: Yujiao Zhao, Luyang Ma, Weijun li, Cheng Zhang, Cong You, Qian Zhao, Haiying Niu, Xiaotian Li, Lixiang Huang, Zhiwei Shen, Yue Cheng, Wen Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate longitudinal changes in pelvic floor support in primiparous women with pelvic organ prolapse (POP) after vaginal delivery, focusing on single- and multiple-compartment involvement.MethodsTwo hundred primiparas after vaginal delivery were prospectively enrolled and underwent pelvic floor MRI at six weeks postpartum. POP was diagnosed and classified into subgroups (single or multiple compartments involved) based on MRI findings. Primiparas with POP underwent repeat MRI at four months postpartum. Pelvic floor measurements, including injury score and functional parameters of the levator ani muscle (puborectal hiatus line, H line; muscular pelvic floor relaxation line, M line; levator hiatus area, LHA; iliococcygeus angle, ICA; levator plate angle, LPA), were assessed on MRI. Measurements were compared among POP subgroups and a normal control group (without POP) at six weeks postpartum. Additionally, changes between six weeks and four months postpartum were analyzed within POP subgroups.ResultsBased on MRI criteria, approximately 41.5% of primiparas were diagnosed with POP, predominantly cystoceles commonly associated with uterine prolapse. Functional parameters of the levator ani, except for LPA at rest, were significantly increased in POP subgroups compared to controls. At four months postpartum, M line, H line, and LPA significantly decreased, and prolapsed organs were elevated in cases with multiple compartments involved, compared to six weeks postpartum. No significant changes were observed in cases with single-compartment involvement during follow-up.ConclusionA substantial proportion of primiparas experienced postpartum POP. Impaired levator ani function contributed to POP. Pelvic floor support improved during early postpartum in cases with multiple-compartment involvement.Keywords: Pelvic floor, Pelvic organ prolapse, Vaginal delivery, Levator ani muscle, Follow-up

Introduction

Vaginal delivery is the most significant known risk factor for pelvic organ prolapse (POP) [1–3]. Women with a history of vaginal birth were ten times more likely to develop POP than those who had cesarean Sect. [4]. Approximately 12.2–28.2% of primiparas experienced POP shortly after vaginal delivery [5]. The process of vaginal delivery could cause nerve or muscle ischemic injury or avulsion of the pelvic floor muscles, contributing to postpartum POP development [6]. Current assessment methods primarily relied on pelvic floor questionnaires [7–9], limiting accurate evaluation of pelvic floor anatomical abnormalities after vaginal delivery.

While most studies focused on the effects of pregnancy and childbirth on the pelvic floor [10, 11], knowledge about the course and progression of postpartum POP remained limited [4, 12]. Our previous longitudinal cohort study investigated morphological changes of the pelvic floor in primiparas with POP over six months postpartum using MRI [13]. Interestingly, the results indicated that postpartum POP persisted during the early postpartum period, contradicting the assumption that the pelvic floor could recover from postpartum biomechanical disorders. We therefore hypothesized that the extent of pelvic organ involvement in prolapse might affect pelvic floor remodeling, explaining inconsistent findings on pelvic floor recovery.

Imaging assessment of the pelvic floor was crucial for understanding POP and its interaction with support structures, particularly the levator ani muscle, a key determinant of postpartum prolapse. Pelvic floor MRI, which provided excellent soft-tissue contrast without ionizing radiation, offered insights into extensive organ prolapse and associated complications [14–16]. Previous studies showed that anterior and middle compartment prolapse evaluated by MRI correlated better with the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) system [17].

This longitudinal MRI-based study investigated changes in pelvic floor support structures and pelvic organ positioning in postpartum women with POP over four months postpartum, focusing on single- and multiple-compartment involvement.

Methods and materials

Participants

Two hundred primiparas at six weeks postpartum after vaginal delivery from May 2017 to May 2019 were prospectively enrolled. The flow chart of study participants is shown in Fig. 1. The research ethics committee of Tianjin First Central Hospital approved the study (No. 2016N089KY), and all participants provided informed consent before enrollment. Inclusion criteria were first delivery, singleton pregnancy, maternal age over 18 years, and complete maternal and obstetric data. Exclusion criteria included a history of pelvic floor disorders (including urinary incontinence, fecal incontinence, and pelvic organ prolapse), previous pelvic surgery, known collagen vascular disorders, and contraindications to MRI.

Flowchart

Maternal and obstetric data were recorded at enrollment. Maternal characteristics included age, body mass index (BMI) before delivery, gestational diabetes, gravidity, and gestational age at birth. Obstetric characteristics included length of the second stage of labor, forceps delivery, fetal biparietal diameter (BPD), femur length, amniotic fluid index (AFI), and fetal birth weight. A prolonged second stage of labor was defined as lasting more than 2 h for primiparas or more than 1 h for multiparas. A normal second stage was defined as lasting about 1–2 h for primiparas and up to 1 h for multiparas. A shortened second stage was defined as shorter than typical for the individual, often less than 30 min.

MR imaging

Preparation

All primiparas were instructed to empty their bladder and drink 200–300 ml of water one hour before the examination to ensure optimal bladder filling. No gel was applied to the vagina or rectum. One physician trained all participants to perform the Valsalva maneuver prior to MRI until they mastered the technique. Primiparas were placed supine with a soft wedge-shaped pad under their knees to simulate the lithotomy position and facilitate effective force during the Valsalva maneuver.

MR protocols

All primiparas underwent pelvic floor MRI using a 3.0 T unit (Ingenia, Philips Healthcare) with a 16-channel body coil. Axial, sagittal, and coronal T2 Fast Spin Echo (FSE) images were acquired at rest (3 mm slice thickness, 0.3 mm gap; field of view [FOV] 26 cm). Axial, sagittal, and coronal single-shot fast spin-echo images were obtained during maximum Valsalva maneuver (6 mm slice thickness, 0.6 mm gap; FOV 28 cm). Sagittal Balanced Fast Field Echo (B-FFE) images centered on the anal canal were acquired during Valsalva (5 mm slice thickness, 0.5 mm gap; FOV 30 cm; flip angle 90°). Primiparas performed the Valsalva maneuver at least five times during the B-FFE sequence.

Imaging analysis

Two radiologists with over five years of experience in genitourinary imaging independently assessed the pelvic floor on MR images. They were blinded to obstetric history, delivery details, and timing of assessments. The pubococcygeal line (PCL), drawn from the inferior border of the pubic symphysis to the last coccygeal joint, served as the reference line for pelvic organ position. Measurements above the PCL were assigned a positive value; those below, a negative value. POP was diagnosed on MR images when the bladder base or anterior cervical lip descended more than 1 cm below the PCL [18], as determined by the two radiologists. Discrepancies were resolved by a third radiologist with ten years of imaging experience. Primiparas with normal pelvic organ positioning on MR images were classified as having a normal pelvic floor.

The primiparas were divided into POP and normal control groups based on pelvic organ position on MRI at the first visit (six weeks postpartum). POP subgroups were classified according to the involved pelvic compartment: cystocele or uterine prolapse was classified as single-compartment involvement, while the presence of both concurrently was classified as multiple-compartment involvement (Fig. 2). All participants with postpartum POP were invited to return at four months postpartum for repeat MRI, as described above.

According to the MRI grading system [16, 19], levator ani muscle (LAM) injury was defined as a discontinuity between the puborectalis and the inferior pubic ramus. No visible defect was scored “0”; fiber loss < 50% scored “1”; fiber loss > 50% scored “2”; and complete rupture scored “3” (Fig. 3). The total score was calculated by summing the left and right sides, ranging from 0 to 6. Two fellowship-trained musculoskeletal radiologists independently evaluated LAM morphology, resolving discrepancies by consensus review.

The definitions of pelvic floor measurements are presented in Table 1, and schematic representations are shown in Fig. 4. The H line, M line, LHA, ICA, and LPA were used to quantify levator ani muscle function, indirectly reflecting pelvic floor support [20, 21]. The B-PCL and U-PCL were employed to assess the positions of the bladder and uterus for diagnosing and staging POP. Changes between rest and maximum Valsalva maneuver were expressed as Δ (maximum Valsalva minus rest), representing pelvic floor mobility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) and R software version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Intra- and inter-class correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated by two radiologists to assess reproducibility and repeatability of all pelvic floor measurements, with values > 0.7 considered reliable. Mean values were used for subsequent analyses.

Maternal and obstetric characteristics were described as means ± standard deviations or as frequencies and percentages. Independent-sample Student’s t-tests, Mann-Whitney U-tests, Chi-square tests, and Fisher’s exact tests were used as appropriate for cross-sectional comparisons between the POP and normal control groups. One-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis, or Chi-square tests were applied for cross-sectional comparisons among POP subgroups and the control group. Dynamic changes in pelvic floor measurements within each POP subgroup at two postpartum time points were analyzed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Initially, 200 primiparas underwent pelvic floor MRI at six weeks postpartum, of whom 83 were diagnosed with POP, yielding an incidence rate of 41.5%. Postpartum POP predominantly involved single-compartment prolapse (cystocele, n = 38; uterine prolapse, n = 2) and multiple-compartment prolapse (cystocele and uterine prolapse, n = 43). A total of 62 (75%) primiparas diagnosed with POP at the first visit returned for re-examination at four months postpartum. Among them, 26 had single-compartment involvement (cystocele, n = 25; uterine prolapse, n = 1), and 36 had multiple-compartment involvement.

Maternal and obstetrical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. Only the AFI differed significantly between the POP and control groups (P = 0.006). At six weeks postpartum, levator ani muscle injury was diagnosed in 41 of 117 (35.04%) primiparas in the control group and 23 of 62 (37.10%) in the POP group, with no significant difference between groups (P > 0.999).

At six weeks postpartum, pairwise comparisons showed that the U-PCL in the MCI-POP group was significantly lower than in the other groups both at rest and during maximal Valsalva maneuver. At rest, LHA, H line, and right ICA were significantly larger in the POP subgroups than in the control group. Additionally, the M line and left ICA were markedly increased in the MCI-POP group compared to controls. The B-PCL was lower in the MCI-POP group than in controls, with no significant difference between POP subgroups. During maximal Valsalva, all pelvic floor measurements were significantly higher in the POP subgroups compared to controls. Meanwhile, the M line was substantially enlarged and the U-PCL decreased in the MCI-POP group relative to other groups. Pairwise comparison of pelvic floor mobility revealed significant differences in ΔU-PCL and ΔH line among the three groups, while other measurements in the POP subgroups were significantly larger than in controls.

In the longitudinal comparison of POP subgroups during the early postpartum period, all pelvic floor measurements except right ICA, whether at rest or maximal Valsalva, showed no significant changes between visits in primiparas with SCI-POP (Fig. 5), as did pelvic floor mobility (Table 4). However, in primiparas with MCI-POP, the H line, M line, and LPA during maximal Valsalva at four months postpartum were significantly shortened and reduced; B-PCL at rest, and U-PCL both at rest and maximal Valsalva were significantly higher compared to measurements at six weeks postpartum (Figs. 6 and 7). Notably, significant changes in ΔB-PCL and ΔLPA were observed in the MCI-POP group during the follow-up period (Table 5).

Discussion

As mentioned above, over one-third of primiparas developed postpartum POP following natural childbirth. Among obstetric factors, only AFI was significantly associated with postpartum POP. Our findings suggest that weakened levator ani muscle function, rather than overt morphological defects, primarily contributes to postpartum POP. Pelvic floor support varied slightly depending on the involved compartments; for example, the M line was markedly enlarged in cases with multiple compartment involvement. Notably, POP involving multiple compartments showed improvement over time, coinciding with restoration of levator ani muscle function, whereas POP limited to a single compartment remained unchanged during the early postpartum period.

A nationwide population-based survey in China reported a 9.6% prevalence of symptomatic POP [22]. However, symptom-based prevalence often underestimates POP detected by physical examination, as many women exhibit subclinical POP without noticeable symptoms. In our study, more than one-third of primiparas were diagnosed with POP based on abnormal pelvic organ positioning on MRI after natural childbirth, despite lacking apparent symptoms. Although our incidence differs from prior reports, it provides important objective data, as the true prevalence of POP in the early postpartum period after first vaginal delivery remains unclear.

Previous studies have identified levator ani muscle injury during childbirth as a key factor linking vaginal delivery to POP development [23, 24]. Our results emphasize that weakened levator ani function, manifested as levator ballooning or microinjur, rather than gross morphological avulsion, is closely associated with postpartum POP. While somewhat unexpected, similar findings have been reported [25, 26]. We propose two interpretations: first, POP may not develop secondary to levator ani avulsion in primiparas due to relatively stable pelvic floor support, where connective tissue plays a major role in younger women. Second, weakened levator ani function may reflect instability within the pelvic floor support system, comprising muscles, connective tissues, and ligaments, potentially due to microinjuries or hormonal changes related to pregnancy and childbirth [27].

The most common type of postpartum POP following vaginal delivery was cystocele, frequently accompanied by uterine prolapse. However, the clinical significance of these pelvic floor abnormalities in the early postpartum period among primiparas remains unclear due to limited longitudinal data. Prior studies have suggested that natural postpartum recovery predominantly occurs within the first three months after delivery [28, 29]. Therefore, we conducted both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses of pelvic floor support changes at six weeks and four months postpartum in primiparas with different POP compartment involvement.

Our results showed that the M line on maximal Valsalva and the mobility of the H line between Valsalva and rest were significantly greater in primiparas with multiple compartments involved in POP compared to those with single compartment involvement. The H line, defined as the distance from the inferior border of the pubic symphysis to the convex posterior border of the puborectalis muscle loop, reflects the distensibility of the levator ani muscle. Primiparas with multiple compartment POP exhibited hypermobility in the H line, indicating increased levator ani distensibility and greater potential for pelvic organ descent. The M line, defined as the perpendicular distance from the posterior aspect of the H line to the PCL, reflects cephalocaudal pelvic organ movement [30]. Compared with single-compartment POP, multiple-compartment involvement showed a more pronounced M line, indicating maximal pelvic floor displacement and reduced support capacity. These findings support our hypothesis that diminished overall pelvic floor support after vaginal delivery underlies postpartum POP.

In a previous study, we observed no significant change in pelvic floor support over time in primiparas with postpartum POP during the early postpartum period. We hypothesized that this may have been influenced by differences in POP extent and a short follow-up duration. In the present study, we focused on changes in pelvic floor support among POP subgroups defined by compartment involvement. The results indicated that while pelvic organ position and support remained stable in the SCI-POP, significant improvements were observed in those with multiple compartments involved.

The H line, M line, and LPA were significantly reduced in primiparas with multiple compartments involved in POP. Concurrently, abnormal pelvic organ positions improved alongside restoration of levator ani muscle strength. These findings suggest that primiparas with multiple-compartment POP may not need excessive concern regarding long-term pelvic floor dysfunction, as functional recovery appears possible. However, persistent single-compartment involvement warrants attention, raising questions about the clinical relevance of mild postpartum POP and the need for extended follow-up.

Several studies have examined changes in pelvic organ support after delivery, yielding varying results depending on participant characteristics and follow-up durations [5, 29, 31]. Our study offers novel insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms and progression of pelvic organ prolapse (POP) following childbirth-an area of ongoing interest in pelvic floor research [1, 32]. Although clinically diagnosed postpartum POP is relatively uncommon, its subclinical presentation may represent an early stage in disease development. We intend to include more positive cases in our research to better characterize the disease trajectory. However, the current study was limited by a relatively high loss-to-follow-up rate, which contributed to a reduced final sample size.

To address this, we conducted a post hoc power analysis based on the effect sizes observed in our data. The estimated statistical power ranged from approximately 0.58 to 0.97 depending on the specific comparisons. While this indicates suboptimal power, it also suggests that some comparisons may have been underpowered, potentially contributing to non-significant findings. In future studies, we plan to recruit a larger cohort and conduct an a priori sample size calculation to ensure sufficient statistical power. With a more robust sample size, we will also be able to conduct multivariable regression analyses to better examine the associations between POP and key anatomical support structures such as the LAM. These steps will enhance the validity and generalizability of our findings.

Our study has limitations. First, postpartum attrition resulted in a limited number of primiparas available for longitudinal analysis at the final visit. Second, we lacked normal primiparas as controls at four months postpartum to compare with POP subgroups longitudinally. Finally, all enrolled primiparas had insufficient electrical activity data of the pelvic floor muscles, which would provide a more precise measure of levator ani muscle function.

Conclusion

Postpartum POP is relatively common among primiparas, most frequently presenting as cystocele often accompanied by uterine prolapse. Dysfunction of the levator ani muscle is the primary factor contributing to POP after childbirth. Recovery of pelvic floor support varies depending on the compartments involved, with significant improvement observed in primiparas with multiple-compartment POP during the early postpartum period. Nevertheless, asymptomatic pelvic organ malposition following vaginal delivery still requires clinical attention.
