The SD1 locus affects primary seed dormancy in winter malting barley
Authors: Karl H Kunze, Travis E Rooney, Daniel Sweeney, Mark E Sorrells
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreharvest sprouting (PHS) resistance and seed dormancy are key targets for malting barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) in environments with a high probability of rain events at harvest. Characterization has been limited in winter malting barley compared to spring malting barley in the United States. We used a modified half‐sibling winter malting barley breeding population to map PHS at maturity and germination traits during after‐ripening to identify marker associations with dormancy and rate of dormancy loss. Seed dormancy locus 1 (SD1) on chromosome 5H has been identified as an H. vulgare L. alanine aminotransferase (HvAlaAT) gene. The SD1 locus was significantly associated with the rate of primary seed dormancy loss in the winter malting barley population, and additional quantitative trait loci (QTLs) for PHS resistance and seed dormancy were identified. Identified markers for HvAlaAT alleles and other QTL for seed dormancy can provide greater precision for selecting lines that have high PHS resistance but short dormancy periods to produce high‐quality malt.

INTRODUCTION

Barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) is grown worldwide for food, animal feed, and malt for brewing and distilling. A significant portion of barley acreage in the United States and Canada is grown for malting and distilling purposes due to the high premiums compared to animal feed and food. Changes in climate, shifting cropping systems, and demand for locally farmed products are pushing the adoption of winter malting barley (WMB) to be grown in an expanding range of environments. Growing barley in new environments can be challenging, as much of the current barley germplasm in North America has been bred for environments where precipitation is infrequent while the grain matures and is harvested. Selection of malting barley for environments where rain is a frequent threat at harvest requires selection of genotypes that do not sprout at harvest but also provide high‐quality malt within a minimal storage time, as malting requires rapid germination.

Primary seed dormancy is defined as the inability of a seed to germinate under favorable conditions (Bewley et al., 2013) and was an evolutionary mechanism to avoid stress environments. Dormancy in barley is induced during seed development and is influenced by genetic and environmental factors such as temperature, water content, oxygen availability, and light (Gubler et al., 2005). Seed dormancy is regulated by the ratio of abscisic acid (ABA) to gibberellic acid (GA) in the barley grain (Finch‐Savage & Leubner‐Metzger, 2006). Peak ABA content occurs toward the end of seed development but decreases as physiological maturity (PM) approaches (Benech‐Arnold et al., 2006). A high ABA/GA ratio maintains dormancy, and a decreasing amount of ABA to GA over time results in a loss of dormancy (Gómez‐Cadenas et al., 1999; Piskurewicz et al., 2008). The duration of dormancy varies widely from days to months based on genotype and environmental conditions during grain fill as well as the period of time after PM (Gong et al., 2014; Rodríguez et al., 2015; Sweeney, Kunze, et al., 2021). Prolonged seed dormancy is an undesirable characteristic for malting barley. Malting barley that has prolonged seed dormancy results in germination that is uneven, has low kernel vigor, and is expensive for farmers/maltsters to store to wait for the seed dormancy to be lost. Barley that has little to no dormancy at PM is prone to preharvest sprouting (PHS), where wet and humid conditions induce starch degradation and germination in the field before harvest (Bradford, 1995). Symptoms of PHS can be visible as emerged radicles and coleoptiles or the seed can undergo pre‐germination where starch begins to degrade before any visible appearance or signs of germination (Li et al., 2003).

Achieving the balance between PHS resistance and high‐quality malt is a key objective for barley breeding in environments where rain occurs at harvest. Genetic sources of PHS resistance and seed dormancy have been well characterized in barley. The large effect quantitative trait locus (QTL), SD1 (seed dormancy locus 1), on chromosome 5H has been mapped extensively in a wide range of barley germplasm (Gao et al., 2003; Lin et al., 2009; Ullrich et al., 2009). Both SD1 and seed dormancy locus 2 (SD2) genes have been cloned and identified as alanine aminotransferase at SD1 (H. vulgare L. alanine aminotransferase [HvAlaAT]) (Sato et al., 2016), and a mitogen‐activated protein kinase kinase 3 at SD2 (H. vulgare L. mitogen kinase kinase 3 [HvMKK3]) (Nakamura et al., 2016). Alleles for both HvAlaAT and HvMKK3 have been characterized by Vetch et al. (2020) and Sweeney, Kunze, et al. (2021) for spring barley germplasm. The mutation L214F in HvAlaAT results in a loss of dormancy over time. HvAlaAT has been one of the first targets of Cas9‐induced mutagenesis in barley, prolonging dormancy in mutant types (Hisano et al., 2022). Despite the known effect of HvAlaAT on long‐term primary seed dormancy, little is known about the exact mechanism (Wei et al., 2019).

Genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) have become the standard practice to identify loci controlling traits of interest in breeding populations. Advances in barley marker platforms such as the Illumina barley 50K single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) chip (Bayer et al., 2017) and the development of improved barley reference genomes (Mascher et al., 2017, 2021) have allowed higher resolution mapping of relevant malting barley traits. Numerous GWAS methods are available, and improvements to models that account for population structure (Yu and Buckler, 2006) using principal component analysis (PCA) (Price et al., 2006) and the inclusion of kinship (K) and population structure (Q) matrices allow for accurate association mapping studies. Increased computational efficiency and flexibility of models in the package “ASRGwas” (Galli et al., 2022) allow for single‐step association analysis. Single‐step association analysis reduces the loss of information that can be associated with two‐step fixed models. (Möhring & Piepho, 2009).

The objectives of this study were to genetically characterize PHS and seed dormancy loss over time in a connected half‐sib WMB breeding population. Our goals were to (a) identify QTL for PHS, seed dormancy, and seed dormancy loss over time after standardizing for PM; (b) conduct a single‐step genome‐wide association (GWA) analysis of seed dormancy traits over all year and timepoint (TP) combinations; and (c) model seed dormancy loss over time to determine winter barley lines of varying seed dormancy loss rates as a criterion for selection. Understanding the genetic basis for PHS, seed dormancy, and seed dormancy loss over time will be invaluable for breeders to select and develop malting barley varieties that have PHS resistance at harvest and a short seed dormancy period in storage.

METHODS

Plant population composition

The populations used in this study were part of the WMB breeding populations at Cornell University. Initial crosses were made in August 2017. Four bi‐parental half‐sibling populations were developed by crossing a common parent Lightning (Hayes et al., 2021), a facultative type barley, to four WMB cultivars: KWS Scala, Flavia, SY Tepee, and Wintmalt. Doubled haploids (DHs) were developed at Oregon State University using anther culture of F1 seed from each cross (Cistué et al., 2003).

DH lines were planted in fall 2019 for the 2019–2020 growing season and fall 2020 for the 2020–2021 growing season in two fields each season in Ithaca, NY. Trials for the 2019–2020 growing season were planted in a modified augmented design of 544 single 1‐m row plots. Each trial consisted of 501 single replicated experimental DH lines and 43 check plots consisting of all parent lines (5), and check cultivar Charles, replicated in blocks across the field. The trials were evaluated at two locations, “Snyder” and “Ketola.” Trials for the 2020–2021 growing season were planted as a preliminary yield trial according to a randomized 480 plot augmented block design of 3‐m × 1‐m trimmed plots. Each trial consisted of 435 experimental DH lines and 45 check plots. Checks consisted of parent lines Lightning and KWS Scala, and the cultivar Endeavor being replicated randomly throughout the trial. The trials were grown at locations “McGowan” and “Ketola,” the latter being the same location as 2019.

Field sampling and phenotyping for PHS

PM was recorded as the date when 50% of the plot lost green color from the peduncle and spike (Copeland & Crookston, 1985). For PHS phenotyping, five spikes per plot were harvested at PM. Samples were then stored for after‐ripening for 3 days and then misted in a greenhouse for 3 days. The spikes were scored for PHS on a 0–9 scale (Anderson et al., 1993).

Due to labor constraints brought on by the pandemic during the 2020 field season, phenotyping capacity was limited, and seed dormancy sampling was prioritized over PHS for that year. PHS was phenotyped on a subsample of 100 lines from one location. The subsample consisted of all facultative types across all four families, parental checks, and Charles. For 2020–2021, all plots from both locations were sampled and phenotyped for PHS.

Field sampling and phenotyping for seed dormancy

Two days after PM, bundles of 15–20 selected mature spikes were harvested, dried for 2 days at 38°C, hand threshed, and stored at −20°C to pause after‐ripening (Nagel et al., 2019). The standardization of harvesting and freezing spikes 2 days after the PM of the plot enabled observation of traits at approximately the same physiological state for all lines.

From the 2019–2020 growing season, 438 and 415 plots were sampled from Ketola and Snyder, respectively. At the Ketola field trial, plots sampled consisted of 408 experimental lines and 30 check lines. At the Snyder field trial, plots sampled consisted of 394 experimental lines and 21 check lines. The 2019–2020 field season was the first observation of the winter barley DH population in field conditions, so it was expected that some lines would have poor agronomic quality where collecting samples was not possible.

From the 2020–2021 growing season, 450 plots and 180 plots were sampled from the Ketola and McGowan field trials, respectively. At the Ketola field trial, plots sampled consisted of 428 experimental lines and 19 check lines. At the McGowan field trial, plots sampled consisted of 171 experimental lines and six check lines. DH lines for the subset were selected randomly. No intentional selection was done from lines evaluated in 2020 to maintain connectivity of experimental lines between the 2019–2020 season and 2020–2021 growing season.

To phenotype seed dormancy, all samples were removed from the freezer at the same time, beginning the after‐ripening process at the same physiological state for all lines. Samples were stored at ambient room temperature for the duration of the experiment. Two replicated germination assays were conducted for each plot for each seed dormancy assay. Additionally, two external check spring malting barley lines known to be nondormant were also included in each dormancy assay. Before each assay, the two experimental spring malting barley lines were removed from the freezer and evaluated to confirm the consistency of conditions when the seed dormancy assays were conducted. (2021). The first modification was the use of 30 kernels instead of 100 kernels. The second modification was an extended germination count from 3 days to 5 days to capture more variation in germination per sample per TP. Genotyping

The WMB DH population was genotyped with the 50K Barley Illumina SNP array (Bayer et al., 2017) at the USDA‐ARS (United States Department of Agriculture–Agricultural Research Service) Small Grain Genotyping Center in Fargo, ND. Marker positions were based on the Morex version 3 assembly and gene annotations referenced Morex version 3 (Mascher et al., 2021) and version 2 (Monat et al., 2019) assemblies. Markers were filtered to a maximum heterozygosity level of 0.01 and a minimum minor allele frequency of 0.05 using rTASSEL (Monier et al., 2022), resulting in a total of 13,452 markers. The R package “SNPRelate” (Zheng et al., 2012) was used to linkage disequilibrium (LD) prune markers using a sliding base pair window of 25,000 base pairs and a maximum LD threshold of 0.9. PCA was conducted with the SNPRelate package. After LD pruning, 7770 markers remained for analysis. Kompetitive allele‐specific polymerase chain reaction (KASP) markers were used to genotype HvAlaAT and HvMKK3 of the population following casual mutations discovered in Sato et al. (2016) and Nakamura et al. (2016), respectively. Details of KASP marker development can be found in Sweeney, Rooney, et al. (2021).

Per TP, GWAS was analyzed using a single‐step approach. The package ASRgwas (Galli et al., 2022) was used to develop the K and Q matrices, filter out missing information, and conduct single‐step association analysis using the raw data as input for each TP, trait, and year combination. RESULTS

Phenotypic distributions

Preharvest sprouting scores presented are a distribution of values across 2020 and 2021. PHS score distribution was skewed toward resistance (Figure 1). Ninety‐five percent of genotypes were classified as resistant (0–2 score), 4.4% were moderately resistant, and 1.6% were classified as PHS susceptible. Average GE was significantly lower in 2021. Heritability for GE was high at early TPs (0.8) but decreased as primary dormancy loss occurred over time. The slight increase in heritability of GE at later TPs is most likely attributed to diseased kernels as seeds were not surface‐sterilized due to population size. GI was initially low (1–1.5) at early TPs and increased as primary dormancy was lost. Unlike GE, GI variation remained after dormancy, resulting in higher heritability (0.75) at later TPs. Like GE, there was significantly more dormancy in 2021 compared to 2020, as initial genetic variation and heritability were low for PM_5 in 2021. After dormancy loss was observed, GI plateaued at a mean GI of 5.6 (Figure 2). Phenotypic correlations between GE and GI were significant between all TPs except for PM_152 (Table S2). PHS phenotypic correlations were not significantly different from GE and GI. Genotypic correlations were high for all traits, suggesting shared genetic control.

Genetics

KASP marker results

KASP marker analysis for HvAlaAT revealed that the common parent Lightning and parent Flavia contained the dormant “C” allele, and parents KWS Scala, SY Tepee, and Wintmalt contained the nondormant “G” allele, making Lightning the donor for the dormant HvAlaAT in the populations. Seed dormancy loss over time by HvAlaAT alleles showed clear differentiation between dormant and nondormant haplotypes (Figures 2 and 3). Experimental lines and the parents were monomorphic for the dormant HvMKK3 allele. Check varieties Charles and Endeavor had the highly nondormant allele (N*) for HvMKK3, a locus identified in spring malting barley to be indicative of high malting quality but also high PHS susceptibility. An analysis of different barley haplotypes for HvAlaAT and HvMKK3 by Sweeney, Kunze, et al. (2021) found that winter barley haplotypes with the nondormant N* HvMKK3 allele had significantly higher PHS scores than any other group, including spring barley N* HvMKK3 haplotypes. However, given the low frequency of the N* allele for HvMKK3 in this population, we are limited in what inferences can be made regarding primary seed dormancy and its potential interaction with HvAlaAT. Nonetheless, Charles and Endeavor served as useful positive controls for PHS and germination throughout the experiment.

Population structure

PCA revealed variation attributed to the first, second, and third principal components (PCs) at 14.55%, 5.69%, and 4.9%, respectively. The PCA plot displays the first two PCs on the x and y axes (Figure 4). The first component separated SY Tepee populations from Wintmalt, KWS Scala, and Flavia populations, with Lightning having an intermediate PCA distance between the two parents. The clustering of Wintmalt, KWS Scala, Flavia, and progeny of those parents had clustered PCA values, suggesting that those parents are highly related relative to Lightning and SY Tepee. Position of genotypes remained largely unchanged when separate recombinant inbred line populations of crosses not including Lightning were analyzed.

Model fitting using a single‐step analysis

Not all combinations of factors resulted in marker trait associations. Fixed effects were identified by Wald tests and by models that did not converge with reduced inclusion of effects. Within years, significant fixed effects included location, replication of seed germination test per plot per TP (Rep), and the spatial dimension of row. Model terms that were not significant by the Wald test for fixed effects or AIC for non‐fixed effects were not included. (Table S1). In analyses across years, year was identified as a significant term as either a fixed effect or residual term for all GI trait models and all GE trait models except for GE 2020/2021 at PM_152. Not all models converged, particularly models for the GE trait as variation was low at early and late TPs. After accounting for model terms, significant marker traits were identified based on false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p‐values using the R package p‐adjust within each GWA analysis. Traits were considered significant if the FDR‐adjusted p‐value was below 0.05 (alpha value). The highest unadjusted p‐value that was below the FDR threshold identified in this study was 3.8738 × 10−4.

Genome‐wide association analysis results

Using an FDR of alpha = 0.05, 48 unique marker trait associations were identified across the genome (Figure 5) (Table S3). Given the highly structured breeding populations, the LD in this population was high, and markers in LD were reported as a single group in Table S3 if r 2 > 0.5 between markers on a specific chromosome. A range of the minimum and maximum positions of significant markers within each LD group is presented as we did not have the resolution to determine where in LD blocks the causal variant was located. Six individual marker trait associations and one LD group located on 5H were detected for PHS. For germination traits, a total of 48 unique locations were detected across 36 TP–trait–year combinations.

(2015). After accounting for LD blocks, gene annotations from barley genome reference versions 2 and 3 were gathered into a list of all markers 2 Mb upstream and downstream of either single markers or the minimum and maximum of an LD block using modifications of the GALLO package. DISCUSSION

Identifying sources of PHS resistance that do not compromise malting quality has been a key target in malting barley breeding. Numerous barley mapping studies have identified dormancy loci such as SD1 and SD2 on 5H and SD3 on 7H, but few have evaluated dormancy loss with extensive germination assays in a WMB breeding population. We found that HvAlaAT alleles at the SD1 locus contributed to a significant difference in the rate of dormancy loss in WMB from 5 days (PM_5) to 152 days (PM_152) after PM (Figure 3). In addition to SD1, we found numerous other regions of low to moderate effect associated with dormancy loss, including markers associated with alpha‐amylase inhibitors and beta‐amylase glucan‐branching enzymes on 2H, two markers for beta‐glucan and diastatic power on 4H, and SD3 on 7H (Romagosa et al., 1999). Identifying multiple regions associated with dormancy loss enables breeders to select for a desired balance of seed dormancy in target environments.

Observed PHS was low in the WMB DH population (Figure 1). PHS scoring occurred at the same post PM as the first TP in seed dormancy assays because PHS samples are harvested at PM, after‐ripened for 4 days and scored 3 days after misting. This was similar to PM_4 when given misting conditions and PM_7 when scored, but PHS measured shoot and coleoptile growth, while germination did not. Despite similar physiological states, the phenotypic correlation of PHS and GE and GI at early TPs was low (0.13) probably because of limited variation (Table S2). This is most likely due to the high dormancy observed in 2021 both with PHS and seed dormancy as well as sampling error.

Mean seed dormancy loss occurred between TPs PM_47 and PM_68 in 2020 and between TPs PM_68 and PM_96 in 2021 (Table 1) (Figures 2 and 3). Genetic variation for GE was initially high in 2020 and significantly reduced once seed dormancy loss was nearly complete. Genetic variation for GE in 2021 increased slightly and decreased once dormancy loss occurred, albeit at later TPs compared to 2020. GI genetic variation for 2020 and 2021 followed similar rates but mean GI values were higher at earlier TPs in 2020 compared to 2021 (Table 1). Compared to GI of spring malting barley populations, WMBs were observed to have lower 1‐day germination values, even after dormancy loss, which is reflected as a penalty in the calculated GI score and high genetic variation at later TPs. The low PHS scores, prolonged dormancy, and moderate GI values suggest that selection for barley with shorter dormancy times and higher GI should be a priority in WMB.

Genetic correlations between GE, GI, and PHS were high at early and later TPs (Table S2). GWA analysis results suggest some common MTA associations between GE, GI, and PHS at a few loci. PHS and GI are similar measurements as both observe rate of germination over time, whereas GE is a binary observation of seed germination. Marker trait association associated with GE PM_152 was in low LD compared to surrounding upstream and downstream markers and could have been associated with different factors contributing to GE, such as diseased kernels from scald (Rhynchosporium commune Zaffarano, McDonald, and Linde sp. nov). We also found a separate LD group identified for HvAlaAT from PHS LD group on 5H (Figure S1). Given the skewed results and low number of samples for PHS, more PHS testing would be needed to differentiate HvAlaAT from not being associated with PHS.

Statistical modeling for GWAS

Fitting single‐step models retains more information compared to using linear mixed models in a two‐stage analysis. Often, errors associated with the first step analysis of fixed effect models have different error variances. If differing error variances are not weighted in the second stage of analysis, this results in a loss of information (Möhring & Piepho, 2009). Using random effects in two‐stage analysis requires that best linear unbiased predictions be deregressed after the first stage, particularly if genotypes in the population are unbalanced (Garrick et al., 2009). Fitting single‐step models for each TP produced challenges, as some models did not converge for some TPs (Table S1). The lack of convergence issues associated with GE and early and late TPs was most likely due to low variation in the trait. Other model terms such as location were significant across many combinations, indicating either field effects or sampling error within locations. Each replication of the seed dormancy TP was scored by one individual, and replication may have been associated with scoring differences. Discrepancies in scoring differences could have resulted from declaring whether a kernel was considered germinated by coleoptile length; however, repeated common checks across replications should help correct scorer bias. Fixed effect terms such as location:row most likely correspond to some form of sampling error in the field. SD1 was tested as a fixed effect in model building to determine potential epistatic effects associated with the SD1 locus. No novel QTL were identified when including the SD1 as a fixed effect

Marker trait associations

Role of HvAlaAT (SD1) in long‐term seed dormancy

The locus SD1 was initially discovered as a seed dormancy QTL in barley by Oberthur et al. (1995) and has since been validated by many researchers (Han et al., 1996; Romagosa et al., 1999). HvAlaAT was cloned by Sato et al. (2016) and Sanger sequenced by Vetch et al. (2020) and Sweeney, Rooney, et al. (2021). HvAlaAT and the SD1 locus have often been studied in the context of epistatic interactions with HvMKK3 of the SD2 locus. This study examined the genetic effect on seed dormancy of WMB with HvAlaAT conditional on the dormant allele state of HvMKK3. HvAlaAT was detected at most TP, trait, and year combinations but was not detected at PM_5 or in PHS GWAS. In a target CRISPR‐Cas9 mutagenesis experiment, Hisano et al. (2022) performed a targeted modification of the L214F causal loci of HvAlaAT. They found that the knockout of HvAlaAT delayed but did not inhibit germination.

ABI5 and ABA‐responsive protein on 5H

Three potential gene candidates were found in the 5H 462–486 Mb LD region based on gene annotations and previous literature. The first gene HORVU.MOREX.r3.5HG0486380 was identified as ABI5 (Abscisic Acid INSENSITIVE 5) (Collin et al., 2020; Seiler et al., 2014) in response to drought tolerance and stress. The second was a GEM (GL2 Expression modulator) GRAM‐containing/ABA‐responsive protein (HORVU.MOREX.r3.5HG0487770.1) (Mauri et al., 2016) located downstream of ABI5. They found that mutant types of the GEM would delay germination. Mauri et al. (2016) also discovered that 24‐h cold treatment eliminated the effects of GEM, which could explain why the locus was detected in PHS assays more frequently than in GE and GI assays, as PHS samples did not undergo any cold treatment. Increases in ABA content in 2021 could have prolonged the effect of this gene in PM_5 for GI (Figure 3). Weather conditions during grain fill in 2021 were dry up until harvest, which could have induced higher ABA associated with this gene. A third potential gene related to seed germination identified by gene annotation includes a 1,4‐alpha‐glucan branching enzyme (GlgB [HORVU.MOREX.r3.5HG0488090.1], a key regulator in amylopectin degradation; Sun et al. 1998; Regina et al. 2010). Isoamylase HvISA3 (HORVU.MOREX.r2.5HG0404420) of similar function was identified in the same region for Morex version 2 (Shu & Rasmussen, 2014). Isoamylases are important for starch degradation as they hydrolyze α‐(1,6)‐glycosidic linkages and debranch amylopectin during grain filling (Gous & Fox, 2017). Starch with higher amylose content is hydrolyzed more slowly by amylolytic enzymes, and higher amylose content has been hypothesized to be a contributing factor for either grain dormancy or delay in germination rate observed by moderate GI values.

Other marker regions

Three candidate genes potentially related to seed dormancy were identified in the 160–469 Mb region on 2H. An alpha‐amylase inhibitor (Mena et al., 1992) (Bdai‐1) (HORVU.MOREX.r2.2HG0176960) was located in this region. The inhibition of alpha‐amylase could delay the breakdown of starch and potentially delay germination. An activated protein kinase and a 1,4‐alpha‐glucan‐branching enzyme (Regina et al., 2010) were also identified. Alpha beta‐glucan branching enzymes are involved in the breakdown of complex starches. On 4H, two marker regions of potential interest for seed dormancy were another alpha‐amylase inhibitor as well as a QTL identified for protein content QGpc.StMo‐4H (Mohammadi et al., 2015). The second LD region on 4H located on the distal end of 4H has been associated with malting quality QTLs QDp.DiMo‐4H (diastatic power) and QBgnm.MT2‐4H (beta‐glucan). Higher diastatic power could result in faster degradation of starches. Variation in beta‐glucan content could be an indirect indicator of seed dormancy, as higher beta‐glucan can indicate some seed dormancy. On 7H, the marker located near the centromeric region was associated with a beta‐amylase, ABA‐responsive binding factor, and glucan endo‐1,3‐beta‐glucosidase. The region was also identified as SD3 in Romagosa et al. (1999).

Selection for seed dormancy

For a barley variety to malt uniformly, there should be no seed dormancy once barley reaches the malt house. In our seed dormancy analysis, the mean time to loss of dormancy occurred at PM_45 to PM_67 in 2020 and PM_67 to PM_96 in 2021. If we assume that WMB does not reach the malthouse until 60–90 days after harvest, most lines in this experiment would have no dormancy by the malting stage. Germination percentage (GE), however, is not equivalent to germination index, and the rate at which barley kernels germinate is another important consideration for malting. Many experimental lines in this population have reached a peak GI of 5.5 compared to Charles, Endeavor, and KWS Scala GI values in the range of 7–9. Initial germination delay could be caused by a number of previously described genes. Future efforts in selection for malting barley seed dormancy should focus on a higher germination index once dormancy is lost.

PHS and seed dormancy considerations

The seed dormancy for most nondormant haplotypes at early post PM and low PHS correlates with the HvAlaAT haplotype that facilitates achieving a balance between PHS resistance at harvest and minimal duration of seed dormancy. In an analysis of the effect of weather covariates on PHS susceptibility, Sweeney, Kunze, et al. (2021) found that WMB PHS was sensitive to increases in temperature during grain fill, but this was conditioned on varying states of the HvMKK3 allele. Dormant HvMKK3 haplotypes resulted in low to moderate PHS scores, which were similar to our WMB population (Figure 1). Developing WMB populations with a parent containing the N or N* HvMKK3 allele is needed to understand the epistatic effects of HvMKK3 and HvAlaAT that are better understood in spring malting barley populations (Sweeney, Rooney, et al., 2021). More research on PHS of WMB lines is needed, particularly in years where conditions during grain fill would result in less seed dormancy. The results of this study indicate that we can select for less dormancy in this WMB population while still maintaining strong PHS resistance.

Improvement of seed dormancy assays

Dormancy assays are labor‐intensive, and many considerations were made to balance the statistical power and allocation of labor. Imaging technologies have the potential to recognize barley kernels, map their location in a petri plate, and potentially measure kernel development over time. This would combine the benefits of germination assays, where we measure initial germination, with the measurements of PHS, where we evaluate shoot and coleoptile growth after 3 days. Developing a more precise understanding of germination, root, and coleoptile growth in the initial days after initiation of germination has important implications for understanding malting quality and PHS alike.

CONCLUSION

Results from single‐step TP GWAS analysis combined with KASP markers validate the significant effect of HvAlaAT in primary seed dormancy of a WMB population. HvAlaAT was observed to significantly affect germination rate, GE, and seed dormancy duration from 12 days post PM to complete dormancy loss. Additionally, we found that the growing year environment had a significant effect on seed dormancy. Genetic regions in high LD with the HvAlaAT KASP marker explained the most variation for seed dormancy traits and were observed at most TP trait combinations. In addition to HvAlaAT, we also found a distinct LD region on 5H associated with GE and PHS. Understanding the role of seed dormancy loss in a WMB breeding population allows more informed selection decisions to reduce seed dormancy negatively associated with malting quality while also maintaining strong PHS resistance in the field.
