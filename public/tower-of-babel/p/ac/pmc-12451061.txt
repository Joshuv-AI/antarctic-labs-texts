Excimer Laser‐Assisted Angioplasty for In‐Stent Restenosis of the Right Coronary Artery in a Diabetic Patient: A Complex Revascularization Case
Authors: Vasavdatta Sharma, Tom Devasia, Monika Jawanjal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a case of a 63‐year‐old male with a history of multiple percutaneous coronary interventions (PCIs) and diabetes mellitus, presenting with exertional angina of 3 months duration. Coronary angiography revealed in‐stent restenosis (ISR) in the right coronary artery (RCA) with heavily calcific disease. Given the severity of symptoms and lesion complexity, the patient underwent laser‐assisted percutaneous coronary intervention using excimer laser. Successful revascularization was achieved with drug‐eluting stent (DES) implantation under intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) guidance. This case underscores the importance of individualized treatment strategies, advanced hardware selection, and the role of excimer laser in ISR management.

Introduction

In‐stent restenosis (ISR) remains a significant challenge in percutaneous coronary interventions, particularly in patients with complex lesion subsets and comorbidities like diabetes mellitus. While drug‐eluting stents (DES) have reduced the incidence of ISR, reintervention in these lesions—especially when associated with calcification or chronic total occlusion (CTO)—requires advanced techniques. Excimer laser coronary angioplasty (ELCA) is an established adjunctive tool that facilitates plaque modification and debulking in ISR, enabling optimal stent deployment and expansion. We present a complex case of ISR in the RCA treated successfully using Excimer laser in a diabetic patient.

Case Presentation

A 63‐year‐old male with a history of coronary artery disease and diabetes mellitus presented with progressively worsening exertional angina over a duration of 3 months, classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class II. He had undergone prior PCI to the left circumflex artery (LCx) and right coronary artery (RCA) in 2013, followed by re‐intervention to the obtuse marginal branch (LCx‐OM) in 2021. He was compliant with his medications. On presentation, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Cardiovascular and systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Coronary Angiography: Revealed calcific single‐vessel disease with significant in‐stent restenosis (ISR) of the RCA. Previously placed stents in the LCx and LCx‐OM were patent. In light of his ongoing symptoms and angiographic findings, the patient was planned for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) to the RCA with laser‐assisted therapy and DES implantation.

Treatment

The procedure was performed via right femoral access. A guiding catheter (JR 3.5 6 French) was used to engage the RCA, and a simultaneous injection from the left coronary system was employed to visualize the distal segment of the RCA due to ambiguous distal cap morphology. Outcome and Follow‐Up

Postprocedure angiography showed excellent final results and flow through the RCA with no residual stenosis or dissection (Figure 7). The patient remained hemodynamically stable and was discharged on day 3 post procedure, on dual antiplatelet therapy and optimal medical management for secondary prevention. At 1‐month follow‐up, the patient reported complete resolution of angina symptoms and good functional status.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the complexity and challenges of managing ISR in the presence of significant symptoms. ISR often involves neointimal hyperplasia and can be complicated by underlying calcification, making lesion preparation critical before stent deployment. ISR usually needs treatment based upon the anatomical and biological mechanism involved. Imaging plays an extremely important role in the management of ISR. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) helps in characterization of the plaque and understanding the mechanism of ISR to optimize treatment [3]. As per Wojciech Wanha et al. [4], the treatment options of ISR include drug coated balloon (DCB) and drug eluting stents (DES). Plaque modification devices used in the treatment of ISR include cutting/scoring balloon, OPN NC balloons, and in severe cases, intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) or excimer laser. Heavily calcified ISRs are usually treated with IVL, and moderately calcified ISR or ISR chronic total occlusions can be treated with ELCA or IVL. When ISR is predominantly because of neointimal hyperplasia, treatment is dependent on the pattern of ISR. For focal ISR, a high‐pressure or scoring/cutting balloon may be sufficient; ELCA or atherectomy may be beneficial in selected cases. For diffuse ISR, atherectomy or scoring/cutting balloon angioplasty followed by repeat DES implantation is typically advised [5].

The use of Excimer laser coronary angioplasty (ELCA) allowed for effective lesion debulking and improved balloon as well as stent delivery and final expansion in the present case. Simultaneous contralateral injection facilitated visualization of the distal RCA, aiding in safe and effective distal wire crossing in this scenario. ELCA is a debulking technique that uses ultraviolet spectrum wavelengths to ablate tissue. It does this by generating heat and shockwaves and thus produces plaque disruption [1]. It may also be effective to facilitate modification of the proximal cap and allow its penetration into a chronic total occlusion [2]. Use of IVUS further optimized stent expansion and apposition, reducing the risk of recurrence by achieving a good post stent minimal stent area.

Despite some early historical studies demonstrating the safety and feasibility of this technique, there are limited recent data to support the systematic use of ELCA as the primary treatment for ISR, and no randomized data are available on the use of ELCA for DES‐ISR. ISR‐CTO, in itself, was suggested to represent its own distinct class of ISR in the Mehran classification. However, in selected cases, it may have an adjunctive role for lesion preparation, particularly for recurrent ISR in the setting of severe calcification. For the rarely encountered patients with undilatable ISR as the result of severely underexpanded stents due to a heavily calcified arterial wall, this technique may facilitate stent expansion, particularly if contrast is injected to induce further barotrauma and microcavitation [6].

Evidence in ELCA stems only from small observational series, but it nevertheless remains a useful option as a bailout strategy when other therapeutic strategies have failed [6]. I Increasing case series data suggest that ELCA can be employed safely and effectively in a number of scenarios with fibro‐calcific disease, including chronic total occlusion recanalization, debulking native, and vein graft disease, and in the presence of peri‐stent calcium in the setting of in‐stent restenosis [7]. Currently, ELCA has been proposed as an attractive treatment option for certain challenging subsets of lesions, such as heavily calcified undilatable lesions or diffuse ISR. In the setting of underexpanded stents, ELCA should be used with caution owing to the possible risk of vessel perforation and severe no‐reflow phenomenon [1]. Cost of ELCA remains a limitation for its use; however, in the future, its indication and usage are expected to be on the rise.

Conclusion

Excimer laser‐assisted angioplasty is a valuable tool in the management of complex in‐stent restenosis, particularly in patients with calcified lesions and prior interventions. This case highlights the importance of tailored revascularization strategies, advanced imaging, and the appropriate use of adjunctive devices to optimize outcomes in challenging scenarios.
