Feasibility of using real-world data to emulate substance use disorder clinical trials: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Guneet S. Janda, Molly Moore Jeffery, Reshma Ramachandran, Joseph S. Ross, Joshua D. Wallach
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionReal-world evidence is receiving considerable attention as a way to evaluate the efficacy and safety of medical products for substance use disorders (SUDs). However, the feasibility of using real-world data (RWD) to emulate clinical trials evaluating treatments for SUDs is uncertain. The aim of this study is to identify the number of clinical trials evaluating treatments for SUDs with reported results that could be feasibly emulated using observational data from contemporary insurance claims and/or electronic health record (EHR) data.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, all phase 2–4 trials evaluating treatments for SUDs registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with reported results were identified. Each trial was evaluated to determine if the indications, interventions, at least 80% of eligibility criteria, comparators, and primary end points could be ascertained using contemporarily available administrative claims and/or structured EHR data.ResultsThere were 272 SUD trials on ClinicalTrials.gov with reported results. Of these, when examining feasibility using contemporarily available administrative claims and/or structured EHR data, 262 (96.3%) had indications that were ascertainable; 194 (71.3%) had interventions that were ascertainable; 21 (7.7%) had at least 80% of eligibility criteria that were ascertainable; 17 (6.3%) had active comparators that were ascertainable; and 61 (22.4%) had primary end points that were ascertainable. In total, there were no trials for which all 5 characteristics were ascertainable using contemporarily available administrative claims and/or structured EHR data. When considering placebo comparators as ascertainable, there were 6 (2.2%) trials that had all 5 key characteristics classified as ascertainable from contemporarily available administrative claims and/or structured EHR data.ConclusionsNo trials evaluating treatments for SUDs could be feasibly emulated using contemporarily available RWD, demonstrating a need for an increase in the resolution of data capture within a public health system to facilitate trial emulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-024-02307-1.

Introduction

Treatment for substance use disorders (SUDs) remains a significant challenge for the US healthcare system. It is estimated that less than 10% of people who need treatment for SUDs receive it [1–7]. The cost of SUDs both in terms of healthcare spending (i.e., $13 billion in 2017 [8, 9]) and burden of disease are high. SUDs accounted for 6% of the total years of life lost to disease and 7% of the total years lived with disability in the US in 2019 [10]. While the evidence guiding treatment decisions for SUDs typically comes from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which are considered the gold standard to evaluate the efficacy and safety of medical products, there are a unique set of challenges to studying populations with SUDs. In addition to recruitment and retention difficulties, RCTs of populations with SUDs must address multiple ethical concerns, especially when the substance in question is illicit or the populations are vulnerable (e.g., in criminal justice settings) [11–15]. These issues have generated considerable interest in the role of observational data to evaluate the comparative safety and effectiveness of interventions for SUDs.

There exists a large volume of patient information within administrative claims and electronic health record (EHR) data generated through routine clinical care (i.e., real-world data [RWD]), which often contains relevant information in diverse clinical environments and patients compared to those found in RCTs [13, 16]. Under the target trial emulation framework, which is designed to draw causal inference from observational data, RWD can be used to emulate the populations, treatments, and outcomes of RCTs, thereby generating evidence on the effectiveness and safety of medical product interventions [13, 16, 17].

There has been considerable interest in the use of RWD to evaluate treatment options for SUDs. Given that there are few unique therapeutics for SUDs, repurposing and off-label usage of these interventions is being increasingly explored [2, 18–22]. These types of evaluations are potentially well suited to capture in RWD since, in contrast to novel investigational agents, the use of therapeutics already approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) are often captured within the EHR and claims data. Several prior studies have used RWD to examine patterns of SUDs and comparative effects of treatment across various clinical contexts [22–27]. Furthermore, in 2022, the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) hosted a workshop focused on introducing the use of observational data for target trial emulation for the evaluation of therapies for SUDs, demonstrating interest at the federal level for the implementation of this approach [23]. However, evidence suggests that many clinical trials may not be good candidates for target trial emulations using claims and structured EHR data as sources of RWD [28, 29], and little is known about feasibility of using RWD to emulate trials evaluating treatments for SUDs.

To help understand the feasibility of using RWD to emulate trials addressing treatments for SUDs, we performed a cross-sectional study of all clinical trials evaluating treatments for SUDs registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with reported results. Our aim was to determine if the indications, interventions, eligibility criteria, comparators, and primary end points of these trials could be ascertained from contemporarily available insurance claims and/or structured EHR data.

Methods

Sample construction

We first searched ClinicalTrials.gov for all interventional studies under the “Substance-Related Disorders” heading in the “condition or disease” field. We included trials regardless of the intervention (e.g., pharmacological, behavioral, device). We then filtered our sample to only include phase 2, 3, and 4 trials. We further limited our sample to trials with reported results to prevent the inclusion of trials that were unable to be conducted due to issues with the design or scope of the trial. We then manually reviewed each trial to identify those specifically designed to reduce or modify the use of a substance in patients with a SUD, including patients with substance abuse or substance dependence, with or without co-morbid conditions (e.g., treatment of alcohol use disorder (AUD) and generalized anxiety disorder). Eligible trials had outcomes such as frequency of use (measured via self-report or laboratory testing), time to relapse, and number of heavy use days.

We reviewed each ClinicalTrials.gov registration and identified the SUD of interest, specific intervention(s) used, eligibility criteria, comparator group(s), and primary end points. To determine the feasibility of using RWD to emulate a trial, we then established whether the characteristics were ascertainable from contemporarily available insurance claims and/or structured EHR data using previously developed methods [30]. When considering information available in the EHR, we specifically determined if the information was captured in routine clinical care (e.g., common laboratory tests such as complete blood count or urinalysis) and if the relevant information was contained in a structured, discrete field (e.g., laboratory values or encounter diagnoses). In contrast, information within unstructured elements in the EHR, such as clinical notes and impressions, was considered to not be ascertainable for the purposes of trial emulation given the limitations of identifying such information at scale. We used EHR’s at two clinical institutions (Yale New Haven Health System and the Mayo Clinic) to determine which characteristics were ascertainable. We also considered what information could be available from claims databases, specifically if there was an International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD-10), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), or Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) code for the data element of interest. Detailed examples of considerations can be found in a supplementary table [see Supplementary File 1].

The primary outcome was the number and percentage of trials in which the indication, intervention, eligibility criteria, comparator(s), and primary end points could be ascertained from insurance claims and/or structured EHR data.

Indications

Indications were considered to be ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data when there was a specific ICD-10, CPT, or HCPCS code for the substance use disorder. Since these coding systems have not yet uniformly adopted the terminology of use disorders, we included codes for substance abuse and substance dependence, since those represent the terms used before the adoption of the “use disorder” naming system.

Interventions

Interventions were classified as ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data if the specific intervention as written was associated with a national drug code (NDC), an ICD-10 procedure code, a CPT code, or a HCPCS code. The intervention had to be coded as specified in the trial protocol. For example, if a trial had an intervention where non-specific psychotherapy was used, we considered any coding for psychotherapy as applicable to ascertaining the intervention. However, if a trial called for a specific modality of therapy (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy), there had to be a code for that specific modality for it to be considered ascertainable.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were classified as ascertainable using contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data if the specific criterion could be determined as fulfilled or not with specific ICD-10, CPT, or HCPCS codes and/or discrete data values captured during routine clinical care and stored in a structured field within the EHR. For example, if a criterion specified that a patient needed to have used opioids at least 5 times in the past week, that criterion would only be considered ascertainable using the EHR if there was a field labeled “frequency of opioid use over the past week”, or a structured response to a question such as “have you use opioids greater than 5 times this past week?”. In contrast, the observation “patient used opioids 5 times over past week” written in a clinical note would not be considered ascertainable. For a trial to be considered to have eligibility criteria ascertainable from contemporary claims and/or structured EHR data, it had to have greater than 80% of its total criteria deemed ascertainable.

Comparators

Comparator groups were classified as ascertainable using contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data when groups were allocated to an arm that received a treatment associated with a national drug code (NDC), an ICD-10 procedure code, a CPT code, or a HCPCS code. Groups were not ascertainable if the treatment was not associated with a code or if the treatment was a placebo or sham intervention. If a trial was single arm or consisted of only experimental arms, we considered the comparator as ascertainable in our overall evaluation.

Primary end points

Primary end points, as defined by the investigators on ClinicalTrials.gov, were classified as ascertainable when they could be found within contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data, including any standardized disease scores or response criteria. For example, end points such as hospitalization, death, or a change in a commonly utilized clinical scoring system such as the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) would be considered ascertainable, while other outcomes such as perceived substance craving were considered not ascertainable.

Statistical analysis

We conducted descriptive analyses to quantify the percentage of trials for which the indications, interventions, eligibility criteria, comparators, and primary end points could be ascertained using contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data. Analyses were conducted using JMP Pro software, version 15.0.0 (SAS Institute Inc).

Sensitivity analysis

Previous trial emulations have demonstrated that being unable to emulate some strict eligibility criteria may not have a meaningful impact on emulation findings [31, 32]. Therefore, to account for the fact that not all eligibility criteria may be relevant for determining the target population, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a 50% cut-off for eligible criteria, as was done in our previous work [30]. As a second sensitivity analysis, we also considered placebo comparator arms to be ascertainable [30]. Although placebo-controlled trials may not always be feasible to emulate [17], prior emulations have approximated placebo comparators using an unrelated active comparator drug that is expected to have no effect on the trial outcome of interest [29, 33, 34].

Results

Sample characteristics

We identified 272 phase 2–4 clinical trials evaluating interventions for the treatment of SUDs with reported results on ClinicalTrials.gov (Fig. 1; Table 1). Most trials were of treatments for alcohol use disorder (AUD) (103, 37.9%), stimulant use disorder (68, 25.0%), or opioid use disorder (OUD) (37, 13.6%).

Indications

Of the 272 trials, 262 (96.3%) had indications for the treatment of patients with SUDs that were ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data (Table 2). Of these, 103 (39.3%) were for the treatment of AUD, 59 (22.5%) for cocaine use disorder, and 34 (13.0%) for OUD. Of the 10 (3.7%) trials with SUDs that were not ascertainable, 6 (60%) were for methamphetamine use disorder, 3 (30%) were for heroin use disorder, and 1 (10%) was for benzodiazepine use disorder.

Interventions

Of the 272 trials, 194 (71.3%) had interventions that were ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data (Table 2). Of these, 183 (94.3%) were drug trials, 5 (2.6%) were combined drug and behavioral therapy trials, 3 (1.6%) were behavioral therapy trials, and 3 (1.6%) were device trials. Of the 78 trials that had interventions that were not ascertainable, 18 (23.1%) were drug trials, 46 (59.0%) were combined drug and behavioral therapy trials, and 14 (18.0%) were behavioral therapy trials. Stratified across the various intervention modalities, 91% (183/201) of trials with a drug intervention, 9.8% (5/51) of trials with a combination of drug and behavioral interventions, and 17.6% (3/17) of trials with a behavioral intervention were considered ascertainable with respect to the intervention (Table 3).

Eligibility criteria

Of the 272 trials, 35 (12.9%) were determined to have at least 80% of their eligibility criteria that were ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data (Table 2). The most common eligibility criteria that were classified as ascertainable were SUD diagnosis (235/272, 86.4%), the administration of specific medications (188/272, 69.1%), and age (172/272, 63.2%). The most common eligibility criteria that were classified as not ascertainable were breastfeeding status (174/272, 63.2%), having a specific pattern of recent substance use (e.g., using cocaine at least 4 times in the past week; 153/272, 56.3%), and conditional modifiers (e.g., uncontrolled disease, active disease; 124/272, 45.6%).

In a sensitivity analysis, 228 trials (83.8%) were determined to have at least 50% of their eligibility criteria ascertainable when using contemporary available claims and/or structured EHR data.

Comparators

There were 244 (89.7%, 244/272) trials that had comparators of which 16 (5.9%) were determined to have comparators that were ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data (Table 2). Of these, 14 (82.4%) were trials with active drug comparators and 2 (11.8%) were trials with a combination of active drug and behavioral therapy comparators. Of the 228 (93.4%, 228/244) trials that had comparators that were not ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data, 191 (83.7%) trials had placebo comparators. The other 37 (16.2%) trials were comprised of 6 (16.2%) trials with drug comparators, 14 (37.8%) trials with behavioral plus drug comparators, and 17 (45.9%) trials with behavioral therapy comparators only.

In sensitivity analyses that considered placebo arms as ascertainable, 208 (76.5%) trials were determined to have comparators that were ascertainable when using contemporary available claims and/or structured EHR data.

Primary end points

Of the 272 trials, 61 (22.4%) had primary end points that were ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data (Table 2). The most common end points that were considered ascertainable were results of routine lab tests such as a urinalysis or toxicology screen (69, 25.4%) and receipt of a medication (10, 3.7%). The most common end points that were considered not ascertainable were end points that quantified a specific amount or frequency of substance use in a given time period (136, 50.0%) and end points that utilized the timeline follow-back method to quantify behaviors or substance use (68, 25.0%).

Trials with ascertainable indications, interventions, eligibility criteria, comparators, and primary end points

Overall, there were no trials that had all five criteria determined to be ascertainable using contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data (Table 2). When repeating the analysis using a 50% cut-off for the ascertainability of eligibility criteria, 1 trial (0.4%) had all characteristics that were ascertainable from contemporary claims and/or structured EHR data. When considering placebo arms ascertainable, 6 trials (2.2%) had all characteristics that were ascertainable from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 272 clinical trials evaluating treatments for SUDs, we found that none of the trials had indications, interventions, 80% of eligibility criteria, comparators, and primary end points that all could be ascertained from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data. Our work, which builds on previous evaluations of the feasibility of trial emulation [28, 29, 35], likely represents the first attempt to characterize trial emulation as it relates to SUDs. These findings highlight the current challenges in emulating SUD trials with RWD and demonstrate the need for improving the resolution in capturing clinical data to enable the viability of target trial emulations.

Our study findings suggest that few to no trials evaluating treatments for SUDs had the majority of eligibility criteria that could be emulated as designed using contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data as sources of RWD. These findings are in line with previous work demonstrating that eligibility criteria provide significant challenges for trial emulation [29, 35], and recent published target trial emulations have highlighted the need to omit or modify eligibility criteria in order to successfully conduct their analyses [36–41]. Our work also complements recent efforts focusing on finding ways to address some of the shortcomings and biases inherent to trial emulation with RWD [42, 43]. While these challenges are not unique to SUD trials, certain criteria such as specific pattern of substance use (e.g., episodic cocaine binges of at least $200 worth at least twice each month), a desire to get treatment, and being a significant suicide risk have specific relevance to the population of patients with SUDs. Patients with SUDs are often excluded from traditional SUD trials based on restrictive eligibility criteria [15], and our findings indicate that many of these criteria are not captured in contemporary RWD, demonstrating a missed opportunity to capture patients that are outside of highly-controlled clinical trials.

Many of the trials in our sample were placebo controlled, and when considering placebo arms as ascertainable, 6 (2.2%) trials had all 5 key characteristics classified as ascertainable. While previous studies have emulated placebo-controlled trials using active comparators expected to have no effect on the outcome of interest [31, 34], it is challenging to identify patient populations receiving placebo proxies that are similar to those receiving the experimental treatment. This may be especially true for the treatment of SUDs, where there are relatively few drugs for treatment and differences in the patient populations able to access different types of treatment. While efforts to find placebo proxies may allow for the emulation of a greater number of existing SUD trials, the use of active comparators in trials may be a more feasible approach to enabling emulation and may be a more accurate evaluation of how new treatments compare to current options.

NIDA has identified the implementation of evidence-based strategies in real-world settings as one of its 2022–2026 priority scientific areas [44], part of which includes trial emulation [23]. Although we found that few, if any, trials for the treatment of SUDs could be emulated using contemporarily available RWD, RWD may provide an avenue to study populations and provide information that is not captured in traditional RCTs, such as cost and treatment effectiveness outside of controlled trial settings and in certain populations, like pregnant people [45–48]. RWD may play a complementary role to RCTs in evidence generation, and possibly even alleviate some of the challenges that SUD trials face in recruitment, since SUD trials can be especially prone to issues with recruitment and bias introduced at the recruitment stage [49, 50]. Furthermore, trial emulations can be used to generate estimates of effectiveness and safety outcomes among more diverse populations that are routinely excluded from clinical trials. As the technology to improve the capture and processing of RWD improves, the utility of RWD for evidence generation will also improve, especially when considering recent efforts to advance the standardization of EHRs and breakthroughs in natural language processing (NLP). However, our findings demonstrate that contemporary RWD may not yet be utilized in the same manner as clinical trial data and that contemporary expectations for RWD may need to be tempered.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not empirically assess frequency of use of structured data fields in the EHR, relying on clinical experience with EHR data to identify fields likely to be commonly used; some of these data may be captured at varying rates across clinicians and health care settings. However, these methods have been used in previous evaluations [28, 29], and were supported with searches for structured data elements in the EHR. Second, we only used the EHR present at 2 clinical institutions for our determinations of ascertainability, both which were based on Epic. However, Epic is the largest single vendor for inpatient EHRs and thus represents a significant portion of the EHR from which RWD is derived [51]. Third, we did not consider advancements in natural language processing for the management of unstructured EHR data. As this technology continues to improve, the ability to extract information from clinical charts will also improve and likely make it more feasible to ascertain information relevant to trial emulation. Finally, we did not consider specialized SUD treatment registries in our evaluations of RWD. While it is likely these registries contain more detailed information, the purpose of our evaluation was to assess how the considerable volume of clinical data in contemporary EHRs and claims data could be utilized for trial emulations.

Future directions

Future investigations could further evaluate how RWD may complement or contextualize clinical trial data, including comparative effectiveness and safety evaluations of SUD treatments among populations that may not be captured in traditional randomized controlled trials due to strict eligibility criteria. Further work on trial emulation can also investigate the utilization of rapidly developing tools such as NLP, which represent a promising avenue to better leverage existing RWD.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of trials evaluating treatments for SUDs, we found that no trial had indications, interventions, eligibility criteria, comparators, and end points that could be ascertained from contemporarily available claims and/or structured EHR data. These findings suggest that current sources of RWD do not have sufficient detail and granularity to enable emulation of trials evaluating treatments for SUDs. While these emulations are unlikely to replace clinical trials, they may be able to play a complementary role to clinical trials in the generation of evidence for the efficacy and safety of medical products.
