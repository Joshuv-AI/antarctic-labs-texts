The predictive value of abdominal fat area and volume for early recurrence of after resection hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Mi Pei, Xiaoqin Yin, Limei Wang, Guojiao Zuo, Yiman Li, Jie Cheng, Chen Liu, Wei Chen, Ping Cai, Xiaoming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbdominal fat area has been reported to be associated with early recurrence (ER) after surgical resection of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). This study aimed to compare the predictive value of abdominal fat area and volume for ER after resection of HCC.MethodsWe retrospectively included 161 patients with single HCC ≤5 cm who underwent resection from May 2015 to June 2021. Visceral fat area (VFA) and subcutaneous fat area (SFA) were measured at L3, L4, and L5 vertebral levels, and L4-derived parameters were retained for subsequent analyses. Visceral fat volume (VFV) and subcutaneous fat volume (SFV) were assessed from the diaphragm to lower margin of L5. Multivariate analyses were performed to identify predictors of ER. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to compare predictive values.ResultsMultivariate analysis identified VFA and VFV as independent risk factors for ER. ROC analysis showed similar predictive values for VFA (AUC: 0.712) and VFV (AUC: 0.730; p = 0.307). The patients were divided into two groups based on tumor size: group A [(d) ≤ 3 cm] and group B [3 cm < (d) ≤ 5 cm]. VFA demonstrated a higher predictive value for group A (AUC: 0.796) compared to group B (AUC: 0.636) (p = 0.049).ConclusionsBoth VFA and VFV showed comparable predictive value for ER in patients with single HCC ≤5 cm, and VFA appeared to have better predictive performance in patients with tumor diameter ≤ 3 cm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04941-z.

Introduction

Liver cancer is the sixth most common cancer globally and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. In China, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the second most prevalent malignant neoplasm, claiming the lives of approximately 300,000 to 400,000 individuals annually [2]. Surgical resection remains an important curative treatment option for selected patients with early-stage HCC [3]. In addition to tumor-related factors, surgical approaches and technical considerations may influence perioperative outcomes and postoperative prognosis after hepatectomy [4]. However, postoperative recurrence remains common and is generally categorized as early recurrence (within 2 years) or late recurrence (after 2 years), with ER accounting for nearly 70% of all cases [5, 6]. Several factors have been identified as significant predictors of recurrence, such as tumor diameter, total bilirubin level (TBiL), and portal vein tumor thrombus, all of which affect long-term survival [7–10]. Recent evidence also suggests that preoperative tumor venous compression is associated with a higher risk of microvascular invasion and satellite nodules in patients with HCC [11].

Recent research has increasingly focused on body composition factors, particularly the accumulation of abdominal fat, as potential cancer biomarkers. Obesity is identified as an independent risk factor for various cancers [12]. Notably, abdominal fat area has been associated with adverse outcomes of HCC after surgery [8, 13, 14]. Compared with single-slice fat area, abdominal fat volume may provide a more comprehensive assessment of abdominal adiposity. Abdominal fat volume, including visceral fat volume (VFV) and subcutaneous fat volume (SFV), is significantly related to obesity and metabolic syndrome. Therefore, we hypothesized that abdominal fat volume may have predictive value for postoperative recurrence of HCC and aimed to compare its performance with that of abdominal fat area. Both abdominal fat area and volume can be measured using routine abdominal CT images.

However, evidence directly comparing abdominal fat area and volume for predicting ER after HCC resection remains limited. This study aims to compare the predictive value of abdominal fat area and volume for ER in patients undergoing hepatectomy.

Materials and methods

Study cohorts

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Army Medical University (KY2026143), and was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee of Army Medical University because of the retrospective nature of the study.

A retrospective analysis was conducted to identify patients diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) from May 2015 to June 2021. A total of 161 patients were included based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) surgically confirmed single HCC lesion ≤ 5 cm, (2) availability of computed tomography (CT) imaging within one week prior to surgery, (3) complete clinical data, and (4) complete postoperative follow-up data for ER assessment within the first two years after surgery. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) presence of multiple tumors (≥ 2 lesions), (2) history of local-regional therapies, chemotherapy, or other liver-directed treatments before surgery, (3) poor-quality imaging data unsuitable for analysis, including severe respiratory motion artifacts, incomplete coverage from the diaphragm to the lower margin of L5, (4) presence of macrovascular invasion or massive ascites, (5) co-existing malignancies other than HCC (Fig. 1).

Clinical characteristics and follow-up

Recorded clinical and pathological parameters included age, gender, etiology, body mass index (BMI), tumor size, presence of liver cirrhosis, history of antiviral therapy, white blood count (WBC), neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, platelet count (PLT), total bilirubin (TBiL), albumin, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage, Child-Pugh score, and surgical approach.

We defined early recurrence (ER) as tumor recurrence occurring within 2 years after surgery [15]. All included patients underwent regular postoperative surveillance according to the institutional follow-up protocol. Imaging follow-up included ultrasound, CT, and/or MRI. During the first two years after hepatectomy, imaging was performed every 3–6 months, followed by every 6–12 months thereafter. Recurrence was diagnosed based on characteristic imaging findings, such as hyperenhancement during the arterial phase and washout in the portal venous or delayed phase. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time interval between surgery and recurrence. Follow-up ended on November 2023. No patient received postoperative adjuvant anti-tumor therapy before documented recurrence or before completion of the early recurrence assessment period.

CT image acquisition

All patients underwent routine multiphase abdominal CT using a dual-source CT scanner (SOMATOM Definition Flash, Siemens, Germany). The CT technical parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 140 kV; tube current, 300 mA; slice thickness, 5 mm; and reconstruction interval, 1.5 mm. The scan ranged from the diaphragm to the superior margin of the pubic bone. An unenhanced CT scan was first obtained before contrast medium administration. After the unenhanced scan, Iohexol was administered intravenously through the median cubital vein at a dose adjusted according to body weight, generally 1.5 mL/kg, with a total volume of approximately 80–120 mL, at an injection rate of 3.0 mL/s. The arterial phase was triggered automatically when the CT value at the abdominal aorta reached 140 HU, followed by the portal venous and equilibrium phases at 60 and 180 s, respectively.

Image analysis

True unenhanced CT images obtained before contrast medium injection, with a reconstructed slice thickness of 1.5 mm, were imported into Mimics Medical 21.0 software for adipose tissue segmentation. The contrast-enhanced phases were not used for fat segmentation. Adipose tissue segmentation was automatically performed using predefined Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds of − 190 to − 30 HU to distinguish adipose tissue from other tissues. The display window setting was used only for visual inspection and did not affect the threshold-based segmentation. Before segmentation, all CT images were reviewed for image quality, and cases with severe respiratory motion artifacts or incomplete anatomical coverage were excluded.

For volumetric analysis, the abdominal region extending from the diaphragm to the lower margin of L5 was selected using the crop tool. Within this range, an initial fat mask was generated according to the predefined HU threshold. The split mask function was then used to separate subcutaneous fat from visceral fat. Subcutaneous fat was defined as adipose tissue located outside the abdominal wall musculature, whereas visceral fat was defined as adipose tissue located within the abdominal cavity. All segmented masks were checked slice by slice by an abdominal radiologist as part of the quality control process. Missed fat regions were manually added when necessary, and non-adipose tissues, including gastrointestinal contents and supradiaphragmatic pericardial fat, were manually removed during post-processing. The “Properties” function was then used to calculate visceral fat volume (VFV) and subcutaneous fat volume (SFV), as seen in Fig. 2.

For area-based analysis, the axial slices passing through the centers of the L3, L4, and L5 vertebral bodies were selected. A new task was created separately at each vertebral level, and the corresponding fat mask was intersected with the axial plane using the “Boolean operations modify” function. The “Properties” function was then used to calculate visceral fat area (VFA) and subcutaneous fat area (SFA) at the L3, L4, and L5 levels, respectively [16–18]; Height-adjusted area indices, including VFAI and SFAI, were calculated separately for the L3, L4, and L5 levels according to previously described methods. Volume-based indices, including VFV index (VFVI) and SFV index (SFVI), were also calculated according to previously described methods [19].

To assess measurement reproducibility, inter- and intra-observer agreement analyses were performed for the directly measured fat parameters, including VFA and SFA at the L3, L4, and L5 levels, as well as VFV and SFV. Two abdominal radiologists with 2 and 5 years of experience, respectively, independently performed fat segmentation and measurement according to the same protocol. Both radiologists were blinded to the patients’ clinical data and recurrence status. Interobserver agreement was evaluated by comparing the measurements obtained by the two radiologists. For intra-observer agreement, repeated measurements were performed 1 week later, with the reader blinded to the initial results. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for all fat parameters. After the reproducibility assessment, the measurements obtained by the senior radiologist were used for subsequent statistical analyses.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 22.0) and MedCalc (version 20.0). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess interobserver reliability between the two radiologists. ICC values were interpreted as follows: <0.40 indicated poor reproducibility, 0.40 to 0.59 fair reproducibility, 0.60 to 0.74 good reproducibility, and 0.75 to 1.00 excellent reproducibility. Continuous variables were compared using the t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, while categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the χ2 test. Variables with p < 0.10 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with a VIF > 5 indicating potential collinearity. Variables with potential collinearity were not entered simultaneously into the same model. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used to evaluate the predictive value of the independent risk factors for ER, comparison between AUC values were performed using the DeLong test.

Kaplan–Meier curves were used to compare RFS between groups during the entire follow-up period, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Univariable Cox regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with postoperative recurrence during the entire follow-up period. Variables with p-values < 0.10 in the univariable analysis, as well as clinically relevant covariates such as antiviral therapy, were entered into the multivariable Cox regression model. Collinearity was also considered during model construction. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 161 patients were included in the final cohort. The average age was 52 ± 10 years, and the majority were male (135 patients, 83.8%). Among the cohort, 68.9% (111/161) of patients had liver cirrhosis, and the median BMI was 22.9 (21.6, 25.2) kg/m². Among them, 90 patients (55.9%) experienced ER, the median time to early recurrence was 356 days (range, 29–731 days), while the remaining 71 patients (44.1%) did not experience ER, the median follow-up duration was 1119 days (range: 735–1764 days), as seen in Table 1.

Before further statistical analyses, measurement reproducibility was assessed. The inter- and intra-observer ICCs for CT-derived fat measurements ranged from 0.818 to 0.922 and from 0.737 to 0.925, respectively, indicating good to excellent reproducibility (Supplementary Table S1).

Selection and analysis of single-slice abdominal fat area parameters

To determine the representative lumbar level for single-slice fat area measurement, VFA measured at L3 and L5 was compared with that measured at L4. VFA at L3 and L5 showed strong correlations with L4-VFA (r = 0.937 and r = 0.859, respectively; both p < 0.001). ROC analysis showed that VFA at L3, L4, and L5 had comparable predictive performance for early recurrence, with AUCs of 0.701, 0.712, and 0.685, respectively. Although L4-VFA showed the highest AUC numerically, the differences between L4 and L3 or L5 were not statistically significant according to the DeLong test. Therefore, L4-VFA was retained as the representative single-slice fat area parameter for subsequent analyses.

The univariate analysis showed significant differences in BMI, VFA, and VFAI between the ER and non-ER groups. After incorporating antiviral therapy into the multivariable logistic regression model, higher VFA remained independently associated with an increased risk of ER (OR: 3.381, 95% CI: 1.663–6.874, p < 0.001). Antiviral therapy was also independently associated with a lower risk of ER (OR: 0.493, 95% CI: 0.247–0.985, p = 0.045), as shown in Table 2.

Comparison of the predictive performance of VFA and VFV for ER

The results showed that the AUC of VFA was 0.712 (95% CI: 0.635–0.780), with a sensitivity of 73.3% and a specificity of 66.2%. The AUC of VFV was 0.730 (95% CI: 0.655–0.797), with a sensitivity of 74.4% and a specificity of 66.2%. Both VFA and VFV demonstrated similar predictive value for ER of HCC, as the difference between their AUCs was not statistically significant (p = 0.307), as seen in Fig. 3.

Comparison of the predictive efficacy of Visceral Fat Area (VFA) and visceral fat volume (VFV) on the early recurrence of hepatocellular carcinoma. The AUC of VFA was 0.712, and the AUC of VFV was 0.730, with similar predictive value between the two measurement methods

The combined area-based model incorporating VFA and antiviral therapy yielded an AUC of 0.719 (95% CI: 0.641–0.797), with a sensitivity of 61.1% and a specificity of 81.7%, showing a predictive performance comparable to that of VFA alone (AUC: 0.712; DeLong test, p = 0.720). Therefore, considering its comparable predictive performance and greater simplicity, VFA alone was retained for subsequent subgroup analysis.

Based on the optimal cutoff value of VFA determined by ROC analysis, patients were divided into a high-risk group and a low-risk group. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that the high-risk group had significantly shorter RFS than the low-risk group (log-rank p < 0.0001; Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis of VFA according to tumor size

VFA was more easily acquired on CT compared to VFV, and therefore, VFA was chosen for further analysis. To further explore the impact of VFA on the prognosis of HCC with different tumor sizes and its predictive value for ER, a subgroup analysis was conducted involving 161 patients. According to tumor size, the patients were divided into two groups: group A [(d) ≤ 3 cm] and group B [3 cm < (d) ≤ 5 cm]. In group A, the AUC for VFA was 0.796 (95% CI: 0.692 ∼ 0.877), with a sensitivity of 88.9% and a specificity of 66.7%. In group B, the AUC for VFA was 0.636 (95% CI: 0.520 ∼ 0.740), with a sensitivity of 60.0% and a specificity of 71.4%. The difference in AUC values between the two groups was statistically significant (p = 0.049) (Fig. 5). Discussion

Our study showed that both VFA and VFV were independently associated with ER after HCC resection and demonstrated comparable, moderate predictive performance. Obesity is a known risk factor for HCC development [20]. Recent studies have revealed that differences in body composition may play a crucial role in HCC prognosis. Notably, visceral fat, which is closely associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome, has been reported to be associated with survival outcomes [21]. Therefore, CT-derived visceral fat parameters may help estimate the risk of postoperative recurrence and may provide additional information for postoperative surveillance planning. The additional time-to-event analyses of RFS during the entire follow-up period further supported these findings. Both VFA and VFV remained significantly associated with postoperative recurrence in Cox regression models, and patients with high VFA showed significantly shorter recurrence-free survival on Kaplan–Meier analysis. These results suggest that visceral adiposity has potential prognostic value for postoperative recurrence.

Several studies have reported associations between visceral fat parameters and ER in patients with HCC. Wu ZQ et al. found that VFAI served as an independent risk factor for ER, with an AUC of 0.802, and the AUC value of VFAI in the external validation group was 0.854. Additionally, Imai et al. also confirmed that increased visceral fat mass index (VFMI) was significantly associated with ER of HCC [20]. However, these studies primarily focused on single visceral fat parameters and did not investigate or compare the performance of VFA and VFV in predicting ER of HCC. Our study adds to the existing evidence by directly comparing VFA and VFV for predicting ER in patients with HCC. Previous studies have shown that VFV and VFA have similar impacts on colorectal cancer survival [22]. Consistent with this finding, our results demonstrated that VFV and VFA showed comparable predictive performance for ER after HCC resection. Importantly, these findings suggest that VFA measured at the mid-L4 vertebral level may serve as a practical alternative to VFV in clinical settings, which is more labor-intensive to measure due to the need for 3D reconstruction and advanced imaging software. Furthermore, VFA can be quickly and easily obtained from a single cross-sectional CT image, making it a more practical option for routine clinical use. Given its simpler measurement process and comparable predictive performance, VFA may be more feasible for routine clinical use.

Our findings suggest that visceral adiposity is associated with ER after HCC resection, although the underlying biological mechanisms were not directly investigated in this study. Several possible pathways may partly explain this association. First, increased visceral fat has been linked to a chronic inflammatory state, with elevated inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-6, and inflammation-based markers [23], which may contribute to a tumor-promoting hepatic microenvironment. Because visceral adipose tissue drains through the portal circulation, inflammatory signals derived from visceral fat may have a direct impact on the liver [24–28]. Second, visceral adiposity is closely related to insulin resistance. Hyperinsulinemia may promote tumor cell proliferation, vascular injury, and angiogenesis-related signaling, thereby facilitating tumor progression and recurrence [20, 29, 30]. Third, adipokine imbalance may also be involved. Increased leptin and decreased adiponectin have been reported to influence immune regulation and tumor-related signaling pathways [31]. Research has demonstrated that these adipokines directly impact tumor cells. Adiponectin counteracts the tumor-promoting effects of leptin by inhibiting the phosphorylation of Stat 3 and Akt, whereas leptin facilitates tumor cell proliferation and growth by engaging multiple signaling pathways [32]. However, these mechanisms remain speculative in the context of the present study because related biomarkers were not measured.

Our study also found that VFA showed better predictive performance for ER in group A [(d) ≤3 cm] than in group B [3 cm < (d) ≤5 cm] (p< 0.05). One possible explanation is that, in larger tumors, tumor-related pathological factors such as microvascular invasion may have a stronger influence on recurrence, thereby weakening the relative contribution of VFA. Cong et al. [33] observed that tumors ≤ 3 cm often exhibit diploid DNA and more favorable biological behavior, facilitating their surgical resection and enhancing long-term postoperative survival. As HCC increases beyond 2–3 cm, moderately differentiated components become more common, whereas well-differentiated components are less frequently observed, and are rarely observed in tumors ≥ 3 cm [34]. Studies have also shown that as tumor size increases, so does the incidence of MVI—rising from approximately 25% in tumors < 3 cm to 63% in tumors 5–6.5 cm [35]. While the exact mechanisms linking tumor size and MVI remain unclear, previous studies have shown that larger tumor diameter is associated with a higher likelihood of MVI, suggesting that larger tumors may have a greater tendency toward vascular invasion and subsequent ER. In addition, tumor-associated lymphatic vessel density has been reported to be associated with poorer postoperative prognosis in hepatobiliary cancers, further supporting the potential role of vessel-related tumor microenvironmental factors in recurrence [36].

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, which may have introduced selection bias. In addition, no external validation cohort was available, and therefore the generalizability of the predictive performance and cutoff values requires further confirmation in larger, multicenter cohorts. Second, some pathological factors closely associated with ER, such as microvascular invasion, tumor differentiation, and resection margin status, were not fully incorporated into the analysis. Third, some metabolic and etiological factors, including detailed antiviral treatment status, were not comprehensively evaluated. Although antiviral therapy was included in the multivariable analysis, treatment intensity and virological response could not be fully assessed. Because most patients had HBV-related HCC, the applicability of our findings to other etiologies remains uncertain. Finally, inflammatory cytokines, insulin resistance indicators, adipokine levels, and CT attenuation characteristics of abdominal adipose tissue were not assessed. Therefore, the biological mechanisms linking visceral adiposity and ER require further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that VFA and VFV showed similar predictive performance for ER after HCC resection, and VFA showed a higher AUC in patients with tumor diameter ≤ 3 cm in this cohort. These findings suggest that CT-derived VFA may serve as a practical imaging parameter for postoperative risk stratification in patients with single HCC ≤5 cm. Future studies may explore whether combining VFA with pathological, clinical, or molecular biomarkers can improve individualized prediction of ER.
