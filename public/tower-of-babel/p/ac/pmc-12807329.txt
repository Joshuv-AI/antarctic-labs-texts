Genomic selection optimization in blueberry: Data‐driven methods for marker and training population design
Authors: Paul Adunola, Luis Felipe V Ferrão, Juliana Benevenuto, Camila F Azevedo, Patricio R Munoz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic prediction is a modern approach that uses genome‐wide markers to predict the genetic merit of unphenotyped individuals. With the potential to reduce the breeding cycles and increase the selection accuracy, this tool has been designed to rank genotypes and maximize genetic gains. Despite this importance, its practical implementation in breeding programs requires critical allocation of resources for its application in a predictive framework. In this study, we integrated genetic and data‐driven methods to allocate resources for phenotyping and genotyping tailored to genomic prediction. To this end, we used a historical blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosun L.) breeding dataset containing more than 3000 individuals, genotyped using probe‐based target sequencing and phenotyped for three fruit quality traits over several years. Our contribution in this study is threefold: (i) for the genotyping resource allocation, the use of genetic data‐driven methods to select an optimal set of markers slightly improved prediction results for all the traits; (ii) for the long‐term implication, we carried out a simulation study and emphasized that data‐driven method results in a slight improvement in genetic gain over 30 cycles than random marker sampling; and (iii) for the phenotyping resource allocation, we compared different optimization algorithms to select training population, showing that it can be leveraged to increase predictive performances. Altogether, we provided a data‐oriented decision‐making approach for breeders by demonstrating that critical breeding decisions associated with resource allocation for genomic prediction can be tackled through a combination of statistics and genetic methods.

INTRODUCTION

Genomic selection (GS) is a form of marker‐assisted selection that uses genome‐wide markers to estimate the genetic merit of unphenotyped individuals (Meuwissen et al., 2001). In GS, a training population (TRS) composed of a set of individuals within the breeding program is utilized to calibrate the relationship between genetic markers and a trait of interest. This methodology became one of the most important tools for guiding plant and animal breeders’ decisions in the 21st century (Hickey et al., 2017). In plant breeding programs, GS can assist with the selection of superior individuals for cultivar development and population improvement before phenotyping, resulting in an accelerated breeding cycle. Despite its potential for maximizing genetic gains and shortening breeding cycles, contemporaneous breeding programs face the challenge of finding a reasonable balance of resource allocation on phenotyping and genotyping, without compromising the predictive abilities of target traits. To address this challenge, breeders can rely on genetic data‐driven methods to maximize the predictive ability at a minimum cost. Optimizations can be performed at the population level by defining the number and type of samples to be phenotyped in the training set, and at the genotyping level by selecting molecular markers with good genome‐wide coverage and accuracy.

Aided by the advancements in sequencing technologies, genome‐wide markers are now available for most crop species. However, in the past decade, several studies have shown that the use of high density molecular markers in GS models is not a guarantee of larger predictive abilities (Norman et al., 2018). This fact opens possibilities for reducing costs associated with genotyping and, therefore, breeding programs have used different approaches to select molecular markers. In a more straightforward form, breeders have relied on a random sampling of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across the genome, with solid results for predictions (De Bem Oliveira et al., 2020; Kriaridou et al., 2020). More formally, other authors have relied on data‐driven methods in which molecular markers are ranked based on population metrics. The ranking of markers based on the minor allele frequency (MAF) is a key example, and it has been reported as a valid alternative to select more common genetic variants (Kainer et al., 2018). Equidistant markers throughout the chromosomes can also be selected to maximize the probability of having markers and quantitative trait locus (QTL) in linkage disequilibrium (LD) (Hemingway et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2015). Depending on the genetic variation in a population, LD pruning is utilized to reduce marker density by removing SNPs in high LD on a locus (Calus & Vandenplas, 2018). Other approaches are more biologically oriented and are grounded on prior knowledge of putative QTL and parent–offspring mismatch rate (Ballesta et al., 2020; Subedi et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2013). Despite the diversity of methods reported in the literature, there are few reports using a systematic method where genetic and data‐driven methods are combined in a unified framework for selecting markers, especially in polyploid species.

Another key aspect of resource allocation for GS implementation is the phenotyping of the TRS. Even with the recent progress in artificial intelligence, computer vision, and phenomics, the collection of accurate phenotypes in large populations and open fields is still costly. To achieve this, breeders have designed TRSs with the minimum number of individuals to be phenotyped that, at the same time, would maximize predictability. To aid in this task, recent studies have used statistical algorithms to leverage the relationship between the target and TRS and, thereby, increase the predictive performance (Lorenz & Nice, 2017; Rincent et al., 2012). Different methods have been proposed and implemented, including choosing training individuals that (i) capture the maximum average genetic relationship or genetic distance between training sets (Avg_GRM, D‐optimal; Maxmin) (Akdemir et al., 2021; Atanda et al., 2021); (ii) minimize the prediction error variance of the genetic values; (iii) maximize mean coefficient of determination (CDmean) (Akdemir et al., 2021; Rincent et al., 2012), and (iv) maximize genetic diversity by stratified random sampling across clusters or genetic groups (Isidro et al., 2015). Overall, these methods are proposing optimal management of genetic diversity, within the bounds of practical constraints, to improve predictive abilities.

Blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum L. and hybrids) is one of the fruit crops that has recently benefited from the routine implementation of GS (Ferrão et al., 2021). The high content and diversity of polyphenolic compounds presented in blueberry have promoted consumer awareness of its health benefits and driven a worldwide increase in planting and consumption (Kalt et al., 2020). Despite this success, the use of traditional phenotypic recurrent selection for blueberry breeding is marred with slow genetic progress and can take up to 15 years before cultivar release. This is a result of the autopolyploid nature of the crop, along with its long juvenile phase (2–3 years), multiyear evaluations, large experimental areas, and sensitivity to inbreeding depression. To circumvent this issue, the blueberry program at the University of Florida (UF) has integrated genomic‐assisted methods in the breeding pipeline (Ferrão et al., 2021). However, the use of GS is still structured on the use of high‐density markers and large training set populations, which requires large investments for genotyping and phenotyping.

Motivated by the potential to guide future resource allocation in plant breeding programs, we proposed the integration of data‐driven and genetic‐based methods for optimizing GS by demonstrating its application in blueberry. To this end, we systematically selected low–mid density molecular markers using a combination of probe filtering criteria, and, in the sequence, designed a training set population for genomic prediction. Therefore, our objective in this study is fourfold: (i) combine statistical and genetic filtering criteria to select an informative set of low–mid molecular markers for genomic prediction, (ii) assess the impact of the genetic marker selection approach on the genetic gain of traits with different architectures across multiple simulated breeding cycles, and (iii) compare different genetic data‐driven optimization algorithms to select the TRS that maximizes the predictive performance. Altogether, we demonstrated that both the number of markers and population size can be optimized to leverage predictive accuracies with reduced costs, optimizing the resource allocation in practical breeding programs. The pipeline can potentially be applied in other crop species for which similar breeding data have been collected to guide future decisions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Phenotypic data

The southern highbush blueberry populations used in this research were generated by the breeding program at the UF. As part of the recurrent selection strategy, the UF blueberry breeding program annually makes up to 200 crosses, after combining phenotypic, pedigree, and molecular information for yield, fruit quality, flavor, disease/pest resistance, and earliness of seasonal production. From crosses to a cultivar, a four‐stage selection approach, which takes 12–15 years, is used. First, from ∼200 crosses, we planted ∼20,000 progenies in high‐density nurseries. The first evaluation cycle is carried out on a 1‐year‐old seedling (Stage I), where approximately 10% of seedlings pass to the second stage (Stage II). In the second year, with more fruits available for evaluation from the individuals advanced to stage II, a new selection (10% of selection intensity) is performed (Table 1). Next, in stage III, the selected genotypes are clonally propagated and evaluated in commercial field conditions, where 10% of the most outstanding genotypes are selected to compose the last selection stage. At Stage IV, phenotypic evaluations are performed in commercial farms across the State of Florida using clonal field plots. Genotypes that perform well across years and locations are released as cultivars. The GS implementation takes place in Stage II, where all plants in this stage are genotyped. The best individuals are selected based on their genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) and used as parents in a new breeding cycle. More details about the breeding stages are presented in Table 1 and the integrated used of recurrent selection and molecular breeding is described by Ferrão et al. (2020).

Herein, we used four TRSs for which phenotypic and genotypic data were systematically collected to be used for GS. The first population (TRS_II14‐15) is a Stage II population composed of 1781 genotypes derived from 117 crosses. Phenotypes and genotypes were collected in 2014 and 2015, as part of the first‐generation dataset used for calibrating our GS models at UF. More details about this dataset are presented by Ferrão et al. (2018) and De Bem Oliveira et al. (2019). The second population (TRS_II2021) is also Stage II with 1081 individuals derived from 256 crosses, with data collected in 2021. Both TRS populations are part of the efforts of the UF Breeding Program to genotyping a more diverse population every 6 years to update our predictive model. The other two datasets are referred to as TRS_hist and consist of phenotypic data collected from 2012 to 2022. In total, the TRS_hist contains 458 individuals, as part of the advanced selection stages of the UF blueberry breeding program (Stages III and IV), for which we have several years of phenotypic data collected (historical dataset). To check the relevance of using large historical data for predictions, we further divided this population into two subsets: (1) TRS_hist12‐21, for which all the phenotypic data were collected from 2012 to 2021, and (2) TRS_hist2022, which contains only the most recent phenotypic data collected in 2022. A summary of the different populations is presented in Table 1.

Phenotypic data for the four populations comprise fruit firmness, soluble solids (Brix), and total titratable acids (TTA). All traits were collected from fresh ripe berries in their respective seasons. The procedure for the phenotypic evaluation is described by Ferrão et al. (2018). Briefly, firmness was measured with Firm‐Tech II and FruitFirm 1000 (BioWorks Inc.). Brix content and TTA were measured from the berry juice using a digital pocket refractometer (Atago USA, Inc.) and an automatic titrator using NaOH (Mettler‐Toldeo, Inc.), respectively. Genotyping

The genotyping of all individuals was carried out with 10,000 biotinylated probes of 120 mer using CaptureSeq technology at RaPiD Genomics, as previously described by Benevenuto et al. (2019). Briefly, raw reads were trimmed and aligned against the 12 largest homologous sets. Draper genome (Colle et al., 2019), and probes were used as targets for SNP calling. Loci were filtered by applying the following criteria: minimum mapping quality of 20; only biallelic locus; maximum missing data of 50%; MAF of 1%; mean depth of coverage of 40; and minimum genotype frequency of 0.01. As blueberry is an autotetraploid species (2n = 4X = 48), we used the updog R package to call the allele dosages based on the read counts (Gerard et al., 2018). After dosage calling, SNPs were further filtered by the quality metrics outputted from the updog package, including allelic bias, overdispersion, sequencing error, and outlying observations. With that, a total of 59,990 SNP markers were obtained. Hereafter, further marker selection was performed using probe analyses for genomic prediction.

Optimization steps

To optimize the resource allocation for GS at the UF Blueberry Breeding Program, we structured the process into two layers. First, at the genotyping level, we tested different filtering criteria to select a panel of probes and markers that maximize the accuracy of prediction results. Second, from the phenotyping standpoint, we addressed the question of how to select the TRS by testing different algorithms and methods.

In the first optimization layer, we established filtering steps to select probes and SNP markers for GS. The blueberry genotyping was performed using the Capture‐Seq methodology, which is based on next‐generation sequencing (NGS) of targeted key regions in the genome. This “target enrichment” method relies on oligonucleotide hybridization probes to “capture” the DNA fragments containing regions of interest. Hence, the number and location of probes directly impact the marker discovery and, ultimately, the genotyping cost. Depending on the genomic regions assessed, we have empirically observed that probes have different quality parameters, harboring a different number of SNPs, or are highly prone to false positive SNP calls. In this research, we started with a panel of 10,000 probes. Of those, we detected SNPs in 9508 probes. In a previous study, after a random sampling of the probes across the genome, De Bem Oliveira et al. (2019) suggested that a number between 3000 and 5000 would be enough for genome prediction in blueberries. Premised on this, 2500 and 5000 probes were used as a reference for probe selection. However, instead of random sampling, we proposed a systematic selection using genetic and data‐driven methods. Below, we provided the detailed criteria used for selecting probes and their intrinsic SNP markers:

SNP distortion: our first criterion was based on the genetic segregation basis. To achieve this, we used a large biparental family described by Cappai et al. (2020). The study was originally designed to build a high‐resolution linkage map in blueberry using 6000 custom‐designed biotinylated probes. After creating the linkage map, we noticed that some probes were enriched by markers with a large number of distorted SNPs. Segregation distortion was computed using the chi‐squared method, based on deviation from expected allelic frequency when biparental crosses are performed in autotetraploid species. In a total of 21,513 SNPs, 10,221 SNPs from 2264 probes were removed for SNP distortion. These set of probes were excluded from the 9508 probes set considered.

Pedigree correlation (r 2): When molecular and pedigree data are recorded, a kinship based on identity by state (IBS) and realized kinship based on identity by descent methods can be paired and compared. To this end, pedigree (A matrix) and genomic (G matrix) relationship matrices were both constructed using the UF genealogical and SNP markers per probe for the individuals, respectively. Subsequently, the off‐diagonal values from the G matrix are regressed against values observed in the A matrix resulting in a formal metric (r 2) to measure similarities. Since the pedigree records at the UF breeding program are deep and well‐curated—with a pedigree file with more than 15000 individuals recorded since 1908—we hypothesized that r 2 coefficients that are high indicate a more similar relationship between genomic and pedigree relationships, which could be an important genetic parameter for ranking the probes.

After computing and ranking all probes based on the metrics previously described, we used the selection index (SI) to pinpoint the most informative probes. For the SI, all seven criteria had the same weight, and we used an agnostic method to rank the probes. Importantly, we parametrize our ranking in a way that small values on the ranking indicate desirable attributes. Thereafter, we summed out the ranking and selected the top 500, 1000, 2500, and 5000 probes for downstream analyses. This is analogous to the Mulamba and Mock (1978) index, commonly reported in plant breeding for multi‐trait selection. Following the probe selection analyses, we tested strategies to update the TRS to reduce costs and leverage predictive abilities. To achieve this, we mimicked a real scenario as described in the following. Every year, as part of the UF Blueberry Breeding Program, around 2000 plants from Stages II are genotyped and the GEBVs are predicted solely based on genomic data. Phenotyping this entire population would be costly, time‐ consuming, and pointless in a scenario of GS application. However, phenotyping a small portion of this population (10%–20%) may be a feasible task, if the predictive abilities increase by doing so. To test it, breeders need to know how many individuals and what is the best strategy to select materials for phenotyping.

To address these points, phenotypic and genotypic data from TRS_II14‐15 and TRS_hist12‐21 populations were used as training information, representing historical data to calibrate the genomic prediction model. Additionally, we used our most recent diverse breeding population (TRS2021)—which has been phenotyped and genotyped—and tested three systematic approaches to guide our decisions on how to select individuals to compose the TRS. First, we selected materials solely based on pedigree information. Thus, with 256 families and 1081 individuals represented in the TRS_II2021, we defined the set of individuals used to update the training set by sampling (i) some families, but all individuals within the family and (ii) across families, a few individuals across all families. Second, we used a genetic data‐driven method and tested combinatorial optimization algorithms. For example, we tested D‐optimal (Akdemir et al., 2021; Heredia‐Langner et al., 2003), Maxmin distance (Akdemir et al., 2021), and CDmean (Laloë, 1993; Rincent et al., 2012). Assuming that these algorithms require a previous understanding of the diversity of the population, they were tested under the genomic (G matrix) assumptions. CDmean was also tested employing pedigree (A matrix) assumption as CDpmean. Implementation of all the combinatorial optimization algorithms was executed with the TrainSel version 2.0 R package (Akdemir et al., 2021). Finally, as our benchmark, we performed random sampling and checked predictive abilities. For a fair comparison, we kept the same sample size across all scenarios and trained the GS models using 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 individuals. The choice of values ranging between 100 and 500 is due to the feasibility of the phenotyping of this number of individuals. A summary of the four‐stage breeding program and the methods used to select the individuals is presented in Figure 1b.

Genomic best linear unbiased prediction was used for genomic prediction of blueberry fruit quality trait performances, written in a matrix notation as y=μ+Zu+e, where y is a vector of BLUEs for a particular trait; μ is the overall mean; Z is an incidence matrix linking observations in the vector y to their respective breeding value in the vector u. Normality was assumed for the genetic and residual effects, where u∼MVN(0,Gσu2) and e∼MVN(0,Iσe2). For the residual, I is an identity matrix; while σu2 and σe2 are the genetic and the residual variances, respectively. Models were fitted using the rrBLUP version 4.6.1 R package (Endelman, 2011).

Cross‐validation

For all the optimization layers defined, the prediction accuracies of GS models were assessed using a 10‐fold cross‐validation. For this, all individuals were randomly divided into 10 subsets, where nine were used as the training set and the remaining as the validation set. The procedure was iterated until the 10 subsets were used as the validation set.

When training set populations were tested, we designed two levels of cross‐validation. First, we used a single population (TRS2021) by selecting 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 individuals to compose the training set, while the remaining 981, 881, 781, 681, and 581 individuals were used as a validation set, respectively. In a second and more realistic scenario of cross‐validation, we investigated the benefits of including external data for predicting the same set of individuals in the TRS2021. For example, we calibrated the GS models using the TRS14‐15 and TRShist, both historical datasets were included for GS in the Blueberry Breeding Program. The two underlying questions are as follows: (i) What are the predictive performances of GS models trained using historical data? (ii) What is the balance between improving the training size by including more phenotypes from past years, at the cost of increasing the genetic dissimilarity between training and target populations?

Stochastic‐oriented simulation

We investigated the long‐term impact of reducing the number of molecular markers in GS by simulating the UF Blueberry Breeding Program using the AlphaSimR package (Faux et al., 2016). To this end, crossings and selections that resemble our breeding program in future years were simulated. We referred to this as an oriented‐based approach, since we used our current populations as founders and reproduced subsequent cycles of recombination and selection using the software. Thus, we initiated our simulation process by providing a polyploid dosage matrix from real individuals. As we do not have access to the haplotype phases—an important factor when creating populations using the AlphaSim R package—we simulated different haplotype phases for our current dataset. Future selections and recombination were driven by using GEBVs, with markers selected under three different scenarios: (i) the entire set of markers (∼50,000 SNP markers), (ii) using the described genetic data‐driven method, and (iii) random sampling. For the genetic data‐driven method, we used all markers from the filtered 2500 probes, which contain approximately 20,000 SNP markers. A similar size of markers was randomly sampled for comparison.

For simulation, we carried out the following pipeline. First, the real genotypic score of a core population with 1081 individuals was selected. We simulated 30 consecutive breeding cycles, by mimicking the recurrent selection UF breeding program where 2000 new individuals, from 50 crosses, are generated every breeding cycle. After using a selection intensity of 10%, the top 200 individuals are selected via GEBV and used as parents in the next generation (Ferrão et al., 2021). We simulated traits representative of the blueberry breeding program, considering three genetic architectures: a trait controlled by 10 QTLs and heritability of 0.80 (representing flavor‐related traits, in particular, volatile organic acids); 50 QTLs and heritability of 0.50 (representing some fruit quality traits, including firmness and size); and a complex trait controlled by 100 QTLs and heritability of 0.30 (yield estimates). Across the different generations, the selection progress was measured based on the parent mean. Thus, we averaged the results from 50 replicates, with each replicate involving an evaluation phase that simulated future breeding using different analysis strategies.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Resources allocation for GS implementation in blueberry has been investigated from different angles, including tests of different statistical methods (Amadeu et al., 2020; De Bem Oliveira et al., 2019; Zingaretti et al., 2020), selection of markers using haplotype‐phased genome information and random sampling (Benevenuto et al., 2019; De Bem Oliveira et al., 2020), and inclusion of prior biological information for prediction (Ferrão et al., 2020). Herein, we provided a data‐driven pipeline to optimize resources related to phenotyping and genotyping that can also be applied to other crops.

Descriptive analysis and population diversity

The descriptive analysis of phenotypic characteristics of the four blueberry populations in Table 2 shows higher mean brix content for individuals in the early‐stage populations (11.56%–11.73%) than advanced populations (10.73%–11.01%). Conversely, populations in advanced stages recorded higher firmness (253.10–258.62 gmm−1) and TTA (0.46% – 0.53%) than early‐stage populations. These results are expected given the fact that a balance between sweetness and acidity in fruits is necessary for enhanced food flavor and meeting the sensory preferences of diverse consumers. Additionally, selecting firmer fruits into advanced stages is imperative for postharvest handling, shipping over a long distance, and longer shelf‐life. For all traits, the empirical distribution was reasonably symmetric (Figure S1). TRS_hist22 had the lowest standard deviation for brix (0.39%) and firmness (10.20 gmm−1) and the highest variance for TTA (0.13%) compared to other populations. Narrow‐sense heritability estimate (h 2) ranged from moderate (0.27) to high (0.71) across traits, populations, and stages. Note that h 2 of a trait describes how much genetic variance of a population can be passed to the next generation.

The values reported for all traits fall within the range reported for blueberry fruit quality traits from previous studies (Amadeu et al., 2020; De Bem Oliveira et al., 2020; Ferrão et al., 2018). On average, all populations presented similar trends and phenotypic performance, likely due to their close genetic relationship. To confirm this, we investigated the genetic diversity in advanced (Stages III and IV) and early (Stages II) breeding stages. We did not observe any clear subpopulation structure across the blueberry populations (Figures S2 and S3). The subdivision of the individuals in each population was quite consistent with the breeding period (2012–2021), while advanced populations showed genetic admixture across early populations. The genetic admixture from advanced (TRS_hist) and early (TRS_II12‐21 and TRS_II2021) stages is expected, given that advanced plant materials are usually used as parents in new breeding cycles.

Genetic and data‐driven approaches guided the selection of targets for genotyping

When checking the probe metrics, we first observed large variability for filtering metrics (Figure 2a). For example, the number of SNPs per probe ranged from 1 to 26, while some probes had up to 34% missing SNPs (Figure 2a). MAF per probe ranged from 0.001 to 0.489 with an estimated mean of 0.059 across probes. Generally, markers with poor behavior were not concentrated in specific genomic regions, a fact that would raise the hypothesis of genome artifacts or structural variations (Figure S4). Also, this finding reinforces our primary premise that different filtering criteria are complementary and required to achieve an optimal subset of markers (Wang et al., 2013; Wellmann et al., 2013). Based on the rankings of the filtering criteria, a comparison of overlapping probes (count or proportion) with the selected top 2500 and 5000 probes revealed a moderate‐to‐low similarity across the filtering criteria (Figure 2b and Figure S5). These results are not surprising given the availability of high‐quality reference genome for blueberry.

The top k selected probes showed good prediction ability at low density

After selecting the highest ranking 2500 and 5000 probes averaged across metrics to represent low–mid density probe panels and used to fit genomic prediction models, we observed that this led to a marginal increase in predictive abilities compared to random selection methods (Table 3 and Figure S6). In addition, the low‐to‐mid panel of probes achieved similar prediction values as using the original total probe set. By ranking the top 10% of individuals based on GEBVs for all probes and selecting 2500 and 5000 probes, we noticed >90% overlap between individuals selected using the subset of probes and all probes (Figure S6a and S6b). The opportunity here is the potential combination of these selection criteria to sample informative probes for developing low‐density genotyping platforms for resource optimization.

Predictive ability was also investigated considering multiple SNP filtering metrics, and different number of probes. Some interesting trends can be highlighted when each metric is analyzed individually (Figure 3a). All filtering parameters showed a similar tendency: higher accuracy values were achieved when more probes were used. Additionally, by comparing the different metrics, the use of a genetic‐based method (R 2 between pedigree and genomic relationship matrices) showed the highest predictive accuracy for brix (500 and 1000 probes), firmness (1000 probes), and TTA (500 and 1000 probes). Furthermore, repeatability also showed better results for brix (2500 probes) and TTA (5000 probes), which highlights the relevance of addressing the probe selection experimentally by using independent repetitions. Overall, we observed a good compromise between number of probes and predictive ability when 2500 and 5000 probes were selected (Figure 3a). We further investigated the distribution of the selected probes in the blueberry genome compared to the full set of probes. In both 2500 and 5000 subsets, probes were well spread across the genome (Figure 3b), made up of ∼22,000 SNPs and ∼37,000 SNPs, respectively. Thus, all the subsequent genomic analyses were carried out using both subsets.

Reduction of SNP density without compromising the predictive ability was reported in blueberry (De Bem Oliveira et al., 2019) and other crops (DoVale et al., 2021; Hemingway et al., 2021; Spindel et al., 2015). However, most studies select probes/SNPs over the genome by random sampling. Herein, the use of data methods slightly performed better than random selection methods. Similarly, Wang et al. (2013), Wellmann et al. (2013), and Kainer et al. (2018) have also combined several marker filtering parameters to reduce marker density without negative effects on the predictive ability. In the scenario of resource allocations, the proposed number of probes (2500) represents a reduction of ∼90% of the original number of probes designed (31,063) when our molecular breeding program was originally structured (De Bem Oliveira et al., 2019).

Simulating the long‐term impact of reducing the number of markers

In this study, we conducted stochastic‐oriented simulations to evaluate GS over a 30‐cycle period, by testing the entire dataset (∼60,000 markers), probes randomly sampled across the genome (∼20,000 markers), and probes selected via data‐driven methods (∼20,000 markers). When comparing the three selection criteria, the use of a genetic data‐driven approach surpassed the performance of random sampling by exhibiting larger mean genetic values when selecting the top 10% of individuals over the cycles (Figure 4). This superiority could be attributed to capturing more informative SNPs during the filtering steps. According to Kainer et al. (2018), using broad data and biological knowledge to select a few SNPs can greatly boost accuracy similar to genome‐wide SNP predictions. Overall, this finding mirrors the real analyses reported in this study, where the genetic data‐driven method for selecting markers performed slightly better than random marker selection and similar to the use of all markers (Table 3).

Stochastic‐oriented simulations. Blueberry breeding program was simulated over 30 cycles using three selection criteria to compute the genomic estimated breeding values: full dataset, selected ∼20,000 markers using data‐driven methods, and sampling ∼20,000 markers randomly in the genome. In every cycle, the data‐driven and random methods were compared by checking the parent mean to the full dataset. QTL, quantitative trait locus.

Another important result highlighted by the simulation (Figure 4) is the dependence between selection gains and genetic architecture. For all simulated traits, in the first 5–10 years of breeding cycles, the genetic gains did not differ across the SNP densities. After the first simulated decade, and without considering new introgressions, we hypothesize that some alleles are fixed in the population and the use of a reduced number of markers (via random or genetic data‐driven methods) has the risk of more likely not to sample markers linked to the causal marker. A similar plateau was created by losses on genetic variance and inbreeding in the population, as also reported in a simulation study by Jannink (2010).

Based on these findings, breeding programs should carefully consider decisions on the marker density. The use of low‐density markers may result in a similar performance to genome‐wide markers, but a plateau in genetic gain can be observed after a few breeding cycles (Figure 4). Herein, we draw attention to the use of stochastic‐oriented simulations to anticipate it. When aiming to implement GS, it is important to maintain effective population size, genetic diversity, and minimize inbreeding across generations to achieve long‐term genetic gain (Bijma, 2012; De Rezende Neves et al., 2018; Jannink, 2010). Some studies have also emphasized the importance of providing more weight to low‐frequency favorable alleles at the beginning of the selection process to balance long‐term and short‐term responses (de Rezende Neves et al., 2018; Jannink, 2010; Sun & VanRaden, 2014). Herein, after using stochastic‐oriented simulations and considering our current population dynamic, we argue on the importance of recalibrating the GS models every 5–10 years. To this end, a more diverse and representative group of individuals should be used, a fact that can prevent genetic drift, accommodate new alleles, improve selection accuracies, and adapt our models to novel climatic and environmental conditions.

Optimizing genomic prediction by designing training sets

To design an optimal training set for GS, we tested a combination of pedigree and data‐driven methods. To this end, all data prior to 2021 were included in our training set, while the phenotypic and genotypic data collected at the TRS_II2021 were considered our target population. This subdivision sought to mimic a real application, where all previous phenotypic data are used to calibrate the models and predict the genetic merits of future genotypes. A central difference here is that our target population (TRS_II2021) was also phenotyped. With that in mind, we addressed common questions underlying the breeder's decision, including (i) what is the real impact of using historical data to predict new populations?; (ii) when feasible, what is the relevance of including a small portion of the target population into the training set?; and finally (iii) how to select potential individuals from the target population to be part of the calibration?

Sampling of individuals from target population sets can be performed in several ways. Herein, we used a combination of pedigree and genetic data‐driven methods and tested six different methods (Figure 5a). Answering the questions about the impact of using historical data for prediction, we first focused on using models trained in 2014 and 2015 (TRS_II14‐15) to predict a population evaluated in 2021 (TRS_II2021). Positive results were observed, with the models presenting low (0.15) to moderate (0.40) predictive ability across traits (Figure 5b—historical scenario). For example, when the theoretical bounder limits were computed using “historical” data, prediction accuracy was lower by 22%, 42%, and 56% for firmness, TTA, and brix, respectively, compared to “all” data. This is expected because of the decrease in relatedness between the historical data and the new population (Werner et al., 2020; Wolc et al., 2015). This decrease in prediction accuracy is likely to be attributable to changes in the LD patterns between markers and QTLs among populations, which can be influenced by selection, genetic recombination, and genetic drift (Rutkoski et al., 2015).

Regarding the relevance of including a small portion of the target population in the training set, small increments in the population size increased the predictive abilities. For firmness, one of the sampling methods reached the upper bound, where “all” data were phenotyped with only 300 individuals. For TTA and brix, a sample size of 400 and 300 individuals from the target population is necessary to reach the upper bound, respectively. Consequently, the last question relies on how to select individuals to optimize the prediction.

As a benchmark, we used random sampling and showed lower performance in almost all scenarios. Similarly, lower predictability results were also obtained when we sampled individuals based on the pedigree, by focusing either on all individuals from the few families or all families with few individuals. For example, by selecting 500 individuals from a few number of families, predictive abilities across all traits was the lowest compared to other sampling methods. Maximum predictive ability of 0.32, 0.53, and 0.42 for brix, firmness, and TTA were obtained using CDmean and D‐Optimal, respectively. These values were the same or slightly better than the scenario where “all” individuals in the validation set were phenotyped. In general, our results are corroborating the findings from other studies with different population structures (Rincent et al., 2012; Sarinelli et al., 2019), where the CDmean showed superior performance for training set optimization. The advantage of CDmean and other combinatorial optimization algorithms is the exploitation of the genetic relationships between the selected training and validation sets. However, a measure of caution should be taken in the interpretation of the results and implementation. The superiority of the genomic CDmean may be controversial, because if an entire population needs to be genotyped, the optimization process on defining core individuals may become pointless. Although reasonable, this counterargument is to use such an approach for optimizing the phenotypic collection of high‐expense traits. This is the case of metabolomic information and the use of volatile organic compounds (VOC) for flavor prediction. While recent studies have indicated large importance of VOC on blueberry consumer preferences (Colantonio et al., 2022), phenotyping a large breeding population is still expensive.

CONCLUSIONS

The implementation of GS in many breeding programs faces the primary bottlenecks associated with the costs of phenotyping and genotyping a large number of individuals. In this study, several probe pruning parameters and training set optimization algorithms were explored in a historical blueberry breeding dataset, aiming to assist in resource allocation for GS. We demonstrated that a combination of statistics and genetics parameters for probe selection enabled the subset of a better panel of molecular markers (2500 probes) without trade‐offs in the accuracy of genomic prediction models, which has the potential of reducing genotyping costs. The assessment of the long‐term implication of marker selection using a simulated blueberry breeding population supports the superiority of the data‐driven method over random selection for simple and moderate trait architectures. In addition, our results also suggested that the inclusion of 300–400 individuals (30%−40%) of the target population into the training set, using CDmean and D‐Optimal sampling methods, can optimize GS predictive abilities. Altogether, we demonstrated the importance of data‐driven methods to achieve better prediction accuracy using historical blueberry data as a model.
