Determinants of adherence to sirolimus among lymphangioleiomyomatosis patients
Authors: Li Gao, Yating Peng, Ruoyun Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) is a low-grade malignant neoplasm characterized by multisystem involvement. Patients with LAM require long-term sirolimus therapy to slow disease progression, yet poor adherence to this medication may lead to reduced lung function and disease deterioration.ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to comprehensively characterize sirolimus adherence in patients with LAM. Specifically, it aims to identify factors associated with poor adherence, explore determinants of self-reported adherence, and compare changes in pulmonary function over 2 years across adherence groups.MethodsWe conducted a questionnaire survey of patients diagnosed with LAM (n = 133). We used the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8) to assess medication adherence, the Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ) to assess illness perceptions, the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) to assess social support, and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) to assess anxiety and depressive symptoms. Multivariate logistic regression was then performed to identify risk factors influencing sirolimus adherence among LAM patients. Furthermore, we compared changes in lung function over the two-year follow-up period.ResultsIn our cohort, 20.3% of LAM patients demonstrated high medication adherence, 41.5% moderate adherence, and 38.1% poor adherence. Additionally, educational level, FEV1/FVC (% predicted), the number of adverse drug reactions ≥ 3, BIPQ scores and anxiety symptoms are associated with medication adherence. Furthermore, patients with better medication adherence showed greater improvements in lung function, exercise endurance, and disease activity than those with poor adherence.ConclusionOver one-third of LAM patients reported poor medication adherence. High FEV1/FVC (% predicted), high BIPQ scores, comorbid anxiety, and low educational level are risk factors. These may help identify LAM patients with poor medication adherence early.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04157-7.

Introduction

Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) is a rare, low-grade, destructive, and metastasizing neoplastic disease that predominantly affects fertile women [1]. Epidemiological data indicate a prevalence of 20.9–26.04 cases per million adult women, based on the latest studies [2, 3]. LAM is characterized by the abnormal proliferation of smooth muscle–like cells that invade the lungs and form diffuse, thin-walled cysts. These cysts cause pulmonary complications, including breathlessness, hemoptysis, pneumothorax, and chylothorax [4]. LAM is also associated with extrapulmonary manifestations, including renal angiomyolipomas, retroperitoneal lymphangioleiomyomas, and pelvic lymphangioleiomyomas [5, 6]. LAM comprises two subtypes: sporadic (S-LAM) and tuberous sclerosis complex-associated (TSC-LAM), with TSC-LAM exhibiting a hereditary pattern [7]. Both subtypes are characterized by mutations in the TSC1/TSC2 genes that hyperactivate the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway. mTORC1, a key regulator of cell growth, metabolism, and aging, plays a well-established role in cancer and stem cell biology [8, 9].

Sirolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, is approved as a first-line treatment for LAM in many countries. This therapy reduces LAM cell proliferation and tissue-organ destruction [10], and correlates with slower disease progression and improved survival [11]. The official American Thoracic Society/Japanese Respiratory Society Clinical Practice Guidelines state that sirolimus improves pulmonary function and quality of life [12]. It also reduces the volume of angiomyolipoma, lymphangioleiomyoma, and chylous accumulation [12]. Clinicians typically recommend low-dose therapy (1–2 mg/d) [13]. While sirolimus delays disease progression, stopping it may worsen pulmonary function [14] and promote angiomyolipoma growth [15]. Sirolimus has side effects and requires regular outpatient follow-up to adjust dosages based on blood drug concentrations. Long-term adherence may be compromised by side effects and economic burdens, thereby affecting disease progression and treatment efficacy.

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have explored medication adherence in LAM patients. Thus, this study aims to characterize sirolimus adherence in LAM patients, identify factors contributing to poor adherence, analyze determinants of self-reported adherence, and compare changes in 2-year clinical characteristics across adherence groups.

Methods

Cross-sectional observational study

The present study enrolled patients diagnosed with LAM according to the 2010 European Respiratory Society guidelines [16]. Data collection was conducted through a structured questionnaire distributed via WeChat’s QuestionnaireStar platform within a dedicated LAM patient group (including individuals diagnosed or treated at the Second Xiangya Hospital) in 2023. The inclusion criteria required participants to be over 18 years of age, have a confirmed diagnosis of LAM, and be willing to participate in the study. The questionnaire gathered comprehensive information, including demographic data, clinical characteristics, current treatment status, and responses to several standardized assessment tools.

Participants completed the questionnaire following on-page guidance, with real-time response tracking enabled via the QuestionnaireStar platform backend. During data collection, the research team dynamically monitored response recovery progress, removing duplicate submissions and excluding invalid responses based on two predefined criteria: a completion time of less than 3 min and abnormal scale scores. A total of 133 questionnaires were collected, among which 118 patients were receiving sirolimus therapy for at least 3 months (Fig. 1). After being diagnosed with LAM, patients with any of the following conditions need to use sirolimus [17]: (1) Forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) % of predicted value < 70%; (2) the decline rate of FEV₁ ≥ 90 mL/year; (3) Symptomatic chylothorax or chylous ascites; (4) Renal angiomyolipoma or retroperitoneal and pelvic lymphangioleiomyomas (the diameter of the largest single tumor > 3 cm); (5) TSC-LAM. We collected basic demographic characteristics, clinical features of the disease, pulmonary function indicators, treatment modalities, and psychosocial questionnaires from all patients.

Treatment adherence was evaluated using the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8) [18, 19]. Adherence levels were classified into three categories: high, moderate, and poor. Specifically, a score of 8 indicated excellent adherence, scores between 6 and 8 denoted moderate adherence, and scores below 6 reflected poor adherence. To identify predictors of adherence, binary logistic regression analyses were performed, with adherence defined as an MMAS-8 score ≥ 6.

The Brief Illness Perception Questionnaire (BIPQ), a nine-item instrument designed to rapidly assess patients’ cognitive and emotional representations of their illness, was employed in this study [20, 21]. The ninth item, an open-ended question, was excluded from analysis. The BIPQ items are divided into three dimensions: cognitive perceptions (5 items), emotional perceptions (2 items), and comprehension perceptions (1 item). The first eight questions collectively yield a maximum score of 80 points, with each item scored on a 10-point scale; higher scores indicate more negative perceptions of the illness.

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), developed by Xiao Shuiyuan [22], was used to assess participants’ level of social support, demonstrating good reliability and validity in the Chinese population. The scale comprises 10 items divided into three domains: objective support (3 items), subjective support (4 items), and utilization of support (3 items). Scores range from 20 to 30, indicating a moderate level of social support, while scores between 30 and 40 suggest a satisfactory level of social support.

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was utilized to measure symptoms of anxiety and depression. Each subscale contains seven items scored from 0 to 3, with a threshold score of 8 used to identify the presence of clinical symptoms of anxiety or depression [23, 24].

Prospective study

We prospectively followed a patient cohort for 2 years, with 22 in the persistently poor adherence group (MMAS-8 < 6), 20 in the persistently good adherence group (MMAS-8 ≥ 6), and 8 in the fluctuating adherence group. Reasons for non-completion included voluntary withdrawal (40), loss to follow-up (18), pregnancy/childbirth (4), and disease progression/complications (6) (Fig. 1). Longitudinal data regarding lung function and MMAS-8 scores were collected at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months. Serum vascular endothelial growth factor-D (VEGF-D) levels and six-minute walking distance (6MWD) were measured at baseline and 24 months, respectively. Baseline was defined as the time point of initial questionnaire distribution and the initiation of follow-up. The MMAS-8 questionnaires were distributed and completed on-site, face-to-face. Serum VEGF-D was quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with the Quantikine Human VEGF-D Immunoassay kit (R&D Systems). Other data were extracted from the electronic medical record system. This data collection aimed to compare the impact of medication adherence on long-term clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Graphs were created with GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2. Normally distributed continuous variables with homogeneous variances were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD) and analyzed using a t-test or a one-way ANOVA. Non-normal or heteroscedastic variables were shown as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were summarized as counts and percentages. Group differences were assessed using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. For multiple group comparisons, if a significant difference was found (p < 0.05), the Least Significant Difference or the Bonferroni correction was applied for pairwise comparisons. Univariate and multivariable logistic regressions assessed associations between predictors and binary adherence status (moderate/high vs. poor). Univariate screening used a significance level of p < 0.05. The final multivariable model, adjusted for covariates, retained predictors with p < 0.05. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (Ethical Code: LYEC2024-0141). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics of the overall population

Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of the overall population (n = 118). All participants in our cohort were female, with a mean age of 44.39 ± 9.46 years. Most were premenopausal women (85, 72.0%) and TSC-LAM (9, 7.6%). Over half had a disease duration of 5 years or less (62, 52.5%). Most reside in urban areas (81, 68.6%), have more than 12 years of education (65, 55.1%), are married (100, 84.75%), and have a monthly household income over 5,000 yuan (60, 50.8%). Clinical comorbidities and organ involvement included pulmonary manifestations, such as a history of pneumothorax (58, 49.2%), pulmonary cystic lesions (16, 13.6%), and chylothorax (17, 14.4%). Extrapulmonary manifestations included renal angiomyolipoma (33, 28.0%), retroperitoneal lymphangioleiomyomas (21, 17.8%), pelvic lymphangioleiomyomas (17, 14.4%), and hepatic lymphangioleiomyomas (10, 8.5%).

Regarding treatment methods, the majority are on sirolimus at doses below 2 mg/day (91; 77.1%). Analysis of Fig. 2 revealed the reasons for non-compliance with sirolimus treatment among 118 LAM patients, encompassing poor memory (33.90%), adverse drug reactions (21.29%), economic difficulty (14.41%), severe infections (7.63%), symptoms improved (5.93%), pregnancy and lactation (4.24%), poor efficacy (1.69%), and others. Moreover, sirolimus adverse effects reported by patients included oral ulcer (68.6%), elevated cholesterol (38.14%), acne (23.73%), menstrual disorder (22.88%), palpitation (16.95%), diarrhoea (10.17%), lower limb edema (10.17%), abnormal liver function (5.08%), pneumonia (2.54%), pyelonephritis (2.54%), mucositis (1.69%), nausea and vomiting (1.69%), and cellulitis (0.85%) (Fig. 3). Additionally, 38 patients (32.2%) reported experiencing ≥ 3 adverse drug reactions. Concomitant treatments included bronchodilators (57, 48.3%), oxygen therapy (45, 38.1%), and surgical intervention (12, 10.2%).

Demographics and clinical characteristics of three groups

Table 2 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of LAM patients stratified by medication adherence. Among the 118 patients on sirolimus therapy, 24 (20.3%) exhibit high adherence, 49 (41.5%) moderate adherence, and 45 (38.1%) poor adherence (Fig. 1). Significant differences in menopausal status were observed between the high and moderate adherence groups (p < 0.05): the high-adherence group included more postmenopausal patients, while the moderate group had more premenopausal patients. Compared with the poor adherence group, the moderate adherence cohort had higher proportions of disease duration ≤ 5 years, monthly household income > 5000 yuan, and ≥ 3 adverse drug reactions (p < 0.05). The high-adherence group, relative to the low-adherence group, included more urban residents and had a lower predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) ratio (p < 0.05). Both high and moderate adherence groups demonstrated higher educational attainment (> 12 years) and lower scores on the BIPQ and disease cognition dimensions than the poor adherence group (p < 0.05). The moderate adherence group also showed significantly lower comprehensive dimension scores than the poor adherence group (p < 0.001). Furthermore, high adherence patients had higher SSRS and subjective support scores (p < 0.05; p < 0.001), and lower levels of anxiety and depression symptoms (p < 0.05) compared to the poor adherence group. The moderate adherence group reported more depressive symptoms than the high adherence cohort (p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study represents the first characterization of sirolimus adherence among LAM patients, with findings indicating that 20.3% demonstrate high adherence, 41.5% moderate adherence, and 38.1% poor adherence. These rates align with the average medication adherence profiles observed across eight rare disease studies, which reported corresponding incidences of 28.2%, 30.4%, and 41.4% for high, moderate, and low adherence, respectively [25]. Independent predictors of non-adherence were identified as low educational level, high FEV1/FVC (% predicted), fewer than three adverse drug reactions, elevated BIPQ scores, and the presence of comorbid anxiety. Over a 2-year follow-up, patients with good adherence showed significant improvements in lung ventilatory function, VEGF-D, and 6MWD.

In our research, we found that patients with low educational attainment exhibited poor medication adherence. This has also been observed in other chronic diseases [26–28]. Patients with a low educational level often struggle to understand drug instructions. They may also lack essential health knowledge and be unable to manage complex medication regimens. Therefore, regular health education and follow-up are crucial for these patients. Such measures help ensure they understand the significance of the treatment and follow specific medication instructions.

We also found that higher BIPQ scores were associated with poorer medication adherence, contradicting the findings of most studies [29, 30]. Typically, high BIPQ scores indicate that patients perceive the disease’s severity and impact more acutely. Nevertheless, it is possible that patients’ overly negative perception of the disease could give rise to anxiety or avoidance behaviors, consequently diminishing their enthusiasm for taking medications.

In our cohort study, 33.9% of patients had comorbid anxiety, and 47.5% had comorbid depression. Comorbid anxiety was linked to poorer medication adherence, aligning with findings in other chronic diseases [30, 31]. Anxious patients may stop medication due to fear of side effects, forget doses because of memory lapses, or lose motivation from emotional stress. These factors impede following medical advice. For example, excessive concern about disease progression or drug risks can reduce medication consistency. Targeted psychological interventions, such as cognitive-behavioral therapy, can improve adherence.

We also found that a higher FEV1/FVC (% predicted) was significantly associated with poor medication adherence. FEV1/FVC (% predicted) is a core indicator for evaluating pulmonary function and the severity of airway obstruction in patients with LAM. When this ratio is lower, the airway stenosis and air trapping are more severe. In patients with relatively high FEV1/FVC (% predicted), airway obstruction is mild, often causing only subtle symptoms. This can lead to the mistaken belief that regular medication is unnecessary, resulting in reduced doses or discontinuation of treatment. In contrast, patients with severe airway obstruction tend to strictly adhere to treatment. This occurs because their symptoms are prominent and their concerns about disease worsening are stronger. Essentially, this association directly reflects how baseline disease severity impacts patients’ willingness to undergo treatment. This phenomenon has also been observed in other chronic respiratory diseases. In one study, patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) who had no acute exacerbations and lower GOLD grades showed poor adherence to inhaled medications [32]. A systematic review [33] further confirmed that asthma severity is positively correlated with treatment adherence.

We also found that adverse drug reactions were correlated with medication adherence. Sirolimus has a relatively narrow therapeutic window [34]. Excessively high blood concentrations can significantly increase the incidence of side effects, including elevated infection risk, hyperlipidemia, and oral ulcers. Conversely, subtherapeutic concentrations fail to achieve the desired clinical efficacy [35]. The observed association between ≥ 3 adverse drug reactions and better medication adherence in this study does not mean that more side effects lead to higher adherence. Instead, it arises from differences in drug exposure levels and monitoring frequency seen with good adherence. On one hand, patients with good adherence strictly follow the prescribed regimen and maintain stable drug concentrations. This helps mild adverse drug reactions directly linked to sirolimus pharmacology, such as oral ulcers and elevated cholesterol, to fully manifest. These patients also adhere to consistent follow-up schedules, leading to more thorough reporting of side effects. On the other hand, most of the ≥ 3 adverse drug reactions recorded in this study were mild and tolerable. Patients often see these as signs that the treatment is working. Furthermore, with few therapeutic options for LAM, fear of disease progression often outweighs concerns about mild adverse drug reactions. It should be emphasized that this association is generally applicable only to mild, manageable adverse drug reactions, whereas severe adverse drug reactions may still substantially reduce patients’ medication adherence.

Moreover, we found that LAM patients with better medication adherence tended to have shorter disease duration, higher income, more urban residence, and higher SSRS scores. Yet, when analyzed together in multivariate regression, none showed a significant independent association with adherence. Patients with a shorter disease duration tend to have better adherence, and a similar phenomenon has also been observed in studies of other chronic diseases. For example, in a study of hypertensive patients’ adherence, the duration of illness was significantly associated with a lower average score on the MMAS-8 [36]. Similarly, in a multicenter cross-sectional study, disease duration in patients with epilepsy was associated with medication adherence [37]. Multiple studies have confirmed that low-income groups and rural residents are more likely to exhibit non-adherent behaviors in the treatment of chronic diseases, possibly due to factors such as greater difficulty in accessing medical resources and heavier economic burdens. For instance, in a cross-sectional study in Erbil [38], low medication adherence among diabetic patients was significantly associated with lower educational levels, unemployment, rural residence, and decreased income. Additional factors, such as social support, also have a positive effect on the treatment of chronic diseases, as both social support and self-efficacy have direct or indirect effects on the medication adherence of patients with type 2 diabetes [39].

Numerous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of sirolimus in the treatment of LAM. Sirolimus is capable of effectively retarding the deterioration of lung function and the progression of pulmonary cystic lesions. In a Multicenter International LAM Efficacy of Sirolimus (MILES) trial, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, after 12 months of sirolimus treatment, FEV1 increased by 153 ml compared with the placebo group (an 11% increase relative to baseline) [14]. In a retrospective study conducted in South Korea [40], DLCO was significantly enhanced following sirolimus treatment. Moreover, qualitative analysis demonstrated that 15.8% to 21.1% of patients experienced a reduction in lung cyst volume [40]. The National Institutes of Health Clinical Center [41] reported that among 19 patients with rapidly progressing LAM or chylous effusions, over an average of 2.6 years of sirolimus therapy, both the predicted FEV1 and the predicted DLCO increased. Similar findings were also observed in several other studies [42–44].

Our study revealed that FEV1 and FVC increased over a 2-year period in LAM patients with persistently good adherence to sirolimus treatment. Nevertheless, with respect to diffusing capacity, no significant differences were observed among the medication adherence groups. Notably, sirolimus typically shows a weaker effect on DLCO than on FEV1, a pattern observed in clinical studies of LAM. This matches the landmark MILES trial [45], which showed sirolimus stabilizes FEV1 in LAM patients, but found no significant difference in DLCO values or decline rates between the sirolimus and placebo groups. In addition, a long-term follow-up study of LAM patients [46] demonstrated that, compared with baseline data, FEV1 and FVC were significantly improved, while no notable change in DLCO was observed. This discrepancy in treatment responses between the indicators may be attributed to the pathophysiological characteristics of LAM. Specifically, the cystic destruction of lung parenchyma and loss of capillary beds in LAM patients are generally irreversible. Sirolimus exerts its therapeutic effect mainly by inhibiting the mammalian target of mTOR pathway to slow disease progression, rather than directly repairing the already damaged lung tissue. Therefore, the improvement of DLCO may require a longer treatment course to become clinically apparent. Furthermore, the exercise endurance of LAM patients with good medication adherence has also been improved, as manifested by an increase in the 6MWD. In addition, in our study, serum VEGF-D levels decreased more rapidly after sirolimus treatment. Several other studies also demonstrated that sirolimus treatment could lower serum VEGF-D levels, which were associated with symptom alleviation and improved quality of life [14, 46].

This study has several limitations. First and foremost, it is a single-center study. Given that it concerns a rare disease, the number of patients under follow-up is rather limited. Consequently, it is necessary to verify with a larger sample. Secondly, for some chronic diseases, the two-year observation period is relatively brief, failing to fully capture their long-term characteristics and changes. In addition, since the data were collected via questionnaires, there is a potential for self-report bias. Moreover, sampling through a WeChat group may introduce selection bias: although WeChat is widely used in China, it cannot be ruled out that some patients may lack access to smartphones or be illiterate, thus failing to be included in the study. In future research endeavors, more objective assessment methods could be employed to investigate the influencing factors that impact adherence. For example, combining patients’ self-reported adherence with Sirolimus trough levels may enable a more accurate and detailed assessment of adherence patterns in the real world.

Conclusions

The present study revealed the distribution of medication adherence among LAM patients as follows: 20.3% demonstrated high adherence, 41.5% moderate adherence, and 38.1% poor adherence. Additionally, several factors related to this adherence were identified, including educational level, FEV1/FVC (% predicted), the number of adverse drug reactions ≥ 3, BIPQ scores, and anxiety symptoms. Paying attention to these factors in LAM patients may help identify poor medication adherence. Furthermore, sirolimus can improve lung function, exercise endurance and disease activity. Patients with better medication adherence showed greater improvements in FVC, FEV1, 6MWD, and VEGF-D over 2 years.
