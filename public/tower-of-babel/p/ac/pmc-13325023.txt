Rationally designed copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex as a dual anticancer and SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor
Authors: Zeinab Mirzaei-Kalar, Ali Akbar Khandar, Jonathan M. White, Hoda Abolhasani, Tahereh Komeili Movahhed, Abolghasem Jouyban
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A novel ternary copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex was rationally designed, synthesized, and thoroughly characterized. DNA interaction studies revealed that deferiprone binds to CT-DNA via groove binding, whereas the copper complex predominantly intercalates. The complex exhibited remarkable cytotoxicity against HT-29, HepG2, and MDA-MB-468 human cancer cell lines, while showing lower toxicity toward normal HEK-293 cells. Compared to cisplatin, doxorubicin, oxaliplatin, fluorouracil, and deferiprone, the copper complex demonstrated significantly enhanced anticancer potency. Flow cytometry analysis indicated late-stage apoptosis induction and G1-phase cell cycle arrest. Gene expression studies showed down-regulation of BCL2 and up-regulation of BAX, Caspase-3, and the BAX/BCL2 ratio, confirming apoptosis-mediated cytotoxicity. In addition, molecular docking revealed strong binding affinities of the complex toward key SARS-CoV-2 targets, including the main protease (Mpro), papain-like protease (PLpro), nucleocapsid N-terminal RNA-binding domain, and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), surpassing several currently available antiviral agents. Collectively, these findings identify the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex as a promising candidate for dual biomedical applications, serving as both an anticancer agent and a potential SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor.

Introduction

Before the emergence of COVID-19 in late 2019, cancer was already one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide,1 and it continues to pose a major global health challenge. A key goal in oncology is the development of agents that selectively inhibit cancer cell proliferation while minimizing toxicity toward normal tissues. Platinum-based drugs have long been the cornerstone of chemotherapy due to their broad-spectrum efficacy; however, their clinical utility is often limited by severe side effects, poor solubility, and the development of drug resistance.2–4 To overcome these limitations, attention has turned to the design of metal-based complexes beyond platinum. Transition metals such as copper,5,6 zinc,7 nickel,8,9 gold,10 and cobalt11 have shown distinct pharmacological profiles, providing promising alternatives for anticancer therapy.

Repurposing clinically approved drugs as ligands in novel metal complexes represents another attractive strategy. Deferiprone, an established iron-chelating agent, offers a well-characterized pharmacological profile, facilitating in vitro and in vivo evaluations and potentially accelerating translation to therapeutic applications. Incorporating such ligands into metal complexes can enhance biological activity and specificity, while leveraging existing safety data.

Since December 2019, the outbreak of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has caused a global pandemic, resulting in millions of deaths worldwide.12–15 Despite the success of vaccination campaigns, the rapid emergence of viral variants and the limitations of current therapeutics emphasize the urgent need for new antiviral strategies. Due to the high risks associated with handling live virus, computational approaches, including molecular docking, have emerged as efficient tools to rapidly screen and predict the antiviral potential of candidate compounds.

In this context, we report the rational design and synthesis of a novel copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex, integrating the biologically active 1, 10-phenanthroline and the clinically approved deferiprone ligand (Fig. 1). The complex was investigated for its dual biomedical potential: DNA binding studies were conducted to elucidate its interaction with CT-DNA, a primary target of many anticancer agents, followed by in vitro cytotoxicity assays against human cancer cell lines (HT-29, HepG2, and MDA-MB-468) and normal HEK-293 cells. Furthermore, in silico molecular docking was employed to evaluate its binding affinity toward key SARS-CoV-2 proteins, providing insights into its prospective antiviral activity. Collectively, this study highlights the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex as a promising candidate for integrated anticancer and antiviral therapy.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Pharmaceutical-grade deferiprone was obtained from Arastoo Pharmaceutical Company (Tehran, Iran). Calf thymus DNA (CT-DNA) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Human cancer cell lines, including HT-29 (colon adenocarcinoma), HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), and MDA-MB-468 (breast adenocarcinoma), as well as the normal human embryonic kidney cell line HEK-293, were acquired from the Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran). All aqueous solutions were prepared using double-distilled water.

Preparation of CT-DNA Solution

A stock solution of CT-DNA was prepared by dissolving a precisely weighed amount of DNA in 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 7.4). The solution was gently stirred at 4 °C for 24 hours to ensure complete solubilization and then stored at the same temperature, remaining stable for up to four days. The concentration of CT-DNA was determined spectrophotometrically using the molar absorption coefficient of ε260 = 6600 L mol−1 cm−1 at 260 nm. The purity of the DNA solution was assessed by the absorbance ratio at 260/280 nm, which was found to be 1.87, confirming negligible protein contamination.16,17

Buffer Preparation

Tris–HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 7.4) was freshly prepared and used for all DNA interaction studies. The pH of the buffer was carefully adjusted using HCl or NaOH as required.

Synthesis of copper(ii) complex

The ternary copper(ii) complex was synthesized via a straightforward one-pot reaction, followed by a slow evaporation crystallization process. A solution of Cu (NO3)2·3H2O (1 mmol, 0.24 g) in 50 mL of methanol was added dropwise to a stirred methanolic mixture (50 mL) containing deferiprone (1 mmol, 0.14 g), 1,10-phenanthroline (1 mmol, 0.20 g), and KOH (1 mmol, 0.05 g). The reaction mixture immediately exhibited a color change from blue to green, indicating the formation of the copper complex. This resulting mixture was then heated under reflux for 4 hours to ensure complete complexation. After the reflux period, the reaction mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature. The solution was then left undisturbed for 72 hours. During this period, the solvent slowly evaporated from the solution, facilitating the nucleation and growth of green crystalline product through a slow evaporation method. The formed green crystals were isolated by filtration. The collected crystals were washed with cold methanol to remove any residual impurities. The product was then dried under vacuum. The yield of the pure crystalline complex was 0.35 g (76% based on deferiprone), with a melting point of 278 °C.

DNA binding

The interaction of deferiprone and the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex with CT-DNA was investigated using UV-vis absorption, viscosity, and competitive fluorescence assays. For absorption measurements, fixed concentrations of the compounds (5 × 10−5 M) were incubated with increasing amounts of CT-DNA (0–4 × 10−4 M, corresponding to [DNA]/[compound] = 0–0.8) at room temperature for 2 h. Absorption spectra were recorded over the 240–400 nm range, and measurements were continued until no further change in the maximum absorbance at 272 nm was observed.

Viscosity experiments were conducted at room temperature with CT-DNA maintained at 5 × 10−5 M, while the compound concentrations varied from 0 to 1 × 10−4 M. Flow times were measured automatically in triplicate with an accuracy of ±0.01 s, and the mean values were used to calculate relative viscosities. The data were plotted as (η/η0)1/3versus [compound]/[DNA] (0–2.0), where η and η0 represent the viscosity of CT-DNA in the presence and absence of the compound, respectively.

Competitive fluorescence titrations were performed to further probe the binding mode. For Hoechst 33 258 displacement assays, a fixed mixture of Hoechst 33 258 (5 × 10−6 M) and CT-DNA (2.3 × 10−4 M) was titrated with increasing concentrations of the compounds (0–0.44 × 10−4 M). Emission spectra were recorded over 480–620 nm with excitation at 380 nm. Similarly, ethidium bromide (EB) displacement assays were carried out by adding the compounds (0–0.58 × 10−4 M) to a preformed EB-CT-DNA complex (5 × 10−6 M EB and 2.3 × 10−4 M DNA). Samples were excited at 525 nm, and emission spectra were monitored between 550–700 nm. These studies provided insight into the binding characteristics of the compounds, indicating whether groove binding or intercalation was predominant.

Cytotoxicity studies

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex, along with standard anticancer agents including cisplatin, oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), and doxorubicin, was evaluated using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay. HT-29, HepG2, MDA-MB-468, and HEK-293 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 6 × 103 cells per well and incubated with varying concentrations of the compounds (0.01–1000 µM) under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 48 h.

After incubation, 200 µL of MTT solution (2 mg mL−1 in phosphate buffer) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 4 h. The supernatant was then removed, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved by adding 200 µL DMSO followed by 25 µL of Sorensen's glycine buffer (0.1 M glycine, 0.1 M NaCl, pH adjusted to 10.5 with 0.1 M NaOH) to each well. The plates were gently shaken for 40 min to ensure complete solubilization of the formazan. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA reader.

Cell viability was calculated according to eqn (1), and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were determined using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.01, GraphPad Software Inc.) by averaging three independent experiments for each cell line.211Cells treated with 1 mM hydrogen peroxide served as a positive control to achieve minimal viability (MinV), while untreated cells cultured in medium alone were used as the negative control representing maximum viability (MaxV).

Apoptosis analysis by annexin V-FITC staining and cell cycle deterrence

Hep G2 cells were cultured in 6-well plates. Afterwards 24 hours incubation, cells were cured by 1 µmol. L−1 concentration of the copper complex for 48 h. Afterward chemical cure, cells well trypsinized, spun and washout with phosphate buffer saline. Cell proliferation at the early and late stages of apoptosis, as well as live and necrotic cells using Annexin-FITC and PI in 1× binding buffer for 20 min at room temperature in the dark was evaluated by flow cytometry and the data were represented as a percentage.

The cell cycle study was investigated by treating Hep G2 cells with 1 µmol. L−1 concentration of the complex for 48 h. Pursuant to the procedure, the cell pellets were secure with 50% ethanol, cured with 5 mg mL−1 RNase A and discolored with the fluorescent reagent solution comprising PI. Finally, DNA fluorescence of PI-discolored cells was assessed by flow cytometry for cell cycle standing.

Molecular docking studies

Molecular docking studies were conducted to investigate the interactions of deferiprone and the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex with key SARS-CoV-2 proteins. Docking simulations were performed using AutoDock 4.2.5 and AutoGrid 4.2.5, with MGLTools 1.5.4 for preparation of input files. The 3D structure of deferiprone was obtained from DrugBank (ID: DB08826), while the structure of the copper complex was derived from its CIF file (CCDC 1986538) using Mercury to convert it into PDB format.

Crystal structures of SARS-CoV-2 target proteins were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank, including the papain-like protease (PLpro, PDB: 6W9C), nucleocapsid N-terminal RNA-binding domain (PDB: 6 M3M), main protease in complex with inhibitor N3 (Mpro, PDB: 6LU7), and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) in complex with cofactors. Protein and ligand files were prepared using AutoDock Tools, removing all crystallographic water molecules to avoid interference in docking.

Each protein was enclosed within a cubic grid box of 126 × 126 × 126 points along the x, y, and z axes, with a spacing of 0.375 Å. Docking calculations were performed using the Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm implemented in AutoDock, with default parameters for all other settings. For each docking run, the conformation with the lowest binding energy, as predicted by the AutoDock scoring function, was selected as the preferred binding mode. The resulting docked complexes were visualized using Chimera 1.10.20 and Discovery Studio (DS45Client) to analyze the binding interactions.

Result and discussion

Structural characterization of the copper complex

Green crystals of the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex were obtained after reflux and subsequent crystallization of the reaction solution via slow evaporation. The structure of the complex was elucidated by FT-IR spectroscopy and single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis.

Comparison of the FT-IR spectrum of the complex with those of the free ligands confirmed the coordination sites involved in metal binding. A broad band observed at 3390 cm−1 was attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of lattice solvent molecules (water or methanol). The strong CO stretching band of free deferiprone at 1632 cm−1 disappeared upon complex formation, indicating coordination through the carbonyl oxygen atom. In the free 1,10-phenanthroline ligand, the CN stretching vibration appeared at 1583 cm−1; this band shifted to lower frequency (by approximately 80 cm−1) in the complex, confirming coordination of the phenanthroline nitrogen atoms to the copper center.23 Additional bands at 1329, 1119, and 1028 cm−1 were assigned to nitrate ions present as counter anions in the crystal lattice.

The 1H NMR spectrum of the copper complex was recorded to further investigate its behavior in solution (Fig. 2a). As expected for Cu(ii) complexes possessing a d9 electronic configuration, the spectrum exhibited broadened resonances and reduced signal resolution due to the paramagnetic nature of the metal center. Such spectral behavior has been widely reported for Cu(ii) coordination compounds and originates from enhanced relaxation effects induced by the unpaired electron.24,25

Inset showed d–d band of the complex with 10−3 mol L−1 concentration (c) LC mass spectra of the complex.

Despite the broadening effect, the spectrum confirmed the presence of ligand-derived proton environments and indicated preservation of the coordinated ligand framework in solution. The absence of well-resolved aromatic resonances is attributed to paramagnetic line broadening rather than ligand decomposition. These observations are consistent with previously reported Cu(ii) complexes containing mixed N/O donor ligands and phenanthroline derivatives.24,25

The electronic absorption spectrum of the complex (Fig. 2b) recorded in aqueous solution and Tris–HCl buffer exhibits intense absorption bands in the UV region (200–400 nm), which are attributed to ligand-centered π → π* and n → π* transitions. In the visible region, the complex displays a broad low-intensity absorption band centered at approximately 639 nm, which is assigned to the d–d transition of the Cu(ii) center.

Since Cu(ii) possesses a d9 electronic configuration, Jahn–Teller distortion commonly results in structural flexibility and distortion of the coordination environment. Therefore, UV-vis spectroscopy alone is insufficient to establish the exact coordination geometry in solution. The observed spectral features support the presence of a Cu(ii) coordination environment in solution; however, they should not be interpreted as definitive evidence for a specific geometry.26–28

Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis revealed that the complex crystallizes in the triclinic system. The ORTEP representation with atomic numbering is shown in Fig. 3a, and selected bond lengths and angles are summarized in Table 3. In the solid state, deferiprone acts as a monoanionic ligand upon deprotonation of its hydroxyl group. The structure features a centrosymmetric binuclear unit, [Cu(µ-O1)2Cu], forming a parallelogram motif. The Cu–O1 bond distances are 1.918(2) and 2.478(2) Å, with Cu–O1–Cu and O1–Cu–O1 angles of 89.77° and 90.23°, respectively. The Cu⋯Cu and O1⋯O1 separations within the binuclear core are 3.127 and 3.139 Å.

Considering the longer Cu–O1 distance (2.478(2) Å) as a secondary interaction, each copper center adopts a distorted square-pyramidal (N2O3) coordination geometry, with one oxygen atom from deferiprone bridging the two copper ions. If the weaker Cu–O interaction is neglected, the copper center can alternatively be described as four-coordinate, bound to two nitrogen atoms from 1,10-phenanthroline and two oxygen atoms from deferiprone in a distorted square-planar arrangement.

LC-MS analysis was performed to further investigate the solution behavior of the complex (Fig. 2c). The obtained mass spectral pattern suggests that the weak axial Cu–O interaction observed in the crystal structure may be partially weakened upon dissolution.

Such behavior has frequently been reported for Cu(ii) complexes containing mixed nitrogen- and oxygen-donor ligands, where Jahn–Teller distortion and ligand-field effects can induce coordination flexibility and lability of axial interactions in solution.26,29,30 Therefore, the coordination environment in solution is more appropriately described as dynamic rather than rigidly preserving the solid-state arrangement.

Each copper atom deviates by 0.114 Å from the mean coordination plane defined by O1O2N2N3. The asymmetric unit consists of [Cu(1,10-phenanthroline)(deferiprone)] H2O NO3. In the binuclear structure, the centroid-to-centroid distance between the deferiprone aromatic ring (C1N1C5C4C3C2) and the phenanthroline ring (N2C8C9C10C11C19) is 3.495 Å, with a perpendicular interplanar separation of 3.361 Å (Fig. 3b). No significant intermolecular π–π stacking interactions were observed between adjacent binuclear units.

DNA binding investigations

Electronic absorption spectrum

Electronic absorption studies and binding analysis

The electronic absorption behavior of deferiprone and the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex was first examined under physiological conditions (Tris–HCl buffer, pH 7.4) in the range of 210–400 nm (Fig. 5a and b). Both compounds exhibited stable spectral profiles over several days, particularly within the 240–350 nm region, confirming their structural integrity in aqueous buffer and validating their suitability for DNA-binding investigations.

Electronic absorption spectra changes of (a) deferiprone and (b) the copper complex at physiological condition at Tris–HCl buffer (10−4 mol L−1, pH = 7.4) at different time points, (c) 3D absorption spectra of deferiprone and (d) copper complex (5 × 10−5 mol L−1) with adding of various quantities of CT-DNA (0 to 4 × 10−4 mol L−1) at pH = 7.4.

Incremental addition of CT-DNA to a fixed concentration of each compound (5 × 10−5 M) resulted in notable spectral changes (Fig. 5c and d). For both deferiprone and the copper complex, progressive hyperchromism was observed without any significant bathochromic or hypsochromic shift in the absorption maxima. Hyperchromism generally reflects perturbation of the DNA secondary structure upon ligand binding. However, the absence of a red shift suggests that classical intercalation between DNA base pairs is not the dominant interaction mechanism in the case of free deferiprone. Instead, this spectral pattern is more consistent with external groove binding, where the ligand interacts with DNA without extensive π–π stacking with nucleobases.31

To quantify the binding strength, intrinsic binding constants (K_b) were calculated using the eqn (2):322where εa, εf and εb correspond to the apparent, free, and fully bound extinction coefficients, respectively.33

The calculated Kb values were 4.8 × 103 L mol−1 for deferiprone and 2.1 × 105 L mol−1 for the copper complex. The relatively low binding constant of deferiprone is characteristic of groove-binding agents. In contrast, coordination to copper dramatically enhanced DNA affinity, increasing Kb by approximately 44-fold. This enhancement can be attributed to several synergistic effects: (i) increased planarity and rigidity upon metal coordination, (ii) the presence of the aromatic 1, 10-phenanthroline moiety capable of π–π interactions, and (iii) the positive charge density around the metal center, which strengthens electrostatic attraction to the negatively charged DNA phosphate backbone.

Although the Kb value of the copper complex (105 order of magnitude) remains lower than that of the classical intercalator ethidium bromide (Kb ≈ 107 L mol−1),34 it is comparable to other phenanthroline-based copper complexes reported to exhibit partial intercalative behavior (Kb ≈ 105–106 L mol−1).35,36 The combination of pronounced hyperchromism and moderately high binding constant suggests a mixed binding mode, where groove binding may coexist with partial intercalative contributions mediated by the phenanthroline ring system.

Given that UV-vis spectroscopy alone cannot definitively distinguish between groove binding and intercalation, complementary viscosity and fluorescence displacement assays were conducted to further elucidate the binding mechanism of the copper(ii) complex with CT-DNA.

Viscosity investigations

Viscosity measurements are widely regarded as one of the most reliable hydrodynamic techniques for elucidating the binding mode of small molecules to DNA, particularly in the absence of crystallographic data. Unlike spectroscopic methods, viscosity experiments directly reflect changes in the effective hydrodynamic length of the DNA helix (L/L0), thereby providing mechanistic insight into structural perturbations induced by ligand binding.

The relative viscosity of CT-DNA in the presence of increasing concentrations of deferiprone and its copper complex was evaluated and expressed as (η/η0)1/3versus the ratio of compound to DNA concentration (Fig. 6), where η and η0 represent the viscosities of DNA with and without compound, respectively. The cubic root relationship correlates viscosity changes with variations in the contour length of the DNA duplex.

Effect of appending amounts of deferiprone and the copper complex (from 0 to 10−4 mol L−1) on the viscosity of CT-DNA ([DNA] = 5 × 10−5 mol L−1) at 298 K.

Classical intercalators insert between adjacent base pairs, causing base pair separation and an extension of the DNA helix. This elongation increases the hydrodynamic length (L > L0) and results in a marked increase in relative viscosity.37 In contrast, groove-binding agents interact along the minor or major groove without significantly altering the axial length of DNA, typically producing negligible changes or slight fluctuations in viscosity.

As shown in Fig. 6, deferiprone induced almost no change in the relative viscosity of CT-DNA over the investigated concentration range, indicating that its interaction does not appreciably extend the DNA helix. This observation strongly supports a groove-binding mode, in agreement with the hyperchromic behavior observed in UV-vis studies.

In contrast, the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex produced a pronounced and concentration-dependent increase in relative viscosity. The substantial rise in (η/η0)1/3 suggests elongation of the DNA duplex, consistent with an intercalative binding mechanism. The enhancement in hydrodynamic length indicates that the complex inserts, at least partially, between adjacent base pairs, disrupting π–π stacking interactions and increasing base pair separation.

The transformation in binding behavior upon metal coordination can be rationalized by structural and electronic factors. Coordination to copper(ii) enhances molecular rigidity and planarity, both of which favor intercalation. Furthermore, the extended π-conjugated aromatic system of the 1, 10-phenanthroline co-ligand significantly facilitates π–π stacking interactions with nucleobases.38 The positive charge density associated with the metal center may also strengthen electrostatic attraction toward the negatively charged phosphate backbone, stabilizing the intercalated state.

Overall, the viscosity results, together with the spectroscopic data, indicate that free deferiprone predominantly interacts through groove binding, whereas complexation with copper(ii) induces a substantial shift toward an intercalative or mixed intercalation-dominant binding mode.

Competitive fluorescence studies for elucidation of binding mode

Absorption titration and viscosity measurements indicated strong interaction between the studied compounds and CT-DNA, and suggested a coordination-induced transformation in binding mode. To further clarify the nature of these interactions, competitive fluorescence displacement assays were performed using two well-established DNA-binding probes with distinct and known binding modes.

The underlying principle of competitive fluorescence spectroscopy is that if a test compound displaces a DNA-bound fluorescent probe, it likely shares a similar binding mode. Changes in fluorescence intensity of the probe-DNA system upon addition of a competing molecule therefore provide valuable mechanistic insight.

EB displacement assay (intercalation probe)

Ethidium bromide (EB) is a classical intercalator whose fluorescence intensity increases markedly upon insertion between adjacent DNA base pairs due to restriction of its rotational freedom.39 If another intercalative ligand competes with EB for binding sites, displacement of EB results in fluorescence quenching.

As shown in Fig. 7b and d, addition of deferiprone to the EB-DNA system produced negligible changes in fluorescence intensity, indicating minimal competition with EB and therefore lack of significant intercalative interaction. In contrast, progressive addition of the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex caused a pronounced decrease in EB fluorescence, demonstrating effective displacement of EB from DNA base pairs.

At the maximum tested concentration (0–0.58 × 10−4 M), the EB displacement percentages were 8% for deferiprone and 57% for the copper complex, clearly indicating that intercalation is the predominant binding mode for the metal complex.

Hoechst 33 258 displacement assay (minor groove probe)

Hoechst 33 258 is a well-known minor groove binder that selectively binds to AT-rich regions of double-stranded DNA, exhibiting enhanced fluorescence upon binding.40 Displacement of Hoechst by a competing ligand suggests groove-binding interaction.

As illustrated in Fig. 7a and c, addition of deferiprone to the Hoechst–DNA system resulted in a substantial decrease in fluorescence intensity, corresponding to a 54% displacement (0–0.44 × 10−4 M). This significant quenching confirms that deferiprone effectively competes for minor groove binding sites.

Conversely, the copper complex induced only a minor reduction in Hoechst fluorescence (5% displacement), indicating weak competition for groove sites.

Mechanistic interpretation

The competitive fluorescence data strongly corroborate the conclusions drawn from UV-vis and viscosity experiments. Free deferiprone preferentially binds to CT-DNA via a minor groove binding mode, as evidenced by significant Hoechst displacement and negligible EB displacement.

Upon coordination to copper(ii), however, the binding mode shifts dramatically toward intercalation. The copper complex efficiently displaces EB while exhibiting minimal competition with Hoechst, consistent with an intercalation-dominant mechanism. This transformation can be attributed to increased molecular planarity and rigidity upon metal coordination, along with the extended π-conjugated system of the 1, 10-phenanthroline co-ligand, which facilitates π–π stacking interactions with DNA base pairs.

Collectively, the competitive fluorescence studies provide compelling mechanistic evidence that coordination to copper(ii) induces a distinct shift from groove binding (free ligand) to predominantly intercalative interaction (metal complex), in excellent agreement with spectroscopic and hydrodynamic findings.

Cell viability assay

The in vitro cytotoxicity of the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex was evaluated using the MTT assay against three human cancer cell lines, HT-29 (colon adenocarcinoma), HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), and MDA-MB-468 (breast carcinoma), as well as one normal human embryonic kidney cell line (HEK-293). The cytotoxicity of free deferiprone was not re-evaluated in this study, as its antiproliferative effects have been previously reported in several cell lines.41–44 For comparative purposes, four clinically used anticancer drugs—cisplatin, oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), and doxorubicin—were tested under identical experimental conditions.

Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of the copper complex and reference drugs for 48 h. The IC50 values derived from dose–response curves are summarized in Table 5, and the corresponding inhibition curves are presented in Fig. 8. As shown, all tested compounds exhibited dose-dependent growth inhibition.

The copper complex demonstrated remarkable cytotoxic potency, particularly against HepG2 and HT-29 cell lines. The IC50 values were 0.099 ± 0.073 µM for HepG2 and 3.697 ± 1.217 µM for HT-29 cells, indicating significantly stronger activity than all four reference drugs under the same conditions. In comparison, previously reported IC50 values for deferiprone against HT-29 cells were substantially higher (123.1 µM), confirming that coordination to copper dramatically enhances its antiproliferative activity.41 Similarly, deferiprone has been shown to inhibit HepG2 proliferation,42 yet the present results demonstrate the clear superiority of the copper complex over the free ligand.

In MDA-MB-468 cells, the copper complex (IC50 = 1.439 ± 1.245 µM) exhibited greater cytotoxicity than cisplatin (>100 µM), oxaliplatin (2.403 ± 1.572 µM), and 5-FU (50.65 ± 1.240 µM), and displayed activity comparable to doxorubicin (1.226 ± 1.208 µM).

The cytotoxic profile in normal HEK-293 cells revealed an IC50 value of 1.987 ± 1.167 µM for the copper complex. Although the complex showed measurable toxicity toward normal cells, its activity was comparable to doxorubicin and lower than that of oxaliplatin and 5-FU under identical conditions. Importantly, at a concentration of 1 µM, the complex induced significant growth inhibition in HepG2 cells while exhibiting relatively reduced toxicity toward HEK-293 cells compared to the standard drugs.

The obtained cytotoxicity results demonstrated that the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex exhibited substantial antiproliferative activity against all investigated cancer cell lines, with the highest potency observed toward HepG2 cells (IC50 = 0.099), followed by MDA-MB-468 (1.439) and HT-29 (3.697) cells. To better assess the biological performance of the synthesized complex, its activity was compared with previously reported Cu(ii)–phenanthroline systems.

Copper-based complexes have emerged as promising alternatives to platinum drugs because of their structural versatility, redox activity, and ability to target multiple intracellular pathways.45–47 In particular, Cu(ii) complexes containing 1,10-phenanthroline have attracted considerable interest as anticancer agents owing to the planar aromatic scaffold of phenanthroline, which facilitates biomolecular interactions and frequently enhances cytotoxicity.48

Ramakrishnan et al. reported ternary Cu(ii) complexes containing l-tyrosine and diimine ligands that displayed notable anticancer activity and DNA interaction properties, demonstrating the beneficial effect of mixed-ligand design in Cu-based systems.49 Similarly, several Cu–phenanthroline complexes have been reported to exhibit significant cytotoxicity in the micromolar range. For example, the demethylcantharidate-bridged Cu(ii)–phenanthroline complex described by Yin et al. exhibited antitumor activity against cancer cells with IC50 values in the micromolar domain.50 Structural modification of the phenanthroline framework has also been shown to markedly influence biological activity; phosphinyl-functionalized Cu(ii)–phenanthroline derivatives displayed enhanced cytotoxicity compared with non-functionalized analogues, highlighting the critical role of ancillary ligands in determining biological response.51 Likewise, mixed-ligand Cu(ii) complexes incorporating phenanthroline with quinoline-, quinoxaline-, and quinazoline-based ligands demonstrated pronounced cytotoxicity toward tumor cells, further supporting ligand engineering as an effective strategy to optimize anticancer performance.52 More recently, a Cu(ii)–phenanthroline complex containing 4-fluorophenoxyacetic acid hydrazide was reported to possess DNA-cleaving properties and selective cytotoxic activity toward cancer cells.53

Compared with these previously reported Cu–phen systems, the present copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex exhibited remarkably enhanced activity, particularly against HepG2 cells, where submicromolar cytotoxicity was achieved (IC50 = 0.099 µM). The observed improvement suggests that incorporation of deferiprone as a biologically active co-ligand may positively modulate the anticancer performance of the Cu–phen framework, providing a competitive advantage relative to several previously reported Cu(ii)–phenanthroline complexes. Based on these observations, a concentration of 1 µM and the HepG2 cell line were selected for subsequent mechanistic investigations.

Overall, the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex emerges as a promising candidate for further development as a potential anticancer agent, particularly against human hepatocellular carcinoma.

Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis and cell cycle distribution

Induction of apoptosis is a critical mechanism underlying the therapeutic efficacy of anticancer agents, as programmed cell death eliminates malignant cells without provoking severe inflammatory responses.54 To determine whether the cytotoxicity of the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex toward HepG2 cells is mediated through apoptosis, Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) dual staining was performed followed by flow cytometric analysis.

After treatment with 1 µM of the copper complex for 48 h, a marked increase in apoptotic cell population was observed compared to untreated controls (Fig. 9a). The percentage of late apoptotic cells increased to 26.4%, accompanied by a measurable elevation in early apoptotic cells (9.84%). This shift from viable to apoptotic quadrants clearly demonstrates that the complex induces apoptosis as a major mode of cell death in HepG2 cells rather than causing nonspecific necrosis. The predominance of late apoptotic cells suggests progression of apoptosis following prolonged exposure to the complex.

(a) The inhibitory impact of the copper complex on Hep G2 cell apoptosis, (b) effects of copper complex on cell cycle repartition in Hep G2 cells.

To further elucidate the antiproliferative mechanism, cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry using PI-stained nuclei. Treatment of HepG2 cells with 1 µM of the copper complex for 48 h resulted in noticeable alterations in cell cycle phase distribution (Fig. 9b). Specifically, an accumulation of cells in the G1 phase was observed (49.84% vs. 47.91% in control), accompanied by a reduction in the S phase (26.46% vs. 28.11%) and G2 phase populations (13.17% vs. 14.25%).

These findings indicate that the copper complex induces G1 phase arrest, thereby inhibiting progression from G1 to S phase and ultimately suppressing DNA replication and cell proliferation. G1 arrest is often associated with activation of cell cycle checkpoints in response to DNA damage or oxidative stress, mechanisms commonly reported for copper-based anticancer agents.

Interestingly, previous studies have shown that deferiprone alone induces S-phase arrest in HepG2 cells,42 whereas the present results demonstrate a shift toward G1 arrest upon coordination to copper. This alteration in cell cycle modulation further supports the notion that metal coordination substantially modifies the biological activity and mechanistic profile of the ligand.

Collectively, the apoptosis induction and G1 phase arrest observed in HepG2 cells suggest that the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex exerts its antiproliferative effects through activation of programmed cell death pathways coupled with inhibition of cell cycle progression.

Regulation of apoptosis-related gene expression in HepG2 cells

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying apoptosis induction by the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex, the mRNA expression levels of key apoptosis-related genes—including BCL2, BAX, and caspase-3—were evaluated in HepG2 cells following treatment. In addition, the BAX/BCL2 ratio, a critical determinant of mitochondrial apoptotic signaling, was calculated.

Caspases are a family of cysteine proteases that play central roles in the execution of programmed cell death. Among them, caspase-3 functions as a key effector caspase, mediating both initiation and execution phases of apoptosis through cleavage of essential cellular substrates.55

Members of the BCL2 protein family tightly regulate the intrinsic (mitochondrial) apoptotic pathway. BCL2 acts as an anti-apoptotic protein by stabilizing the mitochondrial outer membrane and preventing the release of pro-apoptotic factors. In contrast, BAX is a pro-apoptotic member that promotes mitochondrial membrane permeabilization. Overexpression of BCL2 and suppression of BAX are commonly observed in drug-resistant tumor cells, contributing to apoptosis evasion. Conversely, up-regulation of BAX and down-regulation of BCL2 shift the intracellular balance toward apoptosis induction.56

As shown in Fig. 10, treatment of HepG2 cells with the copper complex resulted in significant up-regulation of BAX and caspase-3 mRNA expression levels, accompanied by a marked down-regulation of BCL2 compared to untreated control cells. Consequently, the BAX/BCL2 ratio was significantly increased.

Expression of apoptosis related genes upon the copper complex treatment. RT-PCR analysis of BAX, BCL2, caspse3 and BAX/BCL2 ratio on Hep G2 cells.

The elevated BAX/BCL2 ratio is a well-established indicator of mitochondrial pathway activation. An increased ratio promotes disruption of mitochondrial membrane potential, facilitating cytochrome c release into the cytosol and subsequent activation of downstream caspase cascades, ultimately leading to apoptotic cell death.56

These findings strongly support that the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex induces apoptosis in HepG2 cells predominantly through activation of the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, in agreement with the Annexin V/PI and cell cycle analyses. The coordinated modulation of BAX, BCL2, and caspase-3 further confirms that apoptosis induction is a regulated and gene-mediated process rather than nonspecific cytotoxicity.

Molecular docking studies

Coronaviruses possess a positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome of approximately 30 kb encoding structural, non-structural, and accessory proteins essential for viral entry, replication, and assembly.57 Structural proteins include the Spike (S), Envelope (E), Membrane (M), and Nucleocapsid (N) proteins. Viral entry into host cells is mediated by the Spike glycoprotein, while the E and M proteins play critical roles in virion assembly and morphogenesis. The N protein is located within the viral capsid and is responsible for RNA binding, replication, and transcription regulation.

In addition to structural proteins, several non-structural proteins (nsps) are indispensable for viral replication. Among them, the main protease (3CLpro, nsp5), papain-like protease (PLpro, nsp3), RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp, nsp12) in complex with cofactors nsp7 and nsp8, and helicase (nsp13) are considered promising antiviral drug targets due to their essential roles in the replication–transcription complex.

To evaluate the potential anti-SARS-CoV-2 activity of deferiprone and its copper complex, molecular docking studies were performed against four key viral targets:

• The main protease (Mpro, PDB ID: 6LU7).

• The papain-like protease (PLpro, PDB ID: 6W9C).

• The nucleocapsid protein N-terminal RNA-binding domain (PDB ID: 6 M3M).

• The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase complex (RdRp, PDB ID: 6M71).

For each ligand–protein pair, 100 docking runs were performed, and the conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected for analysis. The docking poses and interaction profiles are illustrated in Fig. 11 and 12.

The copper complex exhibited significantly stronger affinity (−8.28 kcal mol−1), forming interactions with catalytically relevant residues such as Met165, Glu166, Pro168, Arg188, Gln189, Thr190, and Gln192.

Deferiprone interacted with Ala68, Tyr72, Phe79, and Leu80 (−5.36 kcal mol−1), whereas the copper complex formed multiple interactions—including contacts with Asn109, Leu162, Gln269, and Cys270—achieving the highest binding energy among the studied targets (−8.56 kcal mol−1).

Comparative binding affinity

Across all four targets, the copper complex consistently exhibited stronger binding affinities (−8.06 to −8.56 kcal mol−1) compared to free deferiprone (−4.74 to −5.41 kcal mol−1).

Notably, the binding energies of the copper complex toward RdRp are comparable to or stronger than several reported antiviral agents, including chloroquine (−5.1 kcal mol−1), galidesivir (−7.0 kcal mol−1), remdesivir (−7.6 kcal mol−1), tenofovir (−6.9 kcal mol−1), sofosbuvir (−7.5 kcal mol−1), and ribavirin (−7.8 kcal mol−1).58,59

Mechanistic implications

The consistently enhanced binding affinity of the copper complex can be attributed to its increased molecular size, rigidity, extended π-conjugation, and potential for multiple hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Importantly, the ability of the complex to interact favorably with multiple essential viral proteins suggests potential multi-target inhibitory activity, which may reduce the likelihood of resistance development.

While these in silico results provide promising evidence for potential antiviral activity, experimental validation through enzymatic inhibition assays and cell-based antiviral studies is required to confirm the biological relevance of these interactions.

Overall, the molecular docking findings highlight the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex as a promising multi-target candidate for further investigation against SARS-CoV-2.

Conclusion

A novel water-soluble copper–deferiprone–1, 10-phenanthroline complex was synthesized and fully characterized in a triclinic crystal system. Spectroscopic analyses (UV-vis and fluorescence) confirmed strong interactions with CT-DNA, where deferiprone binds via a groove mode, while the copper complex primarily intercalates, as indicated by competitive displacement assays (EB: 8% vs. 57%; Hoechst 33 258: 54% vs. 5%).

In vitro cytotoxicity studies revealed remarkable anticancer activity of the copper complex against HT-29, Hep G2, and MDA-MB-468 cells, surpassing standard chemotherapeutics and deferiprone, while exhibiting moderate cytotoxicity toward HEK-293 normal cells. Flow cytometry demonstrated apoptosis induction predominantly at the late stage, accompanied by G1-phase cell cycle arrest in Hep G2 cells. Real-time PCR analysis confirmed up-regulation of pro-apoptotic genes (BAX, Caspase-3, and BAX/BCL2 ratio) and down-regulation of anti-apoptotic BCL2, supporting apoptosis-mediated cytotoxicity.

Molecular docking studies revealed strong multi-target binding of the copper complex to SARS-CoV-2 proteins, including Mpro (6LU7), PLpro (6W9C), N-protein N-terminal RNA-binding domain (6 M3M), and RdRp (6M71), with binding energies (−8.06 to −8.56 kcal mol−1) superior to several approved antivirals. Free deferiprone exhibited moderate binding (−4.74 to −5.41 kcal mol−1). These results suggest the copper complex can potentially inhibit multiple stages of viral replication.

Overall, the copper–deferiprone–phenanthroline complex demonstrates dual therapeutic potential as a potent anticancer agent and a promising SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor, warranting further preclinical evaluation for development as a multifunctional bioactive compound.
