A comprehensive assessment of the impact of COVID variant and vaccination on venous thromboembolism and mortality
Authors: Samuel P Soff, Richard Moffitt, Anjali Sharathkumar, Margaret A Hall, Rachel Wong, Yun J Yoo, Gerald A Soff, National Clinical Cohort Collaborative COVID-19 Enclave
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCOVID-19 is associated with increased mortality and morbidity. The mortality and adverse outcomes of current era COVID-19 variants have not been comprehensively studied.ObjectivesTo evaluate the association between COVID-19 variants and vaccination status on venous thromboembolism (VTE) and mortality rates, especially among recent strains, compared to a control group with acute respiratory infection (ARI).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the National Clinical Cohort Collaborative database. Inclusion criteria were adult patients with COVID-19 or ARI between March 1, 2020 and November 1, 2024. Cohorts were balanced on demographics, prior healthcare visits, prior anticoagulation/antiplatelet use, COVID-19 vaccination and comorbidities. Primary outcomes were 30-day VTE and mortality using multivariable regression analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to determine the duration of post-infectious VTE and mortality risk.ResultsA total of 756,217 patients with COVID-19 and 678,747 with ARI were included. Patients with COVID-19 had an increased risk of VTE (odds ratio [OR]: 1.45) and mortality (OR: 1.80) compared to the ARI cohort. However, risk was attenuated later in the pandemic, with similar risk between patients with COVID-19 and ARI in the Contemporary Omicron period (May 2022–Nov 2024), especially among vaccinated individuals. Vaccination was associated with a reduced odds of VTE (OR: 0.41) and mortality (OR: 0.41). The post-COVID elevated VTE and mortality risk plateaued after 8 weeks.ConclusionCOVID-19 was associated with increased risk of 30-day VTE and mortality compared to ARI, but was no longer significantly different during the contemporary period (2022–2024) of the pandemic. COVID-19 vaccination was associated with reduced VTE events and mortality.Keywords: COVID-19, vaccine, venous thrombosis

Introduction

In addition to increased risk of mortality, SARS-CoV-2 infection (COVID-19) is associated with systemic inflammation, a hypercoagulable state, and increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Post-COVID-19 VTE rates vary markedly by infection severity, with estimates ranging from 0.1% to 0.9% [5,6] in non-hospitalized patients and 5.2% to 23% in those hospitalized with COVID-19 [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. The risk of VTE is greatest shortly after infection; however, multiple studies suggest this elevated risk may persist beyond the acute phase [12,13]. Long COVID has been associated with a hypercoagulable state, potentially contributing to the persistent post-infectious VTE risk [14]. Compared to other respiratory infections such as influenza, COVID-19 is associated with a significantly higher rate of VTE, particularly in the outpatient setting [5,7,8,15]. This risk is further elevated among individuals with comorbidities such as older age, male sex, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes, heart failure, hypertension, prior VTE, and obesity [5,8,16].

Recent research has also examined the impact of COVID-19 variants on disease severity and VTE risk throughout the course of the pandemic [5,8,17]. The Delta variant was associated with the highest rates of ICU admissions and mortality [[17], [18], [19]], whereas the Omicron variant demonstrated milder disease and lower propensity for hypercoagulability [[20], [21], [22]]. Mortality rates have declined throughout the pandemic, with a 2-fold greater risk of 90-day post-COVID mortality in 2020 compared to infections in 2021–2022 [23]. Furthermore, during the 2023–2024 flu season, COVID-19 and influenza were associated with a similar risk of 30-day mortality [24]. This reduction in COVID-19 severity likely reflects both decreased virulence of later variants as well as increased vaccination [25].

COVID-19 vaccination has been associated with a substantial reduction in post-COVID mortality and thrombotic complications, with some studies reporting a 75% reduction in post-COVID VTE [17,[26], [27], [28], [29], [30]]. In the United States (US), the Moderna, Pfizer, and Janssen COVID-19 vaccines were widely utilized, and while the Moderna and Pfizer vaccines were associated with greater protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection and hospitalization compared to the Janssen vaccine, all 3 demonstrated meaningful efficacy compared to unvaccinated individuals [31,32]. Although there has been considerable public concern regarding a possible increased risk of thrombosis associated with vaccination, multiple studies demonstrated that the risk of acute VTE did not increase following administration of any of the 3 major vaccines [33,34].

Following the end of the Federal COVID-19 Public Health Emergency Declaration in May 2023, with reduced focus on COVID-19 [35,36] and lower rates of vaccination [37,38], it is essential to investigate how the risk of VTE varies across viral variants, particularly the contemporary Omicron variants. Additionally, understanding the duration of VTE risk and the impact of vaccination is critical for accurately assessing long-term risks and guiding clinical decisions in the post-pandemic era. This study aims to evaluate the impact of COVID-19 variants, including contemporary COVID-19 strains, and COVID-19 vaccination on the risk of VTE and mortality after acute infection, utilizing the National Clinical Cohort Collaborative (N3C) COVID-19 Enclave. Given the increase in risk of thrombosis after respiratory infection, individuals with acute respiratory infection (ARI) were selected as a control group to evaluate the COVID-19 specific risk of VTE after infection.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the N3C COVID-19 Enclave, a research database that includes electronic health record (EHR) data from over 60 US healthcare systems. A description of the N3C and the characteristics of the population in the database has been previously published [39,40]. We used a subset of N3C EHR records from the 12 healthcare sites with vaccination data where ≥50% of patient population was vaccinated against COVID-19 over the course of the pandemic. The Stony Brook University Office of Research Compliance determined that the study met criteria for exemption under 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4), IRB2021-00098 MOD025. Access to the data that support the findings of this study are currently paused but stored by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) in the N3C enclave and may become available at a future time.

COVID-19 and ARI cohorts

Inclusion criteria for the N3C cohort were 1) a positive laboratory test or diagnostic code for COVID-19 or ARI between March 1, 2020 and November 1, 2024; 2) age ≥18 years old at diagnosis, 3) at least one healthcare visit from a site with vaccination data where ≥50% of the patient population was vaccinated against COVID-19, and 4) no VTE diagnosis within 0 to 365 days prior to index date (Figure 1). See Supplementary Section S1a for the list of included ARI diagnoses. The index date was defined as the earliest diagnosis or positive test for COVID-19 or ARI. Patients diagnosed with both ARI and COVID-19 within a 30-day period were excluded from the analysis, while those diagnosed more than 30 days apart were assigned to the ARI or COVID cohort based on which diagnosis occurred first. Patients who were diagnosed with a VTE in the year prior to index date were excluded from the analysis, to prevent errors related to coding of pre-existing VTE as incident VTE [17] (Figure 1).

Cohort flow diagram. N3C, National COVID Cohort Collaborative; ARI, acute respiratory infection; VTE, venous thromboembolism.

To assess the impact of viral strains over time, the cohorts were split into 5 time periods corresponding with the predominant COVID variant as per genomic surveillance studies: Ancestral (March 1, 2020 to December 31, 2020), Alpha (January 1, 2021 to May 31, 2021), Delta (June 1, 2021 to November 30, 2021), Omicron BA-1 (December 1, 2021 to April 30, 2022) and Contemporary Omicron Variants (May 1, 2022 to November 1, 2024) [[41], [42], [43]]. Due to numerous rapidly changing Omicron sub-variants from 2022 to 2024, patients were grouped into a single cohort of Contemporary Variants [24,43].

Outcomes and covariates

The primary outcomes were development of 1) VTE and/or 2) mortality within 30 days following the index date. VTE was identified by diagnosis codes for pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis (Supplementary Section S1B for full list of codes), and mortality was determined from death information in the N3C. Demographics and comorbidities diagnosed in the year prior to index date were included. Gender, age, race and ethnicity information were identified as reported in the N3C. Patients were considered hospitalized at index infection if they had a hospital admission between 1 day prior to and 16 days following the index date. Vaccination was defined as the administration of any COVID-19 vaccine prior to index date. Patients who received antiplatelet drugs (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, cilostazole, prasugrel, or dipyridamole) or oral anticoagulants (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban or warfarin) in the 3 to 183 days prior to the index date were identified as recent use of these medications [8].

Statistical plan and analytics methods

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.9.15 and R 4.1.0. Analyses were based on data release version 187 (December 13, 2024) under Data Use Request-7958530 in Palantir’s Foundry platform (2021, Denver, CO), a secure analytics enclave housing the N3C data. A series of analyses were performed to compare the COVID and ARI cohorts, as well as the different variant time periods and effect of vaccination.

Analysis of risk in patients with COVID-19 versus ARI

The primary objective was to compare the risk of 30-day VTE and 30-day mortality between the COVID-19 and ARI cohorts, stratified by time period corresponding to the predominant circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants. For each time-period, the COVID and ARI cohorts were balanced using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on demographics, comorbidities, vaccination status, prior healthcare utilization, and prior antiplatelet or oral anticoagulation use. IPTW balancing was evaluated using Love Plots, with a standardized mean difference of <0.1 indicating effective balancing (Supplementary Section S2). For each variant period, multivariable logistic regression models estimated adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for 30-day VTE and mortality in the COVID cohort compared to ARI (Table 2). The analysis was repeated in vaccinated and unvaccinated cohorts separately, to further assess the association of vaccination on outcomes throughout the pandemic (Supplementary Section S3).

Analysis of risk based by COVID-19 variant

A multivariable analysis was conducted among patients in the COVID-19 cohort to evaluate the risk of VTE and mortality. Multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for demographics, comorbidities, vaccination status, antiplatelet and anticoagulation medications, was used to estimate the adjusted OR for 30-day VTE and 30-day mortality, with variant time period as the exposure variable (Figure 2A,B).

Analysis of the duration of post-infection risk

To assess the duration and trend for VTE and mortality risk post-infection, Kaplan–Meier curves at 180 days post-index date were used to compare vaccinated and unvaccinated patients with COVID-19 to the ARI cohort (Figure 3). Additionally, Schoenfeld residual analyses were utilized with a smoothed best-fit line of locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) to visualize changes in hazard ratios (HRs) over the 90-day post-infection period (Supplementary Figure S4). Results

Overall cohort

A total of 756,217 patients with COVID-19 and 678,747 with ARI were included. Demographics, comorbidities, and other baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Within the COVID-19 cohort, 21.2% were infected during the Ancestral period (N = 160,437), 10.4% during Alpha (N = 78,306), 11.5% Delta (N = 86,952), 20.3% Omicron BA-1 (N = 153,244) and 36.7% during the Contemporary Omicron (N = 277,278) period. The median age of the COVID-19 cohort was 50 years, and 56.6% were female. The cohort was 65% White, 16% Black or African American, 3.9% Asian, and 9.1% Hispanic. Compared to earlier periods, the Contemporary COVID-19 cohort was older, more predominantly white, and had higher rates of comorbidities such as heart disease, kidney disease, and cancer.

COVID-19 vaccination rates increased throughout the pandemic: only 0.2% of the Ancestral COVID Cohort was vaccinated, compared to 10.9%, 37.3%, 60.3%, and 78.4% during the Alpha, Delta, Omicron BA-1 and Contemporary Omicron periods, respectively. The 30-day VTE rate and 30-day mortality rate among hospitalized patients were 1.9% and 8.8% for patients with COVID-19 compared to 1.5% and 6.8% for hospitalized patients with ARI. Non-hospitalized patients with COVID-19 had 0.1% rate of VTE and 0.4% mortality within 30 days, compared to 0.1% and 0.1% for non-hospitalized patients with ARI (Table 1).

Comparison of COVID-19 and ARI cohorts

COVID-19 and ARI cohorts were well balanced with standardized mean differences <0.1 (Supplementary Section S2). Compared to ARI, infection with SARS-CoV-2 was associated with an increased risk of 30-day VTE (OR: 1.45; 95% CI, 1.37-1.52) and 30-day mortality (OR: 1.80; 95% CI, 1.75-1.86) over the course of the pandemic. These risks fluctuated throughout the pandemic, and subsequently declined during the Omicron periods, especially among vaccinated patients (Supplementary Section S3). During the Contemporary Omicron period, there was no difference in 30-day VTE (OR: 1.01; 95% CI, 0.92-1.10), and only a slight difference in 30-day mortality (OR: 1.09; 95% CI, 1.04-1.14) compared to ARI (Table 2).

The increased risk of VTE and mortality associated with COVID-19 was more pronounced among non-hospitalized patients (VTE OR: 1.63; 95% CI, 1.50-1.77; mortality OR: 2.90; 95% CI, 2.74-3.07) than hospitalized patients (VTE OR: 1.29; 95% CI, 1.21-1.37; mortality OR: 1.50; 95% CI, 1.45-1.55). Patients with COVID-19 were also more likely to be hospitalized than ARI patients (OR: 2.28; 95% CI, 2.25-2.31) (data not shown).

Vaccination was associated with a decreased risk difference between COVID-19 and ARI cohorts. Among vaccinated individuals, the risk of VTE and mortality in COVID-19 compared to ARI was reduced (VTE OR: 1.10; 95% CI, 1.01-1.19; mortality OR: 1.24; 95% CI, 1.18-1.30), while COVID-19 in non-vaccinated individuals was associated with more severe outcomes compared to non-vaccinated ARI patients (VTE OR: 1.79; 95% CI, 1.66-1.92; mortality OR: 2.32; 95% CI, 2.24-2.41) (Table 2).

Vaccination analyses

The risk of 30-day VTE and mortality was assessed between balanced vaccinated and unvaccinated cohorts as shown in Table 3. Among all COVID patients, and within each variant period, vaccination was consistently associated with a reduced risk of VTE (OR: 0.41; 95% CI, 0.38-0.44) and mortality (OR: 0.41; 95% CI, 0.39-0.42) compared to non-vaccinated patients. In the ARI control cohort, COVID-19 vaccination was associated with a lower 30-day mortality rate, but not reduced 30-day VTE rate.

Association of COVID-19 variant with outcomes

The multivariable logistic regression analysis among COVID-19 patients comparing 30-day VTE and mortality across the 5 variant periods is shown in Figure 2. For 30-day VTE: compared to the Ancestral variant, Delta posed the highest risk (OR: 1.55; 95% CI, 1.36-1.76), followed by Alpha (OR: 1.21; 95% CI, 1.06-1.38), Omicron BA-1 (OR: 1.12; 95% CI, 0.98-1.28), and Contemporary Omicron (OR: 0.75; 95% CI, 0.65-0.86) (Figure 2A). Other predictors of VTE included hospitalization, male gender, Black or African American patients, cancer, and prior oral anticoagulation use. Asian race, younger age, and vaccination was associated with a reduced risk of VTE. For 30-day mortality in patients with COVID-19, compared to the Ancestral variant, Delta variant again was associated with the highest risk (OR: 1.31; 95% CI, 1.22-1.40), followed by Omicron BA-1 (OR: 1.02; 95% CI, 0.95-1.09), Alpha (OR: 0.87; 95% CI, 0.81-0.94), and Contemporary Omicron (OR: 0.57; 95% CI, 0.54-0.61) (Figure 2B). Comorbidities such as liver disease, heart disease, kidney disease, cancer, diabetes and lung disease were associated with increased mortality. Similar to its effect on VTE, hospitalization was associated with an increase in mortality, as was older age and male gender. COVID-19 vaccination was associated with a reduction in VTE (OR: 0.69; 95% CI, 0.62-0.77) and mortality (OR: 0.40; 95% CI, 0.37-0.42) across the entire COVID-19 cohort (Figure 2).

Duration of thrombotic and mortality risk

The analysis on duration of risk post-infection, with 180-day follow up for VTE and mortality is shown in Figure 3. Kaplan–Meier curves comparing vaccinated and unvaccinated patients with COVID-19 to those with ARI demonstrated that vaccinated patients had a VTE and mortality risk similar to ARI, while unvaccinated patients had significantly worse outcomes. The increased VTE and mortality among the unvaccinated COVID-19 cohort was most significant within the first 60 days post-infection. Beyond 60 days, the rates of VTE and mortality plateaued, and were similar to that of the vaccinated COVID-19 and the ARI cohorts. Schoenfeld residual analyses supported this temporal trend, showing risk convergence around day 60 for VTE and day 80 for mortality (Supplementary Figure S4).

Discussion

This large multicenter database-driven study comprehensively analyzes post-COVID-19 risk of VTE and mortality across different variant periods, in comparison to non-COVID-19 ARI, and the therapeutic effect of vaccination. Leveraging a robust nationwide database, we assessed how the duration and severity of COVID-related thrombotic and mortality risk evolved from March 2020 through November 2024.

COVID versus ARI

Consistent with findings of overall risk of VTE and mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic, we found that COVID-19 was associated with a higher odds (OR: 1.45; 95% CI, 1.37-1.52) of 30-day VTE, and a higher odds (OR: 1.80; 95% CI, 1.75-1.86) of 30-day mortality compared to ARI [5,7,8,15]. The difference in outcomes between COVID-19 and ARI patients was even greater in the outpatient setting compared to hospitalized patients. These findings were supported by 2 similar studies, in which Lo Re III et al. found COVID-19, compared to influenza, was associated with a weighted HR of 1.89 for VTE among hospitalized patients, and weighted HR of 2.86 for VTE among ambulatory patients [5,8]. The underlying mechanisms of this prothrombotic state are likely multifactorial, involving the binding of SARS-CoV-2 to the vascular endothelium and disruption of vascular integrity [44,45], enhanced thrombin activation, platelet activation, and tissue inflammation [4,46,47].

COVID variants

The elevated risk of VTE and mortality associated with COVID-19 declined over the course of the pandemic. During the Ancestral, Alpha, Delta, and Omicron BA-1 periods (March 2020 to April 2022), COVID-19 was associated with a significantly higher risk of VTE and mortality compared to ARI. However, during the Contemporary Omicron period (May 2022 to November 2024), COVID-19 outcomes were not clinically different from those with ARI, especially among individuals vaccinated against COVID-19. These findings are supported by previous studies showing decreased severity with Omicron variants [[20], [21], [22]], and recent reports suggesting comparable mortality between COVID-19 and ARI in 2022–2024 [24,48]. This decline in severity is likely multifactorial, with reduced virulence of emerging variants, increases in population immunity from vaccination and prior infection, and improvements in clinical care, such as increased use of antiviral treatments [23,[48], [49], [50]]. Clinically, this suggests that the risk of VTE associated with COVID-19 is no longer elevated, a significant shift from findings early in the pandemic.

Independent of variant period, several factors were associated with higher risk of VTE and mortality including: older age, male gender, Black or African American race, hospitalization, and cancer, consistent with findings from other studies [5,8]. Notably, prior oral anticoagulation use was associated with an increased risk of VTE among COVID-19 patients, possibly reflecting underlying thrombotic predispositions not fully captured in the data, and has been observed in other studies as well [8,51].

Duration of increased risk

Analysis of 180-day event-free survival curves demonstrated that post-COVID-19 risk of VTE and mortality was most severe in the first few weeks post-infection, but beyond 60 days, rates were similar between non-vaccinated and vaccinated COVID and ARI cohorts, suggesting a return to baseline. Schoenfeld residual analyses similarly indicate that excess risk is most severe in the early post-infection period, with risk declining by approximately 60 days. These findings are consistent with prior reports suggesting that the thrombotic risk from COVID-19 returns to baseline after about 6 weeks [12]; however, some studies have observed a prolonged risk beyond 56 days [12,13]. As a sequelae of COVID-19 that persists beyond 4 weeks, our findings can be considered a manifestation of Long COVID, which has been associated with a persistent prothrombotic state, with ongoing endothelial activation, microclot formation, and chronic inflammation for months after acute infection [14,52,53]. Clinically, our results suggest that while the increased risk of VTE and mortality following COVID-19 may be most pronounced in the early weeks, the risk stabilizes by approximately 8 weeks, with vaccination potentially mitigating longer-term complications, thereby informing strategies for monitoring and management of post-infection risks.

Effect of vaccination

Multiple independent analyses demonstrated that COVID-19 vaccination was associated with improved outcomes. Vaccination was associated with an approximately 60% reduction in both VTE and mortality among COVID-19 patients throughout the pandemic. Logistic regression among vaccinated patients, as well as Kaplan–Meier event free survival curves, further suggested that vaccination appeared to attenuate the difference in severity between COVID-19 and ARI, especially in the Contemporary Omicron period. These findings are supported by multiple studies demonstrating that vaccination reduces severe outcomes following breakthrough infection [17,[26], [27], [28], [29], [30]]. Our data strongly suggest that vaccination was associated with a significant reduction in VTE and mortality among COVID patients at all variant time periods. Additionally, among vaccinated individuals, current era COVID-19 is not clinically different from non-COVID-19 respiratory infections.

Interestingly, even in the ARI control group, COVID-19 vaccination was associated with lower mortality. This may reflect unaccounted confounding, such as health seeking behavior, undiagnosed COVID infections in ARI patients, or co-vaccination with influenza vaccines. Prior studies have similarly reported non-COVID-19 protective effects associated with COVID-19 vaccination, including reduced non-COVID-19 mortality (OR: 0.34 among patients vaccinated with Pfizer vaccine compared to non-vaccinated patients) [54], and lower risk of hospitalization from non-COVID-19 pneumonia, stroke, heart failure, and dementia [55]; however, the underlying cause of this association is poorly understood.

Limitations and conclusion

Our study had several limitations. We excluded patients who had a prior history of VTE, as we would not have been able to reliably differentiate new from pre-existing VTE. This likely led to an underestimation of thrombotic risk, and does not allow for the determination of the VTE risk in patients with a history of previous VTE. The study cohort represents a subset of the N3C patients at sites with adequate vaccination data, and thus may be biased toward patients who were seen at primarily academic centers. Duration of hospitalization declined over the course of the pandemic [56], and longer hospital stays—reflecting greater disease severity and evolving institutional practices [56]—may have independently contributed to VTE/mortality risk [57].

Prior use of direct oral anticoagulants was included as a covariate to adjust for underlying hypercoagulability. Since low molecular weight heparin were omitted to avoid inclusion of short-term inpatient prophylaxis, this may limit the generalizability of our findings. Assessing the impact of vaccines is also challenging due to significant missingness in vaccination data, limiting the reliability of analyses. Similarly, influenza vaccination was not included in our analysis, as they are frequently administered in nonclinical settings (ie, pharmacies) and are therefore incompletely captured in the EHR, leading to potential exposure misclassification. Furthermore, the positive effect of vaccination may be partially confounded by health-seeking behavior, as individuals who are proactive about their health may be more likely to get vaccinated. Ascertainment of COVID-19 and the primary outcome of VTE in EHR data is often limited, including individuals who were diagnosed through home testing, developed a VTE but went undiagnosed or were diagnosed in another health system.

This study provides a contemporary assessment of post-COVID-19 VTE and mortality risk in comparison to ARI. We found that COVID-19 was associated with a 45% increased risk of 30-day VTE and 80% increased risk of 30-day mortality compared to non-COVID-19 acute respiratory infections. This elevated risk was most pronounced early in the pandemic and declined with the emergence of late Omicron variants. The elevated risk of VTE and mortality associated with COVID-19 persisted for approximately 8 weeks post-infection. Vaccination significantly reduced the risk of VTE and mortality in COVID-19 patients. COVID vaccination was associated with a 59% reduced odds of both VTE and mortality within 30 days post-COVID-19. As of 2024, the clinical risk profile of COVID-19 has converged with that of other acute respiratory infections, particularly among vaccinated individuals.
