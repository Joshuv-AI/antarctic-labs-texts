Case report: neuroimaging in Cockayne syndrome
Authors: Shallini Mittal, Krishan Kumar Jain, Sameer Arora, Nikita Jajodia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We report the neuroimaging findings of an 11 year old female with Cockayne Syndrome (CS). It is important to address CS so as to help in evolution of neuroimaging.

Introduction

Cockayne Syndrome (CS) is an uncommon genetic disorder transmitted in an autosomal recessive manner. It is characterized by progressive neurodegeneration, growth impairment, and photosensitivity. Neuroimaging, particularly MRI, is crucial in diagnosing CS and tracking its progression. Typical MRI findings in CS include cerebellar atrophy, white matter abnormalities, and ventricular enlargement. This case report discusses the neuroimaging findings of a patient with CS and compares them with previous studies to provide a more comprehensive understanding of CS-related brain changes.

Clinical case presentation

We present a case of an 11-year-old female with a clinical history of growth delay and tremors in all 4 limbs, along with head tremors and difficulty walking. The patient had been previously referred to the radiology department for further evaluation due to concerns about growth failure and neurological decline. The patient's previous treatments included growth hormone therapy and steroid use at the ages of 1.5 months and 6 years, respectively. On neurological clinical examination, she exhibited gait ataxia and slurred & delayed speech. Her palate was found to be high-arched, and dwarfism was noted during the examination. The clinical presentation of Cockayne Syndrome is highly variable and usually involves a combination of neurological, growth-related, and photosensitivity symptoms. Neurological manifestations include developmental delays, dementia, motor dysfunction, and sensorineural hearing loss. Growth failure is commonly observed, leading to short stature and dwarfism, as seen in our patient. Progressive neurodegeneration often leads to gait ataxia, tremors, and balance issues, with patients typically developing symptoms in the first few years of life. Photosensitivity is a hallmark of the disease, where patients experience skin damage after exposure to ultraviolet light. Other features may include dysphagia, ocular issues, and renal impairment. These symptoms are typically progressive, with a decline in motor and cognitive functions over time, leading to severe disability and, in many cases, premature death.

Neuroimaging features of Cockayne syndrome

MRI characteristics in Cockayne syndrome type I

Boltshauser et al. [1] first described the typical radiological features of CS Type I, which include brainstem atrophy, cerebellar atrophy, and white matter lesions. These early MRI findings were significant in diagnosing CS, as they correlated with the patient's neurological and developmental symptoms. Structural abnormalities on MRI, when considered alongside clinical presentation, played a crucial role in the diagnostic process of CS patients.

Advanced MRI modalities: diffusion tensor imaging and volumetric MRI

In a study by Koob et al. [2], diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and volumetric MRI were used to assess white matter integrity in CS patients. They observed white matter signal changes, particularly in the frontal and parietal lobes, along with cerebellar atrophy. These advanced imaging modalities provide an in-depth view of microstructural changes and have enhanced the understanding of white matter degeneration in CS.

White matter changes and neurocognitive decline

Sugita et al. [3] explored the relationship between white matter changes on MRI and neurocognitive decline in CS patients. They found a direct association between white matter hyperintensities in the frontal and temporal lobes and cognitive impairments. This finding highlights the significance of neuroimaging not only in diagnosing CS but also in monitoring cognitive function and neurological decline.

MRI findings in adult-onset Cockayne syndrome

While CS is often diagnosed in childhood, Cocco et al. [4] described cases where CS presented in adulthood. Despite the later onset of symptoms, MRI findings in adult-onset CS were similar to those observed in pediatric cases, including ventricular enlargement, cerebellar atrophy, and white matter changes. This suggests that MRI remains a critical tool for diagnosis, regardless of the patient’s age at symptom onset.

A rare case of Cockayne syndrome: MRI features

Mundaganur [5] documented a rare case of CS, where prominent cerebellar atrophy and white matter abnormalities were observed. These MRI features are consistent with the typical presentation of CS and further support the importance of MRI in diagnosing and tracking the disease.

Expanding clinical and mutational spectrum of Cockayne syndrome

Laugel [6] reviewed the genetic mutations associated with CS and discussed how these mutations influence radiological findings. Regardless of the specific genetic variant, cerebellar atrophy, ventricular enlargement, and white matter changes were commonly observed, although the severity of these abnormalities varied. MRI, alongside genetic testing, remains essential for diagnosing and understanding the progression of CS.

Diffuse white matter changes and MRI monitoring in CS

Koob et al. [7] further examined diffuse white matter changes in CS patients. Their volumetric MRI and DTI studies revealed progressive white matter degeneration, which correlated with the clinical severity of the disease. These findings underscore the importance of long-term monitoring through advanced neuroimaging techniques, which can assess disease progression and evaluate therapeutic interventions.

Cerebral and cerebellar atrophy in Cockayne syndrome type I

Boltshauser et al. [8] again emphasized the presence of brainstem atrophy and ventricular enlargement in early-stage CS Type I, which were essential for diagnosing CS at the time. MRI findings showed progressive cerebral atrophy, which mirrored the neurological decline seen in affected patients.

Discussion

The neuroimaging findings of Cockayne Syndrome are critical for early diagnosis and monitoring disease progression. MRI consistently reveals cerebellar atrophy, white matter degeneration, and ventricular enlargement in CS patients. These findings serve as a reliable tool in diagnosing the disease and distinguishing it from other neurodegenerative conditions.

The presence of white matter hyperintensities is a central feature of CS, particularly in the frontal and parietal lobes, which has been shown to correlate with cognitive impairments. Sugita et al. [3] demonstrated that these white matter changes are not only indicative of neurological decline but also have a neuropsychological impact, highlighting the necessity of MRI for both diagnosis and disease monitoring.

Cerebellar atrophy, observed in CS patients, often results in gait ataxia and balance issues, contributing to the tremors and motor dysfunction seen clinically. In the current case, the patient's gait disturbance and tremors align with the observed cerebellar atrophy on MRI. The calcifications seen in the right cerebellum on MRI are another unique feature of CS, confirming the disease’s presence in this patient.

The role of advanced neuroimaging techniques, such as DTI and volumetric MRI, enhances our understanding of the white matter degeneration that occurs in CS patients. These techniques offer a more precise view of microstructural damage, which often precedes the onset of clinical symptoms. The use of these imaging tools is critical in tracking disease progression and assessing the effectiveness of any therapeutic interventions.

The genetic diversity in CS mutations contributes to the variability in clinical presentation, as discussed by Laugel [6]. Nevertheless, the common neuroimaging features of cerebellar atrophy and white matter changes remain consistent across different mutations, reinforcing the value of MRI in both diagnosing and monitoring the disease.

In conclusion, neuroimaging, particularly MRI, remains indispensable in the diagnosis and management of Cockayne Syndrome. The characteristic findings of cerebellar atrophy, ventricular enlargement, and white matter changes provide essential information for diagnosing the disease and tracking its progression. The integration of advanced imaging modalities like DTI and volumetric MRI improves our understanding of the disease’s neurological and microstructural changes, guiding clinical decisions and supporting patient care.

Conclusion

Neuroimaging is a cornerstone in the diagnosis of Cockayne Syndrome. The characteristic findings of cerebellar atrophy, ventricular enlargement, and white matter changes provide essential information for diagnosing the disease and tracking its progression. The integration of advanced imaging modalities like DTI and volumetric MRI improves our understanding of the disease’s neurological and microstructural changes, guiding clinical decisions and supporting patient care.
