Submaximal walking increases MicroRNA-126 and angiogenic markers in men and women with peripheral arterial disease
Authors: Amauri Maroto, Tiago Fernandes, Bruno R A Pelozin, Anthony S Leicht, Nelson Wolosker, Luan Morais Azevêdo, Luiz Augusto Riani Costa, Paulo Rizzo Ramires, Cláudia Lúcia de Moraes Forjaz, Natan Daniel da Silva Junior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeVascular dysfunction is a pathophysiological mechanism in peripheral artery disease (PAD). PAD patients exhibit reduced circulating expression of microRNA-126 (c-miRNA-126) and VEGF. Submaximal walking (SW) is recommended in PAD because it increases walking capacity, partially by improving vascular regulation, which can be observed after a walking session. Nevertheless, previous studies were conducted with maximal walking and in men. As women with PAD present worse disease characteristics and greater ischemia, they may be less responsive to SW, which needs to be investigated. Thus, this study compared the acute effects of SW on vascular function, c-miRNA-126 and angiogenic markers in men and women with PAD.MethodsThirty patients (15 women) underwent, in a random order, 2 sessions: control (standing on treadmill) and SW (15 bouts of 2-min walking at moderate intensity intersected by 2-min rest). Circulating gene expression of miRNA-126, SPRED-1, PI3KR2, VEGF, and eNOS were evaluated before and after the interventions.ResultsBoth genders presented similar maximal walking capacity. Vascular function did not change after the interventions in either gender. After SW, both groups presented a similar decrease in PI3KR2 and similar increases in c-miRNA-126, VEGF and eNOS.ConclusionIn men and women with PAD, an acute SW similarly increases c-miRNA-126 expression, which inhibits PI3KR2, enabling the progression of the VEGF angiogenic signaling pathway of PI3K-AKT-eNOS. These results support that SW is equally effective in inducing molecular angiogenic changes in men and women with PAD, supporting its recommendation for both genders. Future studies should address these possible chronic effects of walking.Graphical abstract Keywords: Intermittent claudication, Physical exercise, MicroRNA, VEGF

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) affects 3 to 10% of the adults worldwide(Kroger et al. 2006). Its prevalence increases with age (Norgren et al. 2007; Criqui et al. 2005), being similar or higher in women compared to men at advanced ages (Song et al. 2019). Intermittent claudication (IC), the leading symptom of PAD, is present in around 30% of the patients (Becker et al. 2002; Norgren et al. 2007) and is more frequent in men than women (Sigvant et al. 2007).

Due to obstruction of the arteries of the lower limbs and IC-related pain during walking, the functional capacity of the patients with PAD and IC is significantly reduced (Myers et al. 2008), decreasing their quality of life (Breek et al. 2001). Furthermore, the atherosclerotic disease present in PAD increases cardiovascular risk, resulting in high cardiovascular morbidity and mortality (Harris et al. 1995; Boger et al. 1997). In addition, these consequences of PAD and IC seem to be more severe in women than men (Hirsch et al. 2006). Therefore, the aim of PAD´s treatment is to increase functional capacity, reduce IC symptoms, improve quality of life and ameliorate cardiovascular prognosis, which should be primarily achieved through regular submaximal-intensity walking (Hirsch et al. 2006; Gerhard-Herman et al. 2017).

A central pathophysiological mechanism of PAD is vascular dysfunction (Vita and Hamburg 2010) evidenced by reduced flow-mediated dilation (FMD) (Harris et al. 1995), vascular endothelium growth factor (VEGF) (Palmer-Kazen et al. 2004) and muscle capillarity (Askew et al. 2005; Clyne et al. 1985). It is known that miRNA-126 that modulates the VEGF pathway is also decreased in PAD (Stather et al. 2013), which may partially explain the attenuated vascular response. MicroRNA-126 (miRNA-126) acts by inhibiting two negative regulators of the VEGF signaling pathway: sprouty-related protein 1 (SPRED-1) that inhibits the angiogenic pathway formed by RAF1-MEK-ERK; and phosphoinositol-2 kinase regulatory subunit 2 (PI3KR2) that inhibits the angiogenic pathway formed by PI3K-AKT-eNOS PI3KR2 (Fish et al. 2008).

In a previous study, we showed that an acute bout of maximal walking (i.e., a treadmill test conducted with a specific protocol for this population and limited by maximal claudication pain) increased c-miRNA-126 and VEGF levels in male patients with PAD (da Silva et al. 2015). Nevertheless, it is necessary to confirm whether the same vascular and angiogenic responses can be obtained with submaximal walking since this is the exercise training protocol recommended and used in clinical practice for patients with PAD (Gerhard-Herman et al. 2017). Furthermore, it is also important to investigate whether these responses can also be obtained in women.

Previous literature showed that female patients with PAD and IC have impaired microvascular function in the gastrocnemius (Gardner et al. 2010), presenting greater ischemia (Gardner 2002; Gommans et al. 2015) and lower oxygen saturation (Gardner et al. 2009) during exercise compared with men. These differences suggest that women with PAD may be less responsive to walking-induced vascular adaptations and may exhibit lower walking capacity than men. Nevertheless, in a previous study, we did not observe any differences in walking capacity and postexercise cardiovascular responses between men and women with PAD and similar clinical characteristics (Miyasato et al. 2021). One possible explanation is that the impaired vascular responses in women may have been overcome by the total ischemia induced by the maximal effort in both sexes. However, it is possible to hypothesize that these blunted responses may be present during submaximal walking in women, which deserves investigation since such differences may have implications for tailoring exercise training recommendations for men and women with PAD and IC.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to verify and compare the acute effects of submaximal walking on vascular function, circulating expression of miRNA-126 (c-miRNA-126) and markers of the angiogenic signaling pathway in men and women with PAD and IC.

Materials and methods

Ethical aproval

All participants gave free and informed consent before their inclusion in the study, and details that could reveal the identity of the subjects under study have been omitted. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Human Research from the University of São Paulo (process 5.307.371). The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

This was a randomized controlled trial in which two groups of patients with PAD and IC (men and women) underwent, in a random order (htpps://www.randomizer.org), two experimental sessions: exercise (ES) and control (CS). Participants were evaluated before and after the intervention (exercise or rest, respectively). The main outcomes were blood expression levels of c-miRNA-126, miRNA-126 targets (PI3KR2 and SPRED-1), VEGF and nitric oxide synthase enzyme (eNOS).

Patients

Patients with PAD and IC were recruited from a vascular center. Inclusion criteria were: (i) aged ≥ 50 years; (ii) diagnosis of PAD at Rutherford stages 1–3 (Gerhard-Herman et al. 2017); (iii) ankle brachial index (ABI) ≤ 0.90; (iv) systolic/diastolic blood pressures (SBP/DBP) ≤ 160/105mmHg; (v) absence of revascularization or angioplasty in the past year; and (vii) ability to walk at least 2 min at 2.0 mph. Exclusion criteria included: (i) presence of diabetic neuropathy; (ii) clinical conditions contraindicating exercise (e.g., severe musculoskeletal disfunctions, uncontrolled chronic diseases such as uncontrolled diabetes or hypertension, instable heart disease, and others); (iii) myocardial ischemia or complex arrhythmias during a maximal walking test; (iv) discontinuation of the maximal walking test for reasons other than IC; and (v) changes in pharmacological treatment during the study period.

Procedures

Patients were interviewed to identify age, presence of cardiovascular diseases, comorbidity conditions, and current medications. Ankle-brachial index (ABI), body mass index (BMI) and auscultatory BP were measured according to established guidelines (Barroso et al. 2021; Gerhard-Herman et al. 2017), while clinical autonomic neuropathy was assessed in the diabetic patients following the American Diabetes Association’s battery (American Diabetes 2020). Additionally, all patients underwent a graded walking test until the maximal claudication pain. The test was conducted on a treadmill (Imbrasport, ATL, Porto Alegre, Brazil) following a standardized protocol (2 mph, 2% increment every 2 min)(Gardner et al. 1991). During the test, the patients were monitored by an ECG (Welch Allyn, Inc., Cardio Perfect MD, New York, USA), and the heart rate (HR) at the pain threshold (beginning of claudication pain) was recorded (da Rocha Chehuen et al. 2016). Additionally, walking capacity was assessed by the distance walked until the onset of leg pain (claudication onset distance – COD) and the maximal distance walked (total walking distance – TWD).

Male and female patients who fulfilled the study criteria underwent the two experimental sessions (ES and CS) that were performed in a random order with a minimum interval of 3 days between them. The sessions were held in the morning (between 7 a.m. and 12 p.m.) in a temperature-controlled laboratory (20–23 °C). Patients were instructed to fast for at least 8 h before the sessions, and not to perform vigorous exercise nor to drink alcohol in the previous 24 h. In addition, they had to take their regular medications as normal.

In each experimental session, data were collected before and after the specific intervention. For that, upon arrival at the laboratory, the patients laid down, a catheter was inserted into the antecubital vein, and it was kept patent by salinization. Thus, the pre-intervention period started and lasted 40 min. During this period, a blood sample was collected and, subsequently, HR and BP were measured in triplicate, while baseline blood flow (BBF) and FMD were assessed in the brachial arm at the last 20 min. Then, the patients moved to the treadmill and performed the specific intervention of each experimental session (60 min). In the ES, they performed 15 bouts of 2-min walking interspersed by 2-min of upright passive rest. During the walk, the treadmill speed was kept at 2 mph, and the grade was adjusted to maintain the patient´s HR at the HR obtained at the pain threshold (± 4 bpm) assessed at the graded walking test. A previous study showed that the HR at pain threshold corresponds to a metabolic stimulus higher than the anaerobic threshold, revealing a moderate intensity (Ritti-Dias et al. 2009). In the CS, the patients remained standing on the treadmill. After the intervention, the patients immediately returned to supine position and rested for 50 min (post-intervention). During this period, at 20 min, HR and BP were measured in triplicate, followed by a blood sample collection. Then, BBF and FMD were assessed.

Measurements

Blood pressure

BP was measured on the patient’s dominant arm using the auscultatory technique, employing a stethoscope and a mercury column sphygmomanometer, and accepting the phases I and V of the Korotkoff sounds to identify SBP and DBP, respectively. Mean arterial pressure (MBP) was calculated as MBP=(SBP-DBP)/3 + DBP.

ABI

SBP was simultaneously measured in the brachial artery and posterior tibial artery using the auscultatory method and a handheld Doppler (Martec, DV 6000, Ribeirão Preto, Brazil), respectively. Measurements were performed on both sides of the body, and the ABI was calculated for each leg by the ratio between the ankle SBP and highest brachial SBP. The lower ABI between the two legs was used for sample characterization.

Blood flow and flow-mediated dilation

Blood flow and endothelial function, assessed by FMD, were measured in the brachial artery of the arm with the highest BP. The blood flow velocity was determined by duplex ultrasonography through a linear array (L9-3 RS probe, Logiq E (R7), California, USA) with an insonation angle of 60º. Arterial diameter was determined by an automatic software (QUIPU – Cardiovascular Suite, Pisa, Italy) in recordings lasting 60 s (baseline period). To assess FMD, a cuff was positioned on the forearm just below the cubital fossa and was inflated to 250mmHg for 5 min. After deflation of the cuff, images of the brachial artery and blood flow velocity were recorded for 180s. Blood flow was calculated by multiplying the artery diameter by the mean flow velocity, which was expressed as ml/min. Peak shear stress was measured as peak velocity after deflation of the cuff, which was expressed as s− 1. FMD was calculated by the percentage increase in the artery diameter after occlusion in relation to baseline (FMD (%)=[(peak diameter – baseline diameter)/baseline diameter]*100). Vascular conductance (VC) was calculated by dividing MBP by blood flow and was expressed in arbitrary units (a.u.).

Whole blood RNA isolation

Total RNA, including miRNA, was isolated using the PAXgene Blood miRNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After extraction, the total RNA concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE) and checked for integrity by EtBr-agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples were immediately frozen at −80 °C according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

mRNA and miRNA analysis by real-time polymerase chain reaction

RNA was primed with 0.5 mg/ml oligo(dT) (Fermentas/Thermo Scientific Molecular Biology, Rockford, IL) to generate first-strand DNA. Reverse transcription was performed using Revertaid M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase (Fermentas/Thermo Scientific Molecular Biology). cDNA for miRNA analysis was synthesized from total RNA using gene-specific primers, according to the TaqMan miRNA Assay protocol (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). Real-time quantification of SPRED1, PI3KR2, VEGF, and eNOS mRNA were performed with a SYBRGreen PCR Master Mix, using ABI PRISM 7700 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems). The expression of cyclophilin was measured as an internal control for sample variation in the reverse transcription reaction. Primers were designed using the Primer3 software (http://primer3plus.com/cgi-bin/dev/primer3plus.cgi). The DNA sequence was obtained from GenBank, and primers were made in separate exons to distinguish PCR products derived from cDNA by size from those derived from genomic DNA contaminants. To accurately detect mature miRNA- 126 (INV 2228), the real-time PCR quantification method was performed using TaqMan miRNA Assay Protocol (Applied Biosystems). miRNAs samples were normalized by evaluating U6 expression. Each blood sample was analyzed in duplicate. Relative quantities of target gene expressions were compared after normalization to the values of the reference gene (ΔCT). Fold changes in mRNA and miRNA expression were calculated using the differences in ΔCT values between the two samples (ΔΔCT) and the Eq. 2−ΔΔCT. Results are expressed as percentage of CS baseline.

Statistical analysis

Normality of data distribution and homogeneity of variance were confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Groups’ characteristics were compared by t-tests or chi-squared tests. For the main outcomes, mixed three-way ANOVAs were employed, establishing group (men and women) as the between main factor, and session (ES and CS) and moment (pre- and post-intervention) as the within main factor. When appropriate, the Bonferroni post-hoc test was used, and p ≤ 0.05 was considered as significant (JASP 0.19.3, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Data are presented as means ± SD in the text and tables, while means ± SE were used in the figures for a clearer view.

Discussion

The main findings of this study were that acute SW induced a significant increase in c-miRNA-126 and VEGF, thereby enhancing the PI3KR2 - eNOS angiogenic pathway. Contrary to the initial hypothesis, these responses were similar in men and women with PAD and IC.

In the present study, there was no difference in walking capacity (assessed either by COD or TWD) between the genders, which is similar to a previous study from our group (Miyasato et al. 2021), but differs from other studies (Gardner et al. 2009; Gardner 2002) that have reported lower walking capacity in women than men with PAD using the same test protocol. Nevertheless, as in our previous study (Miyasato et al. 2021), this study employed careful participation criteria to ensure good matching between the genders regarding their physical and clinical characteristics (Table I). This match was essential to ensure that any post-exercise difference between men and women can be attributed to gender and not to physical or clinical differences. In general, previous studies have matched the genders groups only by ABI and age, which may have contributed to the differences found between genders, since the presence of factors, such as comorbidities presence, may affect walking capacity in patients with PAD (Gardner et al. 2016).

Similarly to the walking capacity results, BP responses following submaximal walking also did not differ between the genders. Post-walking SBP and DBP were significantly lower in the ES compared to the CS, revealing similar post-exercise hypotension in men and women with PAD. This result is in accordance with studies that have reported significant BP reduction after acute walking in patients with PAD and IC (Cucato et al. 2015; Chehuen et al. 2021) and with our previous study that showed similar post-exercise hypotension in male and female patients after maximal walking (Miyasato et al. 2021), but it expands the knowledge by showing that BP reduction after walking is not affected by gender even after the submaximal effort that is usually recommended for this population.

Regarding the vascular function, submaximal walking did not significantly alter any of the variables studied (BBF, VC and FMD) in either men or women with PAD. Differently, walking to maximum intensity have been reported to increase BBF (da Silva et al. 2015; Joras and Poredos 2008) and reduce FMD (Joras and Poredos 2008; Silvestro et al. 2002), and the discrepancy between the results may be explained by the fact that maximal effort generates greater ischemia than the exercise employed in this study. However, chronically, submaximal walking training has been shown to increase FMD (Januszek et al. 2014), muscle capillarization and angiogenic markers (da Silva et al. 2022) besides improving walking capacity. Therefore, despite the lack of acute post-walking changes, the long-term vascular adaptations obtained with submaximal walking are beneficial to this population.

Although SW did not promote acute changes in post-exercise vascular function, it similarly modified of the c-miRNA-126, its target PI3KR2, and the angiogenic markers, VEGF and eNOS, in men and women with PAD. It is known that the expression of c-miRNA-126 is markedly reduced in patients with PAD (Stather et al. 2013), which may be, in part, responsible for releasing the negative regulator PIK3R2 pathway, resulting in reduced VEGF and eNOS in this population (Fish et al. 2008). The current study showed, for the first time, that submaximal walking is able to acutely increase c-miRNA-126, decrease PIK3R2, and consequently increase VEGF and eNOS expressions in whole blood, which suggest that this type of exercise may be a trigger for the activation of this important angiogenic pathway that is reduced in patients with PAD. These results corroborate previous studies by our group that have showed the same changes in this signaling pathway in men with PAD after maximal walking (da Silva et al. 2015) and after a period of walking training (da Silva et al. 2022).

The post-exercise responses of c-miRNA-126 expression and the angiogenesis pathway were not different between the men and women with PAD and IC. This similarity occurred with a submaximal walking performed at the same absolute and relative intensities since men and women presented similar maximal walking capacities. Thus, differently than hypothesized, these findings support that, when gender groups are paired by clinical characteristics and functional capacity, women with PAD do not present compromised vascular responses to exercise even during a submaximal effort that do not impose total ischemia. Therefore, the worse vascular function previously reported in literature for women with PAD may be related to their worse functional capacity and not to the gender per se, which should be checked in the future by researchers with specific designs for this comparison. In addition, the fact that acute submaximal walking was equally effective in inducing changes in the molecular angiogenic pathways that may chronically promote vascular benefits in PAD, support that women and men with PAD and IC may equally respond to walking training.

Therefore, the present study revealed two new findings that have clinical relevance. First, it showed that, like maximal walking, acute submaximal walking can stimulate angiogenic pathways in patients with PAD and IC. Second, it revealed that improvements induced by acute walking are similar in men and women with PAD and IC. Together, these findings give scientific support for the recommendation of submaximal walking training for both men and women with PAD and IC as already done in current guidelines (Hirsch et al. 2006).

The current study presents some limitations. First, its results are limited to patients who met the specific inclusion criteria. The length of disease was not assessed, but all patients, men and women, had similar disease severity and walking limitations. Thus, comparison between genders may produce different results if men and women present baseline differences in physical characteristics, clinical status or walking capacity. Additionally, the experiments were conducted with the patients in the fasting state. As the fasting period was similar before both sessions and in both groups, it may not have affected the present results. Finally, it is interesting to note that the study compared the acute effects of submaximal walking, and the chronic effects of walking training may be different, which should be further investigated.
