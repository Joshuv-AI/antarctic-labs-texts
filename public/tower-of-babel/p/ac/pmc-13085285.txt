Efficacy and safety of bictegravir, emtricitabine, and tenofovir alafenamide (BIC/FTC/TAF) versus efavirenz, lamivudine, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (EFV+3TC + TDF) in late-presenting people with HIV rapid initial antiretroviral therapy
Authors: Qinlan Li, Jialu Li, Chengyu Gao, Yining Zhao, Liangxi Zhu, Jiang Xiao, Fujie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the efficacy and safety of BIC/FTC/TAF versus EFV+3TC + TDF for rapid ART initiation in late-presenting (CD4 < 200 cells/µL) people with HIV in the real world.MethodsThis retrospective cohort included 366 ART-naïve adults at Beijing Ditan Hospital (2021–2024). Patients were stratified by opportunistic infection (OI) status and initiated on either BIC/FTC/TAF or EFV+3TC + TDF. The primary endpoint was virological suppression (HIV-1 RNA < 50 copies/mL) at Week 48.ResultsAt Week 48, virological suppression rates were significantly higher with BIC/FTC/TAF versus EFV+3TC + TDF in both patients without OIs (92.4% vs. 62.5%) and with OIs (92.3% vs. 55.9%). Treatment retention was also superior with BIC/FTC/TAF (94.1% vs. 62.3%). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed a higher cumulative probability of maintaining suppression with BIC/FTC/TAF (96.7% vs. 80.4%, log-rank p < 0.001). Pre-treatment drug resistance in NNRTIs was present in 11.6% of the EFV group and 1.4% of the BIC group. Acquired resistance emerged only in the EFV group (10.8% by Week 24). CD4 + T-cell counts in both groups increased. BIC/FTC/TAF was associated with early, stable increases in creatinine and lipids, while EFV+3TC + TDF showed a marked triglyceride rise.ConclusionIn this real-world cohort of late-presenting people with HIV, BIC/FTC/TAF demonstrated superior virological efficacy, treatment retention, and resistance profile over EFV+3TC + TDF, supporting its use in rapid ART initiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13035-w.Keywords: HIV, Late presentation, Rapid ART initiation, Bictegravir, Efavirenz

Introduction

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection remains a major global health challenge. In 2023, approximately 39.9 million people were living with HIV worldwide [1]. Despite expanded access to antiretroviral therapy (ART), many individuals continue to present late for care, defined by a CD4⁺ count < 200 cells/µL or the presence of an AIDS-defining illness at diagnosis [2]. These late presenters face a high risk of morbidity, mortality, and opportunistic infections due to advanced immunodeficiency [3]. In addition, they often exhibit persistent immune dysfunction and remain prone to opportunistic infections (OIs).

With the advancement of rapid initiation recommendations for starting ART within 7 days, same-day ART initiation has gained increasing support. Compelling evidence has demonstrated that same-day initiation significantly shortens the time from diagnosis to virological suppression [4, 5], enabling earlier immune reconstitution and more effectively reducing mortality and loss to follow-up [6, 7].

The backbone regimen of two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) plus a third anchor agent has long been the gold standard for initial ART, as recommended by major international guidelines [8, 9]. Bictegravir (BIC), co-formulated with emtricitabine (FTC) and tenofovir alafenamide fumarate (TAF) as a single-tablet regimen (BIC/FTC/TAF), offers high efficacy, a high resistance barrier, and a favorable safety profile, making it well suited for rapid initiation [10]. In contrast, although historically a first-line option, efavirenz (EFV) use has increasingly been limited by pretreatment resistance and tolerability issues, which may lead to virological failure and treatment discontinuation [11–13]. These challenges have driven a shift toward more robust and forgiving regimens for rapid ART initiation.

While the overall superiority of BIC-based regimens over EFV-based regimens is well established in clinical trials [14], direct evidence for rapid, particularly same-day, ART initiation among late-presenting people with HIV remains limited. This population, characterized by advanced immunosuppression (CD4 < 200 cells/µL) and a high burden of OIs, poses substantial challenges to achieving early and sustained virological success. Therefore, this retrospective cohort study aimed to provide real-world evidence by directly comparing the virological efficacy, safety, and treatment durability of BIC/FTC/TAF versus EFV + lamivudine (3TC) + tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) among late presenters undergoing rapid ART initiation in China.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Beijing Ditan Hospital in China between January 2021 and November 2024. We included ART‑naïve adults (≥ 18 years) with newly diagnosed HIV who were late presenters defined as having a baseline CD4⁺ T-cell count < 200 cells/µL and initiated either BIC/FTC/TAF or EFV+3TC + TDF. Key exclusions were pregnancy, cardiovascular disease, and severe hepatic/renal dysfunction.

Treatment and intervention

Patients were stratified by the presence of active opportunistic infections (OIs) at ART initiation. The BIC group received BIC/FTC/TAF (50/200/25 mg) daily; the EFV group received EFV (400 mg) + 3TC (300 mg) + TDF (300 mg) daily.

In patients without OIs, rapid ART initiation, especially same-day initiation, was optimal choice. In patients with OIs, ART timing was individualized based on the OI types and symptom control as follows: (1) OI patients with symptoms: ART was deferred until symptom control and clinical stabilization, and then initiated as soon as possible. (2) Tuberculosis (TB) infection: All patients in the BIC group were switched to another regimen once TB was diagnosed due to drug-drug interaction, while in the EFV group, ART was started within 2 weeks if baseline CD4⁺ <50 cells/µL, or initiated as soon as possible beyond 2-week anti-tuberculosis if CD4⁺ ≥50 cells/µL [15]. (3) Cryptococcal meningitis: For patients receiving amphotericin B, ART was promptly initiated after 4 weeks of induction therapy [16]. Viral suppression rates were evaluated according to the timing of ART initiation: within 1 day (same-day initiation), 2–7 days, and > 7 days.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was virologic suppression (plasma HIV‑1 RNA < 50 copies/mL) at Week 48. Secondary endpoints included: (1) Immunological recovery (change in CD4⁺ count and CD4/CD8 ratio from baseline to Weeks 24 and 48); (2) Treatment retention and reasons for discontinuation; (3) Safety including longitudinal changes in liver function markers (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], total bilirubin), serum lipid panel (triglycerides [TG], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]), renal function markers (serum creatinine [Cr], estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]), and documented adverse events.

Data collection and laboratory methods

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records. Plasma HIV‑1 RNA was quantified by real‑time PCR (lower limit 50 copies/mL). CD4⁺/CD8⁺ counts were determined by flow cytometry. Biochemical parameters were measured using standard automated analyzers. Genotypic resistance testing was performed at baseline and at virological failure.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the chi‑square test or Fisher’s exact test, continuous variables using the Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t test as appropriate. Primary endpoint: virologic suppression rates at Week 48 were compared between regimens using the chi‑square test. Kaplan–Meier curves with log‑rank test were used to compare the cumulative probability of maintaining virologic suppression. Patients discontinuing for non‑virologic reasons were regarded as virological suppression. Longitudinal changes in CD4⁺ count, CD4/CD8 ratio, and safety parameters within each group were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed‑rank test and visualized with box plots. About the impact of OIs on safety, the absolute change from baseline to Week 48 for each laboratory parameter was compared within each treatment group using the Mann–Whitney U test. All analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0; released 2022; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two‑tailed p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Health Science Ethical Committee of Beijing Ditan Hospital, with a waiver of informed consent for anonymized retrospective data.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 366 late-presenting HIV patients were included between January 2021 and November 2024 and stratified according to the presence of opportunistic infections (OIs) (Fig. 1). We excluded six patients in the BIC group infected with tuberculosis after BIC/FTC/TAF initiation, in which BIC/FTC/TAF was discontinued immediately among these patients after TB diagnosis. In the cohort without OIs, the BIC and EFV groups included 144 and 104 patients, respectively, whereas in the cohort with OIs, the BIC and EFV groups included 78 and 34 patients, respectively. In the cohort without OIs, the patients receiving BIC/FTC/TAF presented a significantly higher baseline viral load (median 4.95 vs. 4.73 log10 copies/mL; p = 0.028) and a lower CD4 count (median 97 vs. 132.5 cells/µL; p = 0.025) than the patients receiving EFV+3TC + TDF. In the cohort with OIs, both BIC and EFV groups exhibited severe immunodeficiency, with similarly low CD4 counts (median 24.0 vs. 25.0 cells/µL) and high viral loads (median 5.36 vs. 5.37 log10 copies/mL). In the cohort without OIs, > 98% of the patients in both the BIC and EFV groups received same-day ART (within 24 h). In the cohort with OIs, same-day initiation was achieved in 38.5% (30/78) of the patients in the BIC group and in 44.1% (15/34) of the patients in the EFV group. An additional 21.8% (BIC group) and 14.7% (EFV group) were initiated within 2–7 days, whereas 24.4% (BIC group) and 17.6% (EFV group) were initiated within 8–14 days. The remaining patients (15.4% in the BIC group and 23.5% in the EFV group) were initiated on ART > 14 days after diagnosis because of severe concomitant OIs requiring prior clinical stabilization (Table 1).

Treatment discontinuation and retention

The treatment retention rate at week 48 differed substantially between the groups. In the cohort without OIs, the retention rate was significantly higher in the BIC group (93.8% (135/144)) than in the EFV group (63.5% (66/104)). A similar advantage was also found in the cohort with OIs, in which the BIC group was observed in 94.9% (74/78) of patients retained versus 58.8% (20/34) in the EFV group. Taken together, BIC/FTC/TAF showed a consistently high retention rate (94.1% (209/222)), whereas EFV + 3 TC + TDF demonstrated substantially lower retention rates (62.3% (86/138)). The causes of ART discontinuation at weeks 24 and 48 are presented in Fig. 1; Table 2; the primary reasons for discontinuation in the EFV group included acquired drug resistance and adverse events, whereas those in the BIC group were predominantly non-virological, including economic burden and tuberculosis co-infection.

Assessment of virological efficacy

The virological outcomes are summarized in Table 2. It was observed that the BIC group showed higher virological suppression rates than the EFV group at 48-week. In the cohort without OIs, virological suppression rates were 92.4% in the BIC group and 62.5% in the EFV group, whereas in the cohort with OIs, virological suppression rates were 92.3% and 55.9% in the BIC and EFV groups, respectively. In the patients with OIs, forty-five (40.18%) cases started ART on the day of their first diagnosis, in which 30 received BIC/FTC/TAF and 15 received EFV+3TC + TDF. In patients started within 1 day or after > 7 days, the BIC/FTC/TAF group showed significantly higher viral suppression rates (90.0% and 93.5%, respectively) than that in the EFV+3TC + TDF group (40.0% and 64.3%, respectively; p = 0.001 and 0.023), while In patients with the initiation time from 2 to 7 days, the BIC group showed a numerically higher viral suppression rate than the EFV group with no significant difference (94.1% vs. 80.0%, respectively; p = 0.411) (Table 3). The cumulative probability of maintaining virological suppression between the two groups was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier curve. The endpoint event was defined as virological failure (HIV RNA ≥ 50 copies/mL). At 48 weeks, the 48-week cumulative virological suppression rate in the patients receiving BIC/FTC/TAF (n = 222) was 96.7% (95% confidence interval (CI): 94.3–99.1), whereas that in the patients receiving EFV + 3TC + TDF (n = 113) was 80.4% (95% CI: 73.2–88.3), respectively. The difference in cumulative virological suppression between the two groups was statistically significant (p < 0.001, log-rank test). During the follow-up period, the cumulative suppression rates in the EFV group at 24 and 36 weeks were 97.3% and 91.8%, respectively (Fig. 2).

Resistance analysis

Resistance analysis results are summarized in Table 4. At baseline, 16 patients (11.6%) in the EFV group and 3 (1.4%) in the BIC group presented non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTIs) resistance mutations, with no resistance to integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs), protease inhibitors (PIs), or NRTIs found in either group. At week 24, 12 patients (10.8%) receiving EFV+3TC + TDF developed new resistance primarily to NNRTIs (10.8%) and NRTIs (8.1%). At week 48, one patient (1.1%) in the EFV group with poor adherence developed NNRTI resistance. Notably, acquired resistance to any drug class was not observed in the BIC group at any time point.

Immunological response

Both the BIC and EFV groups showed significant and continuous immunological recovery over 48 weeks (Supplementary Table 1). In the patients without OIs, the median CD4 + T-cell count increased from 97.0 to 267.0 cells/µL in BIC group and from 132.5 to 291.0 cells/µL in EFV group (p < 0.001). Similarly, in the cohort with OIs, CD4 counts rose from 24.0 to 198.5 cells/µL in the BIC group and from 25.0 to 194.0 cells/µL in the EFV group (p < 0.001, except EFV group from Week 24 to 48: p = 0.004). A consistent and statistically significant improvement was also observed in the CD4/CD8 ratio in all patient groups over 48 weeks (p < 0.01), indicating sustained recovery of the immune response after receiving ART.

Evaluation of laboratory safety

Evaluation of Safety were presented in the Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 2–3). In the BIC/FTC/TAF cohort, the median Cr increased from 69.0 µmol/L at baseline to 81.5 µmol/L at Week 24 and stabilized at 83.5 µmol/L by Week 48, corresponding to a median eGFR decline from 114.70 to 103.60 and 101.45 mL/min/1.73 m², respectively. Median LDL-C level also rose from 2.34 to 2.61 and 2.64 mmol/L at these time points. In contrast, the EFV+3TC + TDF group showed milder changes: median Cr increased modestly from 67.30 to 72.95 and 73.60 µmol/L, while median eGFR decreased from 117.80 to 111.45 and 112.20 mL/min/1.73 m², respectively. Lipid elevation was observed in both groups, with the median TG rising from 1.30 to 1.42 in the BIC group and from 1.16 to 1.49 in the EFV group at week 24. ALT remained within the normal ranges throughout the study period in both groups. Analysis of hepatorenal parameters’ change revealed that in the BIC group, patients with OIs experienced a significantly greater increase in median Cr change (16.60 µmol/L in the patients with OIs vs. 10.02 µmol/L in the patients without OIs, p = 0.003) and a more pronounced decline in median eGFR change (-15.90 mL/min/1.73 m² in the patients with OIs vs. -10.60 mL/min/1.73 m² in the patients without OIs, p = 0.004), whereas no such differential impact was observed in the EFV+3TC + TDF cohort.

Adverse events

The clinically notable adverse events are described in detail in Table 5. Overall, the prevalence of neuropsychiatric, skin, and gastrointestinal adverse reactions was higher in the EFV group, whereas weight gain was more frequently reported in the BIC group. In patients with OIs, one case of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) was reported in the BIC and EFV groups, respectively. Both patients had high baseline viremia (6.2 log₁₀ copies/mL in the patient receiving BIC/FTC/TAF, and 5.69 log₁₀ copies/mL in the patient receiving EFV+3TC + TDF), achieved virologic suppression within 12 weeks, and showed marked CD4⁺ T cell increases (from 58 to 493 cells/µL in the patient in BIC group, and from 21 to 111 cells/µL in the patient in EFV group). In the BIC group, the patient was diagnosed as mycobacterium avium complex (MAC)-associated IRIS, which received anti-MAC treatment with azithromycin, ethambutol and rifabutin [17], and subsequently discontinued BIC/FTC/TAF, and in the EFV group, the patient was diagnosed as extrapulmonary tuberculosis -associated IRIS, which further received anti-tuberculosis treatment.

Discussion

This real-world retrospective study demonstrated that among late-presenting people with HIV, BIC/FTC/TAF achieved robust and consistent virological suppression across all ART initiation times, especially in same-day initiation. Furthermore, it was associated with significantly higher treatment retention and a negligible risk of acquired drug resistance compared with EFV+3TC + TDF.

The observed baseline imbalance reflects that clinicians preferentially selected BIC/FTC/TAF for late-presenters with lower CD4 + T-cell counts and higher viral load, based on the BIC’s rapid and potent antiviral efficacy [18], independent of economic factors. This real-world practice underscores the regimen’s perceived utility in challenging clinical scenarios. Late-presenting individuals with HIV, characterized by advanced immunosuppression (CD4 + count < 200 cells/µL), are at a high risk for OIs, leading to complex clinical management [2, 3]. In this challenging population, BIC/FTC/TAF demonstrated robust virological efficacy, with a 92.4% suppression rate at week 48 in patients without OIs and 92.3% in those with OIs. This study further revealed that, among patients with OIs, the viral suppression rate following same-day initiation varied markedly by groups: 90.0% for those initiated on BIC/FTC/TAF versus only 40.0% (6/15) for those on EFV/3TC/TDF. Among the 9 patients in the EFV group who failed to achieve viral suppression, treatment discontinuation due to adverse drug reactions occurred in 3 cases, acquired drug resistance in 5 cases, and suboptimal adherence in 1 case, respectively, which indicated that, for OI patients with rapid ART initiation, the BIC/FTC/TAF presented better efficacy, safety and tolerability than those in EFV-containing regimen. Notably, among patients with severe OIs necessitating ART initiation after 7 days, BIC/FTC/TAF maintained a high suppression rate of 93.5%, significantly outperforming EFV+3TC + TDF (64.3%; p = 0.023; OR = 8.06). Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, which accounts for variable follow-up time and censoring, confirmed the superior durability of BIC/FTC/TAF, with a 96.7% probability of maintaining virological suppression through week 48, compared with 80.4% for EFV+3TC + TDF (log-rank p < 0.001). Therefore, in the context of rapid ART for late presenters, selecting BIC/FTC/TAF over EFV+3TC + TDF can ensure early treatment success by simultaneously ensuring rapid virological control, promoting long-term adherence and preventing resistance-driven failure.

When rapid initiation was conducted, baseline drug resistance tests were also performed at same time. When the resistance test results are received, if pre-treatment drug resistance (PDR) is found, doctors can promptly and accurately adjust the ART regimen based on resistance results before the occurrence of virological failure (much earlier than the conventional 24-week re-examination point), thus avoiding patients experiencing unnecessary treatment failures and the risk of acquired drug resistance. This process not only ensures the timeliness of rapid initiation but also ensure the patient’s long-term success based on resistance test results, achieving a seamless transition from empirical initial treatment to precise management. Both PDR and acquired drug resistance poses a critical challenge to rapid ART success, particularly when baseline resistance results are unavailable at initiation [19–22]. In our cohort, 11.6% (16/138) of patients of the EFV+3TC + TDF group, which is consistent with a previous report [23], and 1.4% (3/222) of those in the BIC/FTC/TAF group had baseline NNRTI resistance. Notably, none of these three patients in the BIC group experienced virological failure, and no acquired resistance to any drug class was observed in this group at Weeks 24 or 48. Our findings showed that among patients starting EFV+3TC + TDF without the benefit of baseline genotyping, 10.8% (12/111) developed new NNRTI and/or NRTI resistance by Week 24, and one additional case emerged at Week 48 due to poor adherence. The long half-life and low resistance barrier of EFV can create a “window” for viral escape during periods of imperfect adherence, whereas the pharmacodynamic profile of BIC effectively prevents the emergence of resistance even under such conditions [24, 25]. Therefore, selecting a regimen with a high resistance barrier, such as BIC/FTC/TAF, is a crucial strategic safeguard to ensure the durability of rapid ART initiation, especially for late presenters who are at heightened risk of attrition and may face challenges with adherence.

The tolerability profile of EFV+3TC + TDF was notably less favorable and was characterized by adverse events that directly affected the quality of life. Specifically, neuropsychiatric symptoms and skin reactions were observed in the EFV group. These side effects were a direct cause of treatment discontinuation, thereby contributing to the significantly lower retention rates observed in the EFV group (62.3% versus 94.1% in the BIC group). Beyond common side effects, IRIS could occur in late-presenting patients starting rapid ART with OIs. In our cohort, one IRIS case occurred in each treatment group following rapid viral suppression and CD4⁺ recovery. More attention should be paid to late-presenting patients who are susceptible to IRIS after effective antiretroviral therapy.

Laboratory monitoring revealed distinct regimen-specific safety profiles over 48 weeks. The BIC/FTC/TAF was associated with an early, non-progressive increase in serum creatinine levels and a corresponding decline in eGFR, along with progressive increases in LDL-C and TG levels. In contrast, EFV+3TC + TDF induced more modest changes in renal functions and a significant increase in triglyceride levels. Notably, within the BIC/FTC/TAF group, changes in renal function were significantly more pronounced in patients with OIs than in those without OIs. The reason for this might be the active infection itself or the specific nephrotoxicity of the treatment drugs [26], rather than the direct effect of BIC. No such changes were found in patients with the OIs receiving EFV+3TC + TDF. Both regimens maintained stable hepatic functions throughout the study.

This real-world study demonstrated that BIC/FTC/TAF shown high viral suppression rates and good efficacy and tolerability in late presenters. However, this study also has limitations. Firstly, the study was a single-center retrospective one, which might be involved in selection bias and unmeasured confounding factors. Specifically, the bias in the treatment plan led to this selection bias, and the data from a single center may limit the generalizability of the research conclusions to other medical settings. Secondly, the long-term clinical significance of the observed changes in laboratory parameters in this study needs to be verified through prospective studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this real-world study provides robust evidence that BIC/FTC/TAF is a superior choice over EFV+3TC + TDF for rapid ART initiation in late-presenting people with HIV. Its high genetic barrier effectively preempts both baseline and acquired drug resistance, whereas its favorable tolerability profile directly translates into significantly higher treatment retention. Therefore, prioritizing BIC/FTC/TAF in rapid-start protocols, especially in resource-limited settings where same-day resistance testing is unavailable, is a critical strategy for securing individual treatment success, reducing the risk of virological failure, and advancing the public health goal of ending the HIV epidemic.
