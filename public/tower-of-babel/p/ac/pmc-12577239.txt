Impact of HLA-DRB1 SE, anti-citrullinated protein antibodies and smoking on radiographic outcome in Greek patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis
Authors: Evangelia N. Mole, Katerina Tarassi, Alexandra Tsirogianni, Theophilos Athanassiades, Vasiliki Kitsiou, Diamanto Kouniaki, Sousana Gazi, Panagiotis Vlachoyiannopoulos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a complex, multifactorial autoimmune disease, whose aetiopathogenesis involves genetic and environmental factors. It remains unclear whether these factors influence the radiographic damage in RA patients. In this study we aimed to investigate the main effects of shared epitope (SE) alleles, smoking and anti-citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs), as well as their possible interaction on the radiographic outcome, in Greek patients with longstanding RA.MethodsThe study comprised of 300 Greek RA patients, 150 smokers and 150 non-smokers. ACPAs were determined by ELISA, HLA-DRB1 alleles were typed by molecular techniques. Radiographs of hands and feet were scored by modified Sharp/van der Heijde score (SHS).ResultsIn total, 57.7% were ACPA positive and 64% possessed at least one copy of HLA-DRB1 allele. Predictors of radiographic severity were: presence of at least one copy of SE (B: 20.82 [17.76–23.87], p < 0.001), ACPAs (B: 14.4 [10.96–17.84], p < 0.001) and smoking history (B: 12.8 [9.36–16.25], p < 0.001). Presence of *01:01, *04:05 and *10:01 alleles were also significantly associated with more radiographic damage (B: 11.63 [7.36–15.88], p < 0.001, B: 8.8 [3.69–13.91], p = 0.001 and B: 15.56 [11.33–19.79], p < 0.001), respectively. Furthermore, we observed an interaction between smoking, HLA-DRB1 SE alleles in respect to radiographic outcome only in seropositive RA, B: 10.13 [2.03–18.22], p = 0.015, p = 0.01 for interaction. There was no evidence of interaction effect when we analyzed the individual SE alleles.ConclusionsWe observed a significant gene-environmental interaction that influenced the radiographic severity in seropositive RA patients, in addition to its crucial role in the pathogenesis of seropositive RA.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic, autoimmune disease marked by persistent inflammation of synovial joints, leading to progressive joint deformity, functional impairment, disability, and decreased quality of life [1, 2]. Bone erosions typically develop early in the course of RA, sometimes as early as the first year [3]. However, their onset can vary among individuals due to factors such as disease severity, genetic predisposition and treatment efficacy. Several studies have reported that up to 60% of patients have erosions within the first year of disease onset [4, 5] and approximately 10% have joint erosions as early as 8 weeks after the initiation of symptoms [6]. In some patients, the disease process may start before the actual symptoms appear [7]. This is supported by the presence of characteristic autoantibodies long before disease onset [8]. These findings highlight the importance of early diagnosis and intervention in order to prevent joint damage and improve prognosis. Early initiation of targeted therapies, especially in those patients with poor prognostic factors, aims to control inflammation, reduce the risk of bone erosions, and improve long-term disease outcomes [9].

To date, several clinical, environmental and genetic factors have been recognized as poor prognostic markers of joint damage [10–12]. These factors include elevated inflammatory markers, positive ACPA and rheumatoid factor (RF) [13], history of tobacco exposure [14], female gender, age at disease onset and HLA-DRB1 SE alleles [15]. Since RA pathogenesis is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors [16], several studies targeted to confirm the role of genetic factors in prediction of disease outcome which remains controversial [17]. Some studies suggest that SE alleles are associated with more severe radiographic damage [18], while some others do not confirm this [19]. These discrepancies may be due to ethnic differences in study populations, typing techniques and the low statistical power of different studies due to small number of participants.

Rheumatoid factors (RF) and anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs) are autoantibodies commonly found in the blood of patients with RA, that are associated with increased risk of erosive disease [20]. RF is an autoantibody directed against Fc portion of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies, and it is present in up to 80% of RA patients [21]. RF has low diagnostic specificity, but has been associated with more severe disease progression, increased joint damage and higher incidence of developing erosions [21, 22]. ACPAs target citrullinated proteins and are highly specific for RA, while their diagnostic specificity exceeds 90% [23]. ACPAs positivity is strongly correlated with aggressive disease, increased rates of joint damage and elevated risk of developing erosions compared to RF negative patients [24]. The presence of both autoantibodies, particularly when detected together, is associated with higher risk of erosive disease [21, 22, 25].

The HLA-DRB1 gene is a part of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II region that plays a crucial role in immune regulation. HLA-DRB1 SE alleles have been associated with an increased risk of RA development, progression to more severe radiographic outcomes as well as a poorer prognosis in such patients [26]. The precise mechanism by which HLA-DRB1 SE contributes to erosive disease is incompletely understood. However, it is likely that it involves complex interactions between genetic susceptibility factors and environmental triggers resulting in dysregulated immune responses and chronic joint inflammation [26, 27].

Smoking is a well-established environmental risk factor for seropositive RA pathogenesis, correlated also to erosive RA [28, 29]. In addition, smoking has been also associated with more severe disease outcomes, including higher disease activity [30, 31] and unfavorable response to DMARDs therapy [32]. However, conflicting results have been reported regarding the impact of tobacco on radiographic damage, indicating that smoking may act as RA risk factor and also as a disease modifier [33].

Methods

In this cross-sectional study we aimed to investigate the effects of smoking, SE, ACPAs as well as their possible interaction on the radiographic damage, measured by SHS, in a cohort of RA patients with long lasting disease duration.

The study comprised 300 Greek RA patients attending the outpatient clinics of the Department of Pathophysiology, School of Medicine, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens and the Department of Rheumatology, KAT General Hospital of Attica. Patients were prospectively enrolled between 10/2019 and 12/2023. Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) who met at least four of the 1987 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for RA [34] at the time of enrollment and had a disease duration of ≥ 2 years. Patients provided written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria included other autoimmune rheumatic diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, spondyloarthritis), active infection, pregnancy or breastfeeding, and inability to obtain a blood sample or radiographs. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Medicine, National and Kapodistrian University of Athens (approval number 180/18.09.2019), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The data that support the findings of this study will be available upon request from the corresponding author. The data are not publicly available due to privacy or ethical restrictions.

At enrollment, patients provided blood samples and their smoking history was evaluated by questionnaires. Current and past smokers were categorized as smokers, and those who had never smoked were classified as non-smokers. By including past smokers in the smoker category, we aimed to assess the cumulative lifetime impact of smoking exposure. ACPAs were analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), using commercial kits (QUANTA Lite Inova – Diagnostics Inc. San Diego CA 92131). An ACPA antibody titer ≥ 25 IU was considered as positive.

HLA-DRB1 high-resolution typing was performed by molecular techniques: polymerase chain reaction and hybridization with sequence specific oligonucleotide probes and/or specific primers (PCR-SSOP, PCR-SSP) [35, 36] in the Department of Immunology-Histocompatibility of Evangelismos General Hospital of Athens. The HLA-DRB1 *01:01, *01:02, *04:01, *04:02, *04:03, *04:04, *04:05, *04:08 and *10:01 were classified as SE alleles.

Radiographs of the hands and feet were scored by two experienced rheumatologists, S.G. and P.V., who were blinded to the patients’ clinical data, autoantibody profiles, and treatment. They used the modified Sharp/van der Heijde scoring method (SHS). The SHS includes sub-scores for erosions and joint space narrowing. The maximum total score for the hands and feet is 448, with a maximum of 280 points for erosions and 168 for joint space narrowing. Since we were interested in long-term outcomes, we included only patients with a disease duration of ≥ 2 years.

Radiographic scoring showed good agreement between the two readers. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), based on a two-way mixed-effects model with a consistency definition. The ICC for single measures was 0.974 (95% CI: 0.968–0.980), indicating excellent agreement between the readers. The ICC for average measures was 0.987, further supporting the robustness of scoring consistency.

Statistics

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations (mean ± SD). The main effects of individual factors – including ACPA and RF status, smoking and presence of SE alleles – on radiographic outcomes were evaluated by univariate analysis of variance using general linear models (GMLs). Multiple linear regression analyses were then conducted to assess the independent effects of these variables on SHS, as well as to explore their relative importance. In separate multiple regression analyses, we aimed to evaluate the potential interaction effects between smoking and SE on SHS, stratified by ACPA status, while adjusting for potential confounders, such as age, disease duration, gender, treatment, and comorbidities. The SHS was considered as the dependent variable against a full interaction model of SE and smoking in question in both subsets of RA. For each analysis, results were expressed as B coefficients (reflecting the adjusted difference between groups), along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), p-values and partial eta squared (η²) values to indicate effect size. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Demographics

The study population comprised 300 Greek patients with a mean age of 65.18 ± 12.05 years and a mean disease duration of 10.23 ± 6.43 years. Women represented 74.3% of the cohort. More than half of the patients were ACPA-positive (57.7%) and RF-positive (60.7%), while 64% carried at least one HLA-DRB1 shared epitope allele, most commonly 01:01 (22.3%), 10:01 (20.7%), and 04:05 (15.0%) [37]. Half of the cohort were current or past smokers.

Regarding to disease activity, the mean DAS28 was 3.83 ± 1.71. At the time of assessment, 40% of patients were in remission, 16.3% had low disease activity, 27% moderate activity, and 16.7% high disease activity. The mean SHS was 40.24 ± 16.45, indicating a considerable burden of radiographic damage.

Treatment patterns reflected real-world practice, 78.5% of patients were receiving cs-DMARDs, while 54.3% were treated with biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs (b-/ts-DMARDs) (Table 1).

The majority of patients (86%) had at least one comorbidity. The most common conditions included osteoporosis (50.3%), arterial hypertension (49.7%), and dyslipidemia (40.3%), followed by obesity (27.7%) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (15.7%). Less frequent comorbidities included fibromyalgia (21%), secondary Sjögren’s syndrome (14.7%), depression (14.3%), infections (12.7%), cardiovascular disease (9%) and cancer (6.7%).

Furthermore, multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed in order to identify the possible associations of demographic and clinical characteristics with ACPA positivity, smoking and presence of HLA-DRB1 SE. More seropositive (ACPA-positive) patients received b/ts-DMARDs, and older patients were less likely to be smokers. Additionally, the presence of comorbidities was significantly associated with HLA-DRB1 SE allele carriage. Other factors, including disease duration, DAS28-ESR, and treatment with cs-DMARD, were not significantly associated with these outcomes (Table 2).

Effects of ACPA autoantibodies, tobacco exposure and HLA-DRB1 SE on radiographic severity in RA patients

The univariate analysis revealed a significant impact of ACPAs on radiographic severity. Overall, the mean SHS in ACPA positive patients was significantly higher compared to seronegative patients, after correction for disease duration (45.82 ± 15.29 vs. 32.64 ± 14.88, B: 14.4 [95% CI: 10.96–17.84], p < 0.001, η2: 0.186). In the same manner, patients testing positive for RF had significantly higher SHS values compared to RF negative patients (44.51 ± 15.44 vs. 33.65 ± 15.81, B: 11.76 [95% CI: 8.16–15.36], p < 0.001, η2: 0.122).

Tobacco exposure also exhibited a significant correlation with radiographic outcomes. Specifically, the mean SHS among smokers was notably higher than that of non-smokers (46.27 ± 16.91 vs. 34.21 ± 13.55, B: 12.8 [95% CI: 9.36–16.25], p < 0.001, η2: 0.153).

Regarding the presence of SE, a similarly strong association was found. Presence of at least one copy of SE was highly predictive of a worse radiographic outcome (47.73 ± 13.27 vs. 26.93 ± 12.72, B: 20.82 [95% CI: 17.76–23.87], p < 0.001, η2: 0.378). A further increase of the risk was observed among patients who possessed double copies of SE (53.36 ± 14.48, B: 26.41 [95% CI: 22.19–30.63], p < 0.001, η2: 0.339).

Additionally, when we analyzed the different subtypes of SE alleles, only *01:01, *04:05, *10:01 alleles exhibited significant associations with higher levels of SHS (49.07 ± 13.32 vs. 37.7 ± 16.4, B: 11.63 [7.36–15.88] p < 0.001, η2: 0.089, 47.96 ± 13.35 vs. 38.88 ± 16.58, B: 8.8 [3.69–13.91], p = 0.001, η2: 0.037 and 52.29 ± 13.71 vs. 37.1 ± 15.65, B: 15.56 [11.33–19.79], p < 0.001, η2: 0.150), respectively as illustrated in Fig. 1, in which their effect sizes (blue dots) with 95% confidence intervals (horizontal lines) do not cross zero.

Independent factors of radiographic outcome

We performed multiple linear regression analyses, to identify factors independently associated with SHS (dependent variable), as well as the strength of each association. The results of the regression model were adjusted for key covariates (age, gender and disease duration) and other covariates such as treatment and comorbidities, that didn’t affect significantly the regression model. The analyses indicated that HLA-DRB1 SE alleles, smoking and ACPAs were independent predictors of radiographic damage. The presence of HLA-DRB1 SE alleles (Importance: 0.570) was the most significant and impactful factor, followed by smoking (Importance: 0.214) and ACPA positivity (Importance: 0.077). The overall model was highly significant (F model = 64.18, p < 0.001), indicating a strong fit. We separately examined the *01:01, *04:05 and *10:01 alleles, that exhibited significant associations with SHS in univariate analyses of variance, ACPAs and smoking. All alleles were significantly associated with SHS, and *10:01 proved the strongest degree of association (0.278) (Table 3). Finally, we excluded RF from the multiple regression model due to collinearity with ACPAs. The substantial overlap between ACPAs and RF positivity may lead to confounding effects and biased estimates.

Interaction

We further examined the potential interaction effects between the SE alleles and smoking on radiographic outcome, stratified by ACPA status. To do so, we compared the different combinations of SE and smoking in seropositive and seronegative RA patients. Nonsmokers without SE allele served as reference group. Smoking didn’t result in worse radiographic outcomes in SE negative patients (B: 4.09 [95% CI: -3.1–11.28], p = 0.26), while the addition of a single SE allele appeared to be associated with higher SHS values only in ACPA-positive RA (B: 10.13 [95% CI: 2.03–18.22], p = 0.015, p = 0.01 for interaction). These findings suggest a synergistic effect between SE and smoking in promoting joint damage in ACPA-positive RA. In contrast, no evidence of interaction between smoking and SE alleles was detected in ACPA-negative RA (B: 1.57 [95% CI: -6.94–10.08], p = 0.71). In a similar manner, we separately examined the combination of tobacco exposure with different SE allele subtypes; no statistically significant interaction was observed. These findings suggest that the investigated gene–environment interaction influences not only the risk of developing seropositive RA, but also contributes to greater radiographic severity within this subgroup.

Discussion

The most characteristic outcome of RA is a progressive joint destruction, leading to additional declines in joint function and quality of life [2]. Progressive joint destruction occurs in 70% of RA patients within the first two years of diagnosis and is associated with long term loss of function and decline in quality of life [3]. Early identification of patients who will develop more severe disease is crucial for their subsequent course, as it leads to early initiation of therapy.

Numerous studies in the literature have consistently highlighted the significance of ACPAs as a strong indicator for long-term radiographic damage. Versteeg et al. [38], confirmed that ACPA positivity is a significant and independent predictor of long-term radiographic damage [21, 22]. In the same study, 1 in 5 patients developed rapid radiographic progression (18.8%), in the first 6 months of disease, despite the rapid achievement of low disease activity. These patients developed more damage in subsequent years. Notably, even patients in clinical remission may develop erosions [39]. In early diagnosed RA (disease duration ≤ 3 months), the presence of ACPAs at baseline, seems to be highly predictive for erosion development. A recent comprehensive analysis, combining data from DRIVE and DESIRABLE trials, established that ACPAs and RF was strongly associated with structural damage, despite the effective symptom control with cs-DMARDs [40].

Several studies examining the relationship between smoking and radiographic outcomes have yielded conflicting results [41, 42]. The impact of tobacco tends to be different between early and established RA, suggesting complex interactions with immune, hormonal, psychosocial factors, comorbidities, and treatments [43, 44]. In the setting of SWEFOT trial, smoking was identified as a strong independent risk factor for radiographic deterioration among patients with early RA (OR = 2.17 [95% CI: 1.06–4.45]) [29].

Finckh et al., found no difference in the progression of radiographic joint damage or functional disability between current and non-smokers, as well as an unexpected inverse dose-response with current smoking intensity, as heavy smokers exhibited less radiographic disease progression compared to moderate and non-smokers [44]. Notably, all studies demonstrating a significant association between radiographic joint damage and smoking were cross-sectional [41, 45], analyzing data at a single point in time, while longitudinal studies failed to identify any effect [46, 47]. Consequently, these findings suggest that the observed differences regarding the effect of smoking on RA severity may be attributable to study design.

In the same pattern of conflicting results there are some studies attempting to correlate radiographic damage with SE and seropositivity. Although the model was initially proposed to explain the genetic predisposition of RA, subsequent research has shown that SE may be associated with more severe manifestations of disease. Several studies, extremely heterogenous with regard to studied populations, have tried to clarify this relationship but no clear conclusion has been reached [18, 19]. Mattey et al., concluded that RF and SE are independent risk factors for radiographic deterioration in long-standing RA [27]. While in the case of early disease the presence of SE is a strong predictor for early erosions in RF negative patients [17].

In meta-analysis of Gorman et al., the subgroup analysis demonstrated that SE was associated with erosive disease, highlighting the effect of different genetic background among groups. A typical example was the Greek population group, where no correlations between SE and erosive disease were observed (OR = 0.8 [95% CI 0.2–1.5]). In contrast, the study of Northern European and Asian patients confirmed the association (OR = 6.2 and 5.4 respectively) [26]. The Greek population genetically differs from other Caucasians, exhibiting milder disease, both at level of joint inflammation and destruction [48, 49]. Therefore, research of genetic and environmental differences among different ethnic groups is interesting and challenging mainly because of the clinical heterogeneity that exists.

Ioannidis et al. demonstrated a noteworthy correlation between SE alleles and radiographic severity, specifically highlighting the significance of DRB1 *01:01 and *10:01 alleles. So far, few studies within Greek populations have examined the link between SE alleles and erosions, but issues of insufficient statistical power exist. Actually, there is a notable research gap in Greek patients, regarding the effects of SE alleles, smoking, and ACPAs, as well as their potential interactions [50].

To our knowledge, this is the first study in Greek RA patients with long-lasting disease, that targeted to investigate the main and interaction effects of genetic and environmental factors in respect to radiographic outcomes. Positive ACPAs, HLA-DRB1 SE and smoking contributed to a significant worse disease outcome. The same pattern was observed in patients that possessed double copies of SE alleles. In multivariate regression analyses *01:01, *04:05 and *10:01 alleles were independently associated with the SHS and *10:01 allele proved the stronger association.

We also observed a significant interaction between smoking and the presence of SE alleles in seropositive RA, though not when analyzing individual SE alleles separately. The observation that the interaction effect is mainly driven by the presence of SE alleles, underscores their potential contribution to disease severity. In contrast, smoking had a less meaningful impact on SHS. It is well established that smoking induces citrullination and may contribute to ACPAs production, that are associated with worse radiographic damage, indicating that tobacco exposure may exert an indirect effect on the development of erosive disease. Overall, these findings highlight the complex interplay between environmental factors, genetic susceptibility, and clinical outcomes in RA.

While our study offers some valuable insights, its cross-sectional design poses certain limitations, as it limits the ability to infer causality between SE and smoking with radiographic outcome. Although, we collected a detailed tobacco exposure history (onset and duration relative to disease onset), classifying the former smokers as smokers to capture the cumulative lifetime impact of past tobacco exposure, lack of data on passive smoking represents a notable limitation, as it may contribute to unmeasured confounding.

While the long disease duration at enrollment may introduce some degree of recall bias, the structured data collection aimed to minimize this limitation and improve reliability of smoking exposure assessment. However, we acknowledge that recall bias remains a potential source of error. Additionally, due to the cross-sectional nature of our data, we were unable to assess progression of radiographic damage, which limits the ability to assess temporal relationships between exposures and outcome. Another limitation is the relatively small sample size for individual SE alleles, which may have compromised the statistical power of our multivariate analysis, particularly the interaction models. Future studies with larger cohorts may overcome this limitation and elucidate significant associations among different SE alleles, smoking, and SHS.

Our study’s strengths lie in its inclusion of consecutive, non-selected RA patients from a real-world setting of two independent rheumatology centers, thus enhancing the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, our dataset was detailed and complete, including information on treatment history and comorbidities. These variables were assessed and found not to significantly affect the regression models, thus minimizing the risk of confounding.

We also used the SHS, a validated and objective measure of structural damage, thereby limiting subjective bias. Radiographic damage was assessed by two independent readers who were blinded to the clinical data, ensuring objective evaluation. The inter-reader agreement was high (0.987), which supports the reliability and reproducibility of the scoring. Adjustment for key covariates further strengthens the validity of our analyses. Taken together, these aspects support the robustness of our multivariable models and reduce the likelihood of biased estimations or model overfitting.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in our study of long-lasting RA, the presence of SE, ACPAs and smoking was strongly associated with worse radiographic outcomes. A strong gene-environment interaction was observed in seropositive RA, suggesting that while this interaction contributes to the overall pathogenesis of seropositive RA, it may also influence the erosive process to the same extent. This insight could be valuable for further research and understanding of the mechanisms underlying RA and its erosive manifestations.
