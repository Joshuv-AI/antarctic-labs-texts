Late Stent Thrombosis After Biodegradable Polymer Everolimus-Eluting Stent in a Cancer Patient Receiving Bevacizumab
Authors: Tomomi Koizumi, Masao Ono
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe biodegradable polymer everolimus-eluting stent (EES) aims to enhance vascular healing by avoiding durable polymer exposure, but its safety in cancer patients remains uncertain.Case SummaryA 78-year-old man with gastric and hepatocellular carcinoma underwent percutaneous coronary intervention with implantation of a biodegradable polymer EES in the right coronary artery for acute myocardial infarction, followed by dual antiplatelet therapy. After cancer surgery, he received bevacizumab, an anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agent, as well as atezolizumab. On day 199 after stenting, 14 days after his second chemotherapy cycle, he died of sudden cardiac arrest. Autopsy revealed thrombotic occlusion of the stented segment, and histology showed complete re-endothelialization with abundant fibrin around the stent struts, expressing plasminogen activator inhibitor–1 with macrophages.DiscussionThis case illustrates late stent thrombosis despite complete re-endothelialization on the biodegradable polymer EES, suggesting that both the patient's cancer-related prothrombotic state and his anti-VEGF therapy may have overcome the theoretical safety advantage of the stent.Key words: bevacizumab, oncology, stent, thrombosis

Case Report

A 78-year-old man with known gastric cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. The patient had a history of hypertension and was currently undergoing oral medication treatment. While awaiting cancer surgery, he experienced an acute inferior myocardial infarction and underwent successful percutaneous coronary intervention with implantation of a biodegradable polymer everolimus-eluting stent (EES) (3.5 by 18 mm) in the proximal right coronary artery (Figure 1). Intravascular ultrasound after stent deployment showed optimal stent expansion (Figure 2). He was prescribed dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin 100 mg and prasugrel 3.75 mg daily. Two months later, he underwent total gastrectomy and partial hepatectomy (segments S7/S8). Subsequently, bevacizumab (800 mg, 15 mg/kg) and atezolizumab (1,200 mg) were administered intravenously on postoperative days 111 and 134 as part of the treatment for residual hepatocellular carcinoma. On day 199 after stent implantation (14 days after his second chemotherapy cycle), the patient collapsed at home and was transported to our hospital in cardiopulmonary arrest. Resuscitation efforts were unsuccessful, and he was pronounced dead. An autopsy was performed with the family's consent.

Macroscopic examination revealed thrombotic occlusion of the right coronary artery stent, consistent with late stent thrombosis (LST). The stent struts were intact and were not fractured. There was also evidence of fresh myocardial necrosis in the inferior wall, indicating recurrent myocardial infarction. Histological examination of the stented segment demonstrated abundant fibrin deposition encasing the stent struts (Figures 3A and 3B), as shown by phosphotungstic acid hematoxylin staining. Immunohistochemistry revealed strong plasminogen activator inhibitor–1 (PAI-1) expression localized to the pericellular regions within the fibrin (Figures 3C and 3D), and these cells coexpressed inducible nitric oxide synthase and CD163, consistent with activated macrophages. CD34-positive endothelial cells covered the luminal surface (Figure 3E), indicating completed re-endothelialization. There was no evidence of platelet aggregation, as αCD42b staining was negative (Figure 3F).

Discussion

Stent thrombosis remains a serious complication despite the use of drug-eluting stents and dual antiplatelet therapy. LST can occur months to years after implantation and is often triggered by factors such as delayed healing, hypersensitivity reactions, or systemic prothrombotic conditions.1 Cancer and its therapies are well-recognized contributors to a hypercoagulable state.2, 3, 4

In cancer-free populations, autopsy and angioscopic studies of the third-generation biodegradable polymer EES have shown almost complete vascular healing within 6 to 12 months. Migita et al5 reported strut coverage rates of 80% to 85% and mature neointima thickness of approximately 60 μm at 6 months, with minimal fibrin persistence and low inflammation. Other angioscopic studies confirmed that most biodegradable polymer EESs achieve homogeneous neointimal coverage by 12 months, with little thrombus formation.6 In contrast, our case demonstrated abundant fibrin and PAI-1–positive macrophages around the stent struts despite complete re-endothelialization, findings that diverge from the expected benign healing pattern in noncancer patients. This discrepancy implies that cancer-associated prothrombotic states and anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy may override the favorable healing properties of the biodegradable polymer EES.

The biodegradable polymer EES is designed to reduce inflammation and promote vascular healing compared with durable polymer stents. Previous reports using optical coherence tomography have shown favorable healing profiles in cancer patients; however, clinical data on stent thrombosis in this population remain limited.7

In this case, despite complete re-endothelialization, LST occurred after administration of bevacizumab, an anti-VEGF agent known to increase arterial thrombotic events through endothelial dysfunction and procoagulant effects.2,8 The observation of fibrin-rich thrombus with PAI-1–expressing macrophages suggests that impaired fibrinolytic activity may have contributed, although this remains speculative.

This case illustrates that the theoretical benefits of the biodegradable polymer EES may not be sufficient to counteract the multifactorial thrombotic milieu in oncology patients receiving anti-VEGF therapy. Alternatively, it is necessary to consider whether antiplatelet agents alone are sufficient for the selection of antithrombotic drugs after EES implantation in the coronary artery in cancer patients receiving anti-VEGF agent, or whether options such as anticoagulants should also be considered. Careful patient selection, close monitoring, and consideration of intensified antithrombotic strategies may be warranted.

Conclusions

LST can occur in cancer patients treated with biodegradable polymer EES, particularly during anti-VEGF therapy. This case highlights the need for vigilance and further research regarding stent selection and antithrombotic management in this high-risk population.
