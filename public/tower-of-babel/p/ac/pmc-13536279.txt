A Single Centre Feasibility Study of Vacuum Assisted Breast Biopsy Excision as a Non‐Surgical Alternative for Selected Women With Borderline Breast Lesions
Authors: Gelareh Farshid, Suzanna Saloniklis, Kohei Funahashi, Amardeep Sidhu, Janne Bingham, Leong Tiong, Robert Whitfield, Melissa Bochner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Based on Australian and UK figures [1] annually ~2000 women have a surgical breast biopsy (SBB) in Australia for asymptomatic lesions found on imaging, mostly during breast cancer screening. This surgery is not for treatment but is performed after needle biopsies, as a method of last resort to establish a definitive diagnosis. Typically, these women have already undergone core biopsies with non‐malignant results, but for a range of reasons those results are deemed not to have ruled out malignancy, necessitating further investigations. SBB requires hospital admission and general anaesthesia. It involves a surgical breast incision, recovery time, a surgical scar, and costs at least $AUS 4320 per procedure. Overall, this surgery does uncover DCIS or invasive cancer in a third of patients [2], underscoring its value. However, in the remaining two thirds of women SBB finds only benign changes. Breast surgery for benign lesions is an uncontested harm of screening and since it may deter screening participation and the expenditure of resources is a public health concern. If less invasive but equally effective alternatives were available for detecting cancer, up to two thirds of women might avoid the psychologic and physical morbidity and costs of breast surgery.

Current Indications for Surgical Breast Biopsy

A longitudinal study of 814 women undergoing SBB from BreastScreen South Australia (BSSA) [2] documented three principal indications for SBB. (i) 57% women had a core biopsy diagnosis of Borderline Breast Lesions (BBL), a group of histologic lesions notorious for exhibiting non‐malignant changes on core biopsy and yet, when excised promptly, being found to harbour cancer in 5%–35% of women [3]. This is referred to as the upgrade rate of each BBL subtype. (ii) 12% women had benign core biopsy histology but there was Radiology‐Pathology Discordance (RPD) which may signal an off‐target core biopsy and (iii) 7% of women had a core biopsy diagnosis of suspicious but not diagnostic of cancer, the changes not being definitive to permit cancer treatment. After SBB, malignancy was diagnosed in 27.6% of BBL, 16% of RPD and 85% of suspicious lesions.

VAB Ex Technique Overview

VAB Ex is a novel health technology, which is being used overseas, albeit without clinical trial evidence. To date it has minimal uptake in Australia. VAB Ex is fundamentally similar to a core biopsy, but removes more tissue, thereby allowing complete lesion removal for diagnosis. Radiologists performing VAB Ex use live imaging guidance, local anaesthesia and a single 2 mm skin incision to insert the biopsy device, targeting small lesions. Once in optimal position, tissue is biopsied and delivered into a specimen collection chamber via vacuum tubing. By gently and systematically rotating the biopsy device in place, multiple large cores of tissue are removed through the one skin incision until imaging shows the target lesion has been excised. VAB Ex samples permit comprehensive histopathologic assessment and diagnosis, but excision margins cannot be evaluated. Margins are irrelevant for benign lesions, while women diagnosed with malignant lesions on their VAB Ex samples proceed to breast surgery to ensure clear surgical margins and, in case of invasive cancer, axillary nodal staging.

VAB Ex, referred to as Vacuum Assisted Excision (VAE) in Europe, is used in the UK, Europe and recently in New Zealand, in line with various national, opinion‐based policies, for subsets of BBL. Australia does not have a VAB Ex national policy and to date the technique has had limited implementation in this country, without any coordination. Absence of any clinical trial data, paucity of clinical outcomes, limited evaluation of consumer attitudes, few advocates for change, unclear funding access and lack of central coordination are potential factors hampering the uptake of VAB Ex in Australia. As a first step towards determining the role of VAB Ex in the Australian breast care landscape, we wished to implement a pilot study of this technique at our large, metropolitan teaching hospital. This is a feasibility study to determine the acceptability of VAB Ex to Australian patients and healthcare providers, establish the logistics and a model of care, determine the radiologic effectiveness in lesion removal, the histologic outcomes and the adverse events associated with VAB Ex in our hands.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval and Funding

The Central Adelaide Human Research Ethics Committee assessed and approved this pilot study Reference Number 14777. Financial support was provided by Breast Cancer Trials.

Study Design

This is a prospective pilot of VAB Ex as an alternative to SBB for establishing the definitive diagnosis in women who have been diagnosed with a range of borderline breast lesions (BBL) whose risk of a malignant upgrade is low (≤ 20%). This pilot study design was reviewed and approved by the Breast Cancer Trials Scientific Advisory Committee prior to be awarded funding support.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria comprised: (a) Women, aged ≥ 18 years, (b) able to provide informed consent for the procedure; (c) Any method of detection, including mammographic or MRI screening, symptomatic, incidental findings (d) Lesion size on imaging: ≤ 15 mm; (e) Specific histologic subtype of BBL on prior needle core biopsy or VABB eligible for this pilot are: Radial scar or complex sclerosing lesion without atypia, papillary lesion without atypia, flat epithelial atypia, non‐invasive lobular neoplasia of classic subtype (including lobular carcinoma in situ or atypical lobular hyperplasia), mucocele‐like lesion without atypia.

The key exclusion criteria comprised: (a) Inability to give informed consent; (b) women with known or suspected pathogenic mutations in breast cancer disposition genes; (c) women with a prior diagnosis of breast cancer or DCIS in the last 10 years; (d) concurrent diagnosis of a more advanced breast lesion in the same region of the breast (including atypical ductal hyperplasia, papilloma with atypia, radial scar with atypia, concurrent DCIS or invasive breast cancer); (e) The dominant imaging morphology is microcalcifications; (f) The radiologists' expert opinion is that the lesion is technically unsuitable for VAB Ex (e.g., too superficial, too deep, anatomically difficult to access); (g) bleeding tendency; (h) serious concurrent comorbidities that in the surgeon's or radiologist's opinion would pose a contraindication to VAB Ex.

For women with more than one lesion in the breast (ipsilateral, contralateral or bilateral); each lesion's suitability for VAB Ex was assessed independently. Ultimately, this decision was made jointly by the woman and her treating teams in consultation with her treating teams.

Interventions

All patients included in this study were recommended to have a diagnostic excision of their breast lesion. At our centre patients under the care of breast surgeons for the removal of BBL were assessed by their surgeon to determine if they meet the above eligibility criteria for this study. Breast imaging of the potential candidates was reviewed by the study radiologists to determine technical suitability for the VAB Ex procedure.

Eligible women were advised by their surgeon of the recommendation for lesion excision and were provided the options of VAB Ex instead of surgical excision during a clinical consultation. There were opportunities for the patient to ask questions. This face‐to‐face discussion was supplemented and reinforced with an information sheet provided for further consideration. The patient's decision was recorded.

Written consent was obtained from all participants. Appropriately consented, eligible women were referred to the hospital medical imaging department for the procedure to be scheduled with the study radiologists.

The VAB Ex procedure was carried out in line with usual practice for VABB, but with a 7G needle and thorough sampling under image guidance, aiming to achieve total lesion removal. Imaging at the start, during and at the conclusion of the procedure was assessed and was recorded if any residual lesion was visible. Removal of any prior site marker was attempted and recorded. A new site marker was deployed after lesion removal.

After the completion of the procedure, an assistant applied pressure to the area for 20 min to ensure bleeding stops and to reduce haematoma formation. A band aid was applied to the site. A follow up appointment was made for the patient to see a surgeon within 7–10 days and discuss their histologic results. The patient was advised to take simple analgesics (e.g., paracetamol) overnight for pain relief, as required, and to seek medical attention from us if they notice bleeding, worsening pain, swelling or redness, using a telephone number provided. Patients were free to leave following their procedure.

The tissue retrieved was placed immediately in formalin and submitted for histology. In the laboratory, we weighed and measured the samples and embedded all the tissue in paraffin blocks for histology. Ten H & E sections were prepared from each block. The two study breast pathologists evaluated the VAB Ex samples. Immunohistochemical evaluation was carried out, as required.

The histologic findings were discussed at MDT, and a final recommendation was documented in accordance with the schema presented in Figure 1.

For patients who did not undergo surgery, follow up imaging was carried out at 12 and 24 months to assess suspicious interval changes. Patients with cancer were followed up by their treating teams.

Statistical Analysis

The data were imported into a statistical software package (JMP V17). Descriptive analysis of the data was performed. Patient surveys were administered at baseline and provided to the patients after the procedure with a request to return their completed surveys.

Results

Between December 2022 and June 2024, 31 women with 33 lesions were assessed by VAB Ex, two patients having two lesions each. The surgeons report that every eligible woman offered the option of VAB Ex, chose this procedure over SBB.

The mean patient age was 57.8 years. (range 35–85 years). The pre‐procedure needle biopsy diagnoses were papillary lesion: 13 (39.3%) or possible papillary lesion: 1 (3.0%), papilloma: 6 (18.2%), papillary cytology on FNA biopsy: 1 (3.0%), radial scar: 5 (15.2%), epithelial proliferation: 2 (6.1%), adenomyopeithelioma: 1 (3.0%), atypical lobular hyperplasia: 1 (3.0%), non‐specific low‐grade atypia, fibrosis and non‐specific changes were diagnosed in one case each.

The procedures were undertaken with stereotactic imaging guidance in 2 cases and with ultrasound guidance in the remaining 31 cases, all using 7G needles. The radiologists confirmed lesion removal by VAB Ex in all cases. All previously deployed site markers were successfully removed. After excision of the lesions, new site markers were deployed in all cases.

Technical Suitability of VAB Ex Samples for Histologic Evaluation

The mean specimen weight in this study was 1.7 g, range (0.47–6.6 g). The mean number of tissue blocks required to embed all the VAB Ex tissue retrieved was 1.6, with a range of 1–3 blocks. The study pathologists were pleasantly surprised by the high quality of the VAB Ex specimens. In particular, whereas smaller gauge biopsies produce piecemeal samples with some crush artefact, VAB Ex retrieved large segments of the target lesions, often apparently intact, as shown in Figure 2. The size of the tissue and immediate immersion in formalin led to optimal fixation. Pathologists could measure the lesion size in the VAB Ex samples. Some tumours appeared intact (Figure 2C) but histologic assessment of completeness of excision or margins status is not reliable and was not attempted. Margins aside, the other histologic parameters typically evaluated in breast biopsies, such as lesion architecture, cellular composition, nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, presence of calcifications, necrosis, and if relevant lymphovascular invasion could all be assessed in these samples, without special difficulties. The prior site markers were identifiable embedded in the tissue, confirming the procedure was on target. Some neoplastic diagnoses in this series included a rare benign adenomyoepithelioma, and 3 instances of DCIS. Immunohistochemical studies were used as required, and antigen preservation proved satisfactory (Figure 3).

Further Description of Cases of Special Interest

One woman had undergone needle biopsies on 4 prior episodes, without a definitive diagnosis being possible. She finally had a VAB Ex which established a diagnosis of intraductal papilloma, obviating breast surgery. One woman had ADH diagnosed on core biopsy and was considered for SBB. As she was a poor anaesthetics risk and the mammographic calcifications were only 4 mm in diameter, VAB Ex was undertaken. This procedure removed the imaging target successfully and histology found no further atypia. The MDT deemed surgery unnecessary. Another patient, aged 45 years, had a core biopsy diagnosis favouring an adenomyoepithelioma. Removed by VAB Ex, her adenomyoepithelioma diagnosis was confirmed, was of the non‐malignant subtype and breast surgery was not required. Patient 6, a 37‐year‐old woman, had her breast nodule assessed only by FNA cytology, suggesting a possible papillary lesion. She proceeded to VAB Ex, which confirmed an intraducal papilloma, averting both core needle biopsy and breast surgery. Finally, a 35‐year‐old woman had a core biopsy diagnosis of fibrosis, but imaging had raised the possibility of an intraductal papilloma. Her VAB Ex sample established non‐necrotising granulomatous mastitis. Surgery was not indicated but tissue culture may be considered in her subsequent care.

Longer Term Follow Up

In the 2 years since the procedure, one of the 28 women who did not have surgery has had a recurrent intraductal papilloma at the site where her prior intraductal papilloma had been removed. Both lesions had benign histologic features.

The Patient Experience

The surgeons report that all women given the choice of VAB Ex or breast surgery chose VAB Ex. In radiology, surveys were offered to patients to complete at baseline and after the VAB Ex procedure. Twenty‐five women returned both pre and post procedure surveys. In their baseline surveys two patients reported negative symptoms in almost all overall wellness categories, including experience of severe pain and feelings of depression and anxiety, almost all of the time.

In the post procedure surveys, except for these 2 women, all other patients reported no significant pain. The two patients who experienced pain reported symptoms relating to pain and feelings of anxiety and depression on the pre (and post) procedure questionnaires. All patients except one woman reported being able to look after themselves in the 24 h after the procedure. Patients provided high scores in the categories of returning to their full responsibilities, feeling comfortable and in control, and general wellbeing. Excluding our two outliers, there were no reports of symptoms of depression and anxiety, and all women reported being able to communicate with family and friends as usual.

Twenty‐two of 25 patients report high levels of support from hospital doctors and nurses. Anecdotally, the level of multidisciplinary team satisfaction was high, the team being energized in being involved in an innovative project with immediate patient benefits.

Adverse Events

We observed no adverse events requiring medical intervention during this pilot study. Specifically, none of our patients experienced an infection or had a haematoma that required evacuation. In the three instances where the women required therapeutic surgery, the surgeons reported that the VAB Ex procedure did not compromise the technical aspects of the surgery.

Financial and Health System Impacts

After our study, a cost analysis was commissioned by the SA Health Commission of Excellence and Innovation in Health. Compared to a surgical breast biopsy, VAB Ex was estimated to result in cost saving of AUS $2849.67 per patient. Additional indirect benefits of implementing VAB Ex were highlighted, relating to increased health system capacity, enhanced consumers' choice in the delivery of their healthcare, reduced resource requirements, including fewer inpatient admissions, surgery and general anaesthetics use, a reduced carbon footprint and improved environmental impacts of healthcare delivery. Encouraging and supporting innovation in the delivery of healthcare were also noted as positive outcomes of this intervention.

Discussion

Our pilot study of VAB Ex in a large Australian teaching hospital addresses our stated aims. Our patients expressed a unanimous preference for VAB Ex over breast surgical biopsy. Patient surveys corroborated satisfaction with their choice and showed prompt recovery. While some degree of bruising was common, our patients did not experience any procedure related infection or haematomas requiring evacuation. Live imaging documented adequacy of lesion retrieval by radiologists in all cases and removal of any existing site markers prior to deployment of a new site marker. Pathologists found the VAB Ex samples to allow efficient assessment of the target lesions in 1–2 blocks as the mean specimen weight was 1.7 g and all lesions were removed successfully. These well‐fixed samples permitted optimal histology and ancillary investigations. Apart from margin assessment, the standard histologic data were assessed in these lesions and the malignant diagnoses established on VAB Ex samples were verified on the subsequent surgical excisions. In this series of 31 patients, 28 women had benign VAB Ex findings, and 3 had a diagnosis of DCIS. There were no atypical or indeterminate VAB Ex results. Consequently, in this exploratory pilot study of selected low risk borderline breast lesions 90.3% (95% CI 74.2; 98.0%) of our patients successfully avoid breast surgery. An independent cost analysis undertaken by SA Health found savings of $2849.67 per patient.

VAB Ex seems rare among health interventions in bringing about better patient outcomes at reduced costs, while also creating capacity in the healthcare system to expedite cancer patients' access to surgery. It is puzzling therefore to see a rather limited take up of VAB Ex in this country.

Though never evaluated in a clinical trial, VAB Ex is used internationally, most notably in the UK and in Europe, where different sets of guidelines are implemented.

The Clinical Benefits of VAB Ex

A small number of observational studies of the clinical impacts of VAB Ex are published. In the UK as of 2021, among eligible BBL, 73.9% with atypia and 65.1% without atypia were assessed by VAB Ex [4]. Their VAB Ex cancer upgrade rate was 16.6%, similar to upgrade rates with SBB and suggesting effectiveness or at least non‐inferiority for cancer detection.

VAB Ex for selected BBL has been shown to find benign changes in ~65% of women, assisting them avoided surgery [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Over 3–5 years of follow‐up, new cancers did develop in 0%–6.2% of these women. The new cancers were bilateral, rather than at the biopsy site, consistent with the fact that some BBL are risk factors for breast cancer and these women require surveillance. A further 12.5%–13% of their patients had a cancer diagnosed on VAB Ex [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Addressing oncologic safety of malignancies diagnosed first on VAB Ex, there are no published series to suggest that this method of diagnosis of breast malignancies hampers treatment approaches. In the UK and Europe, even high‐risk borderline breast lesions, such as atypical ductal hyperplasia with malignant upgrade rates of 25%–30%, are assessed routinely by Vacuum Assisted Excision (VAE), without oncologic detriments. Successive consensus conferences have recommended VAE as the preferred method of evaluation, after initial biopsies diagnose various BBL, with equivalent cancer upgrade rates to those of surgical excisions [9, 10, 11]. The procedure is a more comprehensive form of vacuum assisted core biopsies, used routinely in the assessment of breast abnormalities, particularly microcalcifications. Theoretically the removal of part of the malignancy by a diagnostic excision, whether surgical or VAB, may interfere with accurate assessment of lesion extent. In practice, a combination of imaging extent, histologic extent in the biopsy procedure and any residual malignancy in the therapeutic surgery allows a workable assessment of lesion extent to be factored into decisions regarding adjuvant therapies.

The advantages of this non‐surgical approach are that VAB Ex may reduce the number of surgical episodes because after a definitive cancer diagnosis on VAB Ex surgeons can plan one‐stage therapeutic surgery, including axillary staging and clear margins. Through identifying women with cancer, VAB Ex provides a personalised basis for selecting patients for breast surgery. However, not every woman benefits from VAB Ex. In 8%–10% of women VAB Ex findings have been indeterminate and these women require SBB or annual screening [4, 5, 6, 7, 8].

Effectiveness and Cosmetic Outcomes

Current pathways subject two thirds of patients who have undiagnosed benign lesions to potentially unnecessary surgery. There are some data showing that VAB Ex safely excises benign lesions in an outpatient setting with acceptable cosmetic outcomes. A cohort of 64 Dutch patients with lesions of 5–51 mm having VAB Ex reported good cosmetic outcomes [12]. Complete excision was achieved in all patients. VAB Ex is an extension of stereotactic breast biopsy, and prior data confirm that procedure is preferred by patients to SBB, due to a reduction of physical discomfort and pain, as evaluated by the Health‐Related Quality of Life and the SF‐36 questionnaires [13]. A 2024 literature review by the Clinical Advisory Group of BreastScreen Australia found low complication rates and high levels of patient satisfaction, including cosmetic outcomes [14].

Cost Comparisons

Driven by lower facility fees and reduced anaesthesia costs, VAB Ex delivers substantial savings compared to SBB. A 2025 independent cost analysis of this patient cohort, undertaken by the SA Commission on Excellence and Innovation in Health, reported $2849.67 cost savings per patient having VAB Ex instead of SBB [15]. In addition to the cost savings, Bartel notes VAB Ex will create capacity in the health system and optimise resource allocation, allowing women with cancer more timely access to surgical care. Greater consumer choice, reduced resource requirements, particularly general anaesthesia, a reduced carbon footprint and environmental impact, and encouraging and supporting innovation in delivery of healthcare are the other benefits they observed. In Western Australia, Watson et al. report the cost of VAB Ex as up to $AUS 670.5, including marker clip costs, versus $AUS 4320 for SBB, delivering a saving of AUS $3650 per procedure [16]. Panzironi et al. reported ultrasound VAB Ex costs of ~422 Euros per procedure [17].

Implementation of VAB Ex in Australia

VAB Ex use is extremely limited and ad hoc in Australia. Only one prior Australian series of VAB Ex used for excision of BBL is available, where Watson and colleagues in WA evaluated 18 low‐risk patients by VAB Ex, confirming its clinical effectiveness and patient satisfaction [16]. Surgeon‐performed ultrasound‐guided VAE has been used to excise a range of biopsy‐proven benign lesions, mostly fibroadenomas and papillomas but also phyllodes tumours, radial scars and incidental malignant lesions, where the procedure was found to be quick, safe, well tolerated and effective [18].

Inconsistencies and Gaps in Existing Clinical Guidelines

The UK breast cancer screening program launched a VAB Ex pathway in 2016. Referred to as Vacuum Assisted Excision (VAE), this procedure is offered only to subsets of BBL ≤ 15 mm [19]. In 2024, the guidelines of the European Society of Breast Cancer Specialists [11] and the 3rd edition of the European/international VAB Ex guidelines [9] endorsed VAB Ex for a range of BBL. Unlike several comparable countries, Australia has no VAB Ex guidelines.

We applaud our colleagues in the UK and Europe who have led the use of VAB Ex. Of necessity, at the outset, this was based on the opinions of experts. Since then, several other guidelines, all also opinion based, have been implemented in Switzerland [5, 9], Germany [20], Europe [11] and recently in New Zealand [21]. Being opinion‐based, the available international guidelines are inconsistent, so even if Australian clinicians wished to adopt VAB Ex, there is no basis for choosing from among a range of disparate guidelines. For example, only the UK guidelines recommend VAB Ex for ADH. Other guidelines are less definitive or exclude all atypical lesions. The latest version of the European guidelines has moved closer to the UK position in permitting VAB Ex for some ADH lesions [9]. Conversely, without offering specific justification, atypical papillary lesions are excluded from VAB Ex in the UK and Europe. We note 12.8% of women with BBL have atypical papillary lesions. Finally, for the potential benefits of VAB Ex to be experienced in Australia, the funding and care pathways for this service should be clarified. BreastScreen Australia (BSA) assessment clinics, breast surgeons, and radiology service providers in public hospitals and in private practice are the major contributors to the care of these women. Each sector has its incentives and constraints. Currently, there is no specific Medicare Benefits Schedule item number for VAB Ex. The MBS item number relating to image guided vacuum assisted breast biopsies (VABB), item 31530, applies to (a) microcalcifications; or (b) impalpable lesion less than 1 cm in diameter. In our study, the mean lesion extent was 6 mm but with a range of (range 3–15 mm). VAB Ex is arguably a more extensive procedure than VABB where rather than mere sampling, larger calibre biopsies are used for systematic complete lesion removal. The arbitrary upper size limit of 10 mm in MBS item 31530 excludes women whose BBL are larger than this size from the funding for the procedure. In the surgical literature, benign lesions up to 50 mm have been removed successfully with VAB Ex [22]. Interventional radiologists' professional judgement may be sufficient but if a specific upper lesion size limit is required for VAB Ex, 15 mm would ensure inclusion of most women with screen detected lesions. Addressing the points of intersection between the groups involved in the care of these women and clarifying the funding sources would be helpful in establishing VAB Ex as a patient centred, efficient, and cost‐effective service in Australia.

This pilot feasibility study is limited by its small size, inclusion of only patients who had low risk BBL and implementation at a single centre. Nevertheless, in the context of several years of international experience with the approach, our findings suggest VAB Ex may be of interest to Australian patients and healthcare providers.

Conclusions

In this pilot study, undertaken at a major teaching hospital, we have shown that eligible Australian women expressed a clear preference for VAB Ex in place of surgical breast biopsy, and Australian breast surgeons were key supporters of this patient‐centred innovation. By extending the use of an existing technology, VAB Ex improves pre‐operative cancer diagnosis rates beyond that achievable by core biopsies and VABB, reducing further the morbidity and costs of surgery for benign conditions. VAB Ex samples are suitable for histologic evaluation.

Unlike many comparable nations, VAB Ex is used minimally in Australia. Establishing national guidelines, clinical care pathways and a funding structure are required for this innovative technology to become available to our patients.
