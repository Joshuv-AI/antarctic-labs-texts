The mediating role of inflammatory factors in the association between dietary index for gut microbiota and hypertension
Authors: Jiaxin Li, Mingyue Yang, Wei Huang, Wenjing Shi, Xue Zhang, Rui Huang, Ying Zhang, Daowen Zhang, Weiwei Zhu, Cancan Liu, Nannan Xu, Huijun Zhang, Kuanlu Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension is a major global health concern, closely linked to cardiovascular, renal, and cerebrovascular diseases. This study investigates the relationship between the dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) and hypertension, focusing on the role of inflammatory factors as mediators. We analyzed data from 21,212 participants in the NHANES 2007–2018 dataset. The results show that higher DI-GM scores are significantly associated with a reduced risk of hypertension, particularly systolic blood pressure. Mediation analysis further reveals that inflammatory factors—specifically white blood cell count, neutrophils, and systemic inflammatory index (SII)—partially mediate this relationship. The mediating effects of white blood cell count, neutrophils, and SII account for 9.07%, 8.64%, and 3.97% of the total effect, respectively. These findings suggest that dietary patterns associated with gut microbiota health are inversely related to hypertension risk, and this association may be partially explained by systemic inflammation. This study highlights the potential of diet as a non-pharmacological strategy for managing hypertension and emphasizes the importance of gut microbiota in blood pressure regulation. Our results provide new insights into the complex interactions between diet, gut health, inflammation, and blood pressure control, suggesting that dietary modifications could be a key intervention in hypertension prevention and management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19929-0.Keywords: Dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM), Hypertension, NHANES, MediationSubject terms: Cardiovascular diseases, Endocrine system and metabolic diseases, Diseases, Endocrinology, Medical research

Introduction

Hypertension, a globally prevalent condition closely associated with vascular, cardiac, renal, and cerebrovascular diseases, significantly contributes to global mortality and imposes a substantial public health burden1. Projections suggest that the global prevalence of hypertension will decline to 20.3% by 20402. However, another study estimates that by 2025, the global hypertensive population will increase by 60% to reach 1.56 billion3. This trend underscores the significant challenge in preventing and managing hypertension globally.

A growing number of studies have found an independent and significant association between sleep disorders4, particularly insufficient hours of sleep, and hypertension. Sleep deprivation (less than 6 h) elevates the risk of hypertension by inducing metabolic disorders and inflammatory responses through mechanisms such as activation of sympathetic nerves, elevated cortisol levels, and increased energy intake. In addition, poor sleep quality may also lead to dysregulation of gut flora, which in turn affects inflammation and blood pressure regulation, suggesting that the sleep-gut-blood pressure regulatory axis deserves further attention.

Diet and lifestyle play critical roles in the development and management of hypertension. Excessive intake of salt, fat, and sugar promotes the onset of hypertension5, whereas healthy dietary patterns such as the DASH and Mediterranean diets have been shown to lower systolic and diastolic blood pressure and reduce cardiovascular risk6,7. Increasing evidence suggests that these effects are not only direct but also mediated through changes in the gut microbiota8,9. For instance, dietary fiber can increase the abundance of beneficial bacteria like Prevotella, while polyphenols promote probiotics and reduce pathogenic species10–12. In contrast, diets rich in red and processed meats may damage the gut barrier and trigger systemic inflammation13. These inflammatory responses—along with microbial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO)—may modulate vascular tone, immune response, and endocrine function, ultimately affecting blood pressure regulation14–16.

Among non-pharmacological interventions, diet is widely recognized as an important factor in blood pressure regulation. For example, DASH diet and Mediterranean diet have been shown to have significant antihypertensive effects. However, recent studies have further pointed out that diet not only acts directly on blood pressure, but also achieves its vasoprotective effects by influencing intestinal microecology. Gut flora is a key intermediary linking diet and blood pressure: a beneficial diet promotes the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), inhibits inflammation, and enhances vasodilatation, whereas a diet high in salt and fat induces dysbiosis in the gut flora and increases harmful metabolites, such as TMAO17,18. Recent studies have shown that gut flora can regulate blood pressure through multiple mechanisms17: including inflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κB, IL-6-mediated), neuroendocrine regulation (sympathetic excitation), and steroid hormone metabolism (corticosterone synthesis). Diet has an important impact on blood pressure levels by influencing the composition of the gut flora, which in turn affects the degree of activation of these pathways.

Numerous studies demonstrates that the gut microbiota can influence blood pressure, suggesting that regulating the gut microbiota profile will lead to blood pressure control19,20. The Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) is a recently proposed tool for assessing the effect of diet on gut microbiota21. The DI-GM includes a total of 14 foods or nutrients that have a beneficial and a detrimental impact of gut microbial characteristics. Beneficial ingredients include 10 ingredients such as green tea, coffee, dietary fiber, and broccoli, and unfavorable ingredients include 4 ingredients such as refined grains and processed meats. The DI-GM was selected through a systematic review of the literature and was able to accurately incorporate foods that directly improve or maintain the health of the gut microbiota. Compared to other dietary indices (e.g., HEI, MDS), the DI-GM includes well-defined foods (e.g., broccoli, avocado, etc.), which is a combination of 14 foods that accurately assesses the quality of the diet related to gut health. Previous studies have demonstrated that DI-GM correlates well with intestinal flora diversity and metabolites; however, its association with hypertension lacks direct epidemiologic study support. The 14 food items included in the DI-GM were selected based on a systematic literature review focused on dietary influences on gut microbiota composition and function21. Unlike broader indices such as the Healthy Eating Index (HEI) or the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), which evaluate overall diet quality or cardiovascular health outcomes, DI-GM specifically incorporates foods with documented impacts on microbiome diversity and metabolite production. This targeted design allows for a more mechanistic interpretation of the diet-hypertension relationship through the lens of microbiota-mediated inflammation.

Therefore, the present study, based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2007 to 2018, aimed to explore the association between DI-GM and hypertension and to further analyze the mediating role of inflammatory factors in it. This study intends to fill in the still unclear link in the diet-gut flora-inflammation-hypertension chain and enrich the evidence of cross-cutting studies in microecological nutrition and cardiovascular epidemiology.

Methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is organized and conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), part of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and is designed to collect data on the health and nutritional status of the population through a nationally representative sample. This ongoing cross-sectional survey is conducted every two years and uses stratified multistage probability sampling methods to ensure that it is representative of the non-incarcerated civilian population of the United States. Participants are interviewed with questionnaires at home and undergo detailed physical examinations and laboratory tests at an ambulatory screening center. Seven data cycles between 2007 and 2018 were used in this study to ensure broad data coverage. All participants provided informed consent prior to the start of the survey, and data collection and study methods were approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board. A detailed description of the methods can be found on the official CDC website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm).

We analyzed the 2007–2018 population in the NHANES database with the following exclusions among the initial 59,842: DI-GM, hypertension missing values (n = 19163), age ≤ 20 years (n = 11325), demographic data (e.g., PIR, alcohol consumption, smoking status, marital status, education) missing values (n = 4745), missing values for other diseases (e.g., CVD, DM) (n = 251), missing values for inflammatory factors (n = 832) and tumors, as well as patients who were pregnant (n = 2314); ultimately, 21,212 participants were included in the present study, and details of the exclusion from inclusion are shown in Fig. 1.

Diagnosis of hypertension

For the purpose of this study, the definition of hypertension followed the standards of the American Heart Association and the International Society of Hypertension. The professional examiners of the NHANES team measured the blood pressure of the participants according to the norms, which can be found on the official website of NHANES (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm). After a 5-minute quiet rest, each participant’s blood pressure was measured three times consecutively in a relaxed state. The average blood pressure values (systolic and diastolic) were calculated following a standardized protocol: diastolic blood pressure (DBP) readings of zero were excluded from the calculation, and if all DBP readings were zero, the average was set to zero. When only one blood pressure reading was available, that single reading was used as the average. In cases where multiple readings were recorded, the first reading was excluded, and the average was computed using the remaining measurements. Criteria for determining hypertension included (1) mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg; (2) mean diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg; (3) self-reported diagnosis of hypertension; or (4) taking antihypertensive medication22. This multiple-measurement averaging method improves the accuracy of blood pressure data and is widely used in epidemiologic studies to ensure the reliability of hypertension prevalence assessments.

Dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) evaluation

Based on the Kase et al. scale21, the DI-GM includes 14 foods and nutrients that are categorized as beneficial or unfavorable based on their potential impact on the gut microbiota. Beneficial ingredients included fermented dairy products, chickpeas, soy, whole grains, dietary fiber, cranberries, avocado, broccoli, coffee, and green tea, all of which were considered likely to have a positive impact on gut health. Unfavorable ingredients, on the other hand, include red meat, processed meats, refined grains, and high-fat diets (≥ 40% of energy provided by fat), which may negatively impact the gut microbiota. DI-GM scores were calculated based on NHANES dietary recall data from 2007 to 2018, and the specific scoring criteria as well as the components are shown in Table S1. Scoring was based on the sex-specific median: 1 point was assigned if beneficial ingredient intake was at or above the median and 0 points otherwise; for unfavorable components, 1 point if intake is below the median and 0 points otherwise. All 14 components in the DI-GM were equally weighted, with each assigned 1 point based on sex-specific median intake thresholds. Avocado was included not solely for its fiber content but for its consistent and multidimensional impact on gut microbiota, including SCFA production, microbial diversity, and epithelial barrier integrity. This decision was supported by multiple human studies showing that avocado intake increased the abundance of beneficial taxa (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Prevotellaceae) and enhanced fecal SCFA concentrations. Higher DI-GM scores represent dietary patterns that are potentially more beneficial to the gut microbiota. In addition, DI-GM scores were further grouped into 0–3, 4, 5, and ≥ 6, and also categorized into favorable (0–10) and unfavorable (0–4) dietary patterns based on microbiota-modulating potential.

We selected a variety of variables to ensure the accuracy of our results, including: age, sex, race, marital status, education, PIR, smoking, and drinking status, similar to published articles23. Do you smoke now?”, categorized as Never, Ever, and Current. SII was defined as platelet count multiplied by neutrophil count/lymphocyte count in 103 cells/mL25. Since classical inflammatory markers such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP) are not available in NHANES, we selected white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil (NEU) count, and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) as surrogate markers. These variables are widely used in epidemiological studies as accessible and representative proxies for systemic inflammation. Notably, a recent NHANES-based study using data from 1999 to 2018, demonstrated that higher SII levels were significantly associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with type 2 diabetes, further supporting its validity as a population-based biomarker of chronic systemic inflammation26.

Statistical analysis

Complex sampling and weighted analyses were used in this study according to the NHANES study guidelines. Baseline characteristics of participants with and without hypertension were described. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Sex differences within hypertension groups were tested using weighted t-tests for continuous variables and weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables. To better present sex-based differences, Supplementary Table 2 was split into Table 2A (non-hypertensive) and Table 2B (hypertensive), and Supplementary Table 3 into Table 3A (non-hypertensive) and Table 3B (hypertensive). These tables show sex-specific comparisons using weighted t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. To address missing data, a multiple filling method was used to minimize potential effects on the study results. To assess the association between DI-GM and hypertension and its components (systolic SBP and diastolic DBP), three multivariate weighted regression models were constructed: model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity; model 3 further included education level, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), smoking and drinking status, BMI, and history of cardiovascular disease (CVD). These covariates were selected a priori based on their established associations with both dietary patterns and blood pressure outcomes, supported by previous NHANES-based and cardiovascular epidemiology studies. Given that DI-GM is a continuous variable and its association with hypertension risk may not be linear, this study used Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) for modeling and analysis. RCS, which has the characteristics of flexible modeling and high fitting accuracy, is commonly used in nutritional epidemiology to reveal nonlinear associations between exposure variables and outcomes. In addition, we analyzed the association of DI-GM with overall risk of hypertension, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) separately using RCS to explore the potential differences in different blood pressure indicators. As SBP and DBP have different physiological regulatory mechanisms, modeling them separately helps to more precisely assess the role characteristics of DI-GM. Stratified analyses were used to explore the stability and interaction of the relationship between DI-GM and hypertension under different population characteristics (e.g., age, race, marital status, education level, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, CVD, and diabetes). In addition, to assess the mediating role of inflammatory factors in the relationship between DI-GM and hypertension, mediation effect analysis was performed, and the selected metrics included white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (NEU), and systemic inflammatory index (SII). Mediation analyses were performed using the Sobel test, Bootstrap method, and quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo simulations based on normal approximation (500 iterations), implemented using the “mediation” package of R. The mediation analysis was performed using the Sobel test, Bootstrap method, and quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo simulations based on normal approximation (500 iterations). All mediation models were adjusted for a consistent set of potential confounders, including age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, education level, PIR, smoking and drinking status, and history of cardiovascular disease. These variables were selected based on prior literature and their known relevance to both dietary patterns and hypertension risk. To ensure consistency and reduce overfitting, a fixed set of covariates was used across all models, and no stepwise selection or data-driven filtering was applied. To ensure methodological consistency, we applied the same covariate structure across both the primary regression and mediation models. Although the cross-sectional design of this study does not allow for a clear temporal causality between variables, mediation analyses can still provide exploratory insights into underlying biological mechanisms. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; https://www.R-project.org/) with the “survey” package for weighted analyses, the “mice” package for multiple imputation, and the “mediation” package for mediation analyses. The level of statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of participants in this study

A total of 21,212 participants from NHANES (2007–2018) were included in this study. Among these participants, approximately 8,780 (41.4%) were hypertensive. Approximately half of the participants were male (n = 10,643; 50.05%), with 67.59% identifying as non-Hispanic White, and the mean age was 46.29 years. Table 1 demonstrates the detailed baseline data comparison. It is clear from the table that patients with hypertension were usually older (mean age 55.27 vs. 41.12 years, p < 0.0001), more likely to be male (p = 0.01), less educated (p < 0.0001), and had lower income (PIR: 2.96 vs. 3.07, p = 0.002). Smoking (p < 0.0001) and drinking habits (p < 0.0001) also differed significantly between the two groups. In addition, for our derived DI-GM, there was also a significant difference between the two groups: the DI-GM was lower in hypertensive patients (4.62 vs. 4.74, p < 0.001). Inflammatory markers, including WBC (7.42 vs. 7.12), neutrophils (4.43 vs. 4.19), and SII (553.65 vs. 513.07), were all significantly elevated in hypertensive patients compared to their non-hypertensive counterparts (all p < 0.0001). Supplementary Table S3 was likewise divided into Table S3A and Table S3B to show the distribution of DI-GM components by sex within each hypertension group. These tables provide detailed comparisons, with corresponding p-values indicating significant differences between males and females in each group. In Table S3 we performed a baseline characterization of the specific composition of the DI-GM according to gender and the presence of hypertension. Association between DI-GM and hypertension

Table 2 demonstrates the relationship between DI-GM and hypertension. A unit increase in DI-GM was associated with a 4% reduction in hypertension risk (p < 0.001), a 30% reduction in systolic blood pressure (p = 0.003), and a 12% reduction in diastolic blood pressure (p = 0.02). After adjusting for a variety of variables, the risk of hypertension could be reduced by 8% (p < 0.001) and the reduction in systolic as well as diastolic blood pressure remained significant. After grouping DI-GM and adjusting for multiple covariates, those with DI-GM ≥ 6 could significantly reduce the risk of hypertension (OR = 0.79, 95%CI = 0.71, 0.88), although statistical significance for reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure was not observed. An increase in beneficial intestinal bacteria was associated with a decrease in hypertension risk and systolic blood pressure, whereas no significant effect was observed for diastolic blood pressure. No significant association was observed between unfavorable gut microbiota and hypertension as well as its composition after adjusting for multiple variables. Discussion

In this study, DI-GM was analyzed as a continuous and categorical variable, and an in-depth analysis of data from 21,212 NHANES revealed a significant association between DI-GM and hypertension. The results showed a negative linear association between DI-GM and hypertension, suggesting that higher DI-GM scores are strongly associated with a reduced risk of developing hypertension. This trend persisted as significant even after adjusting for multiple confounding variables. In addition, the robustness of the findings was verified by various methods, including multiple interpolation, stratified analysis, and sensitivity analysis. In this study, we analyzed the associations between DI-GM and specific blood pressure components. While DI-GM demonstrated a consistent and statistically significant inverse association with systolic blood pressure (SBP), the relationship with diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was less robust across models. This discrepancy may stem from the fact that diastolic blood pressure is more influenced by peripheral vascular resistance and neurohumoral regulation (e.g., sympathetic nervous system activity), whereas systolic blood pressure is primarily determined by large artery stiffness and vascular compliance27. This distinction has been well described in previous literature and supports our interpretation of differential responses to DI-GM. These findings offer new directions for future research to explore the variability of distinct blood pressure parameters influenced by dietary patterns. Furthermore, DI-GM may exert indirect effects on the development of hypertension through the regulation of inflammatory factors such as white blood cell count and neutrophils.

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have revealed the key role of gut flora in the development of metabolic diseases. Gut flora affects the regulatory processes of blood pressure through several mechanisms, among which the regulation of inflammatory pathways and metabolites is particularly important. First, intestinal microecological dysbiosis (dysbiosis) disrupts the intestinal barrier, resulting in bacterial metabolites (e.g., lipopolysaccharide LPS) crossing the intestinal mucosa and entering the circulation, triggering a systemic inflammatory response, which contributes to the development of hypertension28. In addition, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), an important product of dietary fiber fermentation by intestinal flora, have been shown to have anti-inflammatory and blood pressure regulating properties. SCFAs such as butyric acid (butyrate) and propionate can exert antihypertensive effects by binding to G protein-coupled receptors (e.g., GPR41, GPR43), promoting intestinal epithelial homeostasis, modulating immune responses, and thereby affecting vascular tone and the neuroendocrine axis29. Dietary interventions have been shown to reduce inflammation levels and metabolic risk by improving intestinal flora, thereby reducing inflammation levels and metabolic risk. An umbrella review study incorporating multiple meta-analyses noted that probiotic, prebiotic, and synbiotic interventions significantly reduced insulin resistance levels (HOMA-IR) and inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, TNF-alpha) in patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), further supporting the potential of microecological interventions in regulating metabolism and inflammation30. Whereas hypertension is closely associated with insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation, this implies that improving gut microecology through diet may also have indirect but significant benefits on blood pressure control31. At the molecular level, intestinal flora may further participate in the regulation of glucose metabolism and energy homeostasis by regulating bile acid metabolism, increasing GLP-1 secretion, and influencing the AMPK signaling pathway, thus indirectly affecting the mechanism of hypertension29. It is worth mentioning that different flora may play different or even opposite roles in hypertension. For example, Akkermansia muciniphila supplementation may improve metabolic status and reduce intestinal permeability, whereas certain pathogenic genera may enhance inflammatory responses and elevate blood pressure31.

Notably, specific DI-GM components exhibit biologically plausible pathways linking diet to inflammation and blood pressure regulation. Green tea, containing high concentrations of epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), suppresses NF-κB signaling through inhibition of IκB kinase (IKK) activity, thereby reducing transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α32. Furthermore, beyond its direct anti-inflammatory effects, EGCG enhances gut barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins (occludin, ZO-1) and promoting growth of SCFA-producing bacteria such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. A 12-week RCT demonstrated that daily green tea consumption significantly increased fecal acetate concentrations (21% increase vs. placebo) while reducing serum LPS-binding protein levels32, suggesting dual mechanisms synergistically improving metabolic homeostasis.

Conversely, regular consumption of processed meats (≥ 50 g/day) introduces heme iron and advanced glycation end products (AGEs) that promote inflammation through distinct pathways. Heme iron catalyzes ROS generation via Fenton reactions, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome in colonic macrophages and elevating IL-1β secretion33. Simultaneously, AGEs engage RAGE receptors to initiate MAPK/NF-κB signaling cascades, consistent with longitudinal data showing 34% higher CRP levels in high-processed meat consumers from the ATTICA cohort34. These pro-inflammatory effects are compounded by gut dysbiosis: meta-analysis evidence indicates 18% increased Proteobacteria abundance and 24% reduced Bifidobacterium following processed meat intake, accompanied by fecal calprotectin levels exceeding 50 µg/g - a clinical marker of intestinal inflammation33. The resultant endotoxin translocation activates systemic TLR4-mediated inflammation, creating a pathological loop that exacerbates hypertension risk.

Taken together, these mechanisms may underlie the heterogeneity observed in subgroup analyses stratified by gender, age, and ethnicity. In subgroup analyses of gender, age, and ethnicity, we further found possible heterogeneity in the effects of DI-GM. For example, female individuals may be more sensitive to DI-GM in terms of inflammation levels and gut microbial composition, which may be related to the modulatory effects of estrogen on vascular endothelial and inflammatory responses. Conversely, the antihypertensive effect of DI-GM appeared more pronounced in older adults, possibly due to their elevated baseline chronic inflammation. Notably, stronger associations were observed in younger individuals and non-Hispanic Whites. These disparities might stem from socioeconomic and cultural factors: younger populations and non-Hispanic Whites may have better access to nutrient-dense foods (e.g., higher income or education levels) or greater adherence to microbiota-friendly dietary patterns (e.g., Mediterranean diets). Prior evidence indicates that socioeconomic status strongly predicts dietary quality, with marginalized groups often facing barriers to healthy food access35. Cultural preferences (e.g., higher processed meat consumption in certain ethnicities) may further modulate the DI-GM’s applicability. On the other hand, no significant differences were observed in other subgroup analyses (e.g., BMI, smoking and drinking status), which may be related to the sample size or baseline characteristics of certain groups. These results suggest that differences between various subgroups need to be validated in further studies to provide tailored dietary intervention strategies for high-risk populations.

In recent years, research on diet in the management of hypertension has received considerable attention6,35. The DASH diet, a dietary pattern designed to lower blood pressure, emphasizes the intake of whole grains, vegetables, and fruits while limiting saturated fat and sugar intake. Several randomized controlled trials have shown that the DASH diet is not only effective in lowering blood pressure, but also has significant cardiovascular health benefits36. In addition, the Healthy Eating Index (HEI), a tool for measuring diet quality, has been shown to be strongly associated with the risk of obesity, metabolic disorders, and chronic diseases37.

The results of the present study are broadly consistent with these patterns: individuals with higher DI-GM scores had a lower risk of developing hypertension, echoing the benefits observed with the DASH and Mediterranean diets. However, unlike these traditional indices that focus primarily on nutrient balance or disease risk, DI-GM is designed to reflect the microbiota-modulating potential of dietary patterns.

Our findings also align with previous studies on individual dietary components—such as sodium, fiber, and polyphenols—and their effects on gut microbiota and blood pressure regulation. High sodium intake reduces Lactobacillus abundance and promotes Th17-mediated inflammation. In contrast, fiber fosters the growth of SCFA-producing bacteria (Faecalibacterium, Roseburia), enhancing gut barrier function and reducing systemic inflammation. Polyphenols from green tea or berries modulate microbiota composition and inhibit pro-inflammatory pathways like NF-κB.

By focusing on dietary components that influence microbial ecology and downstream inflammatory pathways, the DI-GM offers a complementary perspective to traditional diet indices. This mechanistic orientation enhances our understanding of how diet impacts hypertension through gut microbiota and inflammation, providing insights for future precision nutrition strategies.

While traditional dietary indices such as DASH and HEI emphasize nutrient balance or cardiovascular risk reduction, the DI-GM provides a distinct approach by focusing on dietary patterns that influence microbial composition and metabolite production. This microbiota-specific orientation offers complementary insight into diet–microbiota–inflammation–blood pressure pathways, and may inform precision nutrition strategies targeting gut health.

Despite the large-scale data analysis and important findings of this study based on the NHANES database, there are still some limitations that need to be noted. First, given the cross-sectional design, we cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causation. Individuals with existing hypertension may modify their dietary behaviors, such as reducing processed meat or increasing fiber intake, which could artificially elevate their DI-GM scores. Similarly, chronic hypertension can induce vascular endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress, thereby amplifying systemic inflammation. Furthermore, antihypertensive treatment may potentially influence inflammatory markers such as white blood cell (WBC) counts or neutrophil levels. To address this limitation, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding participants using antihypertensive medications (n = 13,679), which yielded consistent results (Table S4). Although this approach helps reduce bias from disease management behaviors, it does not fully resolve the temporal ambiguity between diet, inflammation, and hypertension. Therefore, while our findings offer valuable insights into the diet-inflammation-blood pressure axis, they should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Future prospective cohort studies with repeated biomarker assessments and randomized controlled trials targeting DI-GM modifications are needed to confirm these temporal relationships. We also included Fig. 3 to illustrate the hypothesized mediation pathways, which effectively functions as a conceptual directed acyclic graph (DAG). Second, the inflammatory factors (e.g., WBC and SII) used in this study may not be sufficient to comprehensively reflect the chronic inflammatory state, and more inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α) can be introduced in the future to increase the precision of measurement. In addition, the DI-GM, a scoring system based on the potential impact of diet on gut flora, does not directly measure flora diversity or abundance and does not cover nutritional factors that are classically associated with hypertension, such as sodium and potassium intake; participant dietary information may also be affected by recall bias. Third, while DI-GM has shown good correlation with gut microbiota composition in U.S. populations, its applicability in populations with distinct dietary patterns (e.g., high intake of fermented foods in East Asia or plant-based diets in South Asia) remains uncertain. Regional variations in microbiota composition and dietary habits may require adaptation of DI-GM for broader use. Future studies should validate this index in diverse cultural and geographic settings to assess its generalizability.

In summary, this study validated the significant association between DI-GM and hypertension based on a large sample of data and revealed that inflammation may play a partial mediating role. The results support the potential application of microecology-based dietary intervention strategies in the prevention and control of hypertension, and future studies should focus on mechanism validation, longitudinal design, and cross-population extrapolation to advance the deeper understanding of the “diet-gut flora-inflammation-blood pressure” pathway. Conclusion

This study identified a significant negative association between DI-GM and hypertension risk using data from NHANES 2007–2018, with stronger effects observed for systolic than diastolic blood pressure. Subgroup analyses suggested that age and ethnicity may influence this association, underscoring the importance of individualized dietary strategies.

Inflammatory factors, including white blood cell count, neutrophils, and systemic inflammatory index (SII), were found to partially mediate the relationship between DI-GM and hypertension. These findings offer insight into the mechanisms linking diet, inflammation, and blood pressure regulation.

Building on established frameworks like the DASH and Mediterranean diets, DI-GM-informed guidance may emphasize increased intake of fiber-rich plant foods, fermented products, and polyphenol-rich items to support gut health and lower hypertension risk. Future randomized controlled trials are warranted to evaluate the impact of DI-GM-based interventions—such as personalized dietary plans or microbiota-targeted education—on inflammatory markers, gut microbiota composition, and long-term blood pressure outcomes.
