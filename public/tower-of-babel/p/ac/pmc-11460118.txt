Dynamic landscape of m6A modifications and related post-transcriptional events in muscle-invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Lili Zhang, Ziwei Chen, Gaoyuan Sun, Chang Li, Pengjie Wu, Wenrui Xu, Hui Zhu, Zaifeng Zhang, Yongbin Tang, Yayu Li, Yifei Li, Siyuan Xu, Hexin Li, Meng Chen, Fei Xiao, Yaqun Zhang, Wei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMuscle-invasive bladder carcinoma (MIBC) is a serious and more advanced stage of bladder carcinoma. N6-Methyladenosine (m6A) is a dynamic and reversible modifications that primarily affects RNA stability and alternative splicing. The dysregulation of m6A in MIBC can be potential target for clinical interventions, but there have been limited studies on m6A modifications in MIBC and their associations with post-transcriptional regulatory processes.MethodsPaired tumor and adjacent-normal tissues were obtained from three patients with MIBC following radical cystectomy. The additional paired tissues for validation were obtained from patients underwent transurethral resection. Utilizing Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing, we characterized the m6A RNA methylation landscape in MIBC, with a focus on identifying post-transcriptional events potentially affected by changes in m6A sites. This included an examination of differential transcript usage, polyadenylation signal sites, and variations in poly(A) tail length, providing insights into the broader impact of m6A alterations on RNA processing in MIBC.ResultsThe prognostic-related m6A genes and m6A-risk model constructed by machine learning enables the stratification of high and low-risk patients with precision. A novel m6A modification site in the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of IGLL5 gene were identified, characterized by a lower m6A methylation ratio, elongated poly(A) tails, and a notable bias in transcript usage. Furthermore, we discovered two particular transcripts, VWA1-203 and CEBPB-201. VWA1-203 displayed diminished m6A methylation levels, a truncated 3’UTR, and an elongated poly(A) tail, whereas CEBPB-201 showed opposite trends, highlighting the complex interplay between m6A modifications and RNA processing. Source code was provided on GitHub (https://github.com/lelelililele/Nanopore-m6A-analysis).ConclusionsThe state-of-the-art Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing and machine learning techniques enables comprehensive identification of m6A modification and provided insights into the potential post-transcriptional regulation mechanisms on the development and progression in MIBC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-024-05701-x.Keywords: Nanopore sequencing, m6A modification, Muscle-invasive bladder cancer, Transcriptional events

Background

Bladder Cancer is one of the most common cancers, with approximately 573,000 new cases and 213,000 deaths estimated worldwide in 2019, and the mortality rate of bladder cancer was ranked No. 9th among all cancers [1–3]. Bladder cancer is a heterogeneous disease which can be roughly grouped into muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) and non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) based on the presence or absence of the depth of tumor invasion. The majority of NMIBC cases are highly treatable, boasting a 5-year overall survival rate that approaches 90%. In contrast, the prognosis for MIBC is considerably more grave, with a 5-year overall survival rate that hovers between 40 and 50% [4]. Despite undergoing radical cystectomy, more than half of the MIBC patients will ultimately succumb to distant metastasis. To date, a series of innovative treatments, such as cisplatin-based chemotherapy, carboplatin-based chemotherapy, immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors, as well as fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) and antibody drug conjugates (ADCs), and antiangiogenic therapy [5], were added into regimen as supplementary options, which further complicates the treatment and decision making, posing a challenge on better understanding the biology of disease and identifying effective biomarkers to predict the outcomes [5].

Epitranscriptome is an essential internal cotranscriptional modifications in gene expression [6] and has been reported to be associated with a myriad of disease including bladder cancer [3]. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is the most abundant internal epigenetic modification in mRNA among eukaryotes as well as in microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and circular RNAs [6]. m6A modification exerts various functions in RNA processing and metabolism, such as alternative splicing (AS) [7], export [8], translation [9], and RNA stability [10], and thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of various conditions [11]. Emerging evidence have shown that RNA m6A methylation also plays essential role in the development and progression of various cancers [12]. This epigenetic modification not only influences the expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes [3, 13, 14], but also regulates cancer cell behaviors, including self-renewal, differentiation, and invasion [15–17]. In bladder cancer, it has been reported that RNA m6A modification can regulate the self-renewal process of bladder cancer stem cells [18] in response to the stress and DNA damage [3, 19]. The m6A modification is meticulously regulated by a suite of methyltransferase enzymes, including “writers”, such as Methyltransferase-like 3/14/16 (METTL3/14/16), Wilms’ tumor 1-associating protein (WTAP), RNA binding motif protein 15 (RBM15) or its isoform RBM15B, and Cbl proto-oncogene-like 1 (CBLL1), “erasers”, such as Fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5), and “readers”, such as YTH N6-methyltransferase domain-containing protein 1/2/3 (YTHDF1/2/3), YTH domain-containing protein 1/2 (YTHDC1/2), Insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1/2/3 (IGF2BP1/2/3), and Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A2/B1 (HNRNPA2B1) [20]. The concordance of these regulators on RNA methylation upon the stimuli of environment and cellular context determines the cell fate. The m6A reader YTHDC1 regulates mRNA splicing by recruiting pre-mRNA splicing factors Serine/Arginine-Rich Splicing Factor 3 (SRSF3) and Serine/Arginine-Rich Splicing Factor 10 (SRSF10) to binding regions of targeted mRNAs [21]. The writer METTL10, an ortholog of METTL16, inhibits RNA splicing and protein production by depositing an m6A mark on the 3’ splice site of pre-mRNA at the S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) synthetase [22]. To date, research on m6A has predominantly concentrated on the investigation of upstream m6A regulatory elements, while a comprehensive characterization of m6A modification patterns and an in-depth understanding of the intrinsic functions of m6A remain limited.

m6A was found to be associated with alternative polyadenylation (APA) [23]. APA is an RNA-processing mechanism that generates numerous transcript isoforms containing distinct 3’ polyadenylation signal (PAS) sites from a single gene locus. APA exerts functions on mRNA maturation, mRNA stability, cellular RNA decay, and protein diversification. By employing these sites in different ways, distinct mRNA isoforms with unique 3’ untranslated regions (3’UTRs) can be generated, which in turn influences the stability of the mRNA [24, 25]. Changes in the 3’UTR can affect the fate of mature mRNA by affecting sequences that harbor cis-regulatory elements identified by miRNAs or RNA binding protein (RBP) [26]. The mRNA stability can also be impacted by modulating the deposition of m6A and the length of poly(A) tails [27]. The poly(A) tail is not a simple static entity but rather a dynamic entity whose length can significantly influence the biological destiny of a transcript [28]. Reduced transcription dynamics of specific genes leads to enhanced m6A deposition, shortened poly(A) tails, and reduced stability of the respective mRNAs [27]. However, the specific role of m6A in the 3’UTR and its influence on the dynamics of poly(A) tails are not yet fully understood.

RNA immunoprecipitation dependent sequencing method (MeRIP-seq) [29, 30] was applied to transcriptome-wide profiling of m6A modifications. This technique involves the selective enrichment of m6A-modified RNA fragments through immunoprecipitation and the identification of enrichment peaks. The accuracy of MeRIP-seq is impaired by high GC contents during sequencing. Notably, m6A tended to occur in the 3’UTR region, where the GC contents are as high as 42.4 ± 11%, generally higher than the genome-wide average of 41% [31, 32]. In addition, the demanding requirement for RNA library construction and strong batch effects in second-generation sequencing prevents parallel comparison of the outputs from multiple targeted sequencing, thus further jeopardizes the investigation of the relationship between m6A and post-transcriptional events. On contrast, Oxford Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing (the third generation of sequencing) identifies the RNA base directly via electrical signals, which enables transcriptome-wide profiling of m6A modifications even at regions with high GC contents. Moreover, long-reads sequencing capability of Nanopore reduces the frequency of alignments in mapping process of traditional methods. Leveraging Nanopore sequencing, it is the first study that jointly explored the relationship between m6A modifications and the post-transcriptional events including AS, APA, and poly(A) tails.

Methods

Patients and samples

Bladder cancer patients were recruited from Beijing Hospital. Patients were all newly diagnosed within one year prior to recruitment, histologically confirmed and previously untreated with chemotherapy, Immunotherapy, and radiotherapy. Patients with a long history (more than 1 year) of non-muscle-invasive disease before referral to Beijing Hospital or with a prior medical history of recurrence were excluded. Paired tumor and adjacent-normal tissues were obtained from radical cystectomy of MIBC patients at Beijing Hospital, among which three pairs as training set and other three pairs as validation set in data analysis. There was no restriction on age, gender, and smoking status in this pilot study with a limited sample size. All fresh tissues were immediately collected and stored in liquid nitrogen before testing. All samples were examined by experienced pathologists, confirming that the tumor tissues comprised > 80% malignant cells and the adjacent-normal tissues comprised normal stromal cells as well as epithelial cells. This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital and Human Genetic Resource Administration of China (HGRAC), and all participants have signed consent forms before testing.

Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions for steps of RNA precipitation, washing, and solubilization. The quality of RNA was checked by Capillary Electrophoresis System and at least 500 ng of mRNA was reserved for Oxford Nanopore Direct-RNA Sequencing. The RNA library preparation was constructed following the manufacturer’s instructions (version: DRS_9080_v2_revT_14Aug2019). The Oxford Nanopore sequencing platform GridION [33, 34] with an R9.4.1 chip (Oxford Nanopore Technology) was utilized for RNA library sequencing. FLO-MIN106 sequencing flow cells along with the EXP-WSH004 flow cell wash kit (Oxford Nanopore Technology) and the SQK-RNA002 direct RNA Sequencing Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technology) were used for direct RNA sequencing. After the library samples passed quality checks, they were added to the chip. The raw electronic signals were collected by MinKNOW software (v3.5.10) [35].

RNA m6A profiling in MIBC

After obtaining fasta5 files from Nanopore sequencing, the electrical signals need to be basecalled, signal re-squiggled and mapped to reference genome. For the first part, signals were basecalled using Guppy (v3.2.4) (https://community.nanoporetech.com/downloads) [36]. Signal re-squiggle and sequence alignment were then performed using Tombo (v1.5.1) (https://github.com/nanoporetech/tombo) with parameter “--rna --corrected-group RawGenomeCorrected_001 --basecall-group Basecall_1D_000” and minimap2 (v2.21) (https://github.com/lh3/minimap2) with “-ax map-ont –L --secondary = no” [37] respectively. Gencode (v35) were used for human genome annotation [38]. m6A analysis was conducted using DENA [39], which involved four main steps: selecting m6A sites, extracting features, training the model, and predicting the m6A sites. Only sites with a read count greater than two were kept for consideration. For m6A ratio, the number of modified reads divided by the total number of reads, was calculated for each site. The upregulated methylation site was defined as a site has higher m6A ratio in tumor than that in control tissue, while downregulated methylation site is vice versa. The altered m6A sites was defined as the sites with p-value of paired t.test < 0.1 when m6A ratio in tumor compares to adjacent-normal tissues. Additional tools including xPore [40] and m6A-net [41] with default parameter were also attempted while obtaining altered m6A sites and the final m6A ratio was calculated by DENA.

Analysis for m6A related post-transcriptional events

Transcript quantification was performed by NanoCount [42]. Differential transcript usage analysis for proportional differences in transcript composition was conducted using Salmon [43] and DTUrtle [44]. To examine the distribution of m6A sites across the genome, a sliding window of 1 M in size was employed, and the resulting data were visualized by RIdeogram [45] package. The metagene plot was created using the metagene2 package (https://github.com/ArnaudDroitLab/metagene2). The length of poly(A) tails was estimated by Nanopolish [46]. APA analysis was performed by QAPA [26], which employed polyadenylation site annotations from small amounts of Nanopore sequencing to accurately measure variations in APA across conditions. miRNA target information was predicted by miRDB [47], and transcript isoforms were visualized using ggtranscript [48].

Functional annotation

GREAT [49] was employed for m6A site functional annotation of altered m6A sites. The input set was all altered m6A sites, in contrast of the background set with all m6A sites in tissues. WhichTF [50] and JASPAR enrichment analysis were applied to identify functional proteins in MIBC regulated by m6A sites [51]. WhichTF [50] provided an ordered list of functional related transcription factors (TFs). JASPAR enrichment tool [51] predicted binding motifs of these enriched TFs from the JASPAR database [52] in the altered m6A modification regions. The RBPmap software [53] were adopted to predict the m6A-related RBPs. The input sequence provided to RBPmap was the 20 bp sequences surrounding the m6A sites.

Machine learning about survival analysis of MIBC

The TCGA Bladder Cancer transcriptome datasets were downloaded from Xena [54]. The subsequent analysis was limited to the population with histological type of muscle invasive urothelial carcinoma, and the population was randomized and grouped into training set and a validation set in 1:9 ratio to ensure impartiality. The end point of study was overall survival, which was calculated from the date of diagnosis to death or the date of last follow-up whichever came first. LASSO regression, a prevalent machine learning method, was employed to identify potential signature variables in Cox regression analysis, focusing on m6A-related genes that is genes with altered m6A site. This analysis was validated using 10-fold cross-validation. The variables with non-zero coefficients, satisfying the assumption of proportional hazards hypothesis, were incorporate into the multivariate Cox regression analysis. The stepwise algorithm was utilized for model selection. Using the m6A-risk model, we assigned a risk score to each sample, with the median score serving as the cut point in the figure. In our research, risk score was 0.178 * FKBP10 + 0.193 * EMP1–0.278 * ZNF524–0.317 * YPEL5–0.574 * BTN2A1–0.551 * DDA1 + 0.564 * EIF3C − 0.291 * RPL13AP5–0.333 * INPP1–0.23 * APOL6 + 0.301 * TMEM109. The code for construction of model was uploaded to GitHub (https://github.com/lelelililele/Nanopore-m6A-analysis/blob/main/STEP2_model.R). R packages survival (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survival), glmnet [55], pROC [56], and survminer (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/survminer/index.html) were used for survival analysis.

The verification of de novo RNA m6A sites and APA

The wet lab experiment was performed in MIBC patients from validation set. A modified protocol was utilized based on a previously described method [57]. For the m6A reverse transcription reaction, 10 µL of solution was prepared by adding 160ng of RNA (1 µl), 1µM of (+) or (-) primer (2 µl), 50 µM of dNTPs (1µL), 0.2µL of Bstl (Bacillusstearothermophilus) (NEB) or 0.5 µL of M-MuLV RT (MRT) (NEB), and 10 × ThermoPol reaction buffer (1µL) or 10 × M-MuLV reverse transcriptase reaction buffer (1µL), and H2O was added to each reaction tuber until a final volume of 10 µL was achieved. The cycling conditions were: 45 °C for 15 min, 85 °C for 3 min, and 4℃ indefinitely for BstI and 42 °C for 30 min, 85 °C for 3 min, and 4℃ indefinitely for MRT. For each PCR, 25 µL of solution was prepared, including RT reaction (1µL), 2 × Taq Master Mix (Vazyme, 12.5µL), and primer mix (10 µM, 1µL), and H2O was added to reach a final volume of 25 µL in each reaction tube. The cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 15 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min, with a holding temperature of 4℃. The same volume of reaction products was loaded for agarose gel electrophoresis. For the qPCR, 10 µL of solution was prepared for each reaction, containing RT reaction (1µL), 2 × Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, 5µL), and primer mix (10 µM, 0.2 µL), and H2O was added to reach a final volume of 10 µL in each reaction tube. The cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 50 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and a final step of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s, 95 °C for 15 s. The cycle threshold (Ct) values were calculated afterward. The primer sequences were listed in the Table S1.

To validate the new m6A site, we utilized a previously established method involving the BstI enzyme and MRT enzyme for retrotranscription (RT) of m6A residues. MRT is an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase that is commonly employed in cDNA synthesis. Additionally, BstI polymerase possesses an intrinsic retrotranscriptase activity. This approach capitalizes on the diminished capability of the BstI enzyme to retrotranscribe m6A nucleotides, allowing for the determination of the relative quantification of candidate m6A regions. MIBC tissue were used for validating m6A sites. Two reverse primers were designed targeting the mRNA methylation sites of IGLL5. One primer was positioned adjacent to the identified m6A residue as primer (+), while the other primer was positioned away from any nearby m6A modification as primer (−). Subsequently, RNA was extracted from tumor tissues and RT reactions using BstI or MRT enzymes. The resulting RT reaction products were then amplified using PCR and QPCR techniques. The HPRT gene served as a negative control for m6A, without any methylation site as previously described. After performing PCR amplification, we observed higher RT efficiency when using primer (-) in combination with BstI at the m6A RNA methylation site of candidate gene compared to using primer (+). HPRT, which has no methylation sites, exhibited similar band intensities between primer (-) and primer (+) in combination with BstI.

The 3’RACE assay was employed to verify APA in tumor and adjacent-normal tissues using the HiScript-TS 5’/3’ RACE Kit (Vazyme RA101). Briefly, cDNA was generated from 1 µg RNA from tumor and adjacent-normal tissues using enzyme mix (reverse transcriptase) and 3’CDS primer in the kit. Two primers including a gene-specific forward primer in the coding area (5’-GGACCGCCAGCCGTGAGCCGTAA-3’) and a reverse primer (Universal Primer Mix) targeting the 3’CDS primer region, were used to PCR amplification of 3’UTR forms of VWA1-203, The PCR product was checked by 1% agarose gel.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). An unpaired Student’s t-test was used for assessing statistical differences between the two groups. The results with P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The overall m6A profile in MIBC

To explore the m6A alterations in MIBC, we conducted Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing on paired tumor and adjacent-normal tissues from three MIBC patients (Supplementary Figure S1). The m6A profile exhibited a widespread distribution across the genome, and the distribution of m6A sites and genes displayed a different pattern (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Figure S2A). Next, we detected the distribution of m6A sites among different regions, and found that m6A sites showed an enrichment in the 3’UTR, followed by the exon regions (Fig. 1B). Given the inherent high prevalence of the 3’UTR and coding sequence (CDS) within the genome, we derived a normalized proportion by dividing the observed distribution by the expected distribution. Our subsequent analysis further confirmed that m6A sites predominantly occur in the 3’UTR and CDS regions, and the third most represented were pseudogenes (Fig. 1C). Finally, we detected the sequencing coverage to eliminate the impact of Nanopore artifacts. The distribution of m6A sites revealed two peaks in the 3’UTR region (Fig. 1D), which is independent of the sequencing coverage (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Figure S2B).

We also conducted a differential gene analysis between the tumor and adjacent-normal tissues (Fig. 1F). Our findings revealed that the clustering based on the expression profiles of m6A regulators could effectively differentiate MIBC tumor tissues from adjacent-normal tissues (Fig. 1G), and the transcripts of these regulators were categorized into three clusters by the elbow method (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Figure S2C, Table S2), indicating that the m6A methylation landscape within MIBC tumors may be modulated by distinct m6A regulators. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the differentially expressed genes pinpointed key pathways implicated in MIBC, notably the PI3K-AKT signaling cascade, VEGF pathway, MAPK signaling, and Wnt pathway, (Fig. 1H). These findings are in alignment with established research, corroborating the significant role of these pathways in the pathogenesis of MIBC [58, 59].

Taken together, these results suggested that the m6A methylation sites are extensively dispersed throughout the mRNA in MIBC tissues, with a notable prevalence in the 3’UTR. Moreover, it may be involved in MIBC progression through that action of m6A regulators.

The function prediction of m6A modifications

To predict the function of m6A modifications, we analyzed the altered m6A sites between the tumor and adjacent-normal tissues (Table S3). The altered m6A sites in MIBC were widely spread across the gene region and didn’t display a significant enrichment (Fig. 2A). The pathway enrichment analysis among the m6A modified genes revealed an enrichment in the pathways related to MIBC development, such as VEGF-related pathway, stem cell pathways (Notch and Wnt), and FGFR-related pathway (Fig. 2B), which was in consistence with the above results of differential gene expression analysis (Fig. 1G). This finding indicates that m6A methylation potentially contributes to tumor progression by modulating the expression of genes integral to the genesis and evolution of MIBC.

Additionally, we conducted a thorough analysis on the transcription factors (TFs)’s targets potentially affected by m6A sites. Notably, no substantial variations were observed in the distribution of the transcription start site (TSS) (Fig. 2C), suggesting that their influence on the initiation of transcription may be limited. Then we identified the top 10 TFs with altered m6A modifications, including HOX, FOX, NR1, NF-κB-related, and p53-related families (Fig. 2D and E), which are key in cellular development and carcinogenesis [60, 61], implying m6A’s regulatory role via TFs. We also predicted RBPs that could bind m6A sites (Table S4), setting the stage for further MIBC research.

Taken together, these results suggest that m6A may contribute to tumorigenesis by modulating the expression of MIBC-related genes and potentially altering the function of TFs and RBPs.

Prognostic model with m6A altered genes in MIBC patients

We conducted an analysis to assess the impact of genes exhibiting altered m6A methylation on the prognosis of MIBC patients utilizing the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. Employing a Lasso regression approach coupled with a stepwise model selection strategy, we identified a cohort of 11 genes that were significantly associated with patient survival outcomes in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model (Fig. 3A). Based on this model, we calculated the risk score of each MIBC patients (Fig. 3B). A threshold of 1.32 was used to dichotomize the MICB patients into high-risk and low-risk groups considering the distribution of patients in terms of risk score (Fig. 3B). Among these 11 genes, four genes (TMEM109, EIF3C, EMP1, and FKBP10) were correlated with a higher risk of prognosis [hazard ratio (HR) > 1], while seven genes (ZNF524, YPEL5, BTN2A1, DDA1, RPL13AP5, INPP1, and APOL6) acted as protective factors (HR < 1) (Fig. 3A and C). In the high-risk group, genes that were assigned a high prognostic score demonstrated elevated levels of mRNA expression (Fig. 3C). Conversely, genes with a lower prognostic score exhibited reduced mRNA expression levels (Fig. 3C). The Log-rank test revealed a significant difference in survival time between the high-risk and low-risk group (Fig. 3D). The median survival time (MST) in the high-risk group was 413 days, while the low-risk group significantly exceeded this, reaching over 5050 days (Fig. 3D). The goodness of fit for Cox Proportional Hazards assumption were met in the model (Supplementary Figure S3). The accuracy for model prediction is more than 70% in ROC curves (Fig. 3E and F), suggests that this model possesses a commendable degree of precision in its predictive capabilities.

Additionally, given the presence of a high number of m6A modification sites in pseudogenes, we also developed a prognostic risk model for m6A-modified pseudogenes (Supplementary Figure S4 and S5). A total of 3 pseudogenes (NSUN5P2, RPL13AP5, and ATP8B5P) were significantly associated with patient survival outcomes (Supplementary Figure S4A). A threshold of 1.24 was utilized to categorize the MIBC patients into high-risk and low-risk groups (Supplementary Figure S4B and S4C). However, the prognostic efficacy of this model was not as effective as the model constructed with the m6A modified genes (Supplementary Figure S5A and S5B).

Isoform level alterations of IGLL5 in MIBC patients

The Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing, with its capability for long-read coverage, allows for the concurrent profiling of m6A modifications and gene expression at the isoform level, as well as the precise measurement of poly(A) tail lengths at the transcript isoform level. This comprehensive approach provides a nuanced view of the post-transcriptional landscape, facilitating a deeper understanding of RNA processing and regulation.

The Immunoglobulin Lambda Like Polypeptide 5 (IGLL5) is a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily. It has been found that IGLL5 is associated with tumor-infiltrating immune cells in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and may have the potential to serve as a prognostic biomarker for ccRCC [62]. In the human genome, the GC content is enriched in the 3’UTRs, averaging 42.4 ± 11% [32], surpassing the genome-wide average GC content of 41% [31]. Notably, the GC content in the 3’UTR of the IGLL5 gene reaches 51.5%, significantly exceeding the average GC content of 3’UTRs across the genome (Fig. 4A). The high GC content significantly increases the error rate during sequencing. Nonetheless, the application of Nanopore sequencing technology adeptly addresses this issue by counteracting GC bias, thereby facilitating the precise identification of de novo methylation events. As a result, we identified a new m6A site (highlighted in red in Fig. 4B) in IGLL5 and found higher m6A levels in adjacent-normal tissues (Fig. 4C). Then the Real-Time Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) based assay was adopt to validate the de novo m6A modification of IGLL5 in MIBC (Fig. 4D and E). This assay can validate the residue specific m6A RNA methylation leveraging the diminished capacity of Bstl (Bacillusstearothermophilus) enzyme to retrotranscribe m6A residues [57]. The PCR of the retrotranscribed samples in IGLL5 showed a lighter band only in the presence of Bstl enzyme and primer (+) lane compared to the band in Bstl enzyme and primer (-) lane, M-MuLV RT (MRT) enzyme and primer (+) lane, and MRT enzyme and primer (-) lane, confirming the existence of de novo RNA m6A methylation site in IGLL5 found in MIBC from Nanopore sequencing (Fig. 4E). Additionally, based on the known microRNAs and RBP database, we predicted some microRNAs, including miR-193b-5p, miR-6855-5p, miR-3170, and miR-5572, and RBPs, including the tumor suppressor Six3 and the MIBC prognostic factor Nr1h3::Rxra, that may bind to this m6A methylation site (Fig. 4B).

The IGLL5 gene has three isoforms (IGLL5-201, 202, and 203) resulting from alternative splicing. In this study, leveraging differential transcript usage (DTU) analysis, we identified an isoform switch with the presence of IGLL5-201 and IGLL5-203 in the tumor and adjacent-normal tissues. Specifically, although the overall expression of IGLL5 showed no significant difference between tumor and adjacent-normal tissues (Fig. 4F), the isoform switch was found that the dominant isoform was IGLL5-201 (72.1% in adjacent-normal tissues and 26.1% in MIBC tissues) in adjacent-normal tissues and IGLL5-203 (27.9% in adjacent-normal tissues and 73.9% in MIBC tissues) in tumor tissues (Fig. 4G).

Furthermore, the relative length of poly(A) tails of two IGLL5 isoforms were measured, and there was a significant difference between MIBC tumor and adjacent-normal tissues. The length of poly(A) tails of IGLL5-201 and IGLL5-203 were notably longer in tumor tissues (Fig. 4H). However, in case of IGLL5, no correlation was found between the methylation values of the m6A site and the length of the poly(A) tails in each sequencing read (Fig. 4I).

Taken together, we found that the tumor tissues exhibit a decreased m6A level, longer poly(A) tails in IGLL5 gene, as well as the isoform usage switch, compared with the adjacent-normal tissues. These findings imply that IGLL5 may play a role in the advancement of tumorigenesis and the modulation of the tumor microenvironment. Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing provides us with the possibility of simultaneously and precisely observing multiple post-transcriptional regulatory events.

Screening of PAS in MIBC and related m6A sites

It has been reported that the 3’UTR modification correlates with APA, a process that affect mRNA stability, translation, and cellular localization [63]. Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing can identify changes in m6A sites and PAS simultaneously. During the screening analysis, we observed significant alterations in both the m6A methylation level and PAS within the 3’UTR of the VWA1 and CEBPB gene (Fig. 5A-5C). These two genes were significantly associated with patient survival outcomes according to the TCGA dataset (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Figure S6A). Specifically, in transcript VWA1-203, the length of 3’UTR was shorter, and the m6A methylation level was lower in tumor tissues compared with adjacent-normal tissues (Fig. 5B and E). While in transcript CEBPB-201, we observed an increased m6A methylation level and longer 3’UTR length in the tumor tissues (Supplementary Figure S6B and Fig. 6C). The RT-qPCR assays confirmed the presence of m6A sites in these two genes within MIBC tissues (Fig. 5F and G, and Supplementary Figure S6C). We also conducted a 3’ Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends (3’ RACE) experiment and discovered that in the VWA1-203, the length of the 3’UTR in tumor tissue is shorter than that in the adjacent-normal tissue (Fig. 5H).

The altered sequences in the 3’UTR could influence regulatory elements such as miRNA targets. Therefore, we predicted the potentially influenced miRNAs in these regions (Table S5). Additionally, we assessed the length of poly(A) tails and observed a notable elongation of the poly(A) tail in VWA1-203 in tumor tissues (Fig. 5I), whereas there was no significant variation in CEBPB-201 (Supplementary Figure S6D).

Taken together, these findings suggested that in tumor tissues, VWA1-203 had a lower m6A ratio, a shorter 3’UTR, and a longer poly(A) tail length. Conversely, CEBPB-201 showed opposite trends. These differential m6A ratio, poly(A) tail length, as well as APA, may have implications for gene expression patterns in the context of tumorigenesis.

Discussion

Nanopore sequencing is a native RNA sequencing strategy that allows for the sequencing of full-length mRNA molecules without GC bias or amplification artifacts [34]. The library preparation preserves the full epigenetic information in the molecule to be sequenced, enabling the identification of associations among epigenetic modification, alternative splicing, alternative polyadenylation, and poly(A) tail length. Unlike previous sequencing methods, Nanopore sequencing can quantify m6A methylation value due to its modification-specific signals for each read. In addition, it can avoid batch effects between experiments. Utilizing the advantage of Nanopore sequencing technology, our study combined epigenetic modification and post-transcriptional events in transcriptome sequencing scale. The current study employed tissues taken from radical cystectomy, which identified altered m6A sites between tumor and adjacent-normal tissues. Additionally, the verified assay tissues were gathered from trans urethral removal of tumors. The functional enrichment analysis indicated that these altered m6A sites were strongly related to tumor hallmark genes and pathways. These m6A sites construct an effective m6A-risk model that can accurately classify patients as high-risk or low-risk for MIBC. In this model, four m6A-associated genes TMEM109, EIF3C, EMP1 and FKBP10 were upregulated in the high-risk group, and ZNF524, YPEL5, BTN2A1, DDA1, RPL13AP5, INPP1, and APOL6 showed an opposite expression pattern (Fig. 4A). Among these genes, EIF3C [64], FKBP10 [65], DDA1 [66]and APOL6 [67] were associated with cancer prognosis. Specifically, EMP1 [68] have been implicated in the regulation of bladder cancer. BTN2A1 expression in cancer cells was correlated with T cell cytotoxicity [69], and YPEL5 has been reported functional in clear cell renal cell cancer and chronic lymphocytic leukemia [70, 71]. Since the m6A sites were found highly enriched in pseudogenes (Fig. 1E), we attempted to construct an m6A-risk model using m6A-related pseudogenes (Figure S2 and S3). However, it was not as effective as the model constructed using m6A-related genes directly.

The m6A modification serves as a signal for mRNA splicing and as a marker for recruiting splicing factors, ultimately affecting alternative splicing [7]. During our analysis of differential transcript usage in MIBC, we focused on the position relationship between altered m6A sites, splice sites, and branch points. However, we did not identify any connections between them. Previous research has also shown that m6A regulates alternative splicing in a specific way rather than on a genome-wide scale [7]. Differential transcript usage analysis showed that IGLL5-201 and IGLL5-203, which have a higher GC content exhibited a usage bias between tumor and adjacent-normal samples. A novel m6A site with a lower methylation ratio and longer poly(A) tail length in the 3’UTR of IGLL5 was found in tumors compared to adjacent-normal tissues, which was verified by RT-qPCR based assay. The altered m6A site was located in a region that overlaps with the tumor suppressor Six3 and the MIBC prognostic factor Nr1h3::Rxra binding targets. Literature indicates that SIX3 appeared to function as a tumor suppressor. Additionally, the expression of Nr1h3 proves to be a prognostic factor for MIBC, and it can form a heterodimer with Rxra. In previous findings, the different m6A sites were found to be associated with NR1 (Fig. 2E), and the JASPAR database showed that the new m6A site was located in the binding regions of Six3 and Nr1h3::Rxra, suggesting its potential involvement in m6A-dependent regulation by influencing relevant TFs. Thus, the altered m6A site may influence protein binding targets, potentially leading to differential transcript usage.

Meanwhile, the long-read sequencing provides a more precise understanding of the relationship between m6A and splicing events. Individual mRNAs in MIBC may contain a series of signals, including m6A sites, that collectively influence PAS and the length of poly(A) tails. Analysis of the transcript VWA1-203 reveals a specific trend in m6A ratio, PAS, and poly(A) tail length. Additionally, high expression levels of VWA1 are associated with poor survivals. It can be inferred that lower m6A ratio leads to shorter 3’UTR, which prevents some essential proteins from binding to their elements and destroys the degradation machinery [72], ultimately leading to improved mRNA stability and increased mRNA levels in tumor tissues. Meanwhile, mRNAs with longer poly(A) tails are translated more efficiently than those with shorter poly(A) tails [73]. In the case of VWA1-203 with a longer poly(A) tail length in tumor tissues, highly translated protein may result in worse survivals. The former research about enhanced m6A deposition, shortened poly(A) tails, and reduced stability of the respective mRNAs also confirmed our speculation reversely [27]. Further experimentation is required to verify the mechanism behind the altered m6A sites in IGLL5, VWA1, and CEBPB.

This is the first study to combine m6A and post-transcriptional event analysis by leveraging Nanopore sequencing technology, a cutting-edge approach that allows for a more nuanced understanding of the transcriptome. Our findings suggest that the altered m6A sites identified in this study may serve as valuable biomarkers, complementing traditional clinicopathological variables in predicting the prognosis of MIBC. This advancement could potentially facilitate physicians in making more informed, individualized treatment decisions tailored to the molecular characteristics of each patient’s tumor. The altered m6A sites were obtained through a comprehensive screening of the whole genome candidate m6A sites in MIBC, highlighting the depth of our analysis. Both functional enrichment analysis and the construction of an m6A-risk model indicated that these sites play a crucial role in tumor development and progression. This suggests that m6A modifications could be key molecular switches in the pathogenesis of MIBC, influencing the behavior of the disease. Furthermore, our analysis inferred intricate relationships among altered m6A sites, alternative splicing, alternative polyadenylation, and poly(A) tail length in genes such as IGLL5 and VWA1. These genes are known to be associated with cancer progression, and our findings imply that m6A modifications might be a regulatory layer that fine-tunes their expression patterns, thereby impacting the tumor’s phenotype. The essential m6A sites were experimentally verified using RT-qPCR based assays, adding a layer of validation to our computational findings. The altered pattern of m6A modifications across the whole genome was thoroughly examined in this research for the first time. This comprehensive characterization will significantly increase our understanding of the role and importance of altered m6A modification patterns in MIBC. These findings have multifaceted clinical implications. First, the m6A sites may serve as prognostic indicators, helping to stratify patients into different risk groups and guide treatment strategies accordingly. Second, given their role in post-transcriptional regulation, these m6A sites could be targeted therapeutically. For instance, drugs that modulate the activity of m6A ‘writers’, ‘erasers’, or ‘readers’ could be developed to alter the expression of cancer-related genes. Third, the distinct m6A signatures identified in this study could pave the way for personalized medicine approaches in MIBC, where treatment plans are customized based on the unique molecular profile of an individual’s tumor. In conclusion, the exploration of m6A modifications in MIBC using Nanopore sequencing technology has opened new avenues for research and clinical application. Future studies should focus on validating these m6A sites in larger cohorts and exploring their potential as therapeutic targets, which could lead to novel treatment modalities for patients with MIBC.

However, there are still some limitations in our study. Firstly, the sequencing was conducted on a limited sample size, with only three pairs of tumor and adjacent-normal tissues from MIBC patients being analyzed using Nanopore technology. Therefore, future studies should aim to include a larger and more diverse cohort to enhance the robustness and applicability of the findings. Furthermore, while our study sheds light on the association between altered m6A sites and post-transcriptional events, the underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood. The complex interplay between m6A modifications and the post-transcriptional landscape is a fertile ground for further exploration. Future experimental investigations should focus on elucidating these mechanisms, potentially uncovering new therapeutic targets and deepening our understanding of the role of m6A in cancer biology.

Conclusions

In this study, we delved into the intricate relationship between m6A modifications and post-transcriptional events including AS, APA, and poly(A) tail dynamic in MIBC for the first time utilizing Nanopore direct-RNA sequencing. Our analysis delineated the m6A RNA methylation landscape in MIBC and developed a prognostic model leveraging genes with altered m6A patterns. We identified a novel m6A modification site within the 3’UTR of the IGLL5 gene, marked by a reduced m6A methylation ratio, extended poly(A) tails, and a significant preference for transcript usage. Furthermore, we characterized two distinct transcripts, VWA1-203 and CEBPB-201, which exhibited intriguing differences in m6A methylation, APA, and poly(A) tail length. In conclusion, our research paints a comprehensive picture of the m6A landscape in MIBC and elucidates the complex interrelationships between m6A methylation and post-transcriptional regulation. This study sheds new light on the potential mechanisms governing the development and progression of MIBC, offering valuable insights for future therapeutic strategies. Moving forward, the next steps in this line of research should focus on translating these findings into clinical practice. This includes validating the identified m6A biomarkers in larger, diverse patient populations and exploring their potential use in diagnostic tests. Additionally, further studies should investigate the therapeutic potential of targeting m6A modifications, such as through the development of small molecules that can modulate m6A ‘erasers’ or ‘writers’. These efforts could lead to the development of personalized treatment plans for MIBC patients, based on the unique m6A signatures of their tumors. By addressing these next steps, we can bridge the gap between our current understanding of m6A’s role in MIBC and its application in improving patient outcomes.
