Effect of stress-ball play on pain and physiological responses during port-a-cath needle insertion in children undergoing chemotherapy: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yeganeh Khaleghi, Neda Ashayeri, Monireh Faghir-Ganji, Fahimeh Sabeti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPort-A-Cath needle insertion in pediatric chemotherapy is often painful and stressing. Distraction is a non-pharmacological method of pain management in invasive procedures in children. The purpose of the study was to investigate the effect of playing with stress ball on pain and physiological indicators at port needle insertion in children undergoing chemotherapy.MethodA randomized clinical trial was conducted with 60 children aged 3 to 7 years, who were randomly assigned to intervention group (n = 30) and control group (n = 30). Ten minutes before the port needle insertion, baseline physiological indicators were recorded and pain scores were measured by the FLACC and Wong-Baker FACES scales. In the intervention group, children played with a stress ball from ten minutes before and during the procedure, while the control group received routine care only. Physiological indicators and the FLACC score during needle insertion, as well as the Wong-Baker FACES self-reporting score immediately after the procedure, were recorded.ResultsAdjusted mean FLACC and Wong-Baker FACES pain scores were significantly lower in the intervention group compared to the control group (P = 0.043, F = 4.266; P = 0.031, F = 4.87 respectively). As for the physiological indicators, heart rate and systolic blood pressure during needle insertion were both significantly lower in intervention group (P < 0.001, F = 21.215; P < 0.001, F = 29.252 respectively). But there were no significant difference in diastolic blood pressure and arterial oxygen saturation (P = 0.051, F = 3.983; P = 0.724, F = 0.724 respectively).ConclusionPlaying with stress ball can decrease pain and improve heart rate and systolic blood pressure during port needle insertion in children. Pediatric nurses should consider the use of stress ball play as a simple, safe and inexpensive nonpharmacological approach for managing children’s pain during painful procedures, especially port needle insertion.Trial registration numberRegistration number IRCT20250318065123N1 on May 10, 2025 (https://www.irct.ir/).Keywords: Pain, Pediatric chemotherapy, Physiological indicators, Play therapy, Stress ball

Introduction

Approximately 400,000 children and adolescents aged 0–19 years are diagnosed with cancer each year worldwide [1]. It is estimated that 13.7 million new cases of childhood cancer will be diagnosed between 2020 and 2050 worldwide [2]. Children receiving chemotherapy most often undergo various invasive and painful procedures, leading to immense pain and stress [3–4]. When caring for a child with cancer, reliable, safe, and suitable intravenous access for long-term administration of chemotherapy drugs is required. A Vascular Access Port (VAP) is widely used in pediatric oncology due to its low complication and infection rates, high convenience, and reduced maintenance costs [5–6]. To maintain the functionality of the port system and prevent infection, port needles must be replaced every 5–7 days. However, this procedure is painful and often induces fear and stress in children [7]. In the study by Gerçeker et al. (2024), using the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale, the mean self-reported pain score of the children in the control group during port access with a Huber needle without any intervention was 5.3 ± 1.8 [7]. Moreover, in the study by Semerci et al. (2021), also using the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale, it was found that the mean pain scores of children in the control group during Huber needle insertion were 5.03 ± 3.35, which indicates moderate to severe pain of this procedure [8].

Pain in children is associated with consequences such as weakened immune systems [9], development of depressive disorders [10], anxiety, fear, and avoidance behaviors, reduced interaction and cooperation with medical staff, reduced quality of care, reduced family satisfaction, avoidance of health care and, as a result, reduced success rates of medical treatment [11–12]. Also, inadequate management of acute pain can lead to chronic pain or post-traumatic stress symptoms [13–14]. Pain may also lead to changes in physiological indicators such as increased heart rate, increased blood pressure, and decreased arterial blood oxygen saturation [15]. Therefore, physiological pain observations are one of the methods of measuring pain along with self-report and behavioral observations of pain [16–17]. Regular monitoring of physiological indicators is a routine component of nursing care in hospital settings, which aims to facilitate early detection of abnormal physiological parameters [18]. Physiological indicators provide a relatively comprehensive picture of the body’s vital functions, and any abnormalities and changes in physiological indicators may be a sign of physical or psychological stress [19].

Children’s responses to pain and distraction techniques vary according to developmental stage [20]. In preschool and early school-aged children (3–7 years), cognitive abilities are still developing, consistent with Piaget’s preoperational stage of development [20]. Coping strategies in this age group are typically concrete and sensory-oriented [20]. Research suggests that active distraction techniques are more effective than abstract cognitive strategies in young children undergoing painful procedures [21]. Therefore, simple tactile and motor-based interventions, such as squeezing an object, may be particularly suitable for this developmental group. Moreover, according to a research by Zhang et al. in 2024, the highest proportion of high-frequency pain occurs at age 3 years (42.32%), followed by age 8 years (23.11%), with an overall decline in intensity across increasing age [22].

Hence, in the care of children with complex medical conditions, pain management is an integral part of nursing practice, which aims to reduce suffering and promote children’s well-being [23]. Nurses play an important role in discovering new coping methods and different techniques for managing children’s pain and have an ethical obligation and responsibility to use the latest scientific evidence to relieve any pain that children experience during treatment [24]. Today, various pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches are recommended for managing children’s pain. The use of distraction methods is one of the most common methods of non-pharmacological pain management in children. One suitable distraction method is the use of stress balls. The potential mechanism underlying the effect of stress balls on pain reduction can be explained through both physiological and cognitive theories. According to the Gate Control Theory, there is a gate-like mechanism at the level of the spinal cord that regulates the rate of pain signals, and sensory/tactile stimuli (such as squeezing a stress ball) can close this gate and prevent some pain signals from passing to the brain [25–26]. In addition, based on the Limited Attentional Capacity Theory, when the attention of a part of the brain is busy with another activity, the ability to process pain stimuli decreases [27]. In this regard, active distraction is more effective than the passive one in reducing pain and anxiety caused by invasive procedures (such as blood sampling and injections) [21].

Although various studies have shown positive effects of stress ball use on controlling emotions and treating stress and anxiety [7, 28–29], pain management [7, 29–32], and improving physiological indicators [33], some studies have reported conflicting findings. For example, Shekhar et al. (2022) found out that the use of stress balls did not significantly affect pain management in children [34], and Kasar et al. (2020), reported no significant improvement in physiological indicators with stress ball use [28]. Therefore, since the results of some studies are controversial and there is limited evidence focusing specifically on young children with cancer undergoing port needle insertion, various studies suggest that further research is needed to expand the use of stress balls in paediatric painful procedures [35–36].

This study aimed to determine the effect of playing with stress balls on pain and physiological indicators during port needle insertion in children undergoing chemotherapy. The results of this study can help pediatric oncology nurses use effective distraction methods in caring for children during painful procedures, especially during port needle insertion. The first hypothesis of the study was that playing with stress ball would reduce pain during port needle insertion in children undergoing chemotherapy, and the second hypothesis was that playing with stress ball would improve physiological indicators during needle insertion in children undergoing chemotherapy.

Method

Design

We conducted a randomized controlled trial (RCT). This study is registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (Registry Code: IRCT20250318065123N1, date: 2025-05-10). Sampling was carried out from May 22 to October 27, 2025, in the Oncology Clinic of Ali Asghar Children Hospital in Tehran.

Participants

In the present study, an a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) for an independent samples t-test to determine the required sample size. Considering the study parameters, including a two-tailed test, effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.74 [37], a significance level (α( of 0.05, and a test power (1-β) of 0.80, the minimum required total sample size was estimated to be 60 participants (30 in each group, based on an allocation ratio of 1). Participants were selected by convenience sampling and subsequently randomly allocated to two groups. Random allocation was performed using the Permutated Blocks method. Specifically, quadruple blocks were generated via the Random Sequence Generator section of www.random.org.

Inclusion criteria were children aged 3 to 7 years with an implanted port who were undergoing chemotherapy, alert and clinically stable, and able to move their hands sufficiently to squeeze a stress ball. Exclusion criteria included cognitive, hearing, or visual impairments based on medical records; the use of relaxation or distraction techniques other than routine hospital care; the administration of sedatives or analgesics within 6 h prior to the intervention; and inability to understand and correctly use the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale. Prior to randomization, children were trained in the use of the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale to ensure comprehension; those unable to demonstrate adequate understanding were excluded from participation.

Pain assessment in this study was conducted using both the FLACC scale (observer-rated) and the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale (self-report). The FLACC scale was completed by a trained nurse independent of the research team at two time points (baseline and during needle insertion) for all enrolled children. Children also reported their pain intensity based on the Wong-Baker FACES Pain Rating scale at baseline and immediately after the procedure.

Of a total of 70 children screened for eligibility, six were excluded due to inability to move their hands (n = 3), visual impairment (n = 1), and cognitive impairment (n = 2). Sixty-four children were enrolled. Of these, four were excluded prior to randomization (one parent declined to provide consent for personal reasons; three children were unable to adequately understand the Wong-Baker pain rating scale after training). The final sample consisted of 60 children aged 3 to 7 years hospitalized in the Oncology Clinic of Ali Asghar Hospital in Tehran, who were randomly allocated to either the control group (n = 30) or the stress ball intervention group (n = 30) (Fig. 1). Prior to participation, written inform consent was obtained from all parents, and all children entered the study voluntarily.

Groups

Intervention group

Each child in the intervention group received a stress ball. The stress balls used in this study were Iranian-made, multicolored soft silicone balls, 6 cm in diameter, featuring small circular protrusions that bulge outward when slight pressure is applied. First, the children were introduced to the stress ball; they held it, practiced squeezing it, focused on it, and explored its colors as part of the distraction activity. Then, the nurse provided standard explanations about the port needle insertion to the child and parents and prepared them for this procedure. Children in the intervention group played with the stress ball from 10 min before the procedure and continued during port needle insertion, which generally takes approximately 5 min, totaling about 15 min of use. All children in the intervention group were observed and recorded by one of the team’s researchers to actively squeeze the ball during intervention.

Control group

Similar to the intervention group, routine ward procedures were provided, including providing a calm environment with the presence of parents, providing complete explanations regarding Huber needle insertion, reassuring participants, and implementing the standard port needle insertion protocol, but no other intervention was performed.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention (use of a stress ball), blinding of participants and researcher was not possible. However, FLACC assessment was performed by a trained pediatric nurse independent of the research team. Also, data entry and data analysis were performed by a statistician who was blinded to group allocation (the intervention group was labeled with code A and the control group with code B).

Data collection tools

Demographic characteristics form

The demographic characteristics form was prepared by the researchers in accordance with recommended practices for researcher-developed demographic instruments, such as those described by Nguyen-Kearns et al., 2025 [38]. The questionnaire collected basic information from parents [39]—including their age, education level, and number of children—as well as details about their child, such as age [40], sex [41], cancer diagnosis [40], length of illness, duration of port placement [41], and any underlying conditions. Parents completed the form themselves.

Physiological indicators observation form

This form was designed to record systolic and diastolic blood pressure (mmHg), arterial oxygen saturation (%), and heart rate (beats/min). These physiological indicators are widely accepted indicators of autonomic nervous system activation during painful and stressful procedures in children which are also routinely monitored in clinical settings, making them valid measures during brief procedures such as port needle insertion [42].

Blood pressure, arterial oxygen saturation, and heart rate were measured using a multisignal patient monitor (Alborz B9, Pooyandegan Rah Saadat Co., Iran). Blood pressure was obtained using an appropriately sized pediatric cuff placed on the right arm. The blood pressure measurement range was 0–255 mmHg with an accuracy of ± 3 mmHg. Arterial oxygen saturation and heart rate were monitored via the integrated Masimo SET pulse oximetry module, with a measurement range of 0–100% (accuracy ± 2% within 70–100%) for oxygen saturation and 25–250 beats per minute (accuracy ± 3 bpm) for pulse rate. A pediatric-compatible pulse oximeter probe was attached to the child’s great toe. The device was factory calibrated, and technical performance checks were conducted by the authorized company representatives. For research purposes, blood pressure was measured twice: once 10 min before port needle insertion (baseline) and once during the procedure. Heart rate and arterial oxygen saturation, while being monitored continuously, were recorded at two time points: 10 min before port needle insertion (baseline) and at the moment of needle insertion. Measurements were documented by the researcher in both the intervention and control groups.

FLACC pain scale

The FLACC scale was developed by Merkel et al. in 1997 as an observational tool for assessing procedural pain in young children [43]. Crellin et al., 2018 confirmed the reliability, validity, and sensitivity of the FLACC scale for assessing procedural pain in children [44]. Persian version of the scale has demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability in pediatric populations in Iran [45]. This scale measures pain intensity using the five behavioral categories of Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability, each scored from 0 to 2, thus yielding a total score range from 0 to 10. Scores of 0 mean no pain; scores of 1 to 3, mild pain; 4 to 6, moderate pain; and 7 to 10, severe pain [46].

The Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale

The Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale, developed in 1988, is a self-report scale that consists of six cartoon faces, representing a range from a smiling face for “no pain” to a crying face for “the worst pain,” with scores from 0 to 10. This scale has acceptable validity and reliability [47] and has been shown to be useful in children aged 3 years and older in validated studies [48].

Data collection process

All port access procedures using a Huber needle were performed in a designated venipuncture room, with the child’s parents present throughout. In both groups, port needle insertion was carried out exclusively with a 24G Huber needle by the same pediatric oncology nurse during the morning shift to ensure procedural consistency. The nurse had six years of clinical experience in a pediatric hematology–oncology unit, including port access and needle insertion.

During the procedure, the nurse provided standardized verbal information to parents and children in this way: “I am cleaning the needle insertion site,” “Are you ready?”, “I am inserting the needle now,” “The needle is inserted,” and “I am applying the adhesive.” All Huber needle insertion procedures lasted approximately 3 to 5 min. No topical medication was applied to the port access site, and no cases required a second insertion attempt during the study period.

Regarding the pain scales, a trained nurse independent of the research team completed FLACC pain scale through direct observation at two time points for both groups: 10 min before needle insertion (baseline) and during the insertion procedure. Moreover, the self-reported pain rating scale (FACES pain rating scale) was also measured in both groups and at two points in time: 10 min before port needle insertion (baseline) and immediately after the procedure. To measure pain based on the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale, immediately after procedure, children were asked to show a cartoon face that represented the most severe pain they experienced during the procedure. Well-worth mentioning, prior to the randomization, children were instructed on how to use the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale and practical assessments were conducted to assess their understanding. Children who were unable to make logical and consistent face choices were excluded from the study. Due to ethical considerations, children were not videotaped. Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences), version 26. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of data distribution. Descriptive statistics included mean and standard deviation (SD) for quantitative variables and frequency and percentage for qualitative variables. Inferential analyses included Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, and independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Additionally, Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was applied to compare groups while adjusting for baseline values. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

The demographic characteristics of children and parents are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the children was 4.57 ± 1.19 years in the intervention group and 4.37 ± 1.14 years in the control group. Leukemia was the most common diagnosis in both groups (83.3% in the intervention group and 96.7% in the control group). The mean duration of port placement was 13.17 ± 13.55 months in the intervention group and 10.23 ± 11 months in the control group. On the whole, the two groups were comparable with respect to child age, gender, diagnosis, disease duration, duration of port placement, underlying conditions, number of siblings, birth order, and parental age and education, indicating homogeneity between groups (P > 0.05).

ANCOVA results indicated that the stress ball intervention had a statistically significant effect on reducing children’s pain during port needle insertion. On the FLACC scale, the adjusted mean pain score was significantly lower for the intervention group, 5.67 (moderate pain), compared with the control group, 7.20 (severe pain) (P = 0.043, F = 4.266), reflecting a reduction of 1.53 points in favor of the intervention. In the same way, the effect was significant for the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale (P = 0.031, F = 4.87), with the adjusted mean score being 1.66 points lower for the intervention group than the control group, 5.17 versus 6.83 (severe pain), which corresponds to moderate pain. The intervention was associated with a medium effect size for both measures (η²=0.069 for FLACC; η²=0.079 for the Wong-Baker FACES pain rating scale).

The intervention also had a significant and more pronounced effect on physiological stress indicators. The mean heart rate during the procedure was significantly lower in the intervention group than in the control group, P < 0.001, F = 21.215; the adjusted mean was 114.96 beats per minute, which is 12.08 beats lower than the control group with a mean of 127.04 bpm. Similarly, systolic blood pressure showed a significant reduction in the intervention group (P < 0.001, F = 29.252). Its adjusted mean was 107.93 mmHg compared to 115.00 mmHg for the control group, which indicates a difference of -7.07 mmHg. Both changes for heart rate (η²=0.271) and systolic blood pressure (η²=0.339) yielded large effect sizes.

For diastolic blood pressure, the effect of the intervention reached a borderline level of statistical significance, with P = 0.051, F = 3.983. Although not statistically significant at conventional levels, the adjusted mean for the intervention group was lower, 58.88 mmHg, compared to the control group, 60.99 mmHg, with a medium effect size, η²=0.065, suggesting a downward trend. On the other hand, arterial oxygen saturation was not significantly affected by intervention, P = 0.724, F = 0.724, and the effect size was negligible, η²=0.002.

Discussion

Pain

This study extends current evidence on non-pharmacological pain management by suggesting that active tactile distraction can meaningfully modulate procedural pain during port needle insertion in pediatric oncology patients. However, this is the first to investigate the effectiveness of playing with a stress ball during the insertion of a port needle in terms of pain. Similar to previous studies [36, 49], the results of this study also underlined that even simple non-pharmacological interventions may effectively reduce procedural pain. Considering the moderate effect size of the intervention, it can be said that the use of a stress ball had not only a statistically significant but also a clinically significant effect on pain reduction. The concordance between self-reported and behavioral pain scales strengthens the validity of the effect and reduces the likelihood that the findings reflect reporting bias alone.

Our study is in line with the results of studies by Gerçeker et al. (2024), Yüksel and Güneş (2024), Tosun et al. (2025), and Sirtin Tumakaka et al. (2020), all of whom reported beneficial effects of stress ball use during painful procedures [7, 29, 31–32]. The consistency of these findings across different procedural contexts suggests that stress balls may operate through a shared attentional and sensorimotor modulation mechanism (referring to the Gate Control Theory of pain). For instance, Gerçeker et al. (2024) reported reductions in pain, fear, and anxiety during phlebotomy [7], indicating that tactile engagement may reduce pain. Similarly, Tosun et al. (2025) and Sirtin Tumakaka et al. (2020) demonstrated reduced pain intensity during cannulation and intravenous catheter insertion [31–32]. Yüksel and Güneş (2024) suggested that incorporating a stress ball into routine care could improve patient experience without additional pharmacological interventions [29]. Because there is a strong relationship between pain and anxiety, this intervention may help to decrease both the physical pain phenomenon, as well as the subjective anxiety, leading to a better patient experience [29].

Our findings differ from those of Yanes et al. (2018), who reported no significant effect of stress ball or hand-holding interventions on pain, anxiety, or physiological indicators [50]. One possible explanation is that high-quality nurse communication and comprehensive preoperative preparation in that study may have minimized additional observable benefit from distraction techniques. Similarly, Shekhar et al. (2022) found that stress balls did not significantly improve pain or anxiety outcomes during local anesthesia administration in children compared with conventional verbal communication methods [34]. Differences in procedural context, baseline anxiety levels, and patient characteristics may explain these discrepancies. For example, children undergoing dental treatment for deep tooth decay may experience different pain expectations and emotional responses compared with children receiving port needle insertion for chemotherapy. These variations suggest that the effectiveness of distraction interventions is context-dependent and may vary according to the type of procedure, patient population, and quality of routine care. Therefore, using a stress ball should be part of a care package and further research is needed to determine under what conditions it is truly effective.

Physiological indicators

The reduced rise in heart rate and systolic blood pressure indicates that the intervention have moderated the body’s autonomic stress response during the procedure. In other words, the use of a stress ball seemed to dampen the physiological responses normally associated with procedural pain and anxiety. This effect can be explained by both psychological and physiological mechanisms. As a form of cognitive distraction, engaging with a stress ball diverts the child’s attention from the painful stimulus by providing repeated tactile and motor activity. This reduces activity in brain structures linked to anxiety, such as the amygdala, which in turn decreases sympathetic nervous system stimulation and catecholamine release, leading to smaller increases in heart rate and blood pressure [51–52]. Additionally, the rhythmic squeezing may activate vagal pathways, enhancing parasympathetic tone, lowering heart rate, and counteracting sympathetic arousal [53]. Together, these mechanisms promote relative calm and positive attentional focus, resulting in reduced cardiovascular responses compared to the control group.

Although heart rate and systolic blood pressure improved significantly by the intervention, changes in diastolic blood pressure and arterial oxygen saturation were not statistically different. Diastolic pressure fluctuates less during brief stressors, and arterial oxygen saturation typically remains within a narrow, high range (approximately 98–100%), making small changes difficult to detect. Motion artifacts and the timing, intensity, or duration of the intervention may also limit measurable effects [54–55]. Thus, the absence of significant changes in these parameters does not imply that the stress ball is physiologically ineffective but may reflect measurement limitations or the need for longer or more robust interventions.

Studies on stress balls’ effects on physiological indicators, particularly in children, remain limited. Yeniğün and Korkmaz (2025) found decreases in blood pressure and heart rate, but not oxygen saturation, in adults undergoing cataract surgery [33], supporting the idea that stress balls modulate sympathetic arousal while having minimal effect on more stable physiological measures. Conversely, Kasar et al. (2020) observed reduced stress but no significant changes in heart rate or blood pressure during dialysis [28]. Their adult population limits generalizability to children receiving chemotherapy, highlighting the need to interpret physiological findings within context.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the randomized clinical trial design and the presence of a control group. The simultaneous use of both the Wong-Baker FACES self-report pain rating scale and the FLACC behavioral pain observation scale, and the simultaneous measurement of physiological indicators that can indicate pain, are other strengths of this study. Moreover, in both the control and intervention groups, an attempt was made to provide the same conditions for the children by providing a calm environment with the presence of parents, providing full explanations about the process, reassuring the participants, and implementing a standard port needle insertion protocol.

One of the limitations of this study was the lack of blinding of the participants/researchers, as complete blinding was not feasible due to the overt and observable nature of the intervention. Another limitation of this study was that the stress ball play intervention time was short and the variables were measured within a short period of time, which may have particularly affected the results of the physiological indicators scores.

Implications for practice

Children in pediatric oncology wards are frequently exposed to painful invasive procedures and suffer from a lot of pain and discomfort. One of the important roles of pediatric nurses is pain management and providing supportive-palliative care in these patients. Based on the results of this study, nurses, as a member of the interdisciplinary care team, can effectively and safely manage pain in children by using active distraction methods such as playing with a stress ball and improve children’s physiological indicators during invasive procedures. This approach can be used as a safe, attractive, low-cost, and easily implementable non-pharmacological method for pain management in painful procedures such as port needle insertion in pediatric oncology departments, especially in preschool and early school-aged children. Therefore, playing with a stress ball is recommended as a complementary method along with other methods of pain management in children and effective verbal and nonverbal communication with the child. Given that using a stress ball does not require special equipment or complex training, and can be easily used by nurses in clinical settings, it is suggested that this method be widely used in inpatient wards and pediatric oncology clinics, particularly when accessing implanted ports.

Conclusion

Playing with a stress ball effectively reduced pain intensity and improved physiological indicators—heart rate and systolic blood pressure—during port needle insertion in children undergoing chemotherapy. By contrast, the lack of significant changes in diastolic blood pressure and arterial oxygen saturation suggests these measures may be less responsive to brief changes in procedural pain or may require longer interventions to yield measurable effects. The overall consistency of findings across self-report, behavioral, and physiological outcomes provides support for the use of stress ball distraction as a simple, safe, and inexpensive non-pharmacological approach for reducing procedural pain in children during painful medical procedures. It is recommended that future studies be carried out to compare this easy and inexpensive technique with other distraction techniques like augmented reality or virtual reality, which are appealing but clearly require more facilities and costs. Also, the effect of playing with a stress ball on pain and physiological indicators in children should be evaluated over a longer period of time.
