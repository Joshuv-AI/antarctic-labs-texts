Programmable memtransistor array with temporal dynamics modulation for efficient time-series data processing
Authors: Dae-won Kim, Yoonho Cho, Seokho Seo, Yujin Kim, See-On Park, Taehwan Jang, Chaebin Park, Young Taek Oh, Jae-Duk Lee, Shinhyun Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hardware-based reservoir computing systems promise energy-efficient time-series data processing. However, their non-adjustable temporal dynamics at the hardware level limit multiscale feature extraction and computing capacity. Here, we present a programmable dynamic memtransistor featuring a dual-functional gate stack that enables hardware-level control of dynamic behavior without constant biasing or input preprocessing. The memtransistor integrates a charge storage layer for volatile temporal processing and a charge trap layer for non-volatile modulation of the band structure, enabling a 5-fold tunability in relaxation time constants. By configuring memtransistors with distinct dynamics in parallel, we realize a wide reservoir computing system capable of processing signals across multiple timescales. This architecture delivers a 40-fold reduction in the error for the multiple superimposed oscillator prediction task compared to a single reservoir baseline, and achieves software-comparable accuracy in forecasting the chaotic Lorenz attractor. Our results establish a compact and energy-efficient hardware platform for scalable wide reservoir computing implementation.

Introduction

The rapid growth of artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things, and big data analytics has led to a surge in data processing demands1,2. However, the conventional von Neumann architecture suffers from inefficient energy usage due to frequent data shuttling between physically separated processing and memory units3. To overcome this bottleneck, brain-inspired neuromorphic computing has garnered significant attention for its massive parallelism and high energy efficiency4–9.

Reservoir computing (RC) is a neuromorphic computing framework that projects input data into a high-dimensional space using a fixed recurrent network, the reservoir, where only the readout layer is trained through lightweight linear regression10. Software-based RC has shown impressive performance in time-series tasks, such as spoken-digit recognition, chaotic system forecasting, and bio-signal analysis, all of which are difficult for conventional neural networks to handle11,12. However, conventional CMOS-based devices used in software-level RC lack the intrinsic dynamic properties for directly processing time-series data, typically requiring the combination of complex algorithms and large-scale integrated devices, which leads to unnecessary energy and hardware overhead13. Accordingly, various hardware-based RC systems have been explored using emerging devices, including memristors14–17, ferroelectrics18–21, electrochemical transistors22,23, nanowire networks24,25, and magnetic systems26,27 as physical reservoirs. These devices exhibit highly nonlinear responses and intrinsic transient dynamics, both of which are essential for temporal data processing with reduced hardware footprint and energy efficiency10. Recent studies have further expanded hardware RC implementations based on memristive devices toward system and application-level demonstrations. For example, in-sensor RC architectures have been proposed to bring computation closer to the sensor front end by directly processing analogue sensory signals28. In addition, system-level implementations using memristor-based fixed temporal encoders and trainable digital readout/projection networks have demonstrated multimodal event-data learning with reduced training cost and improved energy efficiency29. These demonstrations support the practical feasibility of hardware-based temporal data processing.

A key property for an RC system is the short-term memory effect, wherein the reservoir states are determined by both present inputs and those from the recent past, defined by a finite timescale. However, hardware-based RC systems using emerging devices typically exhibit non-adjustable temporal dynamics with fixed nonlinear transformation functions, due to the difficulty of tuning the relaxation time constants or controlling the underlying device physics. Such constraints restrict the RC architecture to a single reservoir design, thereby reducing the capacity for multiple timescale data processing. To address this limitation, a wide RC architecture composed of multiple sub-reservoirs, each operating at a distinct timescale, has been developed30,31. By incorporating units with diverse time constants, the wide reservoir captures temporal correlations across multiple timescales, enhancing the richness and separability of reservoir states, which is beneficial for processing complex and non-stationary time-series data. To implement multi-timescale behavior in hardware-based RC systems, various strategies have been explored, including input masking15,32, utilizing device variations14,16,33, and constant gate biasing34. For example, single-crystalline entropy-stabilized oxides are utilized to achieve diverse temporal dynamics with composition control35, and an indium selenide transistor is developed to achieve tunable relaxation time by applying back-gate voltage18. These strategies have been demonstrated to improve the computational accuracy and processing speed, highlighting their potential advantages for efficient RC systems. However, they often introduce trade-offs, such as circuit complexity, limited reconfigurability, poor scalability, and high energy overhead.

In this work, we present a programmable dynamic memtransistor (PDM) that enables tunable relaxation dynamics for multiscale feature extraction and the physical implementation of wide RC systems. The PDM integrates a dual-functional gate stack composed of a charge storage layer (CSL) for volatile temporal processing and a charge trap layer (CTL) for non-volatile modulation of band bending. The temporal dynamics in the CSL can be precisely adjusted by controlling the electron density in the CTL, which alters the internal energy band structure and enables a five-fold tunability in the relaxation time constant. The 1-transistor PDM structure enables fine-tuned modulation of temporal dynamics at the hardware level, while ensuring high array scalability, fast switching speed (50 ns), and low energy consumption for volatile conductance modulation (0.21 aJ for 100 pulses). These properties allow PDMs with distinct temporal dynamics to process time-series inputs across different timescales in parallel, giving rise to enriched reservoir states. We demonstrate that PDM-based sub-reservoirs can be configured to form a wide physical RC system. This architecture achieves a 40-fold reduction in prediction error for the multiple superimposed oscillator (MSO) task compared to a single reservoir implementation, and performs comparably to a software-based approach on chaotic Lorenz attractor prediction. The proposed PDM, fabricated using a CMOS-compatible process, enables low-power and high-precision time-series data processing, offering a scalable and robust wide RC platform for edge computing.

Results

Dynamic memtransistor for wide reservoir computing

A wide reservoir system has been proposed for increasing the computing capacity of the RC system (Fig. 1a). Each sub-reservoir (Xi(t), i = 0, 1,…,n) has individual characteristics that define the temporal feature extracted by the given sub-reservoir, so that the different sub-reservoirs can capture diverse temporal features at different time scales. Thus, temporal input data can be nonlinearly mapped to a high-dimensional feature space by sub-reservoirs within a wide reservoir layer. The collective reservoir states are processed through the output layer with a simple, linear network layer. Our PDM device enables efficient hardware implementation of a wide reservoir layer (Fig. 1b). The PDM features a gate stack composed of a CSL and a CTL, which independently serve distinct roles in realizing multiple sub-reservoirs with different temporal dynamics. The CSL that employs indium gallium zinc oxide (IGZO) is used as a temporal electron-trapping site for time-series data processing, and CTL consisting of SiO2/HfO2/SiO2 is utilized for realizing multi-states of the PDM in a non-volatile manner. The monolithic gate stack enables modulation of the CSL band structure via the electron density stored in the CTL, resulting in adjustable short-term memory characteristics intrinsic to the CSL. Utilizing these tunable temporal dynamics, each PDM can function as a physical sub-reservoir, and an array of PDMs with distinct electron densities stored in the CTL collectively constitute a wide physical reservoir layer (Supplementary Fig. 1). Figure 1c shows the cross-section transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of the PDM gate stack. As presented, 5 nm IGZO as CSL is deposited on a crystalline n-doped Si channel layer. On top of that, CTL consists of a 3 nm SiO2 tunneling oxide, a 5 nm HfO2 trapping layer, and a 12 nm SiO2 blocking oxide. The tunneling oxide physically and electronically separates IGZO and HfO2 to ensure the function of each layer (Supplementary Fig. 2). The proposed gate-dielectric structure and materials of PDM are extensively compatible with CMOS technology (Fig. 1d and see details in “Methods”). The electronic band diagram of PDM at flat band state is shown in Fig. 1e. This transient electron trapping/relaxation process is highly dependent on the energy band state, which is regulated by the CTL. Modulation of temporal dynamics characteristics

PDM exhibits tunable temporal dynamics depending on the programmed states of the CTL. The effect of CTL programming is confirmed by transfer curves (Fig. 2a). The CTL stack of PDM can be programmed into multiple states by applying VProg with amplitudes ranging from 14 V to 20 V and a fixed width of 1 ms. This CTL programming process induces a positive voltage shift of the threshold voltage (Vth) with decreasing conductance at zero gate voltage. The CTL in PDM can be reset with a negative voltage pulse (−16 V, 50 ms) to a fully-on state with high conductance, which is defined as an erased state. For clarity, the CTL erased state is referred to as state 0, and subsequent states 1 to 7 are defined by increasing VProg amplitude from 14 V to 20 V in 1 V steps, resulting in 8 distinct programmable levels (equivalent to 3 bits). After CTL programming, each PDM state shows the volatile transfer curve with different channel conductance levels (Supplementary Fig. 4). A transmission line method (TLM)-based resistance/potential-drop analysis indicates that the gated region (CTL/CSL/Si) is the dominant contributor to the device resistance, supporting that the state-dependent drain current modulation primarily originates from the gated region (Supplementary Fig. 5). To verify the temporal dynamics modulation effect depending on CTL-programmed states, responsive currents of 8 different CTL-programmed states were measured with a 5 V amplitude and 150 ns pulse width (Fig. 2b). Under the applied pulse, the drain current (−IDS) of the devices abruptly decreases. The current response relaxes toward the initial state after removing the pulse; notably, the current relaxation slows down as the CTL state transitions from state 0 to state 7. The recovery process can be quantitatively characterized with a single exponential function (Supplementary Fig. 6)31. Figure 2c presents the time constant (τ) and base current (−I0) from 50-cycle measurements of each CTL-programmed state under the same input gate pulse (5 V, 150 ns). As the CTL-programmed state increases from state 0 to state 7, the base current decreases while the time constant extends from 0.71 ms to 3.76 ms, corresponding to approximately a five-fold increase. This indicates that the number of stored electrons in HfO₂ effectively modulates the temporal response with low cycle variations (<2%) (standard deviation/mean; σ/μ) (Supplementary Fig. 7). This temporal dynamics modulation effect is well-preserved in various input pulses, indicating that PDM can process time-series data over varying time frames (Supplementary Fig. 8). Building on temporal dynamics modulation under a single input pulse, a paired-pulse facilitation (PPF) effect is characterized across 5 CTL-programmed states (Fig. 2d). It is worth noting that the measured PPF ratio is increased with higher CTL states (state 0 to state 7), as shown in Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 9. This phenomenon can be attributed to the enhanced cumulative effect of CTL programming, which results in slower conductance relaxation of the device. Additional frequency-sweep measurements confirm that, under the applied pulse-train measurement conditions, the CTL-programmed states exhibit distinct frequency-dependent summation behaviors, consistent with state-dependent current response windows under temporal dynamics modulation (Supplementary Fig. 10)34. Figure 2e further exhibits the temporal summation effects of PDM. When the 100 consecutive pulses (3 V, 50 ns) with 10 kHz frequency were applied to the gate electrode, the PDM summed the stimulation in the temporal domain through nonlinear integration and exhibited gradual current relaxation with different dynamics depending on CTL-programmed states (Supplementary Fig. 11). We observed similar temporal summation behavior across 30 devices for all 8 CTL-programmed states (states 0–7), with the device variation in the extracted relaxation time constant remaining within <5% (σ/μ) under the same pulse train condition (Supplementary Fig. 12). Consistently, a device-normalized analysis confirms that the CTL-programmed temporal dynamic modulation profile is maintained across the 30 measured devices (Supplementary Fig. 13). The gate leakage current in response to varying gate bias is shown in Fig. 2f. PDM generates small gate leakage currents of 0.014 pA at a 3 V gate bias and 810 pA at a 20 V gate bias, indicating that gate-side energy consumption for volatile conductance modulation (write operation) over 100 pulses (as shown in Fig. 2e) is 0.21 aJ (3 V × 50 ns × 0.014 pA × 100 pulses), while gate-side energy for CTL programming with the maximum voltage pulse requires 16.2 pJ (20 V × 1 ms × 810 pA), consistent with its high-field tunneling-based operation (Supplementary Fig. 14). The drain-side read energy is separately estimated to be 125 pJ per read operation (2.5 V × 500 nA/10 kHz), using the average drain current across all CTL-programmed states. We believe that the energy consumption of CTL programming is sufficiently low, as this operation is only required for adjusting temporal dynamics (as pre-conditioning) rather than processing time-series input.

To evaluate the state retention of the controlled temporal dynamics, device responses to a single gate pulse (5 V, 150 ns) were periodically measured following the CTL programming operation (Fig. 2g). We observed a change of time constant in <5% after 3000 s in the worst case (state 7), representing the highest electron density stored in the trapping layer. Additional semi-log analysis of the measured retention data indicates a gradual long-term reduction of state separability rather than an abrupt loss of programmed temporal dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 15). This non-volatility of temporal dynamics modulation is dominantly influenced by retention characteristics of the CTL stack, which can be further optimized through thickness control of tunneling oxide and fabrication process.

The endurance of devices is crucial for the high sustainability and performance of physical RC systems. Figure 2h presents the endurance measurement result conducted within the operational range for temporal data processing (measured at the CTL-erased state, state 0). We applied 500 consecutive depression pulses with an amplitude of 5 V and a width of 500 ns, followed by 500 consecutive potentiation pulses (accelerating the recovery of the device’s conductance) with an amplitude of −5 V and 50 μs per switching cycle. The channel current is measured by a read pulse (−2.5 V, 20 μs) at each switching cycle. As a result, stable switching behaviors are confirmed with σ/μ = 1.2% for the low-resistance state (LRS) and σ/μ = 2.7% for the high-resistance state (HRS) over a total of 1 × 107 pulses applied. The same endurance measurement was repeated at the most-programmed condition (state 7), showing stable volatile switching (Supplementary Fig. 16). Across all 8 CTL-programmed states, the CSL-driven volatile current dynamics show <3% variation over 2 × 105 applied input pulses based on sampled cycles, supporting reproducible and state-dependent temporal responses (Supplementary Fig. 17). We further examined the temporal dynamics stability under repeated CTL erase/program cycling and found that the base current and temporal dynamics metrics remain stable up to 1000 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Building on the demonstrated temporal dynamics tunability of the PDM, we evaluated an N-MNIST (Neuromorphic-MNIST) classification task (Supplementary Note 1)29. We first characterized 4-bit temporal-input encoding using experimentally measured PDM responses across different input temporal scales (Δt) and CTL-programmed states, confirming Δt- and state-dependent current response separability (Supplementary Figs. 19, 20). These experimentally measured 4-bit responses were then utilized to construct reservoir features for N-MNIST classification (Supplementary Fig. 21). The N-MNIST results reveal optimal CTL-programmed state range for each Δt, and this range shifts systematically with Δt. This indicates that classification performance depends on matching the input timescale to the reservoir’s temporal dynamics. Accordingly, PDM-based RC platform spanning a broad range of programmed time constants can provide multiscale temporal feature extraction over a wide temporal range, supporting robust performance for temporally diverse datasets18,35.

Mechanism of tunable temporal dynamics

PDM exhibits channel conductance depression in response to positive voltage pulses with nanosecond-scale width, followed by a relaxation of the channel current toward its initial state upon the removal of the pulse. This short-term plasticity, analogous to that observed in biological synapses, arises from electron trapping and de-trapping at bulk defect states in the CSL and possible interface trap states. Figure 3a presents the transient current behavior and transfer curves of the proposed PDM and a control device without CSL. The device without CSL shows negligible transient current responses and a small hysteresis loop, indicating that CSL is a foundation for temporal signal encoding and physical reservoir implementation. The IGZO, which serves as the CSL in the PDM, typically contains a high density of oxygen vacancies38, and interface traps may also exist at the tunneling oxide/CSL interface (Supplementary Fig. 22). When a positive voltage pulse with a nanosecond-scale duration is applied to the gate, electrons from the Si channel may occupy oxygen vacancy defect states (VO⁺ and VO2⁺) and interface traps in the CSL. As electrons are trapped within the CSL, the depletion region in the Si channel expands and leads to a reduction in channel conductance. After the removal of the pulse, these trapped electrons spontaneously escape, resulting in the recovery of channel conductance. To further verify the role of oxygen vacancy-related defects in the CSL, we prepared a control device with a post-O2 annealed CSL, and the result shows that the current-dynamics magnitude is significantly suppressed (Supplementary Fig. 23). This result supports the contribution of oxygen vacancy-related defects, while the overall trap-mediated dynamics should be understood as a combined contribution from bulk defect states and interface traps. To investigate the volatile resistive-switching dynamics with the modulation effect, the activation energy (Ea) of various programmed states of the CTL was calculated from the decaying time constant under the temperature ranging from 294 K to 374 K (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 24)39. It is observed that the calculated activation energies progressively increase from 0.07 eV to 0.24 eV with higher CTL-programmed states, suggesting an increased contribution of deeper trap states in the CSL region to the relaxation process40. Furthermore, as the CTL-programmed state number increases, the number of accessible electronic defect sites also increases, as evidenced by the enlarged hysteresis voltage window (ΔVhys) (Supplementary Fig. 25)41,42. Figure 3c schematically illustrates the band diagrams of the proposed PDM at the erased and programmed states of the CTL under zero gate bias, respectively. The HfO2 trapping layer in CTL plays a crucial role in achieving diverse time constants for multiple sub-reservoirs by enabling stable electron storage. In contrast, the device fabricated without the trapping layer exhibits a limited level of states and a range of time constants tunability (Supplementary Fig. 26). The positive VProg applied to the gate of PDM induces electrons tunneling from the Si channel into the HfO2, where the charges are retained after bias removal, resulting in the band bending in the CSL and Si channel. This band bending triggers electron emission from deep trap states in the CSL (below the Fermi level), converting filled traps into vacant states. Conversely, a negative VProg causes electrons to tunnel back from HfO2 to the Si channel, mitigating band bending and restoring shallow trapping states in the CSL. We speculate that the modulation of temporal dynamics originates from changes in the effective trap state distribution in the CSL region, including bulk oxygen vacancy-related defects and possible interface trap contributions, depending on the CTL-programmed states. Figure 3d, e presents the band diagrams of the PDM during volatile resistive switching, corresponding to the erased and programmed states, respectively. Under nanosecond-scale pulse stimulation in the erased state, shallow traps in the CSL capture electrons, and the subsequent conductance recovery is governed by a low activation energy relaxation process in which the effect of the interfacial step-wise potential barrier is more apparent (Fig. 3d). In contrast, when the CTL is programmed, the modified band bending increases the accessible deep traps in the CSL, and the temporal relaxation becomes increasingly dominated by deep trap-involved relaxation with higher activation energy and longer time constants (Fig. 3e). Multiscale signal processing by temporal dynamics modulation

To construct the wide physical reservoir layer, devices with diverse temporal dynamics are required to function as sub-reservoirs that enable feature extraction more effectively in multiple time scales14. We leverage the tunable temporal dynamics of PDM to implement sub-reservoirs within a wide physical reservoir layer. To demonstrate the potential of the PDM-based wide RC systems, we perform an autonomous prediction task on a multiple superimposed oscillator (MSO), utilizing controllable time constants through distinct CTL-programmed states18,43. The MSO time-series data is generated by summing three sinusoidal functions with distinct frequencies, reflecting multiple inherent timescales (see “Methods” for details). It is synthesized as a pseudo-non-periodic function to deliberately increase the difficulty of accurate time-series prediction. In this aspect, a single reservoir layer consisting of a simple carrier-dynamic device is limited in capturing the features across multiple timescales. Notably, harnessing the tunable temporal dynamics of PDM, we construct a wide physical reservoir composed of sub-reservoirs with distinct CTL-programmed states for efficient multiscale feature extraction (Fig. 4a).Fig. 4Wide RC using memtransistor with tunable temporal dynamics for the prediction of multiple timescales data.a A schematic of a wide RC system. A total of 1000 time steps were used in the MSO autonomous prediction task; the time steps from 1 to 200 were allocated for initialization that prepares the internal states of the reservoir, 201 to 700 for training the readout layer, and 701 to 1000 for testing. The generated time-series input u(t) was linearly mapped to an appropriate voltage range from 1 V to 3 V, and fed into the gate electrode of PDM with drain bias to read the reservoir states. To construct a wide reservoir system, outputs from a single device under 8 distinct CTL-programmed states were collected to minimize the effect of device variations. These states serve as 8 sub-reservoirs that together constitute the wide physical reservoir layer. For training the readout layer, the reservoir states collected from the output currents at present steps to M−1 previous time steps were used in the ridge regression algorithm to calculate the output weights for next time step prediction. In the autonomous prediction task, the generated next time step signal based on the reservoir outputs was fed as the new input to the wide reservoir layer in a closed feedback loop. Therefore, the system can autonomously and continuously predict the time-series data. The dynamic current responses from 8 different CTL-programmed states (state 0 to state 7) at the training step are shown in Fig. 4b. Given the same input sequence, each PDM state exhibits distinct current responses due to the varying degrees of band modulation induced by the density of stored electrons in CTL. The experimentally measured currents and the input data are also examined in the frequency domain, as shown in Fig. 4c. The presented input signal shows the three peaks corresponding to three discrete frequencies. The transformed outputs from PDMs with multiple CTL-programmed states show the various harmonic features, including the input signal due to the nonlinearity of the device and temporal integration effects19. The PDM at a higher CTL-programmed state exhibits stronger harmonic generation effects, meaning that the temporal integration is enhanced based on longer relaxation times. Therefore, each PDM with a different CTL-programmed state can function as a sub-reservoir by generating quantitatively distinct outputs under the same input, and their multiple programmed states can collectively form the wide reservoir layer. We attempt the MSO autonomous prediction based on the experimentally calibrated device model for continuous time-series data processing (Supplementary Fig. 28, Supplementary Note 2). Figure 4d compares the autonomous prediction results of the state 0 single reservoir and the wide reservoir composed of 8 CTL-programmed states; state 0 is used as the single reservoir baseline under the present MSO time step condition (Supplementary Fig. 29). When a single PDM state (state 0) is used as the reservoir layer, signals of fewer than 10 steps can be accurately predicted. The predicted signal states deviate from the ground truth in the subsequent time steps, with a normalized mean squared error (NMSE) over 300 test time steps around 2.9 × 10−1. However, when a wide reservoir with 8 different CTL-programmed states is utilized, the predicted signal accurately follows the ground truth with an NMSE of 6.8 × 10−3, corresponding to a 40-fold improvement in prediction accuracy due to effective multiscale feature extraction. This result shows that prediction accuracy can be substantially enhanced when the reservoir incorporates multiple states spanning a wide range of temporal dynamics. Notably, for the present MSO task under the same evaluation conditions, the accuracy gain approaches saturation as the reservoir is expanded, suggesting that substantial temporal diversity is already provided before the full combination of 8 CTL-programmed states is utilized (Supplementary Fig. 30). Control analyses further support that this improvement primarily originates from the programmed temporal dynamics diversity (τ modulation), rather than from conductance-level modulation among the sub-reservoir states (Supplementary Fig. 31). The impact of these factors is evidenced by varying the number of reservoirs (device states) and the maximum difference in time constants within the reservoir configuration (ΔτMax), where simultaneous increases in both parameters consistently yield higher prediction accuracy (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 32)44. We further performed the long-term prediction task, confirming the robustness and reliability of forecasting capability (Supplementary Fig. 33). Sensitivity analysis of the long-term prediction NMSE to τ variation also confirms the robustness of the wide reservoir configuration (Supplementary Fig. 34). In addition, an MSO input with five frequency components was evaluated in simulation to verify that the performance advantage persists under a broader frequency composition (Supplementary Fig. 35). These findings demonstrate that the PDM-based wide RC system, leveraging its tunable temporal dynamics, outperforms traditional reservoir architectures in time-series prediction while avoiding additional input-layer design, masking processes, and fabrication complexity.

Memtransistor array for wide reservoir computing system

A PDM array was fabricated to demonstrate a wide physical RC system for multiple input signal processing (Fig. 5a). The PDM features a simple three-terminal transistor structure with CMOS compatibility and enables temporal dynamics modulation without additional terminals or constant gate biasing, allowing for highly scalable array integration. In an array structure, PDMs in each row share a common gate line (word line) and a common drain line (bit line), and the reservoir states are read out through the source lines. Based on the PDM array, we performed a prediction task of the time-dependent Lorenz attractor, which is a nonlinear and deterministic 3D chaotic system to model real-world convection phenomena such as atmospheric dynamics45. The 3D Lorenz attractor was decomposed into three 1-dimensional time-dependent signals (x(t), y(t), and z(t)), which were fed into separate rows of the array. As shown in Fig. 5b, the 16 × 16 PDM array is implemented in a 2T (1 PDM–1 select transistor) configuration, in which the select transistor (Sel. Tr) is used to suppress unwanted current paths (Supplementary Fig. 36). The CTL in each PDM can be selectively programmed to distinct states for modulating temporal dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 37). By sequentially programming 8 PDMs into states 0 to 7 and mapping them to source lines 1 through 8, each row in the PDM array functions as a wide reservoir layer, as supported by the state-resolved transient responses measured from the 3 × 8 array (Supplementary Fig. 38). The three 1-dimensional temporal inputs, x(t), y(t), and z(t), were linearly mapped to an appropriate voltage range (1–4 V) and directly fed into the 3 × 8 PDM array row-by-row (to the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd rows, respectively, via the word lines), generating reservoir states that were then transferred to the output layer (Supplementary Fig. 39). For each row, the responses of 8 differently CTL-programmed PDMs were acquired sequentially under the corresponding input. Repeating this procedure for the three rows produced the 24 reservoir state responses in total. After 100 time steps of initialization, 101–1064 were used for training the readout layer and 1065–1564 for testing. The measured current responses of the 3 × 8 PDM array during the training steps are presented in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 40. Although the present measurements used sequential readout, the row-wise PDM array architecture provides a feasible route toward parallel feature extraction with optimized readout circuitry (Supplementary Fig. 41).

The Lorenz attractor prediction result based on the reservoir states obtained from the 3 × 8 PDM array and a simulated readout layer closely matches the ground truth with a small deviation (NMSE: 2.8 × 10−3) (Fig. 5d). For comparison, we also performed prediction using a single CTL-erased state (state 0) across the same array size (3 × 8 array, total 24 devices) and a software-based approach. Where only a single state is utilized, device variations may serve as a physical source of reservoir diversity; however, the lack of controllability and narrow temporal dynamics range result in limited computing capacity (NMSE: 1.2 × 10−2) (Supplementary Fig. 42). By contrast, CTL programming provides a controllable temporal dynamic span across states 0–7, yielding an approximately 4.5-fold modulation of the time constant across the 16 × 16 PDM array (Supplementary Fig. 43). A comparison of the PDM array operation energy between the single RC and wide RC schemes is provided in Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 44. As shown in Fig. 5e, the prediction using the PDM array configured with 8 reservoir states achieves accuracy comparable to the software-based network. We also compared the characteristics of the PDM with previous studies for the physical reservoir layer, including write/read energy and input pulse width (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Note 3), highlighting the intrinsic tunability and high scalability of PDM for wide physical RC systems.

Beyond the present demonstration of the PDM as a programmable multiscale physical reservoir for wide RC, the platform is not limited to this specific architecture. Because the PDM combines volatile temporal processing in the CSL with non-volatile programmability of temporal dynamics through the CTL, it may be extended to other time-domain computing modules beyond RC (Supplementary Note 4). In particular, this functionality may enable adaptive temporal filtering/encoding in near-sensor processing46,47, as well as configurable integration and decay responses in artificial dendritic or neuronal processing blocks17,48. In addition, recent reconfigurable neuromorphic devices have shown that integrating volatile and non-volatile operation modes within a single device platform can enable multifunctional computing applications49. The dual-functional design of PDM may provide a complementary route toward such reconfigurable neuromorphic hardware with programmable temporal signal processing.

Discussion

We have demonstrated a PDM array with tunable temporal dynamics, enabled by a dual-functional gate stack consisting of programmable CTL and temporally responsive CSL. By controlling the stored electron density in the CTL, the transient relaxation behavior of the PDM can be precisely modulated, allowing multiscale temporal responses within a single device. The experimental demonstration of on-demand control over relaxation time constants leads to the generation of enriched reservoir states, underpinning a wide RC architecture without requiring additional input layer preprocessing and circuit overhead. We investigated the underlying mechanism of this dynamic modulation, suggesting that internal band bending from CTL programming operation alters the trap state distribution in the CSL, thereby controlling the activation energy and time constant of relaxation. The PDM exhibited stable and energy-efficient volatile operation, with minimal cycle variations and robust endurance. By leveraging this temporal dynamic tunability, we constructed a wide physical reservoir layer using a single PDM and demonstrated its capability in the autonomous prediction of MSO with high accuracy. We further scaled this concept into an array level, where each row functions as an independent wide reservoir layer for addressing multi-variant temporal signals. The PDM array successfully predicted the Lorenz attractor time-series information, achieving accuracy comparable to that of software-based approaches. Our findings establish a scalable, CMOS-compatible, and energy-efficient platform for physical RC, paving the way for high-precision and low-latency time-series data processing in edge computing systems.

Methods

Device fabrication

A 55 nm thick Silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer was oxidized by a thermal furnace, and the grown oxide was removed by HF, leaving behind a 25 nm thick Si layer. Subsequently, ion implantation (Dose 5 × 1012 cm−2, 18 keV, Phosphorus) and annealing (1273 K, N2 atmosphere, 1 min) were performed for moderate channel conductance. Each mesa pattern on buried oxide was defined by photolithography and Reactive Ion Etch (RIE) with a 5 μm channel width. The photolithography was carried out for CSL patterning on the channel region, and buffered oxide etchant (BOE) cleaning was conducted to remove native oxide. 5 nm IGZO was deposited by radio-frequency magnetron sputtering of an IGZO target (Kurt J. Lesker, USA) using argon as the carrier gas, followed by lift-off to form a CSL pattern. The sample was transferred to an ALD chamber. The gate stack with a tunneling oxide (SiO2) of 3 nm, a trap layer (HfO2) of 5 nm, and a blocking layer (SiO2) of 12 nm was deposited as CTL by a plasma-enhanced ALD system at 473 K. The contact hole pattern was defined by photolithography and CTL on the source and the drain was removed by BOE for the metal pad. Lastly, Ti/Au (55 nm/20 nm) was deposited as an electrode pad for the source, drain, and gate.

Electrical measurement

All DC measurements were performed using a parameter analyzer (Keithley 4200A-SCS) with a conventional probe station. Nanosecond-scale pulses were applied with a Pulse Measurement Unit (PMU), and current responses were measured by a parameter analyzer. A data acquisition system (DAQ, National Instrument USB-6363) and a current preamplifier (Model 1211, DL instruments) were utilized for the endurance test. The repeated switching cycle was applied to the device through DAQ, and a pre-amplifier measured the output current. Custom MATLAB® code was utilized for modulating the pulse magnitude and width. Temperature measurements for activation energy calculation were conducted using a vacuum probe station. The pulse measurements for the mechanism study were carried out in the temperature range of 293 K to 373 K under vacuum conditions (~10−2 Torr). The relaxation time constants were extracted using a single exponential fit of the drain current relaxation. Representative fitting examples for single gate-pulse response measurements are provided in Supplementary Fig. 6. For multiple pulse train (temporal summation) measurements, the time constant was similarly obtained by applying a single exponential fit to the drain current recovery segment after the full gate pulse sequence (with the gate stimulus removed; gate voltage returned to 0 V), following a procedure in ref. 32. Detailed timing parameters are specified in the corresponding figure captions.

Neuromorphic digit classification

For the N-MNIST evaluation, the event-based N-MNIST dataset (Orchard et al.)50 was converted into a 4-frame representation by grouping event information at corresponding pixel positions over four temporal segments. The resulting 4-frame data were binarized using a fixed threshold (0.2), and the binary values of the four frames at each pixel were encoded into a 4-bit sequence (0–15), yielding a 34 × 34 map of 4-bit codes for each sample.

To construct reservoir features, PDM current responses to all 16 possible 4-bit input sequences (0000–1111) were experimentally measured for 8 CTL-programmed states (states 0–7) and for input time intervals Δt = 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 ms. In the 4-bit pulse sequence, each bit was encoded as either a 3 V, 500 ns gate pulse (1) or a 0 V, 500 ns gate pulse (0). A drain bias of −2.5 V was applied, and the response value used for feature construction was the drain current measured at a fixed delay of 450 μs after the 4th pulse.

For a selected Δt and CTL-programmed state, each pixel-wise 4-bit code in the 34 × 34 input map was mapped to the corresponding experimentally measured PDM current response, generating a 34 × 34 reservoir-state map (1156 features). Classification was performed separately for each Δt/CTL-programmed state condition using a logistic regression readout (1156 × 10). The same readout-training protocol and hyperparameters were used for all conditions, with a fixed dataset split of 10,000 training samples and 1000 test samples.
