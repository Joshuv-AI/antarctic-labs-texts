Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is one of the world’s most pressing public health challenges, affecting hundreds of millions of people and imposing a heavy economic and healthcare burden and an associated impaired health-related quality of life interfering with social and occupational activities [9–11].

By 2050, more than 1.31 billion people are expected to have diabetes, both type 1 (T1DM) and type 2 (T2DM), with an age-standardized total diabetes prevalence rate of more than 10% in some countries. T2DM, which accounts for the majority of diabetes cases, is largely preventable and, in some cases, potentially reversible if detected and managed early in the course of the disease [11]. However, there is evidence that the prevalence of T2DM is increasing worldwide, mainly due to a rise in obesity caused by multiple factors, but also as a result of ageing populations, urbanization and lifestyle changes [12]. As a complex disease, diabetes is linked to complications and comorbidities, namely sequelae, that further reduce quality of life. In particular, sequelae represent the down-stream effects or secondary complications that occur due to the progression of the disease, often developed over time. For instance, common sequelae associated with diabetes include cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, neuropathy and retinopathy. The effective management and prevention of sequelae are of critical importance for the reduction of the long-term burden of diabetes on individuals and healthcare systems [13]. Nevertheless, the acquisition of reliable and high-quality data on the progression of sequelae remains challenging due to the heterogeneity of patient monitoring, data collection practices, and socio-demographic disparities [14]. A useful tool for quantifying the severity of a specific health state associated with diabetes sequelae is the disability weight (DW). Disability weights (DWs) are quantitative measures of health loss which are used to reflect the impact on an individual’s functioning in relation to a state of perfect health. DWs which range from 0 (indicating a state of perfect health) to 1 (equivalent to death), provide a standardized method for assessing and comparing the burden of various diseases and health states across populations becoming a critical component in the calculation of Years Lived with Disability (YLDs), which is a key metric in measuring the non-fatal burden of disease within the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework [15].

The assessment of risks associated with worsening sequelae directly impacts patient management, healthcare planning, and policy development. In consideration of the aforementioned factors and the application of the GBD framework, particularly the use of DWs, this study aims to estimate the risk of transitioning from diabetes without complications (“no complication”) to any complication or sequelae in both T2DM and T1DM applying survival analysis methods as a robust investigative tool to examine the risk progression over time. Specifically, the Kaplan-Meier (KM) estimator and the Cox proportional hazards model are used to evaluate the probability of a patient in developing complications, given that they are currently free of such complications. The DW is employed as a means of defining the initial status of "no complications," thereby offering a novel approach to the stratification of patient health and the assessment of risk trajectories. The patients observed were drawn from the Italian Diabetologists Medical Association (AMD) dataset, a longitudinal dataset collected over the period 2005 to 2017. The AMD dataset is the exclusive and most comprehensive source of national outpatient data on diabetes in Italy. It comprises data on diagnoses, rather than self-reported information, and is available for this study thanks to an exclusive research agreement. The longitudinal nature of the data allows for a robust investigation of time-to-event data while accounting for the influence of varying patient characteristics and covariates over the observation period. It is our understanding that this study represents the inaugural attempt in Italy to directly quantify the risk of developing sequelae through a wholly innovative approach based on the direct calculation of YLDs [12], achieved by utilizing prevalence data drawn directly from a dataset in which diabetes was not self-reported and applying disability weights to each sequelae documented in it.

There is a lack of information on the probabilities of progressing from diabetes without complications to different complicated health states and from these to more severe ones.

In fact, Italian literature on diabetes has historically used the AMD dataset to monitor the quality of care [30] and describe clinical indicators, such as glycemic control, blood pressure, and lipid profile. Although essential for clinical surveillance, these studies typically take a descriptive approach highlight the breadth of data collected (hundreds of thousands of patients in numerous annual editions), the presence of assessment tools, and the epidemiological relevance of complication surveillance, but they do not integrate a quantitative model of transition between clinical states [31]. Another example of Italian research focused on complications is the DAI Study, a historic multicenter cohort investigation aimed at estimating the prevalence, incidence, and risk factors associated with macrovascular complications in AMD patients with T2DM [32]. However, even in this case, the analysis focuses on the incidence of macrovascular events and risk determinants rather than on the use of synthetic measures of disease burden to define transition events over time. Considering that, the present study stands out because it uses YLDs, calculated by applying disability weights appropriate to the sequelae documented in the AMD dataset, as a criterion for defining and modeling transition events between clinical states in people with diabetes. This approach allows the temporal dimension of clinical evolution to be combined with a synthetic measure of health loss, thus offering a more integrated quantification of transitions to complications and their severity [12]. Therefore, while some Italian studies describe clinical profiles, quality of care, and incidence of complications based on the AMD dataset, no work has yet been published that has used disability weights and YLDs to formally estimate the risks of transition between clinical-care health states in Italian cohorts of people with diabetes. Specifically this study aims to estimate these risks and to quantify how many years are needed to develop a more severe sequelae according to sex, age, type of diabetes and geographical area.

Methods

Data

The AMD dataset is a national longitudinal dataset, collected in almost 300 centers distributed across the Italian regions. In the absence of a national diabetes registry, the AMD dataset represents the only comparable tool currently in existence that provides clinically diagnosed diabetes mellitus cases in accordance with the American Diabetes Association criteria [16].

The dataset comprises information collected by a computerized medical record about the center, the outpatients’ demographic characteristics and clinical variables such as type of diabetes, sex, geographical area, and the occurrence of sequelae associated with diabetes. Patient data are anonymized using a unique identifier. The DWs come from the GBD setting where they are assigned to each sequelae on the base of a specific health status [15]. In conducting our research, we adhered to the GATHER (Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting) statement at every step.

The YLD threshold used to define the transition between ’no complications’ and ’complications’ is based on the structure of the health states used in the GBD 2017 studies [33], for which a complete list of lay descriptions and related disability weights describing 235 distinct health conditions is available in Table S19. These health states are defined for each non-fatal sequela and allow the loss of health to be quantified on a scale from 0 to 1, thus ensuring a standardized measure of the disability associated with a clinical condition. This configuration is an integral part of the GBD methodology for estimating YLDs and justifies the use of positive YLD values as operational transition criteria. In the case of diabetes, it is reasonable to assume that individuals classified as “without complications” are not in a state of perfect health, since chronic disease can lead to a measurable loss of health even before the onset of clinically apparent complications. In particular, Table S19 shows the matching work carried out by us and the group of clinicians delegated by the AMD. The matching was carried out merging four datasets that comprised 1,924,405 patients spread across all the centers. Exams totalled 89,889,294 records and laboratory tests (blood values/meds) 134,263,099. The working group met several times to discuss the health state description of sequelae in the GBD framework, which corresponds to sequelae in the AMD dataset. The only sequelae that could not be traced, as there was insufficient information in the dataset, were those relating to kidney transplants. For some sequelae, no specific operation was necessary because the tests needed to detect them were directly available in the dataset. This is the case for blindness, neuropathy, diabetic foot, and amputation. For the other sequelae, medical assessments were made thanks to the collaboration of AMD diabetologists. The result was 35 T2DM sequelae matched with the corresponding DW. A DW of 0.049 refers to so called “generic” health state that corresponds to this description: “has a chronic disease that requires medication every day and causes some worry but minimal interference with daily activities”. Concerning the territorial area, for the sake of simplicity, the 21 Italian regions have been mapped into three macro-regions: North (Piedmont, Lombardy, Veneto, Emilia-Romagna, Liguria, Friuli-Venezia Giulia, Trentino-Alto Adige, Valle d’Aosta), Center (Tuscany, Umbria, Marche, Lazio, Abruzzo) and South and Isles (Campania, Puglia, Calabria, Basilicata, Sicily, Sardinia). Based on the matching and related DWs, YLDs were calculated multiplying the prevalence of each sequela for its disability weight providing for each patient an evaluation of their health status (see supplementary material for details and how the comorbidity case was handled) [12].

Survival analysis

Experimental setup

We conducted an analysis including all patients diagnosed with type 1 and type 2 diabetes. The transitioning to complications was examined using Kaplan-Meier estimates of cumulative probabilities and Cod proportional hazards models to estimate hazards. The event for each patient was identified as reaching a YLDs greater than 0.049, corresponding to the disability weight attributed to uncomplicated diabetes mellitus (types 1 and 2).

To ensure a proper baseline for the analysis,patients experiencing the event at time zero have been removed. The characteristics of these excluded patients have been compared with those of the remaining study to assess differences in baseline characteristics (see Table S6, S7, S8, S9).

Survival analyses have been first performed using Kaplan-Meier estimates of survival curves, stratified by diabetes type (type 1 and type 2). Subsequently, the study has been focused on patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. For these patients, Kaplan-Meier product-limit estimates stratified by sex, age classes, and geographical areas have been obtained. To test for differences in survival probabilities within these strata, the log-rank test [5] has been performed.

To obtain an estimate of the hazard ratios and the effect of quantitative covariates on the hazard function Cox proportional hazard models have been fitted as well. The proportionality assumption has been tested via Schoenfeld residuals test [6], and, where appropriate, interaction with time has been introduced in the model.

Although survival time was analyzed using continuous-time methods (Kaplan-Meier estimator and Cox regression), event occurrence was ascertained at clinical visits rather than at the exact time of onset. Consequently, the true event time as a continuous variable is known to lie within the interval between two consecutive visits, leading to interval-censored data. In the present analysis, time was measured in years since entry in the study implicitly assuming that events are approximately uniformly distributed within the year they happened and that visit schedules are sufficiently regular to limit potential bias. Given that patients were followed at least annually, and that the primary focus of the analysis is on relative hazard comparisons rather than absolute risk estimation, this approximation is expected to have a limited impact on the estimated hazard ratios. To assess the robustness of our findings to the treatment of event timing, we performed a sensitivity analysis using interval-censored survival methods on a random subsample of 100000 individuals to limit the computational burden. Survival distributions were estimated using the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator for interval-censored data [35], and differences were assessed using using permutation-based logrank-type tests with Sun’s scores, without assuming proportional hazards. The results of these sensitivity analyses (Table S20) showed survival patterns and group differences consistent in direction with the primary Cox regression results for diabetes type, sex, macro-area, and age.

Discussion

The severity of diabetes is well documented, with a substantial body of literature dedicated to investigating the development of complications that appear to be an inevitable consequence of the disease, as a result of the underlying biological mechanisms [18–20].

In this context, a comprehensive understanding of the disease, including an estimation of the probability of developing complications and the hazard profiles, would undoubtedly facilitate more rational and integrated patient healthcare. Furthermore, it would enable the identification of potential implications for the future management of diabetes mellitus. In relation to Italy, our findings suggest that individuals with T2DM have a 6% higher hazard (HR=1.06) of developing complications compared to those with T1DM. It can be observed that this hazard is subject to an increase over time, which serves to highlight the importance of implementing an effective strategy of prevention and intervention. Furthermore, the results indicate that males tend to show a 10% higher hazard (HR=1.10) when compared with females, which is coherent with the findings of previous studies on sex differences [21–23]. Nevertheless, by quantifying the risk, it is feasible to gain a more profound comprehension of the underlying factors, which can subsequently inform the development of more targeted and gender-specific medical interventions.

Although numerous analyses based on the AMD dataset are available in Italy, these have been used mainly for clinical surveillance and quality of care assessment purposes, describing process and outcome indicators and the frequency of specific complications. Similarly, other Italian studies have contributed to the epidemiological characterization of the risk of complications, but without adopting an approach oriented towards modeling transitions between health states throughout the course of the disease [30–32, 34]. Our study brings two main innovations:The explicit estimation of the hazards of transition between clinical states ordered by severity, also quantifying the time required for progression to more severe sequelae based on sex, age, type of diabetes, and geographical macro-area;Above all, the definition of the progression event using a synthetic measure of disease burden based on disability weights and YLDs, which allows for a standardized comparison between heterogeneous complications.As anticipated, the results seem to suggest that age is a contributing component to the progression of a condition from one without complications to one with complications. This is due to the chronic nature of the disease and the increasing longevity of patients with diabetes [24]. While older patients are at an elevated risk, they stand to gain from a gradual decline per year past the median age. This could be consistent with the efficacy of this particular healthcare setting: for the same age, the longer a patient has been studied the more favourable the prognosis, likely attributable to the establishment of a public center with standardized procedures and a specific focus on outpatient services. Furthermore, the findings indicate a disparity in the likelihood of transitioning from a condition with no complications to a more severe condition, which is stratified by geographic region. The association observed between geographical area and risk of progression to complications must be interpreted with caution. In particular, the higher estimated risk for Central Italy does not allow causal conclusions to be drawn about the underlying determinants, as this study does not include direct information on the organization of services, intensity and quality of care, characteristics of care pathways, or individual and contextual socioeconomic variables. Possible explanations related to differences in the organization of care, follow-up models, diagnostic timeliness, or clinical management should therefore be considered as interpretative hypotheses and not as evidence demonstrated by the data. Finally, in light of the aforementioned results and in the case of a disease such as diabetes, the implementation of targeted intervention strategies to mitigates complications in high-risk subgroups, is of paramount importance. This is further underscored by the fact that the advancement and deterioration of the disease not only results in an increase in direct costs, but also in a subsequent rise in indirect costs, which ultimately fall upon the shoulders of individuals, family and society.

Strengths and limitations

The data used in this study are somewhat dated. However, it was considered necessary to observe the risk of developing more severe sequelae before the Covid-19 pandemic. Although this may be a limitation, it may provide another avenue for research once the updated dataset is obtained and enough time has passed, as Covid-19 may affect people in the long term [26–29].

Another limitation, but also a strength, is that the AMD dataset is not representative of the Italian population, covering approximately 40% of diabetes patients in Italy. Nevertheless, it is the only dataset in Italy that provides longitudinal clinical information on this disease. In fact, part of the geographical differences could reflect heterogeneity in the composition of the population served by the participating centres (e.g., age distribution, duration of illness, clinical severity, or risk profiles), as well as differences in the coding or recording of clinical conditions. Future studies that integrate clinical data with structural and organizational information on health services and socioeconomic indicators may better clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed territorial differences.

Finally, non-parametric and semi-parametric approaches have been used in the statistical analysis to provide robust results. It must be stressed though that although being a very large sample and the only one available of its kind, the AMD data is not a random sample and therefore inferential implications should be considered accordingly. The size of the sample nonetheless, accounting for 40% of the total population of T2DM outpatients, should mitigate this limitation. The use of YLDs to define the event is, on the other hand, a novel approach that allows to consider transitions from a sequela to a worse condition not necessarily within the same pathway of the progression of the disease.

A further limitation concerns the measurement of event time: events were observed at scheduled clinical visits rather than at their exact time of onset. Consequently, the true event time is only known to lie within the interval between two consecutive visits, resulting in interval-censored data. In the primary analyses, time since baseline was measured in years and event times were approximated by the time of detection. This approach implicitly assumes no systematic tendency for events to occur earlier or later within the observation interval within the year. While regular follow-up (at least once a year) and the focus on relative hazard comparisons may to mitigate the impact of this approximation on estimated hazard ratios, the estimation of the baseline hazard and absolute survival probabilities may be affected. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first study in Italy to estimate the risk of moving from a state of health without complications to one with complications in people with diabetes. The findings suggest that the patients most vulnerable to developing complications are older, male, diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, residing predominantly in central and northern Italy. This draws attention to the significant burden of this disease in these regions. It is important to bear in mind that the patients studied were those who had not yet experienced the complication at the beginning of the study.

Nonetheless, an examination of the demographic and geographic profiles of the subset of patients who had experienced the event at the outset of the study revealed a distribution of characteristics that aligned with the identified risk factors.

In light of these considerations, it becomes evident that our findings should inform the development of a more effective health strategy and prevention plan. In addition, Although based on a non-random sample and therefore characterized by limited external validity, the results show solid internal validity and suggest that the methodological framework adopted could potentially be extended and replicated in contexts other than Italy. Furthermore, the results underscore the necessity for stratified interventions targeting high-risk groups. It would be beneficial for future research to investigate transitions from one outcome to a worse outcome and to further evaluate censored data in order to improve understanding of disease progression. Nevertheless, this research presents risks and hazards in a way that can be readily conveyed to policymakers and the general public, as diabetes complications represent a significant burden on both individuals and society at large. Finally, our research suggests that investment in healthcare is a necessary and cost-effective approach to controlling diabetes complications. In order to achieve sustainable development and ensure an enhanced quality of life for both current and future generations.
