Hepatitis A vaccine immunogenicity among seronegative liver transplanted children
Authors: Palittiya Sintusek, Siriporn Khunsri, Preeyaporn Vichaiwattana, Warunee Polsawat, Supranee Buranapraditkun, Yong Poovorawan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The hepatitis A virus (HAV) vaccine is highly immunogenic in general, yet data on its use in liver-transplanted (LT) children is limited. This study aimed to determine the seroimmunity to HAV in all LT children, and the immunogenicity of an inactivated HAV vaccine in seronegative LT children at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital. Seronegative LT children received the inactivated HAV vaccine at 0 and 6–8 months with adverse events monitored for 3 days post-immunization. The result reviewed that among 105 LT children, vaccination records were available for 81%, of which 7.1% and 16.5% with one and two doses of HAV vaccine were immunized before transplantation, respectively. Post-transplantation, 20.1% were seropositive for HAV, with 9.5% due to pre-transplant immunization. Eighty-three seronegative LT children (aged 7.25 ± 4.40 years; 48.6% male) received two vaccine doses. The seropositive rate increased following the first and second doses and reached to 51.5%, and 92.9%, respectively (p < 0.001), with no serious adverse events reported. Age at vaccination and the interval from transplantation to vaccination were risk factors for non-responsiveness (p < 0.001). The study highlighted inadequate HAV vaccination coverage, leaving most LT children susceptible to infection. HAV vaccine proved highly immunogenic and safe, emphasizing the need for improved vaccination strategies before and after liver transplantation.Trial registration TCTR20220110001.

Introduction

Hepatitis A virus (HAV) is a hepatotropic virus that is primarily transmitted via the faecal-oral route. While the disease generally presents mild symptoms and is self-limiting in children, it can progress to fulminant complications, particularly in individuals with compromised immune systems1,2. Those with pre-existing liver disease are at even greater risk for severe disease and fulminant hepatitis3,4. Liver transplantation is a life-saving intervention for children with end-stage liver disease that improves their quality of life and extends survival. However, the immunosuppressive therapy administered post-transplant makes recipients susceptible to various infections, including HAV infection.

Children who are candidates for solid organ transplants including liver transplants should ideally receive all age-appropriate vaccines, including HAV vaccine, prior to transplantation5,6. Serologic response to vaccination should be monitored to ensure effective immunization5,6. Despite these recommendations, the results of various studies conducted on children receiving organ transplants indicated suboptimal vaccination status7–10 and a loss of earlier acquired immunity post-transplantation11–14. To provide basis for most appropriate preventative-therapeutic measures in LT children, adequate information about their immunity status to vaccines preventable infections including HAV is crucial. In this regard, the number of studies in LT children is scarce15–17. This study was designed to determine the HAV vaccination and sero-immunity status among children, who have been liver transplanted more than six months earlier, and to assess the immunogenicity of the HAV vaccine among seronegative LT children.

Methods

Study design and participants

A single-centre study was conducted at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand, from August 2021 to October 2023. The medical records and available vaccination books of all pediatric LT recipients were thoroughly reviewed, encompassing baseline characteristics, vaccination history, basic laboratory findings, and the types of immunosuppressants administered. Inclusion criteria for HAV vaccination were established as follows: (1) participants had to be under 18 years old; (2) participants must have undergone liver transplantation at least six months prior to being considered for HAV vaccination; (3) eligible candidates were those who tested negative for HAV antibody and; (4) participants needed to be in a stable condition at the time of vaccination with no fever or acute illness.

Written informed consent and informed assent were obtained from guardians and participants aged > 12 years at the time of enrolment. For all participants in this study, no liver organs were procured from prisoners. The Thai Red Cross Society and the Excellence Center of Organ Transplantation, Faculty of Medicine, King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand, were the institutions responsible for the procurement of cadaveric and living liver donor organs, respectively. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Research Ethics Review Committee for Research Involving Human Research Participants, Health Sciences Group, Chulalongkorn University (COA No. 1663/2021 IRB No. 775/64) and is in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1983. This study was registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20220110001).

Vaccine and administration

After obtaining informed consent and/or assent, all LT children were evaluated for anti-HAV IgG concentration. Seronegative LT children were enrolled for vaccination and received 0.5 mL of the inactivated HAV vaccine (Vaqta, Merck Sharp & Dohme Corp., UK; lot numbers U005562 and W026231) via intramuscular injection at 0 and 6 months. Anti-HAV IgG concentrations were assessed at four visits; 0, 1, 6 and 7 months after the first vaccine dose (Fig. 1). Immunogenicity study

Pre- and post-vaccination anti-HAV IgG concentrations were measured using an automated chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay on the ARCHITECT i2000SR instrument (ARCHITECT anti-HAV IgG, Abbott, Wiesbaden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The test demonstrated a sensitivity of over 98% and a specificity of over 99.17%, respectively. Samples with a signal-to-cut-off (S/CO) ratio greater than 1.00 were considered seropositive.

Adverse events

All participants were contacted by phone on day 3 after vaccination to inquire about adverse events. The specific local (pain, swelling, redness) and systemic (fever, malaise, vomiting, headache, others) symptoms were queried by a research assistant and recorded after the first and second doses of the inactivated HAV vaccine.

Statistical analysis

Categorical and continuous variables were presented primarily as medians (interquartile ranges (IQRs)) or means (standard deviations (SDs)), and proportions or counts were used when applicable. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, while continuous data were assessed using either Student’s t test or the Mann‒Whitney U test. A p value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. The statistical analysis was conducted using STATA version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Graphical representations and figures depicting the analysed data were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.2.0 for Windows (GraphPad, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Baseline serological immunity to HAV in LT children

The vaccination records and medical data of 105 LT children were reviewed, and anti-HAV IgG antibody concentration was assessed. The anti-HAV IgG-seropositive rate was 21.0%. The median ages of the LT children in the seropositive and seronegative groups were comparable. However, the seropositive group had a significantly higher proportion of males (p = 0.049). Vaccination records were available for 85 (81%) patients, with 6 (7.0%) and 14 (16.5%) of whom had previously received one dose and two doses of an HAV vaccine, respectively. Two LT children received one dose before and one after transplantation, while one child received two doses post-transplant. Notably, 10 (50.0%) LT children received one (n = 5) or two (n = 2) doses before transplantation were seronegative and at least 9/22 (40.9.%) seropositive children had prior HAV infection. Seroconversion rate after inactivated HAV vaccination

Thirteen of the 83 seronegative LT children were excluded from the study; three were over 18 years old, five declined participations, and 5 had undergone liver transplantation less than 6 months prior (Fig. 1). Hence, seventy LT children were included in the study and received two doses of the inactivated HAV vaccine, with a median interval of 203 (182, 245) days. The baseline signal-to-cut-off (S/CO) ratio of anti-HAV IgG titre was 0.24 (0.16, 0.42). After vaccination, the seropositive rate approached to 51.5% and 92.9% following first and second doses of HAV vaccine administration, respectively (p < 0.001). Additionally, S/CO ratio of anti-HAV IgG titre increased from 1.24 (0.53, 2.67) to 12.81 (9.61, 14.42) (p < 0.001). The time from baseline to the first dose and from the first to the second dose of the HAV vaccine was 52.5 (34, 100) days and 201 (182, 245) days, respectively. No significant difference was observed in the interval between doses between the vaccine responders (n = 65) and non-responders (n = 5).

Comparison of seropositive and seronegative LT children after completion of two-dose HAV vaccination

After completion of two-dose HAV vaccination, five (7.1%) children remained seronegative. This group was notably younger at vaccination (1.56 (1.55, 2.34) vs 6.94 (3.8, 10.44) years) and had a shorter interval from liver transplantation to vaccination (0.96 (0.68, 1.31) vs 4.88 (2.67, 7.68) years) compared to the seropositive group (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences in gender, the number of immunosuppressants used, the main type of immunosuppressants used, or the baseline S/CO ratio of anti-HAV IgG titre before vaccination. Additionally, the interval between the two doses of HAV vaccine was similar in both groups (210 (182, 245) vs 182 (182, 196) days).

Adverse events following HAV vaccination

Pain at the injection site and localized myalgia were reported in a few participants after the first dose (2 cases), but no such effects were noted after the second dose. No serious adverse events were observed in any of the participants.

Discussion

The present study highlights the low rate of acquired immunity against HAV infection in LT children, regardless of prior vaccination or natural infection. Among LT children who received HAV vaccination before liver transplantation, the low immunity rate might be the results of poor immune response or loss of immunity after transplantation. However, after liver transplantation, protective levels of anti-HAV IgG were achieved, with a significant increase in seropositive cases and anti-HAV IgG concentrations after 2-dose HAV regimen. Additionally, no serious adverse events of the HAV vaccine were observed in these children. Younger age and a shorter interval between transplantation and vaccination were associated with a higher risk of non-response.

In Thailand, HAV seroprevalence has declined, which aligns with an improved health care system. In 2014, Sa-nguanmoo et al. reported that 7.84% to 21.2% of children aged 1–12 years in Thailand were anti-HAV IgG-seropositive18, which is consistent with the findings in this study. Despite of this finding, the vaccination coverage among LT children, including those awaiting transplantation at our center, remains very low10, similar to findings in Swiss study19 and a survey by the SPLIT Research Group20. This low coverage may be due to the severity of their illness, leaving little opportunity to vaccinate before transplantation20. Even post-transplant, vaccination rates remain lower than in the general pediatric population. There is a pressing need for specific evidence-based guidelines for appropriately immunizing children with liver diseases before and after transplantation. Our study is significant in demonstrating the high immunogenicity and safety of the HAV vaccine in LT children, which should increase confidence among healthcare professionals and provide valuable information for future guidelines and policies.

Detection of anti-HAV IgG generally provides sufficient protection against HAV infection, but in LT adults, the seroprotective effect of anti-HAV IgG can vary widely, ranging from 0 to 97%21–23. Only one retrospective study has explored the immunological response to a booster dose of the HAV vaccine in LT children who were previously immunized but lost immunity post-transplant. In that study (n = 11), St Maurice et al. found that 7 out of 11 (63.7%) LT children achieved seroconversion after the booster dose, though the small sample size prevented identification of nonimmune risk factors17. To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first to assess the immunogenicity of HAV vaccination in LT children, most of whom (85.7%) had not been previously immunized. The seroconversion rate after vaccination was promising, with 92.9% of participants responding positively in the short term, similar to the 97% seroconversion rate observed in children with chronic liver disease reported by Ferreira et al.24. Additionally, our study identified age at vaccination and the interval between liver transplantation and vaccination as potential factors influencing seroconversion after the 2-dose HAV immunization. Although the number and type of immunosuppressants used appeared to be associated with a nonimmune response, this did not reach statistical significance. The findings suggest that older age and a longer time from liver transplantation to vaccination may be linked to a lower dosage of immunosuppressants and stronger cellular immunity in LT children, warranting further investigation.

The HAV vaccine is known for its high immunogenicity, with up to 97% of individuals who receive two doses maintaining detectable anti-HAV IgG antibodies for more than 20 years25. However, studies have shown a gradual decline in anti-HAV antibodies in immunocompromised patients26, including LT individuals21–23,27,28, similar to what we observed in our previous study on the immunogenicity of the hepatitis B vaccine in LT children11,29. In this present study, four children who had received the two-dose HAV vaccination prior to liver transplantation were found to be nonimmune post-transplant, potentially indicating a weak immune response or immunologic loss over time. Therefore, it’s crucial to investigate the risk factors for a weak immune response to HAV vaccination and the potential for immunologic loss over time. Additionally, monitoring of anti-HAV IgG levels in these LT children after HAV immunization is necessary to assess whether a booster dose is required to ensure continued protection.

This study has several limitations. First, although the intervals between the first and second doses of the HAV vaccine in the seronegative and seropositive groups were not significantly different, the intervals between the two doses varied from 6 to 8 months, as we prioritized visits mainly to assess health status rather than for vaccination. Next, the participants in the present study had a high rate of seroconversion after two-dose immunization; therefore, the factors associated with seroconversion might not have been identified because of the relatively small number of participants with non-responsiveness. Finally, the cellular immune response after hepatitis A vaccination was not investigated in this study.

In summary, HAV vaccine was highly immunogenic in LT children. Study showed the proportion of vaccinated children before and after LT was very low and efforts should be made to increase vaccination coverage rates in this high-risk group.
