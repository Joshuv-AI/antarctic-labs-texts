Traumatic abdominal wall hernias: a 20-year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Maria Kristina Vanguardia, Chen Lew, Matthew Lukies, Joseph Mathew, Mark Fitzgerald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraumatic abdominal wall hernias (TAWHs) are uncommon but serious injuries resulting from blunt abdominal trauma. Despite their low incidence, the associated morbidity and mortality rates necessitate effective management strategies. This study aims to assess the incidence, injury patterns, and optimal management strategies for TAWHs.MethodThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at a level-1 trauma center in Melbourne, Australia, spanning 20 years. Patients diagnosed with TAWHs between October 2002 and October 2023 were identified through trauma and radiological databases.ResultsOut of 220 patients identified, 71 were confirmed to have TAWHs. The mean age was 45.2 years. High-speed motor vehicle collisions were the most common injury mechanism. About 54% of patients required intensive care unit admission, and 53.5% underwent trauma laparoscopy/laparotomy. Key factors necessitating immediate operation included CT findings of free gas, perforation, degloving injury, hypoattenuation suggestive of bowel ischemia, and traumatic bowel injury. Additional CT findings significantly associated with intra-abdominal injury were free fluid, hematoma, contrast extravasation to suggest active hemorrhage, and bowel herniation through the hernia defect. The majority of patients without these CT findings were successfully managed conservatively, with the option of elective mesh repair.ConclusionOur study highlights the need for clinical vigilance in high-energy trauma cases and the use of CT in diagnosis. Although trauma laparotomy is essential for suspected intra-abdominal injury, select hemodynamically stable patients may be safely managed non-operatively.Level of evidenceIII, diagnostic test/criteria.Keywords: general surgery; Hernia, Ventral; Multiple Trauma; practice guideline

Introduction

Traumatic abdominal wall hernias (TAWHs) are a rare but serious complication resulting from blunt abdominal trauma. First described by Selby in 1906, TAWHs occur due to shearing forces on the abdominal wall, typically during high-energy blunt trauma, leading to herniation through disrupted muscular and fascial layers without skin penetration or pre-existing hernia defects.1,3 Despite their rarity, with an estimated incidence of less than 0.2% in patients presenting to Australian tertiary trauma hospitals, TAWHs are life-threatening, with a mortality rate of 7–9% reported in modern literature.2 4

Large-scale studies investigating the incidence, injury patterns, and management strategies for TAWHs are scarce due to the condition’s rarity.4,6 Our hospital in Victoria, Australia, is Australia’s largest Level 1 Trauma Center, admitting 10,000 trauma patients per year, of whom 1,700 are defined as major trauma (resulting in death, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, immediate life-saving operation and/or an Injury Severity Score (ISS) >12. There is an associated National Trauma Research Institution registry, and this presents an opportunity to assess the incidence, injury patterns, and optimal management strategies for blunt abdominal wall hernias.

There is a reported 20–60% incidence of bowel injury in TAWH literature, highlighting the association with surgical interventions.2 4 A recent study by Santos et al on 64 TAWHs suggests that specific injury patterns, and patient characteristics are associated with an increased incidence of hollow viscus injury.2 Santos et al further suggest that initial non-operative management with close observation may be suitable for hemodynamically stable patients without peritonitis and with CT findings suggesting no hollow viscus perforation.2

The aim of this single center, retrospective review was to analyze the incidence, injury patterns, and imaging findings of patients with blunt TAWH. A key focus was to identify the clinical predictors of a therapeutic laparotomy among patients without immediate surgical indications such as hemodynamic instability or perforated intra-abdominal viscera. The secondary objective was to evaluate management outcomes and to develop a protocol for TAWH management.

Method

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at an adult Level 1 Trauma Center for patients >15 years old in Melbourne, Australia.

The study sample size was analyzed based on the availability of cases meeting the inclusion criteria within the study period. All patients who met the criteria for TAWH based on diagnosis codes of “traumatic hernia” or “avulsion” of the abdominal wall between October 2002 to October 2023 were included to capture all relevant cases. All inpatients who were admitted at our institution for at least 1 hour and had an intraoperative or radiological diagnosis of TAWH were included. Diaphragmatic hernias and penetrating traumas were excluded. To reduce potential selection bias, data was extracted systematically from electronic medical records and the National Research Trauma Research Institute Registry. Independent data verification was conducted by two authors, and the exclusion criteria were applied consistently. Subjects with incomplete data (n=2) were excluded from the final analysis. Researchers followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for reporting observational studies.7

Observation charts, operation reports, pathological findings, radiology reports, discharge summaries and external hospital referral information were also reviewed. Data collected also included age, gender, length of hospital and intensive care stay, mechanism of injury, diagnostic studies performed (such as white cell count at admission), operation reports, and treatment (such as number of packed red blood cells (pRBCs) within 4 hours of admission).

Early hernia repair was defined as hernia repair during the patient’s initial inpatient stay. The shock index is defined as heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure. In our study, an ISS greater than 12 was used to define major trauma.8 TAWHs were classified using the grading system proposed by Dennis et al, ranging from Grade I to Grade VI (online supplemental table 1).9 10 At our institution, patients presenting with acute abdominal trauma routinely undergo CT imaging as part of standard trauma assessment, with scans performed either preoperatively or postoperatively depending on hemodynamic stability. CT imaging was performed using multiple scanners during the study period. From 2005 to 2014, the GE Lightspeed VCT (64-slice) was used. From 2014 to 2023, imaging was performed using the Toshiba (Canon) Aquilion ONE (320-slice) scanner. Data from before 2005 were included where imaging was available; however, scanner type could not be confirmed for this earlier period.

During the study period there was no management protocol for TAWH. Management was determined by the treating surgeon. After initial resuscitation, management of abdominal wall injuries and associated sequelae included conservative (serial abdominal examination conducted by registrar on morning ward round), diagnostic laparoscopy or trauma laparotomy. Intraoperative contamination was classified according to the surgical wound classification system, based on the degree of gross contamination: clean, clean-contaminated, contaminated, or dirty. Follow-up time was defined as the interval from date of hernia repair to the most recent surgical abdominal examination (whether outpatient or emergency) up to a maximum of 10 years.11 At our institution, all patients were routinely scheduled for a standard postoperative appointment 4–6 weeks after operation, with further follow-up arranged only if clinical issues arise. Recurrence was identified based on either radiological evidence on CT or ultrasound, or clinical diagnosis on follow-up examination. Time to recurrence was defined as the interval between the hernia repair and the date of the confirming imaging or clinical assessment by a surgeon.

Continuous variables were summarized using mean and SD for normally distributed variables, and median and IQR for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarized using frequency and proportion. Ordinal data were compared using the Student’s t-test for parametric data or the Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric data, and categorical data were compared using the two-sided Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s χ² test. Logistic regression analysis was performed to analyze risk factors for laparotomy, with results reported as ORs and 95% CIs. Missing data for follow-up data were handled by excluding specific variables with incomplete data from the analysis, without affecting other data points for the same patient. Variables with missing data were treated as missing for those entries, without further imputation. For patients lost to follow-up (n=13), an intention-to-treat approach was applied; in these cases, it was assumed that they did not experience recurrence to avoid overestimating recurrence rates. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted specifically for follow-up data to assess the robustness of our findings, including rerunning analyses with different assumptions and imputing missing data. This approach confirmed that our primary findings remained consistent across varying assumptions.

To isolate predictors of therapeutic laparotomy among patients without absolute indications for immediate operation, we excluded patients who proceeded directly to laparotomy due to hemodynamic instability, peritonitis, or clear clinical signs such as exposed viscera or pneumoperitoneum. These patients were not included in the primary analysis of predictive factors. The main analytic cohort consisted of patients who underwent CT imaging without an immediate surgical indication. An analysis was conducted by separating patients into two groups.

Group A: patients with TAWH and “positive findings” on laparotomy or laparoscopy that is, those who underwent a therapeutic operation with positive findings intraoperatively for intra-abdominal injury or hemorrhage.

Group B: patients with TAWH and “negative findings” on laparotomy or laparoscopy that is, those who underwent a non-therapeutic operation without evidence of intra-abdominal abnormality intraoperatively other than the hernia itself. This group also included patients conservatively managed.

Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p value of 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.27.0 (IBM Released 2020. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.27.0. Armonk, New York, USA: IBM).

Results

The study identified 220 patients with potential abdominal wall avulsion/herniation from 7,608 blunt abdominal injuries presenting to our institution during the study period. Of these, 71 were confirmed diagnoses of TAWH with an incidence of approximately 0.9% and were included in the final analysis. There were 36 males (50.7%) and 35 females (49.3%) with a mean age of 45.2 years (SD: 18.5). Females with TAWH were older than males (mean 49.7 vs 40.9 years, p=0.043). The median body mass index (BMI) was 26.8 (IQR 7.7). The majority (82.4%) were multitrauma cases, with median ISS being 22.0 with most common associated injuries including thoracic injury (median Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS): 3.0), lower limb injury (median AIS: 1.0), and head injuries (median AIS: 0). Severe thoracic (p=0.117) or lower limb (0.460) injuries were not significantly associated with intra-abdominal injury or hemorrhage on laparotomy/laparoscopy. The median length of stay was 11.0 days (IQR 16.0), with over half (54.9%) requiring ICU admission.

TAWHs were most found in the right lateral abdominal wall, followed by left lateral and right anterior. The distribution of TAWHs is further broken down in figure 1. Drivers were 6.87 times more likely to have a right-sided abdominal wall hernias compared with front seat passengers, motorbike riders or rear left passengers (95% CI 1.77 to 26.76, p=0.003).

The most common mechanism of injury was high-speed motor vehicle collisions (MVCs) (n=54, 76.1%), with most involving restrained occupants, followed by falls (n=8, 11.3%) and direct blows (n=5, 7.0%). Upper abdominal (n=24, 33.8%) and lower abdominal (n=36, 50.7%) injuries were most frequent. Among patients who sustained injuries due to falls, all fell from a significant height greater than 1.5 m, with three cases involving falls from a horse. Median hernia defect size was 20.0 cm2 (IQR 24.0). Over half (n=38, 53.5%) underwent trauma laparotomy/laparoscopy during their acute admission, with five undergoing diagnostic laparoscopy and 33 undergoing trauma laparotomy. Of these, five patients proceeded directly to laparotomy prior to CT imaging due to hemodynamic instability. TAWHs were diagnosed on CT in almost all cases (n=66, 93.0%). Patients with TAWH not initially identified on CT were those who proceeded directly to trauma laparotomy and had CT postoperatively.

On multivariable analysis, lactate at admission as well as CT features of free fluid, contrast extravasation to suggest active hemorrhage, and bowel herniation through the hernia defect (grade V TAWHs and above) were significantly associated with findings of intra-abdominal injury intraoperatively. Of note, on multivariate analysis there was a 125-fold increased odds of intra-abdominal injury found intraoperatively with the presence of intra-abdominal free fluid on CT after accounting for hematoma, active hemorrhage, bowel herniation through the defect, elevated lactate, size of hernia defect and shock index (adjusted OR 383.96 (95% CI 4.50 to 32,743.01), p<0.001). In our cohort, no patients had isolated trace amounts of free fluid on CT; in all cases, free fluid was either moderate in volume or accompanied by other concerning radiological findings. Furthermore, on univariate analysis, hernia defect size (OR 1.02 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.03, p=0.039) was associated with intra-abdominal injury found intraoperatively, but was not significant on multivariate analysis. Factors associated with intra-abdominal injury found intraoperatively are broken down in table 1. 22 patients required activation of the massive transfusion protocol (MTP); however, this was not significantly associated with positive intra-abdominal findings at laparoscopy or laparotomy. The mean number of pRBCs transfused within the first 4 hours of MTP activation was 4.1 units (SD: 6.7). In our study, there was one case of a Grade VI TAWH, where the patient underwent a CT scan prior to operative management. BMI category, smoking status, pre-existing comorbidities, defect thickness through the abdominal wall musculature, and the size or location of the hernia defect were not associated with an increased likelihood of intra-abdominal injury identified intraoperatively.

CT findings that necessitated surgical intervention included free gas, perforation, degloving injury, hypoattenuation suspicious for bowel ischemia and traumatic bowel injury. Furthermore, of three patients with splenic injury, two also required laparotomy. The patient who did not undergo surgical intervention underwent a splenic artery embolization under interventional radiology for an isolated grade IV splenic laceration. Intra-abdominal injuries occurred in 28 patients (39.4%), most commonly involving the small bowel mesentery, small bowel and large bowel, further presented in figure 2. There was no increased risk of intra-abdominal injury with anterior TAWHs in our study (p=0.273). The mean contamination level among patients undergoing emergency hernia repair was 2.3, compared with 2.7 in those who did not have their hernia repaired at the time but underwent a trauma laparotomy. This difference was not statistically significant (p=0.091). Patients with clean (3/4) or clean-contaminated (3/13) operative fields were more likely to undergo early mesh repair compared with those with contaminated or dirty fields (0/5), although this trend did not reach statistical significance (p=0.073).

Ultimately, 50.7% (n=36) underwent surgical hernia repair either in the emergent (n=23) or elective (n=13) setting, with a median follow-up time of 12.0 months (IQR 24.3). These were commonly performed open (n=30) followed by laparoscopic (n=6). Overall, hernia defect size was not significantly associated with early versus later repair (p=0.646) although, increased hernia size was associated with repair at any stage (median (IQR): 15.0 cm2 (16.5) vs 31.0 cm2 (42.2), p<0.001).

All patients attended their standard 4–6 week postoperative follow-up appointment. Among those who underwent early repair, mesh was used in one-third of cases (7/23, 30.4%). The most common technique in this group was sublay mesh repair (3/7, 42.9%), with Prolene (2/7, 28.6%) being the most frequently used mesh type. Other meshes included Symbotex, Permacol, and Bio-A, used in a variety of configurations including sublay, inlay, and onlay. Early repairs were performed in cases classified as clean-contaminated, involving either no hollow viscus injury or only minimal contamination. In patients who underwent trauma laparotomy without immediate hernia repair but later proceeded to elective repair, all repairs involved mesh. Prolene was again the most commonly used (5/13, 38.5%), with other mesh types including composite mesh, Surgipro, and Parietex. Among conservatively managed patients, a subset later underwent hernia repair, all of which involved mesh (7/33, 21.2%). Overall, nearly half (35/71, 49.3%) of the cohort did not undergo hernia repair, most of whom were from the conservative management group, with a smaller number from the laparotomy group.

Recurrence was observed in four patients—two after early repair and two after elective repair. The mean time to recurrence was approximately 34 months. In the elective group, both recurrences occurred in patients who had mesh repairs. In the early repair group, one recurrence occurred after a mesh repair and one after primary repair with PDS suture. Among the patients who experienced recurrence, most had received mesh repairs, though one recurrence occurred after non-mesh repair. There was no significant difference in the rate of open repair between patients who underwent emergency repair and those who had elective repair (20/23 (87.0%) vs 10/13 (76.9%), p=0.438). There were no robotic repairs in either group.

Patients managed conservatively were closely monitored with serial abdominal examinations conducted by a surgical registrar on the unit. In the absence of other general surgical abnormalities, observation typically continued for 24–48 hours. No complications arose from a conservative approach, and more specifically, no patients experienced delayed perforation.

Discussion

This retrospective study, conducted over 20 years at a major trauma center, highlights the incidence, diagnostics, and management strategies for TAWHs. A total of 71 cases were identified, predominantly resulting from high-speed MVCs affecting the upper and lower abdomen.12 13 Our study specifically focused on patients without absolute indications for operation at presentation to identify which radiologic or clinical features on CT should prompt laparotomy. Patients who underwent immediate operation due to clinical instability or overt signs of intra-abdominal injury were excluded from this analysis to isolate more subtle predictors in hemodynamically stable patients. Notably, 53.5% underwent trauma laparotomy, with five requiring operation prior to CT scan. The majority of confirmed cases were repaired surgically, with no significant difference in recurrence rates between acute and elective repair. Key factors necessitating operative management were CT findings of free gas, perforation, and traumatic bowel injury.

Almost half of the patients with TAWH had bowel injuries necessitating surgical intervention, consistent with literature findings ranging from 18% to 90%.4 14 This discrepancy is likely due to the low number of studies investigating TAWHs, with most studies in the literature being case reports. Our findings indicate that conservative management may be safe for individuals without elevated lactate or CT evidence of free fluid, active hemorrhage, or traumatic bowel injury. This may be attributed to the diagnostic accuracy of CT findings in our study, which identified over 90% of hernias on initial trauma scan. Evidence of bowel perforation, or hypoattenuation suggestive of ischemia on CT universally mandated surgical intervention due to the high suspicion of hollow viscus injury. However, in hemodynamically stable patients without perforation and negative CT findings for bowel injury, an initial non-operative approach with close observation was appropriate in 97% of these cases in our study. With this approach, surgeons must remain vigilant with serial abdominal examinations, as delayed perforation can occur, although this was not observed in our study.12 15 16

Our study reveals that elevated lactate as well as free fluid, bowel herniation, and active contrast extravasation suggesting acute hemorrhage on CT were significant predictors of requiring surgical intervention, emphasizing the importance of these findings in decision-making. The size of the hernia defect on CT is also a consideration, although this did not reach significance on multivariate analysis. Given that patient-related factors such as BMI category, smoking status, and pre-existing comorbidities, as well as the anatomic location of the hernia defect, were not significantly associated with intra-abdominal injuries identified at the time of laparotomy, this suggests that radiological and physiological markers may play a more decisive role in guiding surgical decision-making than baseline demographic factors.6 However, it should be acknowledged that patient comorbidities are still likely to influence the overall risk-benefit assessment, particularly regarding survivability and recovery after laparotomy. Netto and colleagues suggest that clinically apparent large anterior wall hernias require urgent laparotomy, whereas occult hernias whereas occult hernias—those identified on CT but not evident on physical examination—can be managed conservatively.15 However, Pardhan and colleagues reported that only 30% of hernias were picked up preoperatively on CT in Western Australia, with the majority discovered at operation.5 This discrepancy may reflect differences in imaging practices across trauma centers.17 18 At our institution, over 90% of TAWHs were diagnosed on CT, likely due to the routine practice of performing CT scans on nearly all trauma patients stable enough for imaging. In contrast, Pardhan’s7 lower CT diagnostic rate may indicate a practice of proceeding directly to laparoscopy/laparotomy in a larger percentage of cases. Given these variations, it is crucial to acknowledge that whereas CT is a valuable diagnostic tool for TAWH, in some institutions, the majority may be diagnosed intraoperatively.

A notable proportion of patients in our study were overweight or obese—an expected finding, as increased intra-abdominal pressure from excess body weight likely predisposes to herniation.5 19 Hernia repair in obese patients is known to be more technically challenging, with higher perioperative complication and recurrence rates.19 For stable patients without signs of intra-abdominal injury, delayed repair allows for interim weight reduction and medical optimization, potentially improving outcomes. Despite this, a third of patients did not undergo hernia repair. Although defect size was not associated with timing of repair, larger hernias were significantly more likely to be repaired overall, suggesting that absolute size may influence clinical decision-making. Some patients may have undergone repair at local centers following discharge, but this could not be reliably analyzed, raising the possibility that a proportion remained unrepaired. This highlights the need to better understand barriers to elective repair after discharge, which may carry unaddressed morbidity.

A study by Wong et al on 47 patients with TAWH proposes a treatment algorithm for TAWHs, indicating that the grade of TAWH itself does not necessitate laparotomy unless it is grade VI.10 They also suggest early repair for anterior abdominal wall hernias due to the risk of concomitant intra-abdominal injury and subsequent strangulation, although this was not a significant finding in our study.10 Similar to our study, Wong et al indicates that evidence of intra-abdominal injury requires urgent surgical intervention, with our study highlighting CT features of free fluid, bowel herniation, hematoma, and active hemorrhage as critical indicators.10 Netto and colleagues found that the majority of patients with asymptomatic CT-diagnosed hernias did not develop symptoms in the follow-up period (ranging 1–16 months), and therefore may be conservatively managed.15 Overall, we propose that if patients present with significant CT findings such as free fluid, bowel herniation through the defect, hematoma, or contrast extravasation suggestive of active hemorrhage, immediate surgical intervention should be considered. The absence of delayed perforations or other complications in our conservatively managed cohort highlights the reliability of CT imaging in guiding management. This is particularly relevant in trauma centers where overuse of laparotomy can contribute to increased morbidity, longer hospital stays, and resource utilization. By identifying specific CT findings that mandate surgical intervention, our study provides a framework for a protocol-driven approach, reducing variability in clinical decision-making. In the appropriate clinical setting, in a hemodynamically normal patient, a diagnostic laparoscopy should be considered if the patient is at a trauma center equipped with the necessary facilities and expertise; otherwise, a laparotomy may be undertaken to promptly address and manage these critical conditions. This approach ensures that patients with severe presentations receive the appropriate level of care to mitigate complications and improve outcomes. Although not statistically significant, our findings suggest that early mesh repair was more often pursued in clean or clean-contaminated settings. In contrast, mesh was generally avoided in cases with contaminated or dirty operative fields, reflecting cautious clinical judgment in the context of infection risk. Although mesh repair is appropriate in those without intra-abdominal injury, larger studies are needed to further corroborate these findings in the acute setting.

Despite the rarity of TAWHs, this study provides valuable insights into their presentation, diagnosis, and management. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective, single-center design introduces potential selection and information biases, and the findings may not be generalizable beyond our tertiary trauma center. The small sample size, particularly the low number of recurrences, limited our ability to assess long-term outcomes or perform time-to-event analyses such as Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Conclusions regarding recurrence, mesh use, repair technique, and timing should therefore be interpreted with caution. Although CT imaging protocols for abdominal trauma remained consistent throughout the study period, with scans performed either preoperatively or postoperatively depending on patient stability, earlier cases may have been undercaptured due to limitations in trauma registry coding and incomplete electronic medical records. Recurrence and repair rates may also be underestimated. Some patients may have been lost to follow-up or received care outside our catchment area, especially if repairs occurred at local hospitals. Inter-surgeon variability in decision-making such as mesh use and contamination thresholds may have further influenced outcomes. The absence of standardized clinical guidelines for TAWH management meant treatment decisions were made on a case-by-case basis, limiting reproducibility. Larger, prospective multicenter studies are needed to establish evidence-based protocols for the diagnostic work-up and management of TAWHs, improve generalizability, and guide future care.

Our retrospective study on TAWHs investigates the diagnosis and management of TAWH over two decades at a major trauma center. We underscore the importance of maintaining clinical vigilance, especially in high-energy trauma cases, and using CT imaging as a key diagnostic tool. Although trauma laparotomy remains indispensable for hemodynamically unstable patients or those with CT findings suggestive of severe intra-abdominal injury, our findings demonstrate that hemodynamically stable patients with negative CT findings can be safely managed non-operatively. This approach avoids unnecessary laparotomies, reduces associated risks, and enables planned elective repair in appropriate cases. By integrating these findings into clinical practice, trauma centers can move towards a more standardized, evidence-based approach to TAWH management, improving patient outcomes and optimizing resource allocation. Future multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and develop formalized protocols for TAWH management.
