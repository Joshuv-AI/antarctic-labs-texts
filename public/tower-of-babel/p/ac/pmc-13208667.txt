Roxadustat in the Treatment of Pure Red Cell Aplasia Associated With Anti-EPO Antibodies
Authors: Blanca Serrano, Ana María Hoyos, Florentino Villanego, María Eugenia Rodríguez Mateos, María Paz Garrastazul, Auxiliadora Mazuecos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractErythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) are essential in managing anemia associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Although iron deficiency and inflammation are the most frequent causes of ESA hyporesponsiveness, a rare but serious complication is pure red cell aplasia (PRCA) caused by anti-EPO antibodies. This condition results in severe anemia and requires discontinuation of ESA therapy. Roxadustat, a hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor, has recently emerged as a promising therapeutic alternative.We report the case of a 72-year-old woman with stage G3bA1 CKD who initially responded to epoetin alfa but developed progressive anemia 11 months later, reaching a hemoglobin level of 5.6 g/dL despite dose escalation. Bone marrow biopsy showed absence of erythroid precursors, and anti-EPO antibodies were detected, confirming PRCA. ESA therapy was stopped, and roxadustat was initiated. Within 7 weeks, the patient achieved transfusion independence with stable hemoglobin levels. Follow-up marrow biopsy demonstrated erythroid recovery, and anti-EPO antibodies became undetectable.ESA-induced PRCA has been linked to subcutaneous administration, formulation changes, and genetic predisposition. Standard treatment includes ESA withdrawal and immunosuppression, although outcomes vary. Roxadustat may restore erythropoiesis without additional immunosuppression, providing both hematologic and immunologic benefit. Further studies are needed to validate its role in this context.Index Words: Anemia, anti-EPO antibodies, chronic kidney disease, pure red cell aplasia, roxadustat

Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) are an essential therapy in the management of anemia secondary to chronic kidney disease (CKD). The most commonly used agents are recombinant human erythropoietin (rHuEPO) analogs, with epoetin and darbepoetin being the most frequently prescribed in our setting.1

However, some patients may show an inadequate response to rHuEPO therapy, most often because of iron deficiency, infections, or inflammation.2 In rarer cases, treatment with ESAs can trigger pure red cell aplasia (PRCA), a condition caused by the development of anti-EPO antibodies that prevent erythropoietin from binding to its receptor and promoting the destruction of erythroid precursors in the bone marrow. This leads to severe anemia and necessitates discontinuation of the drug.3,4

Roxadustat, a reversible inhibitor of hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase (HIF-PHI), has been approved since 2021 for the treatment of anemia in nondialysis -dependent CKD patients and kidney transplant recipients in Spain.5 HIF is a transcription factor that increases endogenous EPO levels, regulates iron metabolism, and reduces hepcidin. Given its distinct mechanism of action, roxadustat has been proposed as an alternative therapy for patients who develop ESA-induced PRCA.3,4

We report the case of a patient with PRCA secondary to EPO therapy who was successfully managed with roxadustat.

Case Report

A 72-year-old woman with a medical history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure was referred to the Nephrology Department in April 2023 for assessment of stage G3bA1 CKD attributed to type 2 cardiorenal syndrome.

Baseline laboratory tests showed hemoglobin (Hb) levels of approximately 10.1 g/dL. A progressive anemia was observed, and treatment with epoetin alfa 3.000 IU weekly was initiated, achieving an adequate response with Hb increasing to 12.1 g/dL after 5 months. Since January 2024, she developed progressive anemia that required stepwise dose escalation up to 9,000 IU weekly. Despite this, hemoglobin levels continued to decline, reaching a nadir of 5.6 g/dL with inadequate reticulocyte production, necessitating red blood cell transfusions. Laboratory evaluation showed a mean corpuscular volume and hematocrit within normal range. In addition, a marked decrease in reticulocyte count (0.2%) was noted, whereas white blood cell and platelet counts remained normal. C-reactive protein levels were slightly elevated (7-15 g/dL; Fig 1).

Evolution of the case report in terms of hemoglobin levels (g/dL). The initiation of treatment with EPO as well as with roxadustat is indicated, with their respective doses. Abbreviations: EPO, erythropoietin; Rox, roxadustat; TIW, three times a week; UI/W, international units per week.

After ruling out other potential causes, a hematology work-up was performed. In June 2024, a bone marrow biopsy demonstrated findings consistent with pure red cell aplasia (PRCA). Given the suspicion, anti-EPO antibodies were requested. The first determination was performed by immunoblot, which is a qualitative technique, and the result was positive.

In July 2024, epoetin was discontinued, and roxadustat was initiated at 70 mg 3 times weekly and later increased to 100 mg after 4 weeks according to the prescribing guidelines. By week 7, she achieved transfusion independence with stable hemoglobin levels. The dose was subsequently tapered to 100 mg 3 times weekly, which she continues to receive with sustained response.

At 6 months after treatment initiation, a repeat bone marrow evaluation demonstrated complete recovery of erythroid precursors. A new determination of anti-EPO antibodies was requested, this time performed by enzyme immunoassay, which is a semiquantitative test, with positivity defined as an index greater than 1. Our patient had an index of 0.29 (Fig 2).

Discussion

PRCA secondary to the use of rHuEPO was first described in the mid-1990s, with an increase in the reported incidence between 1998 and 2004.2,6 This phenomenon was attributed to modifications in the formulation of epoetin alfa, such as the substitution of human albumin by polysorbate-80 as a stabilizer. This modification produced a conformational change of the protein, promoting aggregate formation and thereby increasing the drug’s immunogenicity. Another unconfirmed hypothesis was immune disruption because of exposure to uncoated rubber residues from the syringe plunger. Currently, it is thought that the pathophysiological mechanism, although not fully understood, is multifactorial. It involves external factors, such as the route of administration (subcutaneous vs intravenous) and longer treatment durations, which increase the drug’s contact with antigen-presenting cells in the subcutaneous tissue. In addition, intrinsic patient factors are involved. These include genetic predisposition in carriers of HLA DRB1∗9, advanced age, male, comorbid conditions, immune status, and other factors that cause a break in immune tolerance, favoring the activation of autoreactive T lymphocytes.2,3,6

Recent publications support the use of HIF-PH inhibitors as an alternative in ESA-resistant anemia. Roxadustat has been shown to stimulate endogenous erythropoietin production, improve iron metabolism, and reduce inflammation.7 Real-world data from Japanese hemodialysis patients demonstrate effective hemoglobin stabilization with a favorable safety profile, even after prior ESA exposure.8 These findings support our observation that roxadustat may restore erythropoiesis in immune-mediated PRCA while avoiding immunosuppressive therapy.

The presence of anti-EPO antibodies has been studied mainly in patients receiving hemodialysis, with a variable prevalence depending on the series, which can be explained by the type of technique used and the affinity of the antibody (9-11). The most relevant cohort because of its sample size was the Netherlands Cooperative Study on the Adequacy of Dialysis-2 (NECOSAD-2), in which 1,677 patients were included. The estimated incidence they obtained of anti-EPO antibody positivity from the start of dialysis was 1.27/1,000 patient-years, with only 1 case of PRCA. However, it is still not possible to predict which patients will develop it nor at what moment of the treatment it may appear.9,10

With respect to PRCA, it is an infrequent clinical entity characterized by severe anemia and reticulocytopenia (<2 × 109/L) following the initiation of rHuEPO because of the absence of erythroid precursors. It is associated with the development of anti-EPO antibodies, which block the binding of erythropoietin (both endogenous and recombinant) to its receptor. This occurs via an unknown mechanism, leading to destruction of erythroid precursors and severe anemia. Its diagnostic confirmation therefore requires a bone marrow examination, also to the early detection of antibodies.6,9 Latency is variable, from 3 to 67 months from the start of treatment, with the median at 9 months.3,11,12 In our case, the patient did not have the risk factors reported in the literature. Nevertheless, the condition developed 11 months after the start of EPO, meeting all diagnostic criteria.

This represents a serious complication in the patient with CKD and anemia, for whom the first approach should be discontinuation of the ESA. However, this measure alone is often insufficient to resolve PRCA. Switching analogues is also not recommended because of the possible crossreaction between them. The management currently recommended consists of the administration of corticosteroids at doses of 1 mg/kg/day, to which an immunosuppressive drug (cyclosporine A or cyclophosphamide) may be added to improve the response rate. With this therapeutic strategy, anti-EPO antibodies disappear in 57%-87% of cases.13,14 This percentage increases to 95% in patients undergoing kidney transplantation.14 Anemia is controlled in the acute phase with blood product transfusions; however, it is a problem that usually persists over time in nontransplanted individuals because their CKD compromises endogenous EPO production, even if they have cleared the antibodies.13 Rechallenge with rHuEPO in patients recovered from PRCA with negative antibodies after immunosuppressive therapy has been attempted, with some regaining response; however, data on response rates are limited.6 In the presented case, immunosuppression was not initiated to avoid worsening her comorbid conditions; nevertheless, the patient achieved a successful outcome without needing to reuse ESAs.

Roxadustat has shown efficacy in the context of anemia in CKD patients, with some cases described in China in which the drug was used in ESA-associated PRCA with very good response.3, 4, 5,13, 14, 15, 16 Four of them used it as first-line therapy, with one patient requiring the addition of cyclosporine immunosuppression because of a reduction in Hb levels after an initial response to roxadustat. In another case, it was used as second line after corticosteroid failure when the diagnosis had not yet been established.4 The response was measured through a progressive improvement in Hb and reticulocytes, although some have reported normalization of the erythroid lineage in bone marrow, as well as negativization of anti-EPO antibodies without the need for immunosuppressive therapy, as occurred in our patient.13,16 This represents an advantage because it reduces transfusion requirements and avoids immunosuppression and its associated complications, positioning it as a therapeutic alternative for immune-mediated PRCA. Furthermore, it provides an additional benefit through its role in the regulation of iron metabolism. In our patient, roxadustat allowed sustained transfusion independence in only 7 weeks, with morphological recovery of the bone marrow and negativization of anti-EPO antibodies. This finding provides novel clinical evidence in our setting, documenting not only hematologic efficacy, but also histological and immunological recovery.

In conclusion, anti-EPO antibody-mediated PRCA is an uncommon but clinically significant complication in CKD patients receiving ESA therapy. Its early identification is essential for management, requiring prompt withdrawal of rHuEPO. Roxadustat is proposed as an effective and safe therapeutic alternative that, in addition to correcting anemia through the restoration of erythropoiesis, appears to be capable of eliminating the antibodies without the need for additional immunosuppressive therapy. Nevertheless, the evidence currently available is limited, and these findings must be corroborated in appropriate clinical trials to validate its use in this context.
