Vitamin B12 desensitization and provocation: Ten-year clinical experience☆
Authors: Fatma Dindar Çelik, Kurtuluş Aksu, Özge Göktürk, Yavuz Karahan, Nur Betül Baştuğ İnan, Barış Uslu, Şenay Demir, Enes Çelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVitamin B12 is an essential micronutrient and hypersensitivity reactions (HSR) are considered rare.ObjectiveTo present the characteristics of HSRs to vitamin B12 preparations and to report the outcomes of desensitization and drug provocation tests (DPT) for vitamin B12.MethodsThe retrospective descriptive study included patients who required treatment for vitamin B12 deficiency and were hypersensitive to vitamin B12 or hesitant to use it due to other drug allergies. Low-risk patients based on their index reaction and patients hesitant to use vitamin B12 underwent DPT, while desensitization protocols were performed for patients classified as high-risk. Successful completion defined as the execution of all steps in the desensitization and DPT protocols without any subsequent immediate or delayed reactions.ResultsAmong the 44 patients, 28 patients (63.6%) had a history of HSRs following the use of cyanocobalamin. The remaining 16 patients (36.4%) had no prior history of HSR to vitamin B12-containing drugs but were hesitant to use vitamin B12 due to other drug allergies. DPT was performed in 38 patients (86.4%), while desensitization was performed in 6 patients (13.6%). The mean age was 46.34 ± 14.50 years and 32 (72.7%) of them were female. Skin prick tests (SPT) and immediate reading intradermal tests (IDTs) were administered in 20 patients (45.5%). Patch tests were conducted in 8 patients (18.1%) and delayed reading IDTs were performed in 3 (6.8%). All skin test results were negative. A total of 40 challenges were conducted and an immediate-type HSR was observed in 1 challenge (2.5%). Desensitization was performed in 8 cycle and HSR occurred in 1 (12.5%) cycle.ConclusionDPT is a reliable procedure that provides the assessment of low-risk patients with vitamin B12 hypersensitivity and prevents unnecessary drug restrictions. Additionally, desensitization protocols enable the continuation of vitamin B12 treatment in high-risk patients.

Introduction

Vitamin B12 is an essential micronutrient involved in numerous critical metabolic processes in the human body. Its deficiency can result in dysfunction across multiple organ systems, particularly the nervous and hematopoietic systems, and also impair DNA synthesis.1 The absorption and transport of vitamin B12 involve a complex, multi-step process within the gastrointestinal tract. Conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease and atrophic gastritis, prolonged use of certain medications (including metformin, proton pump inhibitors, and H2 receptor antagonists) as well as surgical interventions involving the stomach or small intestine, are recognized risk factors for vitamin B12 deficiency.2 Moreover, as vitamin B12 is absent in plant-based foods, individuals adhering to vegetarian or vegan diets are also at increased risk of developing a deficiency.3

Vitamin B12 is a molecule that contains a cobalt ion within a corrin ring structure, collectively referred to as cobalamin and it exists in 4 main chemical forms; methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin, hydroxocobalamin, and cyanocobalamin.3 These forms differ based on the substituent bound to the central cobalt atom. Methylcobalamin, adenosylcobalamin, and hydroxocobalamin are bioidentical forms of vitamin B12 found in the human body. In contrast, cyanocobalamin is a synthetic form of vitamin B12. Cyanocobalamin is stable and cost-effective form of vitamin B12 and is commonly used for oral or parenteral supplementation.4 In addition, bioidentical forms of cobalamin are also available as supplements.

Vitamin B12 replacement therapies are generally considered safe and hypersensitivity reactions (HSRs) are rare. However, both immediate and delayed HSRs have been reported in the literature, with severity ranging from isolated cutaneous lesions to anaphylaxis.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Vitamin B12 administration may lead to symptoms such as itching, rash, diarrhea, fatigue, palpitations and a sensation of limb swelling, which are thought to result from a rapid rise in serum cobalamin levels.8 These symptoms may mimic HSRs. Therefore, the diagnosis of cobalamin-related hypersensitivity should be approached with caution, as misdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary treatment restrictions. However, in patients who experience immediate HSRs to vitamin B12, replacement therapy can be successfully administered through desensitization protocols.

In this study, we aimed to present the characteristics of HSRs associated with vitamin B12 preparations and to describe the outcomes of desensitization procedures and drug provocation tests (DPT) conducted with vitamin B12.

Methods

Study design and ethical issues

This retrospective descriptive study was conducted at the Immunology and Allergy Department. The study was carried out in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments, and all patients were evaluated and included in line with good clinical practice guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee. As the study was retrospective in nature, consent to participate was not obtained.

Study population and data collection

The study population consisted of patients with an indication for vitamin B12 therapy who were referred to the Immunology and Allergy Clinic due to a history of HSRs to vitamin B12 preparations or hesitancy to use vitamin B12 because of previous drug allergies. This retrospective study covered a ten-year period and included patients who underwent DPT or desensitization with vitamin B12 between May 2015 and May 2025. Demographic and clinical information of the patients, drug provocation and drug desensitization information were collected retrospectively from the patient's files and hospital information system. Signs and symptoms that occurred during or following vitamin B12 supplementation, physical examination findings at the time of referral, were documented. Patients with missing or incomplete data were excluded from the study. The diagnosis and management of HSRs and anaphylaxis were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) and the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (AAAAI).12

Definitions and criteria selection

DPT was performed in patients categorized as low-risk for vitamin B12 hypersensitivity.13 The classification of low-risk patients was based on the criteria outlined in the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology/European Network on Drug Allergy (EAACI/ENDA) position paper on drug provocation testing.13 Patients with a history of HSRs characterized by a non-urticarial exanthema developing more than 6 h after vitamin B12 intake, involving less than 50% of the body surface area, lasting fewer than 7 days, without danger signs such as fever, lymphadenopathy, systemic or mucosal involvement, blisters, or eosinophilia, and not requiring hospitalization or systemic therapy other than antihistamines, were classified as low-risk. Patients who developed widespread urticarial rash, angioedema, anaphylaxis, wheezing, hoarseness, shortness of breath, respiratory arrest, palpitations, dizziness, hypotension, collapse or required adrenaline therapy following vitamin B12 administration were classified as high-risk.13,14 Desensitization was performed in these patients.

HSRs were categorized as immediate if they occurred within 6 h of vitamin B12 administration and as delayed otherwise.15 Desensitization was considered successful if the patient completed all steps of the protocol, even if there were immediate and/or delayed reactions at intermediate steps.16 Breakthrough hypersensitivity reactions (B-HR) was defined as a HSR occurring during or after administration of cobalamin via desensitization.

The form, route, and dosage of vitamin B12 replacement therapy were determined by the departments managing the patients’ vitamin B12 deficiency (hematology, gastroenterology or internal medicine) according to clinical necessity. In the Immunology and Allergy Clinic, the decision to perform DPT or desensitization was made based on patient evaluation and risk stratification.

Desensitization was performed by the intramuscular (IM) route using the index vitamin B12 formulation. DPT was generally conducted using the vitamin B12 formulation that had caused the index reaction, however, in a small number of patients, an alternative vitamin B12 formulation (hydroxocobalamin) had been prescribed by departments managing the patients’ vitamin B12 deficiency, and the DPT was conducted with that preparation.

Skin tests

Patients underwent skin prick test (SPT) with undiluted cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin at a concentration of 1000 mcg/mL (1:1), and intradermal testing (IDT) at dilutions of 1:10 (100 mcg/mL) and 1:100 (10 mcg/mL). Immediate reactions were evaluated within 15–20 min, while delayed reactions were monitored for up to 72 h. A positive SPT and IDT results were defined as a wheal diameter of ≥3 mm.17

Patch testing with undiluted cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin at a concentration of 1000 mcg/mL (1:1), as well as at a 1:10 dilution (100 mcg/mL) was performed in patients with a history of delayed-type hypersensitivity. Patch tests were evaluated at 48 and 72 h. A positive patch test result was defined as the presence of erythema, infiltration, papules, or vesicles at the application site.18 For IDTs and patch tests, 0.9% sodium chloride solution is used as the diluent.

Drug provocation tests

A three-step IM DPT was performed using 1 ampoule (1000 mcg/mL) of cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin in patients with a suspicious history of immediate-type hypersensitivity. In the initial step 0.1 mL (100 mcg), in the second step 0.4 mL (400 mcg) and in the final step 0.5 mL (500 mcg) of cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin were administered intramuscularly into the gluteal muscles (Table 1). There was a 1-h observation period between each step and the test proceeded to the next step only if no reactions were observed. DPT, either as a single daily therapeutic dose challenge or as a graded dose challenge, was administered to patients requiring oral preparations (for oral provocation, a tablet containing 1000 mcg of cyanocobalamin was used). The graded dose challenge followed a two-step protocol with a 1-h interval: the first dose consisted of 25% of the total tablet dose, followed by the remaining 75%. Following completion of the oral DPT, patients were clinically monitored for an additional 2 h.

In accordance with the EAACI/ENDA position paper on drug provocation testing, patients with unknown reaction histories to vitamin B12 that did not require treatment or emergency intervention underwent two- or three-step DPT.13 Graded DPT was also conducted in patients with a history of multiple drug allergies other than vitamin B12 who were hesitant to use it. In patients with a history of non-immediate low-risk reactions and symptoms resembling side effects rather than HSRs, a single therapeutic-dose provocation (oral or IM) was performed.

Patients were considered to have a positive DPT if they exhibited observable signs and symptoms during the provocation or the subsequent follow-up period, such as skin rash, pruritus, urticaria, angioedema, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, rhinorrhea, dyspnea, tachycardia, hypotension or anaphylaxis.13

Desensitization protocol

The cobalamin desensitization protocol of Caballero et al. was used with some modifications.11 During the first day of desensitization, ampoules containing 1000 mcg/mL of cobalamin (hydroxocobalamin or cyanocobalamin) were diluted with 0.9% sodium chloride to prepare concentrations of 1/10,000, 1/1000, 1/100, and 1/10. As shown in Table 2, these solutions were administered sequentially (0.1 mL, 0.3 mL and 0.6 mL for each concentration) with 15-min intervals between injections. In the final step, 0.1 mL (100 mcg) of cobalamin at 1/1 concentration (undiluted) was administered intramuscularly. On Day 3, 0.1 mL of cobalamin at a 1/1 concentration was administered. On Day 7, the dose of cobalamin administered with desensitization protocol was gradually increased to reach the therapeutic dose of 1000 mcg/mL. Subsequent administrations were performed on days 14, 28, and 56 according to the schedule. No administrations were performed on the days in between. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were obtained by using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (minimum - maximum) or median with interquartile range for continuous variables and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables.

Twenty-eight patients (63.6%) had a history of HSRs following the use of cyanocobalamin. The remaining 16 patients (36.4%) had no prior history of HSR to vitamin B12-containing drugs but were hesitant to use vitamin B12 due to other drug allergies. Among patients with a history of HSR to vitamin B12, 71.4% (n = 20) reported immediate-type reactions, while 28.6% (n = 8) reported delayed-type reactions.

SPTs and immediate reading IDTs were performed in 20 patients (45.5%): 15 with cyanocobalamin, 3 with hydroxocobalamin, and 2 with both cyanocobalamin and hydroxocobalamin. Patch tests with cyanocobalamin were conducted in 8 patients (18.1%), and delayed reading IDTs were performed in 3 (6.8%) patients. All skin test results were negative.

Among the patients who underwent DPT (n = 38), 17 patients (44.7%) tested according to the IM protocol, graded challenge was performed in 18 patients (47.4%) and single-dose provocation was performed in 3 patients (7.9%). A total of 40 challenges were conducted: 16 challenges (40.0%) with oral cyanocobalamin, 19 challenges (47.5%) with IM cyanocobalamin, and 5 challenges (12.5%) with IM hydroxocobalamin (IM challenge with both cyanocobalamin and hydroxocobalamin was performed in 2 patients). An immediate-type HSR was observed in 1 challenge (2.5%), that occurred after a graded dose challenge with oral cyanocobalamin. The patient's reaction occurred 5 h after the final dose, presenting with fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, and headache. The patient was treated in the emergency department with oral antihistamines and intravenous methylprednisolone.

Desensitization with cyanocobalamin was performed in 6 patients, comprising a total of 8 cycles. Four cycles (50.0%) were successfully completed. HSR occurred in 1 (12.5%) cycle; the patient developed pruritus and urticaria accompanied by dyspnea and rhinorrhea on Day 1, approximately 4 h after the last administration. Three (37.5%) cycles could not be completed, 2 were discontinued because the interval between patients’ visits during the cycle was longer than the desensitization schedule (desensitization was reinitiated from the first step on Day 1 in these patients, and the second cycles were successfully completed), 1 cycle could not be completed because the patient did not return for follow-up after the Day-7 administration. Discussion

The primary aim of our study was to evaluate the success rates of vitamin B12 provocation and desensitization. No reactions were observed in 97.5% of a total of 40 challenges, indicating that DPTs may be a viable option in low-risk patients to prevent unnecessary drug restrictions. Furthermore, HSRs occurred in only 1 of 8 desensitization cycles, demonstrating that vitamin B12 replacement may be effectively achieved through desensitization.

Hypersensitivity to vitamin B12 is rare in the general population, and its underlying mechanism has not been fully elucidated. Positive results from the basophil histamine release assay and skin testing indicate an IgE-mediated mechanism.19 Notably, cobalamin structurally contains cobalt.3 Systemic contact dermatitis after methylcobalamin administration has been reported, presenting with palmoplantar eczema and a T-cell lymphomatoid reaction, with a positive cobalt patch test.20 In another report, cutaneous reactions, including hand dermatitis, cheilitis, and stomatitis, have also been described in a cobalt-allergic patient receiving oral cobalamin.21 Based on these 2 case reports, it has been suggested that exposure to cobalamin may trigger allergic skin eruptions in individuals with cobalt allergy. Therefore, in addition to IgE-mediated mechanisms, non-IgE-mediated factors may also play a role in vitamin B12 hypersensitivity. HSRs ranging from isolated cutaneous lesions to anaphylaxis and even death have been reported.22 The possibility of hypersensitivity should be considered in patients requiring vitamin B12 replacement.

Prior to DPT or desensitization, patients are classified according to risk. Risk stratification is based on factors such as the severity of the index reaction, the patient's clinical characteristics and the implicated drugs.13 In low-risk patients, DPT may help avoid unnecessary drug restrictions. Performing a skin test prior to DPT is useful for anticipating potential HSRs; however, standardized concentrations for vitamin B12 skin testing have not yet been clearly established. Published reports indicate that IDTs are typically performed at 1/10 and 1/100 concentrations prepared from the 1000 mcg/mL ampoule of either cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin.8,11 Some reports describe starting at 1/1000 or 1/10,000 concentrations with tenfold increments; however, positive results were occasionally observed at 1/100 but were most commonly seen at 1/10 concentrations.5,23 Prick tests were performed using undiluted cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin.7,8,11 Nevertheless, data on the methodology of patch testing remain limited in the literature. In our study all skin test results of patients were negative; the concentrations used for skin testing were consistent with those reported in previous studies and case reports.5,8,11,23 Given the very low rate of reactions observed during challenges in our study, vitamin B12 skin test results can be considered reliable. In certain circumstances, such as cases with clinical features resembling side effects rather than true HSRs, or in mild MPE, the use of skin tests may be considered optional.13

In this study, a total of 40 challenges were performed in 38 patients; 22 had a history of cyanocobalamin-related HSRs (classified as low-risk), while 16 had no prior HSR to vitamin B12 but had multiple drug allergies and were hesitant to receive vitamin B12 therapy (Fig. 1). HSR occurred in only 1 (2.5%) challenge. This challenge was a graded oral DPT with cyanocobalamin, as the patient had a history of antibiotic allergy in addition to vitamin B12 hypersensitivity. In a retrospective multicenter study, 62% of patients (n = 18) with a history of hypersensitivity to vitamin B12 presented with immediate-type reactions.8 Although this rate is slightly higher in our study at 71.4%, indicates a comparable distribution. Notably, in the aforementioned study, 44% of patients (n = 8) had a documented history of anaphylaxis, with the majority of reactions occurring after IM administration. Nevertheless, the only patient who experienced anaphylaxis in our study also developed the reaction following IM administration. This observation aligns with previous findings and raises the possibility that IM formulations of vitamin B12 may pose a greater risk for severe HSRs, including anaphylaxis.5, 6, 7,11,22,23,26, 27, 28 Larger studies are needed to further clarify the relationship between the route of vitamin B12 administration and the severity of HSRs.

Subcutaneous (SC), IM and oral desensitization protocols for vitamin B12 have been described in the literature; nevertheless, standardised approach has not been established and most published protocols in the literature are primarily reported as case studies.5, 6, 7,11,23 In vitamin B12 malabsorption, oral supplementation is likely insufficient and parenteral supplementation is recommended.29 During our IM desensitization protocol, the therapeutic dose of 1000 mcg was reached by Day 7, thereby initiating effective parenteral vitamin B12 replacement therapy. In our desensitization group, HSR was observed in only 1 cycle (resolved with IM pheniramine hydrogen maleate, intravenous methylprednisolone) and this reaction was classified as a B-HR but the patient declined to continue desensitization. 4 cycles were successfully completed without any HSRs and 3 cycles were not completed due to patients' non-attendance during the desensitization protocol. The prolonged duration of our desensitization protocol may be disadvantageous in terms of ensuring patients’ adherence to the treatment schedule. On the other hand, this approach facilitates the safe administration of IM cyanocobalamin replacement in a hospital setting, under the supervision of experienced personnel and with adequate resuscitation equipment, thereby ensuring successful desensitization.

A key strength of our study is the simultaneous evaluation of both diagnostic and therapeutic approaches in patients with a history of vitamin B12 hypersensitivity. And the main limitation is its retrospective design, which restricted the exploration of additional data. In addition, the small number of patients who underwent desensitization limited the generalizability of the findings. Due to the limited number of hydroxocobalamin challenges, separate evaluation of cyanocobalamin and hydroxocobalamin DPTs was not feasible, and the lack of a control group for skin testing represents another limitation. As IM desensitization is a painful procedure, an alternative protocol has been described in which the cycle starts orally with a saline-diluted cyanocobalamin solution and ends with IM administration of undiluted cyanocobalamin.7 Cyanocobalamin ampoules are primarily intended for IM or SC use, and data on their bioavailability via the oral route are limited; however, successful desensitization through that protocol has been demonstrated. This study did not include experience with that protocol; yet, it may be advantageous in terms of patient comfort, as it requires fewer injections. Further studies are needed to explore desensitization methods that are less painful and more tolerable for patients, as well as to elucidate the underlying immunological mechanisms of vitamin B12 HSRs, particularly in patients with negative skin test results.

In conclusion, the present study investigates the use of DPT in low-risk patients with a history of hypersensitivity to vitamin B12, which may help to avoid unnecessary drug restrictions. Additionally, it includes data on vitamin B12 administration through desensitization, further contributing to the existing literature.
