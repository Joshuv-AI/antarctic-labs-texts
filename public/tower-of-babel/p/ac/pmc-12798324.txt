Evaluation and comparison of contrast-to-noise ratio generated by lithium disilicate and zirconia: A phantom study
Authors: Ben Philip Sylvan Bartlett, Hassem Geha, Rujuta Katkar, Taraneh Maghsoodi-Zahedi, John Patrick Hanlon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study evaluated how restorative materials—specifically, lithium disilicate (LD) (IPS e.max; Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Switzerland) and zirconia—affect the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) in cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) imaging.Materials and MethodsCNR was compared between LD and zirconia (NexxZr T; Sagemax Bioceramics, Federal Way, USA; and Zirlux Transitions; Zahn Dental, New York, USA) using 2 CBCT machines—Planmeca ProMax 3D Mid 2015 (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) and PreXion 3D Excelsior (PreXion Inc., San Mateo, USA)—under multiple exposure settings, with and without metal artifact reduction (MAR). Four cylindrical ingots were scanned in a saline phantom under 10 conditions (Planmeca: Low, Normal, High, ±MAR; PreXion: Rapid, Standard, HD, UHD). CNR was calculated in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and analyzed with the paired t-test.ResultsLD showed the highest CNR on Planmeca without MAR and across all PreXion settings. With MAR on Planmeca, zirconia outperformed LD. For zirconia, CNR improved with larger voxel sizes and MAR, whereas for LD it was optimal with smaller voxel sizes and MAR. Optimal Planmeca settings were Low (400 µm)+MAR for zirconia and High (150 µm)+MAR for LD. On PreXion, Rapid (200 µm) favored zirconia, while HD (100 µm) favored LD.ConclusionCBCT settings should be tailored to the restorative material, diagnostic need, and radiation dose. For zirconia, larger voxel sizes with MAR enhance CNR; for LD, smaller voxel sizes with MAR are optimal. These findings support imaging optimization for patients with dental restorations.

Introduction

The field of dental materials has expanded significantly, offering a wide array of options tailored to specific applications. With the growth of digital dentistry and in-office milling, zirconia and lithium disilicate glass-ceramics have become popular choices for full-coverage dental coronal restorations. Practitioners and patients frequently discuss these materials when considering treatment options.1 Although the physical and clinical properties of these materials are well established, research on their radiologic behavior using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) remains limited. Not all dental materials are fabricated in the same way and may vary by manufacturer.

An artifact in CBCT imaging refers to any feature that does not accurately depict the object being evaluated. Artifacts arise inherently from scanner attributes, including geometry, energy levels, and back-projection techniques. The intrinsic properties of metallic objects within the scan’s field of view (FOV) can further intensify these artifacts, producing beam-hardening and streak effects that degrade image quality and contrast in adjacent areas. The contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) serves as an objective metric to assess the impact of these artifacts on image quality. It is defined as the contrast between a tissue of interest and its background. Higher CNR values indicate better image quality with fewer artifacts.2

Zirconia, a dental coronal restorative material, is categorized by yttria (Y2O3) content, which affects its crystalline structure and properties. This study used 3Y, 4Y, and 5Y zirconia (NexxZr T; Sagemax Bioceramics, Federal Way, USA; and Zirlux Transitions; Zahn Dental, New York, USA). 3Y zirconia, with a primarily tetragonal structure, offers high density and opacity and is well suited to posterior restorations. 5Y zirconia, with a primarily cubic structure, provides greater translucency for anterior restorations. 4Y zirconia balances these characteristics, serving as an intermediate option.3

Lithium disilicate (LD; Li2Si2O5) is a metal-free, particle-filled glass-ceramic represented here by IPS e.max Press LT A1 (IPS e.max; Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein, Switzerland). Its crystalline structure, comprising lithium disilicate needles within a glass matrix, provides good mechanical strength and excellent optical properties for aesthetic restorations. Its silica content supports adhesive bonding, making it versatile for monolithic and bilayered crowns.13

Multiple studies have examined the CNR of artifacts produced by various dental materials, including implants, operative restorative materials, and endodontic filling materials, but none have directly addressed full coverage coronal materials.24567 This study primarily aimed to evaluate the CNR of LD and zirconia to understand their radiographic properties, with CBCT machine and parameter comparisons as secondary considerations to optimize image quality for diagnostic objectives.

This study sought to answer the following questions: 1) Excluding MAR, which dental coronal material has the highest CNR? 2) With MAR applied, which material has the highest CNR? 3) Among zirconia types, which shows the highest and lowest CNR? 4) Which CBCT machine provides the highest CNR values? and 5) Which CBCT parameter set is best for each material tested?

Materials and Methods

IRB exemption

The University of Texas Health San Antonio IRB office determined that the proposed activity (STUDY00000684) is not regulated research involving human subjects under Department of Health and Human Services regulations at 45 CFR 46 and FDA regulations at 21 CFR 56, and an e xemption was granted.

Materials and sample preparation

A power analysis performed with G*Power indicated that a sample size of 5 per scan setting was sufficient, based on an expected effect size of 1.75 from a prior study, power of 0.80, and α=0.05. The achieved power was 0.828, slightly exceeding the target and ensuring the study was adequately powered to detect significant differences. The significance level was set at P<0.05, maintaining a 95% confidence level for all analyses.

One pre-processed cylindrical ingot of lithium disilicate glass-ceramic (IPS e.max Press LT A1) and 3 processed cylindrical zirconia ingots (3Y and 5Y from NexxZr T, and 4Y from Zirlux Transitions) were evaluated. The zirconia ingots were fabricated at a private dental laboratory (Art and Technology Dental Studio, San Antonio, USA) to best replicate the LD ingot dimensions (12 mm height, 9.5 mm diameter); the zirconia ingots measured 12 mm in height and 9 mm in diameter.

This study utilized the Planmeca ProMax 3D Mid 2015 (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) and PreXion 3D Excelsior (PreXion Inc., San Mateo, USA) CBCT scanners at the Center for Oral Health Care and Research, San Antonio. The phantom medium was isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl). A small putty matrix platform centered the ingots within the phantom to prevent motion (Fig. 1). To ensure repeatability, the phantom outline was traced on each scanner platform.

Imaging protocol

Two hundred volumes were included in this study: 60 acquired on the Planmeca and 80 on the PreXion. Each of the 60 Planmeca volumes then had the proprietary metal artifact reduction (MAR) algorithm applied, generating an additional 60 MAR volumes. Unlike Planmeca, the PreXion scanner lacks MAR functionality, so no analogous algorithm was used. The acquisition settings selected reflect commonly used clinical defaults (i.e., parameters a practitioner might train staff to use) thereby maintaining the pragmatic relevance and conciseness of the study.

Planmeca scans used a 10 cm×6 cm mandibular FOV (medium adult) with Low (400 µm voxel, 90 kVp, 5.6 mA, 6 s, 1.8 mGy CTDI), Normal (200 µm voxel, 90 kVp, 8 mA, 12 s, 5.2 mGy CTDI), and High (150 µm voxel, 90 kVp, 10 mA, 15 s, 8.2 mGy CTDI) settings.

PreXion scans used a 10 cm×5 cm mandibular FOV (medium adult) with Rapid (200 µm voxel, 110 kVp, 2.4 mA, 2.3 s, 1.3 mGy CTDI), Standard (200 µm voxel, 110 kVp, 2.4 mA, 7.7 s, 4.1 mGy CTDI), High-Definition (HD) (100 µm voxel, 110 kVp, 2.4 mA, 12.8 s, 7.0 mGy CTDI), and Ultra-High-Definition (UHD) (100 µm voxel, 110 kVp, 2.4 mA, 17.9 s, 9.3 mGy CTDI) settings.

Each ingot underwent 5 acquisitions per setting. For Planmeca, CALM (Corrective Adaptive Logic for Motion) was disabled, whereas ANOI (automatic noise and artifact removal), distinct from MAR, remained active. The MAR algorithm (default “mid”) was applied post-scan to preserve the raw CBCT data. No post-scan brightness or contrast adjustments were made. Slice thickness matched voxel size (e.g., 0.400 mm for a 400 µm voxel). For PreXion, the “use original pitch” option was selected to reflect the actual voxel dimension.

Data analysis

All 200 volumes were exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. For CNR assessment, axial slices were selected at the mid-height of each ingot and analyzed in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA).

An ImageJ macro generated 2 boxes adjacent to the ingot to sample artifact-affected regions. The boxes were placed superior and lateral to the ingot, each positioned one-eighth of its width from the ingot edge. This design accounted for the sporadic nature of streaking and beam-hardening artifacts; sampling 2 areas improved reliability (Fig. 2).

Additionally, a macro created a distant box at the image margin to serve as a control region, where artifact influence was assumed minimal. Each control box was placed 2.75 times its width from the ingot edge. All boxes were identical in size, and ImageJ histograms provided mean gray values and standard deviations for each region (Fig. 2).

The mean gray values and standard deviations from the 2 affected regions were averaged before calculation. The CNR was calculated using the formula: CNR=MeanAffected-MeanControlS.D.Affected2-S.D.Control2 In the formula, the mean represents the average gray value of the sampled area, and the standard deviation reflects local gray-value variability.

Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS v23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). After confirming normality with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, the paired t-test was used to evaluate differences between the affected and control regions.

Results

The CNR for the 4 coronal materials on 2 machines was assessed under 10 parameter combinations. MAR significantly increased CNR, particularly in the Low+MAR setting for zirconia. Without MAR, 3Y zirconia consistently exhibited the highest CNR among the zirconia variants across both machines (Fig. 3).

LD achieved the highest CNR with Planmeca’s High setting with MAR and with PreXion’s HD setting, whereas the zirconia materials performed best with Planmeca’s Low+MAR and PreXion’s Rapid settings (Tables 1 and 2). With MAR, 5Y zirconia had the highest CNR on Planmeca. Without MAR, PreXion produced higher CNR than Planmeca; however, Planmeca with MAR surpassed PreXion’s values.

Table 1 presents Planmeca results without MAR, showing LD with the highest CNR, followed by 3Y zirconia across all settings (P<0.05). CNR increased with higher settings for LD and, to a lesser extent, for 3Y zirconia, with minimal increases for 4Y and 5Y. Significant differences (P<0.05) were observed between LD and zirconia, and among zirconia materials in the Normal and High settings. With MAR, 5Y zirconia achieved the highest CNR across all settings, followed by 4Y, except in the High setting where 3Y outperformed 4Y (P<0.05). Low+MAR yielded the highest CNR for zirconia. LD improved with MAR but ranked lower overall. Significant differences (P<0.05) were observed between zirconia and LD in the Low setting and, for 5Y, among zirconia in the High setting.

Table 2 displays PreXion outcomes, with LD achieving the highest CNR across all settings, followed by 3Y zirconia (P<0.05). LD peaked at the HD setting, whereas 3Y peaked with the Rapid setting. Significant differences (P<0.05) were observed between LD and zirconia and, for 3Y, among zirconia across all settings; 4Y and 5Y showed similar CNR values.

Figures 4 and 5 illustrate the ingot images under the various Planmeca and PreXion conditions, respectively. In Figure 4, each pair displays the contrast between the worst (left) and best (right) parameters for LD, 3Y, 4Y, and 5Y; Figure 5 shows the analogous PreXion results. Subjectively, some images may appear preferable to another even when labeled “best” or “worst,” underscoring the need for quantitative metrics such as CNR to assess image quality objectively.

Discussion

This study evaluated the CNR of lithium disilicate and zirconia (3Y, 4Y, 5Y) using 2 CBCT systems. Without MAR, LD produced the highest CNR overall, and 3Y zirconia led among zirconia types. MAR markedly improved zirconia CNR, especially for 5Y. Although PreXion (without MAR) outperformed Planmeca across materials, Planmeca with MAR yielded higher CNR than PreXion, particularly for zirconia. On Planmeca, low resolution (400 µm) with MAR produced the best zirconia CNR, while the same low setting without MAR produced the lowest values, underscoring the importance of MAR for artifact-prone materials.

Beyond full-coverage restorative materials, prior studies of CBCT imaging across dental materials suggest a trend favoring silicate-based materials over metallic alternatives. Bayrak et al.4 reported a silicate-containing compomer with higher CNR than amalgam, aligning with the present study’s finding of higher LD CNR versus zirconia. Demirturk Kocasarac et al.5 similarly reported higher CNR for silicate-based Biodentine. Studies of zirconia implants indicate that zirconia’s high density increases beam hardening and streak artifacts—particularly without MAR—supporting the observation of lower zirconia CNR without MAR.267 In contrast, LD, with a lower atomic number and density, generated fewer artifacts in the present data. Doriguêtto et al.8 indirectly support these findings by showing that metallic components in crowns (analogous to zirconia here) introduce artifacts that affect detection of gaps adjacent to full-coverage restorations.

CBCT parameters also modulate CNR. Mancini et al.9 found that higher mAs reduced artifacts for zirconia and titanium implants, and that MAR particularly benefited zirconia; Planmeca settings in this study (5.6, 8, 10 mA) showed increasing CNR for both LD and zirconia. Pauwels et al.10 observed that kVp exerts a greater influence on CNR than mA; PreXion’s higher kVp (110 kVp) compared with Planmeca (90 kVp) likely contributed to the higher non-MAR CNR observed for LD and zirconia.

Yang11 reported that smaller FOVs (4 cm) yield higher CNR than larger ones (13 cm). Although the FOVs were fixed in this study (10 cm×5–6 cm), limiting direct comparison, the observation is consistent with field-size effects. Katkar et al.12 noted that thicker export slice thickness increases CNR by lowering noise; in this study, slice thickness equaled voxel size, which may have limited CNR gains under certain parameters.

Applying MAR in clinical contexts must be guided by the diagnostic question. Bechara et al.13 found that MAR improved image appearance but reduced accuracy for detecting root fractures in endodontically treated teeth, with higher accuracy without MAR. Parrone et al.14 reported that Planmeca’s optimization filter did not improve fracture detection. Abu El-Ela et al.15 showed that LD permitted proximal caries detection comparable to non-restored teeth, unlike zirconia and amalgam. Cebe et al.16 found that amalgam generated substantial artifacts; MAR increased specificity but reduced sensitivity for caries detection. Hakim et al.17 confirmed MAR’s benefit for maxillofacial metal implants, and Demirturk Kocasarac et al.5 recommended low-resolution MAR for highly artifact-producing materials such as zirconia.

In the present data, 3Y zirconia—predominantly tetragonal with a dense, uniform lattice—showed the highest non-MAR CNR among zirconia types, likely due to more uniform X-ray interactions and fewer streaks. MAR was most effective for 5Y zirconia’s more cubic lattice, which produces greater artifact burden; this was reflected in the higher MAR-enabled CNR. Buchanan et al.18 reported that zirconia crowns’ high attenuation can yield nondiagnostic, high-contrast radiographs, consistent with the observations in the present study.

This study has limitations, including its in vitro design, which did not fully replicate clinical conditions (e.g., soft-tissue scatter, adjacent restorations, and clinical artifact patterns), and inter-operator variability was not assessed; although a single operator improved reproducibility, it limits interoperability. MAR evaluation was limited to the Planmeca system, which may restrict generalizability of MAR-related findings; different MAR implementations could yield different outcomes. These constraints point to the need for comparative, multi-system MAR studies.

Future research could apply the optimal parameters identified here to caries detection, using ingot thicknesses to simulate implant crowns. Notably, the LD ingot in this study was pre-processed; the literature lacks direct comparisons of pre- versus post-processed LD radiologic properties. Variability among manufacturers should also be considered. Retrospective analyses of existing CBCT volumes–incorporating known scanner specifications, acquisition parameters, and restorative materials–could further elucidate CNR behavior. Manufacturers may also refine MAR algorithms to balance artifact suppression with preservation of diagnostic detail, potentially developing material-specific filters or recommending settings tailored to the restorative material.

In conclusion, considering the in vitro CNR data alongside diagnostic objectives and dose, clinicians should employ larger voxel sizes with MAR when imaging patients with zirconia-based restorations and smaller voxel sizes with MAR for those with lithium disilicate restorations.
