Real-life implementation of prenatal cell-free DNA screening with in vitro fetal enrichment virtually eliminates the need for redraws and improves performance: A cohort study
Authors: Sylvie Giroux, Seyedeh Saideh Daryabari, André Caron, Julie Jeukens, Yves Giguère, Jean-Claude Forest, François Audibert, Emmanuel Bujold, Sylvie Langlois, François Rousseau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeInsufficient fetal fraction is a significant cause of prenatal cell-free DNA (cfDNA) screening failure, affecting 2% to 5% of samples, particularly among women with high body mass index (BMI). We evaluated the clinical impacts of in vitro fetal enrichment in a public prenatal cfDNA screening laboratory, hypothesizing that it would lower failure rates.MethodsThis cohort study analyzed 8551 consecutive samples from pregnant women at an ISO15189-accredited prenatal cfDNA screening laboratory. We compared 4893 samples tested before and 3651 samples after implementing fetal enrichment. Samples were collected from January 2021 to October 2023 from high-risk (4809) pregnant women enrolled in the public Quebec Prenatal Screening Program (including 7 lost to follow-up and who were excluded from the analysis) and low-risk (3550) pregnancies from the Pegasus-2 project and divided into 4 groups. A total of 192 low-risk twin pregnancies were also included.ResultsFetal enrichment doubled fetal fraction and reduced the assay failure rate by more than 10-fold (from 1.6% to 0.14%, P < .0001), enabling all women to receive a risk estimate at their first blood draw, even with a high BMI. It also improved clinical performance metrics.ConclusionPrenatal cfDNA screening with in vitro fetal enrichment enhances accessibility and reliability of prenatal screening, nearly eliminating test failures and providing timely results for all samples, regardless of maternal BMI.

Introduction

Low fetal fraction (FF) is a major cause of prenatal cfDNA screening test failure, which can represent 2% to 5% of all samples.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 This limits clinical accessibility to prenatal screening. The slow increase in FF with increasing gestational age (GA), even if it may reach the required FF later during pregnancy, significantly delays its accessibility. First trimester high maternal body mass index (BMI) is the most common reason for an insufficient fetal fraction.6, 7, 8 Given that the required FF is doubled for twin pregnancies, up to 12% will result in a test failure due to insufficient FF.9

Increasing FF in a sample may decrease the rate of cfDNA screening failure and minimize the need for repeat testing. Reduction of blood redraws would improve screening accessibility overall. Furthermore, because FF is a major driver of prenatal cfDNA screening clinical performance,4 fetal enrichment may also show the extra benefit of improving other clinical performance metrics.

Recently, it has been shown that in vitro size selection of shorter fragments using gel electrophoresis enabled a significant enrichment of cfDNA reads originating from the placenta in prenatal cfDNA screening samples.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 In vitro size selection performed on cfDNA libraries before sequencing has advantages over in silico size selection15 performed after sequencing using computerized estimations of the read length. As opposed to in silico size selection, in vitro size selection removes larger cfDNA fragments from maternal origin before sequencing and thus strongly increases the absolute number of reads of fetal origin. Systematic fetal enrichment by in vitro size selection was introduced in our clinical prenatal cfDNA screening procedure in July 2022.

To evaluate the clinical impact of fetal enrichment, we analyzed prenatal cfDNA screening results from a cohort of consecutive samples from pregnant women collected before and after clinical implementation of systematic in vitro fetal enrichment. Our hypothesis was that in vitro fetal enrichment improves the prenatal cfDNA screening failure rate (and reduce redraws), including among pregnant women with increased BMI.

Materials and Methods

Study design and population

The studied cohort comprised 8551 consecutive prenatal cfDNA screening samples of pregnant women encompassing 18 months before (from January 2021 to June 2022) and 16 months after (from July 2022 to November 2023) clinical implementation of fetal enrichment (Supplemental Figure 1). These included 5001 (4809 high-risk pregnancies and 192 low-risk twin pregnancies), which voluntarily participated in Public Screening (Quebec Prenatal Screening Program) and consented to anonymized research, as well as 3550 low-risk pregnancies recruited for first-tier prenatal cfDNA screening as part of the Pegasus-2 project (see below) during the same period of time. All pregnant women tested in the same clinical prenatal cfDNA screening laboratory (CHU de Québec-Université Laval, Québec city, Canada) between January 2021 and October 2023 were eligible for this study.

Women participating in the Public Screening who underwent prenatal cfDNA screening were classified as high-risk based on various criteria (Table 1). Of these 5001 participants, 2426 were sampled for prenatal cfDNA screening between January 2021 and July 12th 2022 and underwent screening before the implementation of fetal enrichment (Table 2). After this date, between July 2022 and October 2023, 2383 high-risk samples underwent fetal enrichment (see below).13 In addition, starting in 2023, low-risk twin pregnancies became eligible for first-tier prenatal cfDNA screening in our jurisdiction’s Public Screening, and 192 were included in this study. All twin cases were tested without knowing zygosity. Cohort characteristics

The 7 participants that were lost to follow-up (3 in the first column and 4 in the second column) are not included in this table because they were not part of the outcome analyses.

The data set also included 3550 low-risk pregnant women who received first-tier prenatal cfDNA screening as part of the Pegasus-2 research project and recruited either in Québec Province or British Columbia which is 4000 km away from our clinical prenatal cfDNA screening laboratory. These women had a low (baseline) risk for trisomy, were 19 years and older, and had a GA between 10 weeks and 13 weeks and 6 days with a singleton pregnancy at the time of recruitment and blood sampling. A total of 2470 research samples were in the time period before implementation of fetal enrichment (January 2021 and July 12th 2022), and 1080 samples were obtained between July 2022 and March 2023 (end of recruitment) and therefore underwent fetal enrichment.

Participants were divided in 4 groups for further analysis: high risk with and without fetal enrichment and low risk with and without fetal enrichment. (Table 2, Supplemental Figure 1).

The clinical data collected for each participant were provided in the prenatal cfDNA screening requisition form and part of the laboratory report. It included the specific reason for screening, the a priori risk of aneuploidy category (high risk or low risk), GA at blood sampling, date of sampling, and maternal BMI. Prenatal cfDNA screening data/outcomes included geographical site of blood sampling, blood collection tube used, method with or without fetal enrichment, redraw if needed, fetal fraction estimate, low or high risk or aneuploidy (trisomy 13, 18, or 21), failure for other reason than low FF, laboratory turnaround time (calculated with date of laboratory report), redraw received, and its result). All of the above-mentioned data were anonymized before analysis.

In terms of clinical follow-up, pregnant couples with screen-positive cfDNA result were systematically offered invasive diagnostic testing by fetal karyotype (or equivalent). QF-PCR or microarray obtained after invasive testing were considered equivalent to the fetal karyotype with respect to their capacity to confirm or not the presence of a complete trisomy 13, 18, or 21 in the fetus. Systematic clinical follow-up of screen-negative pregnancies was only possible for the Research participants as per the research project protocol, which included a newborn clinical examination at birth. For other screen-negative pregnancies that were part of the provincial public screening program, over the time of this study, only 2 false-negative cfDNA results were reported to the laboratory by health care professionals involved in newborn care and clinical follow-up. When available, pregnancy follow-up data and results of the diagnostic invasive testing were matched with prenatal cfDNA screening data by the clinical laboratory before anonymization and data analysis.

Sample size and addressing potential sources of biases

The study was powered to detect a difference of 0.75% in the redraw rate (for example, 2% vs 1.25%) between the samples without fetal enrichment and those with fetal enrichment, at 80% power with alpha = 0.05. This led to a total needed sample size of 7452 (distributed equally between the presence or absence of fetal enrichment).

To minimize potential biases, all samples received for prenatal cfDNA screening during the time period were included, no changes in the testing protocol occurred during this time period with the exception of the introduction of fetal enrichment approximately half-way into the study time period. Redraws received during the study period were always analyzed using the same cfDNA assay protocol (with or without fetal enrichment) as the initial cfDNA sample. All clinical and laboratory data were collected in the same manner for the duration of the study. The introduction of twin pregnancies toward the end of the study period had the potential to introduce a bias against prenatal cfDNA screening with fetal enrichment because the FF minimal threshold for twin pregnancies was 8% (double that of singleton pregnancies); hence, the likelihood of a redraw was increased potentially influencing the number of redraws in the group with fetal enrichment. We considered it conservative to leave twin pregnancies in the study.

Prenatal cfDNA screening method

Maternal blood sampling for prenatal cfDNA screening was performed either in Streck tubes (catalog number 218997; Streck, La Vista, NE; n = 5705) or K2EDTA (dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) gel blood tube for molecular diagnostics (catalog number GR-455040; Greiner, Kremsmünster, Austria; n = 2846). Maternal plasma was prepared according to published procedures that produce equivalent cfDNA-grade maternal plasma.13 All testing was carried out in an ISO15189-accredited (Standards Council of Canada) public clinical prenatal cfDNA screening laboratory (Québec City, Canada) using a validated in-house genomic massively parallel shotgun sequencing (MPSS) assay. All samples were analyzed using the same prenatal cfDNA screening protocol, except for the fetal enrichment by in vitro size selection using the Lightbench instrument (Yourgene Health) as per a published protocol13 (see also Supplemental Information). Prenatal cfDNA screening was reported as high risk for fetal trisomy when the z-score of a chromosomal ratio of interest was 3.0 or above. Low risk of aneuploidy was reported with a minimal threshold of 4% FF to minimize false-negative screens. When multiple chromosomal imbalances were observed in cfDNA data, no redraw was proposed and, for the purpose of this study, these few cases (n = 7) were not considered.

Statistical methods

In multivariate analysis quantitative variables (FF, GA, and BMI) were treated as continuous variables. In addition, tubes used (EDTA or Streck) and the high- or low-risk groups were categorical variables to evaluate the variation of FF according to the use of enrichment or not.

Statistical tests were performed using XLStat16 or VassarStats Website for Statistical Computation (vassarstats.net). Given our hypothesis that fetal enrichment improves prenatal cfDNA screening performance compared with our prior procedure, we used 1-sided statistics. Significant alpha error was set at ≤0.05. Specific statistical tests used are described along with the results reported. Missing data were dealt with by restricting analyses to individuals with complete data on all variables needed for a particular analysis. Loss to follow-up occurrences were limited to screen-positive pregnancies for which we could not obtain data on invasive diagnostic cytogenetic testing, and these have been excluded from our calculations relative to false-positive and true-positive rates, as well as for receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Reporting of this study is compliant with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology guidelines.17

Results

Table 1 and 2 provide a summary of the characteristics of study participants. Participants were grouped as either 1st tier (low-risk) or high-risk (second tier) cfDNA screening and each group was divided according to whether a fetal enrichment step was carried out. A small statistically significant difference in GA was observed in the 1st tier group, with those with fetal enrichment being sampled, on average, 1 day earlier. There was also a small significant difference between the 2 groups of high-risk pregnancies: those with fetal enrichment were about 2 days more advanced.

Between the 2 groups without and with fetal enrichment (within both first tier or high risk) there was no significant difference in the proportion of women with a BMI < 30 kg/m2 and those in the obese ranges. However, BMI was missing for 27% in the high-risk group without enrichment and 25% of high-risk group with fetal enrichment.

Impact of fetal enrichment on FF according to maternal BMI

Given that earlier GA at blood sampling is associated with lower FF estimates, we evaluated the number of failed prenatal cfDNA screening assays according to GA before and after implementation of fetal enrichment. Before implementation of fetal enrichment, the failure rate was 2.4% for participants tested before 13 weeks of GA (34/1411) and 1.2% (42/3484) for participants tested at 13 weeks or more of GA. With fetal enrichment, the failure rate dropped to 0.1% (1/997) and 0.15% (4/2657) for the same ranges of GA, respectively. Thus in vitro fetal enrichment is likely to ensure a screening result for virtually every sample taken at 10 weeks or more of GA.

Although different causes of assay failure of a prenatal cfDNA screening assay have been reported in the literature (2,3), a blood redraw a few weeks later will be suggested by the cfDNA laboratory only for cases of insufficient FF. In this study, all our cfDNA screening reports asking for a redraw were, indeed, due to insufficient FF.

Screening performance for trisomy 13, 18, or 21

The positive predictive value (PPV) and the ROC curve analysis was studied using diagnostic genetic testing for fetal aneuploidy available for part of the positive screens.

We observed 2 outliers among the euploid samples after fetal enrichment, which could be due to confined placental mosaicism18 or some maternal chromosomal copy-number variant.19 However, this could not be confirmed because the placenta could not be analyzed, and no cytogenetic testing was done on the participants.

In terms of PPV for the combined fetal aneuploidies of interest (13, 18, or 21), we did not observe a statistically significant difference between prenatal cfDNA screening with and without fetal enrichment (PPV without enrichment 79% [65/82]; PPV with enrichment 78% [50/64] including twins; z = 0.168; P value [1-sided] = 0.433). This may be due to the observed false-positive rates (in all samples tested), which are close to 0.5% when using a cutoff z-score of 3.0, which generates, by definition, 0.5% false-positive results among euploid pregnancies.

Interestingly, using a ROC curve analysis, the AUC (area under the curve) calculated among all prenatal cfDNA screening results with a z-score of 3.0 and above revealed an AUC of 0.959 (SE 0.019) without fetal enrichment compared with an AUC of 0.996 (SE 0.007) with fetal enrichment (difference between AUCs: 0.036; z = 1.70; 1-tailed P value = .044). This further supports the likely improved discrimination, after enrichment, between true fetal aneuploid and euploid pregnancies.

We computed the distribution of false positives (FP) and true positives (TP), without and with enrichment according to the cfDNA result (Supplemental Table 1). Among all 31 FP (17 without enrichment and 14 with enrichment), we observed an overall excess of T18 and T13 compared with the 113 TP (χ2 2df = 12.4; P = .002). This phenomenon is consistent with the known lower PPV for T18 and T13. We observed no statistically significant difference in the distribution of FP T21, T18, and T13 conditional on fetal enrichment or not (Fisher’s Exact test (2-tailed) P = .89). The same is true within the distribution of TP conditional on fetal enrichment or not (Fisher’s Exact test [2-tailed] P = .70). These findings suggest that fetal enrichment does not affect the proportion of each aneuploidy reported, either within FPs or within TPs. Although increased FF is expected to reduce the false-negative rate by improving sensitivity, fetal enrichment is not expected to reduce the false-positive rate given that confined placental mosaicism will still occur and that some euploid pregnancies will always yield a z-score ≥ 3.0 because the normal distribution, by definition, stretches beyond this boundary.

Reliable data were available to evaluate the false-negative rate for those pregnant women with systematic clinical follow-up (ie, newborn exam) after a negative cfDNA screen (first-tier part of the research project). A total 2470 (without enrichment) and 1080 (with enrichment) first-tier pregnancy samples with such follow-up data were available, and no false-negative case was identified. This does not provide enough power to detect a difference in the false-negative rate (FNR) nor to compare the cfDNA test sensitivity with and without fetal enrichment. However, we computed the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval (95CI) of the FNR. Of the 2470 samples without enrichment, 2417 negative screens were reported with no false negative (FNR higher 95CI limit with continuity correction ≤ 0.20%). Out of 1080 samples with enrichment, 1066 negative screens were reported with no false negative identified. (FNR higher 95CI limit [with continuity correction] ≤ 0.45%). Two false-negative cfDNA results were observed in the high-risk samples before the implementation of fetal enrichment (Table 3). These were resolved after reanalysis of the stored samples using fetal enrichment, suggesting improved clinical sensitivity.

Cost-effectiveness

A preliminary CE analysis focused on the relative increase in the total laboratory costs of using fetal enrichment for each cfDNA screening assay (overall increased laboratory cost of initial cfDNA assay = 1.08%) compared with the observed consequential reduction of the number of redraws (overall reduction of redraws to test by the laboratory = 1.46%). Thus, the decreased number of extra costs of cfDNA assays due to redraws is larger than the increase in assay costs for enrichment to the lab. We estimate that these numbers are conservative because they do include the following economic advantages of enrichment: (1) the observed 40% reduction (data not shown) in laboratory costs of the extra work invested in the cases with insufficient FF before issuing a cfDNA report, (2) the possibility to increase the number of enriched samples per sequencing pool, and (3) the direct costs of each initial redraw report to the health care system for the follow-up call and medical visit of pregnant women after an inconclusive cfDNA report. Our figures also do not take into account indirect costs of redraws, such as loss of time and pay for women needing a medical visit after an inconclusive cfDNA result, including a cfDNA blood redraw, nor does it factor in the impact of the delay in the final screening result and, if needed, delay in confirmatory testing. Thus, in a public health perspective, in the context of this study, fetal enrichment appears to be more cost-effective than the previous cfDNA screening procedure without enrichment.

Laboratory turnaround time

We evaluated laboratory turnaround times (TAT) before and after implementation of fetal enrichment. Although the median TAT was not different (4 days) with or without fetal enrichment, the mean TAT went from 4.6 to 3.7 days after implementation of fetal enrichment. This suggests a reduction in the number of samples with a long TAT due to laboratory sample reprocessing.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this real-world clinical setting, several advantages were readily observed after the clinical implementation of the fetal enrichment method. First, using fetal enrichment, 99.86% (95IC 99.68%-99.94%) of all samples received at the laboratory and eligible for prenatal cfDNA screening testing were issued a result at first blood draw, leaving only 0.14% of pregnant women needing a redraw as opposed to 1.6% redraws before implementation of fetal enrichment. This corresponds to a more than 10-fold reduction in test failures. Our results also suggest that using fetal enrichment may mitigate the known limitations of cfDNA screening due to a low FF in cases of high maternal BMI, twin pregnancies, or early GA. Thus, access to prenatal cfDNA screening for all pregnant women may be significantly improved when using in vitro fetal enrichment.

Our analyses also showed that fetal enrichment significantly increases the z-scores of cases of fetal aneuploidy (between 7 and more than 50), while leaving most of the false-positive results in the lower range of chromosomal ratio z-scores (between 3 and 6) with almost no overlap. This improved discrimination between aneuploid and euploid pregnancies was also substantiated by our ROC curve analysis of screen-positive results with an invasive cytogenetic diagnostic test.

The current performance of prenatal cfDNA screening together with the low incidence of aneuploid cases makes it difficult to have sufficient power to detect an improvement in screening performances per se, even with a sample size of 8551 (including 113 confirmed aneuploid pregnancies). With fetal enrichment, there were no observed false negatives in either group (high or low risk), while both exhibited about the same number of FP as the same threshold to call a positive screen (z-score > 3) was used. These findings agree with a smaller study published in 2021, which included 1264 patients, 67 positive controls, and a pool of negative controls and concluded to an increase in performance by the selection of shorter fragments by gel electrophoresis.10 Given the increased separation observed between z-scores from euploid and aneuploid samples after implementation of fetal enrichment, it would seem appealing to increase the z-score high-risk threshold to minimize the false positives without compromising sensitivity. However, we believe that larger number of aneuploid samples need to be studied to provide sufficient statistical power to compare false-negative rates against cfDNA analysis without enrichment before implementing such a change.

Of note, the clinical laboratory participates in the College of American Pathologists cfDNA prenatal screening proficiency program and has always succeeded in providing the expected cfDNA screening results. After implementing fetal enrichment, compared with the distribution of other FF estimates of College of American Pathologists EQA participant laboratories, our FF estimates of received samples scored between +3.5 SDI and +6SDI, ie, significantly higher than other clinical laboratories’ estimates. This suggests that few prenatal cfDNA screening laboratories have yet introduced fetal enrichment into their assay procedure.

Study strengths and limitations

Among the strengths of this study are its design as a cohort study, including a large number of prenatal cfDNA screening samples before and after clinical implementation of systematic in vitro fetal enrichment. The study sample size provided enough power to detect differences in test failure rates and also to assess the overall test performance for the detection of the major fetal aneuploidies. The prospective nature of the recruitment included all incoming samples for cfDNA screening. Also, the present cohort included first-tier screening (singleton and twin pregnancies), as well as second-tier screening. Our study design aimed at minimizing possible biases by using the same prenatal cfDNA screening protocol except for the fetal enrichment procedure for all samples received. Finally, we present a summary cost-effectiveness analysis showing the impact of fetal enrichment in a public health perspective. This study has some limitations. First, the fact that, in this study, part of the high-risk group comprised second-tier screening, which is associated with sampling at a later GA could be perceived as a limitation of the study. However, given that the failure rate without enrichment is similar in both the high-risk and 1st tier groups (which have a 12 day difference in median GA), we believe that this phenomenon does not weaken our conclusions. Second, because we are using a highly validated laboratory-developed (in-house) MPSS prenatal cfDNA screening assay for which we have total control of the detailed laboratory and interpretation processes, our results may not be readily applicable to other clinical prenatal cfDNA screening laboratories. However, our general procedure is very similar to other MPSS prenatal cfDNA screening assays, and the Illumina sequencing platform is widely used, suggesting that our results may be generalizable to other similar MPSS platforms. Third, although we could reliably estimate PPV using a total of 144 positive screens with a cytogenetic diagnostic test, we chose not to provide these estimates for individual aneuploidies that are screened in our setting (T13, T18, and T21) because pregnancies with T18 and T13 have a lower incidence than T21. Additionally, the number of negative screens with a systematic clinical follow-up was limited to 3670 average-risk pregnancies divided between the 2 groups (with and without enrichment). Thus, the very low expected incidence of fetal aneuploidy in the low-risk group did not have the statistical power to provide reliable estimates and comparison of the false-negative rates.

Another limitation of this study is that BMI or follow-up data (especially results of invasive cytogenetic testing) were not available for some participants included in the data set. However, as shown by our results, the sample size was large enough to provide statistical power to answer our research questions.

In terms of applicability of our findings, although systematic fetal enrichment of all incoming cfDNA screening samples allows one to maintain a single and efficient cfDNA testing pipeline, a clinical laboratory could decide to use fetal enrichment in a second step only for samples with an insufficient FF at the first sequencing run. Existing libraries of such samples could be pooled, size selected for fetal enrichment and sequenced in a separate sequencing run, then analyzed with the fetal enrichment specific bioinformatic pipeline. This would, however, generate a certain amount of extra sample management and workload, as well as the needed management (and ongoing validation) of 2 different cfDNA testing pipelines, with the associated risk of error. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses may need to be replicated using specific costs of other health care settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in this clinical implementation study in a real-world setting, FF enrichment using in vitro size selection considerably reduced the number of failed prenatal cfDNA screening assays and redraws by providing sufficient FF for a reliable result for virtually every sample received of ≥10 weeks of GA regardless of maternal BMI. This achievement allows all pregnant women to have access to a reliable result, which is especially important given the observed increase in the prevalence of obesity. Moreover, fetal enrichment also lowers the failure rate when performed for twin pregnancies and may provide a means, yet to be demonstrated, to improve the detection of other fetal genetic abnormalities through the analysis of cfDNA. Furthermore, our results suggest an improved discrimination between euploid and aneuploid pregnancies through a much-reduced overlap between prenatal cfDNA screening z-scores of aneuploid vs euploid pregnancies, consistent with an improved screening performance. This improvement came with a minimal cost and minimal delay to yield a cfDNA screening result. Parents’ anxiety and burden of test failures and redraws appear to be much reduced by using enrichment techniques, which provides patients with more accurate and timely screening results.
