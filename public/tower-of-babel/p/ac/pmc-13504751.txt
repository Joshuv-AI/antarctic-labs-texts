Changes in gingival cancer: burden of hospitalization and epidemiological evolution from 2001 to 2023 in Spain. a novel approach using hospital activity registry data
Authors: Mona Zedan, Carmen López-Carriches, Luis A. Moreno-López
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGingival squamous cell carcinoma represents approximately 10% of oral cavity carcinomas. The Spanish Hospital Activity Registry (RAE) covers > 90% of the Spanish population and provides updated epidemiological data. This study aims to describe the epidemiological evolution of gingival cancer in Spain from 2001 to 2023 by analysing sex distribution, age groups, anatomical location (maxilla vs. mandible) and hospitalization costs.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study using RAE data from January 1, 2001, to December 31, 2023. We selected hospital admissions with primary or secondary diagnoses of gingival malignancy (ICD-9 code 143 and ICD-10 code C03), treatment, or regional spread related to the tumour. We selected only the first admission for each patient during the study period. Variables analysed: sex, age, location (maxilla/mandible), and economic cost adjusted to the 2023 Consumer Price Index.ResultsA total of 7,327 admissions were identified, corresponding to 5,623 patients. The number of cases increased from 198 in 2001 to 315 in 2023 (+ 59.1%). The male: female ratio shifted from 1.3:1 (2001) to 0.8:1 (2023), with a female predominance consolidated after 2015. The mean age at diagnosis was greater in women (71.1 years) than in men (65.3 years), p < 0.001. When the location was specified, 63% were mandibular, and 37% were maxillary. The mean cost per admission rose from €7,007 to €13,114. The total hospital cost increased from €1.67 million (2001) to €4.69 million (2023), representing a 180% increase.ConclusionsRAE is a valuable data source for monitoring cancer trends in Spain. Since 2015, there has been a sustained shift toward female predominance in the incidence of gingival cancer, with a greater diagnostic age in women. The economic burden of gingival cancer hospitalization has increased substantially, with both higher case numbers and increased costs per patient.

Background

Epidemiological studies of oral cancer are essential for understanding the evolution of a disease, which is largely influenced by population-level changes in risk factors. Hospital and population-based cancer registries constitute the most important source of epidemiological data for this pathology. These registries report their data to higher administrative levels, REDECAN (Spanish cancer registry network) in Spain, and ultimately to the WHO Global Cancer Observatory, which publishes the received data [1].

According to WHO data, the incidence of oropharyngeal cancer in Spain has decreased in men and increased in women since 2000 [2]. The published data are provided by REDECAN. This organization provides epidemiological data describing population patterns with a homogeneous methodological framework but only from population-based registries operating in the country. In Spain, these registries cover information from approximately 27% of the population, according to 2021 coverage data [3]. These populations do not include Spain’s major metropolitan areas, where 35% of the population resides [4].

Oral cancer constitutes a significant global public health problem. According to GLOBOCAN, 389,846 new cases of oral cancer were diagnosed worldwide in 2022, with an incidence of 4 cases per 100,000 inhabitants [1, 2]. Regarding incidence in the European Union, the average incidence is 4.1 per 100,000. In Spain, according to 2022 data, 4,003 cases of lip and oral cancer have been diagnosed, with an incidence of 3.6 per 100,000 [1]. In 2022, oral cancer caused 188,438 deaths worldwide, including 1,211 in Spain, reflecting its significant clinical, healthcare, and social impact [1, 2]. Among intraoral locations, gingival tumours represent approximately 10% of oral cavity carcinomas in Spain [5], although in other countries, they may account more than 20% of intraoral cancers [6].

Owing to the low coverage of the total Spanish population by REDECAN (less than 27%), alternative data sources have been proposed for the epidemiological study of cancer in Spain, particularly the Spanish Hospital Activity Registry (RAE) [7–10]. This registry collects data from more than 90% of Spanish hospitals, recording each hospital admission with more than 60 variables, including the diagnosis motivating admission. Although not a cancer registry per se, it has been used for epidemiological studies and hospital cost analyses of cancer treatment because of its multiple advantages. Primarily, it covers over 90% of the population from all regions of the country and provides much more updated data than do cancer registries. Additionally, healthcare activity registries record the cost of healthcare, enabling the study of the economic burden of specific pathologies such as gingival cancer. However, as a nonspecialized registry, patients may have multiple admissions for the studied disease and other causes, requiring case selection [8]. Studies comparing the capacity of the RAE to detect incident cancer cases have reported a sensitivity ranging from 80 to 85% and a specificity above 95%, depending on the method used and the tumour location, with high coding concordance (kappa index of 0.88) [7–9]. Therefore, its systematic exploitation is valuable for monitoring cancer epidemiology because of its availability without the delays inherent to registries [9]. Carazo-Casas et al. reported that oral cavity cancer in Spain represents a significant hospital economic burden, with an estimated total cost of approximately €14.8 million annually on average from 2009 to 2019 [10].

The distribution of risk factors for oral cancer in general and gingival cancer in particular has changed over the last 20 years. In Spain, smoking has decreased much more markedly in men than in women, with a later decline in females [11]. In parallel, the incidence of oral cancer has been reported to decrease in men but increase in women with increasing age of disease presentation by sex, which is consistent with tobacco’s causal contribution to oral cancer [12]. These epidemiological changes may not be uniform across all intraoral locations, which are subject to different risk factors. Our group previously reported that the male: female ratio in patients with gingival cancer differs from that in patients with oral cancer overall. In fact, gingival cancer, unlike in other locations, has been more common in women than in men for years [13].

Our objective was to describe the epidemiological evolution of gingival cancer in Spain from 2001 to 2023, analysing the distribution by sex and age groups, anatomical presentation (maxilla vs. mandible), and hospitalization costs.

Methods

Data source and study design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data obtained from the Spanish Hospital Activity Registry (RAE). This service of the Spanish Ministry of Health’s Health Information System records hospital care data throughout the country. Until 2016, the database was called the Minimum Basic Data Set (CMBD), and since then, it has been designated the Specialized Care Registry (RAE). Access to its data is publicly available upon request, and the ID of the patient is anonymized.

All data were fully anonymised at the source and contained no direct or indirect personal identifiers as described in RD 69/2015. As the data has been anonymized, the study is exempt from review by a research ethics committee according to Spanish legislation 14/2007 on biomedical research and data.

We requested data for all hospital admissions from January 1, 2001, to December 31, 2023, for patients diagnosed with oral cancer, using the ICD-9 codes 140–149 (until 2015) and the ICD-10 codes C00-C14 (from 2016 onwards).

Case definition and selection

The RAE registry uses the reason for hospital admission as the primary diagnosis and records up to 20 additional conditions as secondary diagnoses. The registry also records different procedures performed on the patient during each hospital admission, as well as epidemiological variables, tumour morphology, and healthcare cost. As a healthcare database, survival or specific tumour variables, such as TNM stage, are not recorded.

RAE registry records the total cost in Euro of all diagnostic and treatment activities carried out during each hospital admission.

From all the records, we selected admissions of patients with codes 143 (ICD-9) or C03 (ICD-10) in any of their diagnoses, corresponding to malignant neoplasm of the gingiva.

We considered those records that met these conditions as cases for our study:

Thus, our cases involved patients whose hospital admission was motivated by a malignant gingival neoplasm, its treatment, or regional spread.

Once we had identified admissions due to gingival cancer, we analysed these admissions in two different ways. Firstly, we examined the first recorded admission for each patient, regardless of the number of subsequent admissions they may have had. We refer to this as the patient group. On the other hand, we have compiled all admissions due to gingival cancer; this group will be used primarily to study the cost of specialist care for this condition, and we will refer to this data as admission data.

Variables

We analysed the following epidemiological variables present at the time of hospital admission, using the group of patients: sex, age, location (maxilla or mandible).

For the economic analysis, we examined: (a) the total cost of all admissions for all patients with this condition, which we consider to be the total inpatient cost; (b) the cost of each hospital admission; and (c) the cost per patient, including all their admissions. To compare costs across all the years analysed, we adjusted for annual Consumer Price Index (CPI) variation to 2023.

Statistical analysis

We performed a descriptive analysis of the variables and their evolution from 2001 to 2023. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the data. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for noncontinuous variables, while continuous variables were shown as means and standard deviation (SD). Categorical and non-continuous variables were compared using chi-square with a 95% confidence interval and a significance level of 0.05 via the SPSS package (version 30.0.0.0).

Results

Overall trends

The total number of records with a diagnosis of oropharyngeal cancer in any of the diagnoses was 264,688. Among these patients, 9,909 had gingival cancer.

Our sample comprises 7,327 admissions corresponding to 5,623 patients (unique individuals). The number of admissions increased from 239 in 2001 to 358 in 2023 (+ 50.4%). The number of cases (first admissions per patient) increased from 198 in 2001 to 315 in 2023 (+ 59.1%).

Sex distribution

Among the 5,623 patients, 48.9% were men (n = 2,751), and 51.1% were women (n = 2,872). In 2001, men represented 56.6% of the cases, whereas in 2023, men represented 45.1%. The male: female ratio changed from 1.3:1 in 2001 to 0.8:1 in 2023.

The analysis revealed a sustained trend toward female predominance from 2015 onwards (Fig. 1). Although there were specific years with a female predominance before 2015, the pattern has been maintained since 2015, when women consistently surpassed men (2015–2023). The association between year and sex was statistically significant (chi-square test, p < 0.001).

Age distribution

The mean age at diagnosis differed significantly between sexes: 71.1 years (SD ± 12.4) for women and 65.3 years (SD ± 13.7) for men, with a difference of almost 6 years (chi-square test, p < 0.001). The average age at diagnosis was 68.1 years (SD ± 13.3).

The age group distribution (Table 1) was significantly different between the sexes (chi-square test, p < 0.001). In men, the highest proportion of cases were concentrated in the 60–69 years age group (28%), whereas in women, the peak shifted to older ages, with a higher frequency in the 70–79 years age group (32%) and a high proportion in the ≥ 80 years age group (29%).

Hospital costs

Analysing the total number of admission records (n = 7,327) and adjusting recorded costs to 2023 CPI, we found a 180% variation between 2001 and 2023. The total inpatient costs associated with gingival cancer increased from €1.67 million in 2001 to €4.69 million in 2023 (Fig. 2). With some variations throughout the series, the increase has been continuous during all these years.

Discussion

Methodological considerations

This study employed a retrospective epidemiological design based on Spain’s Hospital Activity Registry (RAE, formerly known as CMBD). We analysed the period from 2001 to 2023 with the objective of describing the epidemiological evolution of gingival cancer and its national economic impact at the hospital level. A total of 7,327 admissions of patients caused by this pathology occurred, corresponding to 5,623 patients with gingival cancer during this period.

RAE has been used for the epidemiological study of different cancer types [7–10]. Since the RAE is a healthcare activity registry and not a cancer case registry, we must define what constitutes our study cases. These can be hospital admissions motivated by gingival cancer or all admissions for any cause of patients who at some point have or have had gingival cancer [9, 10]. Expanding the case definition to include secondary diagnoses (up to 20 possible diagnoses) increases the number of cases to study but does not allow differentiation of incident cases and would not permit longitudinal study of their evolution, as admissions of the same patient for other unrelated causes would be repeated.

In a study evaluating RAE data for prostate cancer, the authors concluded that case validation, consideration of error factors, and repeated admissions were necessary [7]. In 2006, Márquez-Cid evaluated differences in case selection and demonstrated that selecting cases based on only primary diagnosis reduces sensitivity but increases specificity in case selection, such that selecting any diagnosis reduces the positive predictive value (PPV) of selection [8]. Bernal Delgado also demonstrated how this more restrictive method increased PPV [7]. Therefore, in our study, we opted to combine primary diagnosis and clinical variables such as regional metastasis; including procedures in case selection also increased the PPV [7], with the objective of improving sensitivity while ensuring specificity.

Another methodological aspect to consider is the change in the registry methodology of this database. As mentioned, in 2016, the ICD edition change was implemented, and some variables were modified. For example, the number of possible diagnoses increased from 14 to 20, the recording formats of some variables were modified, and the number of treatments recorded at each admission also increased. This possibility of alteration in long-term trends has been previously described, but no mechanism has yet been proposed to resolve it [10, 13]. In fact, even in studies not using RAE data, the evaluation of temporal trends can be affected by coding changes between different ICD versions (ICD-9/ICD-10), which can introduce measurement errors and hinder the comparability of results over time [14–16].

This change in the RAE’s methodology may affect the interpretation of trends over time. The number of cases breaks with its trend between 2015 and 2016. This fact should therefore be considered when interpreting the data for the period from 2001 to 2015 and from 2016 onwards.

Sex distribution changes

In our series of first admissions for gingival cancer, an increase in case numbers was observed throughout the study period, increasing from 198 cases in 2001 to 315 in 2023 (+ 59.1%). The sex distribution shows a sustained change over time, with a greater frequency in women. The male: female ratio decreased from 1.3:1 in 2001 to 0.8:1 in 2023, with the female percentage increasing from 43.4% to 54.9% (Fig. 1). Although there were specific years with a female predominance before 2015, the pattern consolidated from 2015, when women consistently surpassed men. The association between year and sex was significant (p < 0.001). The decrease observed between 2015 and 2016 coincides with the ICD-9 to ICD-10 coding change and modifications to the registry methodology, which can be interpreted as an administrative discontinuity independent of the disease; after 2016, the overall trend returns to ascent (Fig. 1).

This behaviour is consistent with the literature describing a reduction in male predominance in oral cancer in recent decades: in a Dutch reference centre, a decrease in the male: female ratio was observed when comparing periods from the 1980s versus 2000–2004 (1.8:1-1.2:1) [17]. Such shifts have been attributed to sex-specific changes in exposure to tobacco and alcohol [11, 18], and the magnitude of these associations varies by population context and oral subsite [19]. In Spain, alcohol consumption trends also differ by sex: weekly consumption fell from 64.8% to 52.4% in men and from 46.4% to 40.8% in women between 2006 and 2023 [20].

With respect to the specific location of gingival cancer, several studies support that it is an entity with a differential epidemiological profile. Barasch et al. [21] reported that male: female ratios were significantly greater in the floor of mouth than in the tongue and gingiva (alveolar ridge included), suggesting sex-specific predilections by subsites and possible etiological differences. Subsequent reviews highlighted the heterogeneity of series and difficulty in comparing results due to low tumour incidence and frequent grouping of locations [22]. In some hospital cohorts of patients with gingival cancer, a male predominance is observed, reinforcing the variability between populations [23]. Differences in male: female ratio between studies may be influenced by diagnostic circuits (clinical series vs. registries). Seoane et al. noted that gingival cancer may present as an advanced disease due to early bone invasion, affecting diagnosis and comparability of results [24].

Additionally, as an explanatory hypothesis for female predominance, the role of potentially malignant disorders with higher prevalence in women is plausible, particularly proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL) [25]. PVL affects more women (64%), and the gingiva is one of the most frequent sites of both PVL (39.6%) and malignant transformation (39.9%), supporting its plausibility as a more relevant pathway of gingival carcinogenesis in females [26, 27].

Age distribution

In the patient’s group, the mean age at diagnosis was 68.1 years (SD ± 13.3). This finding is consistent with the published literature on gingival cancer, where the age at presentation is high. Fitzpatrick et al. reported a mean age of 66.1 years [28]. Lubek et al. reported a mean age of 60.8 years [29], and Santos-Leite et al. 62 [30]. In the largest series to date of primary gingival cancer, Lee et al. [31] reported a mean age of 70.2 years, reinforcing that in different healthcare and population contexts, diagnosis is concentrated at advanced ages.

In our patients, age at diagnosis was greater in women than in men (mean 71.1 vs. 65.3 years), with a difference of almost 6 years. This pattern is consistent with what has been observed in other publications [32, 33], where men tend to have a lower age at diagnosis. Rautava et al. described a mean age of 66 years in men versus 73 years in women [34].

We found that the age group distribution significantly differed between sexes (chi-square test p < 0.001): in men, the highest proportion of cases were aged 60–69 years (28%), whereas in women, the peak shifted to older ages, with a higher frequency of 70–79 years (32%) and a high proportion of cases aged ≥ 80 years (29%). This pattern suggests an overall later presentation in women, with a “shift” toward advanced ages compared with men. These findings for gingival cancer agree with the literature on oral cancer, where it has been described that women are older than men at diagnosis.

Anatomical location

In our group of patients (n = 5,623), localization was specified in 4,093 patients. Mandibular predominance (63%) versus maxillary predominance (37%) was confirmed, with sex differences (female predominance in the maxilla and male predominance in the mandible). Economic burden of hospital care

The cost of oral and head and neck cancer has been the subject of numerous studies in different healthcare contexts. Analyses of economic cost and global disease burden have been published for oral cancer but not for gingival cancer. In our case, we only analysed the economic cost of hospital healthcare for patients with gingival cancer. The published healthcare costs of oral cancer patients vary between €11,000 and €36,000 per patient. These differences depend fundamentally on the patient’s diagnostic stage and the country where the cancer is treated [35–43].

In Spain, Porta-Vazquez [42] reported a cost of diagnostic and therapeutic procedures per patient for oral cavity tumours, without differentiating gingiva, of €15,223 per patient in the 2015–2017 period. To date, we have found only one national study that specifically evaluated the cost of gingival cancer. Using RAE data but without adjusting for annual inflation variation, Carazo-Casas et al. estimated an average hospital cost of €7,822 for men and €7,495 for women [10] for the period 2009–2019, not adjusted to the CPI. In our study, we found that the cost per admission in 2001 was €7,007, rising to €13,114 in 2023, adjusting for price variation during these years (CPI). These results are also not comparable because Carazo-Casas does not discriminate by reason for admission, and we selected admission cases only for procedures related to gingival cancer.

Our results revealed that the total inpatient costs associated with gingival cancer increased from €1.67 million to €4.69, a 180% increase. Nevertheless, to calculate total cancer cost, we should consider other healthcare, social, and labor components, and we have not considered these components in our study. Additionally, the literature presents heterogeneous methodologies for calculating costs—incidence vs. prevalence, microcosting (bottom-up) vs. macrocosting (top-down), and healthcare, social, or mixed perspectives—as well as included components (direct medical, nonmedical, and indirect due to productivity loss), which further complicates the calculation and comparison of total disease costs for cancer [40, 41]. However, it has been estimated that in Spain, the global healthcare cost of assistance, pharmacology, and morbidity treatment for cancer represents approximately 70% of the total cost [40].

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is the use of a national hospital care database with a large sample size, which can provide statistical power and temporal traceability and is especially useful for describing trends and hospital burdens. However, the source data characteristics present some limitations or weaknesses. RAE codes admissions, so redundancy from readmissions may exist, affecting point estimates (potential overestimation of hospital prevalence and underestimation of in-hospital lethality), although it has been argued that, owing to the large volume of records, this redundancy is unlikely to alter long-term trends.

Similarly, losses may exist from private care not being completely captured, and the ICD-9/ICD-10 transition approximately 2016 may introduce discontinuities that are difficult to quantify. Finally, as a healthcare activity database and not a disease registry, the RAE does not incorporate key clinical variables such as TNM and in-hospital survival, so etiological or prognostic inference is beyond the scope of the present work.

Conclusions

To make public health decisions, it is important to detect changes in the epidemiological characteristics of cancer, oral cancer, and gingival cancer in particular.

Although our data only provide estimates of trends in the variables studied, this is the largest sample of patients with gingival cancer, covering the longest period, ever published. If the estimates we found regarding the male: female ratio were to be projected into the future, we believe that specific measures should be included to promote health education, prevent risk factors and diagnose potentially malignant lesions in the female population. Notably, gingival SCC can mimic periodontal pathology, so atypical or nonresponsive gingival lesions require early incisional biopsy and referral to minimize diagnostic delays [44].

Dentists are responsible for diagnosing this disease and are perhaps the only professionals capable of making a differential diagnosis. Their training should be improved so that they can reach a diagnosis earlier. We should consider improving oral cancer diagnostic tools in the dental curriculum and in continuing professional education by enhancing training in oral medicine. The cost of hospital care for patients with gingival cancer has multiplied over the last 20 years, and if this trend continues, it will continue to rise.

Previous studies have reported disparities in access to public healthcare that may affect patient survival, highlighting the importance of investigating the burden of oral cancer to strengthen surveillance efforts [45].

Finally, national mortality projections in Spain [46] underscore the importance of monitoring oral cancer outcomes by sex and age group and of intensifying prevention and early-detection efforts where trends are less favourable.

RAE is a data source with sufficient sensitivity to monitor for cancer in the Spanish population. We found a change in the sex distribution of patients with gingival cancer. Gingival cancer has been more common in women than in men since 2015, with a male: female ratio of 0.8:1. The cost of healthcare for patients with gingival cancer has increased from €8,457 in 2001 to €14,904 per patient in 2023, representing a 76% increase. The sex ratio change reflects changes in the risk factor distribution in the population. The increase in hospitalization burden may be partially explained by diagnostic improvements, population aging, and treatment complexity.
