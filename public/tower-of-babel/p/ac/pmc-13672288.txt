Advance Learning in Oncology - A CT Imaging Dataset of Lung Nodule Metastases from Bone and Soft Tissue Tumors
Authors: Shaobo Han, Hao Wang, Wenjian Sun, Xiang Liu, Wacili Da, Yang Luo, Li Min, Chunbo Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone and soft tissue tumors (BSTTs) are prone to metastasize to the lungs to form pulmonary nodules, which are quite different from other metastatic nodules or primary nodules, such as those from lung, breast, or colorectal cancers. Early detection of these specific lung nodules is crucial for timely intervention and treatment of BSTTs. To promote the development of BSTTs detection algorithm based on deep learning, this paper releases a Computed Tomography (CT) dataset containing 59 patients and 779 BSTTs metastatic pulmonary nodules with pixel-level annotations. We further publish benchmark lung nodule detection experiments on this dataset, achieving F1 score of 0.842, respectively, demonstrating its research potential. We release raw CT data, preprocessing codes for data conversion and coordinate extraction, to enable researchers to obtain precisely calibrated datasets for training deep learning models and promote the utilization of this clinical scientific data.

Background & Summary

Bone and soft tissue tumors (BSTTs) are often malignant, and the malignancies represent a heterogeneous group of mesenchymal tumors with a wide anatomical distribution and more than 80 histological subtypes1, accounting for 1% of all newly diagnosed cancers each year2. These comparably rare diseases form a significant group that affects patients of all ages, including children and adolescents. Most new cases are either intermediate or high-grade tumors and can develop in virtually any site, with the extremities being the most common site of origin3. The malignancies have a propensity for metastasis, with the lungs being the most common site, accounting for 17.8%–25.2% of cases and typically presenting as pulmonary nodules (PNs)4. The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate for patients with pulmonary metastases ranges from 15% to 52%5. As a result, the early detection of these PNs from BSTTs pulmonary metastases is crucial for timely intervention and is also a key determinant of patient prognosis6. Medical experts commonly utilize thoracic computed tomography (CT) for PN diagnosis, as this imaging technique provides detailed three-dimensional visualization of the lungs, allowing for a more precise assessment of PN location, diameter, and density7.

Manual lung nodule detection in clinical settings requires detailed examination of CT scans by trained experts. Even experienced radiologists often need a considerable amount of time and energy to provide an accurate diagnosis. Other adversarial conditions such as long working hours or insufficient experience may result in decreased efficiency and inaccurate diagnoses8. To reduce labor-intensive screening efforts and enhance diagnostic accuracy, Computer-Aided Diagnosis (CAD) technology for lung nodule detection has been actively studied since the 1980s. For instance, Giger et al.9 developed a CAD scheme based on eliminating the background anatomic structures in the lung fields and feature-extraction. Machine learning-based approaches have gained increasing attention10. Ponomaryov et al.11 employed Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithm for the segmentation and classification of lung nodules. More recently, due to the impressive performance of Deep Learning (DL) in computer vision, models like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been applied to medical image processing12. Researchers have proposed a variety of novel methods specifically designed for pulmonary nodule detection tasks. Liao et al.13 utilized a 3-D deep leaky noisy-OR network to assess the malignancy of pulmonary nodules. Zhu et al.14 developed DeepLung, a fully automated lung CT diagnosis system that combines 3D deep learning models for nodule detection and classification.

Deep learning methods usually require large datasets annotated by field experts. Such as LIDC-IDRI, it includes high-resolution lung CT scans from 1,018 patients, accompanied by annotations and diagnostic information from radiologists15. LUNA16, a subset extracted from the LIDC-IDRI dataset, is specifically designed for lung nodule detection challenges. It contains data from 888 patients from LIDC-IDRI with nodule diameters of 3 millimeters or more, with stricter data cleaning and standardization to ensure high-quality annotations16. In addition, Mei et al.17 collected and annotated a large-scale pulmonary nodule dataset named PN9, which contains 8,798 thoracic CT scans and a total of 40,439 annotated nodules. Jian et al.18 developed a dataset specifically for lung nodule detection and classification, which includes 95 scans and a total of 330 nodules. The PNs in these datasets have complex and diverse etiologies, and their characteristics also differ from those of nodules associated with lung metastases from BSTTs. In contrast, pulmonary nodules arising from BSTTs lung metastases exhibit several distinctive characteristics. These nodules are frequently multiple, with a single patient often presenting five to ten nodules simultaneously. In addition, compared with nodules in more heterogeneous public datasets, BSTTs metastatic nodules tend to have larger overall diameters and predominantly present as solid nodules on CT images. Notably, these nodules are invariably malignant by clinical diagnosis, a feature that significantly distinguishes this specific pathology from the mixed benign and malignant lesions typically found in existing public datasets.

To address the limitations of existing public datasets, we developed the BSTT_CT dataset to support detection and segmentation algorithms. In addition, we adopted a standardized and widely used preprocessing pipeline throughout data preparation and experimental validation. This pipeline includes DICOM-to-MHD conversion with unified spatial metadata, voxel spacing normalization, intensity clipping and normalization in Hounsfield units, lung-region masking, and resampling to a consistent resolution. These procedures are designed to reduce variations introduced by different scanners and acquisition protocols, thereby encouraging learning-based models to focus on lesion-related imaging characteristics rather than interferencing factors.

Methods

This dataset exclusively comprises CT scan images of patients with BSTTs lung metastases, collected and annotated in collaboration with the West China Hospital. The dataset includes 779 annotated nodules from 59 patients admitted between 2010 and 2024 (33 males and 26 females; age range: 18-79 years (Fig. 2)). This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (IRB No. 1326, approved on 29 July 2022). The requirement for written informed consent for participation and data sharing was waived by the Ethics Committee, as the data were retrospectively collected from routine clinical practice and were fully anonymised prior to analysis and public release. The released dataset contains no direct or indirect personal identifiers, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national ethical guidelines. Data collection and annotation

The dataset originates from the West China Hospital and is formatted in accordance with Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standards19. The DICOM file format is the standard in the field of medical imaging for storing and exchanging medical images and the associated meta information. Professional clinicians from the West China Hospital within the investigation team of this research, annotate the lung nodules in the CT scans using 3D Slicer20. As a key component of the dataset construction pipeline shown in Fig. 3, we employ a three-stage annotation process to ensure the quality, consistency, and reliability of the annotations (Fig. 4). In the first stage, a professional clinician diagnose each patient and record the slice range containing nodules in a spreadsheet, along with a general description of the region. In the second stage, three annotators undergo specialized training supervised by clinicians, focusing on learning the image characteristics and types of lung nodules and becoming proficient with 3D Slicer. Only annotators who passed the training evaluation were allowed to perform annotations. During annotation, all potential disagreements or uncertain cases were explicitly documented. In the final stage, all annotation results and discrepancy records were systematically reviewed by an experienced clinician. This review process aimed to identify missed nodules, correct inaccurate annotations, and resolve disagreements to obtain a consensus annotation.Fig. 4Clinician-guided three-stage annotation process for lung nodules.

Clinician-guided three-stage annotation process for lung nodules.

Instead of using bounding boxes, we employ pixel-level segmentation for the annotations16, which allows for a more precise representation of nodule locations and reduces noise in the ground truth. DICOM Reading and Conversion

The DICOM files may include images from different devices or scanning protocols, and their order might even be reversed, making them unsuitable for direct analysis. Thus, we convert them into MetaImage Header Files (MHD) and corresponding Raw Image Data (RAW) files to ensure uniform data formatting, making the data easier to utilize. The MHD file describes how to interpret the binary data in the RAW file (such as DimSize, ElementType, ElementSpacing, etc.). The RAW file contains all the voxel data of the images, stored in a binary format. This file has no structured metadata and relies entirely on the MHD file for interpretation.

While DICOM files typically store each image slice as a separate file, MHD and RAW files can consolidate all slices into a single three-dimensional image file, which is crucial for certain 3D reconstruction and analysis tasks. Based on these, we utilize python libraries to read information such as PixelSpacing and ImageOrientationPatient from each patient’s DICOM files and write them into MHD files. The slices are sorted in ascending order based on the InstanceNumber values, and the value of ImagePositionPatient from the first slice is written into the Offset in the MHD file. Finally, the MHD and RAW files are named and stored according to the SeriesInstanceUID.

Mask segmentation

CT scans may cover areas from the neck to the abdomen, resulting in a dataset containing not only the relevant lung regions but also various irrelevant parts, such as bones and the heart, which may interfere the subsequent processing and analysis. For instance, in nodule detection, the presence of extraneous non-lung tissues introduces noise and increases the likelihood of false positives, thereby affecting the accuracy and robustness of the algorithms10. Therefore, we use lung masks to extract the lung region, confining the processing to the lung area and eliminating the need to process the entire image. This approach significantly reduces computational complexity and accelerates processing speed, particularly when dealing with 3D medical images12. Here are the steps to get the masks: We convert the pixel values of the DICOM images into Hounsfield units (HU, a common unit in medical imaging used to measure tissue density and composition21) and apply Gaussian filtering and thresholding to each CT slice to achieve an initial binary segmentation. Coordinate reading

In the previous lung nodule annotation work, we save the annotated files in the seg.nrrd format, but they cannot be directly used for model training yet. To facilitate the use of this dataset, we represent the nodule coordinates using the 3D world coordinates of the center point and the diameter16.

In the seg.nrrd file, the annotated regions appear as clusters of assigned pixel values on individual slices, which typically form spheroid shapes when stacked across multiple slices. After reading the annotation files, we detect these spheroid regions through connectivity checks and calculate their center points and maximum diameters. The data thus enables the calculation of the center coordinates and diameters of the nodules within the image voxel coordinate system21.

Before using the dataset for training and analysis, the voxel coordinates cannot be directly utilized and must be converted into world coordinates. Data preprocessing

To facilitate direct experimentation by users, we perform preprocessing on the dataset as follows: Boundary Extraction and Box Calculation: Read the MHD files and the corresponding segmentation files. The boundaries of the segmentation, defined as the edges of non-zero regions, are determined to compute a 3D bounding box, which is subsequently resampled to a uniform resolution.Intensity Clipping and Normalization: To enhance the visibility of lung structures, the MHD data is clipped to the range of -1200 to 600 Hounsfield units (HU). Values outside this range are set to -1200 or 600, respectively, and the data is normalized to a range of 0 to 255.Mask Dilation and Application: The segmentation mask is dilated to remove small cavities within the lungs. A new mask is then applied to the original data, with values outside the masked lung region set to 170 (corresponding to the normalized HU value for water), thus isolating the lung region22.Resampling and Cropping: The isolated lung region is resampled, and the data within the bounding box is cropped, effectively excluding non-lung regions to reduce noise and data volume12.Label Transformation: Labels from the CSV file are converted to voxel coordinates, and the new resolution is applied. It is important to note that since the data is within the bounding box, the coordinates are relative to the box. The preprocessed data and labels are saved in NumPy format.

Data Records

The dataset has been made publicly available on Zenodo23, with the following link: https://zenodo.org/records/18862454.

The MHD and RAW files are stored into Image.zip, with each MHD file corresponding to a RAW file, totaling 61 pairs. The segmented lung masks are saved in Segment.zip, with 61 pairs of MHD and ZRAW files. Technical Validation

In order to assess the applicability of this dataset for model training, we perform experiments with several benchmark models in the domain of medical image detection. These models include two single-stage models (YOLOv524 and SSD25) and two two-stage models (DeepLung14 and Faster R-CNN26), all of which have demonstrated effectiveness in medical object detection tasks. In this process, we employ Precision, Recall, and F1 as evaluation metrics. The F1 is a metric used to evaluate the balance between Precision and Recall in recommendation or classification systems. It is the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, providing a single score that considers both27. The experimental results indicate a steady improvement in recall and precision as the nodule size increases, suggesting that there is a high potential to improve the models’ performance in detecting smaller nodules. YOLOv5 outperforms all models across evaluation metrics, particularly achieving an average precision (AP) of 0.685 for small nodules, indicating the dataset’s strong applicability for detecting smaller lesions. Additionally, YOLOv5 demonstrates impressive average recall (AR) values of 0.832 and 0.828 for medium and large nodules.

Compared to YOLOv5, SSD exhibits inferior performance across all size categories. Although DeepLung (3D) exhibits a high AR of 0.905 for large nodules, its performance on small nodules is relatively weak. This suggests potential areas for improvement regarding this model. Faster R-CNN (3D) shows stable performance with an AR of 0.758 for medium nodules. These results affirm the value of our dataset for developing future diverse deep learning models, effectively supporting the training and evaluation of various models.
