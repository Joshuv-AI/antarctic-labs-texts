Red palm olein biscuit supplementation modulates gut microbiota in vitamin A deficient rural Malaysian schoolchildren: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Pei Yee Tan, Radhika Loganathan, Soo Ching Lee, Syahirah Nadiah Mohd Johari, Kim-Tiu Teng, Kanga Rani Selvaduray, Yvonne Ai-Lian Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between vitamin A deficiency (VAD) and gut microbiota, with implications to host health. This double-blind randomised controlled trial (NCT03256123) aims to investigate the effects of 6-month red palm olein (RPO)-enriched biscuit supplementation on gut microbiota of 328 vitamin A-deficient primary schoolchildren (aged 8–12 years) in rural Malaysia, randomised to receive either RPO-enriched biscuits (experimental group, n = 145) or palm olein (PO)-enriched biscuits (control group, n = 183). Stool samples are collected at baseline, 3-month and 6-month of supplementation, and analysed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The primary outcome is the changes in gut microbiota composition, while secondary outcomes include alteration in gut microbiota diversity, correlations with blood biomarkers, and microbial network structure. Compared to the control group, the experimental group shows significant reductions in alpha diversity indices (e.g., observed ASVs and chao1, P < 0.001), and increased abundance of potentially beneficial gut bacteria, such as Anaerostipes, UCG-010 and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group (Padj < 0.05), which also positively correlate with improvements in alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, alpha-tocopherol, packed cell volume, and mean corpuscular volume (Padj < 0.05). Microbial network analysis reveals that the experimental group exhibits a more cohesive and stable network, evidenced by higher average degree and clustering coefficient values, and lower average path lengths (P < 0.001). Our findings shed light on the potential of RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation in modulating gut microbiota in vitamin A-deficient children by enhancing microbial network cohesiveness and enriching potentially beneficial bacterial taxa which are linked to enhanced micronutrient and haematological outcomes. These findings lay a valuable foundation and expand the existing evidence base for understanding the relationship between provitamin A carotenoids and gut microbiota.Subject terms: Malnutrition, Microbiome, Nutrition

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), vitamin A deficiency (VAD) stands as one of the major micronutrient deficiencies worldwide, impacting about 190 million children of preschool age residing in countries with low to middle incomes1. The primary cause of VAD often lies in prolonged dietary deprivation of vitamin A2. Furthermore, secondary VAD can arise due to reduced bioavailability of provitamin A, and impairment in absorption, storage, or transport of vitamin A3. Within the human gastrointestinal tract lies a diverse assembly of microorganisms, collectively referred to as the gut microbiota4. The metabolism and absorption of vitamin A can be significantly influenced by these microorganisms, potentially leading to secondary VAD when there is an imbalance in gut microbiota5.

Intriguingly, the interplay of gut microbiota and vitamin A may be bidirectional. Multiple studies have shown that VAD can alter the gut microbiota, leading to adverse health outcomes. Hibberd et al.6 reported that the bacterial community structure and meta-transcriptome were most affected by acute VAD, with an upsurge in the abundance of Bacteroides vulgatus. Similarly, VAD has been found to affect gut microbiota composition in a rat model, and these effects vary with each life stage7. Besides, VAD may impair mucosal barrier functions, contributing to gut dysbiosis8. Conversely, the modulation of human gut microbiota by vitamin A had been demonstrated in a study conducted among Bangladeshi infants, where boys who received vitamin A supplementation within 48 hours of birth showed a notably greater abundance of Bifidobacterium as compared to those in the placebo group9. Moreover, Liu et al.10 demonstrated that a single administration of high-dose vitamin A administration (200,000 IU) led to several alterations in the microbial composition, particularly the proportion of Bacteroidetes/Bacteroidales, among children diagnosed with autism spectrum disorders.

In addition to the administration of high-dose vitamin A, food fortification with preformed vitamin A or provitamin A carotenoids offers a viable and cost-effective approach to enhance vitamin A status of populations11. Red palm olein (RPO), a rich natural source of provitamin A carotenoids (600-750 ppm), alongside other essential phytonutrients like vitamin E, phytosterols, squalene and coenzyme Q10, has gained attention for fortification against VAD12. Prior literature has highlighted the effectiveness of RPO in enhancing vitamin A levels, promoting iron absorption and erythropoiesis, demonstrating anti-inflammatory properties, as well as alleviating xerophthalmia13–15.

It is worth noting that the majority of dietary carotenoids are metabolised by the microbiome in the colon, with only a minor fraction being assimilated in the intestinal tract and transported to body tissues16. Despite this, a significant gap in knowledge persists pertaining the interactions between provitamin A carotenoids and gut microbiota. Scientific literature on the impacts of beta-carotene supplementation on gut microbiota is relatively limited, with most studies relying on animal models17. Furthermore, the effects of RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation on gut microbiota remain an area of unexplored research. In this same cohort, we have discovered that supplementing RPO-enriched biscuits significantly increased provitamin A carotenes, iron status, and erythropoiesis, as well as exerted potential anti-inflammatory properties14. Besides, we demonstrated beneficial effects on Ascaris lumbricoides reinfection when administrated with triple-dose albendazole treatment18.

Here, we show that 6-month supplementation of provitamin A carotenoids-rich RPO-enriched biscuits modulates the gut microbiota of vitamin A-deficient primary schoolchildren in rural Malaysia. We find that RPO supplementation reduces alpha diversity and increases the abundance of potentially beneficial bacterial taxa such as Anaerostipes, UCG-010 and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, which are positively associated with improvements in micronutrient and haematological status. In addition, microbial network analysis reveals enhanced cohesiveness and stability of the gut microbial system following 6-month RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation. These findings highlight the potential of RPO-enriched biscuits to beneficially modulate gut microbiota composition and structure in this vulnerable population.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Figure 1 illustrates the schematic representation of the participant flow in the current study. After excluding 513 subjects (44.1%) who either missed the screening examination or did not meet the inclusion criteria of the study, this study enroled 651 vitamin A-deficient participants. Of these, 328 subjects completed the follow-ups and were subsequently included in the analysis. The remaining 323 subjects were excluded from the analysis due to the following reasons: non-compliance with the allocated supplementation due to absenteeism (n = 27), loss to follow-up due to absenteeism (n = 267), and failure to complete at least 70% of supplementation and/or deworming regime (n = 29). The baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. Overall, the population consisted of 146 boys (44.5%) and 182 girls (55.5%), with a mean age of 9.9 ± 1.3 years. Age and sex distributions were comparable across all groups. A majority of the study cohort (66.2%) were Orang Asli schoolchildren, the indigenous minority population of Peninsular Malaysia, as described in our previously published data19. Most participants (64.2%) hailed from households earning <RM500 monthly. Additionally, 88 subjects (26.8%) were diagnosed with confirmed VAD, while 240 (73.2%) exhibited marginal VAD, and there was no significant disparity in VAD prevalence between the groups. Furthermore, a significant proportion of children (65.6%) were affected by soil-transmitted helminth (STH) infections, predominantly Trichuris trichiura (61.5%), followed by Ascaris lumbricoides (26.2%) and hookworm (22.0%). No participants reported any adverse health effects from the supplementation, and none withdrew due to adverse events.

Sequencing summary and microbial taxonomic composition across timepoints

A total of 63,264,066 quality-filtered sequences were obtained from the samples (N = 984) across three timepoints (baseline, 3-month and 6-month) with a mean frequency of 63,518.14 ± 10,528.32 (SD) sequences per sample. These reads were clustered into 16,621 unique amplicon sequence variants (ASV).

At baseline, the most abundant phyla were Firmicutes (61.0%), Bacteroidota (25.8%), Actinobacteriota (6.0%) and Proteobacteria (4.7%), and this pattern was largely similar across the experimental and control groups, with the exception that the relative abundance of Proteobacteria in the experimental group (6.2%) was 2-fold higher than in the control group (3.3%) (P < 0.001, Student’s t-test) (Fig. 2). Besides, the changes in the relative abundances of the top ten phyla and genera across timepoints were found to be similar among the experimental and control groups.

Reduced microbial richness following RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation

Significant group x timepoint interactions were noted for several alpha diversity indices, including observed ASVs (P < 0.001), chao1 (P < 0.001), and Shannon diversity index (P = 0.01), while no significant group x timepoint interaction was observed for the Simpson index (P = 0.42) (Fig. 3). Based on post-hoc analysis, no significant changes were observed in observed ASVs, chao1 and Shannon diversity index within both the experimental and control groups at 3-month of supplementation. However, a significant decrease in both observed ASV (386.86, 95% CI [374.52, 399.19] to 360.62, 95% CI [347.43, 373.81]; P = 0.03), and chao1 (401.69, 95% CI [388.06, 415.31] to 374.86, 95% CI [360.31, 389.41]; P = 0.04) from baseline to 6-month was found in the experimental group, while no significant difference was observed for the Shannon diversity index (P = 1.00). Conversely, in the control group, an opposing trend from baseline to 6-month was noted for these indices: observed ASV (345.85, 95% CI [334.76, 356.95] to 391.23, 95% CI [371.37, 411.10]; P < 0.001), chao1 (359.23, 95% CI [347.14, 371.32] to 404.24, 95% CI [383.89, 424.58]; P < 0.001) and Shannon diversity index (4.06, 95% CI [4.01, 4.12] to 4.18, 95% CI [4.11, 4.25]; P = 0.04). Comparable findings were obtained following segregation of subjects based on their respective state, indicating the robustness of the analysis with the inclusion of the entire cohort (Fig. S1).

As illustrated in Fig. 5A, a total of 18 ASVs were found to be differentially abundant (20.9% of 86 ASVs) at both 3- and 6-month of supplementation. Among these, the abundances of ASVs classified to genera UCG-010, Ruminococcus and Family_XIII_AD3011_group (all in phylum Firmicutes), Desulfovibrio (further assigned to species Desulfovibrio_piger, refer to Source Data of Fig. S2) (phylum Desulfobacterota), Treponema (further assigned to species Treponema_succinifaciens, refer to Source Data of Fig. S2) (phylum Proteobacteria) were found to be persistently higher in the experimental group at 6-month of supplementation, when compared to the control group (Fig. 5A and Fig. S2). Besides, certain ASVs, including those classified under genera Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, Butyricicoccus, Colidextribacter and Oscillibacter (all in phylum Firmicutes), Slackia (further assigned to species Slackia_isoflavoniconvertens, refer to Source Data of Fig. S2), genus Enterorhabdus and family Atopobiaceae (all in phylum Actinobacteriota), as well as genus Succinivibrio (phylum Proteobacteria), exhibited greater differential abundance in the experimental group only at 6-month, when compared to the control group (Fig. 5B and Fig. S3).

On the other hand, a number of ASVs were found to be more differentially abundant in the control group at both 3- and 6-month, such as genera Blautia, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Megamonas, Fusicatenibacter, Lachnospira, [Ruminococcus]_gnavus_group and Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group (all in phylum Firmicutes), Bacteroides (further assigned to species Bacteroides_stercoris, refer to Source Data of Figure S2) (phylum Bacteroidota), as well as family Enterobacteriaceae (phylum Proteobacteria) (Fig. 5A and Figure S2). Furthermore, several additional ASVs belonged to genera Lactococcus (further assigned to species Lactococcus_garvieae, refer to Source Data of Fig. S2), genera Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 (further assigned to species Clostridium_perfringens, refer to Source Data of Fig. S2), Weissella (all in phylum Firmicutes) were found to be more differentially abundant in the control group at 6-month (Fig. 5B and Fig. S2). Besides, an opposing trend in the ASVs belonging to genus Anaerostipes was observed, where ASV9455 was more enriched in the control group at 3 months, while ASV9432 was more enriched in the experimental group after 6-month of supplementation (Fig. S2).

Enrichment of beneficial gut microbial taxa under RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation linked to improved micronutrient and haematological markers

To investigate the relationships between the changes in gut microbial and clinical blood biomarkers outcomes, we performed Spearman correlation analyses on the delta changes (6-month minus baseline) in abundances of 48 differentially abundant ASVs (based on differential abundance analysis at 6-month) and the corresponding delta changes (6-month minus baseline) in blood biomarker (i.e. retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4), retinol, alpha-tocopherol, alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, red blood cell (RBC) count, haemoglobin, packed cell volume (PCV), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), ferritin, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP)) levels (Fig. 6). A distinct trend emerged, where the changes in the majority of ASVs that were more differentially abundant following 6-month RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation, including genera Family_XIII_AD3011_group, UCG-010, Christensenellaceae_R-7_group, Collinsella, Enterorhabdus, Phascolarctobacterium, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, Slackia, and Anaerostipes, as well as families Ruminococcaceae and Atopobiaceae exhibited significant positive correlations with improvements in alpha-tocopherol, alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, PCV, and MCV (adjusted P values < 0.05). In contrast, the changes in most ASVs that were differentially abundant in the control group, such as Fusicatenibacter, Clostridia_UCG-014, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Bacteroides, Lactococcus, and Lachnospiraceae_NK3A20_group, demonstrated significant negative correlations with these same clinical blood biomarkers (adjusted P values < 0.05).

Longitudinal predicted functional profiling reveals shifts in gut microbial functions following RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation

To study the potential role of the gut microbiota, the functional variations in the microbial communities were explored by employing Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) analysis with the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database to predict microbiota-associated functional pathways. We observed 5 and 73 differentially abundant microbiota-associated functional pathways between the experimental and control groups following 3-month and 6-month supplementation, respectively, after adjusting for baseline and other covariates (Fig. S3). At 3-month, only one pathway (caprolactam degradation) was significantly more abundant in the experimental group, whereas several functional pathways were found to be significantly more abundant in the control group, namely ABC transporters, arginine and proline metabolism, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, as well as D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism. Nevertheless, at 6-month, higher number of pathways (n = 71) with significantly greater abundance in the experimental group as compared to the control group was predicted and a majority of these were pathways involved in metabolism, such as lipid metabolism (i.e. biosynthesis of primary and secondary bile acids), carbohydrate metabolism (i.e. metabolism of fructose, mannose, starch, sucrose, galactose, amino sugar, nucleotide sugar, C5-branched dibasic acid, pyruvate, inositol phosphate, propanoate, glyoxylate, dicarboxylate and butanoate/butyrate, pentose phosphate pathway, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and citrate cycle (TCA cycle)), amino acid metabolism (i.e. biosynthesis of valine, leucine, isoleucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan and lysine, as well as metabolism of histidine, cysteine, methionine, alanine, aspartate, glutamate, glycine, serine, threonine, arginine and proline), followed by the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and degradation. Several other pathways involved in cellular processes, environmental information processing, genetic information processing, human diseases and organismal systems were also found to be more differentially abundant in the experimental group. In contrast, the abundances of two pathways, which were associated with the degradation of geraniol and styrene, were significantly higher in the control group as compared to the experimental group.

Increased microbial network cohesiveness and keystone taxa following RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation

To understand how RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation influences microbial community interactions, co-abundance networks were constructed for both the experimental and control groups across all timepoints (Fig. 7). It is noteworthy that a substantial proportion of large modules across all the networks was dominated by the phylum Firmicutes ( > 70%). In the network, node size was determined by the level of betweenness centrality, which reflects the extent to which a taxon serves as a critical connector or bridge within the microbial community. The most central node in each network consistently belonged to the phylum Firmicutes, except the network at the 6-month timepoint in the experimental group, where ASV1423, belonging to the phylum Actinobacteriota, emerged as the most central taxon.

Based on thresholds of Zi and Pi, several keystone taxa (module hubs, network hubs and connectors) were detected in each network. Intriguingly, the majority of the keystone taxa belonged to the predominant phyla such as Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota and Proteobacteria (Source Data of Fig. 7A–F). Notably, several genera such as UCG-010 (ASV29), UCG-005 (ASV6157), Dorea (ASV9228) and [Eubacterium]_coprostanoligenes_group (ASV5478), were consistently identified as module hubs and network hubs throughout the 6-month supplementation. By 6-month, two additional taxa, including Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group (ASV217) and Lactobacillus_ruminis (ASV14360) were newly identified as module hubs in the experimental group.

A notable increase in the number of edges (connections between ASVs) was observed in the microbial networks of the experimental group throughout the supplementation period, with the edge count at 6-month exceeding baseline levels by more than 2-fold (Fig. 7). In contrast, the control group exhibited a reduction in the number of edges at 3-month, followed by a rebound to approximately baseline level by 6-month. This pattern suggests that the control group experienced only a transient fluctuation in microbial connectivity, whereas the experimental group exhibited a sustained and progressive shift.

Comparison of microbial network topological properties showed that the experimental group exhibited significantly higher average degree and clustering coefficient values at both 3-month and 6-month of supplementation compared to the control group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8A), indicating a more densely connected and cohesive microbial network. Besides, the average path length was consistently significantly lower in the experimental group throughout the 6-month supplementation period (P < 0.001). Although group difference was statistically significant from the onset of supplementation, the small effect size at baseline (r = 0.11) suggests limited clinical relevance initially. However, the effect size increased over time to a moderate level at 3 months (r = 0.33) and further at 6 months (r = 0.41), indicating a progressively stronger effect of the RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation on network connectivity.

At baseline, a moderate overlap was observed (Jaccard index, J = 0.325, P(J ≤ j) = 0.46), indicating no significant difference in keystone taxa structure between groups. These findings confirmed that the experimental group increased key taxa over time.

Discussion

In the present study, significant reductions in alpha diversity indices, including observed ASVs and chao1 were observed after 6-month supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits (experimental group) relative to that at baseline, while the control group exhibited a substantial increase in these two indices. These findings were contrary to previous studies, where the alpha diversities were positively correlated with blood carotenoid concentrations20. Nevertheless, both observed ASVs and chao1 could be largely influenced by the presence of rare ASVs in the samples. A comparable outcome was found between the groups for the Simpson index, which considers both richness and evenness but places greater weight on the dominant ASVs. Hence, the findings of the present study infer that 6-month supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits may reduce alpha diversity, with the effects appearing to be evident in the presence of rare bacteria. We postulate that the anti-microbial activity of carotenoids found in the RPO-enriched biscuits may selectively impact the gut microbiota of the vitamin A-deficient schoolchildren, contributing to the observed decrease in alpha diversity in the current study population21. Significant differences in beta diversities based on the consumption of carotenoid-containing foods and plasma alpha-carotene concentration were also demonstrated in a study conducted by Schmidt et al. 20. However, our study findings are inconsistent with these outcomes, as no significant difference in beta diversities (based on within-subject Bray-Curtis distance and Jaccard distance or first distance) was observed between the experimental and control groups (Fig. 4G and H). Although PERMANOVA (adonis2) analyses showed a statistically significant difference in beta diversities between groups over time (P < 0.05), the effect size was very small (R² < 0.003), indicating minimal contribution of the group × timepoint interaction to overall variance. This small effect size may be due to the complex interplay of multiple factors shaping the microbiome, the detection of statistically significant but biologically minor differences in large sample sizes, and high inter-individual variability masking group-level shifts22–24. The discrepancy in the findings of alpha and beta diversities may be attributed to the different study population, ethnicity, and supplementation strategies (e.g., type, dosage and duration of supplementation) involved in this study compared to the previously reported studies. This study population is composed mainly of Orang Asli schoolchildren with distinct dietary patterns and cultural practices that may influence gut microbiota composition25. Additionally, environmental exposures, sanitation practices, helminthic infection, and genetic factors unique to this population could contribute to the observed microbiota variations26–28. These aspects may partially explain the contradictory findings in comparison to earlier studies.

Based on differential abundance analysis, the 6-month supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits resulted in a significant higher abundance of potentially beneficial ASVs as compared to the control group, for instance those belonging to Christensenellaceae_R-7_group (family Christensenellaceae), Anaerostipes, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, family Atopobiaceae (phylum Actinobacteriota), Butyricicoccus, Enterorhabdus, Desulfovibrio_piger, Colidextribacter, Oscillibacter, Ruminococcus, UCG-010, Family_XIII_AD3011_group, Treponema_succinifaciens and Succinivibrio (family Succinivibrionaceae), as well as Slackia_isoflavoniconvertens. A high abundance of Christensenellaceae is associated with healthy metabolic status in children29 and protective effects against cardiovascular and metabolic diseases30. Anaerostipes, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, UCG-010 and many members of Atopobiaceae family have also been reported to enhance the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are known to have anti-inflammatory properties in the human gut31. As a major SFCA-producing bacteria, Treponema_succinifaciens was commonly found in traditional rural populations, and its abundance was negatively correlated to plasma inflammatory cytokines, circulating biomarkers of gut epithelial barrier damage, and microbial dislocation32. Butyricicoccus, Enterorhabdus and Desulfovibrio_piger were previously reported to be negatively associated with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)33–35. The enhancement in Enterorhabdus was also found to have protective effects against ulcerative colitis (UC), with significant improvement in intestinal barrier integrity and ameliorated inflammation36. Both genera Colidextribacter and Oscillibacter were found to be positively correlated with anti-inflammatory metabolites37,38. In addition, the Ruminococcus and family Succinivibrionaceae were found to possess protective roles in gut inflammation and barrier integrity39,40. Slackia_isoflavoniconvertens is one of the few characterised intestinal bacteria isolated from humans that forms equol, an isoflavonoid metabolite, known to exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties41.

While palm olein (PO) may influence gut microbiota through its fatty acid and phytonutrient content, no studies have directly assessed its microbiota-modulating effects. In this study, since the only difference between the RPO- and PO-enriched biscuits was carotenoid content, observed differences in gut microbiota composition are likely attributable to the high carotenoid levels in RPO rather than PO itself. At 6 months of supplementation, several ASVs belonging to Lactococcus_garvieae and Clostridium_perfringens, genus Weissella, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, [Ruminococcus]_gnavus_group, Blautia, Megamonas, as well as family Enterobacteriaceae were significantly more abundant in the control group as compared to the experimental group. They were observed to be either pathobionts (opportunistic pathogens) or potentially harmful bacteria that are involved in human infections, such as bacteraemia and endocarditis, as well as associated with reduced levels of SCFAs, increased intestinal inflammation, or disrupted intestinal epithelium integrity42,43. It is also important to note that the majority of these ASVs, including Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Blautia, Megamonas, and Enterobacteriaceae, remained persistently more differentially abundant in the control group than in the experimental group at both 3-month and 6-month timepoints, indicating their sustained presence and possible role in the gut microbiota of this group. In contrast, their relatively lower abundance in the experimental group at 6-month suggests a potential protective effects of RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation, which may be attributed to the bioactive phytonutrients in RPO, particularly its high carotenoid content, which could help create a gut environment less conducive to the proliferation of these potentially harmful bacteria. Interestingly, Clostridia_UCG-014 (pylum Firmicutes) exhibited a noteworthy shift from being significantly abundant in the experimental group at the 3-month, to be more significantly abundant in the control group following 6-month supplementation. This genus has been reported to be pro-inflammatory and may compromise gut barrier integrity44.

However, it is important to acknowledge the potential complexities associated with the microbial shifts observed in both groups after 6 months of supplementation. For instance, although Desulfovibrio piger, which was more abundant in the experimental group, has been identified as having protective effects against IBD34, some studies suggest that Desulfovibrio species may promote mucosal inflammation due to their production of toxic metabolites, such as hydrogen sulphide45. The roles of Ruminococcus and Succinivibrionaceae in gut health remain debated, as certain species within these groups have been implicated in both protective and pro-inflammatory pathways46,47. Interestingly, ASVs belonging to Anaerostipes, a genus with known anti-inflammatory properties31, exhibited greater enrichment in the control group at 3-month, but were more abundant in the experimental group at 6-month, highlighting the complexity and dynamic nature of microbial shifts over time. Some genera that were more differentially abundant in the control group, such as Blautia and Weissella, despite being classified as either pathobionts or potentially harmful bacteria, have also demonstrated beneficial effects under certain conditions48,49.

Interestingly, our findings reveal that the changes in gut microbiota after RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation are positively associated with improvements in several micronutrient and haematological markers, including provitamin A carotenoids (alpha- and beta-carotenes), vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol), PCV, and MCV. The increases in alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, and alpha-tocopherol levels alongside the shifts in microbial composition suggest the gut microbiota may enhance the absorption and transport of these antioxidants, which likely to be driven by the changes in gut barrier function and metabolic cross-talk50. Specifically, the enrichment of genera such as Anaerostipes, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and UCG-010 may contribute to enhanced host nutrient status through their roles in SCFA production and modulation of gut barrier integrity, which have been shown to improve nutrient absorption and mitigate systemic inflammation31,51. Improved nutrient uptake may also support better iron absorption, as supported by our earlier findings14, contributing to better haemoglobin production and red blood cell maturation, as reflected in the higher PCV and MCV values. Furthermore, the positive correlations between Collinsella and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group with beta-carotene in this study are consistent with previous in vitro study, which showed that beta-carotene treatment could increase the abundance of Lachnospiraceae, and low and medium doses of beta-carotene enhanced Collinsella abundance52. These findings collectively suggest the potential relationships between gut microbial dynamics and micronutrient bioavailability as well as haematological health. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate the causal relationships and functional contributions of these taxa to micronutrient metabolism and haematological outcomes.

Predicted functional analysis based on PICRUSt2 revealed a significantly higher number of functional pathways associated with metabolism in the experimental group compared to the control group after 6-month supplementation. SCFAs, such as acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are the products of the anaerobic fermentation of carbohydrates and amino acids53. They play important roles in regulating gut barrier function, liver metabolism, kidney function, and immune balance in the peripheral systems54. In the current study, a number of metabolism-associated functional pathways related to the increased production of SCFAs, namely metabolism of propanoate, butanoate/butyrate, fructose, mannose, starch, sucrose, pyruvate, pentose phosphate pathway, citrate cycle (TCA cycle) and glycolysis/ gluconeogenesis (all in the category of carbohydrate metabolism), as well as lysine biosynthesis, and metabolism of histidine, alanine, aspartate, glutamate, cysteine, serine, threonine and methionine (all in the category of amino acid metabolism), were significantly enhanced after 6-month supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits when compared to the control group53,55. These findings suggested that 6-month supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits could enhance the metabolic function of gut microbiota by increasing the release of SCFAs, which also further corroborates the findings of the differential abundance analysis, where a significantly greater enrichment of ASVs related to improved SCFA production was noted in the experimental group. Notably, an increased biosynthesis of bile acids, which are crucial for lipid and fat-soluble vitamin absorption, serving as novel signalling molecules that function as metabolic regulators, was observed following the supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits56. This observation may suggest an improved absorption of carotenoids and other lipid-soluble phytonutrients that are found abundantly in the RPO-enriched biscuits. Besides, bile acids are known to play critical roles in intestinal innate immunity and are associated with anti-inflammatory responses via bile acid-activated receptors57.

Furthermore, the present study highlights that 6-month supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits may exert potential modulatory effect on microbial network architecture of vitamin A-deficient children. The experimental group exhibited a significant increase in network connectivity, as reflected by a greater than 2-fold increase in edge count at the 6-month compared to the baseline level. Conversely, the control group displayed a reduction in the number of edges at 3-month, which subsequently rebounded to near-baseline level by the end of supplementation, implying a transient fluctuation rather than a sustained shift in microbial connectivity. The experimental group also displayed significantly higher average degree and clustering coefficient values, along with a lower average path length after 3-month and 6-month supplementation, further supporting the presence of a more interconnected and locally cohesive microbial network compared to the control group. It has been shown that networks with greater connectivity tend to be more stable, suggesting that stronger interspecific interactions could strengthen the community stability of gut microbiota58. This stability ensures that beneficial symbionts and their associated functions are maintained over time, indicating that RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation could contribute to supporting gut health in the study population. Moreover, after 3-month and 6-month supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits, the composition of keystone taxa differed significantly between the groups, with a greater number of keystone taxa observed in the experimental group compared to the control group at both timepoints. Keystone taxa are the highly connected taxa that contribute greatly to the stability of a microbial community, and as drivers of microbial structure and functioning of its ecosystem59. These findings collectively suggest that 6-month RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation may promote a more interconnected, stable, and diverse microbial ecosystem. Among the identified keystone taxa, both network hubs and modular hubs play a disproportionally crucial role in shaping microbial communities, by connecting the nodes within the entire network or modules extensively60. Notably, the majority of the module hubs and network hubs for both groups consistently belonged to several similar genera throughout the supplementation period, except for Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group (ASV217) and Lactobacillus_ruminis (ASV14360), which were newly identified as module hubs in the experimental group at the 6-month endpoint. Both of these nodes are considered potentially beneficial taxa that were previously reported to be positively correlated with SCFA production, and these findings further support the outcomes of the differential abundance and functional analyses61.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first multi-school supplementation programme involving RPO conducted in Malaysia, which sheds light on the potential modulatory effects of the consumption of RPO-enriched biscuits on the gut health of children with VAD. Besides, the longitudinal nature of this study imparts additional strength to the study, enabling the examination of gut microbiota patterns over a duration of 6 months subsequent to the initiation of the supplementation. Nevertheless, there are several limitations present in this study. One of these is the absence of immunological markers to elucidate the underlying mechanisms responsible for the effects of RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation on gut microbiota. Therefore, future studies would greatly benefit from the inclusion of immunological markers, as this would shed light on the mechanistic pathways underlying the impact of RPO on the host immune system in modulating gut microbiota. It is important to note that the study’s taxonomic resolution is inherently limited due to the use of 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which lacks species-level resolution and direct functional characterisation of the gut microbiota. The identification of microbial taxa at the genus or higher levels restricts definitive conclusions regarding the specific functional roles of individual strains. This limitation underscores the necessity for further metagenomic and metabolomic studies to establish causal relationships between these microbiota changes and human health outcomes. In addition, several studies have reported the significant impacts of albendazole treatment on gut microbiota62,63. Martin et al.64 established that while the overall microbiome composition remained unaffected by albendazole, a significant alteration in Bacteriodetes was observed among subjects who received albendazole and remained infected with soil-transmitted helminth. Hence, the administration of albendazole prior to the commencement of the supplementation programme in the present study has the potential to induce alterations in the gut microbiota of the subjects. However, it should be noted that all the subjects, irrespective of their assigned group, underwent deworming treatment, which therefore may have normalised the effects of deworming. Besides, similar changes in observed ASVs were found in the study participants from Sarawak who received only one dose of albendazole treatment (Fig. S1). It is suggested that future studies incorporate an additional group of subjects who do not receive deworming treatment, to further provide deeper insights into the potential confounding effects associated with the deworming treatment.

Another limitation of this study lies in the use of school-level rather than individual randomisation, implemented to reduce the likelihood of intervention contamination, for instance, the sharing of biscuits among schoolchildren. While this approach facilitated logistical coordination and likely improved adherence to the intervention, it may have introduced potential confounding due to inter-school differences in socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, or baseline nutritional profiles. Nonetheless, in the context of this study, such variation was predominantly associated with school category (Orang Asli versus non-Orang Asli), as all participating schools in each state were drawn from the same geographic region with relatively similar socioeconomic and dietary backgrounds. To mitigate potential bias arising from this design, school category was included as a covariate in the statistical analyses. A high drop-out rate (49.6%) represents another limitation, mainly attributed to challenges in sample collection, which is often perceived as inconvenient or uncomfortable by both children and caregivers. Also, the limited collection window of 3 to 5 days per visit may be insufficient for participants to consistently provide samples at every timepoint, especially when scheduled collection days are missed due to illness, constipation or school commitments. Additionally, as the schoolchildren were given biscuits for home consumption during school holidays, there was a possibility of sharing with family members. However, to minimise the risk, parents and guardians received a detailed briefing before the study commenced, stressing the necessity of exclusive consumption by the participants. Besides, most of the supplementation occurred under teacher supervision at school, as the study timeline was structured to minimise long school holidays. Therefore, any unintended sharing of biscuits is unlikely to have had a substantial impact on the study outcomes.

In summary, the supplementation of RPO-enriched biscuits in vitamin A-deficient primary school children for 6 months was associated with a significantly higher abundance of potentially beneficial gut bacteria, which are positively associated with improved micronutrient and haematological status. Network analysis further suggests that supplementation promotes greater connectivity and stability within the gut microbial ecosystem. These findings highlight the potential benefits of RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation in modulating the gut microbiota in vitamin A-deficient primary schoolchildren, particularly those from Indigenous communities. Further research involving diverse populations and settings, with the integration of metagenomic and metabolomic approaches, is warranted to confirm these findings and assess their applicability to national nutritional intervention programmes.

Methods

Ethical approval and consent

The approval for all procedures involving human subjects in this study was obtained from the Medical Research Ethics Committee, Ministry of Health (MOH) Malaysia (NMRR No: NMRR-16-1905-32547) on 26th January 2018. The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov under identifier NCT03256123 on 21st August 2017. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before the study began, all literate parents or guardians provided written informed consent. Verbal consent was obtained from illiterate parents or guardians, with their thumbprints documented on the consent forms. All verbal consent was formally witnessed and documented. Schoolchildren themselves provided their assent to participate after obtaining informed consent from their legal guardians or parents. All participants received compensation for their time and participation in the study. The compensation consisted of monetary allowance, gifts, and food, which was approved by the ethics committee and intended to cover travel expenses and encourage study completion.

Study design

The primary study was a 6-month double-blinded randomised controlled trial investigating the effects of RPO-enriched biscuit supplementation on blood biochemical status, particularly vitamin A levels of vitamin A-deficient primary schoolchildren14. The sample size for the primary study was determined based on the study among schoolchildren in Burkina Faso which evaluated the changes in serum retinol concentration following 1-year RPO supplementation13. Participant recruitment was carried out between November 2017 and January 2018. The follow-up period extended from January 2018 to November 2018, with a total study duration of approximately 13 months for data collection. Schoolchildren were recruited if they met the following criteria: aged 7-11 years old during the screening phase, diagnosed with confirmed VAD (retinol concentration <0.70 µmol/L) or marginal VAD (defined by retinol concentration 0.70 to <1.05 µmol/L65), and not physically handicapped. Exclusion criteria included the presence of oedema (including severe acute malnutrition or gastrointestinal disorders), allergic to wheat and/or gluten-containing foods, and current enrolment in primary six14,18. In this study, the outcomes related to the gut microbiota were explored as secondary analyses and the sample size was based on that of the primary study.

The participants were recruited from ten rural national schools across five states (Pahang, Perak, Johor, Sabah and Sarawak) in Malaysia14,19,66. The study sites were grouped according to schools, where the experimental schools within each state, the experimental schools received RPO-enriched biscuits while the control schools were given PO-enriched biscuits, four days weekly for a 6-month duration. Randomisation was performed using a computer-generated permutation list, and the students from different schools were matched according to their VAD status, age, sex, and STH infection status. Personnel responsible for distributing the biscuits and processing or analysing the biological samples were blinded to the group assignments. The blinding process was managed by an independent third party (the biscuit manufacturer), who was not involved in the study.

Participants in the experimental group received RPO-enriched biscuits containing substantially higher total carotene content (4.6 mg per 36 g serving) compared to PO-enriched biscuits (0.2 mg per 36 g serving). Schoolchildren in the experimental group received roughly 326.3 µg of vitamin A per day, measured as retinol equivalents (REs), on a weekly basis, thereby meeting approximately 59.3% of the Recommended Nutrient Intakes (RNI) for vitamin A among children aged 7-12 years old67. The detailed nutritional composition of RPO- and PO-enriched biscuits is shown in Supplementary Data 1. As majority of the chosen region exhibited high rates of STH infections18,68, all subjects received deworming treatment. The treatment consisted of three doses of 10 mL Vemizol suspension (Pharmaniaga Berhad, Shah Alam, Malaysia), each 5 mL containing 200 mg of albendazole, administered once daily for three successive days before supplementation initiation. This is an exception for the schoolchildren in Sarawak, who received only a single dose due to the absence of STH infection during the screening. Subjects excluded from the analysis were those either did not complete the entire deworming regimen, received less than 70% of supplementation, or both. A total of 338 subjects (146 boys and 182 girls), aged from 8 to 12 years old (at the initiation of supplementation), were included in the final analysis. The primary outcome of the study was the alteration in gut microbiota composition, while secondary outcomes were the changes in gut microbiota diversity, correlations with blood biomarkers, and microbial network structure.

Stool sample collection and DNA extraction

Stool samples were collected utilising labelled wide-mouth containers with screw-caps at baseline, 3-month and 6-month of supplementation. Immediately after collection, the samples were aliquoted into 1.5 mL cryovials and promptly stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. DNA extraction from the homogenised stool samples was conducted employing the NucleoSpin Soil kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL GmbH & Co. KG, Düren, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Evaluation of both the quality and quantity of the extracted DNA was carried out using the Implen NanoPhotometer® N60/N50 (Implen, CA, USA), Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. The extracted DNA samples were then kept at −20 °C until subjected to 16S rRNA sequencing.

Blood biomarker assessments

Fasting blood samples (approximately 6 mL) were collected by trained nurses and medical assistants from the MOH at baseline and 6-month of supplementation. To prevent light exposure, the samples were covered with aluminium foil and kept in an icebox surrounded by ice. Haematological parameters included RBC count, haemoglobin, PCV, MCV and MCH, were measured using an automated haematology analyser (Sysmex XN-2000, Kobe, Japan). The remaining blood samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 minutes to separate serum and plasma, which were subsequently stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Plasma concentrations of retinol, alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, and alpha-tocopherol were determined by reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent 1260 Infinity, US), following the method described by Kand’ár et al. 69 with minor modifications as outlined by Loganathan et al.70. RBP4 was measured using quantitative sandwich enzyme immunoassay (R&D Systems, USA). Serum ferritin, iron and hs-CRP were analysed using ADVIA 2400 Chemistry System and Centaur XP (Siemens). The haematological and biological analyses, including serum iron, ferritin, and hs-CRP were performed at an accredited clinical laboratory (Pathology & Clinical Laboratory (M) Sdn. Bhd., Malaysia).

Amplification and 16S rRNA paired-end amplicon sequencing

The amplification of the fourth hypervariable (V4) region of the 16S rRNA gene, was performed on the extracted DNA samples using primers set 515-F (5’ –GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA – 3’) and 806-R (5’ – GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT – 3’), following a modified method from Caporaso et al.71. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out using the Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, United States) on the Bio-Rad T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules CA, USA). The protocol for thermal cycling initiated with a one-minute initial denaturation step at 98 °C, proceeded by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C lasting 10 seconds, annealing at 50 °C for 30 seconds, and elongation at 72 °C for one minute.

The PCR products underwent agarose gel electrophoresis (1.7%) for detection. The samples which displayed bright main strips in the range of 400 to 450 bp were chosen for subsequent analysis. After pooling the PCR products were pooled at equidensity ratios, the samples underwent purification using the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Subsequently, the sequencing libraries were prepared utilising the NEBNext® UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, United States) following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. The library quality was evaluated utilising the Qubit dsDNA Broad-Range assay kit (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) on the Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) and quantitative PCR (qPCR). Next, amplicon sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System (Illumina, San Diego CA, USA) at the Novogene Co., Ltd., ensuring a minimum of 60,000 raw paired-end reads per sample, each with a read length of 250 bp, was generated. The sequencing of all samples was conducted in one single run to minimise potential batch effects.

Pre-processing of amplicon sequencing data

The initial quality control of the demultiplexed sequencing reads with barcode and primer sequences removed for 16S rRNA V4 hypervariable region was carried out using the FastQC software (v0.11.8). Subsequently, the Illumina adapter sequences (read length <130 bp) were removed using the Trimmomatic tool v0.39 to reduce noise. After data assessment, the filtered reads were imported into QIIME 2 (Qualitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2) (v2021.8) and subjected to denoising using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2). The denoising process involved the application of specific parameters: –p-trim-left-f 0 –p-trim-left-r 0 –p-trunc-len-f 135 –p-trunc-len-r 135. These procedures resulted in the generation of a set of representative sequences, known as ASVs, for downstream analyses.

Taxonomic classification and bioinformatics analyses

ASVs were taxonomically assigned utilising the SILVA v138 database at 99% identity. The ASV feature and taxonomy tables were imported into the RStudio (R v4.1.2, Vienna, Austria) for the subsequent bioinformatics and statistical analyses. ASV counts were aggregated to the phylum and genus levels to visualise the microbiota composition by groups at all three timepoints in a stacked barplot, using the ggplot function from ggplot2 package (v3.4.0).

For alpha diversity (intra-sample diversity) indices including observed ASVs, chao1, Shannon diversity index, and Simpson index, calculations were performed on the raw datasets utilising the estimate_richness function from the phyloseq package (v1.40.0). The assessment of beta diversity (inter-sample diversity) indices, including Bray-Curtis and Jaccard distances, was performed using the distance function from the phyloseq package (v1.40.0). Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was conducted on each beta diversity distance matrix using the pcoa function from the ade4 package (v1.7-30). Using the ggplot2 package (v3.4.0), the first two principal axes were plotted, and the percentage of variance explained by each axis was shown in the resulting plot.

PICRUSt2 (v2.4.1) implemented in QIIME 2 was used for predicting the functional metagenomes of microbial communities in the samples. The abundance of KEGG orthologs (KOs) was subsequently computed for each sample. Microbial co-abundance network analysis was conducted at the ASV level, and separately for each group and timepoint, using the microeco package (v1.2.2) to explore the longitudinal change in the associations between gut microbiota following supplementation. Prior to the network analysis, ASVs presented in less than 30% of samples and with relative abundance of less than 0.01% in all samples were excluded. SparCC (Sparse Correlations for Compositional data) correlation coefficients between pairwise ASVs were calculated based on 100 iterations, and only correlations with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH)-adjusted P values < 0.01 and with |R | > 0.5 were kept. The fast-greedy modularity optimisation algorithm (cluster_fast_greedy) was used for the detection of community or module, followed by the computation of network attributes, including number of nodes and edge, average degree, clustering coefficient and number of keystone taxa. The determination of topological role for each node relied on the threshold values of within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi): module hubs (Zi > 2.5), network hubs (Zi > 2.5 and Pi > 0.62), connectors (Zi ≤ 2.5 and Pi > 0.62), and peripherals (Zi <2.5 and Pi <0.62). Module hubs, network hubs and connectors are considered as keystone taxa of the networks due to their important roles in network topology72. Network visualisation was performed in Gephi (v0.10.1)73.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were presented as either means with standard deviations (SD) or with 95% confidence intervals (CI), whereas categorical variables were expressed as numbers alongside their respective percentages. Student’s t-test was applied to examine the differences in the baseline relative abundance of the top 10 bacterial taxa at phyla and genera levels between the experimental and control groups.

To evaluate the effects of supplementation over time on gut microbiome composition, beta diversity metrics (Bray-Curtis and Jaccard distances) were analysed across timepoints using PERMANOVA via the adonis2 function (999 permutations) from the vegan package (v2.6.4). In the model, we included the group (experimental or control), timepoint, and their interaction term (group x timepoint), along with additional covariates (state, school category, sex, age and baseline body mass index-for-age z-score (BAZ)) (Supplementary Note 1). Each term was tested marginally using by = “margin” to estimate its independent contribution to variation in microbial composition. To account for repeated measures within participants, stratified permutations (n = 999) were used with blocking applied via setBlocks() based on the subject identifier (SubjectCode). To explore the longitudinal changes in gut microbiota composition within individuals, the “first distance”, which represents the pairwise dissimilarities (Bray-Curtis and Jaccard distance) between consecutive samples (e.g. baseline vs 3-month, baseline vs 6-month) for each subject, was calculated following Bokulich et al. 74. This approach reduces the data’s dimensionality into a single vector of first distances, enabling the analysis of longitudinal changes in beta diversity using techniques such as Linear Mixed Model (LMM), which cannot be directly applied to distance matrices.

The effects of the supplementation on the alpha and beta diversities were assessed by introducing either alpha diversity indices or first distances (for beta diversity) as dependent variables in a LMM. The model was fitted using Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML), a method which provides unbiased parameter estimation for longitudinal data by focusing on the variability that remains after accounting for fixed effects. This analysis was conducted utilising the lme function from the nlme package in R. The model incorporated group, timepoint, their interaction (group and timepoint), state, school category, sex, age and baseline BAZ value as fixed effects, whereas the participant was included as a random effect (Supplementary Note 2). To evaluate the statistical significance of group x timepoint interaction term, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using the function anova.lme from the nlme package. Subsequently, pairwise comparisons were performed using the emmeans function from the emmeans package, and the resulting P value was adjusted using Bonferroni correction. A subgroup analysis was performed to assess the effects of supplementation on the observed ASVs within each state, in order to verify the robustness of the outcomes.

The ASVs and KOs (generated from PICRUSt2) with prevalence of less than 30% and relative abundance under 0.01% across all samples were filtered. To address the compositional nature of the microbiota data, relative abundances of the remaining ASVs and KOs were transformed using centred log-ratio (clr). This approach normalises the compositional data by transforming the relative abundance of each ASVs or KOs into a log-ratio relative to the geometric mean of all features in the same sample. To identify the differentially abundant ASVs and KOs between the experimental and control groups after 3- and 6-month supplementation, ANCOVA was conducted using the clr-transformed values as the dependent variable using the lm function from the stats package in R (Supplementary Note 3). The model also adjusted for baseline abundance and other covariates (group, state, school category, sex, age and baseline BAZ value). To correct for multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the BH procedure, with statistically significance defined as an adjusted P value less than 0.05.

To explore the relationships between the changes in gut microbial taxa abundances and clinical blood biomarker levels from baseline and 6-month of supplementation, Spearman’s rank-based correlation tests were performed on the delta changes (6-month minus baseline) of clr-transformed abundances for 48 significant ASVs identified as differentially abundant in the experimental and control groups at 6-month, and the corresponding delta changes of 12 selected blood biomarkers (i.e. RBP4, retinol, alpha-tocopherol, alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, RBC count, haemoglobin, PCV, MCV, MCH, ferritin, and hs-CRP) related to vitamin A and antioxidant status, iron metabolism and systemic inflammation in each participant. Spearman correlations were conducted using the cor.test function from the stats package in R. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the BH corrections. Only correlations with adjusted P values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Significant correlations were visualised in a heatmap generated using the Heatmap function from the ComplexHeatmap package (v2.24.1) in R.

Differential co-abundance network analysis was conducted to compare microbial interaction patterns between the experimental and control groups throughout the 6-month supplementation period. Statistical differences in microbial network topological properties, including average degree, average path length, and clustering coefficient between groups at each timepoint were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The P values and effect sizes were calculated using the wilcox_test and wilcox_effsize functions from the rstatix package (v0.7.2), respectively, to indicate the statistical significance and magnitude of group differences. To assess the similarity of keystone taxa sets between the experimental and control groups at each timepoint, Jaccard index was computed using the calcJaccard function from the NetCoMi package75. The Jaccard index ranges from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap), reflecting the proportion of shared keystone taxa. Additionally, the probability P(J ≤ j) estimates the likelihood that the observed Jaccard index (j) is less than or equal to a given value under the null expectation. A P(J ≤ j) value < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant dissimilarity between the keystone taxa sets of the two groups.
