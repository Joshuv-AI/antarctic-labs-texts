Indole-3-lactic acid protects the gut vascular barrier following intestinal ischemia injury through AhR/Nrf2/STAT3 mediated claudin 2 downregulation
Authors: Yifan Jia, Muchen He, Fan Wang, Yaqing Zhan, Qiwen Deng, Jiantong Shen, Xiwen Wang, Qi Ran, Wenqi Huang, Yihong Ling, Shihong Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsDysfunction of the intestinal epithelial barrier (IEB) and gut vascular barrier (GVB) contributes to the development of intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (IR) injury. Tryptophan (TRP), an essential amino acid, plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, yet its regulatory effects on the GVB following IR remain unexplored. We aimed to better define the role of TRP in intestinal IR in vivo and in vitro.MethodsMice underwent intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (IR) and were fed control, TRP-recommended (TRP-r), or TRP-sufficient (TRP-s) diets. Fecal metagenomic sequencing analyzed microbial composition, and targeted metabolomics quantified tryptophan and its metabolites in intestinal and serum samples. ILA’s effects on barrier integrity were assessed via tight junction protein expression and FITC-dextran permeability assays. RNA sequencing of intestinal endothelial cells elucidated mechanisms by which ILA modulated GVB function. The STAT3-claudin 2 relationship was validated in vitro by ChIP-qPCR.ResultsTRP supplementation significantly reshaped the gut microbiota, mitigated tissue damage and enhanced the integrity of both the IEB and GVB. Indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), a key tryptophan metabolite, was identified as an important factor in preserving GVB function. Mechanistically, our results show that the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR)/Nrf2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway is essential for ILA-mediated improvement of GVB integrity and downregulation of the pore-forming protein claudin 2.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the dual role of ILA in reinforcing both IEB and GVB functions and shed light on the molecular mechanisms underlying ILA’s GVB-protective effects. This study implicates that ILA or other AhR-activating metabolites may serve as promising pharmacological agents for alleviating IR-induced intestinal damage.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02454-y.Keywords: Intestine, Ischemia Injury, Gut Vascular Barrier, Tryptophan Metabolism

Introduction

Intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (IR) injury is a severe clinical condition with high mortality, often occurring after acute mesenteric ischemia, shock, and organ transplantation [1, 2]. Central to this pathological process is the disruption of the intestinal mechanical barrier [3], which consists of multiple protective layers: the mucus barrier, the intestinal epithelial barrier (IEB), and the recently identified gut vascular barrier (GVB) [4]. The GVB, acting as the final defense line to prevent the translocation of bacteria and their metabolites from entering into the portal circulation, is primarily composed of endothelial cells (ECs), surrounded by enteric glial cells and pericytes [5, 6]. Enteric ECs have tight junctions (TJs) including ZO1, occludin, claudins and adherent junctions (AJs), like β-catenin and VE-cadherin [7, 8]. The protein plasmalemma vesicle-associated 1 (PV1) has been confirmed to be a marker of EC permeability [9]. We previously documented that GVB disruption occurred following intestinal IR [10] and gut-derived sepsis [11]; however, the underlying mechanisms and treatment approaches are still unexplored.

Recent studies have showed that disorders in Tryptophan (TRP) metabolism are strongly associated with various intestinal diseases [12–14]. TRP in the gut is metabolized via three major pathways: the kynurenine pathway, the serotonin pathway mediated by the host and the indole pathway mediated by the gut microbiota [12, 14, 15]. Furthermore, many TRP metabolites, such as indole and its derivatives have been identified as ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), a ligand-activated transcription factor that plays a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal barrier integrity, regulating intestinal homeostasis and mediating hosts’ immune response [16]. Increasing evidence reveals that certain TRP metabolites, like indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), activate AhR-Nuclear factor erythroid 2-realated factor (Nrf2) signaling, subsequently reducing intestinal epithelial cells oxidative stress and promoting intestinal stem cells proliferation [17]. Additionally, indole-3-aldehyde (I3A) has been shown to activate the AhR/IL-10/Wnt signaling pathway to ameliorate chemotherapy- and radiation-induced intestinal injury [18]. I3A also promotes intestinal mucin sulfation via the AhR-Papss2-Slc35b3 pathway, thereby supporting intestinal homeostasis [19]. In contrast, certain other TRP metabolites, such as indoxyl sulfate (IS), may exacerbate intestinal barrier dysfunction [20]. Gasdermin D has been reported to facilitate intestinal tumor development by increasing IL-1β release and Kynurenine production [21]. Since TRP metabolites were documented driven by higher substrate availability and not attributed to a change in the abundance of specific producer species [15], the direct effects of TRP on intestinal barriers following IR injury warrant further investigation.

Our study demonstrated that dietary TRP supplementation significantly improved IEB and GVB function following IR injury. Targeted metabolomics further identified ILA as a key mediator of these protective effects. While previous studies have predominantly focused on the effects of TRP and its metabolites on the IEB, there is a notable gap in research regarding their impact on the GVB. To address this, we utilized isolated intestinal endothelial cells and performed transcriptomic sequencing to deeply investigate the mechanisms by which TRP protected the GVB. Importantly, the AhR/Nrf2/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) axis was found to be essential for the GVB-protective effects of ILA. Overall, our results offer novel insights into the therapeutic mechanisms of TRP and its metabolites, providing potential new targets for the treatment of intestinal IR injury.

Results

Dietary TRP reshapes the composition of the gut microbiome in mice

Following the administration of different concentrations of TRP diets, we analyzed the gut microbiota in mice from the Con (0.1%), TRP-r (0.2%), and TRP-s (0.5%) groups using metagenomic sequencing. PCoA, PCA and NMDS revealed the similarities and differences in gut microbial composition between the groups. PCoA1 (57.53%) and PCoA2 (19.48%) (Fig. 1A) separated the three groups into two distinct clusters. Notably, the TRP-r and TRP-s groups demonstrated short inter-sample distances, indicating greater similarity in microbial community structures, whereas both differed substantially from the Con group. Similarly, PCA1 (74.37%) and PCA2 (12.98%) (Fig. 1B) highlighted the significant differences in gut microbiota composition. These results indicate that the TRP-r and TRP-s groups shared broadly similar microbial taxonomic profiles across multiple classification levels, yet both groups exhibited marked divergence from the Con group. Additionally, the results of NMDS analysis (stress = 0.07, Fig. 1C) further distinguished among these three differing groups. Anosim relative quantitative analysis (Fig. 1D) further confirmed that the dietary TRP-induced changes in gut microbiota composition were statistically significant (R = 0.717, P = 0.001), with no significant difference observed between the TRP-r and TRP-s groups. At the phylum level (Fig. 1E), the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio (Fig. 1H) was significantly increased in the TRP-s group compared to the Con group. At the genus level (Fig. 1F), several key genera capable of metabolizing TRP into indole and indole-like derivates, including Clostridium (Fig. 1I), Anaerotruncus (Fig. 1J), and Bifidobacterium (Fig. 1K) [13,15], were significantly enriched in the TRP-s group. Additionally, the relative abundance of microbiota at other taxonomic levels was also significantly altered after dietary TRP supplementation (Figure S1, A-D). Pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 1G and Figure S1E) indicated that amino acid biosynthesis and metabolic pathways were enhanced in the TRP-s group compared to the Con group. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis (Fig. 1L) revealed significant enrichment of the phenylalanine, tyrosine, and TRP biosynthesis pathways in the TRP-s group compared to the Con group. Importantly, following the intestinal sham operation(Figure S1F), pathological staining of small intestine sample from different groups showed that diets containing varying concentrations of TRP did not significantly alter intestinal mucosal structure (Fig. 1, M and N), nor were there significant effects on body weight changes (Figure S1G) and intestinal inflammatory responses (Figure S1, H–K). Thus, we conclude that dietary TRP leads to significant alterations in gut microbiome composition, without affecting growth, gut inflammation, or intestinal barrier function in mice.

Dietary TRP ameliorates intestinal IR damage by improving dual gut barrier impairment

Following intestinal IR challenged, the pathological staining of the small intestine from different groups revealed that TRP supplementation markedly alleviated intestinal damage (Fig. 2, A and B) and reduced the Chiu’s scores (Fig. 2C), with the most pronounced effects observed in the TRP-s + IR group. Additionally, serum iFABP levels, a biomarker for intestinal injury, were notably reduced in the TRP-r + IR and TRP-s + IR groups compared to the Con + IR group (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, TRP feeding markedly decreased the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (CXCL1, CXCL2, IL-6, and IL-1β) in the intestinal tissues post-IR injury, with the extent of reduction being concentration-dependent (Fig. 2, E and F). In contrast, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 mRNA levels were significantly elevated following TRP supplementation (Fig. 2F). To evaluate the role of dietary TRP on IEB function after IR injury, we assessed the mRNA expression of several TJs, including ZO1, Occludin, Cldn1, Cldn3, Cldn4 and Cldn5. As shown in Fig. 2G, mRNA levels of these TJs were significantly upregulated in the TRP-r + IR and TRP-s + IR groups compared to the Con + IR group. Additionally, as a sign of epithelial damage, we detected significantly increased tight junction protein ZO1 on epithelial cells in the TRP-r + IR and TRP-s + IR groups, whereas it was downregulated in the Con + IR group (Fig. 2, H and I). A reliable marker of GVB disruption is the accessibility of PV1, an integral membrane protein associated to the diaphragms of endothelial fenestrae [5, 22]. As shown in Fig. 2 J and K, the colocalization of PV1/CD34 was significantly reduced in the ileum of TRP-r + IR and TRP-s + IR mice compared to the Con + IR mice. Western blotting further confirmed the similar scenario in the TRP-r + IR and TRP-s + IR groups (Fig. 2, L and M). To assess the functional impacts of GVB damage, we evaluated the translocation of larger molecules across the vascular barrier by measuring the levels of FD70 in liver and plasma. The data showed that the FD70 density in liver (Fig. 2, N and O) and plasma (Fig. 2P) was significantly lower in the TRP-r + IR and TRP-s + IR groups compared to the Con + IR group, indicative of a reduced gut permeability. Notably, the Redundancy analysis (RDA, Fig. 2, Q and R) indicated that the genera Clostridium, Anaerotruncus, and Bifidobacterium were negatively correlated with markers of intestinal damage and barrier dysfunction (iFABP, CXCL1, CXCL2, IL-6, IL-1β and FD70 in plasma), and positively correlated with protective indictors that alleviate intestinal damage and enhance gut barrier function (IL-10, ZO1, Occludin, Cldn1, Cldn3, Cldn4 and Cldn5). This suggests that the enrichment of these genera and their metabolites following dietary TRP supplementation may contribute to the protective effects on intestinal tissues, IEB and GVB against IR injury. In conclusion, these results imply that dietary TRP facilitates tissue damage following intestinal IR injury by enhancing both the IEB and GVB function. Based on the dose-dependent effects of TRP on the alleviation of intestinal damage and the restoration of intestinal barrier function, the 0.5% TRP diet (TRP-s) was selected for the subsequent experiments.

TRP-sufficient diet facilitates ILA production and protects against intestinal IR injury via the AhR pathway

To delve deeper into the relationship between TRP-sufficient diet metabolites derived by gut microbiota and their protective effects on the intestine following IR challenge, we performed targeted TRP metabolomics of serum from mice in the Con + S, Con + IR, and TRP-s + IR groups (Fig. 3A). As shown in Figure S2, metabolites from the kynurenine (Kyn) and serotonin pathways were significantly increased in the serum of TRP-s + IR mice, such as Kyn (Figure S2A), with the exception of kynurenic acid (KYNA, Figure S2B), which has been demonstrated to mitigate the severity of colitis by enhancing the proliferation of intestinal epithelia cells [23]. Meanwhile, levels of 5-HT (Figure S2C) and 5-HIAA (Figure S2D) were also markedly increased. It has been implied that IL-33-mediated 5-HT release promoted gut motility, subsequently preserving intestinal homeostasis [24]. Additionally, we observed the effects of TRP-sufficient supplementation on production of other TRP metabolites, including N-formylkynurenine (NFK, Figure S2E), picolinic acid (PLA, Figure S2F), cinnabarinic acid (CA, Figure S2G) and indole-3-acetaldehyde (IAAId, Figure S2H). Furthermore, the relevant abundance of significantly altered TRP metabolites in serum was presented in Fig. 3B and the levels of tryptophan (Fig. 3C) and indoleacetic acid (IAA, Fig. 3D) were considerably upregulated in TRP-s + IR group compared to Con + IR group, while an opposite tendency was observed in the level of indole-3-propionic (IPA, Fig. 3E) after sufficient TRP supplementation. Notably, compared with the Con + S and Con + IR mice, the TRP-s + IR mice exhibited about a fourfold increase of ILA levels in serum (Fig. 3F). Interestingly, spearman correlation analysis showed that the levels of TRP and ILA exhibited markedly positive correlation with parameters of reduced intestinal inflammatory levels including mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL10 and enhanced barrier function including mRNA levels of ZO1, Occludin, Cldn1, Cldn3, Cldn4, Cldn5 and FD70 density in plasma (Fig. 3G). This led us to hypothesize that among all dietary TRP metabolites, ILA, a proven natural ligand for the AhR receptor, may be the manifestly vital one in protecting the intestines from tissue damage, IEB breach and GVB leakage during IR injury.

To further elucidate the potential role of ILA in protecting against intestinal IR injury, we administered ILA orally for 7 consecutive days prior to inducing intestinal IR. Meanwhile, we simultaneously administered the specific AhR antagonist CH223191 alongside ILA (Fig. 3H). Our results showed that ILA administration significantly reduced tissue damage, as indicated by the restoration of intestinal epithelial structure (Fig. 3I), lower Chiu’s scores (Fig. 3J), and reduced iFABP levels (Fig. 3K). However, these protective effects were inhibited when CH223191 was administered. Consistently, ILA feeding significantly decreased the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (CXCL1, CXCL2, IL-6 and IL-1β) in the ileum, while increased the levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in the ILA + IR group compared to the Con + IR and ILA + CH223191 + IR groups (Fig. 3, L and M). Furthermore, ILA supplementation significantly improved the intestinal barrier, as indicated by increased mRNA expression of TJs, but was exacerbated with the AhR antagonist CH223191 (Fig. 3N). Immunofluorescence staining also showed ZO1 expression was enhanced in the IR model mice concomitantly treated with ILA compared to the Con + IR and ILA + CH223191 + IR groups (Fig. 3, O and P). Collectively, our results demonstrate that a TRP-sufficient diet facilitates the conversion of TRP into the indole derivative ILA, which protects the IEB from dysfunction induced by IR injury through AhR pathway activation.

ILA decreases vascular permeability by reducing claudin 2 expression in intestinal microvascular endothelial cells

To explore the protective effects of a TRP-sufficient diet on GVB function after intestinal IR injury, we specifically sorted intestinal microvascular endothelial cells from small intestines of mice in the Con + S, Con + IR, and TRP-s + IR groups for bulk RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 4A). The mRNA sequencing identified 2283 differentially expressed mRNAs in the TRP-s + IR group compared to the Con + IR group, with 456 upregulated and 1530 downregulated mRNAs. Notably, the mRNA level of claudin 2, a prototypical pore-forming protein, was found to be significantly downregulated in the TRP-s + IR group compared to the Con + IR group (Fig. 4B). GSEA enrichment analysis (Fig. 4C) indicated a significant enrichment and upregulation of the "cell adhesion molecules" pathway in the TRP-s + IR group. Furthermore, differential mRNA expression analysis in endothelial cells revealed that claudin 2 was enriched in KEGG pathways (Fig. 4D) related to cell adhesion molecules and tight junction, and in GO pathways (Fig. 4E) associated with cell-cell adhesion via plasma-membrane adhesion molecules, cell-cell adhesion, cell junction organization, and cell junction assembly.

To further functionally evaluate the impacts of ILA on GVB integrity, we assessed the colocalization of PV1/CD34, PV1 protein expression in the ileum, and the translocation of large molecules across the vascular barrier by measuring the accessibility of FD70 in plasma and liver. Interestingly, ILA supplementation inhibited the upregulation of PV1/CD34 colocalization (Fig. 4F) and PV1 protein expression (Fig. 4, G and H) observed in Con + IR mice, as well as markedly reduced the FD70 density in plasma (Fig. 4I) and liver (Fig. 4, J and K), suggesting enhanced GVB tightness. In corresponding to the bulk RNA-seq analysis of intestinal vascular endothelial cells, ILA administration significantly increased the mRNA expressions of several TJs, including claudin 1, 4, 5, 11 and 16 but did not alter claudin 3, 17, 24 and 25 levels (Figure S3, A-I). Furthermore, both mRNA and protein levels of claudin 2, the prototypical pore-forming protein, were significantly downregulated in the TRP-s + IR group compared to the Con + IR group (Fig. 4, L-N), suggesting reduced GVB integrity impairment. However, the protective effects of ILA supplementation were totally abolished by CH223191, evidenced by increased PV1/CD34 colocalization, PV1 protein expression, FD70 density in plasma and liver, as well as claudin 2 mRNA and protein levels. These results indicate that the indole derivative ILA serves as a pharmacologic treatment to protect GVB integrity by downregulating the pore-forming protein claudin 2 expression and enhancing the GVB tightness.

ILA reduces claudin 2 expression via activation of the AhR-Nrf2 pathway

To explore the mechanism by which ILA regulated claudin 2 expression in the GVB, we constructed a HR model in HUVECs to mimic the pathophysiological process of intestinal IR in vivo and treated cells with ILA (Fig. 5A). As the H2R4 group exhibited an approximately 50% reduction in cell viability (via CCK-8 assay), this reperfusion duration was selected for subsequent experiments (Figure S4A). Under conditions of 2 hours of hypoxia followed by 4 hours of reoxygenation, ILA treatment increased the cell viability significantly from 50% to 80% (Fig. 5C), reduced mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (CXCL1, CXCL2, IL-6 and IL-1β, Fig. 5, D and E), and elevated mRNA levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, we examined the vascular permeability in cultured HUVECs and found that HR exposure significantly increased the expressions of PV1 (Fig. 5, G and H) and claudin 2 (Fig. 5, I-K), both of which were suppressed by ILA treatment.

TRP is metabolized by gut bacteria into indole and indole derivatives that activate the AhR [25]. We observed that ILA treatment significantly increased AhR mRNA levels. To further explore the activation status of AhR signaling, we analyzed several well-defined AhR target genes. The results showed that Ahr, Ahrr, Cyp1a1 and Cyp1a2 mRNA levels were significantly higher in the ILA+HR group compared to the sham and HR groups (Fig. 5, L and M). The mRNA levels of other two notable AhR-dependent genes, Cyp1b1 and IL22, were also significantly higher in the ILA+HR group (Figure S4, D and E). AhR-Nrf2-dependent pathways activation has been reported to enhance intestinal epithelial TJs and mitigate colitis in pre-clinical models [26]. We therefore examined the effects of ILA on the Nrf2 pathway. As shown in Fig. 5, N-P, ILA treatment significantly increased Nrf2 mRNA levels, promoted Nrf2 translocation from the cytosol to the nucleus, and regulated the levels of downstream genes heme oxygenase (HO-1) and NAD(P)H dehydrogenase [quinone] 1 (NQO1) (Figure S4, B and C). HO-1, a well-known Nrf2-dependent gene, provides numerous protective benefits, including removal of toxic heme, protection against oxidative stress and regulation of apoptosis [27]. However, the protective effects of ILA were diminished by the AhR antagonist CH223191, indicating that the ILA’s beneficial actions were mediated by AhR-Nrf2 pathway activation. Since AhR and Nrf2 are transcriptional gene regulators, we further investigated claudin 2 protein expression following ILA treatment in Nrf2 knockdown HUVECs (Fig. 5B). As displayed in Figure S5D and E, pretreatment with Nrf2 siRNA significantly suppressed ILA-upregulated HO-1 and NQO1 mRNA levels in HUVECs under HR conditions. As a result, Nrf2 knockdown significantly abolished the protective effects of ILA, resulting in increased claudin 2 protein expression compared to the ILA+HR group (Fig. 5, Q-T). Collectively, our findings indicate that ILA downregulates claudin 2 expression through activation of the AhR-Nrf2 pathway. This pathway appears essential in mediating the protective effects of ILA on GVB integrity, suggesting a mechanistic link between AhR-Nrf2 pathway activation and reduced vascular permeability.

Nrf2-mediated STAT3 inactivation is essential for claudin 2 downregulation

Previous studies have shown that inhibiting STAT3 phosphorylation can protect the endothelial barrier and reduce vascular permeability [28, 29]. We evaluated the effect of ILA treatment on STAT3 activity and observed that ILA administration significantly reduced STAT3 phosphorylation at Tyr705 (pSTAT3Tyr705) in the ILA + HR group compared to the HR group. However, this protective effect was abolished by using the AhR antagonist CH223191 (Fig. 6, A-C), indicating that ILA treatment suppressed pSTAT3Tyr705 expression through AhR binding. STAT3 has been reported to regulate claudin 2 expression via a STAT binding site in its proximal promoter region [30]. Additionally, STAT3 activation is reported to be controlled by Nrf2, which acts as a corepressor of STAT3 transcriptional activity [31, 32]. To further explore the role of Nrf2-STAT3 pathway in claudin 2 expression, we investigated pSTAT3Tyr705 expression levels in Nrf2 knockdown HUVECs via western blot analysis. As shown in the HR model, Nrf2 siRNA knockdown suppressed the downregulation of pSTAT3Tyr705 caused by ILA (Fig. 6, B and D), indicating that ILA inhibited pSTAT3Tyr705 expression through AhR-Nrf2 signaling. To further investigate the role of STAT3 in Claudin 2 transcription, several binding elements and motifs were identified by the JASPAR website (Fig. 6E). Then, a ChIP-qPCR assay was conducted and the results demonstrated that a significant reduction in STAT3 binding to the Claudin 2 promoter with ILA treatment (Fig. 6, F and G). Consistent with our in vitro findings, intestinal vascular endothelium isolated from Nrf2-deficient mice fed with ILA showed no statistical difference in the expression levels of pSTAT3Tyr705 and claudin 2 between the IR and IR + ILA groups (Fig. 6, H-J). Moreover, Nrf2-deficient mice treated with ILA failed to attenuate the tissue injury following intestinal IR, as indicated by the Chiu’s scores (Fig. 6, K and L), colocalization levels of PV1/CD34 (Fig. 6, M and N) and PV1 protein expression (Fig. 6, O and P) in the ileum. These results demonstrate that Nrf2-mediated STAT3 inactivation is crucial for claudin 2 downregulation in response to ILA treatment, highlighting the Nrf2-STAT3 pathway's role in preserving GVB integrity.

Discussion

Our study provides compelling evidence that dietary TRP supplementation markedly alters gut microbiota composition and enhances both the IEB and GVB following intestinal IR damage. The results highlight a key protective mechanism driven by the TRP metabolite ILA via AhR activation and downstream Nrf2-STAT3 inactivation mediated claudin 2 downregulation. Our work adds novel insights into the protective role of TRP metabolites, particularly their influence on GVB integrity, an area that has received limited attention in previous studies.

As an essential amino acid, TRP plays a critical role in various physiological and pathological processes, including inflammation, oxidative stress, immune regulation, digestive system tumorigenesis, and the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis [13, 14, 33]. In our current study, prior to IR-induced intestinal injury modeling, comparative analysis with varying TRP concentrations in the diet revealed that TRP supplementation did not significantly affect gut inflammation, mucosal damage or mice’s body weight, indicating that TRP supplementation is well-tolerated and does not disrupt cellular metabolism. Meanwhile, it has been documented that different TRP supplementations did not lead to noteworthy differences in the individual community composition, and the increase in TRP metabolites was driven by higher substrate availability but not attributed to a change in the abundance of specific producer species [15]. Our study also sheds light on the role of the gut microbiota in modulating TRP metabolism and its downstream effects. The enrichment of TRP-metabolizing genera (such as Clostridium, Anaerotruncus and Bifidobacterium), and in TRP-supplemented mice was associated with improved barrier function and reduced inflammatory markers. This finding aligns with previous reports identifying these genera as key producers of indole and its derivatives [13, 34–36], which activate AhR and promote intestinal barrier integrity. The observation that dietary TRP reshapes the gut microbiota composition without causing inflammation or mucosal damage further underscores the importance of TRP-microbiota interactions in maintaining gut homeostasis.

Previous studies have shown that indole derivatives, such as IAA [19] and ILA [37], modulate tight junctions and promote epithelial integrity for maintaining IEB function. Clarissa Santos Rocha et al. [38] further reported that ILA was associated with rapid repair of gut epithelial barrier integrity and reduced mucosal inflammation, mediated by IL-22-driven epithelial renewal. Moreover, ILA has previously been reported to ameliorate cell oxidative damage and promote intestinal stem cells proliferation during intestinal IR [17]. Elevated concentrations of ILA are correlated with better intestinal function in cardiopulmonary bypass surgery patients [17] and can reduce intestinal inflammation and predict IBD development [39]. Similarly, our targeted metabolomics in serum revealed that mice fed with dietary TRP displayed a distinctive metabolomic profile compared with control diet-fed mice following IR injury. Specifically, the key metabolite ILA was found to be significantly upregulated both in serum (fourfold increase) and intestinal tissue. Our data found that ILA, a potent AhR ligand, markedly preserved the IEB following IR injury, as evidenced by improved TJs expression, reduced inflammatory cytokine levels, and enhanced GVB integrity. These effects were abrogated by the AhR antagonist CH223191, further confirming the pivotal role of AhR in mediating the protective effects of ILA.

It is notable that most studies on TRP and TRP-derived indole metabolites focus on the intestinal mucosal-epithelial barrier function and its role in maintaining gut homeostasis [17, 40–42]. Beyond the mucus and IEB, the gut is equipped with the GVB, which acts as a key gatekeeper to control the access of molecules and microorganisms into the systemic blood circulation [43] and refers to the bidirectional relationship between the gut and the liver [3]. Defective GVB and subsequent increased blood vessel permeability can be assessed by increased PV1 detection, a blood vessel endothelial-specific transmembrane protein that is associated to the diaphragms of the fenestrated endothelium [44]. GVB impairment has been documented in patients with ankylosing spondylitis, where it is accompanied by significant upregulation of PV1 in the inflamed ileum [45]. Our previous studies have shown that GVB dysfunction occurred following intestinal IR and gut-origin sepsis, and can be alleviated by dexmedetomidine [10] and terlipressin [11]. In the current study, we further demonstrated that increased PV1 expression is associated with liver damage and poor prognosis in patients with intestinal IR injury. Few studies, however, have explored the impact of TRP metabolism on the GVB. To investigate the specific mechanism of ILA action on the GVB during intestinal IR, bulk RNA-seq analysis unveiled noteworthy downregulation of genes in the TRP-s treated group compared to the untreated control group. These downregulated genes prominently enriched in cell adhesion molecules pathway. Subsequent experiments in mice and cells further confirmed these findings. The intestinal TJ proteins analysis upon ILA treatment in mice revealed that ILA significantly decreased the vascular permeability, as well as downregulated the channel-forming claudin 2 expressions in isolated intestinal ECs. Claudin 2, a TJ protein that facilitates paracellular water channels and is highly expressed in leaky epithelial tissues, is documented to be upregulated in IBDs and promotes inflammation [46, 47]. Further in vitro experiments with HUVECs verified that ILA significantly slowed the evolution of inflammation, enhanced the GVB integrity, and decreased claudin 2 expressions under HR conditions.

A recent study indicates that AhR exhibits high activity in lung endothelial cells, where it protects against influenza-induced lung vascular leakage [48]. Similarly, AhR signaling in intestinal endothelial cells has been documented to integrate dietary sues and maintain tissue homeostasis by promoting endothelia cell quiescence and vascular normalcy [16]. In addition, punicalagin has bee identified as a novel selective AhR modulator that targets the p90RSK/AP-1 pathway in inflammatory macrophages, thereby inhibiting macrophage-mediated inflammation and enhancing bactericidal activity [49]. Additionally, global Ahr deficiency is associated with heightened susceptibility to endotoxemia [50]. Nrf2 is a transcription factor crucial for cellular resistance to oxidative stress, often considered as the “master switch” of redox homeostasis in cells. The Nrf2 pathway is also known to protect intestinal barrier in traumatic brain injury [51], IBD [31] and intestinal IR [17]. While AhR’s role at barrier surfaces has been extensively studied in epithelial and immune cells, our study demonstrated that ILA treatment significantly increased the gene expressions of Ahrr/Cyp1a1/Cyp1a2/Cyp1b1/IL22 and HO-1/NQO1 (downstream target genes of AhR and Nrf2, respectively), alongside Nrf2 nuclear translocation in HUVECs. Moreover, the ILA-mediated enhancement of GVB tightness and reduction in claudin 2 expression were inhibited in both si-Nrf2 HUVECs and Nrf2 KO mice’ intestinal ECs. These findings highlighted that the ILA’s role in downregulating claudin 2 expression in ECs and alleviating GVB damage was primarily through AhR binding and subsequently nuclear translocation to activate the Nrf2 expression, further supporting the interdependency of AhR and Nrf2 pathway as previously described [17, 26, 31, 52].

STAT3 is reported to regulate claudin 2 expression via a STAT binding site in its proximal promoter region. Moreover, STAT3 activation is reported to be controlled by Nrf2, which acts as a corepressor of STAT3 transcriptional activity [32]. Our results showed that ILA treatment reduced pSTAT3Tyr705 levels in vivo and in vitro, suggesting inhibition of STAT3 activation. The interaction between STAT3 and the claudin 2 promoter was further investigated using Chip analysis, wherein we observed that ILA decreased the abundance of STAT3 in the claudin 2 promoter under HR conditions. This result aligns with previous reports demonstrating that flavonoid treatment in lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells inhibited the interaction of STAT3 with claudin 2 promotor and reduced claudin 2 expression [30], as well as alpha-tocopherylquinone suppressed claudin 2 expression via Nrf2-mediated STAT3 inhibition and diminished intestinal paracellular permeability [31]. Furthermore, it has been revealed that Bacillus amyloliquefaciens, possibly via the AhR/STAT3 pathway, accelerates intestinal stem cells proliferation and promotes epithelial barrier repair, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for IBD [53]. Though the crosstalk between AhR and Nrf2 pathway is reported to be sequential, with AhR playing a pivotal role, our data show that the observed effect of ILA on claudin 2 is partially due to the effect of AhR on the Nrf2 pathway. Overall, our results show that AhR-Nrf2-STAT3 pathway is essential for ILA-mediated improvement of GVB integrity and downregulation of the pore-forming protein claudin 2.

This research has some limitations. Firstly, while we identified ILA as a key metabolite mediating protective effects, the precise mechanisms of GVB damage caused by intestinal IR remained unclear. Further investigations are needed to explore the potential roles and mechanisms of other TRP metabolites in regulating GVB function, especially those in the kynurenine and serotonin pathways. Next, the activity of critical enzymes in indole pathway warrants further investigation. Future studies should also investigate the long-term effects of TRP supplementation and ILA administration on GVB integrity in larger animal models and clinical trials.

Overall, our study demonstrates that dietary TRP supplementation provides significant protection against intestinal IR injury by reinforcing both the epithelial and vascular barriers. The TRP metabolite ILA, through activation of the AhR-Nrf2 pathway, plays a bi-functional role in suppressing inflammation, reducing epithelial damage, downregulating claudin 2 expression and preserving GVB integrity. These findings provide new insights into the regulation of barrier function by TRP metabolites and underscore the potential clinical application of ILA or other AhR-activating metabolites as novel pharmacological agents to mitigate IEB/GVB dysfunction associated with intestinal IR injury, particularly in patients undergoing surgery, suffering from sepsis, or recovering from shock and trauma.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that the tryptophan-sufficient diet can mitigate intestinal ischemia–reperfusion (IR) injury by stabilizing tight junctions in the gut vascular barrier (GVB). The tryptophan metabolite indole-3-lactic acid (ILA) plays a critical role in this process. It is found that ILA may activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) on intestinal microvascular endothelial cells, promoting the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, which subsequently downregulates the expression levels of pSTAT3Tyr705 and claudin 2 expression, thereby preserving GVB integrity and protecting against intestinal IR injury.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment

All procedures were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval Number 2022–015). Individual patient consent was not obtained since all data used in this study were acquired retrospectively from the laboratory information system without any additional blood sampling or additional laboratory analysis. A total of 13 patients diagnosed with isolated acute superior mesenteric artery occlusion were included based on clinical evaluations and radiologic examinations in this study. Ineffective conservative treatment led to surgical resection of all patients’ diseased jejunum. The expression levels of PV1 in patients’ small intestine were quantitatively assessed by Image J software. Additionally, serum LDH concentrations and APACHE II scores were extracted from the Medical Records System for further analysis.

Animals and operative procedures

All procedures were approved by the National Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-Sen University (Guangzhou, China). 8 to 12-week-old C57BL/6J male mice were procured from GemPharmatech (Foshan, China). Nrf2−/− male mice were obtained from Bestest Biotechnology (Zhuhai, China). The mice were housed in a controlled environment with standard day-night cycles, maintained at optimal temperature and humidity, and provided with free access to food and drink. After anesthetization with pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.), the intestinal IR model was established as we described previously [54]. In brief, a midline laparotomy incision of approximately 2 cm was made to expose the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). The SMA was then occluded using a non-crushing microvessel clip for one hour. Following the hour of ischemia, the clip was removed and restored blood flow for a period of six hours. Normal saline (0.3 ml/100 g, i.p.) was administered during reperfusion to provide fluid resuscitation. After reperfusion, the wound was closed with sterile sutures after being infiltrated with 0.25% bupivacaine. The body temperature of the mice was maintained stable at 37 °C via the heating pad during the whole operative process.

Experimental design

To investigate the effects of dietary supplementation with varying concentrations of TRP on the intestine, mice were randomly assigned to the following 6 groups: (1) Control sham group (Con + S): mice received a control diet containing 0.1% TRP [55] prior to the sham operation. (2) Control IR group (Con + IR): mice received a control diet containing 0.1% TRP prior to the IR surgical intervention. (3) TRP-recommended sham group (Trp-r + S): mice received a standard diet containing 0.2% TRP prior to the sham operation. (4) TRP-recommended IR group (Trp-r + IR): mice received a standard diet containing 0.2% TRP prior to the IR surgical intervention. (5) TRP-sufficient sham group (Trp-s + S): mice received a diet containing 0.5% TRP [41] prior to the sham operation. (6) TRP-sufficient IR group (Trp-s + IR): mice received a diet containing 0.5% TRP prior to the IR surgical intervention. All diets (Dyets, Wuxi, China) were administered for 7 consecutive days and the composition is detailed in Supplemental Table 1.

For ILA supplementation, the mice were orally administered ILA (dissolved in normal saline, 40mg/kg, I157602, Aladdin, China) once every 24 h for 7 consecutive days [39]. The mice were randomly separated into 4 groups: Con + S; Con + IR; ILA + IR, and LA + CH223191 + IR. The AhR antagonist CH223191 [56] (dissolved in a solution of 5% DMSO + 40% PEG300 + 5% Tween80 + 50% ddH2O, 10mg/kg, i.p., S7711, Selleck, America) was co-administered with ILA and given prior to the IR surgery.

Morphometric assessment

At the designated reperfusion time point, the mouse small intestines were excised and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at room temperature. After fixation, the tissues were embedded in paraffin blocks for subsequent processing. Tissue sections were cut to a thickness of 5 µm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard protocols. Histological assessment was performed using the Chiu’s scoring system. In brief, the mucosal damage was Graded from 0 (normal) to 5 (severely damaged) as follows: Grade 0, normal mucosal villi; Grade 1, development of subepithelial Gruenhagen’ s space at the apex of the villus, often with capillary congestion; Grade 2, extension of the subepithelial space with moderate lifting of the epithelial layer from the lamina propria; Grade 3, massive epithelial lifting down the sides of villi, possibly with a few denuded tips; Grade 4, denuded villi with lamina propria and dilated capillaries exposed, possibly with increased cellularity of lamina propria; and Grade 5, digestion and disintegration of the lamina propria, hemorrhage, and ulceration. A minimum of five randomly chosen fields from each mice were evaluated and averaged to determine mucosal damage. Meanwhile, the scoring of the intestinal damage was evaluated independently by two certified pathologists blind to the study groups.

Serum iFABP detection

The severity of intestinal mucosal damage was quantitatively assessed by measuring the portal blood concentrations of intestinal fatty acid binding protein (i-FABP) adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol with a (ELISA) kit (SEA559Mu, Cloud-Clone Corp, USA).

Feces collection and Metagenomic sequencing

Mouse feces were collected at the same time on the same day. Each mouse was placed individually into sterilized cages, one mouse per cage. The bottom of the cage was lined with sterile filter paper and the mice were closely monitored for defecation. Once feces were produced, they were promptly collected using sterile forceps. A sufficient amount of fecal matter (100–200 mg/sample) was transferred into sterile cryogenic tubes. After collecting samples from each group, the feces were immediately stored in a −80°C freezer. Paired-end libraries were constructed using official Illumina kits, which involved fragmenting genomic DNA with ultrasound, repairing the ends of DNA fragments, adding an 'A' base to the 3' ends, attaching sequencing adapters, selecting fragment sizes and performing PCR amplification. Following library quality inspection, high-throughput sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq 6000 system in PE150 sequencing mode. Raw sequencing data were processed to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, and the remaining reads were de novo assembled to construct the metagenome for each sample by MEGAHIT (https://github.com/voutcn/megahit). All coding regions (CDS) of metagenomic contigs were predicted by MetaGeneMark (v3.26) and the unigenes were obtained by clustering the CDS sequences of all samples using CD-HIT. DIAMOND software was employed to align Unigenes with the NR_meta database for species classification at all levels, followed by statistical analysis of α-diversity and β-diversity based on taxonomic annotations. Subsequently, single genes were compared with the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional databases to retrieve annotations for each database. Abundance statistics and differential comparative analysis were conducted for taxonomic annotations, functional annotations, and single-gene expression. Differential Unigenes were subjected to enrichment analysis using the GO and KEGG databases.

Redundancy analysis (RDA) was conducted using the LC-Bio Cloud Platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/home?slide=2) to assess the correlation between significantly altered indole-producing bacterial genera and environmental factors.

Targeted metabolomics

Blood was collected from mice via retro-orbital bleeding, allowed to clot overnight at 4 °C, and then centrifugated for 15 min/3000 rpm. The supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C. Intestinal tissues were excised from the proximal 5 cm of the mouse cecum, rapidly stripped of unnecessary adipose and connective tissues, and cut into small pieces. These tissue samples were washed with pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove blood and fecal matter, dried with lint-free absorbent paper, and stored at −80°C. Both serum and intestinal tissue samples were subjected to targeted metabolomics analysis by AptBio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Briefly, samples were extracted using a solvent mixture of water/methanol/acetonitrile (1:2:2, v/v) and separated on an Agilent 1290 Infinity UHPLC system. Standards were maintained at 4°C in the autosampler, with the column temperature held at 40°C. Mobile phase A consisted of 20 mM ammonium formate with 0.2% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 0.2% formic acid in methanol, at a flow rate of 400 μL/min and an injection volume of 5 μL. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed in both positive and negative ion modes using a 5500 QTRAP mass spectrometer (SCIEX) in Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode. Data analysis was conducted using Multiquant 3.0.2 software to extract chromatographic peak areas and retention times for accurate metabolite identification.

Intestinal endothelial cell isolation

To isolate and purify intestinal endothelial cells, we employed magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) utilizing CD31 MicroBead kits (130–097–418, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Fresh intestinal specimens obtained after reperfusion were immersed in chilled PBS solution to remove residual intestinal contents and blood cells. The tissue was then cut into fine fragments and incubated with an equal volume of 0.5% collagenase type I (C0103, Sigma, USA) for 45 min at 37 °C to facilitate enzymatic digestion. A 70 µm-filter screen was used to remove cell clumps, followed by centrifugation. Then the cells were resuspended in a rinsing buffer composed of the autoMACS® Rinsing Solution (130–091–222, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and MACS® BSA Stock Solution (130–091–376, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). The total cell number was assessed using a cell counter (Countess 3, ThermoFisher, USA). CD31 magnetic beads were added to the cell suspension (1 × 108 cells/sample) and incubated at 4 °C for 15 min to ensure efficient labeling. Next, the separation column was fixed in the MACS magnetic field and rinsed with bubble-free buffer, and the labeled cell suspension was added. After removal from the magnetic field, buffer was added to the column, and pressure elution was performed to collect the eluted CD31-positive cells. Finally, the eluted CD31-positive cells were resuspended in TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) for the extraction of total RNA.

RNA sequencing

Isolated intestinal endothelial cells were sent to Novogene (Beijing, China) for RNA sequencing. RNA was extracted from these cells and its quality was assessed using Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. Samples that met the quality criteria were processed for library preparation and underwent 150 bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. Raw reads were processed using the fastp software to remove adapters, ploy-N, and low-quality reads, yielding clean reads. These clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.5. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software (version 1.20.0), where genes with an adjusted P-value of < = 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was conducted through GO, KEGG and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Gene set enrichment comparisons were controlled for FDR with two sidedPFDR ≤ 0.05 results displayed.

In vivo intestinal permeability

Briefly, all mice were fasted for at least 4 h with access to water. 0.5 g kg−1 70kd Fluorescin Isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran (FD70, ST2947, Beyotime, China) was administered orally. Four hours after gavage, the blood samples and liver tissues were collected. Plasma was isolated, and the fluorescence intensity of FD70 was measured using a microplate reader (excitation at 490 nm, emission at 520 nm; INFINITE F500, Tecan, Austria). Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and sectioned. The intensity of FD70 in the liver was assessed by immunofluorescence using an automatic confocal fluorescence microscope (DMI8, Leica, Germany).

Cell culture and Hypoxia/Reoxygenation model

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured with endothelial cultural medium (ECM; 1001, Sciencell, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; A2160802, Gibco, USA) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin solution (15,140,122, Gibco, USA). HUVECs from passages 2 to 6 were used in all experiments to ensure cellular stability and reproducibility. To establish a hypoxia/reoxygenation (HR) model, HUVECs were first exposed to a hypoxic environment containing 94% N2, 5% CO2, and 1% O2 in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 11,966,025, Gibc, ThermoFisher, USA) without glucose or serum at 37 °C for 2 h. The cells were then reoxygenated by incubating them in normal culture media under 95% air and 5% CO2 at 37℃ for varying durations.

The cultured HUVECs were divided into 4 groups: (1) Sham group: HUVECs were not exposed to HR treatment. (2) HR group: HUVECs were subjected to 2 h of hypoxia followed by 4 h of reoxygenation. (3) ILA + HR group: HUVECs were pretreated with ILA (400 µM) [39] for 24 h before undergoing HR. (4) ILA + CH223191 + HR group: HUVECs were pretreated with CH223191 [39] (10 μM) and ILA (400 μM) for 24 h before HR.

Cell transfection

Transient knockdown of Nrf2 was achieved using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) from GenePharma (Shanghai, China) following the siRNA-mate plus protocol. The transfection procedure was carried out according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates and allowed to reach approximately 60% confluence. For each well, 75 pmol siRNA and 7.5 µl plus of transfection reagent were used. Cells were harvested at 24 h or 48 h after transfection respectively for qRT-PCR or Western blot analysis. The specific sequences of the siRNA or shRNA mentioned above can be found in Supplemental Table 2. Four siRNAs were designed to silence Nrf2 in vitro, and finally the most effective sequence (NFE2L2-homo-1305) was selected for further analysis (Figure. S5A-C).

Cell viability assay

The viability of HUVECs were evaluated using the WST-8 assay (Cell Counting Kit-8, CK04-cck8, Dojindo, China). Briefly, 10 µl of the CCK-8 reagent was mixed with 100 µl of culture medium and added to each well of the culture plate. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to allow for adequate reaction. After incubation, the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Victor Nivo 5S, PerkinElmer, Finland), providing a quantitative evaluation of cell viability.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR assays

ChIP-qPCR assays were performed on HR and ILA + HR cells. The specific experimental procedure was as follows: cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, followed by quenching with 1.25 M glycine for 5 min. The cells were then washed twice with ice-cold PBS, scraped, and transferred to pre-chilled 1.5 ml centrifuge tubes for centrifugation to collect the cell pellets. DNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and 10% of the diluted DNA from each sample was used as Input DNA. The remaining solution was incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies for immunoprecipitation, including STAT3 antibody (Proteintech, 10,253–2-AP, China), H3K27ac antibody (positive control), and rabbit IgG (negative control). The PCR conditions were set with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 49 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing at 60 °C for 1 min. The sequences of qPCR primers are listed in Supplemental Table 3. The signal obtained from each immunoprecipitation was expressed as a percentage of the total input chromatin: % Input = 2^(-ΔCt [normalized IP])*100%, ΔCt [normalized IP] = (Ct [IP]—(Ct [Input] -Log2 (Input Dilution Factor))).

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded mouse intestinal sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Following permeabilization and antigen retrieval, the slides were blocked with blocking buffer (P0260, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies, including PV1 (1:100, ab27853, Abcam, USA) and CD34 (1:100, ab81289, Abcam, USA), on a shaking table at 4 °C overnight and subsequently incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were subsequently counterstained with 4,6-diamidin-2-phenylindole (DAPI). All immunofluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss Axioscan microscope. Quantitative analysis of the staining intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from intestinal tissues was extracted using a specialized RNA extraction kit (LS1040, Promega, USA). RT-qPCR was subsequently carried out using RT SuperMix (R222-01, Vazyme, China) and SYBR qPCR master mix (Q711-02, Vazyme, China) on the Roche LightCycler 480 system. The relative mRNA levels of various genes were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with β-actin and GAPDH as the reference genes for normalization. The sequences of the primers synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China) are provided in the Supplemental Table 4.

Western blot analysis

After tissue or cell lysis, homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 12 min, and then the protein concentration of the supernatant was quantified utilizing the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (23,227, ThermoFisher, USA). The extracted proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. To block non-specific binding, membranes were incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, B24726, ABCONE, China) at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were then incubated overnight with the primary antibodies, including PV1 (1:1000, DF13415, Affinity Biosciences, China) and GAPDH (1:10,000, 60,004-1-Ig, Proteintech, China), in a shaking table at 4 °C overnight and subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using an HRP substrate (WBKLS0500, Merck Millipore, Germany), and imaged with an ImageQuant LAS 500 imager (General Electric). Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software, with normalization to the housekeeping protein GAPDH.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons were initiated by evaluating data normality, followed by the application of either an unpaired Student's t-test, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or Spearman correlation analysis as appropriate for the dataset. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software (La Jolla, CA, USA) and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
