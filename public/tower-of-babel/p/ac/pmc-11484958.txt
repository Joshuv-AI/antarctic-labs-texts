An elastic piezoelectric nanomembrane with double noise reduction for high-quality bandpass acoustics
Authors: Jialin Zhang, Yanjun Liu, Peiyi Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymer piezoelectrics with high electromechanical energy conversion (HEEC) are very promising for flexible acoustoelectric devices. However, reducing thickness and improving ordered polarization and ferroelectricity while maintaining high mechanical strength pose enormous fabrication challenges for polymer piezoelectric membranes—additionally, noise management in the acoustoelectric conversion remains an open issue. Here, we present a hydro-levitation superspreading approach for fabricating polymer nanomembranes with ordered crystalline phases and sub-nanostructures on the water surface. The elastic piezoelectric nanomembrane (EPN) is only 335 nanometers thick and consists of a conductance-stable piezoelectric layer sandwiched between two elastic damping layers. Such an all-in-one EPN can reduce background noise with low autocorrelation in the environment, suppress spurious noise caused by poor circuit contact, and achieve bandpass filtering of acoustic signals at human voice frequencies. This nanomembrane holds promise in repairing the auditory system of patients with tympanic membrane perforation and in a wide range of other acoustoelectric conversion fields.Subject terms: Soft materials, Nanoscale materials

Introduction

Polymer piezoelectrics are promising materials for coupling mechanical and electrical energy1, with broad applications in flexible electronics2,3 and wearable acoustics4. Flexible acousto-electronics in seamless contact with biological tissues typically require polymer piezoelectrics that satisfy high conformability and elasticity to accommodate the varying strains of these tissues5,6. Reducing the thickness of polymer piezoelectric materials is the most effective solution for achieving conformity with biological tissues7. The current methods allow the fabrication of ultrathin polymer piezoelectric membranes by spreading the polymer solutions on solid substrates8. However, the strong adhesion of ultrathin polymer piezoelectric membranes to solid substrates hinders their large-area transfer, especially at nanometer-scale thicknesses. Owing to the atomic-scale flatness and highly dynamic nature of the liquid surface9, spreading polymer droplets on liquid substrates is an ideal alternative approach for fabricating ultrathin polymer membranes from an interface engineering point of view10. Yet, due to the limited tension difference between the polymer droplets and the liquid substrates, the spreading area of polymer membranes has reached a limit.

In addition to efficient fabrication and large-area transfer, well-organized microstructures are also required for the ultrathin polymer piezoelectric membranes made from polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) copolymers. The PVDF copolymers are semicrystalline and exist in five different crystal forms, among which the crystalline β phase exhibits the highest piezoelectric response and ferroelectricity11. High content of the crystalline β phase enables high electromechanical energy conversion (HEEC). However, the inability to invoke error correction and defect tolerance in molecular conformations without external forces and interactions for tailoring the crystalline structures remains a major obstacle to fabricating high-performance PVDF copolymer piezoelectric membranes.

To address these issues, we developed a simple and universal hydro-levitation superspreading approach that can fabricate polymer nanomembranes with ordered crystalline phases and sub-nanostructures on a large scale by dropping polymer solutions containing 4-cyano-4’-pentylbiphenyl (5CB) onto the surface of potassium hydroxide (KOH) aqueous solution (Fig. 1a). The atomic-scale flatness of the liquid substrate ensures the uniformity of polymer nanomembranes. At the same time, its high dynamic characteristics facilitate the large-area transfer of polymer nanomembranes. Moreover, the addition of 5CB can self-organize at the interface between organic solvent and water and create a self-lubricating layer that enables fast and efficient superspreading of polymer droplets (Fig. 1b). In this process, the system generates two active shear forces. One is the fluid shear flow induced by interfacial tension difference, which can drive the alignment of polymer chains. The other is the negentropy flow caused by the phase transition of 5CB, which can drive the transition of polymer chains into the favorable crystalline phases. Suppose the microscale forces and interactions are a passive intervention, such as knife-scraping to prepare an ultrathin membrane. In that case, the system is prone to structure defects and molecular redundancy due to additional interference (Fig. 1c).

In this work, we engineered a 335-nanometer-thick elastic piezoelectric nanomembrane (EPN) with a complex, layered architecture using the resulting ultrathin and uniform nanomembranes. The all-in-one EPN consists of a stiff poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) [P(VdF-HFP)] piezoelectric nanomembrane, which is coated with a conductance-stable silver nanowire (AgNW) network and sandwiched between two soft and elastic ethylene-vinyl acetate copolymer (EVA) nanomembranes. The all-in-one EPN exhibits robust mechanical properties, self-healing ability, controlled water permeability, adaptive air permeability and biocompatibility. More importantly, the all-in-one EPN acts as a high-quality acoustoelectric conversion device that can reduce background noise with low audio autocorrelation in the environment, suppress spurious noise caused by poor circuit contact, and achieve bandpass filtering of acoustic signals at human voice frequencies.

Results

Fabrication of nanomembranes by hydro-levitation superspreading

When a droplet of polymer with low surface tension drops onto the surface of the aqueous solution with high surface tension, the droplet spontaneously spreads, driven by the tension difference10,12, and finally forms the polymer membrane. The polymer droplet failed to achieve the optimal spreading effect due to the low tension difference and high interfacial resistance (Supplementary Fig. 1). To enhance the tension difference between the polymer droplets and the liquid substrates, we increased the surface tension of water by adding KOH. The experimental results indicate that the optimal concentration of KOH is 6 mol L−1, which corresponds to a tension difference of 92 mN m−1 between the polymer droplets and the KOH aqueous solution, with an increase of 52 mN m−1 compared to pure water (Supplementary Fig. 2). To reduce the interfacial resistance of the polymer droplets during the spreading process, we created a self-lubricating layer at the interface between the polymer droplets and the KOH-water solution by adding 5CB to the polymer solutions. The 5CB is a thermotropic liquid crystal with a phase transition temperature of 35 °C13. It is also hydrophobic14,15. Below 35 °C, the 5CB is in a low-activity nematic phase. When the temperature rises to 37 °C (body temperature), the 5CB transitions into a highly active isotropic phase (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). When a droplet of polymer containing 5CB drops onto the KOH-water solution, a hydrophobic layer of nematic 5CB forms at the interface between the polymer solution and the KOH-water solution (Fig. 1b). This layer functions as a self-lubricating layer, reducing interfacial resistance16–18 and ultimately achieving optimal superspreading. To verify the advantages of 5CB, we selected 7CB and 8CB for the same experiment. The spreading area did not significantly differ when different liquid crystals were used as lubricants (Supplementary Fig. 3a, c, d). The advantage of 5CB over 7CB and 8CB lies in its lower phase transition temperature, making it more economical and practical.

As shown in Fig. 2a, a droplet of EVA solution, which contains the optimal content of 5CB, can achieve superspreading within 400 ms and form an ultra-large-sized EVA membrane (hereafter referred to as E-membrane) with a diameter of up to 128 mm (Supplementary Movie 1). The experimental results show that the optimal content of 5CB is equal to the mass of the polymers (Fig. 2b). If the 5CB content exceeds that of the polymer, excessive 5CB could cause visible perforation defects or hinder membrane formation due to its disruption of polymeric continuity. The spreading area and efficiency of the membrane prepared using this hydro-levitation superspreading strategy are superior to those achieved with existing natural and artificial spreading methods (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 1)10,19–22. The spreading time is crucial in determining the structural and mechanical properties of the membrane. When the spreading time is sufficiently fast, the membrane spreads with a larger diameter and a more uniform structure (Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition to soft and elastic E-membranes, we employed this strategy for fabricating high-modulus stiff P(VdF-HFP) membranes (hereafter referred to as P-membranes). The thickness of these membranes is on the nanometer scale, with E-membranes being ~150 nm thick and P-membranes being around 80 nm thick (Supplementary Fig. 5). Due to the highly dynamic nature of the liquid surface, these polymer nanomembranes can be easily separated from the liquid substrate and transferred to various substrates (Fig. 2d), including wafers, plastic substrates and other polymer nanomembranes. Moreover, the E-membrane exhibits a consistent interference color, indicating that its thickness and surface structures are uniform (Fig. 2e).

The molecular dynamics of 5CB in the superspreading process were elucidated by low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). The transverse relaxation (T2) time corresponds to the activity of 1H, with a longer time indicating higher activity23. The ratio of longitudinal relaxation (T1) time to T2 time corresponds to the mobility of 1H, and a larger T1/T2 ratio indicates poorer mobility24. At 25 °C, the 2D low-field NMR map of the E-membranes shows three separated spin populations, with a low-activity and low-mobility spin population assigned to the EVA copolymer (T1/T2 = 737) and the other two spin populations assigned to the 5CB (Fig. 2f). Heating to 37 °C induces an obvious transition of the spin populations assigned to the 5CB from a low-activity and low-mobility solid state to a high-activity and high-mobility liquid state. Notably, the mobility of the spin population assigned to the EVA copolymer significantly increased while its activity remained unchanged (T1/T2 = 273). This indicates that 5CB is not only an interfacial lubricant but also a molecular editing tool capable of generating active shear force to drive the adjustment of the molecular chain structure in the spreading polymer membranes.

The molecular-scale structures of polymer membranes were characterized using differential scanning calorimeter (DSC), circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy and X-ray powder diffraction (XRD). The DSC curve of the spreading E-membrane exhibits a significant increase in crystallinity compared to that of the knife-scraping E-membrane (Fig. 2g). The CD spectrum of the spreading E-membrane indicates that the molecular structure within the spreading E-membrane consists of crystalline twists and amorphous helices and coils (Fig. 2h), which we refer to as anharmonic nanosprings25. The crystallinity change of the spreading P-membrane, similar to that of the E-membrane, exhibits an increasing trend and reaches 33%, which is higher than the 17% for the knife-scraping P-membrane (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Due to the absence of additional forces and interactions capable of overcoming the steric hindrance, the crystal structure of the knife-scraping P-membrane also lacks homogeneity. According to the XRD results (Fig. 2i), the crystal structure of the knife-scraping P-membrane consists of both α and β phase crystal types26,27. In sharp contrast, the crystal structure in the spreading P-membrane consists predominantly of the piezoelectric β phase. The piezoelectric β phase is much higher than piezoelectric materials prepared using traditional methods28,29. The piezoelectric β phase exhibits the highest piezoelectric response, and thus the high proportion of the piezoelectric β phase enables HEEC.

All-in-one EPN design

The fabrication process of an all-in-one EPN can be divided into three key steps (Supplementary Movie 2). First, the E-membrane and P-membrane are bonded using capillary forces at the water surface. Then, plasma treatment is applied to the P side of the E/P-membrane to create a solvophilic surface. A drop of ethanol solution containing AgNWs is dropped onto the solvophilic surface, spreading and forming a stable conductive network of AgNWs (Supplementary Fig. 8). Finally, another layer of E-membrane is added to create an all-in-one EPN with a soft-stiff-soft sandwich structure (Fig. 3a). Each layer of the all-in-one EPN is highly integrated and compatible with one another by seamless adhesion and 5CB welding. The all-in-one EPN has a thickness of only 335 nm (Fig. 3b). The all-in-one EPN exhibits a consistent interference color (Fig. 3c), indicating a seamless integration between the soft E-membrane and stiff P-membrane with no visible defects, such as air bubbles.

The 150-nm-thick E-membrane can withstand a weight of 2 g, resist puncture from a sharp metal object, and maintain notch passivation even when stretched (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Movie 3). A small steel ball repeatedly bounces when it falls onto the E-membrane from a height of 15 cm, demonstrating the high elasticity of the E-membrane (Supplementary Movie 3). The high mechanical strength of the E-membrane is attributed to its abundant crystal content, while its high elasticity arises from the anharmonic nanospring structure. The modulus of polymer nanomembranes was measured using the nanoindentation method. The nanoindentation curves suggest that the E-membrane is a relatively soft and elastic material, with Young’s modulus of 109.8 ± 4.8 MPa. On the other hand, the P-membrane is characterized as a stiff material, with Young’s modulus of 2140.2 ± 131.5 MPa (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 2). The high modulus of the P-membrane is attributed to its high proportion of the piezoelectric β phase. Despite a 20-fold difference in modulus, the reversible recovery distance of the E/P-membrane in the loading-unloading cycle is consistent with that of the highly elastic E-membrane, indicating strong adhesion and seamless conformality between the soft E-membrane and the stiff P-membrane.

The microstructures of the all-in-one EPN were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The SEM image of the E-membrane shows uniform nanometer-scale crack structures (Fig. 3f), while the P-membrane exhibits disordered nanometer-scale perforation structures (Fig. 3g). Despite the disorder in distribution, the perforation diameter remains consistent. The active 5CB is key to the creation of these microstructures. During the superspreading of polymer droplets, the hydrophobic 5CB tends to migrate towards the air side of the membrane due to repulsive forces from water. The soft elastic EVA copolymer binds polymer chains to 5CB through electrostatic interactions30, which hinders its mobility and leads to surface cracks on the E-membrane. In contrast, there appears to be no interaction between the polymer chains and 5CB in the high-modulus stiff P(VdF-HFP) copolymer, allowing for high mobility of 5CB and resulting in perforation of the P-membrane. The magnified SEM image shows interconnected networks of AgNWs in the P/AgNWs membrane, where the intersections are welded by 5CB (Fig. 3h). The 5CB within the P-membrane precipitates when the AgNW ethanol solution is dropped onto it, as 5CB is dissolved in ethanol. The ethanol solution of 5CB tends to aggregate towards the intersection of the AgNWs driven by the Laplace pressure difference (Supplementary Fig. 10)31,32. Furthermore, electrostatic interactions strengthen these welding points. The shift of the cyanide groups assigned to 5CB within the 5CB-AgNWs composite towards higher wavenumbers confirms the electrostatic interaction between 5CB and AgNWs (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Adaptive permeability, self-healing ability and biocompatibility

The all-in-one EPN can hold a large amount of water without any leakage, as shown in Fig. 4. However, when the bottom of the all-in-one EPN touches the water surface, it allows for quick passage of water. (Supplementary Movie 4). Moreover, the all-in-one EPN exhibits adaptive gas permeability, similar to the breathing process (Supplementary Movie 5). Due to its thin geometry and nanometer-scale cracks and perforations, combined with the high activity of 5CB, the water vapor transmittance rate of the all-in-one EPN is as high as 5936 g m−2 day−1 at body temperature (Fig. 4c), which exceeds that of an open bottle (5393 g m−2 day−1) at room temperature and surpasses that of any other ultrathin hydrogel skins reported so far (Supplementary Figs. 12). Additionally, the all-in-one EPN exhibits self-healing ability. The jagged wound on the all-in-one EPN can fully heal within 6 min at body temperature (Fig. 4d). The 5CB at a temperature of 37 °C drives the movement of EVA chains, leading to the self-healing of the rough incision on the surface of the E-membrane (Supplementary Fig. 13). The tissue adhesion and biocompatibility of the all-in-one EPN were further confirmed. The all-in-one EPN demonstrates strong adhesion to the leather, as shown in Fig. 4e. Moreover, human umbilical vein endothelial cells exhibit robust adhesion and spreading behavior on the surface of the all-in-one EPN. (Fig. 4f). The favorable cell morphology and activity indicate that the all-in-one EPN is safe for cells and compatible with biological systems.

Double noise reduction and bandpass human voice recognition

The World Health Organization estimates that 1.5 billion people worldwide are affected by varying degrees of hearing loss33, with over 90 million cases attributed to acute and chronic otitis media34. Tympanic membrane (TM) perforation is one of the major complications of otitis media35,36. When the perforation is large and accompanied by inflammation, self-healing the perforated TM is almost impossible. Therefore, TM replacement is the only solution for treating severe TM perforation. The autograft, which involves using the patient’s cartilage membrane, fascia, or cartilage37, has been widely practiced. The autograft materials, however, lack a complex layered architecture, particularly the high-modulus collagen layer that enables high-quality sound energy conversion (Fig. 5a), resulting in less effective hearing recovery. Additionally, the lack of conformability to the TM in autograft materials can cause them to easily detach due to continuous pressure differences on both sides of the middle ear, leading to recurrent TM perforation. The development of artificial TM alternatives with low air resistance, high vibration conversion efficiency, strong tissue adhesion and excellent biocompatibility is urgently needed.

The biomimetic TM of the all-in-one EPN holds great potential for reconstructing the auditory system in patients with TM perforation (Fig. 5a). Each layer of the 335-nanometer-thick all-in-one EPN is important in subsequent acoustoelectric conversion applications. The nanoscale thickness enhances the sensitivity to low sound pressure signals. The soft and elastic damping E-membrane reduces background noise in the environment by dissipating energy through nanometer-scale vibrations of the anharmonic nanosprings. Moreover, the high elasticity of the E-membrane ensures a low hysteresis response of the all-in-one EPN to sound signals at different frequencies. The AgNW conductive layer, a strain-insensitive conductive network welded by 5CB, enables stable conductivity that minimizes spurious noise caused by poor contact among AgNWs. The high-modulus stiff P-membrane can efficiently convert cross-plane vibrations caused by sound waves into high-quality in-plane vibrations. The P-membrane contains a significant amount of the piezoelectric β phase, which enables HEEC.

The 5CB-welded AgNW networks exhibit a higher and more stable electrical conductivity than the commonly lapped AgNW networks (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 14). The electrical conductivity of the 5CB-welded AgNW networks is seven times higher than that of the lapped AgNW networks. When stretched to 120% of its original area, the all-in-one EPN with 5CB-welded AgNW networks shows a slight increase in resistance change. However, due to the disconnection of lapped AgNW networks during stretching, the all-in-one EPN with lapped AgNW networks experiences a significant resistance increase of 16000%. The lapped AgNW networks, when subjected to microscale strain with a pressure intensity of 0.0005 Pa (100 Hz sound), also exhibit an obvious phenomenon of poor contact (Fig. 5c and testing device in Supplementary Fig. 15). Moreover, the resistance signal drifted after three cycles, suggesting that the lapped AgNW networks had suffered permanent damage. In contrast, the resistance signals of the 5CB-welded AgNW networks remained unchanged, indicating an excellent contact between the 5CB-welded AgNW networks. Owing to the presence of a high-modulus stiff P-membrane with a high content of piezoelectric β phase, the acoustoelectric conversion efficiency of the all-in-one EPN is much higher than that of the knife-scraping P/AgNWs-membrane (Fig. 3d). The trend remained consistent across different frequencies (Supplementary Fig. 16). At a sound pressure level of 56.5 dB, the all-in-one EPN produces an electrical output that is 862 times higher than the knife-scraping P/AgNWs-membrane, and it is 120 times higher even at a sound pressure level of 23.5 dB.

The sound pressure level of the background noise in the test environment is below 23.5 dB within the frequency range of 20–18000 Hz (Supplementary Fig. 17). The background noise is characterized by its lack of a fixed period, frequency and musical note, unlike the sound signals used in the test (100, 120 and 220 Hz) and human voice signals (Supplementary Movie 6). In other words, the audio autocorrelation of the background noise is low. Interestingly, the all-in-one EPN exhibits a nonlinear response to sound pressure levels and precisely transitions at 23.5 dB. This indicates that the presence of the E-membrane with built-in anharmonic nanosprings and the P-membrane with a high content of piezoelectric β phase enables the reduction of background noise with low audio autocorrelation while efficiently converting target signals with high audio autocorrelation. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) [SNR = 20 log (Voutput/Vnoise)] of the all-in-one EPN is determined by using sound waves at a constant frequency of 100 Hz with different sound pressure levels (Fig. 5e). The SNR of the spreading P/AgNWs-membrane is higher than that of the knife-scraping P/AgNWs-membrane due to its more abundant and homogeneous piezoelectric β phase. The single resonance frequency of the spreading P/AgNWs-membrane determines that the SNR increases linearly with sound pressure levels (Supplementary Fig. 18). The SNR of the all-in-one EPN is higher than that of the spreading P/AgNWs-membrane due to the background noise reduction achieved by anharmonic nanosprings within the E-membrane. It should be noted that the SNR of the all-in-one EPN increases significantly after reaching a sound pressure level of 23.5 dB. The all-in-one EPN is a multi-material membrane with soft-hard-soft properties, resulting in multiple resonance frequencies. The sound pressure levels of the 100-Hz sound signal exceed 23.5 dB, resulting in additional resonance signals at different frequencies (300, 500 and 700 Hz) (Supplementary Fig. 19), which fall within the resonant frequency range of the all-in-one EPN. The SNR of the all-in-one EPN is therefore enhanced above the sound pressure level of 23.5 dB.

The performance of noise reduction can be classified into two aspects (Fig. 5f): firstly, the welded AgNW networks minimize spurious noise caused by poor contact between AgNWs; secondly, the soft and elastic E-membrane effectively reduces the background noise (<23.5 dB) with low audio autocorrelation in the frequency range of 20–18000 Hz by dissipating energy through nanometer-scale vibrations of the anharmonic nanosprings(Supplementary Fig. 20). To evaluate the noise reduction performance of the biomimetic TM of the all-in-one EPN in a natural environment, we created a channel that replicates the size of the human external ear canal (Supplementary Fig. 21). The 2D time-frequency spectrum of the audio “biomimetic tympanic membrane” shows a noticeable reduction in background noise after being processed by the layered E/P membrane, as shown in Fig. 5g, h, compared to that processed by the P-membrane. The intensity of the human voice signals did not decrease (The redder the color, the higher the intensity).

By measuring the output voltage of the biomimetic TM at different frequencies, we successfully determined the resonance frequency of the biomimetic TM (Fig. 6a). The resonant frequencies of the biomimetic TM fall within the frequency range of human speech38, indicating that the biomimetic TM can effectively recognize acoustic signals at frequencies commonly found in human voices (Fig. 6b). The output voltage of the biomimetic TM remains unchanged in a noisy environment (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Movie 7). Although seagull sounds with a narrow frequency range can induce vibrations in the in-plane direction of the biomimetic TM, these vibrations are very short-lived, resulting in a slight change to the output voltage of the biomimetic TM (Supplementary Fig. 22). In stark contrast, the biomimetic TM can accurately identify human voices in complex sound environments and can accurately distinguish the content of people’s speech (Supplementary Fig. 23). However, the electrical signals can only reflect the vibration amplitude of the biomimetic TM; they cannot fully demonstrate all the various spatial vibration modes caused by sound waves. To comprehensively demonstrate the spatial vibration modes of the biomimetic TM, we designed an optical lever system capable of visualizing its vibration trajectories (Fig. 6d). Figure 6e shows that the biomimetic TM can accurately convert complex sounds with different frequencies, intensities, tones, timbres and directions into corresponding vibration trajectories (Supplementary Movie 8). When the sound wave is a single frequency, the vibration trajectory of the biomimetic TM is a line parallel to the Y-axis. When sound waves of different frequencies are superimposed, the vibration trajectories extend in the X-axis direction. The result visualizes the coupling states of sound waves with different frequencies, indicating that the biomimetic TM can achieve high-quality conversion of multi-frequency sound waves. The size of the vibration trajectory is directly related to the intensity of the vibration. The length of vibration trajectories varies depending on tones, timbre and directions, and they also exhibit different extended states in the X-axis direction. The biomimetic TM can accurately convert subtle variations in sound waves into corresponding mechanical vibrations.

In summary, we have developed a universal liquid crystal-mediated hydro-levitation superspreading approach for fabricating polymer nanomembranes on a liquid substrate. The liquid substrate ensures the effective large-area separation and transfer of nanomembranes. Liquid crystal 5CB is an omnipotent molecular tool that can self-organize at the liquid-liquid interface and create a self-lubricating layer, enabling rapid superspreading of polymer droplets. This superspreading strategy is universal. The methods developed address the challenge of effectively creating hierarchically ordered structures within nanomembranes. Furthermore, this work may promote the next era of design for acoustoelectric conversion devices that could be applied in flexible film loudspeakers and sound energy generators.

Methods

Materials

N-hexane, tetrahydrofuran, ethanol, and potassium hydroxide (KOH) were purchased from Greagent. Poly(ethylene-co-vinyl acetate) (EVA, vinyl acetate 32 wt%) and poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) [P(VdF-HFP), Mw ≈ 455,000 g mol−1] were purchased from Macklin. Silver nanowires (AgNWs, 5 mg mL−1 dispersed in ethanol, diameter: 35–45 nm, length: 25–35 μm) were purchased from J&K. 4’pentyl-cyanobiphenyl (5CB) was purchased from Meryer. All the chemicals were used without further purification and deionized (DI) water was used in all experiments.

Preparation of spreading solution

The EVA spreading solution, consisting of EVA, hexane, and 5CB, was prepared in a weight ratio of 1:10:1. The EVA spreading solution was stirred continuously at 38 °C for 1 h. The P(VdF-HFP) spreading solution, consisting of P(VdF-HFP), tetrahydrofuran, and 5CB, was prepared in a weight ratio of 1:8:1. The P(VdF-HFP) spreading solution was stirred continuously at 45 °C for 1 h.

Preparation of polymer nanomembranes

The spreading solution was transferred into a 5-mL syringe. The spreading solution (37 °C) was dropped onto the surface of the KOH solution with high surface tension (6 mol L−1). The droplets of the spreading solutions spontaneously spread on the surface of the KOH solution to form polymer nanomembranes. The free-standing polymer nanomembranes were obtained by evaporating the solvent under ambient conditions (25 oC, RH 60%), and they can be directly transferred to the next step.

Preparation of all-in-one EPN

The P-membrane was placed on top of the E-membrane, and the two membranes bonded together due to capillary forces. The resulting composite E/P-membrane underwent a 30 s plasma treatment (O2) before being placed back onto the surface of DI water. Next, a droplet of AgNW solution (0.06 mg mL−1 in ethanol) was dropped onto the surface of the composite E/P -membrane (on the side of the P membrane) and spread spontaneously. The composite E/P membrane allowed ethanol to pass through, while the AgNWs remained on the surface of the P-membrane, creating a conductive network of AgNWs. Another layer of the E-membrane was applied to cover one side of the AgNW surface, forming the final all-in-one EPN.

Preparation of knife-scraping P/AgNWs-membrane

One gram of P(VdF-HFP) was dissolved in 10 mL of tetrahydrofuran and then stirred at 45 °C for 1 h. The polymer solution (40 μL) was dropped onto one end of the substrate and then evenly scraped using the applicator, which moved at a steady rate of 10 mm s−1. The blade applicator was adjusted to a height of 10 ± 2 μm perpendicular to the PET substrate, which had been pretreated with silicone oil. After the solvent evaporated, the PET substrate was removed to obtain a knife-scraping P-membrane. The knife-scraping P-membrane was treated with plasma (O2) for 30 s. Next, a droplet of AgNW solution (0.06 mg mL−1 in ethanol) was dropped onto the surface of the knife-scraping P-membrane and spread spontaneously. The knife-scraping P-membrane allowed ethanol to pass through, while the AgNWs remained on the surface of the knife-scraping P-membrane, creating a conductive network of AgNWs.

Characterizations

The SEM images were captured using an environmental scanning electron microscope (Quanta 250, Czech Republic). Optical images of the self-healing process of an all-in-one EPN were captured using the stereoscopic microscope (Olympus SZX7, Japan). The crystallinity of polymer nanomembranes was measured using a differential scanning calorimeter with a heating rate of 10 oC min−1 under a nitrogen flow. (DSC, Q250, TA Instruments). The nanomembranes under test were superimposed for DSC measurements (5 mg). The height images of polymer nanomembranes were obtained using an atomic force microscope (AFM, MFP-3D Bio, America). The images were captured using silicon-SPM probes (HQ NSC18/AL BS) in tapping mode, with a typical tip radius of <7 nm. The contact angles were measured using a contact angle goniometer (XG-CAMC3, Shanghai Xuanyi-Guangxi, China). The XRD analysis was conducted on a Bruker D8 diffractometer using Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA, λ = 1.5406 Å) (D8 QUEST, Bruker, Germany). The CD spectra were measured using a Chirascan V100 spectrophotometer in quartz cuvettes (A66, Applied Photophysics Ltd). The membranes under test were affixed next to each other onto a quartz substrate for conducting CD measurements. The low-field 1H NMR spectrum was measured on a VTMR20-010V-I NMR analyzer (Suzhou Niumag Analytical Instrument Corporation, China). The surface tension of various solutions was measured using a surface tension meter (ELB-ZL1020). The porosity of the membrane was determined using a mercury intrusion porosimeter (MIP, Micromeritics AutoPore V 9620, America). The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were obtained using a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo-Fisher, America).

Tensile tests

The tested membranes were placed on a PET substrate that had been cut. General tensile tests were carried out on a vertical dynamometer (ESM303, MARK-10, America) at room temperature (25 °C) and RH 60%. The Instron tester is equipped with a calibrated 10 N load cell. The extension rate was 120 m min−1.

Nanoindentation test

The tested membranes were placed on the surface of the slide for testing and the pressing depth was controlled at 80 nm. The testing process uses the displacement control loading method with a standard Berkovich indenter (Hysitron TI 980, Bruker, Germany), meaning that the loading process is controlled using displacement as the signal. The test is divided into three stages: loading, load holding, and unloading, each taking 5 s-2 s-5 s respectively. The duration of each stage is the same for all three depth test processes.

5CB-welded AgNWs tests

The tested membranes were placed on the surface of the slide for testing and alcohol vapor was applied to the surface. Micron EVA fibers were prepared to simulate the aggregation behavior of AgNW-alcohol solution at the intersection (Dynamic processes cannot be recorded using SEM). The EVA adhesive stick was heated to 85 °C by a hot-melt glue gun. After the adhesive stick was completely melted, a molten drop was extruded from the metal nozzle of the glue gun and carefully attached to the surface of the slide for testing using forceps.

Self-healing tests

The tested membrane was placed horizontally on a glass substrate, and cracks were introduced with a syringe needle under a microscope. For healing, a hot table with a stable temperature of 37 oC was placed on the lower side of the glass substrate for 10 min.

Permeability test

All tested membranes were attached to the bottleneck of a glass bottle containing 5 g of deionized water. After fixing the membrane, the glass bottles were stored at 55% humidity, 25 °C, and 37 °C for 24 h. Weigh the glass bottles every 2 h. The results of water vapor transmission rate were calculated according to the equation: water vapor transmission rate = (w0−w1)/S⋅t, where w0 and w1 refer to the original weight and the measured weight after storing the sample for a period t. S is the area of water vapor transmission.

Biocompatibility tests

The human umbilical vein endothelial cells were purchased from Shanghai and maintained in a high-sugar DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA). T The cells were cultured at 37 °C in an incubator and supplied with 5% CO2. The medium should be changed every 2 days. After incubating the material with cells for 24 h, we assessed cytotoxicity using live/dead cell detection kits. The experimental data was repeated at least 3 times to ensure its validity. The cell viability of human umbilical vein endothelial cells treated with different methods was evaluated using the CCK8 method.

Acoustic sensing tests

The voltages of the all-in-one EPNs were measured using a nanovoltmeter (2182 A, Keithley, America). In all cases, samples fixed in a frame were positioned on a stand 10 cm away from the speaker (BT502, Hewlett-Packard, America). A signal generator was used to input the desired frequency and power into the speaker. To better distinguish the vibration mode of the membrane, a mirror was placed on the surface of the all-in-one EPN to redirect a laser beam towards a distant black wall. Any slight deformations of the all-in-one EPN under the mirrors were significantly amplified and detected as measurable laser spot movements on the vertical scale. The displacement and vibrational modal patterns of the all-in-one EPN were concurrently recorded using an iPhone while measuring the output voltage from the samples at different frequencies.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were repeated independently with similar results at least three times.
