Posthospitalization Primary Care Follow-Up and Health Care Utilization in Children With Medical Complexity
Authors: Nassr Nama, K. Casey Lion, Corrie E. McDaniel, Eric R. Coon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study examines rates of primary care follow-up within 14 days of a hospitalization for children with vs without medical complexity.

Introduction

Posthospitalization primary care practitioner (PCP) follow-up is widely recommended, particularly for children with medical complexity, as review of evolving symptoms, medication reconciliation, and care coordination may prevent readmissions.1,2 However, whether children with medical complexity consistently attend PCP follow-up and whether follow-up improves short-term outcomes remain uncertain.3 We hypothesized that children with medical complexity would be more likely than other children to complete PCP follow-up within 14 days of hospital discharge and that follow-up would be associated with lower readmission.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the IBM MarketScan Commercial Database (January 2017 to June 2024). The University of Washington institutional review board determined this study was exempt from review and informed consent because our database use was not human participants research. This study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

We identified hospitalizations for children and adolescents aged 1 to 18 years discharged home with continuous insurance enrollment for 365 days before admission and 30 days after discharge. We excluded hospitalizations related to psychiatric conditions, malignant neoplasms, or transplant or length of stay exceeding 30 days. The unit of analysis was the hospitalization, and children could contribute multiple hospitalizations. Children were classified as having medical complexity if they met criteria for either the Complex Chronic Conditions Classification System version 3.0, or the Pediatric Medical Complexity Algorithm version 3.1, based on diagnoses from the index hospitalization and the prior year.4 Index hospitalization primary diagnosis was categorized using the Pediatric Clinical Classification System. Outpatient follow-up visits within 14 days were identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes (99202-99205 and 99211-99215), excluding claims rendered by nonmedical practitioners (eg, dentistry, pharmacy). Visits were classified as primary care when the clinician specialty was pediatrics, family medicine, internal medicine, preventive medicine, nurse practitioner, or physician assistant.5 Other visits were classified as specialty care.

We compared PCP follow-up within 14 days using a covariate-adjusted discrete-time survival model with logistic regression, days-since-discharge indicators, and sandwich SEs clustered by enrollee (eMethods in Supplement 1). Observations contributed person-days until PCP follow-up, emergency department (ED) visit, readmission, or day 14.

Among hospitalizations for children with medical complexity, we used time-varying Cox models to estimate associations between PCP follow-up and 14-day outcomes, treating follow-up as a time-varying exposure, and censoring at 14 days (or at readmission, as applicable). These specifications ensured the correct ordering of exposure and outcome, such that the exposure was only ever counted prior to the outcome. Outcomes included all-cause readmission, ED visits, laboratory testing, and imaging.

Models were adjusted for prespecified covariates measured from the index hospitalization: age, sex, geographic region, insurance plan type, technology dependence, utilization in the previous 365 days, intensive care unit stay, length of stay, principal diagnosis, and discharge year. Sensitivity analyses used a 7-day window for follow-up and outcomes and excluded nurse practitioner and physician assistant visits from PCP follow-up. P values were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at P ≤ .05. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and R version 4.5.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing) from October 2025 to February 2026.

Results

Among 170 164 hospitalizations, median (IQR) patient age was 9 (4-15) years, 86 998 patients (51.1%) were male, and 72 883 patients (42.8%) had medical complexity. PCP follow-up occurred after 22 306 visits (30.6%) of children with medical complexity vs 40 089 visits (41.2%) of children without medical complexity (Table 1), with lower adjusted odds of PCP follow-up among children with medical complexity (adjusted odds ratio, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.80-0.84). The rate of follow-up for children with medical complexity remained lower when including specialty care (54 758 of 97 281 visits [52.0%] vs 37 922 of 72 883 visits [56.3%]).

In time-varying models restricted to hospitalizations of children with medical complexity, PCP follow-up was associated with greater subsequent health care utilization within 14 days, including readmission (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.31; 95% CI, 1.21-1.42), ED visits (aHR, 1.15; 95% CI, 1.06-1.24), laboratory testing (aHR, 2.74; 95% CI, 2.64-2.85), and imaging (aHR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.24-1.37) (Table 2). Findings were similar across sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

In this cohort study, contrary to our hypothesis, children with medical complexity were less likely than children without medical complexity to complete PCP follow-up after a hospitalization. Among children with medical complexity, PCP follow-up was not associated with lower short-term readmission and was associated with higher subsequent health care utilization.

These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that PCP follow-up causes greater health care use. Instead, the observed association likely reflects residual confounding by indication, in which children with greater acuity, unresolved symptoms, or caregiver concern were both more likely to complete follow-up and more likely to require subsequent care. It is also possible that PCP follow-up visits for children with medical complexity are not always necessary and can lead to overtreatment, as recently demonstrated in certain populations of children without medical complexity.6,7 Determining when PCP follow-up benefits children with medical complexity and when it adds burden or low-value care requires further investigation. Study limitations include limited clinical detail in claims to measure severity, inability to distinguish planned routine follow-up from visits prompted by worsening symptoms or concerns, and restriction to commercially insured children. Claims data cannot capture nonbilled follow-up (eg, clinician telephone check-ins), or important patient-centered outcomes (eg, burden vs reassurance for families).

In this cohort study, posthospitalization PCP follow-up was less common among children with medical complexity than other children, and PCP follow-up among children with medical complexity was not associated with lower short-term health care reutilization. These findings suggest that uniform expectations for PCP follow-up in children with medical complexity do not align with actual practice. Randomized clinical trials are needed to estimate the effects of follow-up on health care utilization and patient-centered outcomes and to identify which children with medical complexity are most likely to benefit.
