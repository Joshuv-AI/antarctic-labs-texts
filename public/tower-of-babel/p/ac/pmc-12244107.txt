Providers’ perspectives on the impact of culture in the recognition and diagnosis of anxiety☆
Authors: Darius B Dawson, Christie G-J Tsao, Patricia Chen, Traber D Giardina, Terri L Fletcher
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe examined providers’ perspectives about how culture affects diagnosing and recognizing anxiety.MethodsWe interviewed providers about diagnosing anxiety (“How do you think culture impacts expression and diagnosis of anxiety?”) and used inductive analysis, with open coding, to allow themes to emerge organically.ResultsWe identified three themes: (1) Impartiality toward Culture: downplaying culture in recognizing or diagnosing anxiety and not acknowledging necessary cultural diagnostic considerations; (2) Acknowledgment of Cultural Impact: detailed variations across different cultural communities in how patients present with and describe anxiety, with differing levels of stigma associated with expressing and treating anxiety; (3) Culturally Integrated Practice: implement culturally informed diagnosis and treatment, with training and self-directed processing on culture and bias.ConclusionsWhile most providers acknowledge that culture impacts accurate diagnosis of anxiety, they vary in how they integrate cultural information. It is important to deliver culturally informed care.Keywords: Anxiety, Culture, Diagnosis, Veterans, Qualitative

Introduction

Anxiety disorders are the most common mental illness in the United States, affecting approximately 19.1 % of adults annually [1], with nearly 33.7 % of people experiencing an anxiety disorder at some time [2]. Providers rely on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria and validated assessment tools to determine anxiety diagnoses, often based on Western constructs limiting understanding of culture [3], [4]. Due to lack of awareness, providers underuse V-Codes, Z-Codes, and cultural formulation guides in diagnostic materials that explore the impact of cultural influences on disorder development [5]. Despite well-established assessment processes, anxiety disorders are often misidentified among minoritized individuals, that is, individuals belonging to groups that are routinely marginalized or oppressed systemically (e.g., due to racial/ethnic background, gender/sexual identity, disability status) [6], [7]. Being minoritized can lead to unique cultural experiences, such as stigma, discrimination, and structural racism, which can impact the etiology of anxiety and potentially lead to inequity in diagnosis and treatment [8]. Individuals facing discrimination, such as LGBTQ individuals and people of color, often fear anticipated discrimination or persecution, possibly increasing their likelihood of developing chronic, severe anxiety [9], [10], [11].

Stigma of mental illness, along with histories of unethical and racist practices in healthcare, hinders individuals’ decision to express anxiety to mental health (MH) providers [6], [9], [12], [13]. Minoritized individuals with anxiety often face self-stigma and limited support around engaging in MH treatment, preventing some from seeking care [14], [15]. Additionally, stigma is associated with increased focus of physiological concerns of anxiety [9] and use of different reference words (e.g., stressed, black out, overwhelmed) for anxiety [16]. Individuals must also circumvent structural racism when seeking care sometimes perpetuated by healthcare providers, limiting their disclosure and preventing return to treatment [17]. Minoritized people display high levels of cultural mistrust towards the healthcare system, which delays treatment initiation, limits disclosure and development of therapeutic alliance, and exacerbates symptomology [1], [18].

Efforts to enhance provider cultural competence, considering the impact of implicit bias on differential treatment of patients, have recently increased [19]. Negative attitudes result in discriminatory actions limiting access to care and reinforcing health inequity [20]. Cultural competence is the ability to care for patients with awareness of diverse values, beliefs, and behaviors, and to tailor treatment delivery to patients’ cultural backgrounds [21], [22]. Patients of minoritized groups tend to desire providers that share or understand their culture but report infrequent encounters with such providers [23]. Despite established associations between culture and anxiety disorders, minoritized patients are still burdened with more severe symptoms, prolonged symptomology, and extended delays in receiving care. However, understanding providers’ thoughts on how culture impacts anxiety and methods they implement to account for culture in assessment and diagnosis of anxiety may impart important clinical implications. The goal of this study was to assess providers’ perspectives on how culture impacts their recognition, diagnosis, and clinical viewpoint of anxiety disorders. Although prior work has used experimental and survey designs to assess implicit bias, few qualitative studies elicit providers’ attitudes about the impact of culture on diagnosis [20].

Methods

Participants

Participants were 32 MH treatment providers from Veterans Health Administration (VHA) clinics nationally. Providers had to be active within the past 3 months and have diagnosed at least 10 patients with a specified or unspecified anxiety diagnosis during the study period (October 1, 2018 – September 30, 2019). A total of 440 providers were contacted, with a 7 % response rate. Our 7 % response rate was due in part to reaching our required participant sample. Qualitative studies aim to provider an in-depth understanding, requiring fewer respondents than quantitative studies [24] The suggested sample size for qualitative studies using a directed content approach similar to this study is 10–20 to gather narrowed, dense, and strong qualitative data [25]. Consented providers completed an audio-only interview via Zoom. Providers were asked for permission to record. The study was approved by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board.

Interview

Study interview data about the impact of culture on anxiety were a subset of a larger interview on provider perspectives on diagnosis of specific anxiety disorders. The guide for the larger study, iteratively developed by the study team, examined providers’ perspectives on knowledge of specific anxiety disorders and attitudes on how specific diagnoses impacted health outcomes. The study team conducted two mock interviews with VHA MH providers (data not included here) to finalize questions. Interviews were completed by the team’s qualitative methodologist (PC). Data included here stemmed from the question, “How do you think culture impacts the expression and diagnosis of anxiety?” We collected provider demographic information at close of the interview. Interviews lasted 45–60 min and were recorded and transcribed. Providers were not compensated for participating.

Analysis

Authors conducted directed content analysis of interview transcripts, using several steps [26]. First, two authors (DBD and CGJT) created a preliminary codebook representing theories and concepts from the literature review. The same two used Atlas.ti 8 Windows qualitative software to apply codes to the transcripts and refine the codebook. Next, they coded transcripts independently, applying codes to full sentences that descriptively represented the code. The research team met biweekly to compare coding and resolve discrepancies. The two authors (DBD and CGJT) exported quotations for all codes into Microsoft Excel to summarize results. They then read and summarized quotes within each code to understand providers’ overarching perspectives, while noting patterns in cultural factors, and then summarized codes into themes representing patterns in perspectives towards culture and the degree of the relationship between cultural factors and anxiety diagnosis. The team created and discussed preliminary themes. Once a salient theme emerged, relevant descriptions and quotations were assigned. Deidentified raw data, interview guide, and codebook are available upon request. Data will be shared via Atlas.ti 8 Windows software and/or through a secure VHA shared folder.

Results

Theme 1: impartiality towards impact of culture on anxiety diagnosis and patients’ stigma in expressing anxiety and seeking treatment

A small subset of providers indicated limited connection between culture and anxiety, highlighting neutral and static approaches when considering anxiety. These providers aligned with empirically supported, diagnostically driven assessment and practice approaches, standardized across patients. Their responses indicated a weak relationship between culture and anxiety, seemingly a “culture-blind” perspective ignoring implicit bias. Providers refrained from addressing the relationship between culture and anxiety, and techniques used to assess and address culture acknowledging the impact their training had on their diagnosis of anxiety, which hindered cultural analysis of patient concerns. Providers noted that the unilateral, hierarchical training model impacts whether cultural assessment is taught effectively, acknowledging that the culture of the training environment can supersede patient-centered care. Providers also downplayed patients’ stigma about anxiety symptoms and potential cultural barriers in seeking help. For providers, having a family background of “high achievers” contributed to thinking of “anxiety as something that’s not necessarily a bad thing.” They normalized anxiety and being diagnosed with anxiety disorders for themselves but did not acknowledge that patients may not necessarily share their perspective.

Theme 2: acknowledgment patients’ cultures can impact expression of anxiety and willingness to seek treatment

Most providers indicated culture impacts patients’ expression of anxiety, reflected in recognition and understanding of anxiety across patients’ identities. Providers acknowledged a variety of cultural backgrounds to consider when diagnosing anxiety, such as gender, race/ethnicity, medical/disability status, veteran status, nationality, and religion. They agreed a cultural perspective on anxiety diagnosis must extend beyond Western diagnostically driven approaches to account for patients’ experiences. They assumed responsibility for recognizing culture and acknowledging potential implicit bias and discrimination-based anxiety, describing many ways anxiety can manifest differently across cultural backgrounds. They described how some cultures “have a different way of labeling” anxiety, and patients use “words that are, not necessarily, ‘I’m anxious’” (African American, male, social worker, Masters). They detailed the important distinction between physical and emotional anxiety symptoms and how certain groups are more likely to report physical symptoms. Discussions around how patients present with anxiety alluded to stigma patients experience when seeking MH treatment. Some descriptions were direct, such as: “African American males, specifically, they really did not want to open up and talk about it. Because it just wasn’t discussed in their homes.” (White, female social worker, Masters).

Theme 3: modifying the diagnostic process to account for both their own and their patients’ cultural impact on recognition of anxiety

Some providers described methods they use to address cultural influences and account for implicit bias, describing assessing cultural impact as part of their process with all patients to prevent potential clinical errors. Providers acknowledged the importance of evaluating culture when making specific and accurate anxiety diagnoses and the bidirectional relationship between culture and development of anxiety. They noted that diagnostic measures are limited in assessing cultural factors associated with anxiety and perceived therapeutic benefits in integrating cultural themes into clinical practice. Some explained how exploring patients’ perspectives on related cultural themes can enhance therapeutic alliance. Providers described routinely processing interactions with patients of different backgrounds and focusing on personal growth to overcome biases that may impact care. First, providers endorsed using empathy to help patients dealing with stigma or having difficulty in disclosing anxiety. Additionally, providers described evaluating their own culture and implicit biases and how they may impact clinical interactions. They also described their experiences “both inside and outside professional training” that helped them “engage with anybody that comes through the door” (African American, male, psychiatrist, M.D., M.P.H.). Some emphasize clinicians’ focusing on their own MH and self-care to best serve patients. Many agreed to getting on a “clean slate” between sessions and after work to prevent personal issues and biases from interfering with patient care.

Discussion

Our findings indicate most MH providers acknowledge a relationship between culture and anxiety disorder development, diagnosis, and treatment. Providers’ perspectives support literature detailing differential presentation of anxiety based on patients’ cultural backgrounds, highlighting preferred expression of physiological symptoms [7], [9]. Results suggest most MH providers strive to provide culturally sensitive, informed care, while attending to potential implicit biases. Literature indicates providers who perform culturally competent care have better patient treatment outcomes and develop stronger therapeutic alliance [23], [27]. Patients connected to providers valuing culture report feeling a sense of dignity and respect, which positively impacts long-term care [28].

We found some providers ignore cultural differences and the impact of implicit bias, taking a “culture-blind” approach to diagnosis, potentially compromising treatment outcomes. Despite our diverse sample, this approach suggests providers rely solely on diagnostic criteria without acknowledging potential cultural influences on symptom presentation [3]. This is concerning, given research suggesting that current diagnostic classification systems, such as the DSM-5, perpetuate racism and ignore social determinants of health [4], [29]. Implicit bias is often activated under stressful working conditions and reinforced through repeated clinical interactions, with patients’ intersecting identities also challenging providers [20]. Furthermore, providers who ignore implicit bias misdiagnose affective disorders and ignore evidence-based guidelines for minoritized populations, resulting in negative, discriminatory treatment outcomes [20], [30]. The impact of implicit bias requires routine professional and institutional awareness and training to address problematic bias [20], [30].

Culturally competent providers take responsibility to understand cultural aspects of health, acknowledge implicit bias, and combat discrimination in healthcare settings [27]. However, providers in our study highlight challenges in providing culturally sensitive care,examining and understanding their own implicit bias and performing routine self-care to prevent clinical errors. Our work supports research suggesting culturally aware providers combine different diagnostic processes to assess cultural influences [31]. Providers’ curiosity about cultural influences may increase understanding of patients’ experiences and reveal their own implicit biases, leading to proactive efforts to provide culturally competent care. Providers’ limited understanding of patients’ cultural experiences may be representative of their implicit biases, allowing for opportunities to eliminate clinical error. Providers in our study detailed the emotional toil of routinely monitoring cultural awareness. Our findings support literature emphasizing development and implementation of trainings to reduce implicit bias in the MH system, especially to account for difficult clinical interactions [30].

Our findings have important clinical implications. Despite most providers assessing cultural influences in patients’ anxiety, patients may not receive accurate MH diagnoses without assessment of cultural factors. More professional trainings need to enhance cultural understanding and reduce implicit bias to account for historical precedence of traditional professional trainings created by White men for White people [29]. Research should continue to explore how to apply culturally competent care, without compromising therapeutic alliance. Additionally, providers acknowledge therapeutic and professional benefits of routine emotional processing. Healthcare systems may consider integrating practices aligned with health disparities frameworks that emphasize combination of multilevel factors (e.g., patient stigma, provider burden, workforce demand, cultural bias in diagnosis) when addressing burden [32]. Furthermore, stigma of MH treatment is a barrier to treatment for many. Healthcare systems might consider implementing culturally informed interventions that address stigma and educate providers on the impact of stigma.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Our results of cultural impacts are based on a subset of data, and provider responses might differ using a larger interview. Future research should examine the relationship of culture in anxiety diagnosis and treatment to support these findings. Second, provider responses were not connected to clinical outcomes; and we cannot infer positive or negative clinical outcomes.
