A LION-optimized GAN for multi-target battery state prediction in battery management system
Authors: Khaled Saleem S. Alatawi, Fahad M. Almasoudi, Wala R. Abd-ElRahman, Husam S. Samkari, Muhammad Shoaib Bhutta, Ghulam Rasool, Abdulelah S. Alatawi, Fahad Y. Alwasabi, M. Tariq Nazir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurately predicting battery target variables is essential for reliable battery management, but the nonlinear and time-varying nature of batteries makes this task difficult. This study introduces GAN-LION, a conditional adversarial regression framework designed for multi-target battery prediction using historical operating sequences. Unlike traditional noise-driven GANs, the proposed model formulates prediction as a deterministic sequence-to-one regression task, where the generator predicts the next target value from a lookback window and the discriminator judges whether the input-target pair aligns with the real data distribution. To specifically examine the impact of optimization, the same adversarial architecture is trained with either the Lion or Adam optimizer and is also compared with CNN-LSTM-GRU and Autoencoder-LSTM baselines. A single end-to-end pipeline is used for preprocessing, normalization, temporal sequence construction, and chronological train-validation-test splitting to avoid information leakage. Results from two benchmark battery datasets indicate that GAN-LION achieves the strongest overall performance. Its advantage is most evident on the more challenging dataset, where it yields lower prediction errors, smoother convergence, and more stable gradient behavior. On the simpler dataset, the performance differences are less pronounced, but GAN-LION still remains competitive and stable. Overall, these results suggest that conditional adversarial regression with Lion-based optimization is an effective and technically sound method for battery target prediction.

Introduction

The performance of lithium-ion batteries within a battery management system can be guaranteed through accurate prediction and estimation of important battery parameters. These parameters include discharge capacity, voltage, current, state of charge, state of health, and the remaining useful life. This will ensure proper utilization of the battery. The modeling of voltage and current behavior within the BMS forms the basis for these predictions1. Nevertheless, due to the inherent nonlinearity of batteries and their dependence on use and external environment, it becomes necessary to create highly accurate models for successful implementation of BMS. The modeling and filtering approaches have some drawbacks, such as the sensitivity of parameters determination, performance degradation resulting from aging, and higher computing complexity with increasing model complexity2. This is why data-driven methods based on machine learning and deep learning algorithms have gained significant importance for the estimation of state-of-charge (SOC) and state-of-health (SOH), as well as remaining useful life (RUL)3. The recent development of artificial neural network algorithms such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, and attention-based mechanisms have demonstrated impressive ability in feature extraction from voltage and current signals4.

Hybrid architectures which leverage the benefits of both CNN and LSTM frameworks in addition to self-attention techniques exhibit superior ability to detect spatio-temporal information leading to enhanced SOC estimation results5. Moreover, transfer learning increases the flexibility of models for adapting to new chemistries and conditions, rendering the use cases for such models broader6. In addition to the above, VMD is used as preprocessing technique to filter out noise from battery time-series data and eliminate anomalies, contributing to enhanced SOH estimation precision7. To summarize, deep learning-based battery management system architectures are changing the landscape of real-time battery monitoring. Deep learning based BMS models offer flexible solutions capable of tackling nonlinearity and uncertainties associated with batteries8. In the future, efforts should be directed towards using different types of batteries under various conditions for testing purposes.

Due to the constraints associated with model-based techniques alone, the past decade has witnessed growing attention toward the development of data-driven and hybrid models for SOC, SOH, and RUL prediction of lithium-ion batteries9. Modeling the highly non-linear and condition-dependent degradation process is still an issue that needs to be addressed, especially for SOH predictions. Hybrid schemes combining CNNs and LSTM neural networks have been successful in mitigating these issues and predicting more accurately10–12. Recent works involving the extraction of multiple features through engineering and using GRUs have demonstrated the effectiveness of utilizing long-range dependencies in time-series data to improve the performance of both SOH and RUL predictions13–15. Further research has focused on multi sensor fusion and conditioning on operating conditions for improved SOC estimation, which has become very important in EVs due to the requirement of real time in situ monitoring16. In addition to classic deep learning models, new efforts have been made towards variational quantum algorithms and novel optimization algorithms to improve the estimation of SOH; researchers have reported superior performance compared to traditional benchmarks17. Physics-based approaches such as hybrid models that combine electrochemistry and data driven learning approaches have also been introduced to increase reliability in BMS designs18. For instance, sequence-to-sequence neural networks combined with H infinity filtering can help in reducing noise and improving the accuracy of the SOC estimation process in different environments.

A related line of work has explored integrated SOC, SOH, and RUL estimation by combining physics-based modeling with machine learning, showing encouraging results for improving both the efficiency and operational safety of electric vehicles19. Even so, important challenges remain. Among the most pressing are the need for larger and more diverse training datasets, the design of lightweight models that can run in real time, reliable generalization across different cell chemistries, temperatures, and aging scenarios, and better interpretability of both the extracted features and the resulting predictions20. As electric vehicles and grid-scale energy storage systems continue to expand, the need for more reliable and capable battery management technologies will only become more critical.

Accurate estimation of the state of health (SOH) and state of charge (SOC) is critical for prolonging the service life of lithium-ion batteries and ensuring safe operation in electric vehicles and stationary energy storage systems21. To address these challenges, a hybrid neural network incorporating multi-scale convolutional attention has been proposed for SOH estimation in fast-charging lithium-ion batteries. The results indicate that this hybrid model can effectively extract degradation-related features from fast-charging data, reduce overfitting, and improve both adaptability and stability under new environmental conditions22. On the other hand, the evolutionary stacking ensemble framework based on the integration of random vector functional link networks and active state tracking LSTMs has been studied to estimate the SOH23. The proposed models utilized the hyperparameter optimization method, i.e., Heap Based Optimizer, for enhancing the performance and generalization capability of SOH prediction models in various applications24. Moreover, temperature-aware transfer learning approaches integrating electrochemical impedance spectroscopy have been used to adapt SOH models in different temperature ranges25. For instance, it has been noted that an SOH model developed in 25 °C could be transferred to the 45 °C environment with acceptable accuracy. Thus, the proposed technique helps to reduce the training data significantly by transferring knowledge from previous tasks, which may be challenging in real-world scenarios where the data acquisition process is costly and time-consuming26,27.

Strategies for transfer learning that account for considerations such as the role of temperature and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) are already demonstrating their potential in mitigating the costs involved in the retraining process when transferring models between varying operational domains28. Meanwhile, multi sensor fusion serves as another avenue to improve robustness in view of the dynamic and varied conditions that arise within electric vehicles. Physics-based and electrochemical hybrid models are other methods that can be adopted in dealing with parametric drift and improving generalization among batteries of various kinds29,30. Finally, there has been progress in employing generative modeling to tackle trajectory and rarely seen cases of degradation within the dataset31–33.

However, many important issues still exist that need to be addressed by BMSs that are ready for deployment. One issue is the problem of making robust models for changes in the domain that could result from alterations in the cell chemistry, operating temperature, and duty cycle without having to incur a significant amount of training effort34–36. Another important issue relates to accuracy and balance between the different predictions37–39. Third, explicit and quantifiable evidence of real time feasibility at battery management system sampling rates is often lacking in existing literature40,41. Recent studies have also explored federated learning for battery management systems, particularly for SOC estimation in electric-vehicle fleets, where privacy preservation and scalable multi-vehicle learning are important. These approaches keep battery data local while aggregating model updates centrally. In contrast, the present study focuses on a centralized benchmark-learning setting to clarify the regression formulation, optimizer contribution, and comparative behavior of the proposed GAN-LION framework under controlled conditions. Thus, the present work is complementary to federated BMS research and may serve as a useful basis for future federated extensions42.

In contrast to existing battery prediction methods that rely mainly on conventional deep learning regressors or standard optimizer settings, the present study formulates battery prediction as a conditional adversarial regression task rather than a classical noise-driven GAN task. Instead of generating unrestricted samples from latent noise, the proposed framework predicts the next target value directly from a historical lookback sequence, while the discriminator evaluates whether the resulting input-target pair is conditionally consistent with the observed data distribution. This design makes the framework more suitable for deterministic sequence-to-one prediction of discharge capacity, current, and voltage. Furthermore, the novelty provided by this paper goes beyond the application of Lion-based optimizer into the structure of the GAN model but also involves isolation of its impact via controlled comparison between GAN-LION and GAN-Adam under the same architecture and loss structure. The research is further strengthened by the use of a comprehensive evaluation framework that includes chronological data splitting, repeated experimental runs, significance testing, convergence analysis, gradient norm comparison, and computational cost assessment.

The purpose of this problem is to predict the important characteristics of batteries such as discharge capacity, voltage, and current by using historical data of their operating status. To investigate this problem, three deep learning architectures are considered: the proposed GAN-LION framework, a CNN-LSTM-GRU baseline, and an Autoencoder-LSTM baseline. Dataset characteristics and reproducibility protocol

Two benchmark battery datasets were employed in this study. Dataset 1 refers to the NASA B0005 lithium-ion battery dataset, whereas Dataset 2 refers to the Toyota Research Institute CH42 dataset. In both datasets, the records were arranged in chronological order and used for sequential prediction. The final learning task was carried out at the cycle level, which means that the models operated on ordered cycle-wise battery records rather than on a single uniform raw sampling frequency during the final prediction stage. The target variables considered in this work were discharge capacity, current, and voltage.

For consistent and reproducible evaluation, an identical data-processing protocol was used for all models. Invalid or incomplete records were discarded, while the remaining samples were kept in their original chronological order. Historical lookback sequences were then generated from the ordered battery records using a sequence length of 12 steps, matching the input dimensions adopted in the experiments. To enhance numerical stability, the input variables were normalized before training. Notably, the normalization was fitted only on the training subset and then applied without modification to the validation and test subsets.

To prevent temporal leakage and support a more realistic deployment-oriented evaluation, each dataset was split chronologically into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% test subsets. The training subset was used for parameter learning, the validation subset for model selection and convergence tracking, and the test subset for final performance assessment. The same preprocessing, normalization, sequence generation, and data-splitting protocol was applied consistently to GAN-LION, GAN-Adam, CNN-LSTM-GRU, and Autoencoder-LSTM, thereby ensuring a fair and reproducible comparison.

Data preprocessing, feature construction, and temporal sequence generation

Battery operating data are naturally nonlinear and time dependent, and they are often influenced by measurement noise, missing records, and abnormal observations. If these issues are not addressed properly, they can distort the training process and produce overly optimistic or unreliable prediction outcomes. For this reason, a structured preprocessing pipeline was implemented before model development. First, the raw battery records were inspected to detect incomplete entries, inconsistent measurements, and obvious anomalous samples. Invalid records were removed, while the remaining data were retained in their original chronological order to preserve the natural progression of battery behavior throughout the analysis.

To enhance numerical stability and promote balanced model optimization, the input features were normalized before training. Because battery variables can vary considerably in scale and dynamic range, normalization helps prevent features with larger magnitudes from dominating the learning process and supports more stable parameter updates across different models. Importantly, all preprocessing transformations were fitted only on the training subset and then applied without modification to the validation and test subsets. This approach prevents information leakage from future samples and ensures a fair comparison among the competing architectures.

After preprocessing and feature construction, the entire dataset was split into training, validation, and test subsets in chronological order rather than by random shuffling. This decision is important for battery prediction because the real-world goal is to estimate future target values from past observations, not from randomly arranged samples. The training subset was used for learning model parameters, the validation subset for model selection and convergence tracking, and the test subset for final performance assessment. By maintaining temporal order throughout the full data pipeline, the study offers a more realistic and deployment-relevant evaluation of model performance.

The chronological split further minimizes the risk of temporal leakage, which may occur when information from future battery states is unintentionally introduced during training. This issue is particularly critical in degradation-related tasks, where neighboring samples often exhibit strong correlations. Therefore, the adopted sequence-generation and data-splitting strategy was intended not only to enhance predictive performance but also to improve methodological rigor and reproducibility. This preprocessing framework establishes a consistent foundation for training both the proposed GAN-LION model and the baseline deep learning models under fair and practically relevant conditions.

Proposed conditional adversarial regression framework

Motivation and conceptual design

To improve battery target prediction under nonlinear and time-dependent operating conditions, a conditional adversarial regression framework is proposed in this study. The central idea is to combine sequence modeling and adversarial learning in a way that is better matched to deterministic battery prediction than a classical noise-driven generative adversarial network. In the present problem, the objective is not to generate unrestricted synthetic samples, but to infer the next battery target from an observed history of operating data. For this reason, the proposed framework is conditioned on the historical battery sequence and is designed as a sequence-to-one regression model.

More specifically, the generator receives a lookback window of historical battery observations and predicts the corresponding next-step target, while the discriminator evaluates whether the resulting input-target pair is consistent with the real data distribution. In this way, adversarial learning is introduced not to replace direct regression, but to complement it. The generator is guided not only to reduce numerical prediction error, but also to generate outputs that are consistent with the temporal structure of the observed battery behavior. This is especially important in battery applications, where the target trajectory is typically smooth, physically constrained, and highly influenced by recent operating history.

Figure 2 presents the theoretical framework of the proposed model. As shown in the figure, the same historical input sequence is shared by both the generator and the discriminator. The generator maps the input sequence to a scalar prediction, whereas the discriminator judges whether the input-target pair corresponds to a real sample or a generated one. This conditional design distinguishes the proposed framework from a conventional vanilla GAN and provides a more suitable formulation for next-step battery target prediction.

Generator network

The generator is responsible for learning the mapping from the historical battery sequence to the next-step target. Its input consists of a multivariate lookback sequence that contains the recent temporal evolution of the battery variables. The role of the generator is therefore to extract informative sequential patterns from this history and transform them into a compact scalar prediction.

In the proposed implementation, the generator is built around a long short-term memory (LSTM) encoder followed by a fully connected regression head. The LSTM is employed because battery behavior depends on sequential relationships rather than isolated observations. It is able to capture short-term fluctuations, slowly evolving trends, and local transitions that are difficult to model using purely feedforward architectures. Once the input sequence has been processed by the LSTM, the hidden representation at the final time step serves as a compact summary of the historical operating state. This representation is then passed through fully connected layers, a nonlinear activation function, and dropout regularization before generating the final scalar output.

This design enables the generator to function as a sequence-to-one regression model while maintaining a relatively simple architecture. It is expressive enough to learn nonlinear temporal relationships, yet compact enough to remain practical for repeated training, optimizer comparison, and convergence analysis. In addition, because the generator predicts the next target value directly from the observed history, the model preserves physical interpretability at the task level, since each prediction is conditioned on real battery observations rather than arbitrary latent noise.

Discriminator network

The discriminator is structured to determine whether a candidate target value is consistent with the historical battery sequence from which it originates. Rather than evaluating a sample independently, it takes as input both the temporal sequence and the associated scalar target value. This target can either be the true measured value or the value generated by the predictor. As a result, the discriminator learns the conditional relationship between the battery’s historical behavior and the plausibility of the corresponding target.

To support this objective, the discriminator also employs an LSTM-based temporal encoder. The input sequence is first processed to produce a hidden representation that summarizes the recent operating behavior of the battery. This encoded sequence representation is then concatenated with the candidate scalar target and passed through a fully connected classification head. By integrating temporal context and the target value within a single decision pathway, the discriminator is able to judge whether the predicted output is coherent with the observed battery history.

This conditional discrimination mechanism is a key aspect of the proposed framework. In a conventional GAN setting, the discriminator usually separates real from generated samples without explicitly considering the measured input sequence that should determine the prediction. By contrast, the proposed model focuses on conditional consistency. As a result, the discriminator delivers a more informative supervisory signal for regression, particularly when the target is expected to follow physically constrained temporal behavior, as in battery prediction tasks.

Joint learning mechanism and architectural summary

The proposed framework integrates direct regression learning and adversarial learning within a single training process. The generator is responsible for predicting the correct next-step target, while the discriminator encourages those predictions to remain conditionally realistic with respect to the observed historical sequence. In practical terms, this means that the generator is trained not only to minimize prediction error, but also to produce outputs that are more consistent with the underlying sequence structure captured by the discriminator.

This joint learning strategy offers two complementary advantages. First, the regression component improves numerical accuracy by directly penalizing the difference between the predicted and true target values. Second, the adversarial component enhances structural consistency by discouraging unrealistic input-output relationships. For battery applications, where the target variable is strongly influenced by recent temporal context and underlying physical constraints, this combined approach can be more effective than relying on regression loss alone.

Figure 3 presents the layer-wise structure of the proposed conditional adversarial model, while Table 1 provides a detailed summary of the generator and discriminator architectures. The generator consists of an LSTM layer followed by fully connected regression layers, whereas the discriminator includes an LSTM encoder and a conditional classification head that operates on the concatenated sequence representation and target value. Importantly, the same architecture is adopted for both GAN-LION and GAN-Adam. As a result, the optimizer comparison presented later in the paper is not influenced by architectural differences. Any performance differences observed between the two variants can therefore be attributed primarily to optimization behavior rather than network design.

Overall, the proposed conditional adversarial regression framework is designed to offer a more appropriate learning structure for battery target prediction than a conventional GAN formulation. By conditioning both the generator and discriminator on historical battery observations, the model preserves the deterministic nature of the task while still benefiting from adversarial regularization. This makes the framework conceptually sound, practically trainable, and well suited to the demands of sequential battery target prediction.

This mapping is written as2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{\widehat{y}}_{t}={G}_{\theta\:}\left({\mathbf{X}}_{t}\right).\end{array}$$\end{document}.

In contrast to a classical vanilla GAN, the proposed generator does not take a random latent noise vector as input. Instead, it directly operates on the observed historical battery sequence. This makes the formulation consistent with deterministic sequence-to-one regression rather than unrestricted sample generation.

The discriminator, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{\phi\:}(\cdot\:,\cdot\:)$$\end{document} and parameterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\phi\:$$\end{document}, is conditioned on both the historical sequence and a candidate scalar target. It receives either the real conditional pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left({\mathbf{X}}_{t},{y}_{t}\right)$$\end{document} or the generated conditional pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left({\mathbf{X}}_{t},{\widehat{y}}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}, and outputs a scalar score indicating whether the pair is real or generated:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{D}_{\phi\:}({\mathbf{X}}_{t},{\stackrel{\sim}{y}}_{t}),{\stackrel{\sim}{y}}_{t}\in\:\{{y}_{t},{\widehat{y}}_{t}\}\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\stackrel{\sim}{y}}_{t}$$\end{document} represents a candidate target value, which can be either the true value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{t}$$\end{document} or the generator output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\widehat{y}}_{t}$$\end{document}. Under this formulation, the discriminator does not assess the target in isolation; instead, it determines whether the target is plausible in relation to the corresponding historical battery sequence. This conditional formulation provides a more informative adversarial signal for battery prediction than a traditional unconditional discriminator.

To train the discriminator, binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss is employed to distinguish real conditional pairs from generated conditional pairs. The discriminator objective is defined as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{L}}_{D} = \frac{1}{2} \left[{\mathcal{L}}_{{{\mathrm{BCE}}}} \left( {{\mathrm{D}}_{{\phi \:}} ({\mathbf{X}}_{{\mathrm{t}}} ,{\mathrm{y}}_{{\mathrm{t}}} ),{\mathrm{1}}} \right){\text{ + }}{\mathcal{L}}_{{{\mathrm{BCE}}}} \left( {{\mathrm{D}}_{{\phi \:}} ({\mathbf{X}}_{{\mathrm{t}}} ,\hat{\text{{y}}}_{{\mathrm{t}}} ),{\mathrm{0}}} \right) \right]}$$\end{document}

In this formulation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{\mathrm{B}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{E}}$$\end{document} denotes the binary cross-entropy loss, where the label \:1 corresponds to a real conditional pair and the label \:0 corresponds to a generated conditional pair. The factor ½ is introduced to balance the contributions of real and generated samples during each discriminator update.

The generator, in turn, is optimized using a joint objective that combines adversarial supervision with direct regression learning. Its loss is expressed as:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathcal{L}}_{G} = \lambda \:_{{{\mathrm{adv}}}} {\mathcal{L}}_{{{\mathrm{BCE}}}} \left( {{\mathrm{D}}_{{\phi \:}} ({\mathbf{X}}_{{\mathrm{t}}} ,\hat{\text{{y}}}_{{\mathrm{t}}} ),{\mathrm{1}}} \right){\text{ + }}\lambda \:_{{{\mathrm{reg}}}} {\mathcal{L}}_{{{\mathrm{reg}}}} (\hat{\text{{y}}}_{{\mathrm{t}}} ,{\mathrm{y}}_{{\mathrm{t}}} ),}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{v}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\lambda\:}_{\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{g}}$$\end{document} are non-negative weighting coefficients for the adversarial and regression components, respectively. The first term guides the generator to produce predictions that the discriminator recognizes as conditionally realistic, while the second term directly penalizes the numerical difference between the predicted value and the true target.

In the implemented framework, the regression component is defined using the L1 loss:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{g}}({\widehat{y}}_{t},{y}_{t})=\parallel\:{\widehat{y}}_{t}-{y}_{t}{\parallel\:}_{1}\end{array}$$\end{document}

This formulation makes the generator objective both numerically grounded and structurally informed. In practice, the regression term carries the dominant weight, while the adversarial term serves as a regularizer that promotes conditionally consistent outputs. Thus, the generator is trained not only to minimize pointwise prediction error, but also to preserve coherence between the predicted target and the observed battery history.

To evaluate whether Lion provides an optimization advantage under the same architecture, the proposed framework is trained with either Adam or Lion, producing the GAN-Adam and GAN-LION variants, respectively. Since the generator, discriminator, and loss functions remain unchanged, any observed differences between these two variants can be attributed primarily to the optimization strategy rather than to network design.

The Lion optimizer is a sign-based momentum method. In the comparative experiments, the same alternating optimization scheme is retained when Adam is used in place of Lion. Therefore, the comparison between GAN-LION and GAN-Adam isolates the effect of the optimizer under a fixed conditional adversarial architecture.

To further improve training stability and ensure a fair comparison, gradient clipping is applied during optimization, and the best-performing model checkpoint is selected according to validation loss. These measures help limit the effects of unstable gradient spikes and improve reproducibility, particularly in adversarial learning settings. Overall, the proposed GAN-LION framework should not be viewed as a classical generative model, but rather as a conditional adversarial regressor whose optimization behavior is explicitly analyzed under Lion and Adam. This formulation is mathematically consistent with the prediction task, clearer in notation, and more closely aligned with the actual training procedure followed in this study.

Baseline models

To evaluate the proposed GAN-LION framework in a fair and technically meaningful manner, two deep learning baseline models were selected for comparison: a CNN-LSTM-GRU model and an Autoencoder-LSTM model. These baselines were chosen because they represent two distinct yet widely adopted strategies for sequential prediction. The CNN-LSTM-GRU model integrates convolution-based local feature extraction with recurrent temporal modeling, while the Autoencoder-LSTM model focuses on latent representation learning combined with sequence prediction. By comparing the proposed conditional adversarial framework with these baselines under the same data pipeline and evaluation protocol, the study can determine whether the observed performance improvements arise from the adversarial-regression formulation and optimizer behavior rather than from generic sequence modeling alone.

CNN-LSTM-GRU baseline

The CNN-LSTM-GRU model is used as a strong hybrid baseline for next-step battery target prediction. The reason for selecting this model is that battery signals often exhibit both short-term local patterns and long-term temporal dependencies. Short-range variations may result from transient operating fluctuations, whereas long-term dependencies reflect the gradual evolution of battery behavior over time or across cycles. For this reason, a hybrid convolutional-recurrent architecture is well suited to capture these two complementary characteristics of the data.

In the implemented baseline, the input sequence is first reorganized so that one-dimensional convolution can be applied along the temporal axis. Next, two Conv1D layers with nonlinear activation are used to extract localized temporal features from the multivariate battery sequence. This convolutional front-end enables the model to detect short-window patterns and local signal structures that may not be captured effectively by purely recurrent architectures. After this local feature extraction stage, the sequence is converted back into a format suitable for recurrent processing and then passed through an LSTM layer followed by a GRU layer. The LSTM is responsible for capturing longer temporal dependencies and memory effects, while the GRU further refines the learned representation using a more compact gated recurrent mechanism. Finally, the last hidden state is passed to a fully connected regression head to generate the prediction target.

Figure 4 presents the overall architecture of the CNN-LSTM-GRU baseline model. As illustrated in the figure, the model is divided into three main functional stages: local feature extraction, temporal modeling, and regression prediction. In the first stage, temporal convolution is applied to capture local patterns from the historical battery sequence. In the second stage, LSTM and GRU layers are used to learn the temporal dynamics of the sequence over time.

Overall, the CNN-LSTM-GRU model serves as a robust non-adversarial reference baseline. By integrating local temporal feature extraction with sequential dependency learning within a unified architecture, it provides an appropriate benchmark for assessing whether the proposed GAN-LION framework offers advantages beyond those attainable with a well-constructed hybrid recurrent baseline.

Autoencoder-LSTM baseline

The Autoencoder-LSTM model is adopted as a second baseline to reflect an alternative modeling approach centered on latent representation learning. In contrast to the CNN-LSTM-GRU baseline, which derives features directly through convolutional and recurrent operations, the Autoencoder-LSTM framework first encodes the input sequence into a lower-dimensional latent representation and then uses this compressed feature space for prediction. This baseline is especially suitable for battery data, where multivariate time-series signals often exhibit redundancy, correlation, and noise. By learning a compact latent representation, the model may retain the most informative structural patterns in the sequence while reducing the influence of irrelevant variations.

In the Autoencoder-LSTM architecture, the encoder maps each input feature vector into a compact latent representation using a series of fully connected layers. The resulting latent sequence is then utilized in two parallel branches. In the first branch, it is fed into a decoder that reconstructs the original input, which encourages the latent space to preserve the key structural information contained in the input features. In the second branch, the same latent sequence is passed to an LSTM-based predictor that captures the temporal dynamics of the encoded representation and converts it into a next-step prediction through a regression head. Through this design, the model integrates reconstruction and prediction within a single unified framework.

Figure 5 illustrates the architecture of the Autoencoder-LSTM baseline. It emphasizes the model’s two-branch structure: a reconstruction branch that retains the information content of the input sequence, and a prediction branch that concentrates on temporal forecasting. Because of this setup, the baseline is more organized than a standard feedforward or recurrent regressor, since the latent space is shaped by both reconstruction accuracy and predictive value. In battery applications, this can be especially useful when the raw sequence includes correlated or noisy features that might otherwise make direct prediction more difficult.

From a comparative standpoint, the Autoencoder-LSTM model serves as a useful benchmark because it helps determine whether compressed latent representations by themselves are enough to achieve strong battery-target prediction. If the proposed GAN-LION framework performs better than this baseline under the same preprocessing, data splitting, and evaluation settings, then the improvement is less likely to come only from representation compression and more likely to result from the advantages of the conditional adversarial learning strategy.

Role of the baseline comparison

The two baseline models were chosen not merely as conventional comparison targets, but as technically meaningful references that help clarify the contribution of the proposed framework. The CNN-LSTM-GRU baseline represents a hybrid local-to-global temporal learning strategy, while the Autoencoder-LSTM baseline represents a latent-representation learning strategy with reconstruction-assisted prediction. Together, they form a balanced benchmark set covering two important families of deep learning methods for sequential battery prediction.

This comparison is important for the interpretation of the results. If the proposed GAN-LION framework demonstrates improved performance relative to both baselines under the same input representation, chronological split, and evaluation criteria, then the improvement can be more credibly attributed to the conditional adversarial regression mechanism and optimizer behavior rather than to routine architectural complexity alone. In other words, the role of the baselines in this study is not only comparative but also explanatory: they help isolate the distinct methodological contribution of the proposed approach.

Furthermore, presenting the baselines with both conceptual figures and detailed architecture tables improves the transparency of the study. It allows readers to understand not only that the models were compared, but also how they were constructed and why they were selected. This level of detail is particularly valuable in battery prediction research, where differences in architecture design, sequence processing order, and regression head configuration can materially affect model behavior.

Results and discussion

In this section, three primary goal variables namely, discharge capacity, current, and voltage are used to compare model performance on two datasets. The Toyota Research Institute dataset is known as Dataset 2, whereas the NASA battery dataset is known as Dataset 1. In order to highlight the models’ relative performance and capacity for generalization, the findings are presented both visually (using plots and curves) and quantitatively (using error metrics and summaries) in the next subsections.

Discharge capacity (Ah)

Performance evaluation and model outperformance

The comparative assessment of discharge capacity prediction for Dataset 1 (B0005) and Dataset 2 (CH42) reveals distinct disparities in model performance and generalisation across various degradation patterns. Figure 6a shows that the true discharge capacity for Dataset 1 follows a non-linear degradation profile. It starts at about 1.328 Ah around cycle 145, goes up to a local maximum of almost 1.360 Ah around cycle 151, and then slowly drops to about 1.288 Ah by cycle 166. It then slowly rises again to about 1.325 Ah at the last cycle, which is around cycle 168. GAN-LION is the model that best fits the real trend. It captures both the early rise and the later fall with smooth transitions. It does slightly overestimate the peak value, predicting about 1.374 Ah instead of the true 1.360 Ah, but it still keeps track of the whole cycle range. In both datasets, GAN-LION provides a more stable and balanced gradient flow than the other datasets. Dataset 1 is more complex, while Dataset 2 is smoother. As can be seen from the results of a paired t-test shown in Table 8(a), the performance gain of the GAN-LION is statistically significant for Dataset 1 relative to the considered architectures, including GAN-ADAM (p = 0.026), AE-LSTM (p ≈ 0.0088), and CNN-LSTM-GRU (p ≈ 0.0041). In the case of the Wilcoxon test, although the statistical difference cannot be claimed due to the small sample size, the fact that all values in the “t-test only” table are statistically significant proves the systematicity of the gain. Concerning Dataset 2 with dynamic and noisy current behavior, the absolute errors obtained by all models are extremely small due to the low signal amplitude. Nevertheless, performance differences still exist. Thus, regarding the metrics of absolute error, GAN-ADAM shows better results than GAN-LION; in terms of RMSE (1.98 × 10−5 vs. 2.08 × 10−5) and MAE (1.67 × 10−5 vs. 1.74 × 10−5). However, in spite of this fact, GAN-LION demonstrates good performance and, importantly, maintains stable standard deviations.

In terms of statistics, however, Dataset 2 yields mixed findings. According to Table 8(b) presenting the results of the t-test, GAN-LION vs. GAN-ADAM is statistically significant (p ≈ 0.0033), while the other two models (AE-LSTM and CNN-LSTM-GRU) yield no significant difference with GAN-LION. It could therefore be inferred that although GAN-ADAM might have performed marginally better than GAN-LION numerically, their performance levels on Dataset 2 were essentially similar. However, in terms of consistency and reliability, GAN-LION seems to have an edge. Notably, the standard deviations obtained by GAN-LION are relatively low compared to those obtained by AE-LSTM and CNN-LSTM-GRU in both datasets, especially Dataset 1.

On the other hand, with regard to computation efficiency, GAN-LION still offers a reasonable compromise. In the case of Dataset 1, it needs around 11.52 s for the training process, where it performs the best at epoch ~ 34, taking about ~ 2.47 s to reach its peak performance level, as shown in Table 9(a). Similarly, when working with Dataset 2, GAN-LION spends ~ 5.39 s before it reaches its optimal performance point at epoch ~ 46.6, as shown in Table 9(b). Even though it takes a little longer than GAN-ADAM to achieve its best, the stability is better.

The most important observation is the fact that GAN-ADAM, although marginally better in Dataset 2 concerning absolute error values, does not exhibit such robustness and stability as seen in GAN-LION models when considering both datasets. Another important observation is the fact that CNN-LSTM-GRU continues to deliver the worst results despite having the highest number of parameters in the model, thus confirming the rule that increased complexity does not automatically lead to higher accuracy. Taking into account quantitatively measured data, statistical significance, stability, and computation time factors, it becomes clear that the best predictive model at the moment is GAN-LION. In less complicated datasets, there may be some variations, but the GAN-LION model clearly outperforms all other tested models in more complicated datasets (Dataset 1), with Dataset 2 showing comparable results.

Performance evaluation and model outperformance

Predictive performance for the voltage (V) parameter in the two datasets, i.e., Dataset 1 (B0005) and Dataset 2 (CH42) is discussed next, shedding light on the predictive power of the developed models to capture fine-grained variation in low-dynamic data. For Dataset 1, as illustrated in Fig. 12a, there is only mild fluctuation in the true value of the voltage with a slight decreasing trend, followed by some recovery towards the end. The voltage begins around ~ 3.472 V (at ~ 145 cycle) and declines gradually up to a minimum value of ~ 3.464 V (at ~ 163 cycle) before climbing again back to a maximum of ~ 3.476 V (at ~ 168 cycle). In comparison with the capacity and current parameters, voltage variation can be regarded as less pronounced; hence, even minor deviation could result in reduced accuracy in predicting the output. The proposed model GAN-LION seems to give the closest predictions to the true value, with predictions ranging from about 3.474–3.479 V. This is based on how well different models can make predictions.

AE-LSTM, on the other hand, has a higher divergence. For example, during the last cycle (~ 168), it reaches about ~ 3.489 V, which is very different from the actual value. This means that it is unstable and doesn’t generalize well. CNN-LSTM-GRU has the highest overestimation, with results that stay essentially the same between ~ 3.485 and ~ 3.486 V. This shows that the model isn’t sensitive to changes over time and can’t predict small changes in voltage. In relation to Dataset 2, depicted in Fig. 12b, the voltage values exhibit greater stability with minimal variations. The voltage begins at about 2.791 V (cycle ~ 104) and drops to its lowest point of about 2.786 V (cycle ~ 107). After that, it fluctuates slightly until the last cycle (~ 120), when it stays between 2.787 and 2.789 V. In this case, all of the models give optimized predictions because the signal is almost always the same. But GAN-LION still does better, predicting the average value of the voltage in the range of ~ 2.7873–2.7875 V with almost no errors. As expected, GAN-ADAM gives similar predictions, indicating equivalent model performance for this simpler signal.

The AE-LSTM model yields slightly smaller values (~ 2.7871 V), which suggests some bias on the part of the model, yet the level of accuracy is acceptable. Conversely, the CNN-LSTM-GRU demonstrates greater inconsistency, especially during the middle cycle period (cycles ~ 109–112), where it falls below ~ 2.7865 V. By comparing the two data sets, one can observe that the GAN-LION model performs better and more accurately than other models in predicting the voltage, regardless of whether small fluctuations are involved (Dataset 1) or when there is minimal change in the signal (Dataset 2). Although the differences between the models’ performance were not as significant for Dataset 2 due to the simpler nature of the signal, the GAN-LION performed better in terms of consistency and trend prediction.

Convergence behavior for voltage (V)

The results of the convergence analysis for the parameter V across the datasets B0005 (Dataset 1) and CH42 (Dataset 2) give additional insights into the performance of the models in terms of stability, efficiency, and ability to generalize on low-variance signals. With regard to Dataset 1, Fig. 13a shows that the training objective of all models decreases rapidly during the early stages of the learning process. Starting from approximately ~ 4.0 for AE-LSTM, ~ 3.5 for CNN-LSTM-GRU, and ~ 1.7–1.8 for GAN-based models (epoch 1), the objective falls considerably within the first 5–10 epochs. Of all models, GAN-LION is the most consistent in its convergence, dropping from ~ 1.75 to ~ 0.40 within ~ 15 epochs and then stabilizing gradually between ~ 0.20 and 0.30 in epochs 60–120, ending up at around ~ 0.25–0.30 (epoch ~ 160).

GAN-ADAM has a pattern similar to the previous two models, with slightly high and stable results at about ~ 0.30–0.40 with some oscillations. AE-LSTM has the minimum validation objective results, attaining results around ~ 0.15–0.25 from epochs 20–80, demonstrating good fitting performance; nonetheless, there is an indication of fluctuations as well as overfitting. As opposed to AE-LSTM, CNN-LSTM-GRU has unstable and fluctuating convergence values, with an increasing trend at later epochs up to ~ 0.50–0.60, implying poor generalization. Regarding Dataset 2, Fig. 13b reveals that the convergence pattern is due to the stability and calmness of the signal voltage curve. Similar to the other datasets, GAN-LION exhibits excellent convergence, commencing from ~ 0.62 (epoch 1) to stable values of ~ 0.64–0.66 from epoch ~ 30 onwards, implying good learning without overfitting.

On the contrary, GAN-ADAM shows a somewhat higher and less consistent convergence curve starting at ~ 0.62 and ending up at ~ 0.75–0.80 with some oscillation tendencies. The most unstable behavior is shown by AE-LSTM and CNN-LSTM-GRU models that demonstrate increased validation objectives over time. Thus, AE-LSTM increases from ~ 0.70 to ~ 1.35 (around epoch ~ 29), whereas CNN-LSTM-GRU demonstrates considerable fluctuation trends with maximum values close to ~ 1.65–1.70 which means an inability to model the signal properly. Comparing convergence between different datasets demonstrates that GAN-LION shows similar trends, which proves smooth and consistent convergence for both datasets. Unlike other approaches (overfitting (AE-LSTM), oscillating (CNN-LSTM-GRU), unstable (GAN-ADAM)), GAN-LION shows an optimum performance and balance in terms of convergence. GAN-LION demonstrates the highest stability during training, allowing predicting small fluctuations (Dataset 1) and constant signal value (Dataset 2).

Gradient norm analysis for voltage (V)

Gradient norms with respect to the parameter of voltage (V) reveal even more about stability and balance in adversarial training on datasets 1 (B0005) and 2 (CH42), where time signals possess little variability and small changes in time. For Dataset 1, in Fig. 14a, one can see that the generator gradient norm (grad_norm_g) with respect to GAN-LION fluctuates moderately in the range of ~ 1.00–1.70 throughout epochs 1–160; its initial values at epoch 1 start from ~ 1.05, and its peak values are observed at ~ 1.65–1.70 (epochs ~ 70–80). These values suggest that learning is active and stable. GAN-ADAM is seen to have somewhat smaller and less stable generator gradient norms (between ~ 0.80 and 1.30).

In the case of discriminator (grad_norm_d), the GAN-LION approach yields extremely low values that range between ~ 0.02 and 0.08 while decreasing slowly through epochs. Such trend guarantees optimal adversarial dynamics without dominance by the discriminator. On the other hand, low discriminator gradients (~ 0.04 to 0.10) are observed in GAN-ADAM but with more volatility. For Dataset 2, the trend in gradient norms illustrated in Fig. 14b is more consistent due to a less volatile voltage signal. In terms of generator gradient norm, GAN-LION has an initial value of ~ 0.45 (epoch 1), after which it gradually increases to values between ~ 0.65–0.70 (epoch ~ 20–25), with some peaks close to ~ 0.78–0.80, eventually converging to ~ 0.65–0.68 by the final epochs (~ 30). The increasing trend is quite smooth and steady. On the contrary, GAN-ADAM produces higher and fluctuating generator gradients between ~ 0.45 and ~ 0.87.

The discriminator of Dataset 2 has relatively consistent and small gradients of (~ 0.06–0.08). The GAN-ADAM discriminator has somewhat larger values of gradients (~ 0.07–0.09), meaning that the model has lesser stability. Cross-comparison of these two datasets confirms that the GAN-LION model consistently keeps the gradient norm at relatively stable levels for both generators and discriminators of different datasets. While the generators have stable and sufficiently large gradients to ensure learning, the discriminators of GAN-LION have strictly controlled gradients, thus eliminating any instability. As a result, GAN-LION ensures consistency in gradient norms when predicting the voltage.

Quantitative performance & outperformance of GAN-LION for voltage (V)

The quantitative analysis of the voltage (V) parameter for Dataset 1 (B0005) and Dataset 2 (CH42) confirms the better performance and reliability of the GAN-LION architecture especially when dealing with signals having minimal dynamic range and highly sensitive to small prediction errors. The network has the lowest error rate of MAE = 0.00576 ± 0.00149 V and RMSE = 0.00633 ± 0.00142 V, outperforming other architectures such as GAN-ADAM (RMSE = 0.00911), AE-LSTM (RMSE = 0.01040) and CNN-LSTM-GRU (RMSE = 0.01633). The results of the Wilcoxon test cannot be considered conclusive because of the limited sample size.

It is important to note that GAN-LION is much better than AE-LSTM (p ≈ 0.0018), as shown in Table 11(b). However, GAN-LION is not substantially better than GAN-ADAM and CNN-LSTM-GRU because the differences in performance between the models are very small. Even so, GAN-LION always produces the lowest mean error value, which backs up the idea that it can generalise well. In terms of consistency, GAN-LION gets similar standard deviations in both datasets, especially in Dataset 1, which has significant levels of variation.

From a computational point of view, GAN-LION maintains a good balance between speed and accuracy. For Dataset 1, it takes about 11.49 s of training time to reach its best performance at epoch ~ 77, with a time-to-best of ~ 5.56 s, as shown in Table 12(a). Table 12(b) shows that GAN-LION learns quickly and reaches its best performance early (epoch ~ 5.2) with very little training time (~ 2.08 s) for Dataset 2. Similarly, while GAN-ADAM performs competitively, it lacks the consistency and stability observed in GAN-LION, particularly across different datasets.

All the findings discussed above conclusively show that GAN-LION outperforms other methods since it provides more optimal and balanced training procedures. In particular, GAN-LION avoids both vanishing and exploding of gradients, thus allowing for more stable training with balanced gradients of the generator and discriminator. Moreover, GAN-LION shows stable convergence without strong oscillations, making it less prone to overfitting, which was typical for AE-LSTM, and instability, as in the case of CNN-LSTM-GRU. Finally, GAN-LION provides better balancing between the process of reconstruction and adversarial learning.

Interpretation, practical implications, and limitations

The superior results of GAN-LION can be traced back to the stability that the model offers in terms of optimization under the conditional adversarial regression setup. In comparison to GAN-ADAM, GAN-LION exhibits smoother optimization properties, thereby resulting in more consistent generator-discriminator gradient updates. This feature becomes particularly important when handling the more difficult dataset where the degeneration dynamics and target trajectories are more nonlinear. The reason why the models’ results do not differ greatly for Dataset 2 is due to the easier target trajectories. Under such conditions, even simpler models can achieve relatively competitive results, which naturally reduces the separation between methods.

From a practical perspective, the results suggest that the proposed framework is most beneficial in settings where battery behavior is more variable and prediction stability is critical. However, the present study also has several limitations. First, the framework was evaluated on two benchmark datasets only, and broader validation across additional chemistries, temperatures, and operating conditions is still needed. Second, the present work focuses mainly on predictive performance and optimization behavior rather than feature-level interpretability. Third, although computational-cost summaries are reported, full hardware-level real-time deployment validation in an embedded BMS environment remains future work. These aspects will be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

This study proposed GAN-LION, a conditional adversarial regression framework for multi-target battery prediction from historical operating sequences. The method reformulated prediction as a deterministic sequence-to-one task and separated the effect of architecture from optimization through a controlled GAN-LION versus GAN-Adam comparison, together with evaluation against CNN-LSTM-GRU and Autoencoder-LSTM baselines. Using a unified chronological evaluation protocol on the NASA B0005 and Toyota Research Institute CH42 datasets, GAN-LION achieved the best overall performance for discharge capacity and voltage prediction and remained competitive for current prediction. Its advantage was more evident on the more challenging dataset, while differences were smaller on the smoother dataset. Overall, the results show that Lion-based optimization improves convergence stability and supports accurate battery target prediction within the proposed conditional adversarial framework. Future work will extend the framework to broader datasets, stronger interpretability analysis, and more practical deployment-oriented validation.
