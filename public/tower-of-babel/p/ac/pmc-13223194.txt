Predicting methane adsorption in coal and shale with white-box and black-box machine learning models
Authors: Amir Hossein Sheikhshoaei, Fahimeh Hadavimoghaddam, Meftah Ali Abuswer, Ahmad Mohaddespour, Abdolhossein Hemmati-Sarapardeh, Saeid Atashrouz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of methane (CH4) adsorption in coal and shale formations is essential for optimizing gas extraction, enhancing storage efficiency, mitigating greenhouse gas emissions, and ensuring safe mining and unconventional reservoir development. In this study, the most comprehensive dataset reported to date was compiled to model CH4 adsorption in coal and shale systems, using pressure (P), moisture content (M), and total organic carbon (TOC) as inputs for coal, and temperature (T), moisture content (M), and TOC for shale. To simultaneously achieve high predictive accuracy and physical interpretability, three transparent white-box models gene expression programming (GEP), group method of data handling (GMDH), and genetic programming (GP) and three advanced black-box models Gaussian process regression (GPR), categorical boosting (CatBoost), and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) were developed and systematically evaluated. The white-box models successfully produced explicit, closed-form mathematical correlations that directly link CH4 adsorption to key controlling parameters, providing transparent, physically interpretable tools that can be readily applied without specialized software. In parallel, the black-box models were employed to benchmark predictive performance, with CatBoost achieving the best overall accuracy, yielding mean squared errors (MSE) of 0.008 and 0.004 and coefficients of determination (R2) of 0.924 and 0.997 for coal and shale, respectively. Model interpretability was further enhanced using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which consistently identified TOC as the dominant factor governing CH4 adsorption in both systems, while leverage-based outlier analysis confirmed the robustness of the optimal models, with more than 99% of coal data and over 95% of shale data falling within the applicability domain. Notably, this work represents the first application of GEP, GMDH, and GP to derive transparent, user-friendly analytical expressions for CH4 adsorption in coal, offering a practical and interpretable alternative to purely black-box machine-learning approaches.Keywords: Machine learning, Coal, Shale, Methane, Adsorption, White-box modelingSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Shale gas and coalbed methane (CBM) are important unconventional natural gas resources that play a major role in the global energy structure and the transition toward cleaner fuels1–5. In both shale and coal reservoirs, a significant portion of methane exists in an adsorbed state typically 20–85% of total gas-in-place, stored on organic matter, clay minerals, and within nanopores6,7. Because adsorbed gas dominates storage in CBM and contributes substantially in shale, understanding methane adsorption is essential for accurately estimating reserves, predicting production performance, and determining key parameters such as gas content and desorption pressure8,9. Shale gas extraction has expanded rapidly worldwide, particularly in the U.S. and China, due to large reserves and its environmental advantages, including lower carbon emissions compared to conventional fossil fuels10,11. However, uncertainties in recoverable reserves persist, largely due to the challenge of quantifying methane adsorption under reservoir conditions.

Methane adsorption is also environmentally significant, as coal and shale formations possess strong sorption capacities and can aid in underground storage of both CH4 and CO₂12. Adsorption mechanisms including physisorption in shales (via mono- and multilayer adsorption) and primarily adsorption-dominated storage in coal play a vital role in long-term gas retention and release13,14. Thorough research on adsorption behavior supports more efficient extraction technologies, safer mining operations, and potentially greenhouse gas sequestration. Improving understanding of these mechanisms is especially important for deep CBM development and for overcoming geological challenges that currently limit recovery in complex reservoirs. Sorption capacity is strongly affected by pressure, temperature, rock composition, TOC, moisture content, clay minerals, and kerogen type, which makes adsorption behavior highly variable and difficult to generalize15–18. To quantify adsorption, researchers traditionally rely on experimental isotherms and adsorption models. Laboratory measurements are typically performed using manometric or gravimetric methods, while adsorption behavior is mathematically described using models such as the Langmuir19 model, BET20 model, various potential or multilayer models21,22, as well as the hPC-SAFT-MPTA23, ADA24, and modified Langmuir models25,26. These models are effective tools for calculating adsorption capacity, extracting characteristic parameters (e.g., Langmuir volume and pressure), and reproducing adsorption isotherms under specific and fixed temperature pressure conditions. However, it is important to emphasize that classical physical adsorption models (e.g., Langmuir, BET, and DR) are condition-specific fitting models rather than data-driven predictive tools. They are calibrated using a limited number of experimental data points under fixed thermodynamic and material conditions and are intended to describe a single adsorption isotherm. Once fitted, these models generally lack the capability to predict adsorption behavior at new temperatures, pressures, or compositional states without re-estimating their parameters. As a result, their applicability is restricted to the calibration dataset, and their predictive accuracy often deteriorates when extrapolated from laboratory-scale, low-pressure conditions to real reservoir environments. In contrast, machine-learning (ML) methods offer a powerful alternative by learning complex, nonlinear, and multivariate relationships directly from data without relying on simplifying physical assumptions such as surface homogeneity or monolayer adsorption. ML models can simultaneously integrate multiple controlling variables, improve predictive accuracy, and provide rapid and reliable estimates of methane adsorption across a wide range of pressure–temperature–composition conditions. Meng et al.10 compared traditional isothermal models, a pressure–temperature unified model, and ML models (ANN, RF, SVM, XGBoost) using pressure, temperature, TOC, and moisture as inputs. The modified Dubinin–Radushkevich (DR) model performed best among traditional models but was limited to isothermal conditions, while XGBoost achieved the highest accuracy and extrapolation capability across shale types. Huang et al.27 developed a Fully Connected Neural Network (FCNN) using inputs pressure, temperature, and PED-derived parameters (ω, ξ, γ) to predict methane adsorption in shale slit nanopores. The model provided ultra-fast, accurate predictions, outperforming traditional experiments and MD simulations, though it was less accurate for nanopores with ω and ξ values outside the training range. Nait Amar et al.28 employed two data-driven models, Gene Expression Programming (GEP) and Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH), to predict methane adsorption capacity in shale gas formations. The models used pressure, temperature, total organic carbon (TOC), and moisture as inputs, based on a dataset of 352 experimental measurements. Both models achieved high predictive accuracy, with the GEP model showing superior performance (R2 = 0.9837, RMSE = 0.0625). Li et al.29 developed an RF model to accurately predict methane adsorption capacity in coal, overcoming the limitations of traditional isotherm adsorption experiments. The study utilized a dataset of approximately 200 experimental samples, with input parameters including temperature, equilibrium pressure, moisture, ash content, and volatile matter. Through hyperparameter optimization and 10-fold cross-validation, the RF model achieved an R2 of 0.98, demonstrating high predictive accuracy. Tavakolian et al.30 applied five ML algorithms (ANN, DT, RF, SVR, XGB) with pressure, temperature, TOC, moisture, CO₂ injection percentage, and rock type as inputs to predict CH4 and CO₂ sorption in shale and coal. RF provided the best predictions with MAE = 0.0864, MSE = 0.0231, RMSE = 0.1520 for CH4 and MAE = 0.0529, MSE = 0.0533, RMSE = 0.2308 for CO₂.

Previous studies have shown that machine learning can effectively predict CH4 adsorption in coal and shale, but most are limited by small datasets, focus on a single formation type, or rely on black-box models with low interpretability. While models like XGBoost, FCNN, GEP, GMDH, and RF achieved high accuracy, they often lack transparency, generalizability, and physical insight.

In this study, we compiled the most comprehensive dataset to date for predicting CH4 adsorption in coal and shale formations. For coal, pressure (P), moisture (M), and total organic carbon (TOC) were used as input variables, whereas for shale, T, M, and TOC served as inputs. Three rigorous, supervised, white-box models, gene expression programming (GEP), group method of data handling (GMDH), and genetic programming (GP), which generate explicit, closed-form mathematical expressions describing the relationship between input variables and CH4 adsorption, were developed to model CH4 adsorption. In addition, three black-box machine learning models, namely Gaussian process regression (GPR), CatBoost, and LightGBM, were employed for comparison. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use GMDH, GEP, and GP to derive explicit mathematical correlations for methane adsorption in coal. The resulting white-box expressions are transparent, user-friendly, and can be evaluated using a simple calculator. Model interpretability and reliability were ensured through SHAP analysis and leverage (Williams) charts, providing both accurate predictions and meaningful insights into the controlling factors. This combined framework of interpretable white-box models and high-performance black-box models provides both accurate predictions and meaningful insights into the key factors governing CH4 adsorption.

Data acquisition

The CH4 adsorption data for coal used in this study were compiled from previously published sources30, resulting in a total of 265 experimental measurements. Similarly, CH4 adsorption data for shale were gathered from various references, and after removing outliers, the dataset comprised 2,994 data points30.The larger shale dataset covers a wide range of experimental and geological conditions, enabling the models to capture complex relationships more effectively and generalize well to unseen data. By comparison, the smaller coal dataset may limit the model’s ability to learn subtle patterns and can increase sensitivity to outliers, highlighting the influence of dataset size on predictive performance and emphasizing the potential benefits of expanding the coal data in future studies. Tables 1 and 2 provide a detailed statistical characterization of the datasets used to predict CH4 adsorption in coal and shale formations, offering insight into the range, central tendency, and distribution of both input and output variables. In the coal dataset (Table 1), TOC varies from 62.2 to 88.5%, pressure ranges between 0.4 and 21.58 kPa, and moisture content spans 0–6.4%, indicating physically reasonable and relatively narrow ranges typical of coal seams. The mean and median values are closely aligned for all variables, suggesting balanced distributions, while the low skewness values (0.06–0.44) indicate near-symmetry. Negative kurtosis values across all parameters confirm platykurtic distributions, implying a lack of extreme outliers and a relatively uniform spread of data. The CH4 sorption capacity ranges from 0.08 to 1.52 cm³/g with a mean of 0.64 cm³/g, showing moderate variability and stable adsorption behavior suitable for modeling. In contrast, the shale dataset (Table 2) exhibits broader ranges, reflecting higher geological and operational heterogeneity. Temperature varies from 30 to 150 °C, pressure from 0.16 to 28.99 MPa, TOC from 0.09 to 16.99%, and moisture from 0 to 10.97%. The larger differences between mean and median values, particularly for temperature, moisture, and CH4 sorption capacity, together with positive skewness values (1.26–2.29), indicate right-skewed distributions dominated by lower values with fewer high-end observations. Kurtosis values further suggest moderate to high peakedness for moisture and CH4 sorption capacity, implying the presence of some influential high-value data points. Methodologies

Gaussian process regression (GPR)

Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) is a probabilistic, non-parametric learning technique rooted in Bayesian inference, in which the mapping between input variables and the target output is represented as a distribution over possible functions rather than a predetermined mathematical expression31. The method is governed by a mean function and a covariance kernel, which together describe the expected behavior of the model and the degree of correlation between data points, allowing GPR to flexibly learn highly nonlinear patterns in the data32. One of the distinctive strengths of GPR is its inherent capability to simultaneously generate predictive values and quantify associated uncertainties, making it especially effective for datasets characterized by noise or limited sample sizes. During model construction, kernel hyperparameters are optimized to maximize agreement with the observed data. While the computational demand of GPR scales unfavorably with large datasets due to the inversion of dense covariance matrices, the method remains attractive because it involves a limited number of tunable parameters and provides physically interpretable predictions with reliable uncertainty estimates33–35.

Light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM)

LightGBM (Lightweight Gradient Boosting Machine) is an efficient gradient boosting framework developed by Microsoft that is designed to achieve high predictive accuracy with reduced training time and memory consumption36. It employs a histogram-based algorithm to discretize continuous features, which significantly lowers computational complexity compared to traditional pre-sorting methods used in conventional GBDT algorithms. LightGBM adopts a leaf-wise tree growth strategy, splitting the leaf that yields the greatest error reduction while applying depth constraints to mitigate overfitting. Its efficiency is further enhanced through Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS), which prioritizes samples with large gradients, and Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB), which reduces dimensionality by combining sparse and mutually exclusive features. In addition, LightGBM supports distributed training and multiple learning objectives, making it scalable and versatile. Owing to these innovations, LightGBM delivers fast training, low memory usage, and competitive or superior predictive performance compared with traditional boosting methods36–38.

Categorical boosting (CatBoost)

CatBoost (Categorical Boosting) is an advanced gradient boosting algorithm specifically designed to handle categorical variables efficiently, achieving high predictive accuracy with minimal preprocessing39. Unlike conventional methods that rely on one-hot encoding, CatBoost encodes categorical features using target-based statistics and employs ordered boosting to prevent target leakage, ensuring that only prior information is used when calculating statistics and thereby improving generalization. Built on the gradient boosting framework, CatBoost constructs an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, with each tree correcting the errors of its predecessors. Its use of symmetric trees, controlled tree depth, and optimized learning rates provides a balance between flexibility and regularization. The algorithm is particularly effective for datasets with mixed feature types, high-cardinality categorical variables, or sparse data, requiring minimal preprocessing while reducing the risk of overfitting40.

Genetic programming (GP)

Genetic Programming (GP) is an evolutionary computation technique that extends the principles of Genetic Algorithms (GA) to the automatic development of computer programs or mathematical models. Inspired by natural selection and genetic evolution, GP begins with a randomly generated population of candidate solutions, typically represented as tree-like structures, where internal nodes perform operations and terminal nodes represent input variables or constants. These candidates are iteratively improved through selection, crossover, and mutation, guided by a fitness function that evaluates their performance on a given task. Over successive generations, GP evolves solutions that better capture the underlying relationships in the data, producing explicit, interpretable models without requiring a predefined functional form. Variants of GP, such as Gene Expression Programming (GEP), Multi-Gene GP, and Cartesian GP, build on this framework to enhance modeling flexibility, efficiency, and accuracy, making GP a versatile and widely used approach for optimization, regression, and system modeling in complex, nonlinear domains41–44.

Gene expression programming (GEP)

Gene Expression Programming (GEP), introduced by Cândida Ferreira in 1999, is an advanced evolutionary algorithm that combines the strengths of Genetic Algorithms (GA) and Genetic Programming (GP) while addressing some of their limitations. Unlike GA, which uses linear chromosomes, or GP, which employs tree-like structures, GEP integrates both concepts by representing solutions as fixed-length linear chromosomes (genotype) that can be expressed as variable-shaped expression trees (phenotype). This genotype-phenotype mapping allows GEP to efficiently explore complex solution spaces and develop explicit mathematical models without requiring a predefined functional form. The algorithm begins with a randomly generated population of chromosomes, evaluates their fitness, and iteratively evolves the population using genetic operators such as mutation, inversion, transposition, and recombination. By selecting high-performing individuals for reproduction and cyclically applying these operators, GEP can generate accurate, interpretable models that capture nonlinear relationships among input-output variables. Its multigene capability further enables the construction of sophisticated models composed of multiple interacting submodels, making it a robust and flexible tool for symbolic regression and predictive modeling45–47.

Group method of data handling (GMDH)

The Group Method of Data Handling (GMDH), introduced by Alexey G. Ivakhnenko in the 1960s, is an inductive, self-organizing modeling approach that constructs multilayer networks automatically based on the complexity of the problem and the available data. Unlike traditional neural networks with predefined architectures, GMDH progressively generates its structure and determines the optimal number of layers and nodes through a training process. Each neuron pair in a layer is connected via a quadratic polynomial transfer function, producing new neurons in subsequent layers, which allows the model to capture nonlinear relationships effectively. GMDH produces explicit, interpretable mathematical expressions linking input variables to the target output, making it a transparent alternative to black-box models. The method excels in pattern recognition, function approximation, and forecasting, particularly in complex, multidimensional systems or when working with limited or highly correlated data. Its evolutionary, polynomial-based approach enables robust and accurate modeling of nonlinear phenomena while providing clear insights into variable interactions48–50.

Results and discussion

The accuracy of the models developed for predicting CH4 adsorption in coal and shale was thoroughly evaluated using statistical error metrics and graphical analyses. The corresponding results are presented and discussed in the following subsections.

For all machine-learning models, key hyperparameters were optimized prior to final training. For GP, GEP, and GMDH models, a structured sensitivity analysis was conducted over commonly recommended ranges reported in the symbolic-regression literature. For machine learning models, hyperparameters were optimized using GridSearchCV with five-fold cross-validation, minimizing the negative mean squared error. The optimal hyperparameter set was selected based on validation performance while ensuring stable generalization to the test dataset. Numerical constants in GP and GEP were constrained within fixed bounds (− 10 to + 10) to avoid coefficient explosion and ensure physically meaningful expressions.

Predictive evaluation

In this study, the dataset was first normalized using StandardScaler to ensure that all input variables were on a comparable scale, which facilitates effective training of the machine learning models. The normalized data were then randomly divided into training and testing subsets, with 80% allocated for model training and the remaining 20% reserved for testing, using a fixed random_state value to ensure reproducibility and consistency of the results. Each model contained a set of hyperparameters that could either be tuned manually or retained at their default values. To systematically determine the optimal combination of hyperparameters, we employed the GridSearchCV technique by defining a parameter grid that specified the possible ranges or values for each hyperparameter. GridSearchCV then exhaustively explored all combinations to identify the set that minimized the negative mean squared error (neg_mean_squared_error). The search was conducted using 5-fold cross-validation to ensure robust model evaluation, and verbose output was enabled to monitor the optimization process. After training the models on the normalized data, the predicted outputs were denormalized to their original scale, enabling an accurate and meaningful evaluation of predictive performance on the test dataset.

SHAP analysis

SHAP analysis was performed on the CatBoost model because it showed the highest predictive accuracy and reliability. Using the best-performing model ensures that feature importance rankings accurately reflect the factors controlling CH4 adsorption. Figure 7a–d presents the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis of the CatBoost model used to predict CH4 adsorption in coal and shale. For coal (Fig. 7a,b), the mean absolute SHAP values indicate that TOC (%) is by far the most influential feature, with a mean SHAP value of + 0.72, followed by pressure (MPa) and moisture (%), with mean SHAP values of + 0.28 and + 0.22, respectively. This result highlights that higher TOC content significantly enhances methane adsorption capacity, while increasing pressure also contributes positively, though to a lesser extent. Moisture exerts a smaller but still notable influence, generally reducing CH4 adsorption at higher values. The SHAP summary plot in Fig. 7b confirms these trends, showing that higher TOC and pressure values correspond to higher SHAP values (positive impacts on adsorption), whereas higher moisture values tend to lower the model output. For shale (Fig. 7c,d), TOC (%) remains the most dominant variable with a mean SHAP value of + 0.58, confirming its key role in controlling CH4 adsorption capacity. However, temperature (°C) emerges as the second most influential factor (+ 0.22), followed by pressure (MPa) (+ 0.19) and moisture (%) (+ 0.13). The SHAP summary plot in Fig. 7d shows that higher TOC and pressure values generally increase adsorption, while higher temperatures and moisture content have negative impacts. Compared to coal, the model for shale exhibits greater sensitivity to temperature, implying that thermal effects play a stronger role in controlling methane retention in shale matrices. Overall, the SHAP analysis demonstrates that TOC is the principal factor governing CH4 adsorption in both coal and shale, but the relative influence of secondary parameters differs between the two systems.

Applicability domain and detection of outliers in the CatBoost model

Following the statistical and graphical assessments, which demonstrated the strong predictive performance of the CatBoost model for CH4 adsorption in both coal and shale, an outlier detection procedure was employed to identify data points that could adversely impact model accuracy. This technique is designed to identify data points that significantly diverge from the general pattern of the dataset. The leverage-based analysis is founded on three key components: the standardized residuals (SR), the critical leverage limit (H*), and the diagonal elements of the Hat matrix (H), which are mathematically defined as follows51–54:

n this framework, ej denotes the raw residual associated with the jth observation, MSE represents the mean squared error of the model, and Hj is the corresponding leverage value for that data point. The leverage technique employs the Williams diagram as a graphical tool to assess the reliability of individual observations by separating them into three distinct classes: valid points, suspicious points, and observations outside the applicability domain. Data points with leverage values lower than the critical threshold H* are considered to lie within the model’s domain of applicability. Among these, samples exhibiting standardized residuals (SR) exceeding ± 3 are labeled as suspicious, indicating unusually large deviations despite acceptable leverage. Accordingly, valid data are defined as points satisfying both H < H* and − 3 ≤ SR ≤ 3, whereas observations with leverage values greater than H* are regarded as outside the reliable prediction domain of the model. As illustrated in Fig. 9a, the CatBoost model detected only two suspicious samples in the coal dataset, accounting for approximately 0.75% of the data, while the remaining 263 samples (99.24%) were deemed reliable. In contrast, analysis of the shale dataset (Fig. 9b) revealed 79 out-of-domain points and 47 suspicious samples, with 2868 data points (95.79%) retained within the valid region. Overall, the Williams plot results demonstrate that the vast majority of observations reside within the acceptable domain, confirming both the robustness of the developed models and the consistency of the experimental datasets used in this study.

Conclusions

This study, for the first time, applies white-box models to accurately predict CH4 adsorption in coal formations, alongside black-box models for comparison, using the most comprehensive dataset compiled to date for coal and shale. The key innovation lies in the combined use of three white-box models (GEP, GMDH, and GP) and three black-box models (GPR, CatBoost, and LightGBM), enabling both interpretable and high-performance predictive modeling. Among the models, CatBoost demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy, achieving MSE = 0.008 and R2 = 0.924 for coal, and MSE = 0.004 and R2 = 0.997 for shale. SHAP analysis of CatBoost revealed total organic carbon (TOC) as the most influential factor controlling methane adsorption, while temperature, pressure, and moisture exhibited secondary, formation-dependent effects. The robustness of the models was further confirmed via the leverage approach, with over 99% of coal data and more than 95% of shale data lying within the model’s applicability domain. Despite these advances, the study has some limitations. For coal, temperature was constant across all experiments, preventing assessment of its effect on adsorption, and the dataset, although comprehensive, does not cover all geological formations or extreme conditions. Additionally, while the current models effectively capture the influence of primary geochemical variables, other important factors such as pore structure, mineralogical composition, and specific surface area were not included. Future research should aim to expand the dataset to encompass a wider variety of coal and shale formations and adsorption conditions, including variable temperature and pressure regimes. Incorporating advanced petrophysical and geochemical descriptors could improve understanding of the physicochemical mechanisms controlling CH4 adsorption and enhance the predictive capability of both white-box and black-box models. Overall, this study provides a novel, reliable, and interpretable framework for predicting CH4 adsorption, offering practical insights for optimizing gas recovery, storage, and reservoir management.
