ProLM: a plasma proteomics pretrained model for the general population
Authors: Shizheng Qiu, Ming Zhao, Xin Chen, Zhishuai Zhang, Huanyu You, Xu Sun, Jirui Guo, Haozheng Liang, Yu Guo, Lu Chen, Jun Lv, Dianjianyi Sun, Ling Yang, Yiping Chen, Huaidong Du, Zhengming Chen, Liming Li, Yang Hu, Guiyou Liu, Canqing Yu, Yadong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plasma proteomics can provide a dynamic molecular readout of human health, but models that learn generalizable protein-expression patterns in population cohorts remain limited. Here we show that ProLM, a BERT-based plasma proteomics model pretrained on 15,499 relatively healthy UK Biobank participants, captures baseline protein-expression relationships and supports prediction of 16 common chronic diseases. After disease-specific fine-tuning, the ProLM-derived proteomic risk score outperformed the Age+Sex model for all 16 diseases, the cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk equation for 14 diseases and a 35-variable clinical PANEL score for 11 diseases. Model interpretation highlighted proteins including GDF15 whose expression changed more than 15 years before clinical diagnosis, and key findings were externally evaluated in the China Kadoorie Biobank. These results support plasma proteomics pretrained models as tools for early chronic-disease risk stratification, while prospective validation is needed before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Chronic diseases impose a large burden on global healthcare systems and society1–6. It is estimated that in Europe, approximately 87% of all deaths are attributable to chronic diseases7. However, given that these diseases are typically driven by intricate interactions between genetic and environmental factors, effectively predicting risk and implementing early screening remain challenging.

Polygenic risk scores (PRS), which represent the weighted sum of risk alleles across the genome associated with specific phenotypes, are widely used to estimate the risk of polygenic diseases8–11. Nevertheless, PRS account for only a fraction of the genetic risk and do not capture the influence of environmental factors, thereby limiting their predictive power12–14. In contrast, plasma proteins are involved in regulating numerous biological processes, and their dynamic variations reflect the combined effects of multiple etiological factors—such as genetic predisposition, environmental exposure, lifestyle, and aging—thus serving as direct indicators of an individual’s physiological state15,16. Consequently, plasma proteomics is increasingly recognized as an early indicator of chronic disease risk.

Although several proteomics-based risk scores have been developed for disease prediction and stratification, traditional techniques face challenges when handling proteomic datasets containing thousands of features17–22. These methods struggle to interpret the complex regulatory networks underlying these proteins. However, few pre-trained models have been developed for large-scale plasma proteomic data that can comprehensively characterize health status and support a range of downstream clinical tasks. Recent advances in artificial intelligence, and particularly in pre-trained language models, offer promising new opportunities to enhance current disease prediction frameworks. The BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) model employs masked language modeling to capture contextual relationships among tokens, enabling it to learn latent expression patterns within the human proteome23. Its attention mechanism assigns different weights to input features, enabling the model to capture long-range, nonlinear dependencies and interactions24. These observations motivated us to test whether a masked-reconstruction model pretrained on plasma protein abundance can learn reusable representations for incident disease prediction.

Here, we present ProLM, a pre-trained proteomic language model built from plasma proteomic data obtained from a relatively healthy population (Fig. 1). The goal of pretraining is to enable the model to learn the regulatory and interaction patterns that characterize a normal, healthy proteome. By capturing latent features of protein expression in relatively healthy individuals, the model establishes a robust baseline representation of the proteomic regulatory network. Subsequently, we fine-tuned the pre-trained model using expression proteomic data from individuals with 16 common chronic diseases, thereby enhancing its capacity for disease prediction. Leveraging the prediction probabilities produced by the model for each sample, we proposed a proteomic risk score (ProRS) to stratify individual risks for chronic diseases. Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The pre-training dataset consisted of measurements of 2873 plasma proteins from 15,499 relatively healthy individuals in the UKB-PPP, all of whom were free of common chronic diseases at baseline and remained free of these diseases throughout follow-up. The baseline characteristics of these individuals were detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Participants had a median age of 53 years (range, 39–70), and 59.6% were women. Most were White (92.5%), and 38.7% reported higher education. Over a median follow-up period of 15.53 years, ending in September 2024, only 210 participants died. The fine-tuning dataset comprised plasma proteomic data from individuals who developed one of 16 common chronic diseases during follow-up—including neurodegenerative, respiratory, endocrine and cardiovascular diseases, as well as cancers—along with 1723 healthy individuals. Among these, primary hypertension and obesity were the most prevalent, affecting 7469 and 4289 individuals, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). Compared with the healthy cohort, the cohort used for fine-tuning had an older median age (60 vs. 53), higher median BMI (27.4 vs. 25.5), and a higher mortality rate during follow-up (14.8% vs. 1.4%). There was no sample overlap between the pre-training and disease-prediction datasets. Additionally, proteomic data from individuals with four disease subtypes were used for fine-tuning: dementia (Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, and frontotemporal dementia), stroke (ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and subarachnoid hemorrhage), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis), and diabetes (type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes) (Supplementary Table 3).

In addition, the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) was used for external validation (Supplementary Table 4). The baseline characteristics of CKB participants differed from those observed in the UK Biobank; for example, they exhibited lower BMI (23.7 vs. 27.4), a higher prevalence of current smokers (30.1% vs. 8.2%), a lower prevalence of current drinkers (15.1% vs. 20.8%), and a higher mortality rate (35.9% vs. 14.8%).

Architecture and pre-training of ProLM

Omics data with continuous numerical features are typically not well suited to direct pre-training. In addition, two protein expression values that are very close should be considered biologically similar. Accordingly, protein expression values within a predefined tolerance margin should be treated as biologically similar. Therefore, we embedded the numerical features using a Hadamard product approach and designed a corresponding pre-training task. We randomly masked a subset of protein expression values and trained the model to reconstruct the values at the masked positions. If the error between the reconstructed values and the original values falls within a tolerable range, the reconstruction is considered successful. This objective enabled ProLM to learn baseline proteomic expression patterns from the masked reconstruction loss.

We pre-trained using plasma proteomic data from 15,499 relatively healthy, unlabeled individuals from the UK Biobank (Fig. 1). Specifically, for each plasma protein, we integrated its sequence and functional information into the corresponding expression features. The features were then segmented into blocks of 512 proteins; after a [CLS] token was appended to each block, the resulting token sequence was fed into the BERT encoder. During the computation of attention weights, we incorporated a protein–protein interaction (PPI) matrix, which was intended to bias attention toward curated PPI evidence. After 99 epochs of pre-training, the ProLM converged and stopped early, achieving a loss of 0.2109, a reconstruction accuracy of 0.8509, a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.2109, a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.2894, and a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.5283 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To provide direct, model-level interpretability, we used Integrated Gradients (IG) to quantify the importance of each protein. We then perturbed the expression levels of the model-prioritized proteins and examined how the model’s performance changed. IG showed that importance was highly concentrated: a small set of proteins accounted for most of the attribution. Disease prediction task

We fine-tuned ProLM using proteomics data from individuals who developed diseases during the follow-up period to perform downstream disease prediction tasks. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used as the primary evaluation metric, while accuracy, precision, and F1 score served as secondary performance measures. The results showed that, by unfreezing only the last eight encoder layers and training for 40 epochs, ProLM achieved strong disease-prediction performance (Supplementary Table 5). ProLM embeddings showed separation between most disease cases and healthy participants (Fig. 3). Across 16 common chronic disease prediction tasks, ProLM achieved the best AUC among the evaluated models in 10 tasks (Table 1). Using DeLong’s test, ProLM achieved significantly better predictive performance than the multilayer perceptron (MLP) in 15 diseases, than Feature Tokenizer Transformer (FT-Transformer) in 13 diseases, than residual networks (ResNet) in 10 diseases, than Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) in 7 diseases, and than Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) in 6 diseases. Overall, its performance was comparable to that of gradient boosting methods, which are often considered among the strongest approaches for prediction on numerical data (Supplementary Table 6). Specifically, ProLM predicted 14 of 16 common chronic diseases with an AUC greater than 0.8, including dementia (AUC = 0.9277), type 2 diabetes (T2D, AUC = 0.9448), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, AUC = 0.9203), heart failure (AUC = 0.9098), and prostate cancer (AUC = 0.9486).Fig. 3t-SNE visualization of proteomic embeddings from disease cases and controls in the fine-tuning datasets.Each panel shows two-dimensional t-SNE projections of ProLM embeddings for one disease-prediction task. Proteomics risk score (ProRS)

We defined the ProRS as ProLM’s predicted probability of incident disease in the model. We found that as the percentile of ProRS increases, the cumulative incidence of 16 common diseases also increases (Fig. 4A). Based on ProRS, individuals were further stratified into three risk groups: high risk (ProRS > 0.66), medium risk (0.33 <ProRS <0.66), and low risk (ProRS <0.33). Kaplan–Meier analyses showed distinct outcome trajectories across the three risk groups (Fig. 4B). Compared with individuals in the medium- and low-risk groups, those in the high-risk group had a markedly greater likelihood of developing disease during follow-up, whereas most individuals in the low-risk group remained healthy throughout follow-up. Specifically, for each of the 16 diseases, individuals in the high-risk group had at least a 16-fold higher risk than those in the low-risk group (Supplementary Table 7). Moreover, we further examined the impact of different risk group classifications on disease risk prediction. For example, whether high risk was defined as ProRS > 0.9, medium risk as 0.1 <ProRS <0.9, and low risk as ProRS <0.1, or alternatively, high risk was defined as ProRS > 0.8, medium risk as 0.2 <ProRS <0.8, and low risk as ProRS <0.2, the significant stratification of disease risk among individuals in different risk groups based on ProRS remained unaffected (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5, Supplementary Tables 8, 9).Fig. 4ProRS-based stratification of incident disease risk.A The relationship between ProRS and the observed event frequencies for 16 diseases. We used Cox proportional hazards (CPH) models for the 16 chronic disease outcomes and evaluated discriminative performance using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index). ProRS achieved a 10-year discriminative performance exceeding 0.7 for 12 diseases, demonstrating relatively high discriminative performance in diseases such as neurodegenerative diseases, respiratory diseases, and cancers (Fig. 5A, Table 2). For instance, Parkinson’s disease had a C-index of 0.853 (95%: 0.840–0.864), and lung cancer had a C-index of 0.903 (95%: 0.890–0.916). We then compared ProRS with three commonly used clinical risk scores: Age+Sex, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk equation, and PANEL. Among the 16 diseases, ProRS provided greater discriminative performance than the Age+Sex model for all 16 diseases, outperformed the ASCVD risk equation for 14 diseases, and outperformed PANEL for 11 diseases. We further evaluated the prediction model by examining calibration and conducting decision curve analysis. Calibration curves were constructed to assess the agreement between predicted risk and observed clinical events (Supplementary Fig. 8). Nearly all disease models demonstrated good calibration, with risk estimates that were neither overly high nor overly low. We then plotted decision curves for ProRS alongside common risk scores to illustrate the trade-offs between benefits and harms of clinical decisions across a range of decision thresholds under different models. For the vast majority of diseases, ProRS showed greater net benefit than models based on Age+Sex or ASCVD, particularly for cancers, neurodegenerative diseases, respiratory diseases, T2D, and stroke (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Integrated Gradients and perturbation analyses identify protein contributions to disease prediction

We performed a complementary attribution analysis using IG on the fine-tuned disease-prediction models. For each disease, we aggregated absolute IG attributions across samples to obtain a global protein-importance ranking and then functionally evaluated these rankings by grouped perturbation of the top-, bottom-, or randomly selected proteins. For dementia, the top IG-ranked proteins were GFAP, NEFL, GDF15, AGER and EDA2R; for diabetes, they included GDF15, DCBLD2, PON3, CTSO and HSD11B1 (Supplementary Data 3).

Grouped perturbation analyses showed that ProLM predictions were primarily driven by the highest-ranked proteins. In permutation experiments, perturbing the top 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 proteins led to progressive AUC declines of 0.16956, 0.23753, 0.31663, 0.42174, and 0.43386 in dementia, and 0.05801, 0.16176, 0.33004, 0.43504, and 0.45100 in T2D, respectively. In contrast, perturbation of bottom-ranked proteins had essentially no effect, and random perturbation produced only minimal changes (Supplementary Fig. 12). Ablation analysis showed the same overall trend but with smaller effect sizes: removing the top 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 proteins reduced the AUC by 0.03522, 0.05358, 0.13233, 0.19145, and 0.27472 in dementia, and by 0.01564, 0.03098, 0.04599, 0.07947, and 0.19399 in T2D, respectively.

Ablation study

The ablation experiments showed that, with all other modules held constant, removing either the protein sequence and functional embeddings or the PPI matrix in the attention mechanism from the pretraining model led to a decline in its performance (Supplementary Table 13).

Replication study

CKB provided external validation in an independent East Asian cohort for available outcomes. For cardiovascular diseases, ProLM achieved AUCs of 0.7901 for myocardial infarction (MI) and 0.7677 for coronary heart disease (CHD). Predictive performance was also retained for COPD (AUC = 0.7634), hypertension (AUC = 0.7339), stroke (AUC = 0.7415), and T2D (AUC = 0.8221) (Supplementary Table 14). Overall, these results indicate that ProLM captures disease-relevant proteomic patterns that transfer to an independent East Asian cohort.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a pre-trained proteomic language model and derived a ProRS for predicting incident chronic diseases. We were able to stratify risk across all 16 common chronic diseases examined, with individuals in the high-risk group being more likely to develop these conditions. Compared to traditional machine learning models, the attention mechanism in ProLM helped elucidate the complex interaction networks among plasma proteins that regulate diseases.

Currently, clinical risk scores and PRS are widely used in disease prediction9,31–33. However, clinical risk scores typically offer relatively low predictive accuracy. PRS have highlighted the genetic predisposition for chronic diseases yet remain limited by their inability to capture dynamic environmental influences and post-translational modifications34. In contrast, our model leverages plasma protein data as a direct readout of the physiological state, based on the premise that protein expression integrates the cumulative effects of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. By pretraining ProLM on data from relatively healthy individuals, we established a baseline representation of the proteomic regulatory network. Without using clinical labels, ProLM separated participants by sex in embedding space, suggesting that it captured biologically meaningful structure in the plasma proteome.

Using independent datasets with disease and healthy labels for fine-tuning, we designed two downstream tasks: disease prediction and disease subtype classification. The fine-tuned model performed strongly in most chronic disease prediction tasks, achieving AUC values above 0.8 in the majority of cases. ProLM was particularly effective for neurodegenerative diseases, metabolic diseases and cancers. Using ProRS, we further quantified disease risk for all individuals, irrespective of case-control grouping; individuals with higher ProRS generally had poorer outcomes. The discriminative performance of ProRS exceeded that of Age+Sex and ASCVD and was comparable to, or in some settings better than, that of a panel comprising 35 clinical variables. ProRS can predict the risk of multiple chronic diseases without clinical information, thereby highlighting the advantage of ProLM in reducing costs associated with data collection.

One of the key innovations of ProLM lay in the incorporation of a learnable PPI bias within the BERT encoder. The attention mechanism provided a model-based estimate of the relative importance of proteins in the prediction task. Many of the candidate protein biomarkers identified by ProLM have previously been associated with the corresponding diseases and belong to druggable gene sets, supporting the biological plausibility of the model-prioritized proteins. GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein) is a classic astrocyte marker that reflects inflammation and glial activation within the central nervous system35. Multiple studies have found that GFAP levels in the cerebrospinal fluid and serum of Alzheimer’s patients tend to increase, and these changes are associated with disease progression and cognitive decline36–39. Neurofilament light polypeptide (NEFL), an intermediate filament protein involved in maintaining neuronal caliber, serves as a prognostic biomarker in various neurodegenerative diseases40–42. Adrenomedullin (ADM) is involved in vasodilation, hormone secretion, cell growth and angiogenesis, and increased levels of ADM are associated with Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia43,44. Additionally, Wnt-9a (WNT9A), a component of the Wnt signaling pathway, is upregulated in the hippocampus of memory-impaired mice and is involved in modulating β-amyloid production and tau phosphorylation—processes that are central to Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis45–47. Growth/differentiation factor 15 (GDF-15), a member of the TGF-β superfamily, is highly expressed in senescent cells and is associated with the chronic pro-inflammatory state linked to age-related diseases48,49. Multiple studies have repeatedly reported an association between GDF15 and adverse neurocognitive outcomes or dementia18,50. Among the top 5 proteins associated with T2D, CDH2 (N-cadherin) is associated with multiple diabetic complications such as diabetic retinopathy and diabetic nephropathy51–53. REN (renin) is part of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and plays a role in regulating blood pressure and electrolyte balance. Activation of the renin-angiotensin system is crucial in the pathogenesis of diabetes54. For example, angiotensin-mediated oxidative stress, inflammation, and increased levels of free fatty acids may lead to β-cell dysfunction in diabetes55,56. Additionally, the activation of the renin-angiotensin system might amplify the effects of other pathogenic pathways involved in diabetes55. GDF15 exerts its effects by binding to the glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor family receptor α-like (GFRAL), which in turn suppresses appetite, enhances thermogenesis and lipolysis, and ameliorates insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis57,58. In addition to reducing appetite, GDF15 promotes weight loss by increasing energy expenditure in muscle tissue59. Notably, rather than being specific to a single disease, GDF15 was highly prioritized in the prediction of eight chronic diseases, suggesting that it may represent a shared biomarker candidate across multiple chronic conditions. In the longitudinal analysis, by examining the dynamic expression trajectory of GDF15 up to 15 years prior to clinical diagnosis, we found that its expression exhibits a significant monotonic increase as the diagnosis time approaches, with a particularly rapid elevation occurring in the year preceding diagnosis. This suggests that GDF15 expression changes were detectable at least 15 years before clinical onset.

Another important innovation was that ProLM incorporated not only protein expression features but also sequence- and function-derived information. Previous research has shown that, for most human prediction tasks, models trained using genomic data from multiple species outperform those trained solely on human sequences60. Therefore, we utilized the ProteinBERT model—pretrained on approximately 106 million representative proteins from throughout the tree of life, as provided by UniRef90—to extract sequence features for plasma proteins61. In addition to traditional sequence information, ProteinBERT includes a protein annotation prediction pretraining task, which enables the model to learn various protein functions. Consequently, ProLM integrates rich protein information from multiple levels and modalities, effectively capturing the distribution patterns of plasma protein expression as well as the inherent complex regulatory networks in proteomics data, which may explain the observed performance across the downstream disease-prediction tasks.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the UK Biobank has a healthy volunteer selection bias, as participants are typically healthier, more educated, and of higher socioeconomic status than the general population. Therefore, ProLM can be considered a proteome-pretrained model for a relatively healthy general population. Second, ProLM includes ~2900 plasma proteins measured on the Olink Explore 3072 platform, which is currently the most common platform used in large prospective biobanks, but this still represents only a portion of the human plasma proteome. Third, the current chunking method divides the proteins into multiple segments for independent processing, which may result in insufficient information exchange between different blocks and potentially impair the model’s ability to capture global dependency relationships. Fourth, although the attention mechanism provides a degree of interpretability, we acknowledge that attention weights may not directly reflect true feature importance. The proteins prioritized by ProLM should be interpreted as model-identified predictive features rather than causal disease drivers. Likewise, replication of directionally consistent associations in external genetic datasets does not establish causality or therapeutic relevance. Fifth, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) assumes missing at random (MAR), which may not hold for proteins below the limit of detection (LOD). In the future, using imputation methods specifically designed for proteomics data or measuring a larger-scale proteome may alleviate concerns about missing-value imputation. Sixth, current medication use may alter protein expression patterns, potentially confounding disease prediction. For example, statins affect lipid-related proteins. Seventh, only a subset of the 16 outcomes examined in the UK Biobank was available for replication, and further validation in additional cohorts remains necessary. Eighth, although ProRS showed encouraging discrimination, calibration, and decision-curve profiles, these cohort-based evaluations do not establish clinical utility in practice. Prospective validation, workflow integration, and clinical impact studies are required before considering implementation. Finally, ProLM showed limited performance in classifying dementia and stroke subtypes, which may reflect difficulty in distinguishing highly similar phenotypes and/or insufficient sample sizes for rare subtypes. Subtype classification may require substantially larger cohorts to achieve adequate statistical power.

Methods

Inclusion & Ethics

This research complied with all relevant ethical regulations. UK Biobank has approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee as a Research Tissue Bank, and all UK Biobank participants provided written informed consent. The CKB study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Beijing, China), the Peking University Institutional Review Board (Beijing, China) and the Oxford Tropical Research Ethics Committee, University of Oxford (UK), and all CKB participants provided written informed consent. Analyses used de-identified participant data accessed under the relevant cohort governance and data-access approvals.

Plasma proteomics

The UK Biobank is a large-scale, prospective cohort study established in 2006 to investigate the genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors that influence health outcomes62,63. With over 500,000 participants, it serves as a vital resource for biomedical research. In collaboration with pharmaceutical companies, the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) aims to analyze the proteins in blood samples from a subset of these participants to identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets64,65. The UKB-PPP employs Olink’s high-throughput, multiplexed proteomic technology to analyze participant proteomes. Between April 2021 and February 2022, proteomic data from 54,219 UK Biobank participants were analyzed using the Olink Explore platform, which utilized up to 2923 unique assays per sample. In this study, proteins that were absent in more than 50% of participants were excluded from the analysis. For the remaining missing values, imputation was performed using the ‘miceforest’ package in Python, with all proteins serving as predictors for the respective imputation.

Incident outcomes

In the downstream tasks, we selected 16 common diseases as prediction tasks, while the subtypes of four common diseases were used as classification tasks. The diagnoses of T1D (E10), T2D (E11), obesity (E66), hypertension (I10), acute myocardial infarction (I21), chronic ischemic heart disease (I25), atrial fibrillation and flutter (I48), heart failure (I50), Crohn’s disease (K50), and ulcerative colitis (K51) were identified using ICD-10 codes (Field 2000, 20002, 3000, 40001, 40002). For conditions such as asthma, dementia (including Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, and frontotemporal dementia), COPD, stroke (including ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, and subarachnoid hemorrhage), and Parkinson’s disease, the diagnosis dates were derived from algorithmic combinations of coded information from baseline assessment data, along with linked data. The date of cancer diagnosis was obtained from a central registry, with cancer types classified according to ICD-10 codes. Death dates were similarly obtained from the central registry. The blood sample collection time was defined as the date of the participant’s first visit to the UK Biobank assessment center (baseline time). In the prediction tasks, participants who were diagnosed with these diseases before the baseline were excluded from the analysis, whereas in the disease subtyping tasks, they were not excluded. The healthy population was defined as participants who had a BMI < 40 and did not have any of these 16 common chronic diseases or any cancers either at baseline or during follow-up, and they were randomly divided in a 9:1 ratio to serve as the pre-training data and the healthy controls for fine-tuning, respectively66–68.

PPI matrix

PPI was defined based on criteria established by Szklarczyk et al., wherein an interaction is recognized when there is evidence of both evolutionary and functional partnerships between proteins69–73. The STRING database was employed to evaluate and quantify the evidence for these interactions, utilizing seven categories: ‘neighborhood,’ ‘fusion,’ ‘co-occurrence,’ ‘co-expression,’ ‘experiments,’ ‘database,’ and ‘text mining.’ Confidence scores, ranging from 0 to 1, were assigned to these associations to indicate the likelihood that each connection is valid.

Pretraining protein language model on relatively healthy populations

Feature embedding layer

The plasma proteomic data used for pretraining were derived from 15,499 relatively healthy individuals from the UKB-PPP. Expression values for 2873 proteins were employed. To enrich the input protein features, we extracted sequence and functional attributes from a pretrained ProteinBERT model. ProteinBERT is a protein sequence language model that has been pre-trained on ~106 million proteins from a broad range of species derived from UniProtKB/UniRef9061. By introducing an innovative pre-training task focused on protein function prediction, ProteinBERT is able to capture a diverse array of functionalities—including molecular functions, biological processes, and subcellular localizations61. Our pre-extracted protein sequence and functional features were first mapped into a hidden space via a linear embedding layer. Simultaneously, the protein expression values were fed through a separate linear layer to ensure dimensional alignment. These two embedding representations were then fused through element-wise multiplication. To capture global information, a learnable [CLS] token was added to the beginning of the fused sequence, and its corresponding output vector serves as a global representation of the entire sequence. Considering the large number of proteins, a chunking strategy was employed with a chunk size set to 512, whereby the [CLS] embedding was computed for each chunk and subsequently averaged across all chunks to obtain the final global feature.

BERT encoder

To accommodate the unique characteristics of proteomic data, we designed a custom BERT encoder23,24. The primary innovation lies in the incorporation of a learnable protein–protein correlation bias matrix into the attention mechanism so that prior information about protein–protein interactions is directly infused into the attention weights. This bias is scaled by a learnable coefficient. More specifically, we redefined the self-attention module. For the given input hidden states, the query, key, and value matrices are computed, and the initial attention scores are obtained via dot product, which are then scaled by the square root of the attention head dimension.

Pre-training and masking strategy

The pre-training dataset was randomly partitioned into training and validation sets using an 80:20 split. The pre-training task used a masking-only strategy. For each batch of protein expression data, 15% of the protein positions are randomly selected for masking. The masking procedure involves: replacing 80% of these positions with zero; substituting 10% with random values sampled uniformly from the range between the minimum and maximum expression values; and retaining the original values for the remaining 10%. For the prediction of the masked positions, we use the MSE loss function. In addition, predictive accuracy was recorded based on whether the predicted value fell within a tolerance equal to half of the standard deviation of the corresponding protein expression values. The overall model performance is ultimately assessed using MSE, MAE, and R² metrics.

Hyperparameter settings

The embedding dimension was set to 768. The Transformer architecture comprised 24 layers with 8 attention heads per layer, and the intermediate dimension of the feed-forward network was configured to be four times the hidden dimension. The AdamW optimizer was employed with an initial learning rate of 5E-05, and a cosine annealing scheduler was used to dynamically adjust the learning rate throughout training74. Pretraining was conducted for 200 epochs (patience = 10).

Integrated Gradients attribution and perturbation tests

For each mini-batch, a subset of proteins was randomly masked, and IG attributions were computed for the model’s masked-position predictions with a mean-expression baseline and a fixed number of integration steps. Disease prediction task

To enable the model to discriminate disease risk, we fine-tuned our pre-trained model. For 16 common chronic disease risks, separate classification models were constructed. Participants diagnosed with these outcome events during follow-up were treated as cases. Building upon the pre-trained model, we added a fully connected classifier head to map the BERT hidden states to a binary probability distribution. During fine-tuning, most of the pre-trained weights were frozen; only the final 8 layers of BERT and the classifier parameters were unfrozen. The fine-tuning process utilized cross-entropy loss as the objective function and the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e−4. Training and validation sets were created by randomly sampling 80% and 20%. The data splitting procedure was chosen to avoid data leakage. Training was conducted for 40 epochs, during which model performance was monitored on the validation set, including accuracy, F1 score, precision, and AUC. The best model was saved for analysis.

To elucidate the protein expression patterns in the diseased populations, we leveraged an attention-based interpretability approach. Specifically, when the model processes input proteins using a chunking strategy, it extracts the attention information corresponding to the [CLS] token from each chunk. The attention scores from the [CLS] token to the proteins (averaged over layers and heads) in each chunk are then aggregated to obtain a global distribution reflecting the protein contributions. The high-dimensional [CLS] embeddings were reduced to two dimensions using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE). A scatter plot of the t-SNE components was generated, where each point was colored according to its true disease label.

Comparison with common baseline models

We compared ProLM with several well-established machine learning and deep learning models that have demonstrated robust performance on tabular data. These models included CatBoost76, LightGBM, MLP77,78, ResNet79, and FT-Transformer80. Both LightGBM and CatBoost employ GBDT frameworks that are highly effective for tabular datasets76,81. The MLP and ResNet represent standard baseline architectures that consistently yield stable performance across diverse datasets. The FT-Transformer, a Transformer-based model specifically tailored for tabular data, has shown competitive performance relative to GBDT approaches on certain tasks. DeLong’s test was used to assess whether the paired AUCs of two models differed significantly.

All baseline models were optimized under the same validation framework. For each disease, data were split into training and validation sets (80%/20%), and hyperparameter tuning was performed on the training set. For LightGBM and CatBoost, 50 candidate hyperparameter settings were evaluated per task, including learning rate (1E−3 to 1E−1), number of trees/iterations (200–2000), tree depth (3–10), leaf complexity, minimum child samples (5–100), and feature fraction (0.6–1.0). For MLP, ResNet and FT-Transformer, 50 candidate settings were evaluated per task, covering hidden dimension (128–1024), network depth (2–6 layers/blocks), dropout (0–0.5), batch size (64–512), learning rate (1E−4 to 5E−3) and weight decay (1E−6 to 1E−2); for FT-Transformer, the number of attention heads (4 or 8) was also tuned. Tree-based models used early stopping with a patience of 50 rounds, and deep-learning models were trained for up to 100 epochs with early stopping based on validation AUC (patience = 10).

ProRS for disease stratification

For each incident disease endpoint, the model-estimated event probability was used as the protein-based risk score (ProRS). Participants were assigned to prespecified risk strata according to their ProRS values: low risk (<0.33), medium risk (0.33 <= ProRS <= 0.66), and high risk (>0.66). Follow-up started at baseline and ended at the earliest occurrence of the target diagnosis, death, or administrative censoring. Differences in disease-free survival among ProRS groups were evaluated using log-rank tests. Confidence intervals for disease-free probability estimates were calculated from Greenwood’s variance estimator.

We used three clinical predictor sets as comparators. The Age+Sex set included only age and sex, providing a parsimonious clinical baseline. The ASCVD-based set followed the variables used in ASCVD risk equations, including sex, age, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive medication use, diabetes history, and current smoking status82,83. The PANEL set represented a broader clinical profile and included demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, anthropometric measurements, blood pressure, medical and medication history, and blood biochemical markers. Specifically, this set comprised age, sex, education, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, BMI, waist circumference, hip circumference, systolic blood pressure, diabetes history, antihypertensive treatment, and the biochemical indicators listed in Supplementary Table 15.

For each disease outcome, survival models were fitted using Cox proportional hazards (CPH) regression. Seven predictor configurations were assessed: ProRS, Age+Sex, ASCVD, PANEL, ProRS+Age+Sex, ProRS+ASCVD, and ProRS+PANEL. Discrimination was quantified using Harrell’s C-index, and 95% confidence intervals were obtained from 1000 bootstrap resamples. Model calibration was examined by comparing predicted event probabilities with observed event rates. Predicted risks were divided into 10 quantile-based groups, and each group was summarized by its mean predicted risk and corresponding observed event rate.

Druggability analysis

To assess the translational relevance of proteins prioritized by the model, we cross-referenced these proteins with curated druggable gene catalogs28,29. Overlapping proteins were annotated according to the three-tier framework proposed by Finan et al.29. Tier 1 includes targets of approved small-molecule or biologic therapies and targets of drug candidates in clinical development. Tier 2 includes genes encoding proteins with known bioactive, drug-like small-molecule binders, as well as genes with at least 50% identity to approved drug targets. Tier 3 comprises proteins with lower similarity to approved drug targets and additional members of major druggable gene families not assigned to Tier 1 or Tier 2.

Longitudinal trajectory analyses

We conducted nested case-control analyses in the UKB-PPP cohort to characterize prediagnostic changes in key proteins, using T2D and dementia as representative outcomes. Cases were participants who developed incident T2D or dementia during follow-up and met the study eligibility criteria. Controls were selected at a 1:2 ratio and matched to cases by sex and age within 2 years. For each outcome, we plotted protein trajectories during the 15 years before the diagnosis or index date for the five proteins most strongly prioritized by the model. Monotonic temporal trends in protein abundance were tested using the Mann-Kendall test. We further fitted linear mixed-effects models that included time, group, and a time-by-group interaction term. The interaction term was used to test whether prediagnostic cases and matched controls showed different longitudinal protein trajectories. A P value < 0.05 for this interaction was considered evidence of differential temporal change.

Integrated Gradients attribution and grouped perturbation analyses for disease prediction

To complement attention-based interpretation, we performed a post hoc attribution analysis using IG for each fine-tuned binary disease-prediction model. IG was computed with respect to the positive-class output using the mean plasma expression vector as the baseline input. Absolute attributions were aggregated across samples to derive a global importance score for each protein, and proteins were ranked according to this score. To test whether the highest-ranked proteins were important for model output, we performed grouped perturbation analyses using k = 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 proteins.

Two perturbation schemes were evaluated. In permutation analysis, the selected protein expression values were shuffled across individuals within a batch. In ablation analysis, the selected proteins were replaced by their mean-expression baseline values. For each kk, we perturbed either the top-ranked proteins, the bottom-ranked proteins, or an equally sized set of randomly selected proteins. Random perturbations were repeated five times, and all grouped perturbation results were summarized using the mean AUC, accuracy, F1 score, precision, and recall.

Disease subtype classification

To test the model’s capability in classifying disease subtypes, we selected dementia, stroke, diabetes, and IBD as representative examples. The classification tasks for dementia and stroke were formulated as multi-class problems, whereas diabetes and IBD were addressed as binary classification tasks. Participants included for fine-tuning were those diagnosed with a specific subtype; patients presenting with multiple subtypes concurrently were excluded from the analysis. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets. Upon the pre-trained model, a classification head—consisting of a dropout layer followed by a fully connected layer—was appended to map the global feature vector to a probability distribution over either three classes or two classes. The fine-tuning process was performed on mini-batches using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 2E−5, and a cosine learning rate scheduler with warm-up was employed. Cross-Entropy Loss was used as the objective function. Training was conducted for 40 epochs, during which model performance was monitored on the validation set using multiple metrics, including loss, accuracy, precision, F1 score, and AUC computed via a one-versus-one strategy for multiclass problems.

Replication study in CKB

The CKB is a population-based prospective cohort study designed to investigate the genetic and environmental determinants of common chronic diseases in the Chinese population84. Between 2004 and 2008, over 500,000 participants aged 30 to 79 years were recruited from 10 geographically diverse regions across China, encompassing five rural and five urban areas to ensure representation of different socioeconomic and environmental contexts. At baseline, all participants completed extensive interviewer-administered questionnaires that captured detailed information on socio-demographic characteristics (including education and occupation), lifestyle behaviors (such as smoking, alcohol consumption, dietary patterns, and physical activity), personal and family medical history, and environmental exposures. In addition to the questionnaire data, trained staff conducted standardized physical examinations to measure anthropometric parameters including height, weight, waist and hip circumference, and blood pressure84. Venous blood samples were collected from participants following an overnight fast and were processed, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. Follow-up of participants was achieved through a combination of active re-survey methods and passive data collection via annual linkages with regional health registries, hospital records, and mortality databases. Follow-up began at the baseline examination date and continued until death, loss to follow-up, or the administrative censoring date (December 31, 2018), whichever occurred first.

The proteomics data were generated from a nested case-control study of cardiovascular diseases (CHD) conducted within the CKB framework and included 3977 participants at the initial selection stage85–89. We evaluated six incident outcomes: MI, CHD, COPD, hypertension, stroke, and T2D. The analytic sample included 1866 MI cases and 2,111 controls, 1980 CHD cases and 1997 controls, 239 COPD cases and 3738 controls, 709 hypertension cases and 3268 controls, 570 stroke cases and 3407 controls, and 262 T2D cases and 3715 controls. Plasma samples were analyzed using the Olink Explore platform, which employs proximity extension assay technology to quantify protein biomarkers with high specificity and sensitivity. Further methodological details and initial findings from this proteomic substudy have been described in previous publications64,65.

Statistics & reproducibility

The model was pretrained on 15,499 participants who met the study definition of relative health at baseline. For downstream analyses, sample sizes were determined by the number of eligible participants available for each task, and no formal power calculation was performed in advance. After the analytic cohorts had been defined, all available observations meeting the prespecified criteria were retained. Sex was included as a covariate in the CPH models. Sex information for UK Biobank and CKB participants was obtained from the records provided by the original cohorts. We did not conduct separate sex- or gender-stratified effect analyses, because the study was designed to develop and evaluate population-level proteomic prediction models rather than sex-specific prediction models. Model development and all statistical analyses were carried out in Python v3.11.
