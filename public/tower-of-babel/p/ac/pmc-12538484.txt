Serological evidence of Q fever in Tibetan sheep in Qinghai Province, China
Authors: Xiaomin Wu, Chao Chen, Te Ba, Lin Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Q fever, caused by the obligate intracellular bacterium Coxiella burnetii (C. burnetii), is a global zoonotic disease. No data is available about C. burnetii infection in Tibetan sheep in Qinghai, China. In this study, 1043 Tibetan sheep serum samples (421 females and 622 males) were collected from 8 regions in Qinghai Province and examined for antibodies against C. burnetii using a commercial ELISA Kit. The overall seroprevalence of C. burnetii infection was 5.9 % (62/1043, 95 % CI 4.5–7.4). Interestingly, C. burnetii infection rates in Tibetan sheep showed no statistically significant correlations with age, geographical region, and gender (P > 0.05). The results of the present survey support the widespread presence of C. burnetii in Tibetan sheep in Qinghai, also providing baseline data for improving livestock management, preventing and controlling Q fever in large ruminants in the region. Further molecular epidemiological investigations are warranted to understand the epidemiology of C. burnetii transmission in Qinghai, China.

Introduction

Coxiella burnetii (C. burnetii) is an obligate intracellular bacterium that causes Q fever [1]. It is primarily transmitted through inhalation of aerosols or contaminated dust, exhibiting significant zoonotic potential and environmental resistance, thereby posing a serious threat to livestock production and public health [2,3]. Consequently, it is classified as a Category B bioterrorism agent and a notifiable disease [4].

C. burnetii causes both acute and chronic infections. In animals, infections are typically subclinical but can lead to reproductive complications such as abortion and stillbirth, alongside reduced productivity, incurring substantial economic losses in livestock industries. In humans, most patients exhibit acute clinical symptoms, presenting as acute pneumonia, hepatitis, or severe flu-like illness. A very small proportion of patients may develop severe chronic forms, characterized by endocarditis, chronic hepatitis, and chronic fatigue syndrome [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

Q fever is widely distributed across the globe, particularly in regions with developed livestock industries, where infection rates are high and economic losses are substantial [11,12]. Qinghai Province is one of China's major livestock hubs, and Tibetan sheep serve as a crucial economic resource for local herders, making their health status of paramount importance. However, research on Q fever in Tibetan sheep in this region remains scarce. Therefore, this study primarily evaluates the infection rate of Coxiella burnetii in Tibetan sheep populations by investigating seroprevalence, aiming to understand the epidemiological status of Q fever in the area. The findings will provide a scientific basis for developing targeted prevention and control measures, reducing economic losses in the livestock industry, and mitigating zoonotic risks.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study involved 8 regions throughout Qinghai Province in China, respectively (Fig. 1). Qinghai Province, located in northwestern China, lies in the northeastern part of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and derives its name from Qinghai Lake, China's largest inland saltwater lake. With an average altitude exceeding 3000 m above sea level, it serves as the source region of the Yangtze River, Yellow River, and Lancang River (Mekong River), earning it the title “Water Tower of China”, a vital freshwater source for billions. The province boasts vast and diverse terrains, encompassing snow-capped mountains, vast grasslands, expansive deserts, and crystalline lakes. Samples

A cross-sectional study was conducted across Qinghai Province during autumn 2023 to investigate C. burnetii in Tibetan sheep populations. The sample size was scientifically determined using the prevalence study formula proposed by Cannon et al. [22]. Based on an expected seropositivity rate of 50 %, a 95 % confidence level, and a 7 % margin of error, the formula calculated that 1043 blood samples needed to be collected from 43 flocks across 8 regions in Qinghai Province of China. This study subsequently adopted a multistage random sampling method: eight major Tibetan sheep farming regions in Qinghai Province were randomly selected, covering the province's primary geographical distribution. Multiple flocks were then randomly chosen from official registries in each region, resulting in the inclusion of 43 flocks in total. Within each selected flock, individual blood samples were collected during routine management activities using systematic random sampling to minimize selection bias. Overall, 1043 blood samples were collected and distributed as follows: 120 samples from Xining City, 160 from Haidong City, 125 from Haibei Prefecture, 119 from Haixi Prefecture, 200 from Hainan Prefecture, 80 from Huangnan Prefecture, 120 from Yushu Prefecture, and 119 from Guoluo Prefecture. In terms of sex, the samples included 421 females and 622 males. By age group, the distribution was: 254 animals (0–0.5 years), 611 (0.5–1 year), 121 (1–1.5 years), and 57 (1.5–2 years).

All the Tibetan sheep were maintained under semi-extensive management systems, characterized by daytime grazing on communal alpine meadows (altitude 2800–4200 m) and nighttime housing in predator-proof enclosures. Epidemiological data including vaccination history, water sources, and breed information were collected through structured interviews with herders. Blood collection protocol: jugular venous samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 4 h before being transported on ice to the laboratory within 2 h. Serum separation was achieved by centrifugation at 3000g for 10 min, with aliquots stored at −80 °C until use.

Detection of C. burnetii antibodies

Anti-C. burnetii IgG antibodies were assayed using ELISA kits, with operations conducted in strict adherence to the protocols provided by the manufacturer. The specific kit employed was the ID Screen® Coxiella burnetii Indirect Multi-species (supplied by IDVET Innovative Diagnostics, Montpellier, France). Optical density (OD) values at a wavelength of 450 nm were determined using an ELISA microplate reader (Multiskan™ FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific™, USA). The signal-to-noise ratio, expressed as the S/P percentage, was computed using the formula outlined below:

S/P%=100×ODtested sample–ODNC/ODpositive control–ODNC

Here, “S” denotes the tested sample, and “P” represents the positive control.

Sera samples were classified as positive for anti-C. burnetii IgG if their calculated S/P ratios exceeded 50 %.

Variations in the infection rate of Tibetan sheep across different geographic locations, sexes, and age groups were analyzed via the Chi-square test using SPSS software (Statistical Analysis System, Version 22.0). Values of P < 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Of the 1043 serum samples collected, the overall seroprevalence of C. burnetii infection was 5.9 % (62/1043, 95 % CI 4.5–7.4), including 5.7 % (24/421，95 % CI 3.5–7.9) female Tibetan sheep and 6.1 % (38/622，95 % CI 4.2–8.0) male Tibetan sheep (Table 1). Distributed among the regions as follows: Xining 4.2 % (5/120, 95 % CI 0.6–7.7), Haidong 4.4 % (7/160, 95 % CI 1.2–7.5), Haibei 6.4 % (8/125, 95 % CI 2.1–10.7), Haixi 7.6 % (9/119, 95 % CI 2.8–12.3), Hainan 6.5 % (13/200, 95 % CI 3.1–9.9), Huangnan 6.3 % (5/80, 95 % CI 0.9–11.6), Yushu 6.7 % (8/120, 95 % CI 2.2–11.1), Guoluo 5.9 % (7/119, 95 % CI 1.7–10.1) (Table 2). The seroprevalence among the different age groups ranged from 4.7 to 8.8 % (Table 3) and was higher in the 1.5–2 years category. Discussion

This study conducted a systematic seroepidemiological investigation of Q fever infection status in Tibetan sheep in Qinghai Province. The overall seroprevalence C. burnetii infection was 5.9 % (62/1043, 95 % CI 4.5–7.4). Compared to the 14.39 % seroprevalence reported in Gansu and Tibet [13], Qinghai's lower rate (5.9 %) may be due to the current semi-intensive management system. This husbandry model has significantly decreased the duration of direct contact between sheep and potential pathogen sources—including wildlife reservoirs and tick vectors—thereby effectively mitigating cross-species transmission risks [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. However, variations in detection methods and sampling strategies across studies could also influence results. Notably, no significant gender-based differences were observed in seropositivity rates, with female and male sheep showing comparable infection rates of 5.7 % (24/421; 95 % CI: 3.5–7.9) and 6.1 % (38/622; 95 % CI: 4.2–8.0), respectively. This finding is consistent with multiple studies indicating that gender is not a significant factor affecting Q fever seropositivity rates [[18], [19], [20]].

Notably, a recent study from Paraguay—the first report of C. burnetii circulation in the country—highlighted the role of C. burnetii in causing abortion and fetal resorption in livestock [19]. The study documented abortion and fetal resorption in ewes during their last pregnancy, as well as orchitis in breeding rams. It was further found that two ewes that had experienced abortion and one ewe with fetal resorption were all seropositive for C. burnetii, with high antibody titers. Moreover, the detection of C. burnetii infection in sheep herds under semi-intensive management underscores the need for continued vigilance. Prior to this study, there had been no reports of human or animal infection with C. burnetii in Paraguay. This first identification of bacterial circulation not only suggests potential economic losses due to reproductive failure in livestock but also raises concerns about health risks to animals, particularly pregnant ones.

Age-related analysis revealed an increasing trend in infection rates, with 4.7 % (12/254, 95 % CI 2.1–7.3) in 0–0.5 years old Tibetan sheep and 8.8 % (5/57, 95 % CI 1.4–16.1) in 1.5–2 years old Tibetan sheep, though no significant differences were detected. This aligns with previous reports indicating higher risks in older animals due to prolonged environmental exposure [21]. However, the narrow age range (≤2 years) in this study may limit the full characterization of age-related infection patterns.

This study has several limitations. First, sampling was limited to eight regions, potentially underestimating province-wide infection rates. Second, the analysis focused exclusively on host factors (age, region, gender), without incorporating environmental and management variables. Third, the inherent limitations of serological testing, including possible false-positive or false-negative results, were not systematically quantified. Future research should expand sampling coverage, incorporate environmental variables, and validate results using multiple detection techniques. These efforts will provide a more comprehensive basis for Q fever prevention in Tibetan sheep, safeguarding both the economic livelihoods of herders and the biocultural heritage of this breed.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, the present report is the first to document the seroprevalence of C. burnetii infection in Tibetan sheep in Qinghai, China. This study showed existence of C. burnetii antibodies in Tibetan sheep, and the seroprevalence of 5.9 % in Tibetan sheep in Qinghai, China. The present survey support the widespread presence of C. burnetii in Tibetan sheep in Qinghai, also providing baseline data for improving livestock management, preventing and controlling Q fever in large ruminants in the region.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by Research Ethics Committee of Qinghai University (No. SL-2023043) in accordance with the Animal Ethics Procedures and Guidelines of the People's Republic of China. Blood sample collection (3 mL per animal) was performed by qualified veterinarians through aseptic left jugular venipuncture using 5 mL sterile vacuum tubes (non-anticoagulated), with all procedures adhering to OIE animal welfare standards. Herd owners provided informed oral consent during participatory sampling procedures, where they voluntarily assisted in animal restraint. Herd data collection was conducted synchronously with biological sampling under standardized protocols.
