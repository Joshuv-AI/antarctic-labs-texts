Irony Comprehension in Monolingual and Bilingual Children With Autism Spectrum Disorder
Authors: Maria Andreou, Stella Lampri, Eleni Peristeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study examined verbal irony comprehension in monolingual and bilingual autistic children, focusing on irony recognition, intention understanding, cue use while deciphering ironic meanings, and error patterns. A low‐verbal, multimodal task was used to minimize linguistic and metalinguistic demands. Measures of language ability, Theory of Mind (ToM), executive functions, and cue use were included. Results showed that bilingualism did not negatively affect irony comprehension, as bilingual autistic children performed comparably to monolingual autistic children and showed no disadvantage relative to bilingual typically developing peers. Across groups, ironic criticism was understood more accurately than ironic praise. Irony recognition was associated with executive functioning, specifically inhibition. In autistic children, intention understanding was primarily predicted by second order verbal ToM and, to a lesser extent, sentence comprehension. Cue analyses revealed that contextual information and facial expressions were the most frequently used sources of information, whereas facial cues were predominantly used by bilingual autistic children in ironic praise. Prosodic cues were rarely reported across groups. No group differences emerged in error patterns. Overall, the findings indicate that bilingualism is not detrimental to irony comprehension in autism and highlight the importance of socio‐cognitive, language, and executive mechanisms in pragmatic processing.Keywords: autism spectrum disorder, bilingualism, executive functions, figurative language, ironic criticism, ironic praise, irony comprehension, language skills, theory of mind

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by enduring difficulties in social interaction and communication, alongside restricted, repetitive, and rigid patterns of behavior, interests, and/or activities that are atypical for an individual's age and sociocultural context (DSM‐5; ICD‐11; World Health Organization 2022). The term spectrum reflects the considerable heterogeneity of ASD, encompassing substantial variability in symptom type, severity, and functional outcomes, as well as wide‐ranging intellectual, cognitive, and language profiles. (Peristeri and Andreou 2025; Peristeri and Andreou 2024; Cordova et al. 2020; Tager‐Flusberg and Joseph 2003; Vulchanova et al. 2015).

While structural language abilities (i.e., syntax, vocabulary) vary considerably in ASD (Andreou, Themistocleous, and Peristeri 2026; Schaeffer et al. 2023; Tager‐Flusberg and Joseph 2003), pragmatic language difficulties are widely regarded a core feature of the disorder (Järvinen‐Pasley et al. 2008; Kalandadze et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2014; Norbury and Sparks 2014; Peristeri et al. 2026; Simmons et al. 2014; Tager‐Flusberg 1999; Tager‐Flusberg et al. 2005). Pragmatic ability refers to the effective comprehension and use of language across diverse social and communicative contexts (Cardillo et al. 2021; Eigsti et al. 2011; Geurts and Embrechts 2008). Importantly, pragmatic difficulties in ASD have been reported across different levels of structural language and intellectual functioning (Loukusa and Moilanen 2009; Tager‐Flusberg et al. 2005; Volden et al. 2009; Vulchanova et al. 2015; Young et al. 2005). Nevertheless, pragmatic performance varies substantially depending on the phenomenon examined (Andreou, Lampri, et al. 2025) and the language and cognitive mechanisms involved in its processing (Andrés‐Roqueta and Katsos 2020; Geurts et al. 2019).

Non‐literal language, or figurative language (FL) refers to linguistic expressions in which intended meaning extends beyond literal interpretation (Gibbs Jr. 1994; Gibbs Jr. and Colston 2012). Through FL, speakers communicate intentions, attitudes, and emotions indirectly, making it a central component of everyday social communication (Agostino et al. 2017; Nippold 2016; Wilson 2011). Reviews (Kalandadze et al. 2018, 2019; Lampri et al. 2023) and experimental studies (Lampri et al. 2024; Panzeri et al. 2022; Yankovitz et al. 2023) consistently report difficulties in FL processing in ASD.

Verbal irony is a representative form of FL and has been extensively studied in both typically developing (TD) and non‐TD populations (Filippova 2014; Winner 1988; Djordjevic et al. 2018; Happé 1993; Stratta et al. 2007). In verbal irony, the literal meaning of an utterance contrasts with the speaker's communicative intent (Gibbs et al. 2014; Wilson and Sperber 2012). For example, a speaker may say “What lovely weather!” on a cold, rainy day, while actually intending to express dissatisfaction with the weather conditions. In this sense, the present study focuses specifically on verbal irony, because it requires the listener not only to reject the literal interpretation of the utterance, but also infer the speaker's intended meaning and communicative intention. Importantly, verbal irony is not a unitary phenomenon. A widely adopted content‐based distinction is between ironic criticism, in which a literally positive utterance conveys disapproval (e.g., saying “That was brilliant” after someone makes an obvious mistake), and ironic praise, in which a literally negative utterance conveys approval (e.g., saying “What terrible luck” to someone who has just won a prize) (Filippova 2014). This distinction is theoretically important because the two subtypes may differ in processing demands and developmental trajectories, with ironic criticism often being more readily understood than ironic praise in both TD and autistic children (Hancock et al. 2000; Pexman and Glenwright 2007; Panzeri et al. 2022; Pexman et al. 2011). Verbal irony is prevalent in everyday communication across family, social, educational, and online contexts (Banasik‐Jemielniak et al. 2020; Pexman et al. 2009; Recchia et al. 2010; Gibbs 2000; Tannen 2005; Ely and McCabe 1994; Olkoniemi et al. 2025; Whalen et al. 2009, 2013).

Irony Comprehension in Autistic and TD Children

For both TD and non‐TD populations, comprehending verbal irony is particularly challenging (Pexman et al. 2019), given the complex cognitive and linguistic processes involved. Irony comprehension requires transcending literal meaning, integrating contextual information, and inferring speaker's intentions (Pexman et al. 2019; Vulchanova et al. 2015), while coordinating shared knowledge, intentions, and emotional states between interlocutors (Panzeri et al. 2022; Pexman 2008; Saban‐Bezalel et al. 2019; Scianna 2024).

The complexity of irony comprehension is reflected in its delayed developmental trajectory in TD children (Filippova and Astington 2010; Pexman and Glenwright 2007). Although early sensitivity has been reported at 3–4 years, reliable comprehension emerges around 5–6 years and continues to develop across childhood (Aguert et al. 2016; Angeleri and Airenti 2014; Dews et al. 1996; Filippova and Astington 2008). Children tend to understand ironic criticism earlier than ironic praise (Pexman and Glenwright 2007), and failures to recognize ironic intent often result in misinterpretation of irony as errors or white lies (Demorest et al. 1984; Hancock et al. 2000; Li et al. 2013; Sullivan et al. 1995; Talwar et al. 2007).

Due to limited longitudinal evidence, the developmental trajectory of irony comprehension in ASD remains unclear. Cross‐sectional studies yield inconsistent findings. Namely, while several studies identify significant differences between autistic and TD individuals (Adachi et al. 2004; Deliens et al. 2018; Diaz 2010; Huang et al. 2015; Imaizumi et al. 2009; Kaland et al. 2002; Li et al. 2013; MacKay and Shaw 2004; Martin and McDonald 2004; Panzeri et al. 2022; Saban‐Bezalel and Mashal 2015; Song et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2006), others report comparable performance (Au‐Yeung et al. 2015; Colich et al. 2012; Garcia‐Molina 2021; Glenwright and Agbayewa 2012; Pexman et al. 2011; Saban‐Bezalel et al. 2019; Williams et al. 2013). Such discrepancies are likely attributable to methodological variability, including differences in participant characteristics, matching criteria, and task demands (Fuchs 2023; Kalandadze et al. 2018; Lampri et al. 2023).

Task verbal demands appear particularly influential. Studies reporting intact performance in ASD often use tasks with reduced verbal demands, whereas studies relying on linguistically complex requirements more consistently report group differences (Adachi et al. 2004; Diaz 2010; Kaland et al. 2002; MacKay and Shaw 2004; Martin and McDonald 2004; Panzeri et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2006). Tasks with high verbal demands typically involve extended verbal or written narratives (Adachi et al. 2004; Diaz 2010), as well as open‐ended questions requiring participants to provide explicit verbal explanations of the speaker's intended meaning (Kaland et al. 2002; Martin and McDonald 2004). In contrast, tasks with reduced verbal demands often rely on visual or multimodal stimuli (e.g., picture‐based scenarios or animations) (MacKay and Shaw 2004), and require minimal verbal output, such as yes/no judgments or forced‐choice responses (Panzeri et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2006). Taken together, these methodological differences may substantially influence performance, particularly in autistic populations, and underscore the need for assessment tools that minimize unnecessary verbal and/or metalinguistic demands on children's pragmatic processing capacities (Andreou, Lampri, et al. 2025). Consistent with this view, visual contextual support improves performance in autistic participants (Garcia‐Molina 2021).

Variability across findings may also reflect differences in the components of irony comprehension examined. Studies suggest that autistic children recognize ironic criticism more readily than ironic praise (Panzeri et al. 2022; Pexman et al. 2011), mirroring TD patterns (Hancock et al. 2000; Whalen and Pexman 2010). Moreover, belief‐level comprehension (recognizing that the literal utterance does not reflect the speaker's view) is often preserved in ASD, whereas intention‐level comprehension (inferring the communicative goal underlying irony) appears more challenging (Diaz 2010; Kaland et al. 2002; MacKay and Shaw 2004; Wang et al. 2006). This distinction is particularly relevant for the present study, which separately examines whether children recognize that the speaker does not mean the literal utterance, and whether they can explain the speaker's communicative intention. Overall, irony‐related difficulties in ASD seem more pronounced at the level of intent attribution.

An additional underexplored issue concerns the role of verbal and non‐verbal cues in irony comprehension. Context, facial expressions, gestures, and prosody are salient cues for irony detection (Burgers and van Mulken 2017; Gibbs and Colston 2001). Although contextual information is fundamental for the interpretation of ironic utterances (Deliens et al. 2018; Scholten et al. 2015), evidence in ASD suggests difficulties in using context to infer ironic intent (Glenwright and Agbayewa 2012; Wang et al. 2006). Prosodic processing difficulties in ASD are well‐documented (McCann and Peppé 2003; Paul et al. 2005), and prosody alone is often insufficient for successful irony interpretation (Deliens et al. 2018; Li et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2006). Importantly, difficulties may lie not in processing single cues but in integrating multiple signals into a coherent interpretation (Nuber et al. 2018; Scholten et al. 2015), aligning with multimodal integration deficit accounts (Vulchanova et al. 2015).

Interactions Between Irony Comprehension, Theory of Mind, Language, and Executive Functions

Although FL comprehension difficulties in ASD are well‐established (Lampri et al. 2023; Morsanyi et al. 2020; Vulchanova et al. 2015; Vulchanova and Vulchanov 2022), their underlying causes remain debated. Theory of Mind (ToM) has been identified as an important predictor of irony comprehension performance (Diaz 2010; Happé 1993; Huang et al. 2015; Saban‐Bezalel et al. 2019; Song et al. 2024). ToM involves attributing mental states to others and understanding that these may differ from one's own perspective (De Villiers and De Villiers 2014; Nguyen and Astington 2014; Premack and Woodruff 1978). Strong links have been reported between irony comprehension and second‐order ToM abilities in both autistic and TD populations (Filippova and Astington 2008; Happé 1993; Nilsen et al. 2011; Sullivan et al. 1995; Winner and Leekam 1991). However, autistic individuals may demonstrate high irony comprehension performance despite reduced ToM abilities, suggesting that irony processing does not rely exclusively on ToM (Panzeri et al. 2022).

Alternative accounts emphasize the role of structural language abilities in FL processing in ASD (Brynskov et al. 2016; Gernsbacher and Pripas‐Kapit 2012; Kalandadze et al. 2018). When autistic and TD groups are matched on language level and task demands are reduced, group differences are often attenuated (Glenwright and Agbayewa 2012; Pexman et al. 2011). Grammatical competence, lexical knowledge, and verbal IQ have also been found to contribute to irony comprehension in ASD (Kaland et al. 2002, 2005; Panzeri et al. 2022; Saban‐Bezalel and Mashal 2018; Wang et al. 2006). Nonetheless, persistent group differences despite language matching, as well as null associations between vocabulary and irony comprehension, indicate that language alone cannot fully account for irony processing difficulties (Huang et al. 2015; Saban‐Bezalel et al. 2019).

Evidence regarding the contribution of executive functions (EFs) to irony comprehension in ASD remains limited and mixed. EFs include inhibition, working memory, cognitive flexibility, planning, and problem‐solving (Andreou et al. 2022; Andreou et al. 2020; Kouklari et al. 2018). While some studies suggest that inhibition and cognitive flexibility support irony comprehension in autistic adults (Saban‐Bezalel and Mashal 2018), more recent work has failed to identify robust associations with EF components such as working memory or inhibitory control (Saban‐Bezalel et al. 2019; Song et al. 2024).

The Current Study

The existing literature suggests that irony processing difficulties in ASD are multifactorial, involving ToM, structural language abilities, and, less consistently, EFs. However, the limited number of studies examining these factors within a single design constrains understanding of the mechanisms underlying irony comprehension in ASD. Moreover, despite the high worldwide prevalence of bilingualism (Grosjean 2019; Marian and Shook 2012), research on FL processing in bilingual autistic individuals remains scarce. To date, most studies have focused on monolingual autistic populations (Kasirer et al. 2020; Panzeri et al. 2022; Saban‐Bezalel and Mashal 2015; Scholten et al. 2015), leaving the potential impact of bilingualism on FL comprehension and production largely unexplored.

Bilingualism is typically defined as the ability to use more than one language adequately in everyday contexts (Grosjean 1993). Earlier assumptions suggested that bilingualism might further compromise language development in ASD (Bird et al. 2012; Yu 2013). However, accumulating evidence indicates that bilingual autistic children often demonstrate language abilities comparable to monolingual peers across multiple linguistic domains (Gonzalez‐Barrero and Nadig 2018; Hambly and Fombonne 2012; Hoang et al. 2018; Kay‐Raining Bird et al. 2016; Lund et al. 2017; Meir and Novogrodsky 2019; Petersen et al. 2012; Uljarević et al. 2016; Valicenti‐McDermott et al. 2013). Enhanced performance has been reported in syntax, narrative skills, and ToM, with such advantages potentially linked to enhanced EF abilities (Andreou et al. 2020; Peristeri et al. 2020; Ratto et al. 2020; Romero et al. 2023; Sharaan et al. 2022).

Evidence for a bilingual advantage in FL processing in ASD remains limited. Beauchamp et al. (2023) found that balanced bilingual autistic children demonstrated better inferencing skills in narrative performance compared to monolingual peers. Andreou, Lampri, et al. (2025) similarly reported that bilingual autistic children outperformed monolingual peers in metaphor production, with this advantage being associated with higher non‐verbal intelligence. Whether such advantages extend to verbal irony comprehension remains unknown.

The present study aims to address this gap by investigating, for the first time, verbal irony comprehension in monolingual and bilingual children with ASD, addressing four research questions (RQs).

Do bilingual autistic children differ from monolingual autistic children and TD peers in their ability to comprehend verbal irony at the ironic belief recognition level, with particular emphasis on ironic criticism and ironic praise?

To what extent do language abilities (specifically, vocabulary and syntax), ToM, and EFs (specifically, working memory and inhibitory control) predict verbal irony and the speaker's intention comprehension in monolingual and bilingual autistic children?

Which cues (contextual information, facial expressions, and prosodic features) do monolingual and bilingual autistic children rely on when interpreting verbal irony?

Can error types pinpoint the sources of ironic criticism and praise comprehension difficulty in monolingual and bilingual autistic children?

Methods

Participants

The study included 31 TD monolingual Greek‐speaking children (17 boys and 14 girls; mean age = 8 years; 9 months, SD = 0.6), and 15 TD bilingual children (8 boys and 7 girls; mean age = 8 years; 9 months, SD = 0.7) all of whom had Greek as one of their two languages; 30 monolingual Greek‐speaking autistic children (25 boys and 5 girls; mean age = 9 years; 6 months, SD = 1.8); and 15 bilingual autistic children (9 boys and 6 girls; mean age = 9 years; 3 months, SD = 1.8). A two‐by‐two mixed design analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Diagnosis (ASD, TD) and Language experience (bilinguals, monolinguals) as the between‐subjects factors, and chronological age as the dependent measure was run. The main effects of Diagnosis, F(1, 87) = 2.191, p = 0.142, η 2 = 0.01, and Language experience, F(1, 87) = 0.005, p = 0.944, η 2 = 0.01, were not significant. The interaction between Diagnosis and Language experience was not significant either, F(1, 87) = 0.061, p = 0.805, η 2 = 0.01.

Participants were recruited from the Peloponnese region in northern Greece through the Centers for Interdisciplinary Evaluation, Counseling, and Support (KEDASY), official state agencies in Greece responsible for assessing and supporting the educational needs of children and issuing formal diagnostic reports. Although a standardized measure of ASD severity was not available, all autistic participants had received a formal clinical diagnosis from a child psychiatrist or developmental specialist according to DSM‐5 (American Psychiatric Association 2013) and ICD‐10 (WHO 1993) criteria, and no significant group differences were observed in key background variables, such as age and non‐verbal intelligence. Importantly, all autistic participants attended public schools' mainstream classrooms, thus indicating a relatively high level of functional adaptation to educational settings, consistent with placement in mainstream education and the absence of intellectual disability. In addition, all children met an additional eligibility criterion of functional spoken language, ensuring that they were able to produce a range of sentence types, refer to events beyond the immediate context, and understand the experimental instructions.

Α bilingualism questionnaire (Andreou 2021) was administered to bilingual autistic children. In addition to Greek, bilingual children spoke French, Spanish, Arabic, Albanian, Russian, Polish, Romanian, Ukrainian, or English. Among the 15 bilingual autistic children, 4 were balanced bilinguals and 11 Greek‐dominant; among the 15 bilingual TD children, 5 were balanced and 10 Greek‐dominant, based on parental reports. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of the Peloponnese (IRB protocol no. 18734/19.9.2023).

Baseline Tasks

In addition to the main experimental task, the study included a series of baseline tasks assessing children's language and cognitive abilities, allowing us to control for general factors that could influence performance. Specifically, non‐verbal intelligence was assessed using Raven's Colored Progressive Matrices (Raven 1995), a widely used measure of non‐verbal IQ. Language abilities were assessed using the Greek Expressive Vocabulary Test (Vogindroukas et al. 2009; Greek adaptation of Renfrew and Mitchell 1997) and the Syntactic Proficiency Test (Andreou, Lampri, et al. 2025; Andreou, Antoniou, and Peristeri 2025). The Greek Expressive Vocabulary Test (Vogindroukas et al. 2009), which consists of 50 black‐and‐white pictures depicting common objects of increasing difficulty, was used to assess children's expressive vocabulary. Children were asked to name each item, and one point was awarded for each correct response. Administration was discontinued after five consecutive errors. Syntactic abilities were assessed using the Syntactic Proficiency Test (Andreou, Antoniou, et al. 2025, Andreou, Antoniou, and Peristeri 2025), a sentence–picture matching task comprising 28 items that examine comprehension of six syntactic constructions, namely passives, clitic constructions, relative clauses, spatial prepositions, complement clauses, and quantifiers. One point was awarded per correct response, yielding a maximum possible score of 28. Theory of Mind skills were assessed using a task comprising a verbal and a non‐verbal version, following the paradigm developed by Forgeot d'Arc and Ramus (2011). Each version included 10 short video scenarios (plus two practice trials) depicting five first‐order and five second‐order false‐belief situations, adapted from classic paradigms such as the Smarties task (Wimmer and Perner 1983) and the Sally–Ann task (Baron‐Cohen et al. 1985). In the non‐verbal version, videos were presented without narration, and children indicated their response by pointing to one of two thought bubbles representing alternative belief states (maximum score = 10). In the verbal version, a narrator described each scenario using simple language, and children answered verbally‐administered questions (e.g., “What will Anna do now?”; “What does Maria think Anna will do now?”) targeting first‐ and second‐order belief reasoning (maximum score = 10; see Marinis et al. 2023). Finally, EF measurements included three tasks assessing working memory and executive control. Specifically, verbal working memory was evaluated using the Backward Digit Recall subtest from the Automated Working Memory Assessment (Alloway 2007), in which children were required to repeat sequences of spoken digits in reverse order. Performance was indexed by the total number of correctly recalled sequences (maximum score = 36). Inhibitory control and attentional flexibility were assessed using the Stroop and Flanker tasks, respectively. In the Stroop task (Stroop 1935), children were instructed to name the ink color of printed color words while inhibiting the semantic content of the word; both accuracy and reaction times were recorded. In the Flanker task (Eriksen and Schultz 1979; Mueller and Piper 2014; PEBL), children judged the direction of a centrally presented target arrow flanked by congruent or incongruent arrows, requiring the suppression of irrelevant information. Accuracy and reaction times on incongruent trials were used as indices of executive functioning.

Irony Comprehension Task

Given the lack of a standardized Greek assessment tool for irony comprehension, a computer‐mediated, pictorial irony comprehension task with relatively reduced expressive verbal and metalinguistic demands was developed for the purposes of the present study, drawing upon previous work on irony processing in ASD (Colich et al. 2012; Saban‐Bezalel et al. 2019). The task was designed to assess the comprehension of two main content‐based subtypes of verbal irony, namely, ironic criticism, and ironic praise. The task comprised 30 pictorial scenarios accompanied by short dialogs (four scenarios serving as practice items and 26 scenarios as main trials). Each dialog could be interpreted either literally or ironically, resulting in nine ironic criticism items, nine ironic praise items, four literal criticism items, and four literal praise items. Scenarios were presented to the participants in a pseudo‐randomized order, with the constraint that no more than three consecutive ironic items of the same type (i.e., criticism and praise) could appear. This strategy was implemented to ensure a balanced distribution between ironic and literal trials, and to minimize the risk of participants becoming overly familiar with either condition. Task administration comprised the following three phases.

Phase 1

Participants viewed each picture while listening to a pre‐recorded dialog, and were subsequently asked a comprehension question targeting the speaker's belief (e.g., “Does the X mean what he/she said?”). Responses were binary (yes/no), thereby minimizing expressive verbal demands, with participants being instructed to respond “yes” if they believed that the speaker meant what he/she said, and “no” if they believed that the speaker did not mean what he/she said. Each correct response regarding the speaker's belief was awarded 1 point, whereas each incorrect response received 0 points. Examples of trials in Phase 1 across the four types (ironic criticism, ironic praise, literal criticism, literal praise) are displayed in Figure 1a–d.

Examples of pictures across the (a) ironic criticism, (b) ironic praise, (c) literal criticism, and (d) literal praise trials of the irony comprehension task.

Phase 2

After providing a response on whether the speaker in the scenario meant what s/he said, participants were next asked to justify their response by referring to the contextual, facial, or prosodic cues that guided their interpretation of the speaker's belief, thereby allowing them to rely on multimodal information rather than solely on extended verbal explanations. All scenarios provided visual contextual information (e.g., the golden medal verifying the pride of the child in Figure 1b), facial expressions, that is, the speaker's face denoted an emotion that was in opposition to the emotion sparked in case of a literal meaning (e.g., the sad facial expression of the boy saying that “he feels wonderful” in Figure 1a), and prosodic cues (i.e., sincere vs. ironic intonation), such as slower speech rate (Rivière et al. 2018; Deliens et al. 2017) and higher intensity/loudness (Anolli et al. 2002; Deliens et al. 2018) usually characterizing ironic (vs. sincere) intonation. Selected examples from the responses of monolingual and bilingual autistic children are presented for illustrative purposes. The use of the three cue‐types (i.e., contextual, facial, and prosodic) was rated separately for children's correct and incorrect responses to the ironic scenarios, thus allowing us to identify the specific sources of information children resorted to when inferring the ironic speaker's belief successfully or unsuccessfully.

Phase 3

We should note that this phase only applied to the autistic (monolingual and bilingual) children of the study, and only to the accurate irony belief comprehension answers, thereby limiting the more demanding verbal component of the task to a subset of trials and participants. In the ironic criticism and praise trials, in which autistic participants accurately indicated that the speaker did not mean what was said, they were next asked an additional “why” question (e.g., “Why did the boy/girl say that?”) targeting the speaker's communicative intention. Given that the ironic speaker's communicative intention across both criticism and praise trials was to convey the opposite meaning of the literal utterance, while expressing at the same time either a critical or a humorous attitude, autistic participants' responses to the “why” question were expected to reflect recognition of the ironic intent. Specifically, autistic children's responses in the ironic trials of the current task were assumed to reflect irony comprehension when they either explicitly referred to irony (e.g., “he/she was being ironic”), stated that the speaker meant the opposite of what s/he said, or provided an appropriate reference to the speaker's attitude (e.g., “he/she said it as a joke/for fun”). More particularly, autistic children's responses to the “why” question were coded into three categories: (a) appropriate responses (i.e., children identified the speaker's ironic intention); (b) no responses (i.e., inability to provide an explanation); and (c) inappropriate responses (i.e., explanations that were off‐topic, or reflected a misunderstanding of irony). An example of an inappropriate response to an ironic criticism trial is the following. A boy and a girl are talking in a garden when the boy introduces his dog, which appears wild and aggressive. The frightened girl exclaims, “What a sweet dog!” When the autistic child participant was asked whether the girl meant what she said, the child correctly answered “no,” justifying her response by noting that “it is scary and it's not on a leash.” However, when further asked why the girl in the scenario said this without meaning it, the autistic child responded that “the girl got confused.” In this case, the autistic child's justification answer was classified as being inappropriate, since the ironic utterance has been interpreted as an error or a misunderstanding, rather than as an intentional use of irony.

Analysis Plan

We first provide descriptive values of the four groups' (monolingual and bilingual autistic children, monolingual and bilingual TD children) performance means in the study's baseline tests (Raven, vocabulary, sentence comprehension, verbal and non‐verbal ToM, DB, Flanker and Stroop). A series of two‐by‐two mixed design analyses of variance (ANOVAs) with Diagnosis (ASD, TD) and Language experience (bilinguals, monolinguals) as the between‐subjects factors, and performance scores in Raven, vocabulary, sentence comprehension, DB, Flanker and Stroop as the dependent measures were run. For the ToM tasks, a repeated measures ANOVA model was used to compare performance differences, using Diagnosis (ASD, TD) and Language experience (bilinguals, monolinguals) as the between‐subjects factors, and Task‐type (verbal, non‐verbal) and ToM‐order (first, second) as the within‐subjects factors. Results are reported separately for each baseline task.

To address RQ1 and RQ2, that is, whether bilingual autistic children would differ from monolingual autistic children and TD peers in their ability to comprehend ironic Criticism and ironic Praise, and whether language and/or cognitive factors would affect groups' irony comprehension performance, we ran generalized linear mixed effects models. Accuracy proportions were entered as the dependent variable, and item and participants were entered as random intercepts. Accuracy proportions were calculated separately for ironic criticism and ironic praise by dividing the number of correct responses by the total number of trials for each irony type. We first created a unified “base” model including Diagnosis (ASD, TD), Language experience (bilinguals, monolinguals), Irony Type (Criticism, Praise), and all associated interactions, allowing direct comparisons between irony types within a single analytical framework. We next added each individual screening measure separately, and examined if each newly added variable improved the model's goodness‐of‐fit. The fit of all competing models to the data was tested simultaneously using established goodness‐of‐fit criteria, such as the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayesian In‐formation Criterion (BIC), and Log‐likelihood. In general, lower values of AIC, BIC and Log‐likelihood indicate a better model fit to the data (Kuha 2004). We retained the variables that significantly contributed to the model fit. All models converged successfully. Inspection of model diagnostics revealed no singular fits and no evidence of model instability. The variables added were: Raven's IQ, Vocabulary, Sentence comprehension, first‐ and second order verbal and non‐verbal ToM, DB, Flanker and Stroop measures. Potential multicollinearities between the covariates were assessed by inspecting variance inflation factors (VIFs). All VIF values were below 3, indicating no problematic multicollinearity.

Next, to investigate ironic intention understanding in the autistic children only, we applied a similar approach as described above. The “base” model included Language experience (bilinguals, monolinguals) and Irony type (Criticism, Praise) as the fixed effects, and intention understanding proportions as the dependent variable. Intention‐understanding proportions were calculated separately for ironic criticism and ironic praise by dividing the number of appropriate responses to the intention question (“why”) by the number of trials in which the participant had correctly identified that the speaker did not mean the literal utterance in Phase 1. Thus, intention‐understanding performance was conditional upon successful irony belief recognition. All other fixed effects (i.e., Raven's IQ, Vocabulary, Sentence comprehension, first‐ and second order verbal and non‐verbal ToM, DB, Flanker, Stroop) were added in the same stepwise approach as mentioned earlier.

To address RQ3, that is, pinpoint the cues (context, facial expressions, prosody) that monolingual and bilingual autistic children relied on to interpret irony, we first calculated cue‐use proportions separately for each cue type (context, facial expressions, prosody) and irony condition (Criticism, Praise). Specifically, for each participant, the number of correctly interpreted ironic trials in which a given cue type was explicitly mentioned by the child was divided by the total number of correctly interpreted trials of the corresponding irony condition. Because participants could refer to more than one cue type within the same trial, cue categories were not mutually exclusive and proportions were calculated independently for each cue type. Due to the moderate‐to‐high skewness of the data (see Section 4), we used nonlinear extensions of Fisher Discriminant Analysis, which allowed us to determine the best group‐separation function operationalized in terms of the cue type(s) used in each group (monolingual autistic, bilingual autistic). Specifically, these analyses were intended to identify which cue type(s) contributed most strongly to group separation within the autistic sample. We generated two discriminant analysis models, one for each irony type (Criticism, Praise). Each model comprised only the cue(s) that significantly improved model fit (stepwise procedure). The stepwise approach allowed us to identify the cue(s) that most strongly teased apart monolingual from bilingual autistic children within each irony condition.

Finally, to address RQ4, that is, examine whether error types (inappropriate responses, No responses) could identify the sources of irony comprehension difficulty in monolingual and bilingual autistic children, we first calculated error proportions separately for each error type and irony type (Criticism, Praise). Specifically, the number of responses belonging to a given error category was divided by the total number of intention‐understanding trials that did not receive an appropriate response in the corresponding irony condition. Thus, the resulting proportions reflected the relative distribution of error types among all unsuccessful intention interpretations. After confirming that the proportions were not skewed, we ran a mixed‐design analysis of variance with Language Experience (monolingual autistic, bilingual autistic) as the between‐subjects factor, and Irony type (Criticism, Praise) and Error type (inappropriate answer, No answer) as the within‐subjects factors, and error proportions as the dependent measure.

Results

Baseline Tasks

Discussion

The present study contributes to the growing literature on bilingualism effects on pragmatic language skills in ASD by examining verbal irony comprehension using a task of reduced verbal and metalinguistic demands. Importantly, this is, to our knowledge, the first study to directly investigate verbal irony comprehension in both monolingual and bilingual autistic children. By jointly considering irony‐type including criticism and praise, the mechanisms underlying irony comprehension such as language, ToM, and EFs, the intention behind the speaker's ironic meaning, the types of cues that autistic children used to comprehend ironic utterances and the error‐types in autistic children's responses in the irony comprehension task, the findings provide evidence that irony comprehension is not negatively affected by dual language experience in autistic children. Specifically, we found that EFs, and more specifically inhibition, proved important in enhancing ironic belief recognition across children, while understanding ironic intentions strongly relied on verbal ToM skills, and to a lesser extent on syntax, in the autistic children. Furthermore, facial expression cues were more strongly associated with bilingualism in autism in the ironic praise trials, suggesting that bilingual autistic children showed greater reliance on this source of information than their monolingual autistic peers. Finally, no differences were observed between the two groups in the type of inaccurate answers they provided (i.e., inappropriate answers, no answers).

More specifically, the first aim of the study was to determine whether bilingual autistic children would differ from their monolingual autistic (and TD) peers in their ability to comprehend verbal irony across criticism and praise. We found that bilingual autistic children did not perform worse than their monolingual bilingual peers. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that bilingualism does not negatively affect language or pragmatic development in autistic populations (Andreou, Antoniou, et al. 2026; Andreou et al. 2020; Park 2014; Peristeri et al. 2024). At the same time, it extends this line of research by demonstrating that bilingual autistic children do not lag behind their monolingual peers in a demanding pragmatic domain such as irony comprehension. Importantly, the comparability of the two groups in irony comprehension emerged despite bilingual children showing lower performance in sentence comprehension, further implying that successful irony processing may not rely exclusively on structural language abilities. Instead, bilingual autistic children may perform on a par with monolingual autistic children in less demanding versions of pragmatic language phenomena, consistent with evidence that reduced verbal demands can support irony comprehension in ASD (Colich et al. 2012; Pexman et al. 2011), possibly because bilinguals may draw more effectively than monolinguals on contextual and pragmatic cues (Brojde et al. 2012; Yow and Markman 2011), such as facial expressions in the current study, as will be explained in more detail below.

On the other hand, ironic praise emerged as the only condition negatively affected by autism, likely due to increased processing demands. This pattern is consistent with previous findings suggesting that ironic praise may place greater inferential demands than ironic criticism (Hancock et al. 2000; Panzeri et al. 2022). One possible explanation for this pattern is that ironic praise requires the reinterpretation of a negative utterance within a positive context. Such sophisticated pragmatic inferencing involved in ironic praise trials may be particularly difficult for populations such as autistic children, who have been reported to experience weaknesses in inferential abilities (Pennisi 2016; Peristeri et al. 2025).

The second aim of the study was to identify the language and/or cognitive abilities supporting intention detection in autistic children. The results showed that intention understanding was primarily associated with verbal second order ToM abilities as well as, to a lesser extent, syntax. Specifically, in ironic comprehension trials, higher verbal second order ToM performance predicted more accurate intention understanding across both autistic groups. High syntactic abilities, as reflected in children's better sentence comprehension scores, were also positively associated with high performance in intention understanding. These findings suggest that intention detection in autistic children relies on the integration of both socio‐cognitive and structural language mechanisms, with ToM playing a central role, and language resources supporting performance under increased inferential demands. This pattern is broadly consistent with previous research highlighting the importance of advanced ToM abilities, particularly second‐order reasoning, in irony comprehension (Happé 1993; Saban‐Bezalel et al. 2019; Song et al. 2024), while also aligning with research showing that language contributes to irony comprehension performance, albeit inconsistently, particularly in autistic populations (Saban‐Bezalel and Mashal 2018). The results further indicate that intention detection in ASD is not supported by a single cognitive domain, but rather reflects the dynamic interaction between ToM and language abilities.

The third aim of the study has examined the cues that monolingual and bilingual autistic children used in irony comprehension. Both contextual and facial expression cues synthesized the repertoire of bilingual and monolingual autistic children's strategies to cope with ironic meanings in the task. Notably, according to the discriminant analysis results, the cue that effectively discriminated between the two groups was facial expressions, with bilingual children using them considerably more frequently than their monolingual autistic peers when answering questions targeting the belief or the intention of the ironic speaker in the task's ironic praise trials. The specific finding offers further support to previous evidence about a bilingualism advantage in the integration of pragmatic referential cues (Brojde et al. 2012; Yow and Markman 2011), also suggesting that this advantage has emerged under the high cognitive demands posed by praise trials. Interestingly, prosodic cues were rarely used across both autistic groups. The low use of prosody may relate to prosodic perception difficulties in ASD, especially when prosody serves pragmatic/affective functions (Hubbard et al. 2017; Paul et al. 2005).

The final aim of this research was to gain insight into bilingual and monolingual autistic children's irony processing strategies through the examination of their error types in the irony comprehension task. No differences have emerged between monolingual and bilingual autistic children in error types, as the proportions of inappropriate responses and “no responses” were comparable across the two groups. This convergence suggests that both groups faced similar underlying difficulties in constructing the speaker's intended meaning, pointing to a common socio‐cognitive constraint rather than qualitatively distinct processing profiles.

Overall, the results show that bilingualism did not negatively affect irony comprehension in ASD. On the contrary, bilingual autistic children did not differ from their TD bilingual peers in irony comprehension, in contrast to the monolingual autistic children who scored significantly lower than their TD peers. Bilingual autistic children also prioritized facial expression cues more efficiently than monolingual autistic peers, although both groups showed marked difficulty using prosody. The findings further underscore the importance of EFs, especially inhibition, in enhancing irony comprehension in ASD, as well as the beneficiary effect of higher‐level socio‐cognitive skills, such as second order ToM in intention understanding in autism.

Limitations and Future Directions

A limitation of the present study concerns the use of cartoon‐based stimuli, which, although allowing for increased experimental control and enhanced child engagement, may not have fully captured the complexity and subtlety of real‐life social interactions. In particular, facial expressions and prosodic cues in cartoon‐based stimuli may be more salient or less nuanced than in naturalistic communication, potentially facilitating performance. As a result, the generalizability of the findings to everyday communicative contexts may be limited. A further limitation concerns the lack of a standardized measure of ASD severity in Greek, which has limited the ability to examine how individual differences in the autistic children's clinical profiles may have related to their performance. In addition, the intention‐level measure was administered only to autistic participants and only following correct belief‐level responses, which may have limited direct comparability across groups. Future research should extend the present paradigm by employing more ecologically valid stimuli, such as videos featuring human actors, in order to examine irony comprehension under conditions that more closely approximate real‐life social interactions.
