Elucidating the toxicity of the plasticizer DPhP: Evidence of neurotoxicity and developmental impairment from in vitro to in vivo models
Authors: Lipeng Zhu, Zhipeng Xie, Xinyang Zhang, Lingfang Yang, Shanni Li, Hang Fai Kwok, Junnan Li, Hui Zou, Siyuan Luo, Zanxian Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As an emerging substitute for conventional phthalates, diphenyl phthalate (DPhP) raises growing environmental health concerns, yet its systemic toxicity profile remains poorly characterized. We used human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) and Caenorhabditis elegans models to study the toxic effects and the underlying mechanisms of DPhP exposure. The results demonstrate that DPhP significantly suppresses cell viability and proliferation. Mechanistically, DPhP induces G1/S phase cell cycle alterations by upregulating cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (p21 and p27) and downregulating CDK-cyclin complexes, consistent with a stress-induced delay in cell cycle progression. In C. elegans, DPhP exposure impairs growth and locomotor behavior. Molecular analyses reveal that these adverse outcomes are associated with the dysregulation of key signaling pathways, including IIS, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK. Notably, DPhP disrupts the expression of neurodevelopmental genes, particularly those governing dopaminergic and cholinergic neurotransmission. Consistent with this finding, DPhP-exposed nematodes exhibit deficits in chemotaxis and learning, accompanied by reduced dopamine and GABA levels, and a loss of dopaminergic neurons, indicating substantial neurotoxicity. Collectively, this study provides comprehensive evidence that DPhP disrupts cellular growth, development, and neural function across models, highlighting its underappreciated health risks and offering crucial insights for environmental safety assessment

Introduction

Environmental pollution poses a global challenge with profound implications for ecosystems and human health. Phthalates, or phthalate esters (PAEs), are a class of chemicals that have been widely used for nearly a century and are increasingly scrutinized due to their extensive application and associated health risks (Wang and Qian, 2021). PAE-containing products are widespread in consumer products and the environment in everyday life. For instance, phthalates are commonly added to toys to enhance plastic flexibility, improving tactile properties for children (Latini, 2005; Yen et al., 2011). Childcare items such as teething rings and soft-sided baby products also frequently contain PAEs (Babich et al., 2020; Odebeatu et al., 2019). In addition, phthalates are present in food packaging materials, where they may leach into food upon heating, and in building materials like vinyl flooring, contributing to indoor environmental exposure (Giuliani et al., 2020). Although the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not banned phthalates, it has issued guidance discouraging the use of certain types, such as dibutyl phthalate (DBP) and di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP), in pharmaceuticals and medical devices (Hubinger, 2010; Mair and Mair, 2009; Traynor, 2002). Diphenyl phthalate (DPhP), a specific type of PAE, is primarily employed as a plasticizer in the production of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and food packaging materials (Kim et al., 2008). PVC is extensively used across consumer and industrial products due to its enhanced flexibility, durability, and workability imparted by plasticizers like DPhP (Bowman and Choudhury, 2016; Hlisníková et al., 2020). However, DPhP can be released into the environment from PVC matrices (Münch et al., 2018; Nagorka et al., 2025). It has been detected in surface waters, including lakes and rivers, as well as in agricultural soils, raising concerns about exposure risks to both humans and aquatic organisms (Baloyi et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2021; Nagorka et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2016).

While, most toxicological studies have focused on DEHP, diisobutyl phthalate (DiBP), and diisononyl phthalate (DiNP) (Zhang et al., 2021a; Zhang et al., 2021b), the toxicity of DPhP remains relatively underexplored. Preliminary in vitro studies suggest that DPhP may disrupt metabolic processes, which could in turn lead to endocrine-disrupting effects, or the two processes may occur in parallel; however, direct evidence of endocrine-disrupting effects leading to adverse outcomes is currently lacking (Faramazinia et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2016; Lv et al., 2022). Moreover, DPhP functions as an environmental hormone with estrogenic activity. In male goldfish, coexposure to microplastics and DPhP decreased testosterone and increased estradiol levels (Faramazinia et al., 2025). Based on these hormonal changes, the authors speculated that DPhP could potentially lead to decreased sperm count and altered reproductive behavior. Parallel effects on female sexual development and reproductive disorders have also been hypothesized, though direct evidence remains lacking (Faramazinia et al., 2025; Lv et al., 2022; Scippo et al., 2004). Despite these concerns, comprehensive data on the toxicological effects and mechanisms of DPhP remain scarce. Although DPhP has been shown to be readily biodegradable under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions (Kim et al., 2008; Yuan et al., 2002) (Kim et al., 2008; Yuan et al., 2002), its continuous release into the environment may result in pseudo-persistence, and its potential for bioaccumulation remains a concern. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate its post-exposure toxicity and inform risk assessments of plasticizer compounds.

Therefore, in this study, we applied Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and Caenorhabditis elegans to examine the toxicity and underlying molecular mechanisms of DPhP in vitro and in vivo. HUVECs were selected as a well-established in vitro model for vascular endothelial toxicity, given the potential of environmental endocrine disruptors to affect cardiovascular systems. C. elegans was chosen as an in vivo model due to its high conservation of stress response pathways, and suitability for rapid assessment of reproductive and developmental toxicity, which are relevant endpoints for DPhP exposure.This research aims to enhance our understanding of DPhP toxicity, support the development of safety guidelines for occupational exposure, and inform regulatory decisions to mitigate environmental and public health risks.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and maintenance

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were routinely cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and a combination of penicillin and streptomycin to prevent bacterial contamination. The culture conditions were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. The cell culture medium was replaced every two to three days to ensure optimal growth conditions (Qiu et al., 2025).

DPhP preparation

DPhP was purchased from MERCK KGaA (Cat:105880). For cell experiments, DPhP was dissolved in DMSO and subsequently diluted with PBS to achieve the desired concentrations (0–10 g/L), ensuring that the final DMSO concentration remained below 0.1%. For in vivo work, K medium (51 mM NaCI, 32 mM KCI) was applied to dilute for different concentration as the final concentration of DMSO was also lower than 0.1%.

MTT assay

The cell viability was determined by MTT method (Zhang et al., 2021b). The MTT assay was conducted to assess cell viability by seeding 5000 cells per well in a 96-well plate, allowing them to adhere for 24 h before treating with various concentrations of DPhP for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, MTT was added to a final concentration of 5 mg/mL and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. After discarding the supernatant, DMSO was added to solubilize the formazan crystals, and absorbance was read at 570 nm to determine cell viability.

Colony formation assay

The cell growth was determined by colony formation assay (Cadoná et al., 2015). Approximately 200 cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and allowed to attach for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with various concentrations of DPhP (0-1 g/L). After 7 days, the cell cultures were washed three times with PBS to remove any non-adherent cells. The adherent cells were then fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution and stained with crystal violet (1%). Images of the stained colonies were captured for observation. Only colonies with a minimum of 5 cells and clearly visible staining (indicating viable/positive colonies) were counted.

Assessment of cell cycle via flow cytometry

The cell cycle was determined using flow cytometry (Hu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025). Cell cycle progression was evaluated using flow cytometry by inoculating 4 × 10^5 cells into a 6-well plate, allowing them to adhere for 24 h before treating with DPhP. After an additional 24-h incubation, cells were harvested, fixed with 70% ice-cold ethanol, and washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, cells were treated with RNase A at 37 °C for 30 min and washed again with PBS three times. Following this, cells were stained with Propidium Iodide (PI) (100 μg/mL) dye for 15 min and analyzed using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX instrument. Data were processed and analyzed with FlowJo software (10.6.2 version) to determine the cell cycle phases.

Western blotting analysis

The protein expression was determined using western blotting method (Li et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2021b). For Western blotting, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer following treatment with DPhP to extract proteins. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE (8% ∼ 12%) and then transferred to a nitrocellulose or PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies specific to the target proteins of interest, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein expression was visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system, and the signals were analyzed to quantify the expression levels of each protein. The details of antibodies are in the supplementary table 1.

Maintenance of C. elegans

The N2 wild type C. elegans, BZ555 (egIs1[Pdat-1::gfp]) and Escherichia coli OP50 were obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC). The worms were kept on the nematode growth medium (NGM) and the OP50 was used as the food resource. The plate was put at 20 °C for culture. The synchronization was conducted using bleach method (Porta-de-la-Riva et al., 2012).

Determination of LD50 of C. elegans

The synchronized worms L1 stage were exposed to different concentrations of DPhP for 24, 48 and 72 h in K medium. When no pharyngeal pumping was observed, the worm was recorded as death.

Determination of the body length

Following 48-h DPhP exposure, nematodes were collected and washed with K medium to remove residual contaminants. Then the worms were subsequently immobilized using NaN₃ (40 mM) anesthesia (dissolved in K medium) and mounted on glass slides for observation using a microscope (Phelps et al., 2025). The body length was measured through ImageJ software (1.46r version).

Determination of the body movements

Synchronized L1 larvae were exposed to DPhP in liquid culture for 48 h at 20 °C. Then nematodes were transferred to fresh liquid media in 24-well plate (5–8 worms per well) and the movement activity was quantified under living conditions by counting body bends per 20-s under a microscope (Wu et al., 2024).

NaCl chemotaxis assay

After 48 h exposure to DPhP from L1 stage in K medium, the worms were placed on a fresh NGM plate to recover for 1 h. Then the worms were quickly transferred on the middle of a new NGM plate with NaCl (100 mM) and water drop on the both ends of a 6 cm plate. After 1 h incubation, the number of worms on the both ends were recorded and the chemotaxis index was calculated according to the previous research (Yang et al., 2020).

Learning index assay

The learning index assay was performed according to the previous report with some modification. After exposure to DPhP for 48 h in K medium, the worms were placed on a fresh NGM plate to recover for 1 h. Then the worms were picked on a new NGM plate without food for 1 h. The worms were quickly transferred to diacetyl solution (10%) for 30 min learning. Then the worms were picked on a new NGM plate for 30 min starvation. This process was repeated 3 times. After the final starving process, the worms were washed using K medium and placed on the middle of a new NGM plate with ethanol (95%, 1 μL) and diacetyl (0.1%, 1 μL) two drops on the both sides, respectively. After 30 min, the number of worms on both sides was recorded and the learning index was calculated according to the previous research (Chen et al., 2024).

Determination of the ROS level

L1 larva was exposed to DPhP for 48 h in K medium. Then the DCFH-DA (2′,7’-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate) kit was used to dye the reactive oxygen species (ROS) in C. elegans for 1 h in the dark (Wu et al., 2024). Then the worms were washed 7 times using K medium to remove the dying solution. Then the worms containing K medium were transferred into a 96-well plate (20–25 worms per well) for detecting the fluorescence intensity using a micro-reader (SpectraMax M5). It should be noted that DCFH-DA is an indirect, non-specific probe, and the resulting fluorescence is best interpreted as indicative of a pro-oxidative shift rather than definitive oxidative stress.

Brilliant blue stain assay

The intestinal intergrity of C. elegans was stained using brilliant blue (Sandner et al., 2023). L1 worms were exposed to DPhP for 48 h in K medium. Then the worms were transferred into a tube containing brilliant blue solutions (0.1%) mixed with OP50 for 30 min. Then the worms were washed 9 times using K medium to remove the dying solution. Finally, the worms were anesthetized for being observed under a microscope.

Determination of gene expression

L1 worms were exposed to DPhP for 48 h in K medium. Then the worms were collected, washed using K medium for 7 timesand frozen instantly in liquid nitrogen using 1.5 mL tube. Then the worms were ground using glass grinding rod and the RNA was extracted using the RNAeasy™ Animal RNA Isolation Kit with Spin Column (Beyotime, Cat: R0027). The RNA was reversed to cDNA using iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-rad, Cat: 1708891) and the gene expression was detected using SYBR method (Bio-rad, Cat: 1725121) (Luo et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2024). The primers were listed in the supplementary table 2.

Determination of the neurotransmitters content

To quantitatively detect the content of neurotransmitters in C. elegans, a commercial ELISA kit was adopted. DPhP was applied to treat L1 worms for 48 h in K medium. Then the worms were washed using K medium for 7 times and collected instantly frozen in liquid nitrogen. The worms were groundand the neurotransmitters content was determined using ELISA kit (WUHAN JILID BIOTECHNOLOGY CO., LTD) (Chen et al., 2021).

Visualization of dopamine neuronal cells

To observe the neuronal cells in C. elegans, BZ555 stain was applied to visualized the cells under a flouroscence microscope. Synchronized L1 BZ555 worms were exposed to DPhP for 48 h in K medium. Then the worms were collected and washed with K medium for 4–6 times. The worms were anesthetized in NaN₃ (40 mM, dissolved in K medium) for 3 min and then observed under a microscope using fluorescence filter (Olympus BX53).

Statistic analysis

GraphPad Prism (8.0.2 version, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analyses. The statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA with multiple comparison tests. All data were expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3, biological replicates).

Results

DPhP attenuates cell viability in HUVEC cells

HUVEC cells were used to assess the cytotoxicity of DPhP on endothelial cells. The MTT assay revealed a decline in cellular viability in HUVECs exposed to DPhP for 24 h, with the reduction in viability being dose-dependent when compared to the control group (Fig. 1A). The cell viability was reduced to 83.51% at 1 g/L (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, we examined the impact of DPHP on the colony-forming capacity of HUVECs (Fig. 1B). The results showed that at a concentration of 0.1 g/L, DPHP significantly hindered colony development from 80.67 to 68.67 colony, and at 1 g/L, the number of cell colony was reduced to 61 (Fig. 1B). Following the cell cycle analysis, we employed Western blotting to determine if DPhP modulates the expression of key proteins governing cell cycle regulation. The cell cycle process is known to be regulated by cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKIs) such as p21 and p27, which in turn modulate the activity of CDKs to affect cell cycle progression (Yoon et al., 2012). Following treatment with DPhP, we observed a significant upregulation of p21 and p27 protein levels, coupled with a marked downregulation of CDK2 expression, which are key regulators of the G1 and S phases of the cell cycle (Fig. 2E) (Lauper et al., 1998). These findings suggest that DPhP could induce cell cycle alterations through the enhancement of CDKIs activity and the suppression of CDKs. At lower concentrations of DPhP, proteins associated with the S phase, such as cyclin A2 (Blanchard, 2000), were downregulated. In contrast, at higher concentrations, proteins related to the G1 phase, including cyclin D3 and cyclin E1 (Gao et al., 2018), were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2E). Additionally, previous studies have implicated FOXO3a in the induction of G1 phase arrest (Li et al., 2022). In line with this, we found that the expression of FOXO3a was also significantly upregulated following treatment with high concentrations of DPhP (Fig. 2E). Thus, these results indicate that DPhP can exert its effects on cell cycle regulation by modulating the expression of key proteins at different phases of the cell cycle, leading to cell cycle alterations.

DPhP exposure led to acute toxicity to C. elegans

Acute toxicity of DPhP was assessed by exposing nematodes to increasing concentrations and measuring survival at different time points. Treatment with 10 g/L DPhP resulted in complete lethality (0% survival) within 24 h, while 5 g/L exposure reduced survival rates to 4.76% during the same period (Fig. 3A). Notably, concentrations ≤1 g/L showed no acute toxicity within 24 h. Concomitant behavioral and developmental assessments revealed sublethal toxicity thresholds. Locomotor capacity, quantified through body bend frequency, demonstrated significant impairment at 0.5–1 g/L, whereas 0.1 g/L exposure caused no observable motility defects (Fig. 3B). Developmental analysis further identified growth retardation across all tested sublethal concentrations (0.1–1 g/L). Body length was reduced by 10.61% at 0.1 g/L and by 58.16% at 1 g/L relative to untreated controls (Fig. 3C) Morphometric validation through high-resolution imaging (Fig. 3D-G) confirmed dose-dependent growth inhibition. DPhP exposure led to the dysregulation of gene expression

DPhP had a significant effect on gene expression in C. elegans, and there were differences in gene expression changes at low and high concentrations. At low concentration (0.1 g/L), DPhP exposure significantly suppressed the insulin/IGF-1 signaling (IIS) pathway through coordinated downregulation of core components including daf-2, sgk-1, akt-1, akt-2, and pdk-1 (Fig. 4B). The expression of jnk-1 and hsp-16.2 was upregulated (Fig. 4C). In contrast, high-concentration exposure (0.5–1 g/L) elicited activation of IIS pathway genes (daf-2, age-1, akt-1, pdk-1, akt-2), accompanied by significant upregulation of hsf-1, skn-1, and sir-2.1, along with antioxidant enzymes (gst-4, sod-3) and immune response mediators (pmk-1) (Fig. 4C) (Shivers et al., 2010). Notably, the observed downregulation of jnk-1 and hsp-16.2 at high doses may indicate collapse of stress response capacity (Neumann-Haefelin et al., 2008). In addition, after DPhP exposure, the expression of clk-2, nsy-1, sek-1, ctl-1, and hsp-16.2 increased, indicating that DNA damage, immune response, and protein stress may have been induced. The expression of let-363 and sip-1 decreased, which may indicate that DPhP exposure interfered with the metabolic process of nematodes (Smith et al., 2023).

DPhP exposure led to negative effect on the intestinal and nervous systems

We next evaluated intestinal integrity in nematodes using brilliant blue staining, revealing preserved intestinal structure (Fig. 4D), though 0.5 and 1 g/L DPhP exposure induced characteristic intestinal tortuosity. Gene expression analysis showed concentration-dependent effects: low concentrations suppressed apoptosis-related genes (ced-3, cep-1), cell polarity regulators (par-6), and signaling components (mtm-6, egl-8) (Fig. 4E), suggesting impaired stress adaptation through apoptotic pathway interference and polarity disruption. Conversely, high concentrations activated ced-3, cep-1, and par-6 expression, indicating toxicant-induced apoptosis activation and cellular remodeling. Notably, neurotransmitter metabolism genes (ace-3, ace-4, ace-1, eat-4, dat-1), anti-apoptotic ced-9, and ion regulator nhx-2 were universally upregulated across concentrations. We further used chemotaxis assay to verify the effects of DPhP exposure on the nervous system. After exposure to DPhP, the chemotaxis of C. elegans to NaCl was significantly reduced (Fig. 5A). In addition, diacetyl can be used as a simple learning model for C. elegans to assess their learning ability (Arai et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 5B, as the concentration of DPhP exposure increased, the learning index of nematodes gradually decreased, revealing that DPhP exposure may damage nematode neurons and disrupt nematode memory and cognitive abilities. Besides, the dopamine and GABA contents were reduced as the worms were exposed to DPhP (Fig. 5C and D). Furthermore, BZ555 strain was applied to visualize the expression of dat-1. It should be noted that the effects observed at the two highest concentrations (0.5 and 1 g/L) may partly reflect generalized high-dose toxicity, as these concentrations also caused significant lethality and growth inhibition. Therefore, we interpret these findings cautiously and focus on lower concentrations (0.1 g/L) for DPhP-specific effects where general toxicity is minimal. Thereby, 0.1 g/L concentration was selected to assess the integrity of dat-1 neurons. As shown in Fig. 5E, compared with the control group, DPhP exposure damaged the formation of dopamine neuronal cells. Discussions

DPhP is widely utilized across various industrial sectors, particularly in the plastics industry, and personal care products, contributing to its pervasive presence in everyday environments and widespread human exposure. While the toxicological effects of other phthalate esters—such as DEHP and diisooctyl phthalate (DIOP)—have been extensively studied, including their roles in inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress, disrupting calcium homeostasis, and triggering oxidative damage and apoptosis (Guo et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2024). The toxicological profile of DPhP remains poorly characterized. Hence, this work has primarily focused on in vitro and in vivo side effect of DPhP, to provide critical insights into the mechanisms underlying DPhP toxicity.

MTT and cell colony results pointed to an inhibitory influence of DPhP on the growth and colony-forming potential of HUVECs (Fig. 1). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a substantial increase in the proportion of cells in the G1 phase following DPhP exposure, implicating its involvement in modulating key molecular pathways at the G1/S checkpoint. Rather than indicating a complete cell cycle arrest, these changes are more consistent with a stress-induced cell cycle perturbation. Specifically, exposure to a high dose (1 g/L) caused a G1-phase accumulation that likely reflects a non-specific stress response associated with the borderline cytotoxicity observed at this concentration. In contrast, the S-phase accumulation detected at 0.1 g/L—a concentration with minimal loss of viability—may represent a more chemically specific perturbation of cell cycle progression. This effect is consistent with the cytotoxic properties observed in other phthalates, such as DEHP, which has been shown to induce G1 arrest in rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells via downregulation of CDK2 and CDK4 (Hussein et al., 2023). The DPhP concentrations used (0.1–10 g/L) exceed environmental levels but were chosen for mechanistic hazard identification of this understudied plasticizer. While direct extrapolation to human exposure requires caution, DPhP has been shown to be readily biodegradable under aerobic and anaerobic conditions (Kim et al., 2008; Yuan et al., 2002); however, its continuous release may result in pseudo-persistence, and its potential for bioaccumulation remains a concern. DPhP has been detected in water/soil at ng/L–μg/L levels. Multi-pathway human exposure and typical 100-fold safety margins support that our findings can inform hazard assessment at lower doses. Future chronic low-dose studies are warranted. Importantly, our study highlights the role of the transcription factor FOXO3a in mediating DPhP-induced cell cycle alterations. DPhP exposure led to increased FOXO3a expression, which in turn activated downstream targets P21 and P27—key cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKIs). As CDKIs, P21 and P27 inhibit the activity of cyclin-CDK complexes (e.g., cyclin D–CDK4/6 and cyclin E–CDK2), thereby preventing phosphorylation of the retinoblastoma (Rb) protein and halting progression from G1 to S phase (Baker and Reddy, 2012). The combined upregulation of P21 and P27 and downregulation of cyclins synergistically reinforced the inhibitory effect on cell cycle progression. Given the central role of endothelial cells in maintaining vascular homeostasis, the DPhP-induced cell stress responses observed in HUVECs may carry implications beyond basal cytotoxicity. Endothelial dysfunction—commonly manifested as loss of viability, cell cycle dysregulation, and a pro-oxidative shift—is recognized as an early initiating event in atherosclerosis, hypertension, and other cardiovascular diseases. Therefore, the decreased cell viability, cell cycle perturbation, and oxidative stress elicited by DPhP in HUVECs could collectively constitute a potential endothelial injury phenotype that warrants further investigation in more complex cardiovascular models. However, it must be emphasized that these observations are derived entirely from an immortalized human umbilical vein endothelial cell line exposed at concentrations above environmentally relevant levels; hence, extrapolation to in vivo cardiovascular outcomes remains speculative at present. Future studies should prioritize validating these findings in primary endothelial cells, co-culture systems, or transgenic animal models to clarify the impact of DPhP on endothelial function and overall cardiovascular health.

To further investigate the toxicological effects of DPhP in vivo, we employed C. elegans as a model organism. Our results demonstrate that DPhP exposure induces multifaceted toxicity in nematodes, including growth inhibition, impaired motility, and a pronounced oxidative stress response. DPhP exposure led to a concentration-dependent increase in DCF fluorescence, suggesting enhanced cellular oxidant generation. Excessive ROS can damage biological macromolecules, such as nucleic acids and proteins, leading us to infer that DPhP exposure promotes a pro-oxidative state, which negatively impact nematode growth and development, ultimately resulting in growth retardation and reduced motility. Elevated oxidative stress disrupts systemic homeostasis, adversely impacting development, reproduction, and survival (Sies, 2015). These effects are likely driven by excessive ROS generation, which interferes with essential cellular processes such as division and differentiation (Ye et al., 2015). Despite the limitations of the DCFH-DA method, these fluorescence increases are best viewed as indicating a pro-oxidative shift rather than definitive oxidative stress, and confirmation would require complementary methods. Consistent with our in vitro findings, DPhP exposure significantly disrupted the cell cycle in C. elegans. Specifically, L1 larvae exposed to DPhP for 48 h exhibited growth inhibition closely linked to cell cycle alterations, impeding cellular proliferation during critical developmental stages. These results confirm that DPhP-induced cell cycle alterations is observable across both cellular and organismal models, underscoring its broad toxicological impact.

The insulin/insulin-like growth factor signaling (IIS) pathway, particularly the daf-2 and age-1 genes, plays a central role in stress response regulation (Murphy and Hu, 2005; Sun et al., 2017). While their upregulation may transiently suppress DAF-16 activity, prolonged DPhP exposure likely activates DAF-16 indirectly via oxidative stress, which may initiate metabolic reprogramming, reducing energy expenditure and delaying growth as an adaptive response (Xiao et al., 2021). Ultimately, DAF-16 enhances stress resistance by upregulating antioxidant genes (Cao et al., 2022). Activation of the p38 MAPK pathway, indicated by increased expression of nsy-1, sek-1, and pmk-1, further supports the presence of a stress response. This pathway is essential for innate immunity and cellular survival under oxidative stress (Inoue et al., 2005; Troemel et al., 2006). However, downregulation of hsp-12.6 suggests compromised protein repair capacity, while upregulation of hsf-1 and hsp-16.2 indicates protein damage likely caused by elevated ROS levels (Carranza et al., 2020; Govindan et al., 2018). The transcription factor skn-1 was also upregulated, promoting antioxidant gene expression (e.g., sod-3, gst-4) to counteract oxidative stress (Motta et al., 2020; Pohl et al., 2019). Despite these protective mechanisms, persistent ROS accumulation suggests that cellular defenses are overwhelmed, and may lead to damage of lipids, proteins, and DNA, and potentially contributing to physiological decline (Zhu et al., 2022).

DPhP also modulates apoptotic and autophagic pathways. Upregulation of ced-9 and ced-4 implies activation of apoptosis, while altered expression of let-363 may impair autophagy, resulting in the accumulation of damaged cellular components (Liu and Hengartner, 1999). This imbalance likely contributes to growth retardation and motor dysfunction. Neurotransmission was also affected. NaCl-sensing neurons in nematodes can be attracted by NaCl, an essential substance in nematode culture (Dekkers et al., 2021). Then, with the exposure to DPhP, the chemotaxis of nematodes to NaCl was significantly reduced, indicating that DPhP exposure may have destroyed NaCl-sensing neurons, resulting in a weakening of nematodes' ability to recognize NaCl (Fig. 5A). Upregulation of dat-1 and eat-4 suggests disruption of dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling, which are critical for neuromuscular coordination (Ma et al., 2025; Refai et al., 2021). Increased expression of ace-1 and ace-3 may reduce acetylcholine levels at neuromuscular junctions, further impairing motor function, thereby reducing learning and cognitive abilities (Combes et al., 2001). These findings indicate that DPhP-induced neurotoxicity is mediated by both oxidative stress and neurotransmitter dysregulation. Additionally, upregulation of clk-2 and nhx-2 suggests disturbances in energy metabolism and ion homeostasis, while downregulation of mtm-6 and egl-8 may impair cell signaling and development (Ahmed et al., 2002; Ching et al., 2020; Ericson et al., 2014; Nehrke, 2003). Collectively, these gene expression changes provide mechanistic insight into DPhP-induced growth inhibition and behavioral deficits.

In summary, DPhP exposure in C. elegans leads to growth retardation, reduced motility, and and a pro-oxidative shift reflected by increased DCF fluorescence through the modulation of multiple pathways, including IIS, p38 MAPK, apoptosis/autophagy, neurotransmission, and metabolism. These responses reflect an attempt to mitigate DPhP-induced stress, albeit at the cost of physiological function. To enhance translational relevance, future research should incorporate mammalian models, such as rodents, to validate DPhP-induced cell cycle dysregulation and assess potential transgenerational effects.

Conclusion

This study employed in vitro cell models and the in vivo model organism C. elegans to systematically investigate the toxicological effects of DPhP and elucidate its underlying molecular mechanisms. Experimental findings demonstrated that DPhP exposure induced the G1 phase cell cycle alterations in cells, which mechanistically linked to the upregulation of p21 and p27, coupled with reduced expression levels of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and cyclins. In C. elegans, DPhP exposure resulted in significant developmental abnormalities, including reduced body length and impaired locomotor activity. Furthermore, DPhP triggered an elevation of ROS levels in nematodes, concomitant with dysregulated expression of IIS pathway components and the p38 MAPK signaling cascade. Further analysis revealed aberrant expression profiles of neurodevelopment-associated genes and intestinal differentiation regulators, suggesting that DPhP may disrupt neuroendocrine networks critical for growth modulation and stress adaptation. This work provides the important evidence that DPhP exerts its toxic effects through multimodal mechanisms, including disruption of cell cycle regulation, oxidative stress imbalance, and interference with neural signaling pathways.
