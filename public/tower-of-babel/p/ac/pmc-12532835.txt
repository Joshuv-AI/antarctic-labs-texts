Comparative virulence study of locally circulating peste des petits ruminants virus (PPRV) strains in indigenous Ethiopian goats
Authors: Fasil Aklilu, Hagos Ashenafi, Tesfu Kassa, Hassen Chaka, Demeke Sibhatu, Dereje Shegu, Abde Aliy Mohammed, Redeat Belaineh, Menbere Kidane, Hagos Asgedom, Tesfaye Chibssa, Getnet Mekonnen, Asegedetch Sirak, Solomon Gebredufe, Maurice Byukusenge, Catherine M Herzog, Vivek Kapur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeste des Petits Ruminants (PPR) is a highly contagious viral disease affecting small ruminants with considerable socioeconomic impacts in Ethiopia. Despite its importance, comparative virulence of locally circulating PPR virus strains in indigenous goats remains poorly characterized. This study evaluated six distinct PPRV strains (four lineage-IV, two lineage-III) in experimentally infected Ethiopian goats. Twenty-one Central highland breed goats (12–30 months) were randomly allocated into six experimental groups and one control group, with three goats in each group and experimental animals inoculated intranasally (104.5 TCID50 per ml). Clinical, serological, molecular, and pathogenesis data were collected.ResultsFive of six strains produced consistent clinical manifestations beginning at 4 days post-infection (dpi), with seroconversion by 7 dpi and viral antigens detectable in nasal swabs from 3 dpi. No substantial lineage-specific differences in virulence were observed between lineage-III and lineage-IV strains. One lineage-IV isolate (38,920/19) produced significantly milder clinical manifestations (P < 0.05) and no seroconversion, suggesting potential strain-level variation. Gross and histopathological findings were consistent across virulent strains, with nasal swabs proving most reliable for early diagnosis.ConclusionsThis first comparative study of Ethiopian PPRV strains establishes similar virulence profiles across lineages in local goats, suggesting that lineage classification alone may not predict clinical outcomes—an important finding for regional disease control strategies. The reduced virulence in one strain suggests factors beyond genomic lineage may influence experimental outcomes, though additional work with standardized protocols is needed to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05073-4.Keywords: Comparative virulence, Experimental infection, Indigenous Goats, Pathogenesis, PPRV, Strain characterization

Introduction

Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR), also known as 'goat plague' or 'Kata' [1], is a highly contagious and frequently fatal viral disease that affects both domestic and wild small ruminants [2]. The disease has a severe socio-economic impact on rural poor farmers [3], who heavily rely on small ruminant rearing, due to its high morbidity and mortality rates. In most PPR infected regions, it poses a significant threat to small ruminant production, greatly impacting livelihoods of poor farmers who predominantly own sheep and goats [4]. Since its emergence in Côte d'Ivoire [5], the disease has progressively expanded its geographical range from Africa to the Middle East, Asia, and more recently, Europe [6, 7]. The etiological agent of the disease (PPR virus) belongs to family Paramixoviridae, genus Morbillivirus and species Morbillivirus caprinae. The disease primarily manifests in an acute form, clinically characterized by symptoms such as depression, fever, oculo-nasal discharges, oral lesions, pneumonia, and diarrhea, often resulting in death. However, the sub-acute form is observed, particularly in sheep in endemic areas [8] and a subclinical form in atypical hosts like cattle [9–11].

During field outbreak investigations, a spectrum of clinical outcomes is typically observed, ranging from mild to severe respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms, and in some cases, resulting in death [12–14]. Previous studies have shown that different species and breeds of small ruminants are affected by PPR to varying degrees. For instance, a research on different PPRV strains investigated their virulence in West African dwarf goats and indicated that virulence characteristics might serve as a valuable marker for classifying PPRV strains [15]. Diop et al. (2005) also noted variations in the sensitivity of different goat breeds to the same PPRV outbreak during field investigations and concluded that this variation in susceptibility is more related to the breed than to differences in the virus itself [16].

Furthermore, current understanding of PPR pathology and pathogenesis has been largely inferred from studies of the closely related rinderpest virus (RPV) and other morbillivirus infections [17]. Only a few studies [18–22] have examined the pathology of PPRV, leading to a limited understanding of the disease's pathology, clinical progression, and outcomes in susceptible hosts like goats. Furthermore, in recent years, there has been limited focus on the diversity of viral strains and their potential to produce varying clinical outcomes [15]. To assess the impact of these differences, including their potential for transmission, further studies are required in regions where PPRV is enzootic or endemic.

To date, PPRV strain virulence studies have not been undertaken on locally circulating strains in Ethiopia and there is a lack of knowledge on how viral strain relates to clinical outcomes observed in local Ethiopian goat breeds. Hence, this study focused on characterizing locally circulating PPRV strains and assessing their virulence in experimentally infected local Ethiopian goat breed.

Material and methods

Experimental animals

Goats of indigenous breed (Central highland breed), sex all males, aged between 12 to 30 months, were obtained from animal markets in North Shoa, Ejere, and Degem, all within a 200-km radius of Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Animal selection criteria involved factors such as age, overall health, breed validity, and absence of PPR vaccination history. Age determination relied on dental examination [23] and information provided by the sellers. Animals were subsequently transported to the Animal Health Institute (AHI). Seronegative status was confirmed by competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (c-ELISA, ID.Vet, Grabels, France) after 3 to 4 days of arrival. Seronegative goats were acclimatized for about 10 to 15-days, ear-tagged, and randomly divided into experimental and control groups.

In total, 21 goats each were randomly allocated across 7 barns: 6 for experimental groups and one for the negative control group, with each barn housing 3 goats. Study design details can be found in Supplementary (S) Text S1.

Virus

Viral suspensions were prepared using Vero Dog Slam (VDS) cells at AHI laboratory. Viral strains were chosen from archived outbreak investigation samples. Selection criteria included: geographic location, collection year, and test result. Specifically, candidates had to pass two consecutive tests—RT-qPCR and virus isolation—demonstrating positivity in both, as well as meeting quality control standards for sequencing.

In this study, a total of six strains were utilized, four belonging to lineage-IV, and two from lineage-III. Virus strain characterization work was conducted at AHI (RT-qPCR and viral culture) and at Penn State University (whole-genome sequencing (WGS)). Viral suspension titer, passage number, codes, geographic location, lineage type and animal species, age, sex and sample type collected and processed for the purpose are presented in Table 1. Each experimental goat received a total virus dose of 1 ml (concentration 104.5 TCID50 per ml), with 0.5 mL administered per nostril during inoculation as per [11, 24].

Clinical investigation and scoring

After infection daily comprehensive clinical examinations were conducted on all goats to detect the emergence and progression of clinical signs indicative of PPR [11, 12, 24–26]. Veterinary attention was provided as described in Text S2. As per the criteria set by Pope et al. (2013), goats with a rectal temperature of 39.5 °C or above were categorized as febrile.

Sampling and sample processing

Sera and ocular, nasal, and rectal swabs from experimentally infected goats were collected at 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 12 days post-infection (dpi). These were tested for viral antigen and RNA. Only serum samples were collected and tested from control animals on the same dpi and only c-ELISA was performed to confirm seronegativity, and that swab sample testing was not done because of biosecurity and economic considerations.

Detection of PPRV antigen and PPRV/RNA was performed using immunocapture-ELISA (Ic-ELISA, ID Screen® PPR Antigen Capture kit, ID.Vet, Grabels, France) and real-time RT-qPCR, respectively. The PPR Ic-ELISA was used only on nasal and rectal swabs from experimental goats, utilizing the method as described in [24, 27].

RT-qPCR was conducted on both swab and tissue samples (see Gross and Histological Sample section below) collected throughout the study and positive specimens underwent virus isolation to confirm the presence of viable virus. Viral RNA extraction was conducted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA was utilized in an RT-qPCR assay targeting the nucleoprotein (N) gene of PPRV using specific primers and probe as described by [28]. Detailed procedure of RT-qPCR is presented in Text S3. Viral isolation occurred in Vero Dog Slam (VDS) cells, following the protocol outlined by [29]. A detail virus isolation procedure is outlined in Text S4.

Baseline seronegativity and experimental antibody responses for anti-PPRV-N protein antibodies were evaluated using the ID Screen® PPR Competition kit (ID.Vet, Grabels, France) as described in [24, 27]. Moreover, sequencing and phylogenetic analysis for lineage-level classification were conducted as per the methods outlined in Simon King et al. (2023).

Gross and histopathological examination

Postmortem tissue samples were obtained from experimental goats after euthanasia by captive bolt stunning. Penetrating type captive bolt (Text S5) was used, followed immediately by exsanguination to ensure death. These protocols were in full compliance with relevant AHI, Animal Research Scientific and Ethics Review Committee (ARSERC) and consistent with AVMA guidelines (2020).” The timeline was one goat per barn/strain (a total of six goats per day) sacrificed, starting at 7 dpi, followed by 10 dpi, and concluding at 12 dpi to aid in the collection of tissue samples. We set 12 dpi as end date for this experiment based on primary objective of the study (pathogenesis study during early disease course) and previous pathogenesis studies recommendations [11, 24, 25]. Subsequently, a comprehensive postmortem examination was conducted on each animal to evaluate for gross pathological changes. In total, 14 tissue specimens were collected, including the retropharyngeal Lymph Node (LN), palatine tonsil, third (3rd) eyelid, nasal mucosa, tongue apex, tracheal LN, bronchial LN, mediastinal LN, cranial lung, caudal lung, mesenteric LN, spleen, jejunal and ileal Peyer's patches, and colon.

Tissue and LN inspections were meticulous, focusing on localized areas affected by the PPR virus. Documented changes, including any deviations in structure, morphology, and color, and histopathological changes induced by PPRV infection were also systematically scored by measuring the extent of the lesions, with scoring criteria detailed in [24]. For detail procedure on histological analysis and grading refer to Text S6.

Data analysis and visualization

Analysis and visualization were conducted in R statistical software (version 4.1.3), employing the ggplot2 package for generating figures. Local regression (LOESS) smoothed curves with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, calculated through t-based approximation, were incorporated into the plots using the stat-smooth function to provide a visual summary of the collective data trends across the study duration. For comparative clinical score and tissue positivity percentage analysis we applied a non-parametric test (Kruskal–Wallis test) using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.1, as the data did not follow a normal distribution and the sample size was small.

Results

Clinical signs following inoculation with different PPRV strains

Goats experimentally infected with six distinct strains of PPRV (four Lineage IV and two Lineage III) exhibited the typical clinical signs of PPRV, except for one strain (strain 38,920/19), which only caused a mild nasal discharge between 6 to 9 days post-inoculation (dpi). The majority of strains showed clinical signs starting from 4 dpi including general signs, fever (≥ 39.5 °C), oculo-nasal discharge, congested mucous membrane, and at later stage respiratory signs, diarrhea and some goats manifested erosive mouth lesions. Examples of some of the observed clinical signs are presented in (Figs. S1A-1D). Immune response to infection with different PPRV strains

The immune response to the virus strains was evaluated using a c-ELISA test. The 2 out of 3 experimental goats infected with PPRV lineage-IV (24,475/19), had detectable anti-PPRV antibody starting from 7 dpi. By 10 dpi, all goats in the group were found seropositive. All goats inoculated with other lineage-IV virus strains (2014/Habru and 25,870/21), were found to have detectable anti-PPRV antibody from 7 dpi until the end of the experiment (12 dpi). Conversely, goats inoculated with another lineage-IV virus, (38,920/19), remained seronegative throughout the experiment. Goats inoculated with lineage-III viruses (26,173/19, and 35,368/19), tested seropositive with c-ELISA from 7 dpi until the end of the experiment (12 dpi). Negative controls remained negative until the end of the experiment (see Fig. 4).

PPRV tissue tropism on various postmortem tissues and LNs samples was evaluated by performing RT-qPCR (Table S2). Overall, at 7 dpi, an average of 88.09% (74/84) of tissue samples tested positive. Gross and histopathology findings

Gross pathology

Goats infected with different strains from both lineages-IV and III manifested PPRV characteristics grade-2 lesions. Grade-2 signs and lesions included swelling with edema, congestion and hemorrhage in most of the inspected tissues and lymph nodes (LNs), distinct signs on lung tissue like hepatization and meaty appearance (Figure S2) and emphysema, necrotized thickened mucosa of jejunum, congested Peyer’s patch and catarrhal enteritis. However, a goat infected with lineage-IV, 38,920/19 strain and sacrificed at 7 dpi, showed grade-1 gross lesions, like slight enlargement in some of inspected LNs and slightly hepatized cranial lung. While goats sacrificed at 10 and 12 dpi’s and infected with the same virus strain (38,920/19) showed no typical gross lesions observed on inspected tissues and LNs. Detailed individual strain gross pathology results are presented in Text S10.

Histopathology

Histopathological lesions characterized as grade-2 lesions were consistently observed beginning at 7 dpi in sacrificed goats. These grade-2 lesions:- included lymphocyte cell depletion (characterized by pyknosis and karyolysis), congestion, and hemorrhage of the head, lung-associated, and mesenteric lymph nodes, as well as in other inspected non-lymphoid organs. In contrast, goats inoculated with 38,920/19 lineage-IV strain showed only mild histological lesions from one goat sacrificed at 7 dpi. The remaining two goats infected with 38,920/19 strain and sacrificed at 10 and 12 dpi’s showed no histological signs. Detailed histopathological findings for specific tissues can be found in Text S11 and Figures S3A – 3F.

Discussion

The current study characterized and compared PPRV strain virulence in indigenous Ethiopian goats for 6 locally circulating strains. In this study, viruses from two identified lineages, Lineage-IV and Lineage-III, produced nearly similar clinical outcomes in the inoculated goats except the very mild signs observed from 38,920/19, a lineage-IV strain marked by significant (P < 0.05, 0.0039) difference in clinical scores with other experimental groups. This study built on previous PPR virus pathogenesis studies, which showed that various strains produced a range of clinical outcomes. Historically, mild infection was seen with the lineage-II strain, acute to mild disease with the lineage-III strain, acute disease with the lineage-IV strain, and per-acute fatal disease with the lineage-I strain [15]. To date, one other study has compared PPRV pathogenesis and tissue tropism in goats [21]. Moreover, a study conducted in the UK reported no major lineage-specific differences in virulence among the six virus strains tested on British male goats [30]. The current study both aligns with and diverges from the previous works, and establishes similar virulence profiles across two lineages in local goats, suggesting that lineage classification alone may not predict clinical outcomes—an important finding for regional disease control strategies.

To our knowledge, this is the first report describing PPR virulence in a local breed of goats in Ethiopia. Although the strains used belonged to different lineages (L-III and L-IV) and had varying passage numbers (second to fourth), most of them caused similar clinical signs of disease. However, there were slight differences in the severity of symptoms observed both during anti-mortem and postmortem inspection times among the groups. The study, although conducted with a limited number of goats (three per group/strain), demonstrated that disease severity (including clinical signs, virus shedding pattern (viremia), tissue positivity and gross and histopathological alterations) varied at the strain level within the same lineage. Goats inoculated with virus strains, 24,475/21(IV), 2014/Habru(IV) and 35,368/19(III) showed peak temperature (rectal temperature ≥ 410C) while other strains, 26,173/19(III) and 25,870/21(IV) manifested lower temperature and strain 38,920/19 showed lowest temperature record (Fig. 1andTable S1). Virus antigen shedding (viremia) pattern also showed variation. Shedding was prominent in strains 24,475/21(IV), 2014/Habru(IV) and 25,870/21(IV) while strains 38,920/19(IV), 26,173/19(III) and 35,368/19(III) marked lower shedding pattern (Fig. 2). Variation in disease outcomes at the strain level was also been observed in prior research. Studies by Eloiflin et al. (2022) and Couacy-Hymann et al. [15] identified differences in virulence between two strains from PPRV lineage-I. According to Eloiflin et al. (2022) [17, 18, 18, 19, 19] the strain they used resulted in mild disease symptoms, whereas Couacy-Hymann et al. [15] documented a severe disease outcome from the same lineage-I with a different strain.

While we observed some variation in disease manifestation, statistical analyses revealed no significant differences in clinical signs, viremia, viral tissue tropism, or gross and histopathological findings between lineage-III and lineage-IV groups (P = 0.61 for tissue positivity). Five of the six virus strains produced characteristic PPR clinical signs including fever (≥ 39.5 °C), oculo-nasal discharge, congested mucous membrane, and at later stage respiratory signs, diarrhea and some goats manifested erosive mouth lesions starting from 4 dpi, consistent with previous field and experimental studies [14, 15, 18, 31–33].

The notable exception was strain 38,920/19 (lineage-IV), which produced significantly milder symptoms (P < 0.05) compared to other strains and performed poorly across all evaluation criteria, exhibiting only limited serous nasal discharge. This finding contrasts with earlier reports that lineage-IV PPRV, particularly those originating from Asia, typically display high virulence and cause acute disease [15]. The reduced virulence could reflect genuine biological differences or technical factors related to laboratory handling. Virus culture passages can produce defective interfering particles, which might reduce virulence by hindering the replication of standard virus [30, 34]), and serial passages are well-documented to attenuate PPRV [35, 36] and that confirmation will require neutralization assays and longer-term studies. Interestingly, our preliminary sequencing efforts (to be reported in a future study) have not yet revealed genomic variations that would explain this phenotypic difference, suggesting complex interactions between viral genetics, handling history, and host responses may be involved. These observations highlight the importance of considering both biological properties and technical factors when interpreting experimental outcomes in PPRV research.

In this study, no mortality occurred up to 12 dpi across all groups. This differs from some previous studies which reported mortality within 7–12 dpi, with lineages-I, III and IV PPRV [15], and from 9–21 dpi, with lineage-IV strain (PPR/Izatnagar/94), [37]. However, one study did not find mortality during the observation period up to 28 dpi [38], similar to our study. Absence of mortality in our study might be due to various factors including virus infectious dose used, breed, use of relatively shorter duration of observation prior to sacrifice (12 days), and potentially altered virulence due to cell culture passaging [30, 34].

Most of the experimental goats seroconverted starting from 7 dpi in agreement with previous studies [11, 15, 18, 36], while goats infected with the 38,920/19 strain failed to seroconvert and were serologically negative up to the end of our experiment on 12 dpi. This could be explained by decreased strain virulence or failure to infect with appropriate dose and shorter duration of the experiment (12 dpi).

Viral kinetics from swabs samples and viral predilection sites in postmortem tissues and LNs (Table S2 and Fig. 2) were in agreement patterns described in previous studies [18, 31, 39, 40].

PPRV antigens were detected in both nasal and rectal swabs from 3 to 12 dpi. Detection in rectal swabs was intermittent and occurred at a later stage of the disease. These findings align with previous studies [11, 25], which also reported PPRV antigens detected later in the disease course in rectal swab samples and that the detection was intermittent. The results from this study suggest that nasal swabs are the most suitable samples for molecular diagnosis of PPRV (Fig. 2) as has been in previous studies [25].

Limitations of the study

This study had several methodological constraints that should be considered when interpreting the results. The small group sizes (n = 3 per strain) limited statistical power, potentially masking subtle differences between strains. The relatively short observation period of 12 days may have precluded detection of late-developing clinical manifestations or mortality that might have occurred beyond this timeframe. Additionally, our use of only Central highland goat breed restricts the generalizability of findings to other Ethiopian indigenous breeds, particularly those from lowland areas that may have different susceptibility profiles. While we characterized the strains by lineage, more detailed genomic analysis would be valuable for identifying specific genetic determinants of virulence variations observed, especially regarding strain 38,920/19.

Conclusion and recommendations

This comparative assessment of locally circulating PPRV strains in Ethiopia provides important baseline data for understanding strain virulence in indigenous goats. Our findings demonstrate that both lineage-III and lineage-IV PPRV strains produce similar clinical outcomes. The reduced virulence of one lineage-IV strain highlights the complex interplay between viral genetics, laboratory handling, and host factors in determining disease outcomes.

Although this study has isolated and characterized different virus strains from the commonly circulating lineages (LIII and LIV) in the country, they remain controllable with the currently available Nigeria 75/1 (L-II strain) vaccine. However, to strengthen these findings and address current limitations, we recommend future studies incorporate: (1) larger sample sizes to increase statistical power; (2) extended observation periods beyond 12 dpi to capture the complete disease progression; (3) inclusion of diverse goat breeds from both highland and lowland regions to assess breed-specific susceptibility; and (4) comprehensive genomic and phenotypic characterization of viral strains to better understand the molecular basis of virulence differences. Such expanded research would provide more robust evidence to inform targeted control measures for this economically significant disease affecting Ethiopia's small ruminant population.
