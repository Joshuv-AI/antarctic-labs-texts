Validation of the parkland grading scale in predicting the critical view of safety during laparoscopic cholecystectomy: A prospective cohort study with implications for future artificial intelligence ground truth establishment
Authors: Hoai Kim Nguyen, Thien Lai Vo, Ho Tran, Thanh Phuoc Bui, Xuan Binh Dau, Phuoc Cong Thanh Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsThe critical view of safety (CVS) prevents bile duct injury, but severe inflammation hinders its achievement. This study evaluated the parkland grading scale (PGS) as a real-time intraoperative predictor of CVS attainment in a Southeast Asian population, establishing standardized clinical data for future artificial intelligence applications.MethodsThis prospective observational study (January–June 2025) included 88 consecutive patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy by a single surgeon. PGS was assessed upon initial laparoscopic inspection. We analyzed correlations between PGS, CVS attainment (Strasberg’s criteria), operative time, and bail-out procedures using Spearman’s correlation and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsSevere inflammation (PGS grade 4–5) was observed in 31.8% of patients. PGS exhibited a strong negative correlation with CVS score (p = −0.652; p < 0.001) and strong positive correlations with operative time and blood loss. A PGS threshold of 4 accurately predicted CVS failure (area under the curve = 0.863). Bail-out procedures were necessary in 11 cases (12.5%), all occurring in the PGS ≥ 4 group. Advanced age and diabetes mellitus were independent risk factors for CVS failure.ConclusionsThe PGS serves as a precise intraoperative early warning system. A score ≥ 4 indicates a significant risk of CVS failure, prompting safer bail-out strategies. Additionally, this study provides a standardized dataset vital for training future autonomous surgical risk assessment models.Keywords: Parkland grading scale, Critical view of safety, Bail-out procedures, Artificial intelligence

INTRODUCTION

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) is recognized as the gold standard for treating benign gallbladder disease due to its minimally invasive nature and quick recovery time. However, iatrogenic bile duct injury (BDI) remains a serious complication, with incidence rates between 0.2% and 0.7% [1]. The primary cause of BDI is often the misidentification of anatomical structures within Calot’s triangle. The impact of BDI is significant; it leads to considerable morbidity and potential mortality. A recent cost analysis revealed that the surgical repair of major BDI places a heavy financial burden on the healthcare system, with costs rising sharply as the severity of the injury increases [2].

To reduce this risk, Strasberg and Brunt [3] proposed the “critical view of safety” (CVS) in 1995 as a definitive approach for anatomical identification. Achieving CVS requires three essential criteria: (1) clearing the hepatocystic triangle of fat and fibrous tissue, (2) separating the lower third of the gallbladder from the liver bed to expose the cystic plate, and (3) ensuring that only two structures (the cystic duct and artery) are visible entering the gallbladder. While strict adherence to CVS significantly lowers the risk of BDI, achieving it can be challenging in cases of severe inflammation. Recent studies suggest that aggressively pursuing CVS in situations involving dense fibrosis or a “frozen” Calot’s triangle can inadvertently heighten the risk of injury [4]. Additionally, commonly used preoperative grading systems, such as the Tokyo Guidelines 2018, heavily depend on preoperative imaging to assess severity [5]. As a result, these predictors may not always accurately represent the true level of intraoperative technical difficulty, which is often only uncovered during direct laparoscopic inspection [6].

Preoperative predictors of surgical difficulty, including patient characteristics (e.g., body mass index [BMI] > 30 kg/m2, diabetes mellitus, and a history of upper abdominal surgery) to specific biliary findings (e.g., empyema, wall thickening, impacted stones, or history of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography [ERCP]) have been extensively studied [7]. In contrast, the intraoperative assessment of inflammation and adhesions has received much less focus, despite being critical factors that can hinder the achievement of CVS. In routine clinical practice, surgeons often subjectively describe the severity of inflammation within Calot’s triangle, without a standardized system to objectively quantify the technical challenges involved.

To address these gaps, Madni et al. [8] developed the parkland grading scale (PGS), a new intraoperative scoring system that evaluates the severity of gallbladder inflammation based solely on visual anatomical landmarks. The scale ranges from grade 1 (no inflammation, clear anatomy) to grade 5 (severe inflammation, completely obscured anatomy) [8]. Previous research has shown that the PGS is a strong predictor of conversion to open surgery, operative time, length of hospital stay, and the risk of bile leaks [9,10]. Additionally, applying this scale right after the initial laparoscopic inspection (post-first-trocar placement) enables surgeons to proactively adjust their surgical strategy—such as considering a “bail-out” procedure—particularly in challenging cases where achieving CVS may be unsafe.

Recent studies have globally substantiated the value of the PGS in predicting the difficulty and complication risks associated with LC [9,10]. At the same time, there is increasing interest in integrating this scoring system into artificial intelligence (AI)-driven surgical assistance platforms for automated video analysis [11,12].

Data on the direct correlation between the intraoperative parkland score and the feasibility of achieving the CVS is limited, particularly in the Asian population. This geographical validation is crucial because the pathological spectrum in Southeast Asia significantly differs from Western studies. In Vietnam, Binh Dan Hospital handles a high volume of complex hepatobiliary conditions, where chronic infections and brown pigment stones often lead to severe anatomical distortion and scleroatrophic changes.

Investigating the relationship between the PGS and CVS attainment in this context serves three main purposes: (1) improving surgical safety through the early identification of challenging cases; (2) aiding the training of young surgeons with a quantifiable, visual, and easily applicable tool; and (3) creating a foundational clinical dataset that may support future AI applications for recognizing anatomy and predicting surgical difficulty.

Therefore, this pilot study aims to preliminarily validate the correlation between the intraoperative PGS and the likelihood of achieving the CVS in patients undergoing LC at our institution. Based on these findings, we intend to propose practical clinical applications and future directions for integrating this scoring system into standardized surgical practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This prospective observational study was carried out at Binh Dan Hospital, a major tertiary referral center for hepatobiliary surgery, from January to June 2025. We analyzed data from 88 patients who underwent LC. To minimize technical variability and ensure consistency in intraoperative assessments, all procedures were performed by a single surgeon who had completed the learning curve. This learning curve was defined as having performed at least 60 cases before the study began, a threshold that aligns with recent risk-adjusted cumulative sum analyses indicating proficiency plateaus ranging from 21 to 97 cases [13].

The PGS, which consists of five severity grades, served as the basis for assessing surgical difficulty (Table 1, Fig. 1). This scale was compared with the achievement of the CVS in accordance with international standards (Table 2).

Participants

The study population consisted of patients who met the following inclusion criteria:

- Age ≥ 18 years

- Diagnosis of benign gallbladder diseases (including symptomatic cholelithiasis, acute or chronic cholecystitis, and benign lesions such as adenomyomatosis or gallbladder polyps)

- Underwent either elective or emergency LC

- Availability of complete and accessible medical records.

Patients were excluded if they presented with:

- Concomitant surgical procedures (e.g., common bile duct exploration, liver resection)

- A history of complex biliary tract surgery

- Suspected or confirmed hepatobiliary malignancy

Data collection

Comprehensive data were collected prospectively, covering the following variables:

- Preoperative characteristics: Demographics, comorbidities, and history of ERCP.

- Laboratory and Imaging findings: Bilirubin levels, liver enzymes, and ultrasound characteristics (gallbladder wall thickness, presence of pericholecystic fluid, stone size).

- Intraoperative parameters: The parkland grade (determined immediately after the first trocar insertion), operative time, estimated blood loss, need for gallbladder decompression, bile spillage, placement of an additional (fourth) trocar, conversion to open surgery, and any vascular or biliary injuries.

- Outcomes: The primary outcome was the CVS score achieved (graded 0–6 according to Strasberg’s criteria). Intraoperative and postoperative complications were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). Spearman’s rank correlation assessed the relationship between the parkland grade and the CVS score, operative time, and estimated blood loss. Categorical variables were compared using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For continuous variables, the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for two groups, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for three or more groups. Multivariate logistic regression identified independent factors associated with the likelihood of achieving CVS. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis evaluated the predictive value of the parkland score for CVS failure. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Binh Dan Hospital (IRB no. 156/HĐĐĐ-BVBD). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

The study included 88 patients with a mean age of 49.1 ± 15.5 years (range: 21–92 years). The male-to-female ratio was 4:5, and the mean BMI was 24.2 ± 3.5 kg/m2. Regarding preoperative comorbidities, 12 patients (13.6%) had a history of ERCP, and 10 patients (11.4%) were being treated for diabetes mellitus.

Intraoperative findings and surgical outcomes

The distribution of gallbladder inflammation according to the PGS was as follows: grade 1 (n = 23, 26.1%), grade 2 (n = 23, 26.1%), grade 3 (n = 14, 15.9%), grade 4 (n = 6, 6.8%), and grade 5 (n = 22, 25.0%). Regarding the CVS, 66 patients (75.0%) achieved a score > 4, with 42 patients (47.7%) attaining a perfect score. Complete failure to achieve CVS occurred in 3 cases (3.4%), including two cases of Mirizzi syndrome (type I and type III) and one case of grade 5 inflammation complicated by liver cirrhosis.

The mean operative time was 73.9 ± 34.4 minutes. An additional fourth trocar was necessary in 9 cases (10.2%) to assist with dissection and exposure. Intraoperative complications were primarily minor injuries to the cystic artery, which were managed during the procedure. The mean estimated blood loss was 50 mL, and no iatrogenic bile duct injuries were reported. One case (1.1%) required conversion to open surgery due to severe inflammation (parkland grade 5) and bleeding from an atrophic liver bed. Postoperative complications mainly consisted of pericholecystic fluid collections (n = 16, 18.2%), which were treated conservatively. No instances of postoperative bile leakage or biliary obstruction were noted. The mean hospital stay was 2.9 ± 1.6 days (range: 1–7 days).

Predictors of CVS attainment (logistic regression)

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified age and diabetes mellitus as independent factors significantly associated with CVS attainment. Age exhibited a negative correlation (B = –0.159; p = 0.028; odds ratio [OR] = 0.853; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.739–0.983), indicating that each additional year of age decreases the odds of achieving CVS by approximately 14.7%. Diabetes mellitus was significantly associated with CVS failure (OR = 0.002; p = 0.005). However, the wide confidence interval (95% CI, 0.000–0.150) suggests cautious interpretation, likely due to the small sample size in this subgroup. Other factors, including gender, history of ERCP, leukocyte count, bilirubin levels, liver enzymes, gallbladder wall thickness, and pericholecystic fluid, did not show statistically significant associations (p > 0.05).

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed a strong negative correlation between the parkland grade and the CVS score (rho = –0.652; p < 0.001). Conversely, the parkland grade exhibited strong positive correlations with both operative time (rho = 0.663; p < 0.001) and estimated blood loss (rho = 0.688; p < 0.001). Additionally, the CVS score was strongly negatively correlated with operative time (rho = –0.673; p < 0.001) and blood loss (rho = –0.692; p < 0.001). Operative time and blood loss also showed a very strong positive correlation (rho = 0.838; p < 0.001). All correlations were statistically significant at the p < 0.01 level (Fig. 2).

Group comparisons (Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U)

The Kruskal-Wallis test showed a significant association between parkland grade and both operative time and CVS attainment.

Operative time: There was a statistically significant difference in operative time across the parkland groups (H = 42.504; df = 4; p < 0.001). The mean rank increased progressively with higher grades: grade 1 (28.13), grade 2 (31.50), grade 3 (44.75), grade 4 (56.67), and grade 5 (71.73). This indicates that higher parkland scores were associated with longer operative times.

CVS score: Significant differences were also observed for CVS scores (H = 41.186; df = 4; p < 0.001). The mean rank decreased as the parkland grade increased: grade 1 (64.63), grade 2 (48.24), grade 3 (49.07), grade 5 (23.07), and lowest in grade 4 (20.92).

Post-hoc analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.005) revealed significant differences in operative time and CVS scores among specific groups:

Operative time: Parkland grade 1 exhibited significantly shorter operative times compared to grade 5 (53.04 ± 19.05 minutes vs. 110.45 ± 26.98 minutes; U = 17; Z = –5.40; p < 0.001). Similarly, grade 2 (59.40 ± 27.89 minutes; U = 45; Z = –4.76; p < 0.001) and grade 3 (68.57 ± 21.61 minutes; U = 33.5; Z = –3.95; p < 0.001) also had significantly shorter times than grade 5 (Table 3).

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that the PGS had a strong predictive value for the failure to achieve CVS, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.863 (SE = 0.041; 95% CI, 0.783–0.943; p < 0.001). The optimal cut-off value was identified at a parkland score of ≥ 3.5, which yielded a sensitivity of 77.3% and a specificity of 83.3%. This indicates that a parkland score of ≥ 3.5 is a reliable threshold for predicting a high risk of CVS failure (Fig. 3). Since the PGS is assessed using discrete integer values in clinical practice, this statistical threshold corresponds to a clinical PGS of ≥ 4, warranting immediate consideration of bail-out strategies.

DISCUSSION

The results of this study revealed a strong, statistically significant negative correlation between the PGS and the achievement of the CVS in LC (rho = –0.652; p < 0.001). This finding supports the observations of Madni et al. [8,9], who noted that the parkland score reflects the severity of inflammation in Calot’s triangle, a crucial factor for the exposure and identification of anatomical structures. As the parkland grade increases, particularly at grades 4 and 5, Calot’s triangle often becomes completely obscured by dense fibrotic tissue, which significantly decreases the likelihood of attaining CVS and increases the risk of BDI [1,3]. This aligns with the Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons expert consensus reported by Pucher et al. [14], which identified “establishing the CVS” as the most important factor for safe practice, highlighting that failure to achieve it due to inflammation is a primary precursor to injury.

In our study, the increased risk significantly influenced surgical decision-making. As a result, bail-out procedures were required in 11 cases (12.5%), which included 1 conversion to open surgery, 2 subtotal cholecystectomies, and 8 fundus-first dissections. Importantly, these risk-reduction strategies were used only in cases with high parkland grades, highlighting the scale’s effectiveness in predicting the need for alternative surgical approaches [9,10].

ROC curve analysis revealed that a parkland threshold of ≥ 3.5 is a strong predictor of CVS failure (AUC = 0.863; sensitivity: 77.3%; specificity: 83.3%). This finding is consistent with Lalisang et al. [4], who noted significantly higher rates of CVS failure in patients with parkland grades 4–5. Thus, conducting a parkland assessment immediately after trocar insertion provides valuable early prognostic information, enabling surgeons to proactively implement risk-reduction strategies—such as fundus-first dissection, subtotal cholecystectomy, or open conversion—to lower the risk of BDI [1,3]. Supporting this predictive capability, Lee et al. [15] demonstrated a significant correlation between the PGS and the Tokyo Guidelines severity grading, confirming that higher intraoperative grades are reliable indicators of increased surgical difficulty and the likelihood of conversion. Subgroup analysis revealed a clear distinction in outcomes beyond the general correlation: the low-grade parkland group (grades 1–2) achieved significantly higher absolute CVS attainment rates and shorter operative times compared to the high-grade group (grades 4–5). Specifically, the parkland grade 1 group had a mean CVS score of 5.91 ± 0.29, which was significantly higher than the scores for grade 4 (3.17 ± 2.48; p < 0.001) and grade 5 (3.68 ± 1.56; p < 0.001). These findings align with the research of Lalisang et al. [4] and Liu et al. [16], who identified severe inflammation as the primary factor influencing surgical difficulty and CVS failure. Similarly, Asai et al. [17] developed a surgical difficulty grading system based on intraoperative findings. They confirmed that factors such as diffuse scarring in Calot’s triangle, comparable to high parkland grades, are critical determinants that categorize a case as “grade C” or highly difficult, often necessitating bail-out procedures. Notably, the parkland grade 3 group exhibited operative times and CVS scores that were intermediate between the two extremes. However, differences compared to the low-grade and high-grade groups did not always reach statistical significance. This suggests that parkland grade 3 represents a surgical “gray zone” of difficulty, where surgeon experience and technical adaptability play a crucial role in achieving CVS. This finding aligns with recent analyses by Ghamarnejad et al. [13], who emphasized the impact of the learning curve on outcomes in intermediate-difficulty cases. Future multi-center studies utilizing standardized grading are necessary to further clarify this risk stratification.

In terms of operative time, parkland grade 1 had an average duration of 53.04 ± 19.05 minutes, which was significantly shorter than that of parkland grade 5, which averaged 110.45 ± 26.98 minutes (p < 0.001). Similar significant differences were observed when comparing grades 2 and 3 to grade 5 (p < 0.001). The increased duration in high-grade cases can be attributed to dense fibrotic dissection, challenging anatomical identification, and the need for additional maneuvers, such as fourth trocar placement [6,16,18]. These findings align with Madni et al. [8,9], who observed that each incremental increase in the parkland score is associated with longer operative times and higher rates of surgical strategy modification. Furthermore, recent data from Ghamarnejad et al. [13] emphasize that prolonged durations in complex inflammatory cases highlight the increased technical demands and the need for a cautious approach to ensure safety.

Our study revealed a strong positive correlation between the parkland score and estimated blood loss (rho = 0.688). This correlation highlights the greater tissue trauma linked to severe inflammation and adhesions, as previously noted by Madni et al. [8.9], Ward et al. [11], and more recently by Liu et al. [16] in their evaluation of surgical difficulty in LC. Multivariate logistic regression identified advanced age and diabetes mellitus as independent risk factors for not achieving CVS. Notably, despite the wide confidence interval resulting from the small subgroup size (n = 10), diabetes remains significantly associated with CVS failure (OR = 0.002).

Clinically, this is supported by the findings of Hassan [7] and Egawa et al. [19], who attribute this difficulty to chronic inflammation and microvascular impairment related to diabetes. These factors contribute to dense fibrosis and obscure anatomical planes.

Regarding future perspectives, the standardized video documentation and clinical grading in this study have potential that extends beyond immediate risk stratification. While current surgical AI research primarily focuses on algorithm development [11,20], the lack of high-quality, clinically validated data continues to be a significant bottleneck. By rigorously correlating intraoperative visual grading (parkland score) with objective outcomes (CVS), our dataset can serve as a foundational step for future AI applications. Previous studies have highlighted that consistent clinical labeling is a crucial prerequisite for training machine learning models to automate surgical phase recognition and predict surgical progress [11,20,21]. Therefore, this study presents a clinical framework that may enhance the future development of autonomous risk assessment systems for LC.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, it utilized a single-center design with one surgeon. While this approach was intentional to maximize internal validity by eliminating inter-operator variability in parkland grading, it inevitably restricts the external validity (generalizability) of our findings. Consequently, the predictive thresholds identified here (e.g., parkland ≥ 4) may need calibration when applied to surgeons with varying experience levels or in centers with different patient demographics. Second, as a pilot study aimed at establishing ground truth for AI, our sample size of 88 is sufficient to demonstrate proof-of-concept for data labeling, but it is limited for robust multivariate analysis of rare risk factors. This limitation resulted in wide confidence intervals for certain predictors, as seen with diabetes mellitus.
