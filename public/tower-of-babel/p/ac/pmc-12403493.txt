Identification and validation of the cellular senescence-associated molecular pattern and diagnostic markers for osteoporosis
Authors: Tengyan Liu, Jiashuang Fan, Jianyun Fang, Zhuan Qu, Yaxin He, Kai Yang, Jianlin Yang, Juye Zhang, Dan Yang, Lifen Dai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoporosis is an age-related skeletal disorder with an increasing burden of osteoporotic fractures worldwide, it is urgent the identification of reliable molecular characteristics to prevent the progression of severe osteoporosis.MethodsTwo datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and selected the cellular senescence-related genes (SRGs). Introduction

Osteoporosis is a typical age-related skeletal disorder with bone mineral reduction, bone microarchitecture destruction, and a high risk of fragility fractures [1]. Bone mineral density (BMD) T score of −0.25 or less is a diagnostic criterion produced by WHO that is used to define osteoporosis or not [2]. OP is considered a silent epidemic that affects 200 million men and women around the world, vertebral and hip fractures cause increased morbidity and mortality and eventually lead to enormous healthcare costs [3–6]. A cross-sectional study has revealed that the high prevalence of osteoporosis and fracture in China, the similar prevalence of vertebral fracture among women and men, as well as the prevalence of vertebral at older ager higher than other clinical vertebral [7]. With the aging of the global population, prevention, and management of osteoporotic fragility fractures emerge as important to mitigate the progression of severe osteoporosis. Therefore, it is very necessary and important to identify reliable biomarkers for early diagnosis.

Aging is the single largest risk factor for many chronic diseases, osteoporosis is a canonical aging-related endocrine disease [8, 9]. Genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, and altered intercellular communication are the hallmarks of aging [10]. Cellular senescence is considered a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest and distinct cellular phenotypic alterations [11]. Accumulation of cellular senescence is one of the age-related changes of osteoporosis and significantly contributes to bone aging [12, 13].

The bone microenvironment is a complex niche that consists of three major cell types, including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and osteocytes, as well as other marrow cells, including hematopoietic precursors, pluripotent mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), human skeletal stem cell (hSSC), unique periosteal stem cell (PSC), and populations of immune cells [14]. Increasing evidence has demonstrated the presence of senescence with aging in multiple cell types in the bone microenvironment, which involve skeletal cells, marrow cells, and immune cells [14–16]. Cell senescence plays an important role in mediating age-related bone loss and targeting these cells could prevent age-related bone loss [14, 16]. Additionally, several peripheral blood biomarkers have been proposed for the diagnosis and treatment of OP [17, 18]. Notably, inconsistencies between peripheral blood and bone marrow markers underscore the need for further investigation to better understand their biological significance and clinical utility[19]. Despite advances in blood-based diagnostics, studies investigating senescence-related biomarkers in peripheral blood for OP are still lacking. The potential value of identifying cellular senescence signatures in blood lies in their ability to reflect systemic aging-related alterations that may contribute to bone degeneration. To address this gap, our study aimed to identify and validate senescence-associated molecular patterns in peripheral blood that could serve as novel diagnostic markers for OP.

The previous study highlights that Fatty Acid Binding Protein 3 (FABP3) contributes to cellular senescence by regulating the bone-fat balance during aging through targeting optineurin (OPTN), which plays a critical role in the senescence of MSCs [20]. Furthermore, high temperature requirement A 1 (HTRA1) and Nuclear protein 1 (NUPR1) have been identified as critical senescence-related genes (SRGs) that promote bone loss in aging mice [21]. Despite these findings, the majority of SRGs and their underlying molecular mechanisms involved in osteoporosis progression remain poorly understood. In particular, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding the role and clinical utility of peripheral blood–based senescence markers in OP.

In the present study, we comprehensively identified the SRGs associated with osteoporosis progression and investigated in peripheral blood, aiming to uncover key molecular players involved in systemic aging processes contributing to bone loss. We further investigated the association between cellular senescence-related molecular patterns and immune cell characteristics in peripheral blood, as immune dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a contributor to age-related osteoporosis. To pinpoint the most informative diagnostic markers, we constructed two machine learning models to select candidate SRGs with high predictive value for OP, which enabled the reduction of dimensionality and minimization of overfitting while ensuring robustness. Finally, we validated the expression of these candidate genes using clinical peripheral blood samples to confirm their potential clinical applicability and diagnostic relevance. Together, our study provides a systematic characterization of senescence-associated molecular features in OP and highlights peripheral blood-based senescence markers as promising non-invasive diagnostic tools.

Materials and methods

Data downloaded and processing

Gene expression profiling of OP patients was obtained from the GSE56815 and GSE62402 datasets by accessing the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). In the GSE56815 dataset, the transcriptomic data of blood monocytes in 40 high bone mineral density (high-BMD, 20 pre- and 20 postmenopausal) and 40 low-BMD (20 pre- and 20 postmenopausal) subjects were generated by Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array. The transcriptomic data of blood monocytes in 5 high-BMD and 5 low-BMD subjects of the GSE62402 dataset was generated by Affymetrix Human Exon 1.0 ST Array [transcript (gene) version].

A total of 278 cell senescence-related genes (SRGs, Table S1) were downloaded from the CellAge: The Database of Cell Senescence Genes (CellAge: The Cell Senescence Gene Database).

The gene markers of 28 immune cells were downloaded from the TISIDB: an integrated repository portal for tumor-immune system interactions (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/download.php).

Identified SRGs in OP

Consensus clustering

The “ConsensusClusterPlus” package was performed to quantitatively estimate the number of unsupervised classes across 40 OP patients with low BMD based on DE-SRGs. The optimal number of clusters was confirmed by implementing a consensus matrix plot, consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot, relative alterations in the area under the CDF curve, and a tracking plot. T-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) was performed to evaluate the classification performance based on gene expression profiles of DE-SRGs, and the t-SNE plot was drawn using the “ggplot2” package.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

The “limma” package was utilized to screen for DEGs between distinct SRG-related patterns with the criterion of |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.5 and p-value

Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA)

SsGSEA was used to evaluate the immune cell infiltration in OP samples according to the gene markers of 28 immune cells. The samples were ranked based on the gene expression levels in the samples. Then, the genes in the input gene set (immune gene set) were searched and their expression levels were summed. GSVA score of these gene sets was obtained to evaluate the abundance of immune cells in each sample. The distinct immune cell types were determined by the Wilcoxon test. Moreover, we also evaluated the expression levels of cytokines between distinct SRG-related patterns by the Wilcoxon test.

Feature selection and model evaluation

Random Forest (RF) and support vector machine–recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) models were used to select the characteristic genes to construct a training model for the prediction of the occurrence of OP. The GSE56815 dataset was randomly divided into a training set for model development (7/10, 28 high-BMD, and 28 low-BMD) and a test set for model validation (3/10,12 high-BMD, and 12 low-BMD). The “RandomForest” package was performed to construct an RF model with ntrees = 800 and mtry = 12 to select candidate SRGs among the DE-SRGs for the occurrence of OP predicting. The candidate genes were selected based on the 10-fold cross-validation, and the accuracy of the model also been evaluated by the 10-fold cross-validation curves. SVM-RFE is a supervised machine learning algorithm to select relevant characteristics and remove redundant characteristics. The “e1071” package was used to perform SVM-RFE to apply feature selection via ten-fold cross-validation. The key SRGs for the prediction of the occurrence of OP were obtained by overlapping the candidate SRGs from RF and SVM-RFE models, respectively. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) were used for estimating the diagnostic efficacy based on the training set, test set, and external validation set (GSE62402 dataset). ROC curves were drawn using the “pROC” package.

Construction of a nomogram

A nomogram model was developed using an “rms” package based on the selected candidate SRGs to predict the prevalence of OP patients. The calibration curve was used to assess the accuracy of the model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was drawn using the “rmda” package to evaluate the performance of the model. The clinical impact curve was plotted using the “rmda” package to evaluate whether decisions based on the model were beneficial to the patient.

Patients and specimens

Peripheral blood of twenty-seven clinical osteoporosis patients and twenty-seven healthy adults was obtained from the Fuwai Yunnan Cardiovascular Hospital. All procedures of this study are in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed content was obtained from every participant before the study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Yunnan Cardiovascular Hospital (The ethical review number: IRB2017-BG-028).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis

5mL peripheral venous blood was collected with an EDTA-K2 anticoagulant blood collection tube. The Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs) from each sample were isolated by using density gradient centrifugation at 1500 r/min for 15 minutes. Total RNA was extracted from PBMC samples using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using an All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qPCR was performed using an TB Green Fast qPCR Mix (Takara, Dalian, China) and an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The qRT-PCR results were analyzed with the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was performed in triplicate to obtain the average value. Primer sequences of the genes analyzed were as follows: PDPK1: forward primer (5’-CAGCAACATAGAGCAGTA-3’); reverse primer (5’-CTTCTCCAACAACAACCT-3’). TRIM28: forward primer (5’-CTGGAGAGTGACAACAAC-3’); reverse primer (5’-ATCCACAATCATCTTGAGG-3’). WWP1: forward primer (5’-AGAAGGACTTGATTATGGT-3’); reverse primer (5’-TAATGGTTGATGCTGGAT-3’). GAPDH: forward primer (5’-TTGCCCTCAACGACCACTTT-3’); reverse primer (5’-TGGTCCAGGGGTCTTACTCC-3’). The unpaired t-test was used to compare the mean values of two groups of samples. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Identification of two cellular senescence-related molecular clusters in OP

Based on the expression profiles of 50 DE-SRGs in OP, 40 OP patients with low-BMD were stratified into two distinct clusters (cluster 1, n = 25; cluster 2, n = 15, Fig. 3A, Figure S1). Screening of DEGs and their functional enrichment between two cellular senescence-related molecular clusters

We further investigated the significance of the senescence-related molecular clusters in OP. A total of 110 DEGs (94 upregulated and 16 downregulated) were identified between two clusters (Fig. 4A, Table S3), these DEGs were significantly different expressions between the two clusters (Fig. 4B). Then, we explored the potential molecular mechanisms of these DEGs in OP. As shown in Fig. 4C, we found these genes were mainly enriched in immune-related biological functions, such as neutrophil degranulation, neutrophil activation involved in immune response, neutrophil-mediated immunity, neutrophil activation, and myeloid cell differentiation, etc. (Table S4). Moreover, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were significantly associated with osteoclast differentiation, Rap1 signaling pathway, platelet activation, chemokine signaling pathway, focal adhesion, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and B cell receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 4D, Table S5).

Immune landscape in two cellular senescence-related molecular clusters

Based on the correlation between SRGs and immune-related biological functions, we then evaluated the abundance of immune cells in two clusters using ssGSEA. As shown in Fig. 5A, we found the abundance of immune cells enriched in two clusters, central memory CD8 T cell, T follicular helper cell, type 1 T helper cell, regulatory T cell, activated dendritic cell, and monocyte were increased, but effector memory CD4 T cell, type 2 T helper cell, CD56bright natural killer cell were decreased in cluster 2 than cluster 1 (Fig. 5B). We also found the distinct expression of cytokines between two clusters, resulting in CCL3, CCL11, CTSB PLAU, TIMP2, PLAUR, and TNFRSF1A being upregulated, but IL1A and PIGF were decreased in cluster 2 compared with cluster 1 (Fig. 5C).

Identification and validation of a cellular senescence-related diagnostic gene signature for OP

RF and SVM-RFE algorithms were applied for feature selection among 50 DE-SRGs based on the training set (7/10 samples in the GSE56815 dataset). The number of random forest trees with the minimum error of the RF model was 800 (Fig. 6A-B), 10 DE-SRGs (PDPK1, WWP1, TRIM28, TP53, ITPKB, MDH1, SYK, MAP2K3, MCRS1, and ALOX15B) were selected and validated as the candidate SRGs based on the 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 6C-E). And 11 DE-SRGs (TRIM28, MCL1, NOTCH3, TP63, GRK6, PDPK1, WWP1, HK3, LGALS3, HDAC4, and CTNNAL1) were selected as candidate SRGs using SVM-RFE with minimum error (Fig. 6F-G). Moreover, reverse cumulative distribution of residual and boxplots of residual were used to evaluate the ability of RF and SVM-RFE models, resulting in both RF and SVM-RFE having minimal residuals (Fig. 6H), indicating that RF and SVM-RFE models were considered as the best models to predict the occurrence of OP. ROC curves also demonstrated that RF and SVM-RFE models have high accuracy for predicting OP occurrence (Fig. 6I-J). Therefore, three intersected SRGs (PDPK1, TRIM28, and WWP1) were selected as the key genes (Fig. 6K). The predictive ability of PDPK1, TRIM28, and WWP1 was confirmed by ROC curves (Fig. 6L-N) in the training set (7/10 samples in the GSE56815 dataset), test set (3/10 samples in the GSE56815 dataset), and external validation set (GSE62402 dataset).

Discussion

The aging of the population is associated with an increasing burden of osteoporotic fractures worldwide [7, 22, 23]. Nowadays, the diagnosis of osteoporosis is based on adequate clinical evaluation, imaging, and laboratory tests, such as conventional radiology to evaluate spinal fractures, BMD testing, magnetic resonance imaging, bone scintigraphy, and ultrasound [24]. In other words, due to the lack of reliable early diagnostic options for osteoporosis patients, the current diagnosis is based on the presence of osteoporotic features. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find potential biomarkers that can be used for early diagnosis. Senescence is a complex biological process that has a significant impact on osteoporotic progression [25]. Increasing evidence indicated that accumulation of the senescent cells in aging bone is the leading cause leads to osteoporosis, suppressing the senescence-associated secretory phenotype and targeting the senescent cells might be a treatment option for aging-osteoporosis [14, 26–29]. However, the specific biomarkers and therapeutic mechanisms of the cellular senescence-associated phenotype in OP remain unclear and require further exploration.

In the present study, we screened out 50 DE-SRGs associated with osteoporosis. Then, we found osteoporosis patients were divided into two distinct cellular senescence-related molecular clusters based on the differential expression of 50 DE-SRGs. The two clusters were mainly enriched in osteoclast differentiation, Rap1 signaling pathway, platelet activation, chemokine signaling pathway, focal adhesion, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and B cell receptor signaling pathway. And the distinct abundances of immune cells between two clusters, central memory CD8 T cell, T follicular helper cell, type 1 T helper cell, regulatory T cell, activated dendritic cell, and monocyte were increased, but effector memory CD4 T cell, type 2 T helper cell, CD56bright natural killer cell were decreased, as well as CCL3, CCL11, CTSB PLAU, TIMP2, PLAUR, and TNFRSF1A were upregulated, but IL1A and PIGF were decreased in cluster 2 compared with cluster 1. On the other hand, we also selected the three diagnostic markers (PDPK1, TRIM28, and WWP1) from the 50 DE-SRGs based on RF and SVM-RFE algorithms. Both in the training set, test set, and validation set, we constructed and validated a predictive nomogram based on accuracy and sensitivity to osteoporosis prediction.

Previous studies have demonstrated that osteoporosis exhibits the chronic low-grade inflammatory phenotype with changes in immune cell status and cytokine expression by immune cell-autonomous and paracrine effects [30]. B cells and T cells play important roles in pathological bone loss [31]. B cells are a major regulator of osteoclast formation and differentiation [32]. Regulatory T cells can suppress osteoclast differentiation [33], and T helper cells are mainly involved in promoting osteoclastogenesis [34], the balance of the T helper cells/regulatory T cells is key for osteoporotic progression [35]. Moreover, the Rap1 signaling pathway and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway are also involved in the cross-talk between bone and the immune system by regulating the cells in the bone microenvironment, including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), macrophages, T- and B-lymphocytes, dendritic cells, and neutrophils [36]. Activation of the Rap1/MAPK signaling pathway promotes osteoblast proliferation and differentiation in OP rats [37]. Inhibition of the NF-κB-activated NOD-like receptor signaling pathway ameliorates osteoclastogenesis and bone loss in ovariectomized (OVX) mice [38]. The above evidence indicates that SRGs are involved in the regulation of the osteoporotic progression by modulating the immune cells and non-immune cells crosstalk in the bone microenvironment, which demonstrates that cellular senescence regulates osteoporosis through both cell-autonomous and paracrine effects on the bone microenvironment.

Our previous analyses have dissected that cellular senescence plays a crucial role in osteoporotic initiation, development, and immune modulation, then the cellular senescence-associated diagnostic markers of OP were selected. The 3-phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1 (PDK1 or PDPK1), the master upstream lipid kinase of the PI3K-Akt cascade, acts as an important role in phosphorylation and activation of protein kinases A, B, and C (AGC) family kinases, including Akt, to activate downstream effectors [39]. PDK1/PDPK1 acts as a crucial role in the malignant biological behaviors of multiple cancer types [40]. A recent study indicates that PDK1/PDPK1 is involved in osteoporosis, which functions an important role in RANKL-induced osteoclast formation by activating the Akt-GSK3β-NFATc1 signaling pathway [41]. Our results consistently this finding, upregulation of PDK1/PDPK1 in osteoporosis. The tripartite motif-containing protein 28 (TRIM28) is a transcription corepressor, also known as Kruppel-associated box-associated protein 1 (KAP1) or transcriptional intermediary factor 1β (TIF1β) [42], that interacts with histone deacetylase and methyltransferase complexes [43]. TRIM28 plays an important role in regulating endogenous retroelements during early embryonic development [44, 45]. A vitro study has demonstrated that the knockdown of TRIM28 inhibits adipogenesis [46], remaindering that TRIM28 may influence the balance of osteoblast/adipocyte by influencing adipogenesis. WW domain-containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (WWP1) is a multifunction protein, containing an N-terminal C2 domain, four tandem WW domains for substrate binding, and a C-terminal catalytic HECT domain, that plays a crucial role in ubiquitin transferring [47]. WWP1 widely implicates several diseases, such as cancers [48–50], infectious diseases [51, 52], neurological diseases [53, 54], and aging [55–57]. It has been demonstrated that targeting WWP1 promotes human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) differentiates into osteoblasts [58], and disruption of the complex between WWP1 and its adapter Schnurri-3 can increase the bone deposition in osteoporosis patients [59]. A recent study also demonstrated that WWP1 acts as a potential diagnostic biomarker in osteoporosis and is downregulated in postmenopausal osteoporosis patients [60].

Our study represents the first comprehensive characterization of cellular senescence-related molecular features in osteoporosis, identifying three diagnostic biomarkers (PDPK1, TRIM28, and WWP1) that critically regulate the dynamic equilibrium between osteoclast/osteoblast and adipocyte/osteoblast differentiation within the bone microenvironment. Despite these significant findings, several limitations warrant consideration. First, potential inter-dataset heterogeneity that may affect model robustness. Second, constrained sample size in our clinical cohort, necessitating validation through larger, more diverse population studies to ensure broader applicability. Third, inherent methodological constraints associated with bioinformatics approaches, including risks of overfitting and algorithm-dependent biases. In addition, this study focused on identifying senescence-related subtypes in OP, rather than stratifying patients based on disease severity. Furthermore, it is important to note that the publicly available datasets used in this study were generated from isolated peripheral blood monocytes, while our validation experiments were conducted on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), which include both monocytes and lymphocytes. This difference in cell composition may influence gene expression profiles and affect comparability between datasets and validation results. Senescence-related markers identified in purified monocytes might be diluted or altered in the mixed PBMC population, potentially impacting sensitivity and specificity. Although PBMCs better represent the systemic immune environment and are easier to isolate, future studies focusing on purified cell subsets are needed to clarify cell-type-specific roles of these markers in osteoporosis. Despite this limitations, our work highlights the transformative potential of multi-omics integration in advancing mechanistic insights and facilitating clinical translation. Future studies should consider stratifying patients according to OP severity to better elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying disease progression.

Conclusion

Our study provides a comprehensive landscape of cellular senescence in osteoporosis and identifies as well as validates senescence-related gene signatures with potential diagnostic value. These findings offer novel insights into the molecular underpinnings of osteoporosis and suggest promising peripheral blood-based biomarkers that could serve as both diagnostic and therapeutic targets. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the diagnostic signatures were validated in both public datasets and a clinical cohort, further validation in larger, independent external cohorts is necessary to fully assess their robustness and generalizability. Additionally, functional experiments are warranted to elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms and biological roles of these genes in osteoporosis pathogenesis. Future efforts should also explore the feasibility of translating these transcriptomic markers into practical diagnostic tools and compare their performance with existing serum-based biomarkers.
