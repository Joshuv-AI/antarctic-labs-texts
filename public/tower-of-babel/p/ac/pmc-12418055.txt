A Survey Study on the Roles, Responsibilities and Workforce Characteristics of Radiation Oncology Nurses in Australia
Authors: Michelle Roach, Pauline Rose, Diana Ngo, Anisha Pradhan, Eliza Bailey, Sylvia Barker, Stacey Buxton, Meg Hughes, Karen Jones, Paula Macleod, Kirsten Oataway, Gabrielle Vigar, Gemma McErlean
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionRadiation Oncology Nurses (RONs) are key members of the radiation oncology team; however, the role of the RON is not always clear. The aims of this study were to provide an overview of the RON workforce in Australia, to describe the roles and responsibilities of RONs, and to identify challenges and job satisfaction of RONs.MethodsThis was a two‐part multi‐methods survey study. Two self‐reported questionnaires were distributed online using purposive and snowball sampling: Radiation Oncology Facility Survey (ROFS) and RON Workforce Survey (RONWS). The surveys used tick boxes, Likert scale and open and closed‐ended questions. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables; mean/standard deviation for continuous variables.ResultsA total of 42 responses were received for the ROFS and 139 for the RONWS, representing all States and Territories in Australia except the Australian Capital Territory (ACT): 52% were metropolitan and 50% were public facilities. Only 17% reported radiation oncology specific training, but 58% had plans to further their education. 80% reported being satisfied with their career and position, believing their role was recognised and generally respected among colleagues and patients. Areas of dissatisfaction included salary, staffing resources and opportunities for advancement.ConclusionThis is the largest study of RONs in Australia, revealing that RON is a satisfying oncology specialty. Data from this study can be used to identify opportunities for improvement in staffing, engagement and retention of RONs, possibly leading to the development of national guidelines for radiation oncology nursing in Australia.Keywords: job satisfaction, radiation oncology nursing, radiation oncology nursing education, roles and responsibilities, workforce

Introduction

Radiation oncology nurses (RON) play a crucial role in supporting patients with cancer undergoing radiation therapy. Their responsibilities span the entire treatment journey, from initial consultation, treatment planning, on‐treatment assessments, telephone triage, to follow‐up care [1]. RON roles encompass a range of nursing levels, including Advanced Practice Nurses, Clinical Nurses, Registered Nurses (RNs) and Enrolled Nurses (ENs) [2]. However, despite their role in patient care, there is considerable ambiguity about the day‐to‐day work of RONs, as well as limited information about their job satisfaction, role clarity and role autonomy [3, 4, 5]. While research on RON roles has been conducted internationally [6, 7] studies specific to the Australian context remain scarce [2, 8, 9, 10, 11]. This lack of Australia‐specific data hinders our understanding of the unique challenges and opportunities faced by RONs in this country.

The Australian Department of Health and Ageing (2009) conducted a Radiation Oncology Workforce Planning Study in Australia [12]. This and other Australian workforce studies [13] collected information on radiation oncology professionals and service provision, focusing on the ‘Radiation Tripartite’—Radiation Oncologists (ROs), Radiation Therapists (RTs) and Radiation Oncology Medical Physicists (ROMPs). The role of RONs, though acknowledged, was not explored. Over time, recognition of RONs' value to radiotherapy patient care has grown. In 2019, RONs, sponsored by the Cancer Nursing Society of Australia (CNSA), were formally recognised as one of the key professionals in radiation oncology. The Tripartite thus became the Radiation Oncology Alliance [14]. But, despite this recognition, there remains a stark contrast between the wealth of workforce reports, staffing models and standards available for the other three radiation oncology professions and the paucity of such information for RONs.

In 2015 ‘Practice guidelines for the care of patients undergoing radiation therapy’ was published in Australia [15], yet the RON role has not been clearly defined across the country, and capability statements are lacking. Historically, RON practice has evolved based on anecdotal staffing models and the number of departmental technologies and patients treated [9, 16] rather than on evidence‐based standards. Given these gaps in knowledge and standardisation, the Radiation Oncology Specialist Practice Network (RONSPN) of CNSA identified the need for a study of the RON workforce in Australia to provide a foundation for future improvements in RON practice and patient care.

Aims of the Study

This study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the current RON workforce in Australia. Objectives include (1) documenting the roles and responsibilities of RONs, (2) identifying issues, needs, concerns and challenges faced by RONs in their workplaces, and (3) understanding RON attitudes toward work practices, job satisfaction, educational opportunities and career development.

Methods

Survey Design and Instruments

This was a multi‐methods study undertaken between August and November 2022 using two online cross‐sectional surveys: the Radiation Oncology Facility Survey (ROFS) and the Radiation Oncology Nursing Workforce Survey (RONWS). Both surveys included quantitative and qualitative questions, were anonymous and voluntary, and survey completion was considered an indication of implied consent. The ROFS survey included 37 questions about equipment, services and RON staffing in radiation oncology facilities. The RONWS used 32 questions to collect demographic data about the RONs, their current roles and responsibilities, attitudes toward their job and satisfaction/dissatisfaction with their role (Data S1). These surveys were separate as RONs may not have been able to answer the ROFS, which required managerial/departmental level knowledge. The surveys used tick boxes and Likert scales adapted from previously used, publicly available surveys: the United States Workforce Study: The Radiation Oncologists' and Residents' Perspectives [17], the Australian Workforce Sustainability in Radiation Oncology (WSRO) Survey [18], and the Radiation Oncology Skill Mix and Work Analysis study [19]. Author permission was sought to use specific parts of these surveys, and modifications were made to make the questions relevant to nurses rather than radiation oncologists, physicists and radiation therapists. This modified survey was then pilot tested with 10 RONs to assess face and content validity. The ROFS took approximately 15 min to complete while the RONWS survey took approximately 10 min to complete. No revisions to the surveys were required after pilot testing.

Sampling

Surveys were sent out via email with a link to REDCap—an electronic data capture tool (project‐redcap.org). To increase the response rate, nursing managers received three follow‐up emails at two‐week intervals from the RONSPN research team. No further contact was made beyond this point. For the ROFS, all Radiation Oncology Facilities operating in Australia were eligible, while for the RONWS, all RONS of all levels working in these facilities could participate. The study employed purposive and snowball sampling techniques. Facilities were identified through publicly available data sources [20]. At the time of survey distribution, there were 100 operational radiation oncology facilities in Australia, 40 of which were public, with the remainder being private facilities. The investigators utilised their RON networks to distribute the ROFS to Nurse Unit Managers (NUM) or departmental managers. These managers were then asked to distribute the RONWS to their RON staff. Only RONs could complete the RONWS, but all departmental managers could complete the ROFS regardless of whether or not they were RONs. If managers were also RONs, they were asked to complete both surveys. Unfortunately, there is no national‐level census data available for the number of RONs in Australia.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using Jamovi v2.3 (www.jamovi.org). Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical and continuous variables. Three sets of Likert scale responses were analysed, with responses 1&2 and 4&5 merged. Nursing roles were grouped into Senior Nurse (N = 29), Clinical Nurse (N = 22), RN (N = 77) and EN (N = 11) categories. As the ROFS and the RONWS utilised separate surveys, they have been analysed separately; although some managers who are RONs may have completed both surveys.

Qualitative data resulting from this study will be analysed using thematic analysis; however, it will be reported elsewhere due to the large amount of data and word constraints.

Results

Radiation Oncology Facility Surveys (ROFS)

Of the 100 operational radiation oncology facilities in Australia, 30 facilities declined to participate due to workforce issues. Out of the remaining 70 facilities, 42 (60%) responded to the survey, representing all states in Australia and the Northern Territory. Public facilities accounted for 51%; 38% were private, 9% public/private and 2% private not for profit.

The majority of RON nursing managers were Australian, female, aged 41–50 and working full‐time as NUMs for over 2 years. While 35% had postgraduate qualifications, only 14% had received specialised training in radiation oncology (Table 1).

All departments reported being equipped with advanced radiation treatment technologies and treated most types of cancer. The number of patients treated daily varied depending on the availability of treatment machines and techniques in each department. Only four centres (9.5%) treated paediatric patients, usually treating up to three patients per day. Palliative treatments ranged from 0 to 50 patients daily in the departments, while same day ‘Plan and Treat’ services, where a patient can be planned or simulated for treatment and treated on the same day, are offered to 0–10 palliative patients daily on average. Additionally, the number of patients for radiation treatment planning with CT/MRI scans ranged between 1 and 20 per day per department. Operating hours were reported for 42 facilities: 57% (n = 24) opened at 8 am, 21% (n = 9) opened at 7.30 am and another 21% (n = 9) opened at 7 am. One facility reported opening at 6 am on specific days of the week. Regarding closing times, most facilities (61%) closed at 5 pm (n = 17) or 5.30 pm (n = 9), with the latest reported closing time being 9 pm (n = 1).

Radiation Oncology Nursing Workforce Staffing

Forty‐two facilities reported a total of 214 RONs nationwide: 66 RONs employed full‐time, 148 employed part‐time, and 38 casual staff, with an average of 3.32 nurses rostered per shift. Ninety percent (90%) of respondents reported having nursing cover for all operating hours, while 40% of facilities reported RONs working on‐call (emergency treatment) rosters. Regarding interdepartmental rotation, 36% of respondents reported that RONs rotate across departments within cancer services, with the majority rotating into the Chemotherapy Unit (67%) and Medical Oncology Day Ward/Unit (53%).

A majority (74%) of respondents reported having an evidence‐based guideline or model of care for RON practice. Of the 41 facilities, 11 (26%) reported operating nurse‐led clinics, on an average of 22.3 h per week (range 4–40 h), supporting an average of 33.3 patients per week (range 6–100 patients). These clinics were primarily conducted in Outpatient Departments/Clinics (64%) and the majority were run by RNs (73%). Notably, 10 of the 11 facilities identified the weekly nursing assessment, which is considered part of ‘standard care’, as ‘nurse‐led clinics’.

Radiation Oncology Nursing Workforce Survey

In total, 139 RONs responded to the workforce survey, representing all States and the Australian Capital Territory (ACT). Since the workforce survey was distributed indirectly by senior nurses in the departments and a large percentage did not participate, data on the total numbers of RONs was unavailable; thus, it was not possible to calculate the response rate.

Demographics of Respondents to RONWS

Demographic characteristics of RONWS respondents reveal that the majority were female (93%), RNs (55%) and worked part time (69%). Thirty‐seven percent reported being in their current position for more than 2 years and were aged 41 to 50 years old (29%). The most frequent highest level of education for respondents was a Bachelor of Nursing degree (40%), while the remaining 60% had a range of levels of education from certificate through to a master's degree. Twenty‐four respondents (17%) reported having received specialised online radiation oncology education via eviQ (www.eviq.org.au) and on‐site job training; and 58% of all respondents planned to further their education in their specialist field. Regarding work settings, 60% worked in metropolitan departments, with 58% in public facilities and 9% in private facilities (Table 1).

Current Roles and Responsibilities of RONs

Work activities performed by RONS were listed under three categories: Direct Care Activities (DCA), Indirect Nursing Activities (INA) and Unit Related Activities (URA).

Direct Nursing Care Activities (DCAs)

Most RONS provide patient education, manage toxicities/symptoms, perform/assess observations, manage skin care/dressings and provide discharge interventions. Senior nurses generally assess medication needs, while EN/Endorsed Enrolled Nurses (EENs) generally perform departmental tasks such as bladder scans, administer medication and escort patients to wards for admission (Table 2).

Unit Related Activities (URAs)

The most common URAs performed by nurses were the daily checks of the emergency trolley and glucometer, cleaning and restocking equipment and preparing paperwork for the following day (Table 4), with some variations across different groups. For instance, most RNs reported checking the equipment daily, such as the emergency trolley and checking drugs, while ENs/EENs mostly performed cleaning duties and restocking equipment and were less involved in preparing paperwork for the following day. These tasks mainly involved cleaning, stock management and restocking Radiation Oncologist's consultation rooms, and 48% of respondents reported that some non‐nursing tasks could be done by other categories of staff.

Educational Opportunities and Career Development

The majority (70%) of RONs reported that their departments provide regular education to nursing staff, with 14 facilities (33%) having a dedicated educator. Educational opportunities were widely available: 95% of nurses could attend radiation therapists' in‐services if interested, 79% had opportunities to attend educational activities, and 75% received support to attend conferences. Half of the RONs (50%) reported attending MDT meetings. Education was primarily conducted by NUMs or senior RONs. However, disparities were evident, particularly for EN/EENs, with only 45% reporting support for education and conferences. Most RONs indicated that opportunities for education and growth were moderate for senior staff but lower for the EN/EEN group.

Attitudes and Job Satisfaction

RONs across all levels reported high levels of job satisfaction, finding their work rewarding and experiencing a strong sense of accomplishment. They also held their colleagues in high regard. Workforce issues such as burnout, frustration with daily duties and employee turnover were reported as low across all nursing levels (Table 5). Career satisfaction varied among different RON categories: CNs and RNs were highly satisfied (95% and 84% respectively), while EN/EENs and senior RONs were moderately satisfied (72% and 69% respectively). Overall satisfaction with a career as a RON was high (> 80%). The majority of respondents believed that RON is valued within the MDT as perceived by RNs (62%) and EN/EENs (91%). The NUM group reported being often unable to complete their duties within the paid time, while RNs and EEN/ENs were moderately able to do so. However, approximately 61% of the respondents agreed that they could finish their job to their satisfaction within the paid time available. Most RONs felt respected by others in the workplace (54%) and the community (59%). Notably, RONs perceived their work to be highly valued by patients (95%), with most reporting radiation oncology as a satisfying specialty (91%), giving them a sense of pride in being able to support patients during their treatment (86%) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study is the first of its kind in Australia to investigate the role of RONs nationally. It revealed valuable insights into the profession, including identifying important workforce themes such as RONs enjoying the variety in workload and their high satisfaction with their work. Overall, the study results underscore the dedication and resilience of RONs, while also identifying areas for growth and development in education and role recognition, which can further enhance RON within the multidisciplinary oncology team [16].

The fluctuating workloads reported in this study may result from changes in patient acuity caused by increased numbers of new patients and palliative patients, emergent adverse patient conditions, medical emergencies and urgent admissions [21]. There may also be higher numbers of palliative or ‘Plan and Treat’ patients, and emergency ‘add‐ons’ that frequently occur. There were also variations in the activities performed by RONs across the various nursing levels, and nearly half (47%) of respondents reported too much administration work and cleaning duties involved in their daily duties that could be allocated to other departmental staff [22]. A quarter of departments reported running a nurse‐led clinic. However, the definition of this type of nursing intervention may be variable, including a review clinic by an advanced practice nurse or the standard ‘on treatment’ reviews that occur as part of standard nursing care [23, 24, 25].

However, despite some participants reporting being under‐resourced and having to undertake many non‐nursing duties, they reported positive perceptions of the role and satisfaction with their work. The RONs reported aiming to build trusting relationships with patients/families that may support their treatment experience. The ability to positively impact patients' lives, spend time with them to build trusting relationships, and advocate on their behalf emerged as important components of the RON role. RONs demonstrate adaptability by rotating across other cancer service areas as needed, showcasing their diverse skill set and potential for broader impact in patient care.

The reported need for specific radiation oncology post‐graduate education aligns with Tapsall's [24] assertion that ongoing leadership and specialist training are crucial for delivering effective and efficient care models. In previous research, it was found that 97% of nurses ‘learned via on‐the‐job through clinical experience’, which highlights the practical, hands‐on nature of the profession and the potential for developing structured mentorship programs [25, 26, 27].

However, this highlights opportunities for improvement in staffing models, facility planning and executive‐level recognition of RON as important for continuity of patient care during radiation therapy. Toxicities worsen with increased radiation dose across the course of radiation; an important aim of RON is to reduce episodes of hospitalisation and minimise treatment breaks [2]. Although some non‐nursing tasks were reported, this presents an opportunity for nursing management to optimise role definitions and enhance direct patient care time [16]. The development of staffing algorithms for RON in the radiation oncology context is work that needs to be ongoing in Australia. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) has provided a staffing model for one Linear Accelerator plus Brachytherapy Unit, which can be extrapolated to other departments [28]. Business models across the public and private sectors in Australia may have specific staffing models for RONs. It is difficult to compare with international RON roles as there are so many differences, including between high‐income and low‐income countries, and by models of practice such as in Scandinavian countries where the nurses are the radiation therapists [29]. Much of what we know in RON in Australia today is based on the early leadership from the USA, particularly in the 1990s [30].

Limitations

One of the biggest limitations of this study was that a large private radiation oncology provider chose not to participate due to staffing issues, which meant that only 50% of the private sector was included. Another limitation was the classification of nursing roles nationally, where it became clear that each state in Australia has different terms for nursing designations, leading to possible misinterpretation of some responses. A further limitation was that not all department managers were nurses, or some may have been nurses with dual responsibilities for medical oncology and radiation oncology departments, possibly resulting in a lack of investment in the survey. These issues may have contributed to a lower response rate, making it difficult to calculate the actual numbers of RONs in Australia and ultimately affecting the generalisability of the study.

Future Directions

This study revealed RON to be a generally satisfying nursing specialty, which may be encouraging for new nurses considering advancing their careers in this field. In areas of limited RON staffing, it appears that a range of tasks could be undertaken by other departmental staff, such as administration and cleaning staff. This would allow nurses to spend available time providing more direct patient care, which is a consideration for Nursing Executives. When addressing the training needs of the current RON workforce, it would be beneficial for higher education institutions to consider developing post‐graduate radiation oncology‐specific nursing education programmes to meet the gaps identified in this study and to complement departmental in‐services and online platforms like eviQ [26].

Future research should consider undertaking a similar study using a different recruitment method that might provide more information on the numbers of RONs nationally and also determine any changes in attitudes and perception of the RON role in the Australian context. The data from this study may be used to lead to the development of a national best practice Model of Care/Nursing Guidelines/Standards for RON practice across Australia as the current state of RON practice in Australia lacks standardisation.

Conclusion

In summary, this is the largest study of the RON workforce in Australia and has provided previously unexplored insights into the roles and responsibilities of RONs and their attitudes to their practice. The results of this study may be used to identify opportunities for improvement in staffing for increasingly complex activity in radiation oncology and to develop national guidelines and standards for RON, including staffing, across Australia, which are currently missing.

Radiation therapy is a dynamic specialty. As RON practices change and evolve aligned with rapidly changing radiation technologies and techniques, there will be an increasing need for interprofessional collaboration and education across the whole radiation team. Cooperative relationships with the ROs, RTs, ROMPs and Allied Health professionals will improve knowledge‐sharing, ensuring each member of the team is able to effectively and safely provide patient care and treatment across the course of the radiation therapy.
