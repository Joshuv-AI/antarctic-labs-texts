The effect of work life quality on job satisfaction in critical care nurses
Authors: Şule Olgun, Cansu Hazal Yanardağ, Zeynep Kılıç, Derya Gerçeker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe objective of this research was to explore how nurses’ perceptions of work-life quality influence their job satisfaction within intensive care settings. The study also aimed to determine the effect of work life quality on job satisfaction of critical care nurses.MethodsThis investigation utilized a descriptive, cross-sectional design. Participants included 136 intensive care nurses employed across public and private hospitals in İzmir between March and July 2024. Data were gathered through structured face-to-face questionnaires, which incorporated three validated instruments: “The Nurse Information Form”, “The Nursing Work Life Quality Scale”, and “The Minnesota Job Satisfaction Questionnaire”. Statistical analyses were conducted using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, independent samples t-tests, and one-way ANOVA to identify both relational patterns and group-level distinctions.ResultsThe analysis revealed a robust and statistically significant positive association between the quality of work life and job satisfaction (r = 0.717, p < 0.001). Job satisfaction levels differed significantly by gender and parental status (p < 0.05); however, no comparable variation was observed in work-life quality across these factors. Additionally, marital status, educational background, and type of intensive care unit demonstrated no significant influence on either variable (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe results clearly illustrate that the perceived quality of work life is a crucial determinant of job satisfaction among nurses in critical care units. Strategic interventions aimed at improving working conditions could substantially enhance motivation, professional commitment, and staff retention, thereby strengthening both individual well-being and institutional effectiveness within healthcare organizations.Keywords: Critical care, Job satisfaction, Quality of work life, Nursing

Introduction

Critical care units within hospitals are specialized departments organized into levels 1, 2, and 3, where nurses deliver continuous, 24-hour care to patients whose conditions require close and constant monitoring. The health conditions of critical care patients, who undergo continuous examinations, treatments, and care throughout the day, are critical and require close monitoring. Mechanical ventilation and circulatory assist systems are employed in intensive care units to deliver both fundamental and advanced life-sustaining care to patients [1]. For this reason, the work of critical care nurses is intense and stressful. The long working hours of critical care nurses, the inadequacy of their salaries relative to their efforts, conflicts with patients and their families, excessive workload in the workplace, conflicts with management, and similar situations negatively affect their quality of life at work [2]. Work-life quality is an essential concept in the nursing profession. It encompasses the overall organizational environment and the policies that affect employees’ professional and personal well-being [3]. The concept broadly encompasses initiatives that enhance employees’ work life by strengthening their physical environment as well as their psychological, emotional, and social well-being [4].

Another crucial factor affecting nurses’ professional performance is job satisfaction, which arises when an employee’s expectations align with the characteristics and conditions of their job [5]. Job satisfaction reflects the degree to which individuals find their work environment mentally and physically rewarding [6]. Job satisfaction is important in every profession, but in the healthcare sector, particularly in nursing, job satisfaction is being undermined by many negative factors. According to the literature, factors such as irregular working hours and days, the use of different technologies that are constantly changing and require working under time pressure, and performing life-threatening tasks and responsibilities while being paid low wages harm job satisfaction among healthcare workers, particularly nurses. The nursing profession, which operates within a multidisciplinary environment characterized by intense stress and pressure, and the lack of professional respect, places nurses in a higher-risk group compared to other occupational groups in terms of job satisfaction [7–10].

Low job satisfaction in nurses leads to physical problems such as migraine, coronary artery disease, and sleep disorders; mental health issues like anxiety, helplessness, depression, and increased alcohol, tobacco, and substance use; professional and family issues like hopelessness, disappointment, decreased work performance, disengagement from work, deteriorating family relationships, and interpersonal communication problems; organizational issues like hidden strikes, work slowdowns, and increased disciplinary problems; and social issues like burnout syndrome [11–14]. A review of the literature revealed that, although many studies have examined work-life quality and job satisfaction separately, research exploring their relationship, especially among critical care nurses, is limited. Considering that these nurses work under intense pressure and in shift-based systems, it is crucial to evaluate their work-life conditions and identify factors that may enhance their well-being and professional engagement. Addressing the scarcity of research in this area, the present study explored the influence of work-life quality on the job satisfaction of nurses serving in critical care units. This study aimed to determine the effect of work life quality on job satisfaction of critical care nurses.

Population and sample

The study population included nurses actively employed in intensive care units at one private and one public hospital located in Izmir, Turkiye, between March and July 2024. The sample size estimation was conducted with the G*Power 3.1 application to obtain a 95% confidence level while maintaining a 5.0% allowable error rate. Considering potential data loss, the study was completed with 136 participants. Data collection was conducted through face-to-face surveys.

The population of this study consists of nurses working in the intensive care units of the hospitals where the research was conducted. A sampling method was used, specifically convenience sampling, a type of non-probability sampling. Within the scope of the study, nurses actively working in intensive care units during the data collection period and meeting the inclusion criteria were contacted. The purpose of the study was explained to the participants, participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained.

Nurses who agreed to participate in the study and completed the data collection instruments within the specified period were included in the sample. Nurses who were on leave, lacked sufficient intensive care experience, or completed the questionnaire incompletely were excluded from the study. The sample size was determined considering the population size and based on the results of the power analysis.

Data collection tools

Data were obtained using three instruments: the Nurse Information Form, “the Nursing Work Life Quality Scale”, and “the Minnesota Job Satisfaction Questionnaire”. These tools were chosen to assess the participants’ demographic and professional characteristics, as well as their perceptions of “work-life quality” and “job satisfaction”.

Research hypothes

H1

There is a statistically significant relationship between the work life quality and job satisfaction levels of intensive care nurses.

Nurse introduction form

Prepared by researchers based on a literature review [1–15]. The form consists of 14 questions covering gender, age, marital status, number of children, educational status, social security, place of residence, income level, place of work, satisfaction with occupation, choice of occupation, willingness to choose the same occupation if given the opportunity, and whether the institution is private or public.

Nursing work life quality scale

Originally developed by Brooks (2001) and adapted into Turkish by Şirin (2011) [9, 10], this scale measures nurses’ perceived work-life quality. The Turkish version contains 35 items divided into five subscales: work environment, relationships with managers, working conditions, job perception, and support services. To collect responses, a five-point Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 for “Strongly Disagree” to 5 for “Strongly Agree,” with reverse coding applied to items 3, 11, 16, and 20. Higher total scores indicate better perceived work-life quality. In previous studies, Cronbach’s α coefficients ranged between 0.83 and 0.89, and in this study, reliability was confirmed with α = 0.891.

Minnesota satisfaction questionnaire

Developed by Weiss et al. (1967) and translated into Turkish by Baycan (1985) [11, 12], this instrument evaluates overall, intrinsic, and extrinsic job satisfaction. It includes 20 items rated on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (“Not at all satisfied”) to 5 (“Very satisfied”). The overall satisfaction score is calculated by averaging the item scores, while subscale scores measure satisfaction related to internal job characteristics (e.g., achievement, recognition, responsibility) and external factors (e.g., work conditions, management, pay). Higher scores indicate greater job satisfaction.

Data analysis

In this study, Jamovi version 2.6.26 was used for statistical analyses. The significance level was set at 5.0% (α = 0.05). The normality assumption was evaluated by examining the skewness and kurtosis coefficients of the variables; since all values fell within the ± 1 range, the distributions were considered normal. Accordingly, parametric methods were preferred in the analyses.

To determine the relationship between variables, the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient was used since the normality assumption was met. In pairwise group comparisons, the Levene test was first used to assess the homogeneity of variances; when variances were homogeneous, the independent samples t-test (Student’s t) was applied, and when variances were not homogeneous, the Welch t-test was used. In comparisons involving three or more groups, the Levene test was used to test for homogeneity of variances; since variances were found to be equal across all groups, differences between group means were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (One-Way ANOVA, Fisher’s F test). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to examine whether the data exhibited a normal distribution; additionally, kurtosis and skewness coefficients (confirmed to be between + 1.5 and − 1.5) were checked. As a result of these analyses, it was determined that the data exhibited a normal distribution, and parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA, Pearson correlation) were used in the analysis.

Likewise, no significant differences emerged regarding job satisfaction or work-life quality scores, regardless of marital status, educational background, or the type of intensive care unit where participants were employed (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). The Welch t-test further confirmed that having children did not produce significant variation in either scale (p > 0.05).

Overall, the results demonstrate that gender was the only demographic factor associated with a meaningful difference in job satisfaction. In contrast, other variables, such as marital status, education, and work unit, did not have a measurable effect on either job satisfaction or perceived work-life quality.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between the “quality of work life” and “job satisfaction” among intensive care nurses and found a strong, statistically significant positive correlation (r = 0.717, p < 0.001). This finding largely parallels the results of national and international studies in the literature, such as Chu et al. (2014) and Darganpourpour et al. (2020) [13, 14]. This confirms that the perceived quality of the work environment is one of the most fundamental factors determining nurses’ job satisfaction.

Intensive care units are, by their nature, environments with high psychological work demands (critical patient monitoring, need for rapid decision-making). If these high demands are balanced with a high level of “job control” (autonomy, skill use) and “social support,” employees exhibit high job satisfaction. Our findings reveal that the quality of work life offered by the institution (managerial support, physical conditions, and autonomy) acts as a fundamental buffering mechanism enabling nurses to tolerate this heavy workload.

On the other hand, there are significant differences in the literature regarding the effect of demographic variables on job satisfaction and the quality of work life. In our study, variables such as marital status, education level, and the unit worked in did not have a significant effect on these two factors. This finding contradicts the results reported by Erenoğlu et al. (2019), who argue that age, unit, and shift system are determinants [15]. The main reason for this inconsistency may be the strong “adaptive capacity” that intensive care nurses develop against different work environments and stressors. In addition, the fact that gender made a significant difference in job satisfaction in our study, while no such difference was found in the study by Tamer and Öztürk (2018), can be explained by differences in gender roles or perceptions of work-family conflict in our sample group. One of the most striking findings of the study is the professional attitudes of nurses. Although 84.6% of the participants stated that they loved their profession and 76.5% stated that they chose it willingly, it is thought-provoking that only 44.9% stated that they would choose the same profession again if they had the opportunity. This result proves that the high motivation felt at the beginning of the profession is eroded over time due to factors such as working conditions, heavy workload, and chronic stress. As emphasized by Tamer and Öztürk (2018), institutional and professional dissatisfaction is the most important risk factor that reduces the quality of work life [16].

The research results show that hospital administrations should make psychosocial support mechanisms and “debriefing” sessions routine for intensive care nurses. In addition, increasing the autonomy of nurses in clinical decision-making processes will play a critical role in preventing loss of motivation. It is recommended that future studies be conducted with longitudinal designs that track the effect of improvements in the quality of work life on job satisfaction over time. Furthermore, qualitative interview methods should be used to gain a deeper understanding of the loss of motivation among the 40% who, despite loving the profession, do not wish to choose it again.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between quality of work life and job satisfaction. Second, the use of a convenience sample from a single geographical region may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, data were collected using self-reported instruments, which may introduce response and social desirability bias. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. Future longitudinal and multi-center studies using probabilistic sampling methods are recommended to strengthen causal inference and external validity.

Conclusion

The research findings reveal a strong and statistically significant positive relationship between work life quality and job satisfaction among intensive care nurses (Table 3). This demonstrates that improvements in institutional facilities directly trigger professional satisfaction. When evaluated within the framework of the scale, this result suggests that in intensive care environments with extremely high workloads and stress levels, improving work life quality acts as a ‘control and support’ mechanism. According to the model, psychosocial support and physical improvements provided to nurses balance the stress created by high work demands, protecting employees from burnout and shifting job satisfaction to the ‘active work’ phase.

However, the fact that only 44.9% of nurses stated they would choose their profession again, despite 84.6% loving their job, indicates that the initial high motivation has undergone significant erosion over time. This ‘motivational mismatch’ points to the high work demands highlighted in Karasek’s model, which overshadow existing control mechanisms and reward systems over time. In conclusion, implementing structural reforms such as shift arrangements to protect nurses’ health, satisfactory wage policies, career development opportunities, and institutional stress management programs is a strategic necessity for both employee well-being and the sustainability of patient care quality.
