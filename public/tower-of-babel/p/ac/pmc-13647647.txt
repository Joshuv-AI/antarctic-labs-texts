Rehydration failure despite rapid weight regain in elite para judo athletes with visual impairment: a descriptive repeated-measures study
Authors: Tolga Eski, Hasan Basri Taşkın, Salih Karaman, Bayram Ceylan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify changes in body mass and hydration status, alongside rapid weight loss (RWL) methods, in elite Para judo athletes with visual impairment.MethodsUsing a cohort repeated-measures design, twelve elite Para judo athletes with visual impairment (6 males, 6 females; age: 33.1 ± 4.3 years) were monitored. Measurements occurred at three distinct time points: one week before the competition (T1), at the official weigh-in (T2), and on competition day (T3). Body weight was measured using a calibrated digital scale, and hydration status was evaluated through midstream urine samples analyzed for urine specific gravity (USG). Hypohydration was defined as a USG ≥ 1.020.ResultsAthletes experienced a significant reduction in body weight, losing 3.89 ± 0.79 kg (5.2%) from T1 to the official weigh-in at T2. Following the weigh-in, athletes regained significant weight with a mean value of 3.44 ± 0.71 kg (4.8%) until the morning of the competition (T3). Hydration markers worsened alongside weight reduction; USG levels increased significantly from 1.023 ± 0.002 at T1 to 1.031 ± 0.002 at T2, indicating severe dehydration. Although USG values decreased significantly to 1.026 ± 0.001 by T3, they remained significantly higher than baseline levels. Notably, all of the athletes (100%) presented hypohydration one week before the competition, at the official weigh-in, and before the start of the competition.ConclusionElite Para judo athletes with visual impairment utilize aggressive RWL strategies, resulting in significant weight fluctuations and severe dehydration at the time of weigh-in. Despite experiencing substantial body weight regain during the recovery period prior to the match, the athletes fail to adequately rehydrate, starting the competition in a continuous state of hypohydration.

Introduction

In judo, an Olympic combat sport, athletes compete in weight classes to ensure they compete on equal terms and to minimize the risk of injury [1]. Conversely, for athletes who cut weight to compete in a lighter weight class, and thus face opponents who are lighter and less powerful, this situation becomes an advantage [2, 3]. Furthermore, Reale, Cox [3] notes that athletes who have medals in competitions gain more weight after the weigh-in compared to those who do not, suggesting that losing weight before the weigh-in to compete in a lower weight class puts athletes at an advantage. The official weigh-in for judo competitions takes place approximately fifteen hours beforehand [4]. This situation leads athletes to use rapid weight loss (RWL) methods such as fasting, wearing sweat suits, or using saunas, which can cause health issues like dehydration [1, 5]. RWL can lead to cardiovascular problems resulting from a decrease in plasma volume and increased blood volume, chronic hormonal imbalances, suppression of the immune system [6], and impaired mental health and eating habits [7, 8]. RWL in judo athletes typically begins at an early age, around 12–15 years, and is both common and severe [9, 10].

Hypohydration, a typical side effect of RWL, is observed in judo athletes across all competitive levels, age groups, and genders. Judo athletes typically compete in a hypohydrated state, with urine specific gravity (USG) values above 1.020, indicating mild to severe dehydration [4, 11–13]. Elite judo athletes exhibit significant hypohydration throughout the competition week; USG levels are 1.023 ± 0.002 one week before the competition and 1.030 ± 0.001 at the official weigh-in [14]. Similarly, athletes exhibited high levels of hypohydration on training days despite ad libitum fluid intake. Young judo athletes (U-15) are hypohydrated at the beginning and end of training sessions [15, 16]. Moreover, the majority of elite judo athletes (92.6%) exhibited high levels of hypohydration despite not being in a weight-cutting period and having access to fluids at an international training camp; these athletes were classified as hypohydrated at all measurement times (USG > 1.020) [17]. Even acute dehydration leads to performance decrements in judo athletes with significant decrease in hand grip strength, judogi grip strength test, and worse judo specific test index [18].

Although the International Judo Federation (IJF) extended the post-weigh-in recovery period from 2 h to 15 h in 2013 to protect athlete health [19], the literature indicates that judo athletes frequently begin competitions in a hypohydrated state. This chronic problem highlights the need to reassess the risks associated with RWL practices and optimize hydration protocols. While hydration habits in able-bodied judo athletes have been extensively documented, data in the literature regarding visually impaired Para-athletes is quite limited [20]. Visually impaired individuals face a series of additional challenges in meeting their physiological needs compared to the general population. In particular, common self-monitoring techniques relying on visual cues, such as assessing urine color (UC), are not functional for this group of athletes. Furthermore, daily routines such as food selection, portion control, and tracking fluid intake become more complex due to the lack of visual feedback and limited access to adapted educational materials [21]. Visually impaired athletes competing in para-judo may be in a more vulnerable position regarding the adverse effects of RWL practices due to a combination of physiological and psychological factors stemming from their disability [20]. Visual impairment can limit the safe implementation of weight management strategies and the athlete’s ability to monitor their own condition (self-monitoring), thereby increasing the likelihood of resorting to inappropriate or risky RWL methods [22, 23]. While the prevalence of RWL is generally known in the literature, studies examining RWL in this special population are typically limited to small sample sizes and data based on athletes’ self-reports [24], and thus fail to adequately explain how disability factors interact with weight management behaviors. This gap in the literature highlights the need for more comprehensive studies to shed light on the prevalence, extent, and safe management strategies of rapid weight loss among Para judo athletes with visual impairment. Therefore, the present study aimed to identify changes in body mass and hydration status, as well as rapid weight loss methods, among Para judo athletes with visual impairment one week before competition, during official weigh-in, and on the morning of the competition. We hypothesized that athletes would exhibit inadequate hydration status alongside significant weight loss and weight gain.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cohort repeated-measures design study aimed to monitor the acute changes in body weight and hydration status via USG a week before (T1), at the official weigh-in (T2), and on the competition day (T3) in elite Para judo athletes with visual impairment during a real competitive environment. Participants

Due to the highly specific and limited population of elite Para judo athletes with visual impairment in Türkiye, a convenience sample of twelve athletes (female n = 6) voluntarily participated in this study. To evaluate the statistical adequacy of this sample size, a sensitivity power analysis was conducted using GPower (Version 3.1.9.7; Universität Kiel, Kiel, Germany). The analysis indicated that with a total sample size of 12 participants, a significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80, and an assumed correlation among repeated measures of 0.50 for a one-way within-factor repeated measures ANOVA (3 measurement times), the minimum detectable effect size (f) was 0.39. Athletes’ baseline characteristics can be seen in (Table 1). The eligibility criteria to participate in the study included having at least 5 years of judo experience, being part of the national Para judo team, and using rapid weight loss strategies during the study period. Written informed consent was obtained from each athlete before all measurements, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Kastamonu University Non-Interventional Clinical Trials Ethical Committee (2026/97).

Measurements

Body composition measurement

Measurement of body composition at the baseline for descriptive purpose was carried out using a bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) device (TANITA BC-545, Japan). To ensure the accuracy of the segmental body composition data, athletes were advised to refrain from any intense physical activities or heat exposure, such as saunas or hot showers, for at least 12 h before the assessment [25]. Participants were instructed to arrive at the laboratory in a post-absorptive state, having fasted for a minimum of 4 h, and to empty their bladders just before the measurement. Furthermore, to address orthostatic fluid shifts, athletes were asked to stand for 5 min before the test to prevent temporary changes in segmental impedance from affecting the results [26]. All measurements were taken with bare feet and hands, ensuring that the electrode contact points were clean and free from excess moisture or creams. Information regarding sex, height, and age was input into the BIA device, and the athlete mode was selected. BIA is a suitable tool for assessing body composition in individuals with varying levels of physical activity [27]. Each athlete’s body fat percentage was recorded.

Hydration status

To assess hydration status, a midstream urine sample was obtained from each athlete just before any body weight measurements were taken. Athletes were advised to refrain from consuming vitamin B-complex supplements, ergogenic aids with artificial dyes, and foods high in pigments (such as beets and blackberries) for 24 h prior to testing to ensure precise hydration evaluations and reduce confounding factors [28]. To avoid the impact of sudden hyperhydration on urine clarity, athletes were also restricted from consuming large amounts of fluids in the hour leading up to the assessment, ensuring that urine was not influenced by temporary changes in kidney output [29]. The samples were collected in plastic cups, and USG was measured using a digital refractometer (ATAGO PAL-10 S, Japan). The urine samples were immediately analyzed for USG and then promptly discarded. According to the ACSM position stand recommendations, USG was categorized as hypohydrated if it was ≥ 1.020 [30].

Body weight changes

Participants’ actual body weight was measured using a calibrated digital scale in the morning one week prior to the event (T1), on the day of the competition (T3), and in the evening just before the official weigh-in (T2). The official weigh-in took place at 19:00, nearly 15 h before the event began. All measurements were conducted using the same scale. Weight loss methods

The methods employed for RWL during the period between the baseline assessment and the official weigh-in were evaluated using a modified version of the validated questionnaire developed by Artioli, Gualano [7]. To ensure high ecological validity and minimize recall bias, the instrument was administered to the athletes at their place of residence (hotel) immediately preceding the official weigh-in. The questionnaire was specifically structured to identify the prevalence and types of weight-reduction strategies utilized during the final weight-cut phase. All participants completed the assessment in less than five minutes, ensuring a minimal respondent burden during the critical pre-competition period.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using JASP (Version 0.19.3.0). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test alongside an evaluation of skewness and kurtosis (thresholds of ± 2). Descriptive statistics are reported as mean, standard deviations (SD), and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). To determine differences in body weight and USG across different measurement times, a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was performed. The assumption of sphericity was tested using Mauchly’s test. In cases where sphericity was violated (p < 0.05), degrees of freedom were adjusted using the Greenhouse-Geisser correction if the epsilon (ϵ) was < 0.75, or the Huynh-Feldt correction if ϵ > 0.75. When a significant main effect was identified, post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Holm’s correction. Effect sizes for the RM-ANOVA were reported as partial eta squared (ɳ2p), with values of 0.01 to 0.06, 0.06 to 0.14, and > 0.14 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively [32]. For pairwise comparisons, Cohen’s d was calculated and interpreted as small (0.2), medium (0.5), and large (≥ 0.8). The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The results of the one-way RM-ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time on body weight (F(1.00, 11.04) = 288.8, p < 0.001, ɳ2p=0.963, ES=Large). Due to a significant violation of the sphericity assumption as indicated by Mauchly’s test (χ2(2) = 49.54, p < 0.001, ϵGG=0.502), Greenhouse-Geisser corrected degrees of freedom were utilized for the analysis. Post-hoc comparisons with Holm correction demonstrated significant differences across all measurement intervals (p < 0.001). Specifically, athletes experienced a significant weight reduction from one week prior to competition (75.16 ± 15.31 kg) to the official weigh-in (71.27 ± 14.52 kg), reflecting a mean decrease of 3.89 ± 0.79 (5.2%) kg (d = 0.26, p < 0.001). Following the weigh-in, body weight significantly increased to 74.71 ± 15.23 kg on the day of competition, representing a mean regain of 3.44 ± 0.71 kg (4.8%) (d =-0.23, p < 0.001). The analysis for USG followed a similar trend to body weight, indicating significant fluctuations in hydration status across the study period. A one-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time on USG (F(2, 22) = 76.56, p < 0.001, ɳ2p=0.874). Post-hoc analysis using the Holm correction showed significant differences between all measurement points (p = 0.003). Specifically, USG levels increased significantly from one week prior (1.023 ± 0.002) to the official weigh-in (1.031 ± 0.002), indicating a state of dehydration (d=-5.03, p < 0.001). Although USG values decreased significantly by the time of the competition (1.026 ± 0.001, d = 3.37, p < 0.001), they remained significantly higher than the baseline levels recorded one week before the competition (d=-1.66, p = 0.003) USG changes can be seen in (Fig. 3).

As for the athletes’ hydration classification, all of the athletes presented hypohydration a week before the competition, at the official weigh-in and before the start of the competition.

Gradual dieting (4 athletes), fasting (6 athletes), skipping meals (9 athletes), increased exercise (11 athletes), training with plastic suits (10 athletes), and restricting fluids (11 athletes) were used by the athletes to lose weight within a week before the competition.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine body weight and hydration changes as well as weight loss methods in Para judo athletes with visual impairment during the week leading up to a real competition. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study presenting body weight and hydration status changes in this underrepresented population. The main findings of the study were that elite Para judo athletes with visual impairment underwent significant fluctuations in both body weight and hydration status during the pre-competition week. Specifically, athletes utilized aggressive RWL strategies, most notably fluid restriction and increased exercise, to achieve a significant reduction in body weight (3.89 kg, 5.2%) leading up to the official weigh-in. This weight loss was accompanied by a critical increase in USG (from 1.023 ± 0.002 to 1.031 ± 0.002), indicating a state of significant dehydration at the time of the weigh-in. Furthermore, while a substantial weight regain (3.44 kg, 4.8%) occurred prior to the match, the athletes failed to return to baseline hydration levels (mean USG of 1.026 ± 0.001), with 100% of the participants remaining hypohydrated on the day of the competition.

The findings of this study reveal that Para judo athletes with visual impairment experienced a significant decrease in body weight and that they intensely resorted to RWL methods during the week leading up to an official competition. Previous studies indicated that RWL methods are quite commonly used by able-bodied judo athletes prior to competition [5, 14, 33, 34]. Furthermore, it has been emphasized that 96% of elite judokas listed in the IJF world ranking use RWL methods, highlighting the high prevalence of RWL methods among judokas prior to competitions [27]. The results of the current study revealed a significant decrease of 3.89 kg (5.2%) in body weight observed prior to competition weigh-in, indicating that elite Para judo athletes undergo RWL. Para judo athletes with visual impairment also may have resorted to RWL methods for reasons such as increasing their chances of success in competition [2, 3], feeling psychologically prepared, and experiencing increased focus and a sense of commitment [35]. Additionally, athletes regained 3.44 (4.83%) kg during the approximately 15-hour period leading up to the morning of the competition day. Kons, Gheller [24] reported that weight gain in Para judokas with visual impairment was 2.9 ± 1.2 kg for men and 2.2 ± 1.8 kg for women. Ceylan, Baydil [4], however, reported that the average weight gain during a 15-hour recovery period in healthy judo athletes was 2.3 ± 1.3 kg (3.3 ± 2.1%). Furthermore, it is known that when the interval between weigh-in and competition exceeds 15 h, athletes perceive the recovery opportunity as an advantage and resort to riskier weight-loss methods [36]. The findings of the present study support recommendations to limit the time between the official weigh-in and the competition to prevent the adverse effects of RWL and dehydration [37].

During the one-week period prior to the official weigh-in, the USG values of Para judo athletes with visual impairment showed a significant increase from 1.023 ± 0.002 g·mL to 1.031 ± 0.002 g·mL as a result of fluid loss associated with the use of RWL. Although USG values decreased (1.026 ± 0.001 g·mL) on the day of the competition, they did not return to the levels observed one week prior, and USG values on the day of the competition remained significantly higher than those from one week earlier. The literature reports that able-bodied judo athletes exhibit hydration profiles similar to those in our study during training and competition periods [4, 12–15, 17, 38]. Ceylan, Barley [14] determined USG values in elite judo athletes as 1.023 ± 0.002 g·mL one week before the competition, 1.030 ± 0.001 g·mL at the official weigh-in, and 1.017 ± 0.007 g·mL 24 h after the match. It is believed that the return of the USG value to normal hydration levels [39] following the official weigh-in is associated with a 24-hour recovery period [36]. In the literature, USG values of 1.021 and above are classified as indicative of high-level dehydration, while values above 1.030 are classified as indicative of severe dehydration [30]. Accordingly, it was determined that Para judo athletes with visual impairment were in a more hypohydrated state than healthy athletes during competition weigh-ins based on hydration classification, they were in a state of significant dehydration one week prior to the competition, serious dehydration on the day of the weigh-in, and significant dehydration on the competition day. Indeed, a recent study confirms that, Para judo athletes with visual impairment exhibit chronic dehydration due to difficulties in maintaining fluid balance during training and daily fluid intake despite theoretically possessing sufficient hydration knowledge [20]. Moreover, the current study highlighted chronic hypohydration in this population based on the hydration status at the beginning of the study regardless of the USG values at weigh-in and pre-competition. All athletes presented high level of hypohydration at first measurement, meaning it could be a cultural norm among judo athletes. The inability to visually monitor fluid intake and urine output in this special population may be related to deficiency to compensate the fluid loss during weight loss process or trainings.

In this study, the most commonly used RWL strategies were increased exercise (11 athletes) and restricting fluid intake (11 athletes), while the most preferred method following these strategies was training with plastic suits (10 athletes). In a study where the authors identified RWL methods among visually impaired judokas, Kons, Gheller [24] reported that the most commonly used methods were fluid restriction (50%), gradual dieting (40%), and increased exercise (33%). Additionally, they noted that while the weight loss in visually impaired athletes resembled a genuine weight loss process, they began this process earlier. In other studies conducted with able-bodied judo athletes, the most commonly used weight loss methods were increased exercise, skipping one or two meals, training with plastic/rubber suits, training in deliberately heated environments, and sauna use [5, 7]. Considering that RWL and dehydration can lead to acute kidney injury [40], a decrease in blood volume and reduced stroke volume [41], continuous monitoring of hydration levels in visually impaired athletes is of critical importance for maintaining blood volume, cardiovascular health, and kidney function during and after prolonged weight loss periods.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. While weight loss methods were identified using a validated questionnaire, the study did not continuously monitor exact fluid intake volume or nutritional composition during the 15-hour post-weigh-in recovery period. Moreover, the precise dietary factors contributing to the athletes’ inability to fully rehydrate by the morning of the competition remain unidentified. Finally, assessing hydration relies on multiple biomarkers; integrating continuous blood variables alongside USG could provide a more comprehensive physiological profile during the RWL phase.

Conclusion

This study highlights that elite Para judo athletes with visual impairment engage in aggressive rapid weight loss strategies, predominantly through fluid restriction and increased exercise, leading to significant reductions in body weight prior to official weigh-ins. These practices induce a state of severe hypohydration at the time of the weigh-in. Crucially, despite having an approximate 15-hour recovery window and achieving substantial body weight regain, all athletes failed to restore their baseline hydration status, entering the competition in a hypohydrated state. Given the unique challenges visually impaired athletes face regarding visual self-monitoring of hydration, these findings underscore the urgent need to develop specialized, accessible educational materials and non-visual monitoring protocols. Implementing tailored weight management interventions is essential to protect the health and optimize the performance of Para judo athletes. Future multi-center studies with larger cohorts are needed to generalize these findings and allow for gender-specific comparisons.
