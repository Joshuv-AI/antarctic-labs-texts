Buprenorphine Substitution as a Tapering Strategy for Opioid Discontinuation in Patients with Chronic Pain: A Nonrandomized and Proof of Concept Study
Authors: Célian Bertin, Nicolas Kerckhove, Florent Ferrer, Bruno Pereira, Christian Duale, Damien Richard, Nicolas Authier, Noémie Delage
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLong-term use (≥ 3 months) of opioids for chronic noncancer pain (CNCP) shows limited benefit and often leads to dependence, especially when tapered too quickly. Standard opioid discontinuation typically involves gradual dose reduction, yet many patients fail to complete it. Meta-analyses show buprenorphine reduces withdrawal severity, increases treatment retention and completion rates, and facilitates analgesia and smoother transitions in patients with chronic pain, with low adverse effects and high initiation success.MethodsThis single-center, prospective, nonrandomized proof-of-concept study assessed the efficacy of an outpatient buprenorphine-based discontinuation strategy in patients with CNCP and opioid dependence who had failed standard tapering. Participants first attempted gradual tapering; those unsuccessful transitioned to buprenorphine: 4–8 mg/day for 1 month, followed by tapering over up to 9 months. Success was defined as complete opioid cessation at 9 months, confirmed by urine analysis (including buprenorphine). The primary outcome was the success rate of the buprenorphine strategy, with ≥ 60% considered effective. A secondary outcome compared success rates between strategies.ResultsOf 20 patients, six (30.0%) successfully withdrew using standard tapering. Fourteen failed; 11 of them transitioned to buprenorphine. Of these, seven (63.6%) achieved full discontinuation. While not statistically significant, the buprenorphine group showed a higher success rate than standard tapering (p = 0.076; OR = 4.08 [0.90–21.23]).ConclusionsBuprenorphine substitution appears at least as effective as conventional tapering and may benefit patients unable to succeed with tapering alone. Introduction

Opioid analgesics have been increasingly prescribed for chronic noncancer pain (CNCP) in many countries [1–4]. However, strong scientific evidence supporting their long-term efficacy remains lacking [5–8] and causes significant adverse effects, including tolerance, withdrawal syndrome, opioid-induced hyperalgesia [9], and opioid use disorder (OUD), as seen in the North American opioid crisis [10–13]. Higher prescription doses also increases the risk of dependence and OUD [14–16], sometimes with fatal consequences [10, 17, 18]. The standard discontinuation strategy for patients with CNCP and opioid dependence is gradual tapering of long-term opioid therapy. However, this approach remains empirical, with no validated scientific protocol. For example, a Danish study highlighted that gradual tapering is often impractical due to high dropout rates in patients with CNCP [19], this justify exploring alternatives to the current tapering-only approach.

The strategy proposed in this study is innovative: it involves using buprenorphine not in the traditional OUD treatment framework, but as a transitional substitution to facilitate tapering in patients with physical opioid dependence who do not meet OUD criteria. This distinction is essential, as patients with CNCP physically dependent on opioids but without signs of misuse often fall into a clinical gray area. Addiction specialists may be reluctant to intervene outside the OUD context, fearing destabilization of fragile pain control, while pain physicians and general practitioners are often unfamiliar with buprenorphine. It is still wrongly perceived as a drug reserved for “addicts”, and its analgesic properties remain underappreciated. Furthermore, buprenorphine’s pharmacological profile as a partial µ-opioid receptor agonist raises concerns about interactions with full opioid agonists and the risk of precipitated withdrawal, especially in outpatient settings. These cultural, organizational, and pharmacological barriers contribute to missed therapeutic opportunities. Despite this, buprenorphine is considered safer than full opioid agonists due to its pharmacological properties as a partial opioid agonist [20], resulting in a ceiling effect on respiratory depression [21], lower mortality rates observed in clinical practice [22], an improved cardiac safety profile [23], and reduced euphoric effects compared with full opioid agonists [24]. Nonetheless, caution is warranted with concomitant central nervous system depressants [25], and naloxone reversal may be more challenging [26].

This study develops and evaluates a supervised, structured alternative outpatient discontinuation strategy that leverages buprenorphine’s dual properties as an analgesic and a tapering agent to meet this unmet clinical need. While buprenorphine has shown efficacy in opioid-dependent patients with CNCP [27], further studies are needed to determine the optimal starting dose, formulation, frequency, and the best strategy for rotation. Additionally, buprenorphine’s ability to reduce opioid-induced hyperalgesia is particularly relevant in the context of long-term opioid therapy [27–29].

Methods

All subjects provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Study Objectives

The primary objective was to evaluate the success rate of an outpatient discontinuation strategy involving a stepwise approach: patients with CNCP first attempted opioid tapering with the standard method, and in case of failure, underwent a temporary opioid rotation with buprenorphine.

The secondary objectives were to: (i) describe the success rate of standard tapering prior to buprenorphine initiation; (ii) estimate the time required to achieve successful tapering at each step of the strategy; (iii) determine the average effective dose of buprenorphine used for discontinuation; and (iv) assess the impact of buprenorphine on key clinical parameters, including pain, sleep, anxiety, depression, and quality of life.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Eligible participants had to be suffering from CNCP, treated with an opioid analgesic for more than six months, and be affiliated with the French national health security system.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients were excluded if they had an OUD characterized by craving or misuse assessed by DSM-5 and Prescription Opioid Misuse Index (POMI) [30]; were undergoing other tapering strategies or treatment like ketamine; were undergoing new analgesic treatment for less than three months; had a contraindication to buprenorphine; were pregnant or breastfeeding; were participating in another interventional clinical trial or were under legal protection measures.

Once these prerequisites were confirmed, participants could be included if they met at least one of the following criteria:

Presence of physical opioid dependence assessed using the Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS). This dependence was defined by opioid withdrawal symptoms, without compulsive drug-seeking behavior (craving) or loss of control characteristic of OUD.

Standard Gradual Tapering of Long-Term Opioid Therapy (Step 1)

The patient had to follow the various stages of dosage reduction according to the reduction protocol defined in advance and standardized for each opioid analgesic over a period of 9 months, regardless of the investigator. The patient was contacted by phone 48–72 h after each step in the reduction of its opioid dose to assess the symptoms of physical withdrawal using the Subjective Opiate Withdrawal Scale (SOWS), and was reviewed in consultation every month by the pain specialist.

In case of failure, the patient discontinued its opioid and buprenorphine started immediately (step 2, see below).

Temporary Opioid Rotation with Buprenorphine (Step 2)

Buprenorphine was administered sublingually every morning for a maximum duration of 9 months. The initial dose was 4 mg/day (± 2 mg), in accordance with the recommendations [31]. This dosage could be increased by depending on tolerance and effectiveness, assessed during follow-up phone calls made during the first week, to determine the optimal dose tailored to the patient’s individual needs and according to the symptoms of physical withdrawal using the SOWS scale. The predefined maximum dose was 10 mg/day. The tapering was then gradual, decreasing by 0.4–2 mg every 4 weeks until complete discontinuation. Buprenorphine was dispensed monthly during follow-up visits.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the evaluation of the success rate of the buprenorphine-based discontinuation strategy at the 9-month follow-up. In the absence of a reference study at the start of our study, we have considered it as a valuable therapeutic alternative if a minimal successful tapering rate of 60% was observed for the buprenorphine-based discontinuation strategy. The recent study of Ito et al. [29], which demonstrated a success rate of tapering opioids equal to 48.4% using a buprenorphine-based discontinuation strategy, supported our hypothesis. For the two steps of the study (standard tapering and buprenorphine), successful discontinuation was confirmed by the absence of opioids, including buprenorphine, in urinalysis at the end of the tapering period. Regardless of the step of the study, discontinuation failure was considered to have occurred when the patient was unable to reduce their opioid or buprenorphine doses by 9-months (failure could be declared before the 9-month follow-up period based on the clinician’s opinion and/or the patient’s wish to stop discontinuation). In clinic, these failures are mainly caused by persistent withdrawal symptoms and/or suboptimal pain control, requiring clinical reassessment of pain management and withdrawal symptoms.

All questionnaires were performed at inclusion, end of standard strategy (step 1), 1-month follow-up after buprenorphine starting (step 2) and last follow-up visit.

Safety

Any adverse events were collected daily by the patient and during the planned phone calls and visits by investigators. Adverse events were categorized according to their type, intensity and treatment-related effects by investigators.

Study Design

This study was designed as a nonrandomized, proof-of-concept and sequential trial (also called stepwise treatment strategy). Specifically, all patients first attempted tapering with standard care (step 1). Only those who did not succeed with this first-line strategy were subsequently transitioned to buprenorphine (step 2). Buprenorphine was introduced for one month, and gradual buprenorphine tapering was performed until complete discontinuation. All the steps are detailed in Fig. 1 and Supplemental Material.

Statistical Consideration

Sample Size Calculation

A multistage Fleming design was chosen and justified by the exploratory nature of the study and the potential limited number of eligible patients, reflecting the recruitment challenges of this specific population in a single center. This design allows for efficient sequential analysis while minimizing patient exposure to potentially ineffective strategies. It is particularly well suited for detecting an early signal of efficacy without compromising methodological rigor. If results fall well below the predefined efficacy threshold in the first stage, the study can be stopped early for futility, optimizing resource use and reducing the ethical risk of pursuing a nonbeneficial protocol. This design was considered fully appropriate for a proof-of-concept study evaluating an innovative alternative in a setting of therapeutic impasse [32].

A two-stage design was adopted, and the maximum inefficiency level was set at 30% [33]. According to our clinical experience, the minimum required level of efficacy (successful discontinuation rate) was set at 60% [29]. The type I error set at 5%, and statistical power at 90%. Thus, in the first stage, if at least 7 patients out of 11 (≥ 60%) receiving buprenorphine had a negative result in their urinalysis, the study was stopped early for efficacy. If this threshold was not met, recruitment continued into the second Fleming stage. In this second stage, if at least 12 patients out of 19 (≥ 60%) receiving buprenorphine achieved a negative result in their urinalysis, the study was considered positive; otherwise, it was considered negative.

Statistical Analysis

The primary endpoint was the successful discontinuation rate after buprenorphine rotation, defined as the absence of opioids in the urinalysis, and was reported with a 95% confidence interval. The analysis followed the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle (all patients receiving buprenorphine).

Because of the sequential design, the comparator (standard tapering) included the whole cohort at baseline, whereas the buprenorphine group comprised only patients who failed the standard taper. Therefore, comparisons between the two strategies were descriptive in nature and should not be interpreted as outcomes from independent parallel groups.

To complement the primary endpoint, we reported the outcomes of standard tapering (step 1) and buprenorphine rotation (step 2), and described the overall discontinuation success across the sequential pathway. Survival analysis (Kaplan–Meier) was used to illustrate time to discontinuation failure.

The evolution of clinical parameters (questionnaires) was analyzed using longitudinal data analysis with the Friedman test. For nonrepeated (cross-sectional) data, standard statistical tests were applied. Quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median [interquartile range (IQR)], depending on data distribution (normality assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test). Quantitative variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers (n) and percentages (%) and were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test in cases of small sample sizes. Statistical analysis was performed using Stata software (version 15, StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Results are presented according to the sequential design of the study: all patients first underwent the standard tapering strategy (step 1), and those who failed this approach subsequently entered the buprenorphine rotation phase (step 2). Accordingly, the analyses describe outcomes across these two consecutive phases rather than parallel independent groups.

Population

Twenty patients were included between September 2017 and December 2022. The long recruitment period was due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which halted the study for 2 years. The study population was predominantly male (12/20, 60.0%), with a median age of 41.5 years [29.5; 53.5]. Most patients had not completed higher education (15/20, 75.0%), were not professionally active (11/20, 55.0%), and were in a relationship (12/20, 60.0%). The types of pain experienced by the patients were distributed as follows: 60.0% (12/20) nociplastic, 45.0% (9/20) nociceptive, and 30.0% (6/20) neuropathic. Forty percent of patients had at least two different types of pain, and 70.0% (14/20) had been experiencing pain for more than 5 years. The median pain intensity was 5.5 [4.0; 7.0]. No significant differences were observed between groups (Table 1).

Analgesic Treatments

Three opioids were involved in the study: tramadol (60.0%, 12/20), morphine (15.0%, 3/20), and oxycodone (25.0%, 5/20). Patients were also being treated with other concomitant analgesics, mainly paracetamol (6/20, 30.0%), venlafaxine (4/20, 20.0%), and pregabalin (4/20, 20.0%), with half of the patients taking at least three different analgesics (including opioids). No significant differences were observed between groups (Table 1).

The median starting oral dose of opioids (in oral morphine equivalent) was 85.0 [43.8;127.5] mg/day, which was similar regardless of the success of the discontinuation (success = 65.0 [52.5;85.0] mg/day; failure = 95.0 [41.3;172.5] mg/day; p = 0.46). At the initiation of buprenorphine, the previous median oral dose of opioids before buprenorphine rotation was 80.0 [52.5;107.5] mg/day. The median starting oral dose of buprenorphine was 4.0 [4.0;7.0] mg/day, which was similar regardless of the success of the discontinuation (success = 4.0 [4.0;6.5] mg/day; failure = 5.0 [4.0;6.5] mg/day; p = 0.84) (Supplementary Table 1). The evolution of opioid and buprenorphine dose reductions for each patient were shown in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2.

Primary Analysis

Among the 20 patients who initially received the standard tapering strategy, the success rate of opioid discontinuation was 30.0% [9.9;50.1] (6/20). The 14 patients who failed this first-line strategy were then eligible for buprenorphine rotation, of whom 11 entered the ITT population (2 refusals to continue on buprenorphine, 1 with antecedent of buprenorphine allergy). Within this sequential subgroup, the success rate with buprenorphine was 63.6% [35.2;92.0] (7/11), corresponding to a number needed to treat (NNT) of 3.0 [1.5;85.4] (Table 2).

Primary outcome

Although not based on two independent parallel groups, exploratory comparative analyses were conducted to illustrate potential differences between the two steps of the sequential strategy. Logistic regression yielded an odds ratio (OR) of 4.08 [0.90–21.23], suggesting a higher likelihood of success with buprenorphine, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.076, Table 2). Similarly, Fisher’s exact test produced an OR of 4.08 [0.68–26.05] with a p-value of 0.128.

Survival analysis (Fig. 2) further illustrated the kinetics of discontinuation outcomes across the sequential pathway. The time to discontinuation failure tended to favor buprenorphine (p = 0.073). The median time to successful discontinuation was 8.0 [6.0;9.0] months for buprenorphine versus 6.0 [5.3;7.5] months with standard tapering (p = 0.21). In contrast, the median time to discontinuation failure showed a significant difference, with a longer time to failure in the buprenorphine step (5.0 [3.25;6.25] months) compared with the standard step (1.3 [0.3;2.8] months, p = 0.047).

Overall, despite the absence of a statistically significant difference in success rates, these results suggest that buprenorphine rotation may be at least as effective as standard tapering, and potentially more favorable in prolonging the time to discontinuation failure within a sequential treatment pathway.

Secondary Analyses

Patients who successfully withdrew from opioids using the buprenorphine-based strategy (n = 7) also showed improvements in anxiety (p = 0.015) and sleep (p = 0.043) at the end of the study. No statistically significant improvement was observed in other outcomes (depression, pain, quality of life) and no symptom of withdrawal was observed (COWS) (Supplementary Fig. 3). For patients who successfully discontinued opioids using the tapering standard strategy (n = 4), there were no significant variations in any of the secondary outcomes, and no withdrawal symptoms were observed at the end of the study (data not shown).

Safety

Only 3/20 (15%) patients reported having experienced adverse events (excessive pain, erysipelas, depression, cardiac disorders), only one of whom reported it while taking buprenorphine, and this was unrelated to the treatment. There were no overdoses, hospitalizations, or emergency room visits related to opioid treatment. Buprenorphine therefore appears to be a very well-tolerated treatment.

Discussion

Main Results

Our study did not reveal a statistically significant difference between gradual tapering and rotation to buprenorphine. Nevertheless, despite the limited power imposed by the small sample size, the observed trends—namely an almost two-fold higher success rate and a markedly longer time to failure in the buprenorphine group—suggest that this strategy may be at least as effective, and potentially superior, to conventional tapering for patients receiving long-term opioid therapy. Although mostly not statistically significant (except for anxiety and sleep in the buprenorphine group)—likely due to the limited power of this pilot study—our findings on depression, anxiety, quality of life, and sleep scores suggest a trend toward improvement across all participants, regardless of the study arm. Several factors may contribute to this outcome, as these domains are interrelated. Possible explanations include personal satisfaction from successfully completing opioid tapering, reduced opioid-related adverse effects, and improved pain control in some patients switched to buprenorphine. The literature further supports the potential benefits of buprenorphine rotation from opioid analgesics, with reported improvements in depression and sleep scores [34, 35]. Moreover, buprenorphine’s ability to better control opioid-induced hyperalgesia, which is sometimes mistaken for tolerance, further supports its relevance in patients receiving long-term opioid therapy [27, 28].

A review by Srivastava et al. [36] highlighted the efficacy of buprenorphine for opioid discontinuation management and its favorable safety profile compared with traditional opioids. These findings are consistent with our observations, as patients following this strategy did not experience worsening pain or excessive withdrawal symptoms, as reflected by low COWS scores. However, unlike some studies—such as Kang et al. [37] on the use of transdermal buprenorphine patches in cancer pain—our study did not demonstrate a significant effect on pain or quality of life. This discrepancy may be attributed to our small patient sample size, the CNCP studied population, the oral formulation of buprenorphine, or the limited duration of post-discontinuation follow-up.

Regarding the feasibility and tolerability of transitioning long-term opioid-treated patients with CNCP to buprenorphine, our findings align with existing literature [27]. These results further support the potential of buprenorphine as a safe option for mitigating risks associated with long-term opioid therapy.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several strengths, notably its innovative approach proposing an outpatient structured tapering strategy involving temporary substitution and close monitoring. This buprenorphine-based strategy is easy to implement for healthcare professionals, most frequently confronted with opioid analgesic dependence in CNCP, such as addiction specialists, pain physicians, and general practitioners. The use of urinalysis to confirm opioid cessation strengthens the reliability of the results. By evaluating comorbidities, such as anxiety, depression, quality of life, and sleep disturbances, our study provides a broader perspective of the effects of opioid discontinuation.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged, such as the nonrandomized, proof-of-concept design. This design was deemed appropriate in this exploratory phase to guide the development of future controlled trials. Recruitment and retention of opioid-dependent patients were particularly challenging; a pragmatic sequential design facilitated participation and mirrored actual care pathways. A crossover or parallel randomized design would have exposed some participants to repeated tapering failures, increasing their burden and introducing potential bias in outcome interpretation. A sequential design was also chosen to better reflect real-world clinical practice, consistent with an approach in which the most conventional strategy—often considered the most ethical—was applied first before proposing an innovative alternative.

Another limitation is the small sample size (n = 20), which reduces the statistical power and limits the ability to detect significant differences according to opioid type. Despite the promising trend favoring the buprenorphine strategy, the small sample size and the sequential design limit the robustness of the analysis. This study recruited patients exclusively from a single chronic pain center, reflecting its monocentric nature. Nevertheless, many patients with CNCP and opioid dependence are predominantly managed in primary care settings. Recognizing buprenorphine as a relevant alternative to assist these patients represents a significant advance in care provision.

Our findings suggest a potential benefit, but larger randomized studies are needed to confirm the efficacy of this new buprenorphine-based tapering strategy for analgesic opioid dependence. Additionally, longer follow-up periods are needed to assess the sustainability of abstinence and the evolution of pain post-discontinuation.

Conclusions

This proof-of-concept study highlights the potential of buprenorphine substitution as an effective and well-tolerated alternative strategy for opioid tapering in patients with chronic noncancer pain, particularly those who have failed a conventional gradual opioid tapering protocol.

To confirm and extend these results, a phase 2, randomized, controlled, open-label, multicenter trial is planned. This approach will rigorously assess the efficacy and safety of buprenorphine in a broad range of patients while identifying clinical profiles most likely to benefit from this alternative. The planned extended follow-up will also provide valuable data on the sustainability of abstinence and the evolution of pain post-discontinuation.

A thorough evaluation of buprenorphine as a tapering strategy for opioid analgesic dependence in CNCP is essential, as determining its place relative to classical tapering strategies is crucial. Integrating buprenorphine into clinical guidelines for opioid discontinuation management, especially in outpatient and primary care settings, could represent a major advance in improving patients’ quality of life and reducing risks associated with long-term opioid treatments.
