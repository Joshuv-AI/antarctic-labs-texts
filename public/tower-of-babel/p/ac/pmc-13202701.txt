Deletion of the D345L gene attenuates ASFV and induces protection against homologous and heterologous challenge by enhancing host innate immunity
Authors: Xuefei Sun, Min Zheng, Nan Li, Xiaoying Jia, Teng Chen, Shoufeng Zhang, Huixian Yue, Qixuan Li, Boli Ma, Fengjie Wang, Junnan Ke, Yiqian Jiang, Zhuo Hao, Rongliang Hu, Yanyan Zhang, Faming Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAfrican swine fever (ASF) is a highly contagious and lethal disease caused by African swine fever virus (ASFV), with a mortality rate approaching up to 100%. At present, the functions of multiple genes of ASFV remain to be elucidated. In this study, we demonstrate that pD345L is a late-expressed viral protein and localizes to the viral factory. The recombinant strain JX23-02ΔD345L was obtained from the genotype I/II strain JX23-02. In vitro assays demonstrated that the deletion of D345L remarkably impairs viral replication. Further analyses revealed that JX23-02ΔD345L significantly up-regulated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α) and IFN pathway components in PAMs, induced pyroptosis, apoptosis, and interferon responses. In vivo animal experiments demonstrated that the JX23-02ΔD345L strain was completely attenuated in pigs, failing to induce any typical clinical signs of ASF even with a high-dose inoculation. Moreover, a single immunization with JX23-02ΔD345L can elicit specific humoral and cellular immune responses against ASFV, conferring 80% protection against intramuscular challenge with the homologous strain JX23-02 and 60% protection against the heterologous strain SY18. These results reveal D345L as a crucial virulence-related gene for ASFV pathogenesis, which is highly correlated with viral replication and the host antiviral response. Furthermore, JX23-02ΔD345L represented a candidate for the development of effective and safe ASFV vaccines. This finding provides a foundation for the research on ASFV and offers an important target and evidence for vaccine development.

Introduction

African swine fever (ASF) is a highly contagious, haemorrhagic, and fatal disease that spreads among domestic pigs and wild boars, with a mortality rate of up to 100% [1]. Currently, there are no completely safe and effective vaccines available. ASF has caused considerable losses to the pig industry worldwide and is a notifiable animal disease mandated by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH, www.woah.org). Its pathogen, African swine fever virus (ASFV), is a large and complex double-stranded DNA virus [2].

Based on the B646L gene sequences, ASFV can be classified into 24 genotypes [3–5]. In 1957, a genotype I strain was transmitted from Angola to Portugal and subsequently spread to neighbouring European countries [1]. In 2007, a highly pathogenic genotype II ASFV (Georgia07) emerged in Georgia and spread rapidly to adjacent countries [6–8]. The SY18 strain, first reported and prevalent in China in 2018, is also a highly virulent genotype II strain [9]. In 2021, two low-virulence genotype I strains, HeN/ZZ-P1/21 and SD/DY-I/21, were identified in China, which can induce persistent infections in domestic pigs [10]. In 2022, a recombinant genotype I/II ASFV strain was isolated in China. This strain exhibits extremely high contagiousness and lethality, and attenuated live vaccines MGF505/360 and EP402R gene-deleted strain based on genotype II fail to provide full protection against infection with this recombinant virus [11]. In 2023, a recombinant genotype I/II ASFV strain was detected in northern Vietnam. Similarly, the genotype II-based gene-deleted attenuated live vaccines (ASFV-G-ΔMGF and ASFV-G-ΔI177L) cannot confer effective protection against this strain [12]. The emergence of genotype I/II ASFV strains poses new challenges to ASF prevention and control as well as vaccine development. These studies also demonstrated that ASFV can undergo recombination to generate new strains in the field that may lead to severe consequences. Therefore, biosafety should be prioritized on the premise of pursuing protective efficacy.

ASFV has a 170–193 kb genome encoding 150–167 open reading frames (ORFs) [13,14]. The proteins translated from these ORFs mediate viral replication and modulate the functions of host cells. To date, only a small subset of these proteins has been identified to be associated with virus–host interactions, immune regulation, and viral virulence, whereas the biological functions of the majority of proteins remain largely undefined [15]. Therefore, the identification of ASFV virulence genes is of great significance for vaccine development and mechanistic research.

The pD345L is considered a Lambda-class exonuclease that participates in DNA repair. Studies have demonstrated that pD345L negatively regulates the cGAS-STING-mediated NF-κB signalling pathway by inhibiting the activity of inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B kinase (IKKα/β), which results in reduced transcription of tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-β (IFNβ), and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1α (IL-1α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-8 (IL-8) [16]. To date, research on D345L has been limited to the mechanistic investigations; it remains unclear whether D345L acts as a virulence gene of ASFV, serves as an essential protein for viral replication, or associates with host immune response.

In this study, we identified an important virulence gene of ASFV, D345L, which is a late-expressed gene localized in viral factories and is highly associated with viral replication and immune evasion. We constructed and identified the gene D345L-deleted mutant JX23-02ΔD345L using the genotype I/II strain JX23-02 as the backbone via homologous recombination. Compared with the parental strain, JX23-02ΔD345L exhibited significantly impaired in vitro replication capacity, and induced the up-regulation of inflammatory regulators (IL-1β, TNF-α), antiviral factors (IFN), and their corresponding regulatory molecules. In vivo experiments demonstrated that the mutant was completely attenuated, with no viral shedding detected during the immune observation period. Moreover, it conferred partial protection against both the parental genotype I/II strain JX23-02 and genotype II strain SY18. This study provides a crucial theoretical and experimental foundation for the development of ASFV vaccines and for the prevention and control of ASF.

is highly conserved among ASFV strains

As for the JX23-02 isolate, the D345L gene is located at 142,161–143,198 bp of the viral genome and flanked by the genes D205R and S183L. The ORF of the D345L gene consists of 1038 bp, encoding 345 amino acids. Nineteen pD345L amino acid sequences derived from ASFV isolates of different genotypes were obtained from NCBI, including four genotype I/II strains, three genotype I strains, four genotype II strains, two genotype Ⅲ strains, one genotype Ⅳ strain, one genotype Ⅴ strain, one genotype Ⅷ strain, two genotype Ⅸ strains, and two genotype Ⅹ strains. Homology assessments showed that the D345L gene shared 91.7–100% nucleotide identity and 91.9–100% amino acid identity across all isolates, demonstrating that pD345L is a highly conserved protein among ASFV strains. All ASFV isolates archived in GenBank to date retain the D345L gene. The pD345L protein is uniformly 345 amino acids in length across most ASFV isolates, with the sole exception of ASFV/Warmbaths: a 3-nucleotide insertion in its D345L gene introduces an extra threonine (T) residue at positions 118–119, altering the protein’s canonical length (Figure 1). It remains unclear whether mutations in D345L contribute to the diverse virulence profiles of different ASFV strains. pD345L is a late-expression protein and localized in the viral factory

The temporal expression pattern of D345L was evaluated at the transcriptional level via reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), using ASFV early gene p30(CP204L) and late gene p72(B646L) as controls, using GAPDH as the housekeeping gene. The results showed that although the transcription of D345L was restricted to low levels within the first 6 h post-infection (hpi), it was detectable throughout the remaining infection cycle. D345L and B646L showed very similar transcriptional kinetics (Figure 2(a)) and protein expression profiles (Figure 2(b)), with both expressed at the late stage of infection. Moreover, cytosine arabinoside addition significantly suppressed the transcription of D345L (Figure 2(a)), which confirmed that pD345L was a late-expression protein. Deletion of the D345L gene significantly impairs viral replication in vitro

The strategies to construct the D345L gene-deleted mutant JX23-02ΔD345L are shown in Figure 3(a). The D345L gene was deleted from JX23-02 using homologous recombination and was replaced by a reporter of mCherry, which was controlled by the ASFV p72 promoter. After transfection, a two-round process of precisely picking the fluorescent cells was applied to screen the viruses with satisfactory replication. Then, three-round of limited dilution was used to obtain the deletion mutants without the parental virus (Figure 3(c)). Viral DNA from JX23-02ΔD345L and JX23-02 was extracted to amplify the D345L gene using specific primers. No band of 531 bp in size was detected in JX23-02ΔD345L, indicating no parental strain contamination (Figure 3(b)). Western blot analysis demonstrated no pD345L expression in JX23-02ΔD345L, while pD345L was detected in JX23-02 (Figure 3(f)). Whole-genome sequencing analysis of ASFV confirmed that the D345L gene at positions 142,161–143,198 of JX23-02 was replaced by the mCherry cassette. Nine nucleotide mutations were identified in the non-coding regions, including a C to G substitution at position 19,993 and 186,639 bp, a G to C substitution at position 73 and 256 bp, a G to A substitution at position 30 bp, a GG insertion at 14,754 bp, and a T insertion at position 180,543 bp (Table S1). The growth characteristics of JX23-02ΔD345L were compared with those of virulent parental strain JX23-02. The multistep growth curve showed that although JX23-02ΔD345L replicated rapidly at the early stage of viral infection, its replication capacity continued to decline after 24 hpi. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to observe virus particles. Viral factories and icosahedral structures similar to those of JX23-02 were observed (Figure 4(a)). JX23-02ΔD345L induces stronger immune response, innate immune response, and inflammatory responses in PAMs compared to JX23-02

To investigate whether JX23-02ΔD345L activates host antiviral immunity in vitro, RNA-seq was performed to analyse antiviral immune response in PAMs infected with JX23-02ΔD345L or JX23-02. Compared to JX23-02 infected PAMs, JX23-02ΔD345L induced significantly higher expression of interferon-stimulated genes (IFIT1, IFIT2, IFIT3, ISG15, and IRF7), the inflammatory cytokine (IL-1β), the tumour necrosis factor (TNF), and cell pyroptosis regulatory factor (Caspase-1, Caspase-3, and Caspase-10) (Figure 5(a)). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses revealed that these genes were predominantly enriched in pathways related to innate immune response, inflammatory response, and antiviral factors (Figure 5(b,c)). Reverse transcription-quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) was conducted to further validate the enhanced induction of IFN-associated antiviral factors and inflammatory cytokine levels. Consistent with the RNA-seq results, JX23-02ΔD345L significantly up-regulated the levels of IFN-stimulated factors MX1, ISG54, ISG56, IRF7, and IRF8, and extremely significantly up-regulated the expression levels of IL-1β and TNF-α. These results indicate that JX23-02ΔD345L triggers a stronger host antiviral immune response in PAMs than JX23-02, including innate immunity, inflammatory signalling pathways, and antiviral factors (Figure 5(d–n)).

JX23-02ΔD345L activates pathway proteins of pyroptosis and apoptosis in PAMs

Cells that were infected with JX23-02ΔD345L appeared the earlier cytopathic effect (CPE) and earlier abscission than JX23-02. The above results also showed a significant increase in the levels of TNF-α and IL-1β, which are closely related to cell pyroptosis and apoptosis. In order to determine the causes of these phenomena, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and cell activity tests were conducted. The results showed that compared to PAMs infected with JX23-02, the LDH release of JX23-02ΔD345L-infected cells was significantly increased at 6, 12, and 24 hpi (Figure 6(a)), and the cell activity was significantly decreased (Figure 6(b)), suggesting that the deletion of D345L in ASFV significantly damaged host cells. JX23-02ΔD345L induces an antiviral response by activating the interferon pathway

Since JX23-02ΔD345L up-regulated the antiviral response by increasing the transcription levels of IFNα and IFNβ, and their downstream effector molecule MX1, ISG15, ISG54, ISG56, and IRF7, we conducted Western blot assays on the downstream effector molecule, p-STAT1, and the interferon-stimulated gene ISG15 to further validate these results. Consistent with the above findings, both p-STAT1 and ISG15 showed up-regulation at 24 hpi, indicating that the absence of the D345L significantly enhanced the antiviral response of host cells during the late stage of viral replication (Figure 6(d)). The densitometric analysis is shown in Figure S1. These results suggested that D345L plays an important role in the host antiviral response.

JX23-02ΔD345L provided partial protection against homologous and heterologous virus challenge

After a 28-day immune observation, Groups 1 and 3 were challenged with homologous virus JX23-02. The rectal temperatures and clinical symptoms were recorded daily for 21 days. It is shown in Figure 7(d) that all the pigs in the control group (Group 3) exhibited elevated rectal temperatures(>40.5°C) 4 days post-challenge (dpc), and presented ASFV clinical signs like lethargy, recumbency, and convulsions, which persisting for 3–4 days, and succumbed to infection in 6–7 dpc (Figure 7(b)). Among the five pigs in the immune group (Group 1), 012 presented a rise in rectal temperature (>40.5℃) at 9 dpc (Figure 7(d)) and began to show anorexia and other typical clinical symptoms of ASF on 15 dpc. On 19 dpc, the body temperature dropped sharply to 38.5℃, then succumbed to ASF (Figure 7(b)).

Groups 2 and 4 were challenged with the heterologous virus SY18. Two pigs of the control group presented fever (above 40.5℃) 5–6 days post-challenge (Figure 7(d)), exhibited severe ASF infection symptoms, such as anorexia, lying down, convulsions, and cyanosis, and were ultimately euthanized at 9 and 10 dpc, respectively (Figure 7(b)). Among the 5 pigs in the immune group (Group 2), 032 and 033 began to have a fever 10 and 11 days post-challenge (Figure 7(d)), and died on 19 and 18 dpc, respectively (Figure 7(b)).

Viral DNA residues were detected in survived pigs

All pigs were tested for virus residue in tissue samples (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, mandibular lymph node, inguinal lymph node, mesenteric lymph node, tonsil, and bone marrow) by qPCR. In the challenge control group (Groups 3 and 4), high copy numbers of viral DNA were detected in all tissues. Pigs developed ASF (012, 032, and 033) carried lower copy numbers of viral DNA. However, pigs (Groups 1 and 2) that survived the challenge also detected residual challenge virus DNA in tissue samples except for the kidney, indicating that the virus has not been completely eradicated (Figure 9(d)). But we failed to isolate live viruses from these tissue samples.

Necropsy and pathological analysis

No characteristic haemorrhagic lesions associated with ASFV were observed in the immune group (Groups 1 and 2); the gross morphology and structure of the organs remained essentially normal. In contrast, pigs in the challenge control group (Groups 3 and 4) exhibited haemorrhage, haemorrhages necrosis, and tissue necrosis in organs, consistent with the pathological characteristics of typical infection with ASFV (Figure S3(a)).

Histopathological examination (H&E staining) was conducted to further confirm the gross pathology results. Pigs sacrificed at 7, 14, and 21 days post-inoculation showed no pathological lesions in all organs. Groups 1 and 2 that survived the challenge of JX23-02 and SY18 maintained normal tissue architecture. No significant haemorrhage, necrosis, or severe pathological lesions were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, or lymph nodes. Mild atrophy of the splenic white pulp and lymphopenia were occasionally noted. For the pigs in Groups 3 and 4 that were challenged with JX23-02 and SY18 as controls, the liver, spleen, lungs, and lymph nodes exhibited extensive necrosis accompanied by haemorrhage (Figure S3(b)).

Discussion

ASFV has a large genome that encodes over 150 proteins with distinct functions. The expression of such a large number of proteins results in highly intricate virus–host interactions between ASFV and its host. However, the limited understanding of ASFV gene functions, particularly those associated with viral replication, immune evasion, and virulence, has severely impeded the development of vaccines. Our study demonstrated that D345L is a late gene for ASFV replication, and it localizes in the viral factory. The genotype I/II D345L gene-deletion mutant strain JX23-02ΔD345L exhibited attenuated virulence by inducing cellular apoptosis and pyroptosis, and it conferred partial protection to immunized pigs via the activation of the interferon signalling pathway.

In this study, the D345L gene-deleted mutant JX23-02ΔD345L can stably replicate in PAMs, demonstrating that D345L is a non-essential gene for ASFV replication, which was inconsistent with the findings in previous studies [16]. Laser confocal microscopy observation revealed that pD345L localized in the viral factory. Deletion of D345L significantly impaired viral replication capacity, indicating that D345L is highly correlated with viral replication. However, deletion of the D345L gene did not impair viral single-round replication, but significantly attenuated multistep replication, indicating that D345L is dispensable for intracellular virus replication but is essential for efficient cell-to-cell spread or virus infection. A smaller amount of mature virus particles containing the viral core was observed in the D345L gene-deleted strain, which was not the same as the reported TEM results of other gene-deleted strains. For instance, the absence of the E120R and H240R gene results in the formation of aberrant tubular structures [19,20]. These observations suggested that D345L may be involved in modulating viral assembly. However, this conclusion is only a superficial observation based solely on TEM and requires further validation due to the limited number of virions and the limitations of electron microscopy observation. The regulatory role of D345L in viral assembly and its severity remains to be further studied. It is generally believed that viral proteins expressed in the early stage play a role in regulating the expression of other proteins, or regulating the internal environment of host cells, such as promoting viral replication by regulating the interferon response [21–23]. Proteins expressed in the late stage are involved in viral assembly, virion formation, viral virulence, and apoptosis [24,25]. Our investigations into the JX23-02ΔD345L indicated that, as a late-expressed protein, the deletion of D345L not only affects viral assembly and attenuates viral virulence but also stimulates pyroptosis and apoptosis pathway proteins during the mid to late phase of viral replication. Furthermore, JX23-02ΔD345L stimulates the type I interferon pathway proteins in the late phase of replication, indicating that D345L is also closely associated with immune evasion.

Limited knowledge of ASFV pathogenicity and immune evasion mechanisms greatly restricts the development of safe and effective vaccines. In the process of ASFV replication, the host produces a series of antiviral responses to eliminate the invading virus. Accordingly, ASFV encodes a series of proteins to inhibit the occurrence of this response during reproduction. As an innate immune response, IFN plays an important role in the host antiviral response following ASFV infection. Previous studies have shown that viral proteins I73R, DP96R potently suppress the cGAS-STING-mediated activation of the IFN-β promoter [26,27]; I329L functions as an inhibitor of the TLR signalling pathway to repress IFN-β production [28]; E120R, MGF360-4L blocks IFN-β synthesis by inhibiting IRF3 phosphorylation [29,30]; E151R impairs type I IFN production through the degradation of the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRAF6 [31], MGF300-2R inhibits the host innate immune response by mediating the degradation of IKKα and IKKβa [32], and p22 inhibits the JAK-STAT signalling pathway by promoting the TAX1BP1-mediated degradation of the type I interferon receptor [33]. Taken together, these studies illustrated that ASFV has evolved a multifaceted strategy to inhibit IFN pathways to counter the host antiviral responses. These genes are also represented as promising targets for the development of gene-deletion ASF vaccines. For instance, the ASFV-GZΔI73R, with the I73R gene deleted, exhibited complete avirulence in pigs and provides effective protection against challenge with the parental ASFV strain [23]. The ASFV-SY18ΔE120R with deletion of the E120R gene was fully attenuated and partially protected against the challenge of the parental strain [19]. Additionally, former research has demonstrated that pD345L negatively regulates the cGAS-STING-induced NF-κB signalling pathway by blocking the activity of inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa-B kinases (IKKα/β), which consequently leads to reduced transcription of TNF-α, IFN-β, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1α, IL-6, and IL-8 [16]. In this study, we confirmed at the viral level that infection with JX23-02ΔD345L resulted in a marked up-regulation of p-STAT1, thereby promoting the up-regulation of ISGs. Taken together, these results suggested that D345L is highly correlated with viral immune evasion, highlighting its potential as a key target for gene-deletion vaccines.

ASFV dynamically modulates apoptosis and pyroptosis of host cells to create a favourable microenvironment for infection and replication [34–36]. In the early stage of infection, ASFV manipulates host signalling pathways with its proteins to inhibit apoptosis and pyroptosis, thereby prolonging viral replication. For instance, as a member of the inhibitor of apoptosis protein (IAP) family, A224L inhibits apoptosis by suppressing the expression of TNF-α and caspase-3 in the early stage of infection [37]. CD2v interacts with host colony-stimulating factor 2 receptor alpha (CSF2RA) to activate the JAK2-STAT3 pathway and inhibit apoptosis, in order to sustain cell viability and facilitate viral replication [38]. pA179L, a BCL-2 family apoptosis inhibitor, is important in reducing apoptosis in infected cells [39]. pH240R strongly inhibits the transcription, maturation, and secretion of IL-1β, and suppresses the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby reducing virulence [40]. In this study, we presented a new gene, D345L, which is highly correlated with apoptosis and pyroptosis. The absence of D345L results in the up-regulation of the inflammatory regulator IL-1β and pyroptosis factor GSDMD and the apoptosis-promoting factor BAX, demonstrating that D345L is an important component of ASFV in regulating host cell functions.

Although our studies of JX23-02ΔD345L demonstrated that deletion of the D345L gene altered innate immunity, antiviral signalling, pyroptosis, and apoptosis. These changes conferred a certain protection against ASFV in immunized pigs. However, our research only focused on phenotype-related alterations and did not directly investigate the function of pD345L; this represents a limitation of our study. In future work, we will concentrate on this aspect.

The identification of genotype I/II recombinant ASFV indicates that the virus naturally generates mutants with enhanced replication and immune evasion capabilities. Gene-deleted vaccines targeting genotype II cannot provide complete protection against these recombinant strains [11,12]. Moreover, mutant with the deletion of EP402R and MGF505/360 based on genotype I/II also fails to confer complete protection against homologous challenge, making the already difficult control of ASF even more challenging. Therefore, the research on ASFV virulence genes and the development of vaccines with favourable biosafety are of the most urgency. Our study confirmed that deletion of the D345L gene completely attenuated viral virulence, which is similar to the fully attenuated single-gene deletion vaccine obtained by deleting the E120R gene [19]. However, studies on the E120R-deletion strain only investigated protective efficacy against the parental strain, without evaluating cross-protection against heterologous strains. Our studies complement this limitation and provide a more comprehensive understanding. Although JX23-02ΔD345L only provided partial protection, it exhibits higher biosafety compared with other deletion mutants [41,42]. The gene I/II deletion strain JX23-02ΔD345L can provide partial protection against both the gene I/II parental strain and the gene II heterologous strain within the 21-day challenge observation period. However, viral DNA could be detected in tissues, indicating that the complete clearance of ASF was not achieved. With the extension of the observation period, there might be a risk of viral reactivation or even death. In future studies, we will explore methods such as increasing the immunization dose, increasing the number of immunizations, and adding the use of adjuvants to enhance the clearance effect of the virus, and extend the post-challenge observation period to further determine whether JX23-02ΔD345L has good safety, immunogenicity, and protective efficacy.

In conclusion, our research for the first time used the genotype I/II deletion mutant JX23-02ΔD345L as a research model, confirming that D345L is highly related to viral replication and immune evasion. pD345L is located in the viral factory and may be involved in viral assembly. JX23-02ΔD345L induces inflammation to reduce virulence and activates interferon signalling to provide partial protection in pigs. This study also indicated that the gene deletion strain based on the gene I/II type can provide protection against both the parental strain and the virulent strain of genotype II, offering a new approach for the development of safe and effective attenuated ASFV vaccines.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Changchun Veterinary Research Institute (Review ID: IACUC of CAS-12-2021-011). ASFV propagation and animal studies were conducted in an Animal Biosafety Level 3 (ABSL-3) laboratory.

Sixteen 4–6-week-old Landrace pigs weighing approximately 15 kg were randomly divided into four groups. Group 1: n = 6, inoculated with 105.0 50% tissue culture infective doses (TCID50) of JX23-02ΔD345L, challenged with 102.0 TCID50 parental strain JX23-02. Group 2: n = 6, inoculated with 105.0 TCID50 of JX23-02ΔD345L, challenged with 102.0 TCID50 SY18. Group 3: challenge control, n = 2, inoculated with Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (RPMI 1640), challenged with 102.0 TCID50 JX23-02. Group 4: challenge control, n = 2, inoculated with RPMI 1640, challenged with 102.0 TCID50 SY18. Pigs were euthanized at 7, 14, and 21 dpi for tissue collection (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, cervical lymph nodes, inguinal lymph nodes, mesenteric lymph nodes, tonsil, and bone marrow). Viral DNA of all blood samples, swabs, and tissues was quantified by qPCR. All tissue homogenates were filtered and infected in PAMs, then passaged every 5 days for a total of three passages. Body temperatures and other clinical symptoms were monitored daily post-challenge. Blood and swab samples were collected every 7 days. At the end of the observation period, all mentioned tissues and organs were collected for the detection of viral load by qPCR.

Cells and viruses

PAMs were prepared from bronchoalveolar lavage, and BMDMs were differentiated from the bone marrow of 2–3 months old piglets as previously described [43].

The ASFV genotype II SY18 strain (GenBank accession no. MH766894) was isolated from clinical samples during the first ASF outbreak in 2018, China. The ASFV genotype I/II JX23-02 strain (GenBank accession no. PP712069.1) was isolated from a diseased pig that succumbed in 2023, China. In the phylogenetic tree constructed based on the p72 gene, JX23-02 clustered in the same clade as genotype I and genotype I/II recombinant strains in the phylogenetic tree based on the p54 gene, JX23-02 clustered with genotype II strains and genotype I/II.

All the cells were subjected to DNA/RNA extraction and nucleic acid detection to exclude contamination with virus infection, which includes ASFV, classical swine fever virus (CSFV), porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV), pseudorabies virus (PRV), porcine parvovirus (PPV), and porcine circovirus 1/2 (PCV1/2). Furthermore, to remove contaminants in the culture media, cell cultures were harvested and centrifuged at 5,000×g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cell debris. The resulting cell culture supernatant was then centrifuged at 35,000×g for 2 h at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and slowly layered onto the top of a discontinuous sucrose gradient (20–40–60% w/v). After centrifugation at 30,000×g for 2 h at 4°C, the purified virus (a thin white band) between the 40% and 60% sucrose layers was collected, resuspended in PBS, and centrifuged at 30,000×g for 1.5 h at 4°C to remove sucrose.

Indirect immunofluorescence assay

Indirect immunofluorescence assay was performed to determine the localization of D345L as a method described previously, with some modifications [44]. Briefly, PAMs were infected with JX23-02 (MOI = 1). After 48 h, the PAMs were washed and then fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 30 min at 4°C. After blocking with 5% skimmed milk powder, mouse anti-pD345L serum and Cy3-labelled Goat Anti-mouse IgG (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), FITC-conjugated P54 monoclonal antibody (in-house prepared) [43] and Hochest (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were used to localize pD345L, p54, and nucleus. The results were observed under Laser Confocal Scanning Microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

Construction of recombinant JX23-02ΔD345L

Recombinant ASFV was generated by the homologous recombination method as previously reported [45]. The homologous recombinant transfer vector pΔD345L was constructed, which contains 1122 bp left homologous arm (nucleotides 141,039–142,160) and 1119 bp right homologous arm (nucleotides 143,198–144,316) of the D345L gene, and the mCherry gene was used as a fluorescence reporter under the control of the ASFV p72 promoter. The recombinant plasmid was transfected into BMDMs and then infected with JX23-02 with an MOI of 1. Fluorescent cells were selected, rinsed, and distributed into BMDMs in 96-well plates for two rounds. The recombinant virus was purified by an additional three rounds of limited dilution.

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis of JX23-02ΔD345L

To evaluate the genetic stability of JX23-02ΔD345L, the full-length sequence of the virus genomes was determined by next-generation sequencing. Total DNA was extracted from cell-cultured viruses, and 1 µg of DNA was used for sequencing using Illumia NovaSeq 6000, PE150 (Novogene Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China). The average depth is 240, and the total map is 44,949,765.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

PAMs were infected with either JX23-02 or JX23-02ΔD345L at an MOI of 1 and harvested at 12, 24, and 48 h. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 10 min and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C for 12 h. The fixed cell pellet, approximately the size of 3–5 mm3 in volume, was processed for sectioning and placed onto copper grids. Samples were subsequently stained with 2% osmium tetroxide and lead citrate. Ultrathin sections were observed and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Virus growth kinetics and TCID₅₀ assay

PAMs were infected with the parental strain JX23-02 and the mutant virus JX23-02ΔD345L, respectively, at an MOI of 0.01. Viruses were collected at 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and 144 hpi, and the viral titers were determined to generate virus growth curves. For a single-step growth curve, PAMs were infected at an MOI of 1 and collected at 6, 12, 24, and 48 hpi. PAMs were cultured in 96-well plates and infected with 10-fold serial dilutions of JX23-02 or JX23-02ΔD345L, with eight replicates for each dilution. Infected cells were detected by staining with FITC-conjugated anti-p30 monoclonal antibody (prepared in-house) and observing fluorescence-positive cells under a fluorescence microscope. Viral titers were calculated using the Reed-Muench method.

RNA-Seq analysis

PAMs were infected with either JX23-02 or JX23-02ΔD345L at an MOI of 1, and cells were harvested at 6, 12, and 24 hpi for RNA extraction. The samples were sent to Tsingke Biotechnology (Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) for RNA-seq analysis. GO functional significant enrichment analysis was performed to identify significantly enriched GO terms among differentially expressed genes, thereby revealing the biological functions with which these genes are significantly associated. Using the hypergeometric test, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to identify pathways that are significantly enriched in differentially expressed genes compared with the whole-genome background. GO functional enrichment was performed with a threshold of P ≤ 0.05 for significance.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Primers and methods were obtained in former studies [19,43]. Cytosine arabinoside was used as the inhibitor of late gene expression to confirm the late-expression profile of pD345L. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript FAST RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Dalian, China). RT-qPCR was conducted with TB Green (Takara, Dalian, China). GAPDH was used as a control to quantify the expression of target genes. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, using GAPDH as the housekeeping gene. The primers of D345L were: D345L-F: 5′-TGTCGATGTCCTCTGTGGAT-3′, D345L-R: 5′-CTACTTTGGATGGAAGGTC-3′.

LDH release

LDH Release Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used to compare the LDH release in uninfected PAMs, JX23-02 infected PAMs, or JX23-02ΔD345L-infected PAMs according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, PAMs infected with Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (RPMI 1640) (mock), JX23-02, or JX23-02ΔD345L were suspended at 400 g, 5 min at the time points of 6, 12, and 24 hpi. One-hundred and twenty microlitres of the supernatant from each well was taken, respectively, and added to the corresponding wells of a new 96-well plate, then 60 μL of LDH assay working solution was added to each well, respectively. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized and incubated at room temperature (approximately 25°C) in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, the optical density (OD) at 490 nm (OD490 nm) was measured using a microplate reader (BIO-RAD, USA). The value of OD490 nm showed a positive correlation with LDH activity.

Cell activity

Cell activity was evaluated in uninfected PAMs, JX23-02 infected PAMs or JX23-02ΔD345L-infected PAMs using Cell Counting Kit 8 (absin, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Ten microlitres CCK-8 was added into PAMs mentioned above at different time points of 6, 12, and 24 hpi. The culture plate was incubated at 37℃ for 4 h. OD450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BIO-RAD, USA). The value of OD450 nm showed a positive correlation with LDH activity.

Western blot

Total cellular proteins were extracted using Radio-Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) lysis buffer with EDTA and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1.5 h. The following primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C, anti-β-actin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), anti-p30 and anti-p72 (prepared in-house) [19], anti-pD345L (prepared in-house), anti-IL-1beta (CST, China), anti-GSDMD (proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-Caspase3 (CST, China), anti-Bcl2 (CST, China), anti-BAX (CST, China), anti-ISG15 (proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-p-STAT1 (CST, China), and anti-STAT1 (CST, China). After five washes with Tris-buffered saline Tween-20 (TBST), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 1 h. After three washes with TBST. Protein expression levels were detected using the Tanon 5200 imaging system (Tanon5200).

Quantification of the ASFV load

ASFV genomic DNA was extracted using the FastPure Viral DNA/RNA Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Probe qPCR targeting the ASFV B646L gene (encoding the p72 protein) was performed to determine the ASFV load in samples using Premix Ex Taq (probe qPCR) reagent (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). Copy numbers of the ASFV genome samples were calculated according to the standard curve established by the detection of the standard plasmid containing the ASFV B646L gene (p72).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs)

Indirect ELISA kits were used to detect p54 (prepared in-house) [19,43] and p30 (JNT, China) antibodies, and a blocking ELISA kit was used to detect p72 (LSBIO, China) antibodies following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, positive control (P, n = 2), negative control (N, n = 2), and serum samples (S) were added to the ELISA plate and incubated at 37℃ for 30 min. HRP-conjugated sheep anti-pig IgG was then added and incubated at 37℃ for 30 min. TMB substrate was added to initiate the colour reaction, and stop buffer was used to stop the reaction. The OD450 nm was measured using the iMark™ Microplate Reader (BIO-RAD, USA). S/P values and PI values were calculated under the instructions of the manufacturer.

Enzyme-linked immunospot assay (ELISpot)

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from EDTA-treated whole blood of pigs on day 21 dpi (Solarbio, China). ELISpot assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions (IMMUNOSpot, USA). Briefly, inactivated ASFV SY18 was added to the culture plate. PBMCs were added at 100 μL/well and incubated statically at 37℃ for 20 h. Porcine IFN-γ was added and incubated at room temperature for 2 h, followed by the addition of Strep-AP diluent and incubation at room temperature for 30 min. Colour development was achieved using a blue substrate solution for 15 min at room temperature, followed by rinsing with tap water. After air-drying, the spot-forming cells (SFC) were counted using the ELISpot reader system (IMMUNOSpot, USA), and the number of spots per well was converted to the number of spot-forming cells per million cells (SFC/10⁶ cells).

Detection of cytokines in pig serum

The levels of IFN-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, and TNF-α were detected to compare the differences in antiviral responses and inflammatory responses induced by JX23-02ΔD345L and parental strain JX23-02. Serums (three of JX23-02ΔD345L-inoculated pigs and two of JX23-02-inoculated pigs) were sent to LAIZEE BIOTECH to conduct the experiment. Briefly, 25 μL of standard substances and samples were added to the premixed microparticle cocktail. After incubation, premixed biotin-Ab and SA-PE were added in order. One-hundred and twenty microlitres Reading Buffer was added to read the plate by Luminex 200.

Pathological analysis

Heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and lymph nodes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed tissues were dehydrated, stained with haematoxylin, differentiated with hydrochloric alcohol, blued, stained with eosin, dehydrated with alcohol, cleared with a clearing agent, and mounted with neutral balsam. The sections were observed under a microscope and scanned for imaging to be used for pathological analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.6 software (Inc., La Jolla, USA). Differences between experimental and control groups were evaluated by Student's t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The error bars in all the experiments represent the standard deviation (±SD or SE). P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.005, ****P < 0.001; ns, not significant).
