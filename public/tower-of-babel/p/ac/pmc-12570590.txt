Age-stratified circulating immune signatures reveal non-invasive biomarkers for colorectal cancer detection
Authors: Julia del Prado-Montero, Jenny Guevara-Martínez, Ramón Cantero-Cid, Pablo Mata-Martínez, Francisco J Cueto, Roberto Lozano-Rodríguez, Verónica Terrón-Arcos, Rebeca Abad-Moret, Esteban Díaz-Serrano, Rebeca Pérez de Diego, María Fernández-Velasco, Carlos del Fresno, Laura Hurtado-Navarro, Eduardo López-Collazo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgeing is a well-recognised factor influencing immune competence; however, elderly individuals remain underrepresented in clinical trials, resulting in gaps in our understanding of their immune responses to disease. To address this gap, we investigated circulating soluble immune checkpoints (sICs) and cytokines in the elderly and middle-aged individuals, both healthy and patients with colorectal cancer (CRC). Our findings revealed a decline in sICs and cytokine levels in healthy elderly individuals compared to their middle-aged counterparts. Key analytes —Galectin-9, sLAG-3, sPD-L1 and sTIM-3— effectively distinguished age groups in healthy volunteers (HVs). However, these differences were not observed when applying this signature in CRC patients, where the combined analysis of plasma Galectin-9, IL-10 and CXCL10 did reliably discriminate between elderly CRC patients and age-matched HVs. This result highlights the potential of these immunological factors as non-invasive biomarkers for cancer detection in this population. In contrast, a much larger number of soluble markers was needed to distinguish CRC patients from HVs among the middle-aged. Collectively, these findings underscore the relevance of observing age stratification for biomarker discovery and lay the groundwork for further exploration of soluble immune mediators in ageing related to tumour pathophysiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12979-025-00535-7.Keywords: Ageing, Colorectal cancer, Soluble immune checkpoint, Cytokine, Immunosenescence, Biomarker

Introduction

The global trend of population ageing, accompanied by increasing life expectancy, has contributed significantly to the rising incidence of cancer among older individuals [1, 2]. Currently, approximately 20 million new cancer cases are diagnosed each year, with around 75% occurring in individuals aged 55 and above, thus reinforcing the notion of cancer as an ageing-related disease [3, 4]. Despite the crucial role of geriatric oncology in modern medicine, elderly patients remain markedly underrepresented in clinical trials, with less than 15% of participants in registered randomised controlled trials belonging to this demographic group [5–7].

One of the pathophysiological processes associated with age is “inflammaging”, a term first coined by Franceschi and colleagues in 2000. This concept establishes a link between three distinct fields of study: immunity, chronic inflammation, and ageing [8]. The underlying causes of this phenomenon are multifaceted, including genetic predisposition, environmental factors, the accumulation of senescent cells, chronic infections, and intestinal barrier disruption, among others [9]. Its sustained presence progressively impairs the body's ability to respond to stressors. When considered in the context of other age-related immune alterations, this chronic inflammatory state fosters a condition known as “immunosenescence”, which is characterised by the gradual deterioration of immune function [10].

In detail, immunosenescence encompasses structural and functional changes in immune organs, such as thymic involution, which leads to diminished production of T cells [11]. The resulting dysfunction affects innate and adaptive immunity alike, rendering elderly individuals more susceptible to infections, autoimmune diseases, and age-related malignancies [12]. Hallmarks of this decline include memory T cell exhaustion, reduction in the naïve T cell pool, and increased expression of inhibitory immune checkpoints (ICs) such as PD-1, LAG-3, and TIM-3 on T lymphocytes, all of which impair the capacity to generate effective antitumour responses [1, 13–15]. In parallel, chronic low-grade inflammation, the presence of autoantibodies, and the accumulation of immunosuppressive cell populations further compromise immune competence in ageing individuals [16–19]. Furthermore, the decline in humoral immunity, characterised by reduced B cell numbers and lower production of high-affinity antibodies, adds further complexity to the immunosenescent landscape [20, 21]. Collectively, these age-related changes hinder the antitumour efficacy of the immune system and limit the success of immunotherapies in elderly patients [22, 23].

Traditionally, immunological ageing has been assessed through alterations in immune cell surface markers, gene expression, or the presence of specific infections [24, 25]. However, such analyses often rely on advanced technologies, including flow cytometry, which may not be readily available in all clinical settings. Consequently, this underscores the pressing need to identify accessible, circulating biomarkers of this ageing that can be reliably translated into clinical practice [26].

Cytokines, as soluble mediators of inflammation, have long been implicated in the development and progression of cancer and other pathologies [27, 28]. Concurrently, ICs serve as key regulators of immune homeostasis and tolerance, rendering them especially relevant in age-related immune remodelling [29, 30]. Whilst previous studies have individually investigated soluble ICs (sICs), cytokine dynamics, ageing and cancer progression, the integrated relationship between these factors – particularly the combined profiling of sCIs and cytokines – across ageing and both healthy volunteers (HVs) and colorectal cancer (CRC) patients remains insufficiently characterised. Due to that, in the present study, we investigate these soluble immune mediators in HVs and CRC patients, aiming to shed light on their utility as biomarkers of immunosenescence and their potential to inform therapeutic strategies in ageing and cancer.

Materials and methods

Participant recruitment and eligibility criteria

Peripheral blood samples were collected from colorectal cancer (CRC) patients (n = 69; Table 1) at the Colorectal Surgery Department of La Paz University Hospital (Madrid, Spain), following written informed consent. All patients had a confirmed diagnosis of CRC prior to enrolment, were evaluated in the outpatient clinic, and were subsequently reviewed by the hospital’s Colorectal Tumour Committee. Detailed clinical information for this cohort is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Blood samples were collected from patients upon admission to the hospital, approximately 24 h before surgery. This indicates that none of the patients had received any prior treatment. For certain analyses, patients were stratified by age into two groups: ‘Middle-aged CRC’ (mean age 61 ± 8 years; range 33–69; n = 28) and ‘Elderly CRC’ (mean age 80 ± 6 years; range 70–91; n = 41). Although the World Health Organization (WHO) defines elderly individuals as those aged over 60, increasing life expectancy has prompted a revised interpretation, with the term now commonly referring to individuals aged 60–85 years [31]. For this study, a threshold of 70 years was used for age stratification.

The control group, obtained from the Donor Service and Digestive Surgery Service of the same hospital, comprised HVs (n = 54), categorised into ‘Middle-aged HVs’ (mean age 54 ± 7 years; range 41–68; n = 36) and ‘Elderly HVs’ (mean age 81 ± 5 years; range 75–92; n = 18) respectively. All control participants were free of acute illness or immunodeficiency and met the inclusion criteria at the time of recruitment (Table 1). Exclusion criteria included any form of immunodeficiency, ongoing immunomodulatory treatments, a history of ischaemic heart disease, chronic kidney disease, or any oncological or haematological malignancy within the preceding five years.

Plasma collection

Plasma samples were collected from EDTA-anticoagulated venous blood using a density gradient of Ficoll-Plus solution (GE Healthcare BioSciences), following standard centrifugation. Subsequently, plasma samples were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Soluble immune checkpoint and cytokine detection assay

sICs and cytokine concentrations in plasma samples were quantified using the LEGENDplex™ (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) determination assays, according to manufacturer instructions. Concretely, to measure sICs, the Human Immune Checkpoint Panel 1 (11 key soluble immune checkpoint proteins: CD25 (IL-2Ra), 4-1BB, CD27, B7.2 (CD86), CTLA-4, PD-L1, PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3, Galectin-9 and TGF-β) was used. For cytokine quantification, the Human Essential Immune Response Panel (13 human cytokines, including: interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17A, interferon (IFN)-γ, TNF-α, MCP-1, CXCL10 and, Free Active TGF-β) was used. Prepared samples were acquired on a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analysed using LEGENDplex software (v.8, Biolegend).

Statistical analysis and bioinformatic processing

Statistical analyses and graphical representation of the obtained results were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc). Normality of the samples was determined with the Shapiro–Wilk test. All data are shown as mean values and errors bars represent standard error (SEM). For two-group comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U test was used in non-parametric data, and the t-test was used in parametric data. The p values are indicated with asterisks, and ranges are noted in the figure legends; p > 0.05 was considered not significant (ns). Spearman correlation test was used to perform simple linear regression.

Binary logistic regression models were constructed using the backward Wald method with IBM SPSS v.23 (Armonk, NY). This stepwise approach starts with the full set of candidate independent variables and repeatedly removes and reintroduces them based on their statistical significance. For each variable retained in the final model, a corresponding coefficient (B-factor) was calculated to reflect its impact on the differentiation between groups. Final scores were obtained using the formula: Score = ([Parameter A] x B-factor A) + ([Parameter B] x B-factor B) + … + ([Parameter X] x B-factor X), as previously described [32, 33]. Classifying scores were used to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, where the area under the curve (AUC) and optimal cut-off values were obtained from the Youden index. The selection of the optimal regression model aimed to identify as few variables as possible while retaining those with the strongest statistical significance.

Moreover, for data UMAP visualization and hierarchical clustering, we used the software Graphext (Madrid, Spain), using Euclidean metrics, n_neighbors = 30, 127 n_components = 10 and min_dist = 0.01.

Results

Participants characteristics

Between 6 July 2020 and 18 June 2024, a total of 54 HVs and 69 CRC patients were recruited at La Paz University Hospital. HVs, recruited in the Donor Service, aged 41 to 68 were categorised as middle-aged HVs, while those aged 75 to 92, recruited in the Digestive Surgery Service, were classified as elderly. CRC patients were similarly stratified into middle-aged or elderly groups based on whether they were under or over 70 years of age, respectively (Table 1). As previously stated, this stratification has been conducted in accordance with the WHO definition of ageing [31].

Age-related variations in soluble immune checkpoint and cytokine profiles in healthy volunteers

To investigate the hypothesis that age influences the expression of circulating immunological factors, we analysed the levels of a panel of sICs (s4-1BB, sB7.2, sCD25, sCD27, sCTLA-4, Galectin-9, sLAG-3, sPD-1, sPD-L1, sTGF-β and sTIM-3) and cytokines (IL-1b, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17, IFN-g, CXCL10, MCP-1, TNF-a, and TFG-b) in plasma samples from a cohort of 54 HVs. The age range of the cohort was from middle-aged to elderly.

Unsupervised data analysis using Graphex enabled the unbiased identification of three distinct clusters that clearly differentiated elderly (cluster 1) from middle-aged (clusters 2 and 3) HVs (Fig. 1A). No age- or sex-related differences were observed between clusters 2 and 3 (Supplementary Fig. 1 A and B). In terms of sICs secretion, levels of sB7.2, sCD27, Galectin-9, sPD-L1, sPD-1 and sTIM-3 were significantly lower in the elderly group compared to the middle-aged cohort (Fig. 1B). Conversely, no significant differences were observed in s4-1BB, sCD25, sCTLA-4, sLAG-3 or sTGF-β levels (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). As shown in Fig. 1C, levels of several cytokines—including IL-1β, IL-2, IL-10, IL-12p70 and IFN-γ—were also significantly reduced in the elderly. In contrast, no age-related differences were found in IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-17A, CXCL10, MCP-1, TGF-β and TNF-α levels (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

Combined soluble immune checkpoint and cytokine profiles distinguish middle-aged from elderly healthy volunteers, but not colorectal cancer patients

Given the observed differences in sICs and cytokine levels between middle-aged and elderly HVs, we sought to identify specific patterns that could serve as hallmarks of age-related immune status. To this end, we applied a binary logistic regression model with backward stepwise selection, incorporating all the immunological variables assessed. This approach iteratively removes the least informative predictors at each step, ultimately yielding a model comprising only statistically significant variables, independent of their individual contributions in isolation [32, 33].

The resulting model generated a composite score based on four key factors —Galectin-9, sLAG-3, sPD-L1 and sTIM-3— highlighting their association with ageing. Evaluation of the model via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis yielded an optimal cut-off value of 182, determined using the Youden index, corresponding to a sensitivity of 82.41–100.0 and a specificity of 90.36–100.0 (95% CI). This threshold demonstrated perfect sensitivity and specificity, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.000 (95% CI: 1.000–1.000; p < 0.0001; Fig. 2A). The cut-off effectively distinguished middle-aged from elderly HVs (Fig. 2B), independently of the biological sex (Fig. 2C-D), and a Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association (r = −0.6152, p < 0.0001) showing a progressive decline in values with increasing age (Fig. 2E).

We next tested whether this scoring system could similarly stratify CRC patients by age. However, when applied to the CRC cohort, the same cut-off failed to discriminate between middle-aged and elderly patients (Supplementary Fig. 2).

These findings suggest that while this combined analysis of sICs and cytokines is a robust marker of ageing in HVs, its discriminatory power may be limited in the context of disease, such as CRC.

Elevated levels of soluble immunological factors in middle-aged colorectal cancer patients compared to healthy volunteers, are indicative of disease presence

We first compared the levels of sICs and cytokine levels between the middle-aged individuals, comprising HVs (mean age 54 ± 7 years; range 41–68); n = 36) and CRC patients (mean age 61 ± 8 years; range 33–69; n = 28).

The expression levels of the sICs sCD25, sCD27, Galectin-9 and sTIM-3 (Fig. 3A), along with the cytokines IL-1β, IL-8 and CXCL10 (Fig. 3B), were elevated in CRC patients relative to HVs. In contrast, the remaining soluble analytes —s4-1BB, sB7.2, sCTLA-4, sLAG-3, sPD-1, sPD-L, sTGF-β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17A, IFN-γ, MCP-1, TGF-β and TNF-α— did not show statistically significant differences between groups (Supplementary Fig. 3A–B).

Following a 12-step selection process, the final optimised model incorporated a combination of 12 soluble analytes: sB7.2, sCD25, sCTLA-4, IL-2, IL-8, IL-12p70, IFN-γ, CXCL10, sLAG-3, MCP-1, sPD-L1 and TNF-α. A specific pattern of soluble immunological factors of potential clinical application identifies elderly colorectal cancer

Given the importance of identifying novel markers that illuminate age-related processes, we compared sICs and cytokine levels between elderly HVs (mean age 81 ± 5 years; range 75–92; n = 18) and CRC patients (mean age 80 ± 6 years; range 70–91; n = 41). Notably, the sICs s4-1BB, sB7.2, sCD25, sCD27, Galectin-9, sPD-1, sPD-L1 and sTIM-3 (Fig. 5A), as well as the cytokines IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IFN-γ and CXCL10 (Fig. 5B), were significantly elevated in CRC patients compared with HVs. Additional analytes, including sCTLA-4, sLAG-3, sTGF-β, IL-2, IL-17A, MCP-1 and TNF-α were also assessed; however, no significant differences were observed between the groups (Supplementary Fig. 4A–B).

After identifying distinct expression patterns in elderly HVs and CRC patients, our next objective was to evaluate their predictive potential for identifying the presence of the disease in this cohort.

Although random clustering based on the full set of studied variables allows for group separation (Fig. 6A), a binary logistic regression model was applied to achieve a more efficient variable reduction while maintaining discriminatory power. Employing a backward stepwise regression approach incorporating all the immunological variables analysed (sICs and cytokines), the final optimised model incorporated a combination of Galectin-9, IL-10 and CXCL10. This predictive tool demonstrated exceptional performance, yielding an AUC value of 1.000 (95% confidence interval: 1.000–1.000) as determined by ROC analysis (Fig. 6B). The analysis generated an optimal cut-off value of 97.50, as estimated using the Youden Index. When applied as a comparative metric between elderly HVs and CRC patients, this threshold effectively discriminated between the two groups, with higher levels observed in the latter (Fig. 6C). Random clustering of this cohort using the variables selected by the model not only preserves but also enhances group differentiation (Fig. 6D). These findings underscore the strong discriminatory power of the predictive model in identifying ageing populations with CRC.

Discussion

Biomarkers of ageing, including sICs and cytokines, are increasingly recognised as essential tools for elucidating the mechanisms underpinning age-related diseases, notably cancer. In CRC, a malignancy predominantly affecting older adults [34], immunological alterations associated with ageing, particularly those involving IC pathways, are likely to influence both disease progression and treatment outcomes. In addition, ageing is accompanied by a persistent, low-grade inflammatory milieu, commonly referred to as inflammaging, which further shapes the tumour microenvironment and may contribute to cancer initiation and therapeutic resistance. Importantly, both immunosenescence and inflammaging may directly impair the efficacy of IC inhibitors in older patients, by fostering immunosuppressive niches and dysfunctional immune responses [8]. This is especially relevant in light of the clinical success of ICs inhibitors across multiple cancer types [25, 35]. Yet, older adults remain significantly underrepresented in cancer immunotherapy trials, creating a knowledge gap that hampers therapeutic optimisation for this demographic group [5, 7].

The primary aim of this study was to explore differences in sICs and cytokine levels between middle-aged and elderly HVs. We observed a marked decline in sICs —including sB7.2, sCD27, Galectin-9, sPD-L1, sPD-1 and sTIM-3— as well as in the cytokines IL-1β, IL-2, IL-10, IL-12p70 and IFN-γ with increasing age. Furthermore, a ROC analysis demonstrated a robust AUC for a score including Galectin-9, sLAG-3, sPD-L1 and sTIM-3 as predictors of age in HVs. These results reinforce the need to characterise age-specific immune signatures and identify robust biomarkers of immunosenescence. In contrast, within the CRC cohort the discriminative capacity of these candidate biomarkers was reduced, suggesting that tumour-associated factors, such as stage, localisation, and patterns of immune infiltration, may exert a more pronounced influence on the immune landscape than chronological age alone [36, 37]. Furthermore, systemic inflammation and tumour-induced immune dysregulation could alter circulation levels of soluble ICs, potentially overriding the age-related differences observed in HVs. Likewise, as cancer is frequently associated with features of immunosenescence, middle-aged CRC patients may already exhibit an aged immune phenotype, thereby reducing the utility of the score generated from healthy individuals [38, 39].

According to our data, the differences between CRC patients and HVs in the middle-aged group are characterised by a low number of ICs (sCD25, sCD27, Galectin-9 and sTIM-3) and cytokines (IL-1β, IL-8 and CXCL10). In order to obtain a precise discrimination between both cohorts, the model required an extensive number of immunological markers —sB7.2, sCD25, sCTLA-4, IL-2, IL-8, IL-12p70, IFN-γ, CXCL10, sLAG-3, MCP-1, sPD-L1 and TNF-α— resulting in an AUC of 1. Despite its effectiveness, the implementation of this model in clinical practice may be challenging due to the extensive number of soluble markers required.

On the contrary, a plethora of soluble immune mediators —including s4-1BB, sB7.2, sCD25, sCD27, Galectin-9, sPD-1, sPD-L1, sTIM-3, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IFN-γ and CXCL10— were significantly elevated in elderly CRC patients compared with age-matched HVs. This systemic increase likely reflects the immune dysregulation commonly associated with malignancy in elderly individuals [30, 40]. Among these, the combined quantification of Galectin-9, IL-10 and CXCL10 yielded high accuracy in discriminating elderly CRC patients from their healthy counterparts. These soluble factors, which have been individually reported to increase with age, possess immunomodulatory functions within the context of cancer [41–43]. The Galectin-9/TIM-3 axis has been demonstrated to suppress anti-tumour immunity, both innate and adaptive, with the consequence of promoting T cell exhaustion and immunosuppression [44, 45]. IL-10 is a highly effective anti-inflammatory cytokine that, in certain cases, has been observed to suppress effective anti-tumour immunity [46]. Also, it has been established that the recruitment of activated immune cells, such as T lymphocytes and natural killer cells, to the tumour microenvironment is a key feature of the action of CXCL10. However, it is important to note that excessive and chronic elevations of this chemokine can lead to immune exhaustion and persistent inflammation within tumours [47]. Collectively, these markers reflect critical pathways altered in CRC and support their utility as a combined signature distinguishing elderly CRC patients from healthy counterparts.

As previously discussed, immune-senescence is defined as a natural complex physiological process in which organ functions slowly change with age, highlighting thymic involution [11, 30]. It has been well established that circulating lymphocyte counts decline with age [21, 30, 48], and that T lymphocytes, in particular, become increasingly prone to senescence, anergy, and exhaustion [30, 35]. These processes impair their effector function and reduce the production of key immunomodulatory molecules such as IFN-γ [49, 50]. Additionally, age-related immune dysfunction has been linked to the upregulation of surface IC proteins —including sPD-1, sCTLA-4, sTIM-3, sLAG-3, and sTIGIT— on immune cells [22, 51].

Interestingly, our findings reveal that plasma levels of sICs were lower in elderly HVs. It is important to highlight that surface expression of IC molecules on lymphocytes does not necessarily correlate with their soluble concentrations in plasma or serum. This discrepancy can be attributed to the distinct mechanisms governing sIC generation, which include alternative splicing and proteolytic cleavage [52, 53] and are further regulated by enzyme activity, microenvironmental factors, and systemic inflammation [54, 55]. Furthermore, the distribution of immune responses across different anatomical regions, such as lymphoid tissues or tumour microenvironments, may not be reflected in the circulation [56]. Thus, surface-bound and sICs should be viewed as complementary but non-redundant indicators of immune function.

A similar principle applies to cytokines. The secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines by senescent cells forms a core component of the ageing process [10, 22]. In the present study, these were found to be elevated in patients diagnosed with CRC, irrespective of age, and in the healthy middle-aged population when compared to elderly individuals. Given the variability in cytokine release influenced by multiple factors and their inherently short half-life [10, 57–60], integrating cytokines with other soluble immunological factors, such as ICs, enhances the robustness of our data in identifying novel age-related immune biomarkers with potential clinical significance.

Taken together, this study provides a comprehensive characterisation of sICs and cytokines in a cohort of HVs and CRC patients across both middle-aged and elderly populations. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The study design was based on a single blood sample per participant, thereby precluding longitudinal analysis of immune ageing trajectories. Its cross-sectional nature further limited comparisons to inter-individual variation, rather than capturing within-subject immune dynamics over time. In addition, the cohort was geographically confined to the Madrid region, potentially limiting the generalisability of the findings to broader populations.

Nonetheless, in consideration of existing evidence, this study makes a significant contribution through the implementation of an age-stratified analysis of soluble ICs and cytokines in both healthy individuals and CRC patients, thereby offering insights into their potential as biomarkers of immunosenescence.

Future research should aim to longitudinally monitor immune parameters within individuals across time, incorporating deep profiling of immune cell subsets. Moreover, immune cell infiltration within tumour tissues should be analysed and correlated with systemic levels of soluble immune mediators. This integrative approach will enhance our understanding of how immune ageing manifests at both systemic and tissue-specific levels, and its implications for cancer development and therapeutic response.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive characterisation of the soluble immune profile in the elderly population, uncovering distinct age-related signatures that appear to be disrupted in the context of CRC. Notably, soluble Galectin-9, IL-10 and CXCL10 emerged as promising biomarkers for CRC detection in elderly individuals. While these findings represent an important step towards identifying soluble indicators of immune-senescence, further investigations are warranted to elucidate their broader implications for the ageing immune system and their potential utility in guiding novel therapeutic strategies in oncology.
