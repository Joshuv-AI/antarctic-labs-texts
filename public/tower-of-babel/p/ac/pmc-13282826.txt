Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of the Efficacy of Shorter Duration Modified Constraint-Induced Movement Therapy (mCIMT) Programs
Authors: Pavlina Psychouli, Charalambos Anastasiou, Ioannis Mamais, Nefeli Efstathia Papasalourou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy of a specific group of modified constraint-induced movement therapy (mCIMT) protocols, including ≤39 hours of therapist-supervised training combined with ≥10 h/d of restraint, on upper limb motor function in adults with chronic stroke.Data SourcesPubMed, Scopus, EBSCO, and the Cochrane Library were systematically searched up to March 20, 2025, using MeSH terms related to stroke, constraint-induced therapy, and upper limb function.Study SelectionRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adults with chronic stroke who received mCIMT compared with conventional rehabilitation were included. Exclusion criteria included multimodal interventions or insufficient outcome data. Of 6486 records, 5 RCTs (n=239) met the inclusion criteria.Data ExtractionTwo reviewers independently extracted study and participant characteristics, intervention details, and upper limb outcomes. Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials-2, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE).Data SynthesisMeta-analysis revealed significant larger improvements for the intervention group compared with controls posttreatment: Wolf Motor Function Test Functional Ability (mean difference [MD]=0.59; 95% CI, 0.23-0.94), Motor Activity Log Quality of Movement (MD=1.08; 95% CI, 0.61-1.55), and Amount of Use (MD=1.01; 95% CI, 0.24-1.79). Wolf Motor Function Test Performance Time improved modestly (standardized mean difference=-0.36; 95% CI: -0.68 to -0.04). At follow-up, only Motor Activity Log scores demonstrated sustained gains. Heterogeneity was moderate to high for some outcomes, and overall evidence strength was rated very low because of small sample sizes and high risk of bias.ConclusionsmCIMT protocols requiring fewer therapist hours but extended restraint time yield meaningful improvements in upper limb function among chronic stroke survivors, with real-world use gains persisting at follow-up. These findings suggest that optimizing, rather than maximizing, therapy intensity may improve feasibility and adherence, supporting broader clinical adoption. Further large-scale, high-quality RCTs with long-term follow-up are warranted to refine dosing guidelines and tailor interventions to patient subgroups.KEYWORDS: Constraint-induced movement therapy, Occupational therapy, Physical therapy, Rehabilitation, Stroke, Upper extremity

Stroke motor rehabilitation is a field of significant scientific interest, driven primarily by the increase in the absolute number of incident (70%) and prevalent (85%) strokes, as well as deaths (43%) from stroke over the last 30 years.1 One of the most debilitating consequences of stroke is upper limb dysfunction, which severely limits the ability to perform daily activities.2,3 Different treatment options have been explored by researchers over the last years with constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT) remaining one of the most researched motor intervention method in stroke rehabilitation.4

The aim of CIMT is to reverse learned nonuse, and it consists of 3 main elements: (1) intensive and gradually increased in difficulty training of the affected upper limb; (2) restriction of the unaffected upper limb; and (3) a comprehensive behavioral transfer package (TP) designed to ensure that the more affected limb is used during most waking hours outside therapy.5

Despite the promising functional benefits of CIMT, its clinical application remains limited because of the demanding duration and intensity required for both therapists and patients. Over the past decades, various modified versions, collectively known as “modified CIMT” (mCIMT), have been developed to create more practical protocols while preserving the therapy’s effectiveness. These protocols vary in intensity, duration, frequency, and delivery methods.6 In many cases, however, mCIMT differs from the classic CIMT protocol not only in therapy intensity but also by substantially reducing or even omitting the TP. This modification may influence overall effectiveness, as evidence indicates that the TP is one of the strongest contributors to real-world functional improvement.7,8 On the other hand, although numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of mCIMT and its superiority over conventional approaches,5,9,10 few have evaluated the same protocol. This highlights significant variability in mCIMT protocols, with limited evidence supporting each specific variation.

A recent systematic review categorized mCIMT protocols based on total treatment duration and splint wearing time.11 The findings revealed statistically significant improvements (P<.05) in upper limb motor function across nearly all measures, regardless of the protocol’s duration and intensity. Although intensity is a core component of CIMT, studies suggest that higher intensity doesn’t always lead to better outcomes, especially in early or severely impaired stroke patients. This may be because of factors like fatigue, reduced compliance, or cognitive overload, indicating that optimal rather than maximal dosing may be key to effectiveness.12, 13, 14, 15 However, the systematic review9 noted that most researchers favored moderate-to-low total treatment durations (<39h) combined with moderate to high restriction times (>10 h/d).11

Study aim

The objective of this study was to investigate the functional effects on upper limbs of the most widely implemented mCIMT protocols reported in the literature. The review was restricted to individuals with chronic stroke, as chronicity may constitute a critical factor influencing protocol effectiveness.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis have been registered in PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024497710). The investigators conducted this systematic review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.16 This study has received an exemption from the Cyprus National Bioethics Committee.

Study selection

The Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, Study design (PICOS) framework was used to formulate the research question, as follows: the population included chronic stroke adults, the intervention was mCIMT, the comparison group participated in any other physiotherapy/occupational therapy protocol, the outcomes of interest were related to upper limb function, and the study design included randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Inclusion criteria

In addition to the above, this review included studies in which one of the interventions consisted of mCIMT, defined as total therapist-supervised training time of ≤39 hours combined with restraint of the less affected upper limb for ≥10 h/d. One of the comparison groups received alternative forms of rehabilitation or therapeutic protocols. The literature search encompassed studies published up to March 20, 2025.

Exclusion criteria

Studies involving participants with acute and subacute stroke or other neurologic disorders were excluded, as well as those that did not examine the effects of mCIMT on upper limb motor function. Studies were also excluded if mCIMT was combined with other interventions, such as pharmaceutical treatments (eg, BTX-A), or if they were incomplete, lacked specific numeric results, or were classified as gray literature. Studies focusing on kinematic analyses or brain physiology measures were also excluded.

Study selection

Studies were selected through a 2-step screening process. In the first step, 2 independent reviewers screened all identified records for relevance through title and abstract to exclude those that did not meet the selection criteria at this stage. In the second step, full-text articles of the studies were reviewed to ensure they met the selection criteria. Final decisions were made after an additional review by a third reviewer.

Data extraction

The investigators extracted key variables from the studies and summarized them in tables. These tables classified the studies based on the treatment protocol, including therapist time, hand restriction time, and protocol distribution in time, as well as outcome measures related to upper limb motor function. Data analysis was performed using Microsoft 365 Excel (2021).a

Risk of bias assessment

Two researchers (S.N. and M.Z.) independently assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials-2. This tool evaluates potential bias in 5 domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results.17 Each domain was rated as low, high, or having some concerns. Based on the ratings in these domains, studies were assigned an overall risk of bias rating of low, some concerns, or high. Conflicting assessments were resolved through the involvement of a third researcher (C.A.). The strength of evidence was determined using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.

Data synthesis and analysis

Quantitative synthesis was conducted through a meta-analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of mCIMT in improving upper limb motor function among chronic stroke survivors. Meta-analytic procedures were performed using Review Manager (RevMan 5.4) software,b applying fixed- or random-effects models based on the degree of heterogeneity.

Effect sizes were calculated as either mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% CIs, depending on the scale and consistency of measurement units across studies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with thresholds set at 25% (low), 50% (moderate), and 75% (high) to guide model selection and interpretation. Subgroup analyses compared posttreatment effects with follow-up outcomes, whereas chi-square tests were employed to assess differences between time points.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted when appropriate to assess the robustness of findings, particularly in relation to study quality and sample size.

Strength of evidence

Based on the GRADE framework, the strength of evidence was very low for all outcome measures; thus, the confidence in the outcomes is very limited.

Outcome measures

This review examined outcome measures related to upper limb motor function, with the meta-analysis limited to those reported in >1 study. The most frequently employed measures were the Motor Activity Log (MAL), assessing both quality of movement (MAL-QOM) and amount of use (MAL-AOU), and the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), evaluating performance time (WMFT-T), and functional ability (WMFT-FA). Three studies included follow-up assessments: one at 1 month, one at 3 months, and one at 6 months postintervention. Follow-up evaluations were conducted relative to baseline, with all 3 studies reporting both within-group and between-group comparisons (table 5).

Meta-analysis

Wolf Motor Function Scale Performance Time

Three studies were included in the analysis for WMFT-T.133,135,136 The pooled SMD for the posttreatment period was -0.36 (95% CI, -0.68 to -0.04; I2=0%; P=.42). In the follow-up period, the pooled SMD was -0.18 (95% CI, -0.85 to 0.50; I2=61%; P=.08; fig 2).

Wolf motor function scale performance time.

Wolf Motor Function Scale Functional Ability

Two studies were included in the analysis for WMFT-FA.132,134 The posttreatment pooled mean difference was 0.59 (95% CI, 0.23-0.94; I2=0%; P=.32). However, at follow-up, the pooled MD was 0.46 (95% CI, -0.75 to 1.67; I2=88%; P=.004; fig 3).

Motor activity log quality of movement

Three studies were included in the analysis for MAL-QOM.132,134,135 The pooled MD for posttreatment was 1.08 (95% CI, 0.61-1.55; I2=61%; P=.08). The follow-up analysis produced a pooled MD of 1.02 (95% CI, 0.67-1.37, I2=0%, P=.81; fig 4).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that mCIMT protocols with ≤39 hours of therapist-supervised training and ≥10 hours of restraint per day produce meaningful improvements in upper limb motor function among individuals with chronic stroke. Consistent posttreatment benefits were observed in real-world limb use (eg, MAL), motor capacity (eg, Fugl-Meyer Assessment), and functional ability (eg, Wolf Motor Function Test), with some improvements maintained at follow-up.

More specifically, the meta-analysis revealed statistically significant improvements across all outcome measures posttreatment. However, at follow-up, only the MAL demonstrated sustained gains. The overall strength of evidence remains very low, primarily because of the limited number of studies and the fact that one134 of the 3 studies that included follow-up measurements had a very small sample size, leading to nonsignificant results throughout the study duration.

Nevertheless, when the goal is to improve functionality, it is essential to apply the MAL at regular follow-up intervals. The MAL is used in CIMT not primarily as an outcome measure but as a key component of the behavioral TP. Items on the MAL to which patients initially respond with “0” or other low scores are typically embedded into the training schedule to promote increased use of the affected hand and arm across a variety of activities—even unilateral tasks that patients would not normally attempt once the restraint is removed. As a result, improvements in MAL scores are expected during any CIMT program. These statistically significant changes mainly reflect the unmasking of the limb’s existing functional potential rather than new gains in capacity. The most meaningful indicator of real-world, lasting improvement is whether MAL scores remain elevated—or continue to increase—at follow-up, when the nonaffected hand is no longer restrained. This sustained improvement is one of the key reasons why follow-up MAL assessments should always be included in CIMT studies. Importantly, in this review, the 2 higher quality studies132,135 included both reported sustained MAL improvements at follow-up.

These findings align with and extend previous literature. Previous reviews show wide variability in mCIMT protocols (eg, 30min to 3 h/d for task practice, with daily restraint periods ranging widely), but still identify measurable benefit even with reduced intensity schemes.11 A recent metaregression136 found that in chronic stroke, better MAL outcomes are observed with a constraint of ≥6 h/d and about 6 hours of training per week. Daily intervention time was not found to lead to a significant difference in functional upper limb performance. Another meta-analysis of mCIMT137 demonstrated favorable effects on upper limb motor function in chronic stroke patients compared with standard care. The study suggested that the intervention period should be ≥2 weeks, and each training session should last between 30 and 60 min/d to yield positive outcomes, without, however, specifying the type of stroke patients that might be an important moderating factor. It might be the case that longer intervention durations may reduce patient interest and adherence, ultimately diminishing the rehabilitation outcomes.138

Another important issue concerns the partial or complete use of the TP. Within the studies included in this review, some implemented a substantial portion of the original TP,135 others incorporated partial TP components,133,134 whereas others did not include identifiable TP elements at all, representing a notable deviation from the original protocol.131,132 This partial or absent TP constitutes a modification of the original CIMT model that is evident in many mCIMT studies, which, however, may reduce overall effectiveness,7,110 especially when combined with reduced supervised training. When TP elements are included, even partially, the nature of the therapy shifts from an “intensity-driven” model to a distribution-of-practice model, where less time is spent in therapist-led shaping, but more total time may be spent using the affected limb in the real-world, through structured, supported, or self-directed activities. Thus, reduced supervised training does not necessarily reflect lower total therapeutic input; rather, it reflects reallocating effort toward unsupervised, ecologically valid functional use, which is the very mechanism the TP was designed to exploit. This offers a plausible explanation for why positive results persisted in studies with fewer supervised hours.

This review adds to the literature by specifically isolating a protocol defined by reduced therapist contact time coupled with prolonged restraint. In contrast to earlier work57,59 suggesting a linear dose-response relationship, this analysis supports the growing view that “optimal” rather than “maximal” dosing yields the most clinically meaningful outcomes. This is particularly important for chronic stroke survivors, who may be less tolerant of high-intensity training because of fatigue, comorbidities, or reduced motivation.

The clinical implications are important. By demonstrating efficacy in protocols requiring fewer therapist hours, this study supports the integration of mCIMT into routine practice, especially in resource-limited settings. A protocol requiring fewer than 39 hours of therapist-supervised practice per week can be more easily implemented in either inpatient rehabilitation centers or outpatient settings compared with the original CIMT protocol. Additionally, supervision delivered through telerehabilitation may offer a cost-effective and efficient alternative.121,139,140 High adherence to splint use, combined with structured but feasible training, may explain the observed functional gains. This may also underscore the importance of including the home-administered components of CIMT, most of which are included in what is known as the TP. These results suggest that mCIMT can help overcome one of the main barriers to CIMT implementation—its intensity and time demands—thereby broadening access to evidence-based stroke rehabilitation.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. Only 5 RCTs met the inclusion criteria, with most rated as having a high risk of bias. Sample sizes in included trials remain modest, increasing the risk of type II error for some outcomes. As a matter of fact, the very low strength of evidence revealed in GRADE is mostly because of small sample sizes, few studies testing the specific mCIMT protocol, and large heterogeneity in effect sizes, probably reflecting the variability in patient characteristics but also the variations in the implementation of the protocol. Many of the included studies assessed only short-term postintervention outcomes; long-term follow-up was less common, making it challenging to know how durable the effects are. Finally, participant characteristics (severity of impairment, cognitive status, and so forth) may moderate response, but the current literature, including this study, lacks sufficient power to explore these subgroups fully.

Future research should focus on RCTs with sufficient sample numbers comparing different mCIMT dosing protocols11 to define thresholds beyond which additional intensity yields minimal improvement. Adding follow-up measurements of ≤6 months is crucial in order to clarify the durability of gains. Finally, stratifying by patient baseline characteristics (eg, impairment severity, motivation, and cognitive status) may allow tailoring of mCIMT to those most likely to benefit.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study supports the effectiveness of mCIMT protocols that combine moderate therapist input with substantial daily restraint for improving upper limb motor function in chronic stroke patients. These findings suggest that clinically feasible, less resource-intensive versions of mCIMT can achieve outcomes close to those of more intensive protocols, highlighting the importance of optimizing rather than maximizing treatment intensity.
