Prenatal diagnosis of fetus in fetu with spontaneous movement of the parasitic twin
Authors: Takumi Sano, Hideki Tomiyama, Tomohito Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFetus in fetu (FIF) is a rare congenital anomaly in which a malformed parasitic twin is enclosed within its host. A right-sided fetal intra-abdominal mass was incidentally detected during routine antenatal ultrasonography at 26 weeks of gestation in a 40-year-old gravida 4, para 2 woman. Ultrasonography at 27 weeks showed a right-sided, well-encapsulated solid and cystic mass with skeletal elements and gentle arterial flow toward the solid component. Real-time assessment revealed a brief spontaneous adduction-like inward movement of the parasitic lower limb, suggestive of FIF rather than teratoma. At 35 weeks, fetal magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a well-encapsulated right intra-abdominal mass with internal low-signal-intensity structures corresponding to the bony elements seen on ultrasonography. Following birth, the mass was surgically excised and histopathology confirmed FIF. This case illustrates the diagnostic value of detailed dynamic imaging for recognizing FIF and may contribute to the understanding of its pathophysiology.Keywords: Case report, Fetus in fetu, Prenatal diagnosis, Ultrasonography

Introduction

Fetus in fetu (FIF) is a rare congenital anomaly [[1], [2], [3]] in which a malformed parasitic twin is found within the body of its host. The estimated incidence is approximately 1 in 500,000 live births. With the advancement of prenatal imaging modalities, such as ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), FIF can now be diagnosed before birth [3,4], with typical characteristics including an encapsulated mass containing a vertebral axis and organogenesis, differentiating it from a teratoma that lacks such organization [2,3]. Here, we report a rare case of FIF, in which spontaneous lower-limb movement of the parasitic fetus was clearly visualized on prenatal ultrasonography, suggesting possible residual neuromuscular activity.

Case report

A 40-year-old gravida 4, para 2 woman with a history of one spontaneous abortion at 6 weeks of gestation had no significant medical or obstetric history, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy or eclampsia, and received routine antenatal care at a local clinic. At 26 weeks of gestation, a fetal intra-abdominal mass was incidentally detected during routine antenatal ultrasonography. A detailed ultrasound examination at 27 weeks of gestation revealed a well-encapsulated solid and cystic mass measuring 32 mm in diameter, with a 19-mm solid component, located near the lower pole of the right kidney. The solid component comprised highly echogenic foci, consistent with bone-like structures. Color Doppler imaging revealed faint arterial flow directed toward the solid component, and a structure resembling a vertebral column was identified, suggesting the diagnosis of FIF rather than a teratoma. Ultrasound also demonstrated male external genitalia in the parasitic twin, consistent with the male gender of the host fetus (Fig. 1).

During real-time ultrasound examination, spontaneous movement of the parasitic lower limb was noted (Supplementary Video 1). The motion appeared to be a brief adduction-like inward movement of the leg rather than typical flexion or extension. As the limb was markedly deformed and internally rotated, precise characterization of the movement was difficult.

Serial ultrasonography at 30 and 32 weeks of gestation showed persistence of the encapsulated mass with increasingly recognizable vertebral and long-bone structures and external morphology (Fig. 1C-E). The mass enlarged in proportion to fetal growth; however, serial assessment showed no compression of adjacent organs or fetal hemodynamic compromise. The patient was referred to our hospital at 35 weeks of gestation for further management. Fetal MRI at 35 weeks of gestation demonstrated a well-encapsulated mass in the right fetal abdomen, showing internal low-signal-intensity structures corresponding to the bony elements seen on ultrasonography (Fig. 2). Although the fine skeletal anatomy was better delineated on ultrasonography and postnatal computed tomography (CT), fetal MRI confirmed the right-sided intra-abdominal location and encapsulated nature of the lesion, supporting the prenatal diagnosis of FIF.

Fetal magnetic resonance imaging at 35 weeks of gestation. T2-weighted image shows a well-encapsulated mass in the right fetal abdomen. The arrow indicates internal low-signal-intensity structures corresponding to the bony elements seen on ultrasonography.

Because there was no maternal or fetal compromise, no anticipated need for emergency neonatal surgery immediately after birth, and no suspected delivery obstruction in the setting of cephalic presentation, there was no indication for elective cesarean delivery. Vaginal delivery was therefore planned. At 39 weeks of gestation, spontaneous labor occurred, and a male infant weighing 3068 g was delivered vaginally, with 1- and 5-minute Apgar scores of 9 and 10, respectively. The umbilical arterial pH was 7.296, with a base excess of 3.0 mmol/L. Postnatal CT showed well-formed bones and an identifiable vascular pedicle within the mass (Fig. 3). Surgical excision was performed on day 6. Intraoperatively, the mass was adherent to the retroperitoneum, and a structure resembling a spermatic cord with an attached testis was identified. A cord-like vascular structure arising from the host’s right internal iliac artery was confirmed as the feeding vessel. Histopathological examination revealed skeletal muscles, vertebral and limb bones, epididymal tissue, and an amniotic-like membrane. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and the infant was discharged on day 13. Follow-up at 3 months of age revealed normal growth and no recurrence (Fig. 3).

Discussion

FIF is characterized by the presence of an organized vertebral axis, limb buds, and organogenesis [2,3], which distinguishes it from a fetiform teratoma that lacks such structural organization. FIF results from aberrant embryogenesis of a monozygotic diamniotic twin during early development, leading to the inclusion of 1 twin within the other. The retroperitoneum is the most common site, accounting for over 70% of reported cases. Prenatal diagnosis has become increasingly feasible, and characteristic imaging findings enable the differentiation of FIF from other abdominal masses [3,4].

The feeding vessel originated from the host's right internal iliac artery, suggesting that the parasitic twin received relatively stable arterial perfusion. This vascular configuration may have supported adequate perfusion of differentiated tissues such as skeletal muscle and peripheral nerve fibers. Sustained perfusion of these metabolically active tissues could have supported residual neuromuscular excitability and may have contributed to the transient autonomous limb motion observed on ultrasonography.

Previous reports on FIF have rarely described spontaneous motion of the parasitic twin [[5], [6], [7]]. Most reports referred to cases of vascular perfusion or structural differentiation without clear evidence of active movement. Few studies have documented the dynamic activity of the extremities or cardiac pulsation within the parasitic fetus. In the present case, the parasitic twin showed well-developed limb and long-bone elements rather than only rudimentary limb buds, and ultrasonography demonstrated an adduction-like inward movement of the parasitic lower limb, independent of the host’s activity. This finding may be compatible with residual neuromuscular activity supported by autonomous vascular supply. This case has inherent limitations due to its single-case nature and image quality affected by fetal position and acoustic shadowing.

Conclusion

Detailed dynamic prenatal imaging can provide useful diagnostic clues for fetus in fetu. Observation of spontaneous movement within an encapsulated, organized mass may be compatible with residual neuromuscular excitability supported by autonomous vascular supply.
