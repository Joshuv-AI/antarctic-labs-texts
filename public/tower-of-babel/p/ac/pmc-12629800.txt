Vorapaxar enhanced mitochondria-associated ferroptosis primes cancer immunotherapy via targeting FOXO1/HMOX1 axis
Authors: Qian Zhou, Yuming Sun, Songtao Du, Yating Dian, Lei Yao, Hui Su, Ziyu Guo, Yu Meng, Yixiao Xiong, Zhili Deng, Xinwei Kuang, Xiaowei Liang, Hong Liu, Guangtong Deng, Xiang Chen, Furong Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment, yet challenges persist, such as resistance and lethal thromboembolism, necessitating dual-purpose strategies. Targeting ferroptosis emerges as a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy efficacy, prompting our investigation of antiplatelet agents that simultaneously promote ferroptosis and mitigate thromboembolic risks. Through systematic screening of 20 Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved antiplatelet agents, we identify vorapaxar as a potent pro-ferroptotic drug. Mechanistically, vorapaxar binds forkhead box O1 (FOXO1), inhibits its phosphorylation at Ser256, and facilitates nuclear translocation to upregulate heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1), promoting mitochondrial iron overload and mitochondria-associated ferroptosis. Vorapaxar enhances immunotherapy-induced tumor ferroptosis and antitumor immunity across diverse melanoma models, including B16F10 tumor-bearing mice, Braf/Pten-driven spontaneous melanoma mice, and peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-humanized mice. Clinically, high FOXO1/HMOX1 co-expression correlates with improved immunotherapy response and progression-free survival. These findings position vorapaxar as a promising adjunct to immunotherapy, offering a dual benefit for cancer patients requiring both antithrombotic therapy and immunotherapy.

Introduction

Immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint blockades (ICBs), has emerged as a highly promising therapeutic modality for addressing various malignancies.1 Despite their transformative potential, acquired resistance and immune-related adverse events (irAEs) in a significant proportion of patients pose significant clinical challenges.1 Among these irAEs, cancer-associated thromboembolism is the leading cause of mortality, highlighting the urgent need for prophylactic antithrombotic strategies in high-risk populations.2,3 Consequently, co-administration of antiplatelet agents in cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy is often warranted in clinical practice. This raises the intriguing possibility of identifying an antiplatelet drug capable of simultaneously addressing the dual challenges of thromboembolism and immunotherapy resistance in cancer patients.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, has recently emerged as a potent adjunct to cancer immunotherapy.4,5 ICBs-activated CD8+ T cells potentiate tumor ferroptosis through interferon-γ (IFN-γ)-mediated downregulation of the glutamate-cystine transporter xCT subunits.6 Supplementation with arachidonic acid within the tumor microenvironment further amplifies ferroptosis via acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4)-driven biosynthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids (PUFA-PLs), enhancing the efficacy of antitumor immunotherapy.7 Conversely, tumor cells exhibiting resistance to ferroptosis, characterized by high expression of solute carrier family 13 member 3 (SLC13A3) and TYRO3, are associated with diminished immunotherapy responsiveness.8,9 Importantly, restoring tumor sensitivity to ferroptosis by inhibiting their expression has been shown to overcome immunotherapy resistance, suggesting that enhancing tumor ferroptosis may represent a viable strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance.

Antiplatelet agents, including aspirin and purinergic receptor P2Y12 inhibitors, have been shown to suppress platelet-derived transforming growth factor β signaling and enhance T cell-mediated antitumor immunity.10 Beyond their primary antiplatelet effects, several antiplatelet drugs exhibit pleiotropic antitumor activities, including inhibition of angiogenesis, suppression of metastatic dissemination, and disruption of cancer cell extravasation.3,11,12 However, the potential role of antiplatelet agents in modulating tumor ferroptosis and their interaction with immunotherapy has remained largely unexplored. Recent studies have repurposed the pro-ferroptotic properties of several drugs to enhance cancer immunotherapy by amplifying tumor ferroptosis, motivating further investigation into whether Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved antiplatelet drugs could similarly augment immunotherapy efficacy through ferroptosis promotion.13,14

In this study, we conducted a drug screening of 20 FDA-approved antiplatelet drugs across seven pharmacological classes and identified vorapaxar, a protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR1) inhibitor, as a potent pro-ferroptotic drug in cancer cells. We further demonstrated that vorapaxar synergizes with ferroptosis inducers to promote mitochondria-associated ferroptosis. Mechanistically, vorapaxar engages forkhead box O1 (FOXO1), inhibiting its phosphorylation at Ser256 and promoting its nuclear translocation, leading to upregulation of heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) transcription, thereby promoting mitochondrial iron accumulation. Additionally, vorapaxar enhances immunotherapy-mediated tumor ferroptosis and antitumor immunity across multiple preclinical melanoma models, including B16F10 tumor-bearing mice, Braf/Pten-driven spontaneous melanoma mice, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC)-humanized mice. Clinically, patients with elevated co-expression of FOXO1 and HMOX1 showed improved immunotherapy efficacy and extended progression-free survival. These findings position vorapaxar as a promising adjunct to immunotherapy, especially for cancer patients requiring concurrent antithrombotic treatment.

Results

Screening of the antiplatelet drugs to identify vorapaxar as pro-ferroptotic agent in cancer cells

To identify antiplatelet agents capable of enhancing ferroptosis, we screened 20 antiplatelet drugs across seven pharmacological classes, including PAR1 inhibitors, αIIbβ3 integrin inhibitors, adenosine diphosphate (ADP) receptor blockers, cyclooxygenase inhibitors, thromboxane A2 synthetase inhibitors, thrombin inhibitors, and others. These drugs were evaluated in conjunction with the ferroptosis inducer RSL3, a glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inhibitor, in A375 and SK-MEL-28 (SK28) melanoma cell lines, both of which are relatively resistant to ferroptosis.15 Among the agents tested, the PAR1 inhibitor vorapaxar emerged as the most effective enhancer of RSL3-induced cell death (Figures 1A, 1B, and S1A). Furthermore, the results from the combination experiments that employed various concentrations of RSL3 and vorapaxar indicated that the average combination index (CI) was notably below 1, suggesting a significant synergistic effect (Figure 1C). To determine the specific form of cell demise elicited by the co-administration of vorapaxar and RSL3, we performed rescue experiments using a panel of established pharmacological inhibitors that target various modes of cell death. We observed that the toxic effects of the vorapaxar and RSL3 combination were alleviated by the reactive oxygen species (ROS) inhibitor N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO), while inhibitors of apoptosis (Z-VAD-FMK), necroptosis (necrostatin-1s), and autophagy (chloroquine) were ineffective (Figures 1E and S1B). Consistent results were obtained with propidium iodide (PI) staining, showing that cell death induced by the combination was significantly reduced by DFO and the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (Figures 1F and S1C). To further substantiate ferroptosis as the form of cell death induced by the combination, we systematically analyzed multiple established markers of ferroptosis. We observed significant elevations in levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), lipid ROS, and mRNA of prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 and ChaC glutathione specific gamma-glutamylcyclotransferase 1 (CHAC1) in co-treated cells compared to monotherapy or control (Figures 1G, 1H, and S1D). Moreover, the increase in lipid ROS induced by co-treatment was completely reversed by Fer-1 (Figure 1H). Given that RSL3 induces ferroptosis by inactivating GPX4, we further validated this effect in GPX4-deficient cell lines (Figures 1I and S1E). Three-dimensional (3D) tumor spheroid models, which exhibit more tumor-like properties, reached a similar conclusion (Figure 1J). We also validated the impact of vorapaxar on enhancing ferroptosis induced across multiple cancer cell lines, including human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (MHCC97-H and HCC-LM3), lung adenocarcinoma cells (NCI-H1975), and human colorectal carcinoma cells (SW480) (Figure S1F). Additionally, vorapaxar showed a synergistic effect with another ferroptosis inducer, imidazole ketone erastin (IKE) (Figure S1G). All these underscored the broad potential of vorapaxar to promote ferroptosis in cancer cells.

Vorapaxar combined with RSL3 induces mitochondrial dysfunction by increasing ferrous iron levels in mitochondria

Several canonical proteins have been identified as key regulators of ferroptosis, in which solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), GPX4, apoptosis-inducing factor mitochondria-associated 2 (AIFM2/FSP1), and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) counteract ferroptosis, whereas ACSL4 facilitates ferroptosis by catalyzing the production of PUFA-PLs.16 We therefore hypothesized whether vorapaxar enhances ferroptosis by modulating these canonical ferroptosis-related regulators. However, vorapaxar does not affect their expression across varying concentrations and treatment durations (Figure S2A). Consistently, the levels of glutathione (GSH), oxidized glutathione (GSSG), and GPX enzyme activity—responsible for the functions of SLC7A11 and GPX4—as well as coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10), which is associated with the FSP1 and DHODH axis, also demonstrated no significant changes following vorapaxar treatment (Figures S2B–S2E). Additionally, an increase in cell death was observed in DHODH-deficient cells after vorapaxar treatment in the presence of RSL3, suggesting a DHODH-independent mechanism involved in the pro-ferroptotic activity of vorapaxar (Figure S2F).

We further assessed the mRNA levels of iron metabolism-related molecules, including lipocalin 2, ferritin light chain, transferrin receptor (TFRC), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of the prostate 3 (STEAP3), solute carrier family 40 member 1 (SLC40A1), and solute carrier family 11 member 2 (SLC11A2), and found that vorapaxar treatment did not significantly alter their expression (Figure S2G), as well as cellular ferrous iron levels compared to RSL3 treatment alone (Figures S2H and S2I). We then performed RNA sequencing on various treatment groups, including DMSO, RSL3 alone, vorapaxar alone, and the combination of RSL3 plus vorapaxar (Figures S2J and S2K). As anticipated, the ferroptosis-related pathway was significantly enriched in the combination group, further supporting our experimental findings (Figure S2L). Interestingly, compared to DMSO, a series of pathways associated with mitochondria, including respiratory system development, regulation of mitochondrial organization, and regulation of mitochondrial membrane potential, were significantly elevated in the combination group (Figure 2A). This prompted us to explore the impact of vorapaxar and/or RSL3 on mitochondria. Morphologically, melanoma cells in the combination group were characterized by mitochondrial atrophy, reduced cristae, and increased membrane density compared to cells in other groups (Figure 2B). Functionally, mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP levels were significantly disrupted by the co-administration of vorapaxar and RSL3 (Figures 2C and 2D). We therefore wondered whether RSL3 plus vorapaxar induced mitochondria-associated ferroptosis, which is primarily accompanied by elevated levels of ferrous iron in the mitochondria.13,17 As anticipated, the level of ferrous iron in mitochondria was significantly highest in the combination group, which could be reversed by the administration of DFO (Figures 2E and S3A). Vorapaxar combined with RSL3 induce mitochondria-associated ferroptosis primarily through upregulating mitochondrial HMOX1

The accumulation of ROS, especially lipid ROS, in the mitochondria has been demonstrated to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and eventually trigger mitochondrial-associated ferroptosis.13,18 We observed that the combined treatment group showed a notable increase in both mitochondrial and total lipid ROS (Figures 2F and 2G). Notably, these elevated levels were effectively scavenged by a mitochondria-targeted ROS scavenger, mito-TEMPO, rather than by non-mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants, TEMPOL (Figures 2F and 2G). Consistently, mito-TEMPO, rather than TEMPOL, demonstrated greater efficacy in mitigating the cytotoxic effects induced by the combination treatment (Figures 2H and S3B–3D). Collectively, these results suggest that the combination of vorapaxar and RSL3 induces mitochondria-associated ferroptosis.

To clarify the key targets mediating the mitochondria-associated ferroptosis caused by the combination treatment, we integrated the library of genes upregulated upon drug combination with information from the Library of Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures program, which encompasses tens of thousands of gene sets that indicate transcriptional responses to a wide array of chemical compounds (Figure S3E). Through this strategy, we identified that the genes upregulated by the drug combination in melanoma cells significantly overlapped with those upregulated by treatment with withaferin A, which induces ferroptosis by increasing the labile ferrous iron pool following excessive activation of HMOX119 (Figure S3F). Interestingly, our RNA sequencing results revealed that HMOX1 was the most significantly upregulated gene in cells treated with the dual-drug regimen (Figure 2I). HMOX1-related pathways, such as the heme degradation pathway and the endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway, were also significantly activated (Figure 2J).17,20 We further validated the upregulation of HMOX1 mRNA and protein levels in mitochondria after combination treatment (Figures 2K, S3G, and S3H), suggesting the involvement of HMOX1 in vorapaxar and RSL3-induced mitochondria-associated ferroptosis. Notably, HMOX1 activator hemin exhibited a pro-ferroptotic effect in the context of RSL3 treatment (Figure S3I). In contrast, HMOX1 inhibitor zinc protoporphyrin IX (Znpp) significantly attenuated the increases in mitochondrial ferrous iron levels, mitochondrial and total lipid ROS, and cell death induced by the co-treatment of vorapaxar and RSL3 (Figures 2L, 2M, and S3J–S3N). HMOX1 depletion also inhibited the cell death induced by co-treatment in A375 and SK28 cells (Figures 2N and S3O). These findings support that the upregulation of mitochondrial HMOX1 is responsible for the mitochondria-associated ferroptosis induced by the combination of vorapaxar and RSL3.

Vorapaxar facilitates mitochondria-associated ferroptosis by targeting the FOXO1/HMOX1 axis

To investigate the regulatory mechanism underlying mitochondria-associated ferroptosis, we focused on elucidating the transcriptional regulation of HMOX1, particularly given the marked increase in HMOX1 mRNA levels observed in the combination group (Figures 2I and S3G). Notably, the significant elevation of HMOX1 mRNA in the combination group was completely abolished by cycloheximide (CHX), a protein synthesis inhibitor that functions through ribosomal blockade (Figure 3A). This observation indicates that the upregulation of HMOX1 expression in the combination group was driven by a newly synthesized transcription factor. Previous studies have shown that the upregulation of HMOX1 mRNA levels involves various transcription factors, including FOXO1, signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), hypoxia inducible factor 1(HIF1), YY1, AP1, and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2).21,22,23 We initially assessed the activity of these transcription factors in the combination group and found a significant enhancement in FOXO1 transcriptional activity (Figure 3B). Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) revealed a markedly increased binding peak of FOXO1, as well as the nuclear activation mutation FOXO1A3, at the HMOX1 promoter (Figures 3C and S4A). Notably, FOXO1 inhibitor AS1842856 led to a dose-dependent reduction in HMOX1 mRNA levels induced by the combination treatment (Figure 3D). Consistently, AS1842856 almost completely suppressed the increase of total and mitochondrial lipid ROS, mitochondrial ferrous iron, and cell demise induced by the co-treatment of vorapaxar and RSL3 (Figures 3E–3I). Furthermore, knockdown or knockout of FOXO1 inhibited HMOX1 protein expression and reversed the pro-ferroptotic effects of RSL3 as well as the combined treatment of RSL3 and vorapaxar (Figures 3J, 3K, and S4B–S4E). Conversely, overexpression of FOXO1 increased HMOX1 protein levels and sensitized cells to RSL3-induced ferroptosis (Figures S4F–S4H). To clarify the regulatory effect of vorapaxar on FOXO1, we first knocked down coagulation factor II thrombin receptor (F2R), a classical downstream target of vorapaxar, using short hairpin RNA (shRNA) and small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Figures S4I and S4J). However, F2R inhibition did not influence the melanoma sensitivity to ferroptosis, indicating that vorapaxar enhances ferroptosis through an off-target mechanism (Figures S4K and S4L). We speculated whether vorapaxar enhances RSL3-induced mitochondria-associated ferroptosis by directly targeting FOXO1. To validate this hypothesis, molecular docking analyses were conducted and indicated that vorapaxar potentially interacts with FOXO1, with a docking score of −2.5 kcal/mol (Figures 3L and 3M). Subsequently, bio-layer interferometry analysis confirmed that vorapaxar directly binds to the full-length FOXO1 protein, with a calculated equilibrium dissociation constant of 8.67 μM (kon of 8.32×103 M−1s−1, koff of 7.21 × 10−2 s−1), demonstrating a strong and specific interaction (Figures 3N and 3O). Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) revealed that vorapaxar increases the thermal stability of FOXO1 in A375 and SK28 cells, supporting the interaction between vorapaxar and FOXO1 (Figure 3P). FOXO1 translocation to the nucleus is essential for its transcriptional activity, a process that is significantly inhibited by Ser256 phosphorylation of FOXO1.24 Interestingly, we found the binding site between vorapaxar and FOXO1 is located near Ser256 (Figure 3M), and a significant reduction in the phosphorylation of FOXO1 Ser256 was observed following vorapaxar treatment (Figure 3Q). Consistently, the nuclear translocation of FOXO1 was enhanced in the combination group (Figure 3R). These findings suggest that vorapaxar directly interacts with FOXO1, inhibiting the phosphorylation at Ser256 and thereby promoting the FOXO1 nuclear translocation, which facilitates RSL3-induced ferroptosis.

Vorapaxar-enhanced ferroptosis inhibits tumor growth through the FOXO1/HMOX1 axis

To verify the in vitro results and evaluate the therapeutic potential of combining vorapaxar with ferroptosis induction in cancer treatment, we established tumor-bearing mice by subcutaneously implanting either GPX4-knockout or control A375 cells into nude mice, considering the pro-ferroptotic effect of RSL3 through GPX4 inhibition.25 Tumor-bearing mice were randomly allocated into different groups and treated with vehicle, vorapaxar alone, or vorapaxar in combination with the ferroptosis inhibitor liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1), the HMOX1 inhibitor Znpp, or the FOXO1 inhibitor AS1842856, once tumors reached a size of 50–100 mm3 (Figure 4A). Consistent with previous study, we observed limited effects of GPX4 ablation on tumor growth in vivo.15,26 However, vorapaxar treatment significantly inhibited tumor progression in GPX4 knockout models without body weight loss (Figures 4B, 4C, S5A, and S5B). Importantly, co-treatment with Lip-1, Znpp, and AS1842856 mitigated tumor growth inhibition induced by vorapaxar in the GPX4 knockout tumors (Figures 4B, 4C, and S5A). Furthermore, vorapaxar treatment significantly increased the expression of the ferroptosis marker 4-hydroxynonenal (4HNE) in GPX4 knockout tumors, an effect that was nearly entirely reversed by Lip-1, Znpp, and AS1842856 (Figures 4D and 4E). Given that the poor solubility and unfavorable pharmacokinetic properties of RSL3 limit its use in animal models for ferroptosis amplification,27 we employed IKE, a suitable ferroptosis inducer in vivo,28 to evaluate the efficacy of vorapaxar in combination with IKE for inhibiting melanoma progression. As anticipated, the combination of vorapaxar and IKE significantly reduced tumor development in A375 xenograft nude mice compared to monotherapy. This effect was completely abrogated by Lip-1, Znpp, or AS1842856 (Figures 4F–4H and S5C). No significant changes in body weight were observed following the treatments (Figure S5D). Furthermore, the co-administration of vorapaxar and IKE also synergistically inhibited melanoma growth and prolonged survival without weight loss in immunocompetent B16F10 tumor-bearing mice, with these antitumor effects reversed by Lip-1 (Figures 4I–4L, S5E, and S5F). To further assess whether vorapaxar potentiates IKE-induced ferroptosis in a spontaneous melanoma mouse model, a genetically engineered mouse model of Braf/Pten-driven melanoma was utilized, in which 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT)-induced activation of the Cre recombinase system leads to BRAFV600E expression and PTEN inactivation (BRAFV600E/PTENnull) in cutaneous melanocytes, leading to melanoma initiation and progression.29 To initiate melanoma development, we topically treated Tyr::CreER +/−; Braf CA/wt; Pten lox/lox mice (3–5 weeks old) with 5 mM 4-OHT on the dorsal skin for three consecutive days, resulting in 100% tumor penetrance within 12–15 days (Figure 4M). Following this, mice with visible melanomas were randomly assigned to receive either IKE or the combination of IKE and vorapaxar starting on day 15 post-Cre activation (Figure 4M). Tumor volume and mouse survival were subsequently monitored. Compared to IKE monotherapy, the co-administration of vorapaxar and IKE markedly inhibited tumor development, as evidenced by smaller tumor volume and inguinal lymph nodes with less pigmentation at the 36-day endpoint, without notable changes to body weight or various organs (Figures 4N, 4O, and S5G–S5I). Importantly, vorapaxar also significantly extended survival in Braf/Pten-driven melanoma mice treated with IKE (Figure 4P). A markedly enhanced 4HNE expression was shown in Braf/Pten-driven melanoma tumors with co-administration (Figures 4Q and 4R). All of these findings support that vorapaxar potentiates ferroptosis induction in vivo, thereby suppressing tumor progression.

Vorapaxar potentiates anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in suppressing melanoma by promoting ferroptosis

Recognizing that ICBs serve as an in vivo model for ferroptosis due to their anticancer effects partially via inducing ferroptosis, we hypothesized that vorapaxar could augment the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy by promoting ferroptosis.6,7 To investigate this hypothesis, we employed a combinatorial therapeutic strategy involving vorapaxar and anti-programmed cell death 1 (anti-PD-1) immunotherapy in murine models. Immunocompetent B16F10 tumor-bearing mice were established and treated with either immunoglobulin G (IgG, control) or anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb), with or without vorapaxar (Figure 5A). As anticipated, anti-PD-1 monotherapy showed moderate efficacy in inhibiting tumor growth, while vorapaxar significantly boosted the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 therapy, resulting in a marked reduction in tumor weight and volume compared to IgG, anti-PD-1 alone, or vorapaxar alone, without causing body weight loss (Figures 5B–5D and S5J). We also performed flow cytometry to further analyze the effects of vorapaxar on the anti-tumor immune response induced by anti-PD-1 therapy (Figure S5K). The results indicated that the effector functions of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, indicated by IFN-γ and granzyme B (GZMB) expression level, were increased in response to anti-PD-1 therapy, and this increase was significantly amplified when combined with vorapaxar (Figure 5E). Furthermore, we assessed whether the co-administration of vorapaxar and anti-PD-1 therapy was sufficient to inhibit Braf/Pten-driven melanoma progression. For this, 4-OHT-induced Braf/Pten-driven melanoma mice were randomly assigned to receive either control IgG or anti-PD-1 mAb with or without vorapaxar starting on day 15 post-Cre activation (Figure 5H). However, co-administration of vorapaxar and anti-PD-1 mAb markedly inhibited tumor development and extended survival in Braf/Pten-driven melanoma-bearing mice, without inducing weight loss or gross organ toxicity (Figures 5I–5K and S5L–S5N). Additionally, combination treatment resulted in a significant increase in 4HNE expression and enhanced infiltration of GZMB+ CD8+ T cells in Braf/Pten-driven melanoma tumors (Figures 5L, S5O, and S5P). These data collectively suggest that the combination of vorapaxar and anti-PD-1 therapy synergistically inhibits melanoma progression by promoting ferroptosis and enhancing CD8+ T cell-mediated immunity, presenting a promising therapeutic strategy for treating immunotherapy-resistant “cold” tumors.

Due to the significant effect of vorapaxar on enhancing immunotherapy-mediated tumor suppression, along with the increased expression of HMOX1 and nuclear FOXO1, we next investigated the potential of its target axis FOXO1/HMOX1 as a molecular subtype for predicting immunotherapy efficacy in melanoma. Hence, bioinformatics analyses were performed on the ICBs-treated Xiangya melanoma cohort,32 stratifying patients into four subgroups based on FOXO1/HMOX1 co-expression levels: low FOXO1 and low HMOX1, high FOXO1 and low HMOX1, low FOXO1 and high HMOX1, and high FOXO1 and high HMOX1. These analyses indicated that patients with high expression of both FOXO1 and HMOX1 exhibited significantly elevated levels of ferroptosis, CD8+ T cell infiltration, T cell inflammatory gene expression profiles (GEPs), and cytolytic activity (CYT), which were further corroborated by previously reported melanoma datasets (Figures 6G, 6H, S6A, and S6B).33 Consistently, analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas_skin cutaneous melanoma (TCGA_SKCM) datasets also demonstrated that patients with high expression of both FOXO1 and HMOX1 tended to exhibit more favorable immunostimulatory cell characteristics (Figure S6C). Furthermore, improved responses to immunotherapy and prolonged progression-free survival were observed in ICBs-treated melanoma patients with elevated levels of both FOXO1 and HMOX1 (Figures 6I, 6J, and S6D). Collectively, these findings suggest that the FOXO1/HMOX1 axis may serve as a molecular predictor of PD-1 mAb therapy efficacy.

Discussion

The integration of ferroptosis induction with ICBs represents a promising frontier in cancer therapy. In this study, we identified vorapaxar, an FDA-approved antiplatelet agent, as an enhancer of ferroptosis and ferroptosis-mediated immunotherapy. Mechanistically, vorapaxar directly binds to FOXO1, inhibiting its phosphorylation at Ser256 to drive nuclear translocation and transcriptional upregulation of HMOX1, thereby promoting mitochondrial iron overload and mitochondria-associated ferroptosis (Figure 6K). These findings position vorapaxar as a promising candidate to enhance immunotherapy efficacy and provide an approach to overcome the dual challenges of thrombosis and immunotherapy resistance in cancer patients.

Vorapaxar is an orally active antagonist of the PAR1 receptor, approved in 2014 to reduce the risk of recurrent cardiovascular events, particularly in patients with a history of myocardial infarction or peripheral arterial disease.34 Beyond its primary indication, vorapaxar has been demonstrated to inhibit the proliferation of diffuse large B cell lymphoma cells and promote apoptosis in vitro.35 However, the in vivo effects of vorapaxar on tumors remain unclear. In our study, we found that vorapaxar significantly enhances ferroptosis and markedly inhibits tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo when used in conjunction with a ferroptosis inducer. Paradoxically, a recent study indicated that PAR1 activation contributes to ferroptosis following sciatic nerve crush injury via the Hippo-YAP/ACSL4 pathway, whereas inhibition of PAR1 by vorapaxar alleviates ferroptosis in this context.36 Importantly, our study showed no significant changes in A375 and SK28 melanoma cells in response to ferroptosis induction after silencing or knocking down the F2R gene, which encodes PAR1. This discrepancy may be attributed to intercellular heterogeneity and suggests the potential involvement of a non-PAR1 off-target mechanism in the ferroptosis-promoting effects of vorapaxar.

The FOXO protein family is transcription factors and includes four isoforms in humans: FOXO1, FOXO3, FOXO4, and FOXO6.37 The FOXOs are primarily regulated through post-translational modifications.24,37 Multiple phosphorylation sites of FOXO1 have been identified that modulate its activity and nuclear import.24 Notably, AKT-mediated phosphorylation of FOXO1 at Thr24, Ser256, and Ser319 has been reported as a primary mechanism of FOXO1 inactivation in cancer.24,38 In our study, we demonstrate that vorapaxar can directly bind to FOXO1, inhibiting its phosphorylation at Ser256 and promoting its nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity in the presence of the ferroptosis inducer RSL3, ultimately resulting in ferroptotic cell death and tumor inhibition. Previous studies have reported the role of FOXO1 in ferroptosis. For instance, Ding et al. demonstrated that downregulating FOXO1 significantly inhibits the ferroptosis of pancreatic β cells in diabetes mellitus,39 and Zhou et al. recently showed that overexpression of FOXO1 significantly induces ferroptosis in neutrophils of mice suffering from traumatic brain injury-induced depression by disrupting TFRC/FTH1-mediated iron homeostasis.40 Our study corroborates these findings, demonstrating that FOXO1 plays a role in ferroptosis-related iron homeostasis. Notably, HMOX1 is positively regulated by FOXO1, but the specific role of the FOXO1/HMOX1 axis in ferroptosis has been poorly understood.21,41 Our study reveals a mechanism whereby vorapaxar-induced FOXO1 nuclear translocation drives HMOX1-mediated mitochondria-associated ferroptosis through compartment-specific iron dysregulation.

To validate our in vitro mechanistic findings in vivo, we initially employed immunodeficient mice treated with vorapaxar and ferroptosis inducers, consistent with established approaches.42,43,44 This approach minimizes confounding effects from adaptive immunity, thereby isolating tumor cell-intrinsic mechanisms observed in vitro.45 Then, we assessed the relationship between ferroptosis and immunity, while emerging research emphasizes that enhancing ferroptosis is crucial for overcoming both intrinsic and acquired resistance to immunotherapy.16,46 For instance, Lin et al. and Wang et al. demonstrated that increasing tumor cell susceptibility to ferroptosis by modulating the SLC13A3-mediated itaconate/NRF2/SLC7A11 pathway or the MerTK-mediated ERK/SP1/SLC7A11 pathway enhanced the efficacy of immunotherapy.8,9 Furthermore, targeting bromodomain-containing protein 4 was shown to improve the efficacy of immunotherapy by enhancing ferroptosis in melanoma.14 Our findings further corroborate this perspective, revealing that the upregulation of the FOXO1/HMOX1 axis mediated the enhanced ferroptotic effects of vorapaxar, leading to improved immunotherapy efficacy. Clinically, high co-expression of FOXO1 and HMOX1 correlated with better immunotherapy outcomes in melanoma patients. Collectively, these results underscore the significance of ferroptosis as a promising strategy to enhance immunotherapy and highlight the potential of FOXO1/HMOX1 axis as a cancer therapeutic target and predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response.

Limitations of the study

Some limitations or future works need to be addressed in this study. Firstly, our conclusions are constrained by the facts that our in vitro studies focused on limited cancer cell lines, and our in vivo studies focused on subcutaneous and spontaneous melanoma models. These models do not fully recapitulate the complexity of human tumor microenvironments. Thus, future works should also check the generalizability of these findings in the melanoma patient-derived xenograft models and across additional tumor types. Secondly, platelets play pivotal roles in tumor progression, serving as key mediators in cancer biology by facilitating metastatic spread through the physical shielding of circulating tumor cells, shaping immunosuppressive microenvironments, and promoting pathological angiogenesis.11 The potential role of vorapaxar’s platelet-targeting properties in antitumor effects and immune response improvement warrants deeper exploration. For instance, does vorapaxar disrupt platelet-mediated activation of cancer-associated fibroblasts, or normalize aberrant tumor vasculature to enhance drug penetration and reduce hypoxia-induced immunosuppression? A more detailed investigation of these mechanisms will further facilitate the application of vorapaxar in cancer treatment. Thirdly, the elevated risk of hemorrhagic and thrombocytopenic complications inherent to antiplatelet therapies remains a critical consideration. Clinical data from cardiovascular trials reported a 4.2% incidence of moderate to severe bleeding in patients undergoing vorapaxar therapy.47 Therefore, careful risk stratification is required for cancer patients, particularly those with pre-existing thrombocytopenia or coagulation disorders, to ensure the safe and effective use of vorapaxar in oncology settings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) and male NCG mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmatech. Female C57BL/6 mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from the Hunan SLAC. Tyr::CreER+/+; Braf wt/wt; Ptenlox/lox mice and Tyr::CreER−/−; Braf CA/CA; Ptenlox/lox mice were purchased from The Jackson laboratory, and crossed to generate Tyr::CreER+/−; Braf CA/wt; Ptenlox/lox mice. All mice were housed in a controlled facility that maintained specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. The Ethical Review of the Animal Care and Use Committee of Central South University granted ethical approval for all murine-related experimental protocols and procedures.

Subcutaneous tumor mouse models

Human A375 melanoma cells (2×106/mouse) in 100 μL PBS were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 4-6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice. Once tumors reached ∼100 mm3, animals were randomized into treatment groups receiving vehicle control, vorapaxar (10 mg/kg, p.o., daily; Selleck, Cat#S8067), IKE (40 mg/kg, i.p., daily; Selleck, Cat#S8877), or their combinations with or without liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1; 10 mg/kg, i.p., daily; Selleck, Cat#S7699), Znpp (10 mg/kg, i.p., daily, MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-101193), or AS1842856 (10 mg/kg, i.p., daily; MedChemExpres, Cat#HY-100596). In parallel experiments, control (sgCtrl) or GPX4 knockout (sgGPX4) A375 cells (2 × 106/mouse) were implanted subcutaneously into nude mice. Mice bearing sgCtrl tumors received vehicle or vorapaxar, while those with sgGPX4 tumors were treated with vorapaxar in the presence or absence of Lip-1, Znpp, or AS1842856.

Additionally, immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously inoculated with B16F10 melanoma cells (2×105/mouse) into the right flank. Upon tumors reaching ∼100 mm3, mice were randomized to receive vehicle control, vorapaxar (10 mg/kg, p.o., daily), IKE (40 mg/kg, i.p., daily), or the combination with or without Lip-1 (10 mg/kg, i.p., daily). For evaluating vorapaxar and ICB combination therapy, B16F10 tumor-bearing mice were treated with control IgG2α (100 μg per mouse, i.p., every 3 days; BioXcell, Cat#BE0089), anti-PD-1 mAb (100 μg/mouse, i.p., every 3 days; BioXcell, Cat#BE0146), vorapaxar plus IgG2α, or vorapaxar plus anti–PD-1. Tumor volumes were measured every 3 days using calipers and calculated as (length × width2) × 0.5, and body weights were recorded in parallel. To assess the survival of animals, mice bearing tumors exceeding 2,000 mm3 were defined as “dead.”

Genetically engineered mouse models

Spontaneous melanoma models were established in 3- to 5-week-old male Tyr::CreER+/−; Braf CA/wt; Ptenlox/lox mice by topical application of 2 μL of 5 mM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat#H6278) in ethanol to shaved dorsal skin once daily for 3 consecutive days. Treatment was initiated on day 15 following Cre activation in mice with visible melanomas. Mice were randomly assigned to treatment with IKE (40 mg/kg, i.p., daily), or IKE in combination with vorapaxar (10 mg/kg, p.o., daily). Similarly, for evaluating vorapaxar and ICB combination therapy, mice were randomized to receive control IgG2α (200 μg/mouse, i.p., every 3 days), anti-PD-1 mAb (200 μg/mouse, i.p., every 3 days), vorapaxar alone, or vorapaxar in combination with anti-PD-1 mAb. Tumor growth was monitored every 3 days by digital photography (with a size reference) in combination with manual caliper measurements. Tumor volume was calculated in two phases: pre-confluence volume was estimated as area × height × π/6, where area was derived from image-based analysis using ImageJ; post-confluence volume was calculated using the formula length × width × height × π/6 based on direct caliper measurements.48 For survival analysis, tumor-bearing mice with tumors area exceeding 150 mm2 were defined as “dead.”49

Humanized mouse models

NCG mice (4–6 weeks old) were subcutaneously inoculated with A375 human melanoma cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse). After seven days, when the tumors reached ∼100 mm3, human PBMCs (5×106 cells in 200 μL PBS) administered via intravenous injection. Three days post-engraftment, mice were treated with toripalimab (100 μg/mouse, i.p., every 3 days; TopAlliance) with or without vorapaxar (10 mg/kg, p.o., daily). Tumor volumes and body weights were measured every 3 days.

Cell lines

The human melanoma cell lines A375 and SK-MEL-28 (SK28), and the HEK293T cell line were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco). The murine melanoma cell line B16F10 was sourced from ATCC and cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco). The human non-small cell lung carcinoma cell line NCI-H1975, human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HCC-LM3, and the human colorectal carcinoma cell line SW480 were obtained from the Procell Life Science&Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line MHCC97-H was purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). NCI-H1975, HCC-LM3 and MHCC97-H cells were maintained in complete DMEM medium, while SW480 cells were cultured in complete McCoy’s 5A medium (Gibco). The complete media were prepared by supplementing the respective basic media with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. All cells were grown at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Method details

Screening of FDA-approved antiplatelet drugs

A subset of 20 FDA-approved antiplatelet drugs was selected from a comprehensive FDA-approved drug library containing 2697 compounds (Selleck, Cat#L2000). The complete list of these antiplatelet drugs is provided in Table S1. For the screening assay, A375 and SK28 cells (6,000 cells/well) were plated onto 96-well microplates. After incubating 24 h, cells were then treated for 6 h with DMSO (vehicle), RSL3 (2.5 μM), individual antiplatelet drugs (10 μM), or their combination. Cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 assay (Bimake, Cat#B34302) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Treatment combination analysis

A375 and SK-MEL-28 (SK28) cells were treated with vorapaxar and RSL3 (MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-100218A) at varying concentrations (0, 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, and 10 μM) for 6 h. The combinatorial effects were assessed by calculating the combination index (CI) using the Chou–Talalay method via CompuSyn software. CI values were interpreted as follows: CI > 1, antagonism; CI = 1, additive effect; CI < 1, synergy.

Cell death analysis

Cell death was evaluated using propidium iodide (PI) staining (Invitrogen, Cat#P1304MP). A375 and SK28 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (30,000 cells/well) and treated for 6 h with DMSO, RSL3 (2.5 μM), vorapaxar (5 μM), or their combination, with or without ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 4 μM; MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-100579), DFO (100 μM; Cat#HY-B1625), Mito-TEMPO (10 μM; Cat#HY-115386), or ZnPP (10 μM). Following treatment, cells were incubated with 5 μg/mL PI for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. Images were captured using a Nikon microscope, and quantitative analysis was performed via flow cytometry (BD Biosciences). The FlowJo software (version 10.4) was utilized for analyzing the flow cytometry data.

Generation of 3D spheroids

Tumor spheroids were generated by seeding 10,000 cells per well into 96-well plates pre-coated with 50 μL of 1.5% agarose (BioFroxx, Cat#1110GR100). After 48 h to allow spheroid formation, cultures were treated for 6 h with DMSO, RSL3 (2.5 μM), vorapaxar (5 μM), or their combination, with or without Fer-1 (4 μM), DFO (100 μM), Mito-TEMPO (10 μM), or AS1842856 (10 μM; MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-100596). Spheroids were subsequently stained with 5 μg/mL PI for 30 min at 37°C in the dark and imaged using a Nikon fluorescence microscope.

Malondialdehyde assay

Relative MDA levels were measured using a commercial Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit (Merck, Cat#MAK085) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, A375 and SK28 cells were treated for 3 h with DMSO, vorapaxar (5 μM), RSL3 (2.5 μM), or their combination, then lysed on ice in MDA Lysis Buffer supplemented with butylated hydroxytoluene. Lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were incubated with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) at 95°C for 60 min. The samples were then cooled on ice for 10 min and allowed to reach room temperature to stabilize the MDA-TBA adducts. Absorbance was measured at 532 nm to determine MDA concentrations.

Glutathione assay

Intracellular levels of reduced (GSH) and oxidized (GSSG) GSH were quantified using a GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat#S0053) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A375 and SK28 cells were treated with DMSO, vorapaxar (5 μM), or IKE (20 μM) for 12 h. Cell pellets were harvested and lysed in three volumes of protein removal reagent via three freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min. Supernatants were diluted 1:10 and used for GSH quantification. For total GSH measurement, 10 μL of diluted lysate was mixed with 150 μL of working solution containing GSH reductase and 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), followed by the addition of 50 μL of 0.5 mg/mL nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) after 5 min. Absorbance was recorded at 412 nm. GSSG levels were determined following pretreatment with a GSH scavenger. Reduced GSH was calculated as: GSH = Total GSH −2 × GSSG.

GPX activity assay

GPX activity was assessed using the GSH Peroxidase Assay Kit (Abcam, Cat#ab102530), which quantifies NADPH consumption at 340 nm. This assay is based on a coupled enzymatic reaction wherein GPX catalyzes the reduction of hydroperoxides using GSH, generating GSSG, which is then recycled back to GSH by GSH reductase, consuming NADPH. A375 and SK28 cells were treated for 3 h with DMSO, vorapaxar (5 μM), RSL3 (2.5 μM), or their combination, followed by homogenization in assay buffer and centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Supernatants were incubated for 15 min at room temperature with a reaction mixture containing GSH reductase, GSH, and NADPH to deplete background GSSG. The reaction was initiated by the addition of cumene hydroperoxide, and absorbance at 340 nm was recorded at 0 and 5 min to determine GPX activity.

Coenzyme Q10 assay

Cellular CoQ10 levels were measured using the Human CoQ10 ELISA Kit (Cusabio, Cat#CSB-E14081h). SK28 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of vorapaxar (0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, or 10 μM) for 12 h or with 5 μM vorapaxar for 0, 3, 6, 9, or 12 h. Following treatment, cells were harvested, homogenized in PBS, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatants were then combined with HRP-conjugated enzyme and incubated for 40 min at 37°C, washed five times with assay buffer. TMB substrate was added and incubated protected from light at 37°C for 20 min. Reactions were terminated with stop solution, and absorbance was recorded at 450 nm.

Reactive oxygen species assay

Cells seeded in 6-well plates were treated with DMSO, 2.5 μM RSL3, 5 μM vorapaxar, or a combination of both, in the presence or absence of 4 μM Fer-1, 10 μM TEMPOL (MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-100561), 10 μM Mito-TEMPO, 10 μM Znpp, or 10 μM AS1842856 for 3 h. Post-treatment, cells were incubated at 37°C for 20 min in the dark with 500 μL staining solution containing either 2.5 μM MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen, Cat#M36008) for mitochondrial ROS, 1 μM MitoPerOx (Cayman, Cat#18798) for mitochondrial lipid peroxides, or 1 μM C11-BODIPY 581/591 (Invitrogen, Cat#D3861) for total lipid ROS. Cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 200 μL PBS for immediate flow cytometry analysis. Oxidized C11-BODIPY and MitoPerOx signals were detected using 488 nm excitation and a 515/20 nm bandpass filter (FITC channel), while MitoSOX Red was detected using 561 nm excitation and a 586/15 nm bandpass filter (PE channel). A minimum of 10,000 gated cells were analyzed per sample using FlowJo software (version 10.4).

Ferrous iron assay

To assess intracellular and mitochondrial ferrous iron levels, cells were treated for 1 h with DMSO, 2.5 μM RSL3, 5 μM vorapaxar, or their combination, with or without 100 μM DFO, 10 μM Znpp, or 10 μM AS1842856. Following treatment, cells were washed twice with serum-free medium to minimize serum-derived iron interference. For total ferrous iron detection, cells were incubated with 1 mL of 1 μM FerrOrange (Dojindo, Cat#F374) at 37°C for 30 min in the dark and analyzed directly by flow cytometry or fluorescence microscopy without further washing to prevent staining leakage. For mitochondrial ferrous iron, cells were stained with 5 μM Mito-FerroGreen (Dojindo, Cat#M489) under the same conditions, followed by three washes with serum-free medium prior to analysis to reduce background signals. For fluorescence imaging, mitochondria were co-stained with 200 nM MitoTracker Red (Beyotime, Cat#C1035), and nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst (Beyotime, Cat#33258). Imaging was performed using the BioTek Cytation 10 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

To assess mitochondrial membrane potential, A375 cells were treated with DMSO, 2.5 μM RSL3, 5 μM vorapaxar, or their combination for 1 h, followed by incubation with PBS containing 250 nM rhodamine 123 (MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-D0816) and 200 nM MitoTracker Red (Beyotime, Cat#C1035) at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. After staining, cells were washed three times with pre-warmed complete medium and incubated in 1 mL of pre-warmed complete medium prior to imaging. Images were obtained with BioTek Cytation 10 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

ATP assay

Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using an ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat#S0026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A375 and SK28 cells seeded in 6-well plates were treated with DMSO, 2.5 μM RSL3, 5 μM vorapaxar, or their combination for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were lysed in 1 mL of lysis buffer, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Supernatants were collected, and luminescence was recorded using the luminometer module of the BioTek Cytation 10 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

Mitochondria isolation

Mitochondria were isolated using the Mitochondria Isolation Kit for Cultured Cells (MedChemExpress, Cat#HY-K1060) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A375 and SK28 cells were cultured in 10-cm dishes and treated with DMSO, 2.5 μM RSL3, 5 μM vorapaxar, or a combination of RSL3 and vorapaxar for 2 h at 37°C. A minimum of 2 × 107 cells was collected per condition. Following PBS washes, cells were centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min, and 1 × 107 cells were resuspended in 500 μL of ice-cold isolation buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. After incubation on ice for 10 min, cells were homogenized using a glass homogenizer until viability dropped below 30%, as determined by acridine orange/propidium iodide (AOPI) staining. The homogenate was centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min to pellet the mitochondria. The resulting supernatant was subjected to a further centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min to obtain cytosolic fractions free of mitochondrial contamination.

Fit docking assay

The human FOXO1 protein structure was retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) and initially modeled using AlphaFold (PDB: AF-Q12778-F1). The predicted structure underwent extensive energy minimization and optimization through Schrodinger’s Protein Preparation Workflow (Maestro version 13.7.125), including hydrogen bond network optimization, restrained minimization, and protonation state assignment at physiological pH (7.4 ± 0.2). Potential binding pockets were systematically identified using SiteMap analysis with default parameters, while ligand structures were prepared using LigPrep module, generating possible ionization states and tautomers at the target pH. Molecular docking simulations were performed using Glide’s extra precision (XP) mode, with grid boxes (size: 20 Å) centered on the identified binding pockets. To account for protein flexibility, induced fit docking protocols were implemented, allowing for side chain and backbone conformational adjustments during ligand binding.

Bio-Layer Interferometry assay

Purified recombinant FOXO1 proteins (ACMEC, Cat#AC82106) underwent biotinylation utilizing EZ-Link NHS-LC-LC-Biotin kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#21343). In summary, recombinant FOXO1 proteins and EZ-Link NHS-LC-LC-Biotin were mixed at a 1:1 molar ratio in PBS. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently dialyzed using Pierce Zeba (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#89882) to eliminate any unreacted EZ-Link NHS-LC-LC-Biotin.

Bio-Layer Interferometry utilized a GatorPrime instrument from GatorBio. All assays were conducted at 30°C with continuous shaking at 1000 rpm. The assay buffer included PBS, 0.02% Tween 20, and 0.5% DMSO, with the pH adjusted to 7.5. Biotinylated FOXO1 proteins were attached to streptavidin (SA) biosensors (GatorBio) by immersing sensors in protein solutions. Following this, SA biosensors were rinsed in the assay buffer for 120 s to remove nonspecifically attached proteins, ensuring stable baseline readings before initiating association–dissociation cycles with test compounds (vorapaxar, 0.40–33.33 μM). The raw kinetic data were analyzed using GatorOne software (version 2.15.5). The resulting data were evaluated based on a 1:1 binding model, allowing for the calculation of Kon and Koff values, from which Kd values were derived.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay

For intact-cell CETSA experiments, A375 and SK28 cells were seeded in 10-cm dishes and treated with 10 μM vorapaxar, 10 μM AS1842856, or DMSO for 1 h at 37°C upon reaching 50–60% confluency, ensuring a minimum of 1 × 107 cells per condition. Following treatment, cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min. Pellets were resuspended in 500 μL pre-chilled PBS containing 1 mM PMSF. Aliquots (50 μL) were transferred into nine 1.5 mL tubes and heated at 3°C intervals from 40°C to 64°C for 3 min, followed by cooling to room temperature for 3 min. Cells were then lysed via three freeze–thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen and incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing every 10 min. The resulting soluble fraction was diluted with 5×SDS loading buffer for subsequent western blot analysis.

Lentiviral transduction and RNA interference

Human short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting F2R, FOXO1 and a non-targeting control were obtained from OBiO Technology Corp., Ltd. Human F2R siRNAs were sourced from AuGCT Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and the FOXO1 overexpression vector was acquired from Youze Biotechnology Co., Ltd. For CRISPR-mediated gene editing, single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting GPX4 and HMOX1 were cloned into the pLenti-CRISPR-V2 plasmid. Two distinct sgRNAs were used to target HMOX1: 5′-CACCGCTATGTGAAGCGGCTCCACG-3′ and 5′-CACCGGGAGGAGATTGAGCGCAACA-3′. The sgRNA targeting GPX4 was 5′-GGTGAAGCGCTACGGACCCA-3′. The FOXO1 sgRNA plasmid was purchased from Beyotime. Transfections were carried out using TurboFect reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat#R0532) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lentiviral particles were generated in HEK293T cells, and viral supernatants were collected 48 h post-transfection. Transduced cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin (Basalmedia, Cat#S250J0) for 3 days.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using Magzol reagent (Magen, Cat#R4801), and reverse transcription was performed with the HiScript Q RT SuperMix kit (Vazyme, Cat#R223-01). Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix (Bimake, Cat#B21703) on the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transcriptional variations were determined via the ΔΔCt approach, normalizing against ACTB. Primers used for quantitative PCR are listed in Table S2.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat#P0013C) supplemented with protease (Bimake, Cat#B14002) and phosphatase (Bimake, Cat#B15002) inhibitor cocktails. Protein concentrations were measured with the BCA reagent (Beyotime, Cat#P0011). Equal amounts of protein were resolved via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature or with 5% BSA when probing phosphorylated proteins, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C. The primary antibodies employed in this study included the following: GPX4 (CST, Cat#52455, 1:1000), SLC7A11 (CST, Cat#12691, 1:1000), ACSL4 (Santa Cruz, Cat#sc-365230, 1:100), HMOX1 (Proteintech, Cat#10701-1-AP, 1:1000), TOMM20 (Abcam, Cat#ab186735, 1:5000), DHODH (CST, Cat#26381, 1:1000), FOXO1 (CST, Cat#2880, 1:1000), p-FOXO1 (Ser256, Selleck, Cat#F0800, 1:1000), FSP1 (CST, Cat#24972, 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, Cat#60004-1-Ig, 1:50000), α-Tubulin (Proteintech, Cat#11224-1-AP, 1:5000), ACTB (Proteintech, Cat#66009-1-Ig, 1:20000). Membranes were subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using Clarity Western ECL Substrates (BIO-RAD, Cat#1705061) on a gel image analysis system (LI-COR, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

Melanoma tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin-embedded according to standard procedures. Tissue sections were blocked with goat serum (ZSGB-Bio, Cat#ZLI-9056) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting FOXO1 (CST, Cat#2880; 1:200), 4HNE (Abcam, Cat#ab46545; 1:300), or HMOX1 (Proteintech, Cat#10701-1-AP; 1:200). Signal detection was carried out using the Universal Two-Step Polymer Detection System (mouse/rabbit enhanced polymer method; ZSGB-Bio, Cat#PV-9000), and staining was developed with a DAB Substrate Kit (ZSGB-Bio, Cat#ZLI-9018), following the manufacturers’ instructions. Images were captured at 400× magnification using a light microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

Multiplex fluorescent immunohistochemistry

Multiplex fluorescent immunohistochemistry was performed using a commercial kit (AiFang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Tissue sections underwent two sequential rounds of staining with antibodies against GZMB (Abcam, Cat#ab255598; 1:1000) and CD8α (Abcam, Cat#ab217344; 1:1000), each coupled with a distinct tyramide signal amplification system. Between staining cycles, antigen retrieval was conducted using sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Servicebio, Cat#G1202) for 20 min to remove residual antibodies and prevent cross-reactivity. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Abcam, Cat#ab104139). Imaging and pseudo-coloring were performed using the Vectra Polaris Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (PerkinElmer), and image analysis was carried out with Phenochart software (version 2.2.0).

Immunofluorescence cytochemistry

Cells cultured on sterilized coverslips in 24-well plates were fixed with pre-cooled methanol for 10 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (Sangon Biotech, Cat#A600198) for 5 min, and subsequently blocked with goat serum for 1 h. Cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with FOXO1 primary antibody (CST, Cat#2880; 1:200), followed by a 1 h incubation at room temperature with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Cat#A21207; 1:1000). Coverslips were mounted using fluoroshield mounting medium containing DAPI, and images were acquired using the BioTek Cytation 10 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader.

Flow cytometry

Tumor tissues were excised, mechanically dissociated, and filtered through 40 μm cell strainers to obtain single-cell suspensions. Fc receptor blockade was performed using anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (BioLegend, Cat#101320), and cell viability was assessed with the Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, Cat#423102). Cells were stimulated for 5 h with Cell Stimulation Cocktail (eBioscience, Cat#00-4975-93), followed by surface staining for 30 min at 4°C using APC/Cyanine7-CD45 (BioLegend, Cat#103116), APC-CD3 (BioLegend, Cat#100236), PerCP/Cyanine5.5-CD4 (BioLegend, Cat#100434), and PE/Cyanine7-CD8α (BioLegend, Cat#100722). Intracellular cytokine staining was conducted following fixation and permeabilization with the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience, Cat#00-5523-00), using PE/Dazzle 594-conjugated GZMB (BioLegend, Cat#372216) and FITC-conjugated IFNγ (BioLegend, Cat#505805) antibodies. Stained cells were analyzed by FACS LSRFortessa (Biosciences, USA). Data were further analyzed by FlowJo software (version 10.4).

RNA sequencing and data processing

A375 melanoma cells were treated with DMSO, 2.5 μM RSL3, 5 μM vorapaxar, or a combination of RSL3 and vorapaxar, each in triplicate, followed by total RNA extraction and sequencing. RNA sequencing reads were trimmed and quality-checked using FastQC software (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC). The human reference genome (GRCh38.p12 assembly) was utilized for mapping reads with HISAT2 software. To assemble the plotted reads into transcripts or genes, Stringtie software along with the genome annotation file (hg38_ucsc.annotated.gtf) was employed. DESeq2 was employed to calculate gene differences between each pair of treatments, with |fold change|≥1.5 and adjusted p-value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

LINC data processing

The top 400 upregulated genes between the combination treatment group (Vorapaxar+RSL3) and the control group (DMSO) were used as the gene set for the combination treatment’s action. The enrichment score for this gene set between small molecule treatment groups and controls was calculated using GSEA, utilizing data from LINC2020 (https://lincsproject.org/).

Datasets collection and processing

Sixty-two pretreatment tumor specimens from melanoma patients undergoing anti-PD-1 therapy were collected from Xiangya Hospital.32 Data quality was assessed using FastQC software (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC). Reads were mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 software to obtain BAM files. The assembly of reads into transcripts or genes was performed using Stringtie software in conjunction with the genome annotation file (hg38_ucsc.annotated.gtf). Ribas’s cohort dataset was collected from https://github.com/ParkerICI/MORRISON-1-public37037616.33 TPM (transcripts per million mapped reads) and log2 transformation were used to normalize for sequencing depth and gene length biases.

Calculation of pathway scores

The ferroptosis gene set WP_ferroptosis was obtained from MsigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb). Gene sets related to immune infiltration were derived from published articles.50,51,52 The ssGSEA algorithm in R was utilized to calculate the scores of ferroptosis and immune infiltration-related gene sets in each sample group. Detailed gene signatures for WP_ferroptosis and immune infiltration were listed in Tables S3 and S4.

Immune therapy outcome-related scores calculation

The T cell inflammation status for each specimen was quantified using the gene collection established by Ayers et al.52 To evaluate cytotoxic potential, researchers determined the geometric average of two cytolytic markers (GZMA and PRF1). Additional immunotherapy-relevant parameters were analyzed through TIDE computational platform, including IFNγ response, CD8+ T cell, microsatellite instability score, cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration, tumor M2 subtype, related macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, and T cell exclusion scores.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The results were presented as mean ± SD and analyzed through GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1). Statistical comparisons included two-tailed unpaired t-tests for dual-group analysis, one-way or two-way ANOVA for multiple groups. Fisher’s exact test/χ2 test was used to compare the proportion of patients responding to immunotherapy among the four groups (high FOXO1 + high HMOX1 group, high FOXO1 + low HMOX1 group, low FOXO1 + high HMOX1 group, low FOXO1 + low HMOX1 group).The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to compare the differences in immune therapy outcome-related scores between the high FOXO1 + high HMOX1 group and the low FOXO1 + low HMOX1 group. Survival outcomes were determined through Kaplan-Meier analysis with log rank testing, with statistical significance assessed. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, denoted as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
