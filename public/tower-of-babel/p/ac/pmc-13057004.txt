Assessment of lipid mediators in the urine of patients with Lyme disease, tick-borne encephalitis and human granulocytic anaplasmosis
Authors: Michał Biernacki, Elżbieta Skrzydlewska, Marta Dobrzyńska, Piotr Czupryna, Justyna Adamczuk, Anna Moniuszko-Malinowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To assess the host response to mono- and co-infection of tick-borne encephalitis (TBE) virus with Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato and Anaplasma phagocytophilum bacteria, as well as the possibility of using the analysis of the level of endocannabinoids and eicosanoids in urine for effective diagnostics. Urine of patients with Lyme disease (LD) (in the form of erythema migrans (EM) or neuroborreliosis (NB)), TBE, diagnosed by ELISA-based detection of serum and CSF anti-TBEV IgM and IgG antibodies in all cases, human granulocytic anaplasmosis (HGA), co-infection of TBEV, LD, and healthy individuals (Control Group, CG) was analyzed. A GC/LC–MS/MS platform was used to identify changes in the phospholipid metabolite profile in urine. Lipid peroxidation product levels were estimated by measuring 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE by GC–MS/MS and F2-isoprostanes 8-isoPGF2α by LC–MS/MS). Endocannabinoids and eicosanoids levels were assessed using LC–MS/MS. Statistically significant differences in the concentration of 8-isoP (EM, NB, TBE vs. CG) were observed in patients’ urine before treatment. However, the analysis of endocannabinoids showed significant differences in oleylethanolamide (OEA) concentration (TBE vs CG). The study of pro-inflammatory eicosanoid levels showed a difference for: prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) (TBE vs CG), leukotriene D4 LTD4 (EM vs HGA), and 5-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (5-HETE) (EM vs NB), assessed before treatment. The levels of anti-inflammatory eicosanoids showed a difference for prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) (EM vs CG) and 15-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (15-HETE) (TBE vs CG). However, the results in the level of phospholipid metabolites from patients after therapy did not show statistically significant differences compared to the value before treatment and between groups. Tick-borne diseases are accompanied by changes in lipid metabolism, both ROS- and enzyme-dependent, which can be identified even in urine. Urinary lipid mediators show potential as supportive markers but require validation in larger patient cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40464-z.

Introduction

The incidence of vector-borne diseases is increasing every year, accompanied by significant mortality. These diseases account for 17% of all infectious diseases and are responsible for over 700,000 deaths per year1. Worryingly, 80% of the world’s population is susceptible to one or more of these diseases. Vectors include, among others, ticks carrying a wide range of pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and protozoa, which cause infections usually characterized by nonspecific symptoms, making diagnosis and appropriate pharmacotherapy difficult, and consequently making tick-borne diseases a significant clinical problem1,2.

In the early stages of Lyme disease (LD), ca. 70% of infected people develop a skin lesion called erythema migrans (EM). If left untreated, the infection can spread through the bloodstream, lead to disseminated LD, and cause inflammatory complications in the joints, heart, muscles, and nervous system, leading to the most serious clinical form—neuroborreliosis (NB)3. Patients with TBE were treated symptomatically with anti-odematous and anti-inflammatory drugssupportive therapy including anti-inflammatory and anti-edema treatment. However, EM does not occur in all patients and may not be noticed by them, which may prolong the diagnosis of Lyme disease and, consequently, delay appropriate treatment, which may result in serious health consequences4. Anaplasmosis is usually asymptomatic, but if left untreated, it can lead to multi-organ failure, which can be fatal. Therefore, it is recommended that all patients with suspected or documented human granulocytic anaplasmosis (HGA) should be treated with doxycycline5. TBE occurs in two phases. In the first phase, flu-like symptoms appear, such as fever, headache, muscle and joint pain, which last up to 7 days, after which most patients recover spontaneously. However, in some patients, after a few days of feeling relatively well, the second phase occurs, which appears as meningitis, encephalitis, and meningoecephalomyelitis. Due to the lack of drugs active against the tick-borne encephalitis (TBE) virus, patients can only be treated symptomatically. There is effective vaccination, but the vaccination rate in different countries varies greatly, which does not guarantee the complete protection of society 6.

The greatest diagnostic and, consequently, therapeutic problems result from the fact that the Ixodes ricinus tick can transmit several pathogens simultaneously, causing co-infections, which may lead to nonspecific symptoms and significantly complicate the diagnosis and treatment of patients2. The consequences of infections with a single pathogen, but especially co-infections caused by pathogens transmitted by ticks, are metabolic changes in the host body related to the development of oxidative stress and inflammation, as well as modifications of components, especially macromolecular ones, of the body7,8.

In such conditions, there is an increase in, among others, reactive oxygen species (ROS)- and enzyme-dependent modifications of phospholipids7. ROS-dependent generation of lipid peroxidation products results in an increase in the level of small-molecule, unsaturated, reactive aldehydes (e.g., 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA)) and cyclization products, including iso- and neuroprostanes9, while the enzymatic hydrolysis of phospholipids promotes the increase in the level of endocannabinoids (with the participation of phospholipases and diacylglycerol (DAGL)) and free polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) (with the involvement of phospholipases A (PLAs)). However, endocannabinoids undergo further enzymatic metabolism (involving fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) and monoacylglycerol lipase (MAGL)) with the generation of free PUFAs10. However, free PUFAs, due to further enzymatic metabolism (dependent on cyclooxygenases (COXs), lipoxygenases (LOXs), and cytochrome P450 (CYP450)), generate various eicosanoids11.

Endocannabinoids and eicosanoids, generated due to the enzymatic metabolism of phospholipids, play an essential role in the development of inflammation and participate in the generation of oxidative stress12. Endocannabinoids are a family of lipid mediators involved in physiological activities, but also in the development of many pathological conditions, which they cause through the activation of G-protein-related receptors, mainly cannabinoid receptors13. However, the most frequently analyzed eicosanoids produced as a result of the enzymatic metabolism of free arachidonic acid11 include prostaglandins (PGs) and thromboxanes generated by COXs, as well as leukotrienes, lipoxins, and hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HETE). Produced by LOX. These lipid mediators regulate systemic metabolism mainly by activating G protein-coupled receptors12. Recently, their importance in the prolonged inflammatory response has attracted particular attention because, unlike acute inflammatory processes, chronic inflammatory processes, including those observed in the central nervous system (CNS), are essential for developing pathology14.

Therefore, it is believed that dysregulation of the level of lipid mediators plays an essential role in the etiology of tick-borne diseases and may accompany long-term complications15. Therefore, taking into account the role of eicosanoids and endocannabinoids in both the induction and resolution of inflammation, both in establishing a precise diagnosis and in assessing the effects of therapy, it seems necessary to analyze these compounds in the patient’s body, especially in cases of suspected bacterial and viral infections, transmitted by ticks. However, taking into account the invasive procedure of collecting cerebrospinal fluid and blood and the multi-stage procedures for analyzing these compounds in biological fluids (cerebrospinal fluid and blood), attempts are made to assess the level of these compounds in the human body by monitoring their level in urine16.

Therefore, considering the role of eicosanoids and endocannabinoids in both the induction and resolution of inflammation, the study aimed to check the body’s response to mono- and co-infection of TBE with Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato and Anaplasma phagocytophilum and the possibility of using the analysis of the level of endocannabinoids and eicosanoids in urine for effective diagnostics.

Urine samples for analyzing, after the addition of BHT, were stored at -80 °C for approximately 1.5–2 years (to assemble the experimental group). It was previously verified that such conditions did not alter the levels of the analyzed parameters.

All patients were evaluated in the Department of Infectious Diseases and Neuroinfections and the Outpatient Clinic of the Department of Infectious Diseases and Neuroinfections, Medical University of Bialystok, Poland, in the years 2018–2020, twice, before and after treatment. Patients with EM were treated with doxycycline 2 × 100 mg per 14–21 days. Patients with NB were treated with intravenous ceftriaxone for 21 days. Patients with HGA were treated with doxycycline 2 × 100 mg every 10 days. Patients with TBE were treated symptomatically with anti-odematous and anti-inflammatorydrugs. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Local Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Bialystok (Poland), No. R-I-002/169/2018. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients and healthy donors.

EM was diagnosed based on clinical manifestation and history of tick bite or exposure to tick bite. Based on clinical picture, lymphocytic pleocytosis in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and intrathecal synthesis of anti-Borrelia burgdorferi antibodies, definite NB was diagnosed.

Anaplasmosis was diagnosed according to the case definition by the CDC based on clinical presentation, tick bite exposure, and a positive PCR assay in skin biopsy or blood. Anaplasmosis was diagnosed when all three criteria were fulfilled.

TBE was diagnosed according to European Academy of Neurology guidelines, based on clinical symptoms, anti-TBE antibodies (diagnosed by ELISA-based detection of serum and CSF anti-TBEV IgM and IgG antibodies in all cases), and lymphocytic pleocytosis in the CSF.

TBE + LD were diagnosed, when in one patient, two diseases were confirmed.

Methods

Determination of the level of lipid peroxidation products

Lipid peroxidation in urine was estimated by measuring small molecular weight reactive aldehydes: 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE) and F2-isoprostanes (8-isoPGF2α).

The 4-HNE analysis was performed using gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (7890A GC–7000 with triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA) based on Luo’s method17. 4-HNE was derivatized to their respective PFB oximes utilizing PFB hydroxylamine as the reagent. Benzaldehyde-d6 was added to urine samples as an internal standard. Samples were deproteinized after incubation for 5 min with methanol at room temperature and then extracted with hexane. The hexane layer was evaporated and N,O-bis (trimethylsilyl) trifluoroacetamide in 1% trimethylchlorosilane was added, then 1 µL aliquots were loaded onto the column. The chromatography column was a 30 m length, 0.25 mm internal diameter, HP-5mS fused-silica capillary column (Agilent, USA). The following parameters were modified relative to Luo’s method: the thermal gradient and the ions monitored. The thermal gradient started at 50 °C and was held for 1 min (corresponding to the solvent delay) to minimize peak broadening of early-eluting analytes. The temperature was then ramped in three steps: first to 200 °C at 10 °C/min, second to 220 °C at 3 °C/min, and finally to 310 °C at 20 °C/min. The quadrupole was set to 150 °C, the MS source to 230 °C, and the mass transfer line to 280 °C. The analyte was measured in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode. The ions monitored for the PFB oxime of 4-HNE and benzaldehyde-d6 were m/z 333.0, 181.0, and 245.0, respectively. The 4-HNE level was normalized to the creatinine level and is presented in nmol/mg creatinine.

F2-isoprostanes (measured as 8-isoPGF2α) level was performed using the modified method of Coolen and colleagues18. Samples were purified by SPE before analysis of 8-iso PGF2α by LC–MS/MS. The first urine samples were mixed with the internal standard (8-iso-PGF2α-d4). Briefly, urine samples were acidified to pH 3 and centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. SPE was carried out with Sep-Pak Vac 3 cc (200 mg) SPE columns (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The supernatant was applied to a column preconditioned with 1 mL methanol and 1 mL water with acetic acid. The column was then washed with 1 mL water with acetic acid and 1 mL heptane. The 8-iso-PGF2α was eluted with ethyl acetate/heptane/methanol (50/40/10). Eluates were transferred to an eppendorf tube and evaporated to dryness under nitrogen atmosphere at room temperature. The following parameters were modified compared to Coolen’s method: the chromatographic gradient, the column, and the mass spectrometer settings. Finally, the residue was dissolved in 50 µL mobile phase (acetonitrile/water (80/20) with 0.05% v/v acetic acid) and analysed by LC/MS was carried out on a Agilent Technologies 1290 Infinity II UHPLC module coupled to a 6475 LC/TQ(triple quadrupole) tandem mass spectrometer controlled by Agilent OpenLab Control Panel (6475A) software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Eclipse Plus C18 analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm particle size). A gradient program was performed with a combination of 0.05% acetic acid in water (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B) and was performed with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min, starting with an initial composition of 10% B, which increases linearly to 40% B within 4 min. Next the following gradient was employed: 4.0–8.0 min 40% B, 8.0–9.0 min 40 -100% B. After holding 100% B for 3 min, the initial conditions were restored for 6 min before the subsequent sample analysis. The other operating parameters for the mass spectrometer were as follows: gas temperature: 350 °C, gas flow: 5 l/min: nebulizer: 45 psi, sheath gas temperature: 250 °C, sheath gas flow: 11 l/min, capillary: 4500 V, nozzle voltage: 500 V. Ionization was achieved using electrospray in the negative electrospray ionisation (ESI) using MRM mode. Quantitation was achieved by monitoring the native compound transition, m/z 353.2 → 193.1, with the corresponding internal standard transition, m/z 357.2 → 197.1. The urine F2-isoprostane level, normalized to the creatinine level, is presented in pg/mg creatinine.

Determination of the level of endocannabinoids

Endocannabinoids level was measured using the modified method of Luque-Córdoba and colleagues19. The sample preparation was performed based on the publication by Watkins and colleagues20. Samples were purified by SPE before analysis of endocannabinoids by LC–MS/MS. The first urine samples were mixed with internal standards (anandamide-d8 (AEA-d8) and oleylethanolamide-d4 (OEA-d4)). Briefly, urine samples were deproteinized with aceton and centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min at 4 °C. SPE was carried out with Oasis HLB 3 cc (60 mg) SPE columns (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The supernatant was applied to a column preconditioned with 1 mL methanol and 1 mL water. The column was then washed with 5% methanol with acetic acid. The endocannabinoids were eluted with methanol and ethyl acetate. Eluates were transferred to an Eppendorf tube and evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen atmosphere at room temperature. The following parameters were modified compared to Luque-Córdoba method: the chromatographic gradient, the column, and the mass spectrometer settings. Finally, the residue was dissolved in 50 µL mobile phase (ACN/water 70:30 v/v with 0.1% formic acid) and analysed by LC/MS carried out on a Agilent Technologies 1290 Infinity II UHPLC module coupled to a 6475 LC/TQ tandem mass spectrometer controlled by Agilent OpenLab Control Panel (6475A) software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a 120 EC-C18 analytical column (3.0 × 150 mm; 2.7 µm particle size). A gradient program was performed with a combination of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B) and was performed with a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, starting with an initial composition of 70% B, which increases linearly to 80% B within 5 min. Next, the following gradient was employed: 5.0–10.0 min 80–88% B, 10.0–16.0 min 78 -100% B. After holding 100% B for 4 min, the initial conditions were restored for 5 min before the subsequent sample analysis. The other operating parameters for the mass spectrometer were as follows: gas temperature: 300 °C, gas flow: 12 L/min: nebulizer: 35 psi, sheath gas temperature: 250 °C, sheath gas flow: 11 L/min, capillary: 3500 V, nozzle voltage: 500 V.

Ionization was achieved using electrospray in the positive electrospray ionisation (ESI) using MRM mode. Quantitation was achieved by monitoring the native compound transition as follows: m/z 348.3 → 62.15 for AEA, m/z 326.0 → 62.0 for OEA, m/z 300.3 → 62.0 for palmitoylethanolamide (PEA), m/z 356.2 → 63.05 for AEA-d8, and m/z 330.20 → 66.15 for OEA-d4. The urine endocannabinoid level is normalized to the creatinine level. The levels of OEA and PEA were expressed in pmol/mg creatinine, and AEA was expressed in fmol/mg creatinine.

Determination of the level of eicosanoids

Eicosanoids level was performed using the modified method of Watkins and colleagues20. Samples were purified by SPE before analysis of eicosanoids by LC–MS/MS, which was previously described in the methodology on endocannabinoids. The only difference was the addition of the following internal standards to the sample (leukotriene B4-d4 (LTB4-d4), prostaglandin D2-d4 (PGD2-d4), 15-deoxy-Δ12,14-PGJ2-d4 (15-d-PGJ2-d4), and 15-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid-d8 (15-HETE-d8)). The following parameters were modified compared to Watkins method: the chromatographic gradient, the column, and the mass spectrometer settings. LC–MS/MS was carried out on an Agilent Technologies 1290 Infinity II UHPLC module coupled to a 6475 LC/TQ tandem mass spectrometer controlled by Agilent OpenLab Control Panel (6475A) software (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Eclipse Plus C18 analytical column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm particle size). A gradient program was performed with a combination of 0.1% acetic acid in water (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B), and was performed with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, starting with an initial composition of 25% B. The gradient increased from 25 to 95% B over 14.5 min and returned to 25% B by 15 min, followed by 1 min at 25% B to re-equilibrate the column. The other operating parameters for the mass spectrometer were as follows: gas temperature: 300 °C, gas flow: 13 L/min: nebulizer: 35 psi, sheath gas temperature: 250 °C, sheath gas flow: 11 L/min, capillary: 4000 V, nozzle voltage: 1500 V. Ionization was performed using negative ESI in MRM mode, allowing efficient detection of [M − H] − ions formed by deprotonation of the acidic carboxyl groups. Quantitation was achieved by monitoring the native compound transition as follows: m/z 351.3 → 271.2 for PGE2 and PGD2, m/z 315.2 → 271.2 for 15-d-PGJ2, m/z 335.20 → 195.10 for LTB4, m/z 495.45 → 177.10 for LTD4, m/z 319.2 → 257.2 for 5-HETE, m/z 319.2 → 179.1 for 12-HETE, m/z 319.0 → 301.2 for 15-HETE, m/z 295.0 → 277.2 for 13-hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (13-HODE), m/z 355.0 → 275.3 for PGD2-d4, m/z 319.3 → 275.2 for 15-d-PGJ2-d4, m/z 339.10 → 197.10 for LTB4-d4 and m/z 327.0 → 226.2 for 15-HETE-d8. The urine eicosanoid level is normalized to the creatinine level. The levels of eicosanoids were expressed in pmol/mg creatinine.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed by Statistica 13.0. Normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Groups were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test and the Mann–Whitney U test. Results before and after treatment were compared with the Wilcoxon test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

One of the fundamental determinants of physiological homeostasis is the maintenance of redox balance at the cellular and tissue levels, which depends on tight regulation of metabolic processes and antioxidant defense mechanisms21. The presence of infectious agents, including bacteria and viruses, may disrupt this balance, leading to oxidative stress and associated metabolic alterations21. The involvement of oxidative stress in tick-borne diseases has previously been investigated in tick-borne encephalitis, Lyme disease, and human granulocytic anaplasmosis, with demonstrated disturbances in redox balance and related metabolic changes in plasma, serum, and cerebrospinal fluid8,13,15.

Increased activity of pro-oxidant enzymes and enhanced generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) constitute a recognized component of the host response to pathogen invasion22. In the context of infectious diseases, ROS may exert both protective and detrimental effects. On the one hand, they contribute to pathogen control through direct oxidative damage and by supporting immune mechanisms such as pattern recognition receptor signaling, autophagy, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and T-cell- mediated responses23. On the other hand, excessive or sustained ROS production may lead to oxidative stress in the host, resulting in modification of key cellular components, including lipids and proteins22. Previous studies have demonstrated that infection with the TBE virus, including cases with bacterial co-infections, is associated with the development of oxidative stress in the host organism, reflecting activation of host defense mechanisms7. Similar oxidative stress- related responses have been described in a wide range of viral and bacterial infections. Lipids, as key structural components of biological membranes, also function as bioactive molecules involved in cellular signaling pathways and metabolic regulation, including during infectious processes. Many viruses replicate in the cytoplasm of infected cells and depend on host lipid components for genome replication and assembly24. Moreover, infection- associated remodeling of host membrane phospholipids has been shown to facilitate viral replication and contribute to evasion of antiviral defense mechanisms25. Previous research on the level of blood and cerebrospinal fluid has shown that both bacterial and viral infections and co-infections are accompanied by oxidative stress, which favors the functioning of pathogens but disturbs the host’s metabolism, among others. At the level of lipid metabolism15. Oxidative stress accompanying viral encephalitis, as well as Borrelia burgdorferi and Anaplasma phagocytophilum infections, promotes modifications of lipid metabolism not only ROS, but also enzyme-dependent, including mainly phosphatases, cyclooxygenases, and lipoxygenases26, whose activity usually increases under conditions of oxidative stress, especially in viral infections27.

The high degree of oxidative stress in the host body is indicated by the increased levels of products of ROS-dependent phospholipid metabolism, assessed in this study by the increased levels of 8-isoprostaglandin F2α observed even in the urine of patients infected with TBE virus, Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato and Anaplasma phagocytophilum. Similar changes in urine have been previously observed in patients with Lyme disease and neuroborreliosis, including increased levels of lipid peroxidation products such as 4-HNE, 4-HHE, 4-ONE, MDA, and acrolein9,28. The changes in lipid metabolites level observed in urine are consistent with the lipid metabolism abnormalities identified in patients’ blood15. Prostaglandin, 8-iso-PGF2α, is an isoprostane produced by nonenzymatic peroxidation of arachidonic acid in membrane phospholipids. Studies have shown that 8-iso-PGF2α in urine may be the most reliable marker for assessing lipid peroxidation in chronic diseases and neuroinfections28.

Literature data confirm that oxidative stress accompanying viral tick-borne encephalitis and Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato and Anaplasma phagocytophilum infections also promotes modifications of lipid metabolism controlled by enzymes, mainly phosphatases, cyclooxygenases, and lipoxygenases26, the activity of which usually increases under conditions of oxidative stress, especially in viral infections27. Consequently, increased activity of lipolytic enzymes leads to the metabolism of PUFAs into lipid mediators, including endocannabinoids and eicosanoids.

Endocannabinoids are a family of signaling molecules, metabolites of phospholipid PUFAs, and are involved in many metabolic and neurological disorders10. This group of compounds includes AEA and N-acylethanolamine (NAE) derivatives, including OEA and PEA13. AEA has been detected in many tissues, including the central nervous system and biological fluids of the human body, such as blood and urine13, and its biological effects result from the activation of membrane receptors, mainly those related to G protein10. In contrast, NAE, OEA, and PEA have been described as ambient compounds because they generate similar physiological effects to AEA, but have little or no affinity for cannabinoid receptors29. Among the endocannabinoids assessed in the urine of patients with tick-borne diseases, there was a significant increase in the concentration of OEA and PEA in the urine of patients with TBE. The increase in the level of these endocannabinoids in urine results from their increased generation primarily in diseased tissues, including the skin, CNS, and blood cells. Therefore, considering the directions of metabolic action, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects and modulation of the immune response, lipid amides, changes in the level of OEA and PEA can be considered the body’s response to the infection. The anti-inflammatory effects of OEA are well established. They are the result of activating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPAR-α), which results in suppression of pro-inflammatory factors, including cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), and Toll-like receptors-4 (TLR4), as well as the reduction of the pro-oxidant inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)29. Similarly to OEA, PEA also binds PPAR-α receptor and initiates inhibiting the generation of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) and NF-κB, as well as the expression of adhesion molecules (intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), P-selectin)30.

Suggesting that OEA and PEA in urine originate mainly from infected tissues, it seems reasonable to assume that OEA in brain tissue and/or cerebrospinal fluid may promote the reduction of ROS levels while increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px)), which consequently contributes to limiting the process of lipid peroxidation and the formation of reactive metabolites such as 4-HNE13. In bacterial infections, OEA also exhibits anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties31. However, in a clinical trial on COVID-19 patients, supplementation with boron citrate containing OEA significantly lowered the levels of both high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and IL-1β and considerably improved O2 saturation and respiratory rate32. In studies on rats administered lipopolysaccharide (LPS), as a component of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria extensively used for modeling neuroinflammation, it was shown that administration of OEA reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, NF-κB, as well as lipid peroxidation products such as MDA, and also PGE2—pro-inflammatory eicosanoid31. It was also suggested that the observed changes are due to the direct action of OEA on the intestinal barrier, where it modulates inflammation and prevents the translocation of bacteria from the intestinal lumen into the systemic circulation33. However, chronic administration of OEA to mice fed a standard diet changes the fecal microbiota profile by changing the Firmicutes: Bacteroidetes ratio in favor of Bacteroidetes, reducing the level of Firmicutes (Lactobacillus) and the expression of intestinal cytokines secreted by immune cells34. Whereas PEA inhibits the release of histamine, prostaglandin D2, and TNF-α induced by allergens in isolated canine skin mast cells, and also regulates the activity of these cells35. Thus, PEA can be used as an immune checkpoint. The increase in OEA levels observed in patients with TBE, which has both anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, suggests that these compounds may enhance the body’s protective functions against viral infection and alleviate the inflammation in patients.

Another group of investigated lipid mediators is eicosanoids, which are products of the enzymatic metabolism of free unsaturated PUFAs, mainly free arachidonic acid, by the COXs, LOXs, and CYP450 pathways11. In human blood, increased activity of the following enzymes, LOX-5 and COX-1/2, has been observed in both viral and bacterial infections caused by ticks15. Moreover, viral and bacterial infections, Chandipura virus (CHPV), and mouse treatment with LPS induce COX-2 expression in the mouse brain36. Therefore, in urine, significant changes in the levels of eicosanoids belonging to the anti-inflammatory compound group (15-HETE, PGD2) and the pro-inflammatory group (PGE2, 5-HETE) are demonstrated. However, a significant increase in the level of PGE2 was observed only during TBE virus infection. This may be because synthesis of PGE2 is upregulated by cyclooxygenases upon stimulation by diverse proinflammatory stimuli, including interleukin (IL)-1β, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, whose levels were enhanced in tick-borne infections37. PGE2 was also shown to regulate inflammation and the immune system by controlling cytokine expression/concentration38 and inhibiting various pathways, including nitric oxide (NO) production, interferon (IFN) and TNF-α secretion, apoptosis pathways, and viral receptor expression39. Furthermore, PGE2 may directly influence viral infection by increasing virion production and release, impeding virus binding and replication, and/or promoting viral gene expression38. Another study showed that Gram-negative bacteria, including Borrelia burgdorferi, evoke a more robust PGE2 response in human monocytes40. However, in our study, statistically significant changes in the level of PGE2 in bacterial infections were not observed in the urine. These results also show that the levels of eicosanoids with anti-inflammatory properties, such as 15-d-PGJ2 and 15-HETE, also increase during TBEV infection. Similar relationships are demonstrated in patients with EM, but an additional increase in the level of prostaglandin PGD2 is observed.

In the urine of patients with anaplasmosis caused by the Gram-negative bacterium Anaplasma phagocytophilum, no statistically significant changes in the concentration of any tested eicosanoids are observed compared to healthy people. However, the above patients have lower levels of LTD-4 than those with EM. This indicates a significant increase in the level of eicosanoids in the early symptoms of the disease, resulting from bacterial infection. However, it is known that during viral infection of the CNS, the activity of the inflammasome responsible for the induction of inflammation is modulated by a metabolic mechanism involving PGD241. The brain’s most abundant prostaglandin has pro- and anti-inflammatory properties, depending on the receptor it activates.

The microglial receptor DP1 activation is known to increase the expression of the inflammasome inhibitor PYDC3, thereby protecting the nervous system from inflammation41. However, activation of DP1 receptors during the incubation period and DP2 receptors during the symptomatic phase by PGD2 causes lymphopenia42. Moreover, it was found that the transduction of h-PGDS-PGD2 signaling can increase the expression of heme oxygenase (HO-1), which has both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, reducing the impact of the development of inflammatory diseases43. Moreover, it is known that PGD2 can undergo further non-enzymatic metabolism to produce 15d-PGJ2, which activates PPAR-γ receptors44 and reduces the effectiveness of NF-κB, which promotes the reduction of inflammation in various viral infections45. Therefore, the above changes in the level of anti-inflammatory lipid mediators can be associated with both the type of pathogen and the stage of disease advancement, as is the case with EM and neuroborreliosis. However, in the early stages of the disease, the body’s anti-inflammatory reaction is immediate. Moreover, an increase in the level of 15-HETE is observed in the urine of patients with TBE. 15-HETE is a lipid mediator at the intersection of various inflammation pathways46. Both eicosanoids are agonists of the PPARγ receptor, which regulates the expression of the host and viral genome, resulting in increased viral replication46 while weakening the expression of viral mRNA and, consequently, the inflammatory response in the patient’s organism47.

Regardless of the above changes, a significant increase in the level of pro-inflammatory 5-HETE acid was significantly different between patients with EM and NB. Interestingly, the level of inflammatory eicosanoids, such as 5-HETE in urine, was considerably higher in patients with EM than in patients with NB, which may be due to the increased activity of 5-LOX and 12-LOX characteristic of the early-stage diseases48. In such a phase, 5-LOX metabolites release cytokines, TNF-α, IL-1α, IL-1β, and express tissue factor on cell membranes, which was observed in the case of viral infection caused by SARS-CoV-249. Other studies have shown that LOXs are involved in, among others, different phases of influenza virus infection50. Moreover, it was found that during bacterial infection, the attachment of 5-HETE to phospholipids increases the production of superoxide anion radical and interleukin-851. Considering the diverse data related to the human body’s response to various types of pathogens, including bacterial, viral, and mixed, it is difficult to predict how the body will respond, depending on the patient’s pre-infectious condition.

Conclusion

In summary, it can be stated that tick-borne diseases are accompanied by changes in lipid metabolism, both ROS- and enzyme-dependent, which, due to modern analytical methodology, can be identified even in urine.

The increase in the level of lipid peroxidation products in the urine of patients with viral infections (TBE), bacterial infections (EM, NB, ANA) and co-infections (TBE + LD) before treatment confirms the dysregulation of the redox balance and increased enzymatic metabolism of phospholipids in the patient’s body, which is also reflected in the level endocannabinoids and eicosanoids in the body of patients and consistently observed in their urine. An increase in the level of anti-inflammatory lipid mediators (OEA) is observed in the urine of patients with TBE, which may indicate that the body has initiated defensive actions. However, the level of eicosanoids can also be related to the stage of disease advancement, especially in the case of EM and NB. The important limitation of our study was the sample size, especially of patients with HGA and also the fact, that the patients with different forms of LD received different antibiotic treatment, which could potentially influence on the results. The obtained results therefore indicate the potential of using the assessment of lipid mediators for differential diagnosis purposes, but require research on larger groups of patients.
