Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses reveal novel R2R3-MYB transcription factors control anthocyanin biosynthesis in broccoli
Authors: Quan Zhang, Shuhui Song, Holger Budahn, Ning Liu, Yunhua Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen broccoli is a very frequently consumed, nutrient-rich vegetable, whereas purple broccoli, known for its high anthocyanin content, is not so common. To investigate the anthocyanin biosynthesis in broccoli, one green broccoli and four purple broccolis were subjected to integrative analyses with metabolomics and transcriptomics. Our comprehensive examination revealed that in difference to other Brassica vegetables, the main anthocyanins accumulating in purple broccoli are delphinidin compounds, especially delphinidin-3-O-glucoside-5-O-galactoside. Transcriptomic analysis demonstrated significant variations in the expression levels of genes such as BoF3′H, BoDFR, and BoANS among the samples, suggesting the involvement of upstream transcription factors, regulating these genes. Furthermore, the integration of transcriptomic data and transient functional validation identified two MYB transcription factors, BoMYB147 and BoMYB148, as key regulators of anthocyanin biosynthesis. In conclusion, this study identifies the types of anthocyanins present in purple broccoli and enhances the understanding of the regulatory genes involved in anthocyanin synthesis. It provides genetic insights and a conceptual framework for future molecular breeding strategies directed toward enhancing the quality of purple broccoli varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07298-9.Keywords: Broccoli, Transcriptomics, Metabolomics, MYB, Anthocyanin

Introduction

Broccoli (Brassica oleracea var. italica) is one of the major vegetables within the Brassica oleracea varieties, originating from the Mediterranean coast and now widely cultivated around the world [18]. Its edible florets and stalks are rich in phytonutrients, vitamins, and bioactive phytochemicals, offering a wide range of health benefits [29, 45]. Through natural selection and artificial breeding, traditional broccoli varieties have consistently inherited the genetic traits necessary for forming green curds [24]. However, to satisfy consumer demands for more variability and aesthetic appeal, researchers started to develop purple broccoli. The lack of molecular markers for breeding purple broccoli presents a significant challenge to accelerating breeding efforts. Therefore, it is particularly urgent to identify the genes that control anthocyanin synthesis and to apply modern biotechnologies to utilize these genes.

Anthocyanins, secondary metabolites of flavonoids unique to plants, are the primary compounds responsible for color formation in fruits, vegetables, and flowers [41]. These compounds possess a basic flavonoid structure characterized by a C6 (A) -C3 (C) -C6 (B) carbon skeleton, which consists of two benzene rings (the A and B rings) [25]. Based on the substituents at the 3- and 5-carbon positions on the B ring, natural anthocyanins are classified into six types: pelargonidin, cyanidin, delphinidin, malvidin, peonidin, and petunidin [33]. Anthocyanins are produced through the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, which is primarily influenced by structural biosynthetic genes and transcription factors (TFs) [1]. Structural biosynthetic genes can be categorized into early biosynthetic genes (EBGs) and late biosynthetic genes (LBGs) [8]. EBGs, including chalcone synthase (CHS), chalcone isomerase (CHI), flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H), and flavanone 3′-hydroxylase (F3′H), are responsible for the biosynthesis of dihydroflavonols. LBGs, such as dihydroflavonol reductase (DFR), anthocyanin synthase (ANS), and UDP-glucose: flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT), facilitate the conversion of dihydroflavonols into various anthocyanins in plants [30]. The transcription factors involved in anthocyanin biosynthesis mainly include MYB, bHLH, and WD40 family proteins, which can form an MBW complex to regulate the expression of structural biosynthetic genes, thereby influencing anthocyanin accumulation [11, 39]. Notably, MYB TFs have been identified as major regulators in anthocyanin biosynthesis [21].

The MYB protein family is extensive and functionally diverse across eukaryotes, classified into four classes based on the number of adjacent repeats: 1R-MYB, 2R-MYB (R2R3-MYB), 3R-MYB, and 4R-MYB [8]. The R2R3-MYB class is the largest MYB subfamily and is involved in the regulation of the flavonoid biosynthesis, affecting anthocyanin accumulation in plants [13]. COLORED1 was the first R2R3-MYB protein shown to be associated with anthocyanin synthesis, promoting its accumulation in the aleurone of maize [7, 28]. Subsequently, numerous R2R3-MYB proteins have been confirmed to function as regulators of anthocyanin synthesis in various plants. For instance, AtMYB21 regulates the expression of AtFLS1 to facilitate flavonol biosynthesis in Arabidopsis stamens [47]. In blue-colored berries, VmMYBA1, VmMYBPA1.1, and VmMYBPA2.2 induce anthocyanin accumulation by activating the expression of DFR and ANS [20]. BrMYB2, isolated from the purple Head Chinese cabbage cultivar 11S91, significantly enhances anthocyanin production [14]. In onions, the anthocyanin activator AcMYB96 binds to the promoters of ANS and UFGT to promote anthocyanin biosynthesis [23]. Advances in color gene identification provide opportunities for rapidly improving crop color by fine-tuning the expression of MYB transcription factors.

Broccoli cultivation in China began on a large scale in the 1980 s, and today, China is the largest producer of broccoli in the world. However, purple broccoli remains extremely rare in the market [44]. There is a significant potential for breeding high-nutrient broccoli with enhanced anthocyanin content. Several PCR-based molecular markers were developed to identify the anthocyanin biosynthesis-related gene in cauliflower [6]. However, there are still relatively few reports on the molecular markers related to the curd color of broccoli. Previous reports indicated that a 14-bp insertion in the promoter of BoTT8 caused purple pigmentation in the hypocotyls of young broccoli seedlings, although its effect on curd color remains unknown [29]. Several studies have shown that changes in the expression of structural genes can affect broccoli curd color, but they all focus on the same gene [24, 40, 44]. Manipulating the expression of transcription factors can have significantly impact on the anthocyanin production [27]. However, few transcription factors regulating curd color and anthocyanin synthesis in broccoli are currently known. Therefore, identification of transcription factors that regulate anthocyanins in broccoli is critically important.

To address this knowledge gap and improve breeding efficiency, this study aims to identify anthocyanin profiles and concentrations in five broccoli varieties. Additionally, transcriptome analysis will assess the expression levels of relevant genes. By combining genome-wide identification of R2R3-MYBs in broccoli, we expect to reveal potential MYB factors regulating anthocyanin synthesis. The findings could pave the way for innovative strategies to enhance anthocyanin accumulation in broccoli.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The experimental materials used in this study comprised the ‘G1’ inbred line of green curd, and four purple curd inbred lines with different anthocyanin accumulations, named ‘C1, C2, C3, and C4’, which were selected randomly from the germplasm resources based on the degree of curd color. These broccolis were planted at the Sijiqing experimental farm of the Beijing Vegetable Research Center in Beijing, China (39°9′ N, 116°3′ E). Given that the color of broccoli curd is mainly determined by their sepals, sepal samples of five broccoli Lines were collected from 12-week-old plants. The samples were peeled off from plants and immediately frozen with liquid nitrogen.

Metabolomic analysis

The qualitative and quantitative analysis of anthocyanin metabolites in the sample was conducted by MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The samples were ground into powder, and fifty milligrams of freeze-dried powder were dissolved in 0.5 mL of methanol/water/hydrochloric acid (500:500:1, V/V/V). After two extractions, the supernatants were combined and passed through a 0.22 μm pore-sized filter to remove impurities. Subsequently, the supernatant obtained in the previous step was used for LC-MS/MS detection using the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform. Three biological replicates of each inbred line were conducted for metabolomic analysis in this study.

RNA-seq analysis

Three biological replicates were conducted for transcriptome analysis. RNA extraction and RNA-seq were performed as previously reported [46]. The cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina sequencing platform by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome of Brassica oleracea (JZS 2.0, http://www.brassicadb.cn/) using Hisat2 v2.0.5. Differential expression analysis among five broccoli lines were calculated, based on DESeq2. Genes that met the criteria of |log2Fold Change| ≥1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed. The volcano map and enrichment analysis was plotted using the Personalbio genescloud platform (https://www.genescloud.cn/) based on differentially expressed genes. The heat maps were plotted using TBtools (https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools) [4]. All changes in gene expression are presented in Table S1.

Identification of the R2R3-MYB family genes

The R2R3-MYB family protein sequences of Arabidopsis were obtained from TAIR 10 (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). Then, using 125 Arabidopsis R2R3-MYB protein sequences as queries to perform blast search against the reference genome of Brassica oleracea (http://www.brassicadb.cn/) to obtain the candidate R2R3-MYB protein sequences of B. oleracea. Finally, the NCBI conserved domain database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) and the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) were used to screen the proteins that belong to R2R3-MYB type. The basic properties of R2R3-MYB proteins in B. oleracea were estimated using ExPasy (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/protpar-ref.html) [10].

Phylogenetic analysis

The neighbor-joining tree was constructed using MEGA 11.0 [35], containing 125 Arabidopsis R2R3-MYB proteins and 236 B. oleracea R2R3-MYB proteins. Bootstrap analysis was performed with 1,000 replications. The Evolview tool was used to visualize the evolutionary tree [15]. The gene structure of the R2R3-MYB in broccoli was analyzed by Gene Structure Display Server (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/index.php) [16].

Chromosomal distribution and gene collinearity analysis

The detailed physical positions of BoMYB genes were obtained from the genome annotation files of B. oleracea, which was downloaded in Brassica database (http://www.brassicadb.cn/). The results were visualized using MG2C software (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.1/) [3].

Transient overexpression assays

In order to verify the function of BoMYBs in regulating anthocyanin synthesis, the full-length coding regions of BoMYB60, BoMYB147, BoMYB148, BoMYB234 were cloned from purple broccoli and fused to pSuper1300 vector, respectively. The amplification of the coding sequences of BoMYB29 and BoMYB48 failed. Then, the Agrobacterium tumefaciens (GV3101) cells containing integrated plasmids were introduced into tobacco leaves alone. The empty vector served as a negative control to elucidate the effect of the target genes. In addition, the infection solution containing BoTT8 was mixed with the MYB infection solution and then injected into the tobacco leaves. The infiltration sites on leaves were collected for imaging and the methods to detect anthocyanin contents as previously reported [22] with minor modifications. Thirty mg of leaf tissue were dissolved in 300 µL of extraction buffer (1% HCl–ethanol) and subsequently incubated at 60℃ for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatants were collected. Then, the supernatant (20 µL) was separately mixed with potassium chloride–hydrochloric acid buffer solution (pH = 1.0) and sodium acetate buffer solution (pH = 4.5), and the absorbance was measured at 530 nm (A530) and 700 nm (A700) using multi-mode detection platform (SpectraMax Paradigm, Molecular Devices, Austria). The absorbance differences of the sample at different pH values were first calculated using the formula △A= (A530-A700)pH1.0-(A530-A700)pH4.5. The anthocyanin concentration was determined using the formula . Four independent biological replicates were measured.

qRT-PCR

qRT-PCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System with TB Premix Ex Taq II and ROX Reference Dye II (Takara, #RR820A). Relative gene expression level was analyzed using the comparative critical threshold (2−△△Ct) [26]. The expression of β-actin was used as the internal control [49]. The primers used in this study were listed in Table S9.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Student′s t test or one-way ANOVA. Significant differences refer to statistical significance at P < 0.05 or P < 0.01. All the measurements were performed by three independent biological replicates at least.

Results

Identification and quantification anthocyanins in broccoli curd

To explore the anthocyanins levels in the curd stage we tested five broccoli inbred lines of different color. Phenotypic observations indicated that the color differences in broccoli florets reflect the color of the sepals (Fig. 1A). Through targeted metabolomic analysis of anthocyanins, a total of 59 compounds were detected in five broccoli inbred lines, including naringenin, quercetin-3-O-glucoside, proanthocyanidin B1, and 56 types of anthocyanins (Table S2). A count of 13, 38, 44, 45, and 46 anthocyanins were detected in G1, C1, C2, C3, and C4, respectively. Six common anthocyanins were detected in all five samples, but their content varies significantly among the different samples (Fig. 1B). The cyanidin content is higher in green curds than in purple curds. In G1, the content of cyanidin is 61.99 µg/g, accounting for 99.44% of the total anthocyanin content, with the main forms being cyanidin-3-O-glucoside and cyanidin-3-gentiobioside. In purple broccoli, the cyanidin glycoside contents were 56.67 µg/g, 57.97 µg/g, 48.22 µg/g, and 46.10 µg/g in samples C1, C2, C3, and C4, respectively, primarily consisting of cyanidin-3-O-glucoside. However, the predominant anthocyanin in purple broccoli is delphinidin, which accounted for 67.71%, 70.72%, 74.39%, and 69.23% of the total anthocyanin content in C1, C2, C3, and C4, respectively. Malvidin, pelargonidin, peonidin, and petunidin anthocyanins are all present in very low proportions in both green and purple materials (Fig. 1C, Table S2).

To elucidate how variations in individual anthocyanin concentrations contribute to total anthocyanin differentials, we analyzed the relative abundance of each differential anthocyanin based on absolute quantification data from five curd samples (Fig. 2C and S1). Taking cyanidin content in G1 and C1 as an example, the specific calculation formula is as follows: (the percentage of the difference in cyanidin-type anthocyanin content between C1 and G1 relative to the difference in total anthocyanin content between C1 and G1), the change ratios of the other types of anthocyanins are calculated in the same manner. Compared to the G1 variety, cyanidin was found to be downregulated in the C1, C2, C3, and C4 samples, with the respective proportions of change accounting for − 4.048%, −2.228%, −8.138%, and − 15.107% of the total differential anthocyanins. In contrast, the proportions of increase for delphinidin were observed to be 99.68%, 95.04%, 101.695%, and 110.09% in the C1, C2, C3, and C4 samples, respectively. The absolute content changes for petunidin were recorded as 4.065%, 6.133%, 5.413%, and 3.979% in the C1, C2, C3, and C4 samples, respectively. The content of pelargonidin, malvidin, and peonidin had remained largely unchanged (Fig. 2D-G). These results indicate that delphinidin is a key factor in determining the formation of purple coloration in the curd. Further findings revealed that delphinidin-3-O-glucoside-5-O-galactoside is the predominant anthocyanin accumulated in purple curds, while it is nearly absent in green curds. (Fig. 2H).

Transcriptomic analysis of five broccoli varieties

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying anthocyanin biosynthesis in broccoli, a transcriptomic analysis was performed using the five distinct broccoli curd samples. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted and indicated that the biological replicates of each sample exhibited consistent profiles. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 21.21% and 28.16% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 3A). In total, 52,127 annotated genes were detected as being expressed in broccoli curd. According to the established criteria for differentially expressed genes (DEGs), a total of 15,464 genes (|log2Fold Change| ≥1 and FDR < 0.05) were detected as differentially expressed in at least one group (Table S1), among which 3,271 genes were found to be differentially expressed in all four comparison groups: C1_vs_G1, C2_vs_G1, C3_vs_G1, and C4_vs_G1 (Fig. 3B). Compared to G1, 4,508, 3,889, 4,345, and 4,721 genes were significantly upregulated in groups C1, C2, C3, and C4, respectively. In contrast, the analysis revealed that 3,907, 4,487, 4,767, and 4,023 genes were significantly downregulated in purple broccoli (Fig. 3C). To explore the biological functions of DEGs, KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted, and revealed that DEGs are involved in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 3D and S2, Table S3). The results indicate that the differential expression of flavonoid biosynthesis genes contributes to the color variation of broccoli florets.

Regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway in the five broccoli varieties

The anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway in plants is well characterized. So, homology analysis was performed based on the protein sequences of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes from Arabidopsis, resulting in the identification of 26 homologous genes in the B. oleracea genome (Table S4). Previous studies have indicated that these anthocyanin biosynthesis genes positively regulate anthocyanin synthesis. In this study, the anthocyanin content in samples C2 and C3 was significantly higher than that in samples C1 and C4, whereas sample G1 had the lowest anthocyanin content. The expression variations of BoF3H1, BoF3’H, BoDFR, BoANS, and BoUFGT among the five varieties are similar to the changes observed in anthocyanin phenotypic variation (Fig. 4 and S3-S4). These results indicate that these genes may be key factors contributing to the differences in color of broccoli florets.

Transcription factor proportions in differential gene expression

Transcription factors are also key regulators of anthocyanin biosynthesis in plants. In this study, the expression of several structural genes, involved in anthocyanin synthesis, was found to be altered, indicating the potential presence of upstream transcription factors that regulate their expression. An analysis of the proportion of various transcription factors among all differentially expressed genes revealed that MYB-type transcription factors had the highest representation, accounting for 9.51% (Fig. 5A). MYB transcription factors have been confirmed as key regulators of anthocyanin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis, suggesting potential consistency in broccoli as well. Compared to the Arabidopsis genome, Brassica crops have undergone a recent whole-genome triplication [2]. Comparative synteny analysis has revealed that 198 MYB genes in Arabidopsis are collinear with 325 genes in B. oleracea (Fig. 5B), indicating that there may be a greater number of MYB genes regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis in B. oleracea.

Identification, characterization, and phylogenetic analysis of R2R3-MYB genes in broccoli

To investigate which MYB genes are involved in the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis, 125 A. thaliana R2R3-MYB proteins and R2R3-MYB conserved domain sequences were used to verify all possible R2R3-MYB members in B. oleracea. The identified members of BoMYB genes were renamed as BoMYB1 to BoMYB236 (Table S5). Subsequently, the detailed physico-chemical properties and sequence information of the 236 MYB proteins were subsequently analyzed, with BoMYB108 and BoMYB197, identified as the smallest proteins at 182 amino acids (aa) and exhibiting the lowest molecular weight of 21.06 kDa. Conversely, BoMYB68 was found to be the largest R2R3-MYB protein in B. oleracea, comprising 1,365 aa and possessing the highest molecular weight of 152.83 kDa. The theoretical isoelectric points (pI) of these proteins ranged from 4.76 (BoMYB133) to 9.87 (BoMYB134), with 142 proteins exhibiting pI values less than 7, classifying them as acidic proteins. Conversely, 94 proteins had pI values greater than 7, categorizing them as basic proteins. The instability index analysis revealed that 94.9% of the proteins exhibited instability indices greater than 40, while only 12 proteins displayed indices below 40, indicating that the majority of R2R3-MYB proteins are predicted to be unstable which was consistent with previous reports [48]. The aliphatic index of BoMYB proteins ranges from 49.42 to 88.31, with a mean of 66.84 (Table S5). The grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) of R2R3-MYB proteins all exhibited negative indices, suggesting that they all belong to hydrophilic proteins.

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the proteins sequences of the 236 BoMYB proteins and 125 AtMYBs [34], all of which belong to R2R3-MYB family. The phylogenetic tree was divided into three main clades, namely Group I, Group II, Group III (Table S6). Most of the MYB transcription factors belong to Group 3, which includes 228 BoMYBs (Fig. 6).

Chromosome locations and gene duplication analysis

The location of BoMYB genes on the chromosomes were also investigated and visualized. A total of 236 BoMYB genes are distributed across nine chromosomes, with the highest number located on chromosome 4, totaling 45 genes, while chromosome 6 has the lowest number, containing only 21 genes. Based on the classification of the phylogenetic tree, a total of eight genes belonging to Group 1 and Group 2 were scattered across six chromosomes. In contrast, Group 3 genes are uniformly distributed across the chromosomes. Chromosome 4 has the lowest representation with only 18 genes, while chromosome 3 exhibits the highest representation with a total of 44 genes (Fig. 7A-B).

Gene duplication events are important for the expansion of gene families, primarily including tandem repeats and segmental repeats [48]. In this study, a total of 194 pairs of duplicated BoMYBs were identified using the MCScanX program, which all belonged to segmental repeats (Table S7). These findings suggest that some BoMYB genes may have arisen as a result of specific events during gene duplication, which may serve as key driving factors in the development and evolution of BoMYB genes.

The expression patterns and function analysis of DEGs to screen key MYB proteins

Based on transcriptome data, MYB genes that are differentially expressed between green and purple curd materials were screened. A total of 76 MYB genes (highlighted in red) were identified, distributed across nine chromosomes (Fig. 7A). To further screen for MYB genes involved in the regulation of anthocyanin synthesis, the expression patterns of all differentially expressed genes (15, 464 DEGs) among the five materials were classified based on the Elbow Method (Fig. S5). While the Elbow method identified k = 2 as the mathematically optimal clustering point, we found k = 6 provided superior resolution of distinct gene expression patterns, enabling identification of gene clusters exhibiting coordinated variation with phenotypic traits (Fig. 8A). The correlation analysis between all differential genes and anthocyanin phenotypes was performed and visualized using a Heatmap, in which deeper colors indicated higher correlations. The average absolute correlation coefficients within each cluster were computed, revealing that Cluster 1, Cluster 3, and Cluster 4 exhibited significantly higher correlations with the phenotypes compared to other clusters (Fig. 8B). Subsequently, KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted on the genes within these three clusters. Several pathways were found to be significantly enriched, with the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway being particularly prominent in Cluster 4 (Fig. 8C-E, Table S8). This finding suggests that the genes in cluster 4 may play an important role in the regulation of anthocyanin synthesis. Meanwhile, we found there were 13 MYB genes enriched in Cluster 4, indicating that these MYB genes may participate in the regulation of anthocyanin synthesis.

Identification of differentially expressed MYB genes related to anthocyanin accumulation

The expression changes of 13 MYB genes that enriched in Cluster 4 in different broccoli materials were analyzed. They almost expressed highly in purple broccoli (Fig. 9A). This appeared to show a positive correlation with the changes in anthocyanin content. Subsequently, the correlation between gene expression and anthocyanin content was calculated. It was found that six MYB genes were significantly correlated with the anthocyanin content, suggesting their potential involvement in regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis. These genes were named as follows: BoMYB29, BoMYB48, BoMYB53, BoMYB60, BoMYB147, BoMYB148, and BoMYB234 (Fig. S6). To further validate the ability of these genes to regulate anthocyanin synthesis, attempts were made to clone the genes from purple broccoli. However, only the full-length transcript sequences of BoMYB60, BoMYB147, BoMYB148, and BoMYB234 were successfully obtained. Subsequently, these genes were transiently expressed in tobacco leaves, but no significant pigment accumulation was observed compared to the negative control (Fig. S7). Concurrently, combinations of these genes with BoTT8 were also established to inject into tobacco (Fig. 9B), as previous studies had demonstrated that TT8 can synergistically regulate anthocyanin synthesis with MYB protein [36]. The results of the transient expression showed significant pigment accumulation in the red circles marked as 3 and 4 (Fig. 9B), indicating that MYB147 and MYB148 can jointly promote anthocyanin synthesis with BoTT8. The analysis of anthocyanin content in tobacco leaves, which were injected with the infection solution, further indicated that MYB147 and MYB148 can synergistically induce anthocyanin accumulation with TT8 (Fig. 9C). These results enrich the understanding of anthocyanin regulatory genes in broccoli.

Discussion

Anthocyanins are common secondary metabolites in nature, and their content determines the color of plant flowers. Currently, the broccoli commonly found in the consumer market is green, while purple broccoli, which is rich in anthocyanins, is still relatively rare. In this study, we conducted transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses using green and purple broccoli to identify the main types of anthocyanins in purple broccoli and to screen for transcription factors involved in its formation. This work lays a solid foundation for future molecular design breeding of purple broccoli.

Broccoli, cabbage (Brassica oleracea var. capitata), and cauliflower (Brassica oleracea var. botrytis) all belong to the Brassica genus of cruciferous vegetables. There is substantial research on the types of anthocyanins found in purple cabbage and purple cauliflower. Cyanidin-3-sophoroside-5-glucoside, cyanidin 3-glucoside, and cyanidin-3,5-diglucoside are typical components of red cabbage pigment [31]. Cyanidin 3-(coumaryl-caffeyl) glucoside-5- (malonyl) glucoside has been identified as the main anthocyanin in purple cauliflower [6]. However, studies on the anthocyanin types present in purple broccoli remain uncertain. Some reports have indicated that the primary anthocyanin accumulated in purple broccoli is cyanidin [43], while other researchers have found that delphinidin is the main anthocyanin present in broccoli [40]. Additionally, there are studies on the change of color in broccoli after cold treatment, but these studies have not clearly identified the types of anthocyanins accumulated [24, 44]. In this study, a total of 59 types of anthocyanin compounds were detected in five samples of broccoli. Among them, only 13 types were found in green broccoli, and their absolute content was negligible (Table S2). The main anthocyanin accumulated in purple broccoli is delphinidin, with delphinidin-3-O-glucoside-5-O-galactoside being the most abundant substance. This result reveals that the main types of anthocyanins accumulated in broccoli differ from those found in other cruciferous vegetables. This may be a result of different selective pressures experienced by cruciferous vegetables during their evolution. In addition, previous studies have found that the main anthocyanin in broccoli is cyanidin, which could be attributed to existing variations among broccoli resources. However, this issue warrants further investigation to elucidate the evolutionary relationships within the anthocyanin synthesis network in broccoli.

The biosynthesis of anthocyanins is primarily controlled by structural genes and regulatory genes. Structural genes encode functional proteins that directly determine anthocyanin synthesis, including early structural genes include PAL, C4H, 4CL, CHS, CHI, F3H, and F3’H; and late structural genes DFR, ANS, and UFGT. In ornamental kale (Brassica oleracea var. acephala), BoDFR1 is a key gene regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis [9]. In purple barley, the expression of HvnF3’H influences the formation of anthocyanins [5]. Additionally, the expression of the ANS gene in eggplant shows a positive correlation with anthocyanin content [32], the UFGT gene was highly expressed during the ripening process of black currant [37]. In the differentially expressed genes identified in this study, we detected the following: six PAL genes, four C4H genes, one 4CL gene, four CHS genes, one CHI gene, one F3H gene, one F3’H gene, one DFR gene, two ANS genes, and five UFGT genes (Fig. 4). However, by comparing the expression levels and absolute anthocyanin content among different materials, we found that only the expression of BoF3H1, BoF3’H, BoDFR, BoANS1, BoANS2, BoUFGT75C1, and BoUFGT79B1.1 corresponded to the phenotypic variations (Fig. 4 and S3). F3H and F3’H are key enzymes that control different branches of anthocyanin synthesis [12], while DFR, ANS, and UFGT are critical enzymes involved in the later stages of anthocyanin synthesis [30]. These results indicate that, although the types of anthocyanins primarily accumulated in broccoli are relatively unique, the synthesis regulatory network is still similar to that of other plants.

Regulatory genes indirectly control anthocyanin synthesis by influencing the expression of structural genes. In this study, the expression changes of structural genes in different broccoli varieties, such as BoF3′H, BoDFR, BoANS, also showed a significant positive correlation with anthocyanin content (Fig. 4 and S3). These results suggest the presence of upstream regulatory factors that influence the expression of these genes. MYB proteins are common regulatory factors in anthocyanin synthesis, PavMYB10.1 involves in anthocyanin biosynthesis and determines fruit skin color in sweet cherry [19], MdMYB1 MdMYB10, and MdMYB110a genes positively regulate anthocyanin synthesis in apple [17], AcMYB96 promotes anthocyanin accumulation in onion [23]. Our results demonstrated a significant association between the expression patterns of seven MYB genes and anthocyanin phenotypes. (Fig. S6). In Arabidopsis, MYB-bHLH-WD40 (MBW) complex is crucial for the regulation of anthocyanin accumulation, while the specificity and activity of the MBW complex towards target genes are mainly determined by the MYB and bHLH proteins, and the synergistic action of PAP1 (MYB) and TT8 (bHLH) can significantly enhance the accumulation of anthocyanins [42]. In this study, we found both MYB147 and MYB148 can regulate and promote anthocyanin synthesis together with BoTT8 through transient expression validation (Fig. 9B-C), indicating that the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway in broccoli exhibits a certain degree of conservation with that in Arabidopsis.

Previous report showed that Brassica crops have undergone a whole-genome triplication event during evolution [2]. However, compared to the at least four MYB genes that regulate anthocyanin synthesis found in Arabidopsis, only two MYB genes were identified to regulate anthocyanin synthesis in broccoli. This may be due to the selective retention and loss of different genes during polyploidization. For example, the FLC (FLOWERING LOCUS C) gene has three homologous genes in Chinese cabbage, while other genes related to flowering time control, such as GI, have retained only one copy [38]. Therefore, the MYB genes may have also experienced gene loss events after genome triplication. However, more genomic analyses will be needed in the future to verify this hypothesis. In the meantime, two MYB genes in broccoli have been identified that possess anthocyanin regulatory functions, thereby enriching the available genetic resources for future studies on anthocyanin regulation in broccoli.

Conclusion

Our study employed transcriptomics and metabolomics to compare the differences between green and purple broccoli, identifying delphinidin-3-O-glucoside-5-O-galactoside as the predominant type of anthocyanin in purple broccoli. We further elucidated the gene regulatory network involved in the formation of purple broccoli and screened two MYB transcription factors that regulate anthocyanin synthesis. This result provides a theoretical basis for the molecular design breeding of high-quality purple broccoli in the future. Additionally, this study offers insights for exploring the differences in anthocyanin types among different Brassica vegetables.
