Introduction

Abdominal tuberculosis (TB) has become an increasingly common site for extra-pulmonary TB. It can involve various areas, including the gastrointestinal tract, peritoneum, lymph nodes, or solid organs. Gastrointestinal TB constitutes a small percentage (1–3 %) of all TB cases globally. It can manifest either alongside pulmonary TB or independently [1]. Individuals with underlying immunocompromised conditions, whether iatrogenic or acquired, such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, are at the highest risk for developing abdominal TB [2,3]. They are also at increased risk of developing active TB due to the reactivation of latent infection. Additionally, HIV infection is a risk factor for the rapid progression of TB following initial infection [4,5].

Diagnosis of abdominal TB presents several challenges owing to the nonspecific characteristics of its symptoms, which often leads to delays in diagnosis and the onset of complications [6]. Abdominal TB has the ability to imitate various other abdominal conditions, making clinical suspicion critical for prompt diagnosis and improved prognosis. Effective recognition, combined with a multidisciplinary approach, is essential for the effective management and treatment of this condition. This case was written following the SCARE 2023 guideline [7].

Case presentation

A 33-year-old male presented to the emergency department with a five-day history of severe abdominal pain, initially in the epigastric region, then localizing to the right lower quadrant (RLQ). He described the pain as constant and non-radiating, with no nausea or vomiting, but noted loss of appetite and fever. His medical history was unremarkable, except for recent weight loss. Social history included regular alcohol and tobacco use. The patient had a history of intravenous drug use and identified as homosexual, both high-risk factors for HIV.

Examination revealed RLQ tenderness without rebound or guarding. Vital signs were stable except for a fever of 38.4 °C. Lab tests showed elevated inflammatory markers (ESR 70 mm/h, CRP 54 mg/L), leukocytosis (12,400/mm3 with 70 % neutrophils), anemia (Hb 10.5 g/dL), and thrombocytopenia (101,000/mm3). HIV was diagnosed during hospitalization.

Abdominal ultrasound indicated an abnormal appendix with significant bowel distention in the lower abdomen, suggesting acute appendicitis. Chest X-ray (Fig. 1) showed right upper lobe consolidation, without pleural effusion. Chest CT was done due to suspected concurrent pulmonary TB. Chest CT scan (Fig. 2) revealed consolidation and fine nodular infiltration with a tree-in-bud appearance in the right upper lobe, consistent with bronchiolitis. The abdominal X-ray showed no pathological findings (Fig. 3).

The patient initially presented with clinical signs and ultrasound findings suggestive of acute appendicitis. However, during surgery, a perforated cecal mass (Fig. 4) and diffuse lesions across the small intestine (Fig. 5) were discovered, leading to the diagnosis of peritonitis secondary to cecal perforation. Histopathological analysis subsequently revealed necrotizing granulomatous colitis, confirming gastrointestinal TB. In addition, HIV was diagnosed during hospitalization, providing an explanation for the atypical presentation and the patient's increased susceptibility to tuberculosis.

The final pathology confirmed necrotizing granulomatous colitis, with granulomatous lymphadenitis in the lymph nodes, corresponding to the perforated cecal lesion. Histopathological examination showed ulcerated colonic surfaces and multinucleated giant cells, pointing towards an infectious cause. PCR testing confirmed the presence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), solidifying the diagnosis of gastrointestinal TB, though cultures remained negative. Given the atypical presentation of abdominal tuberculosis, an HIV test was performed during hospitalization. The test was prompted by the suspicion that the abdominal TB might be associated with an underlying immunodeficiency, which can result in atypical manifestations of TB. The patient was diagnosed with gastrointestinal TB and initiated on a standard treatment regimen of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol, as recommended for TB management. Once stabilized on anti-tuberculous therapy, he also began antiretroviral therapy for HIV. By the time of discharge, the patient's general health had improved, and he was in stable clinical condition.

In the short-term, the patient recovered without major complications such as anastomotic leakage, although a small incisional hernia developed, which remains untreated. Long-term, the patient is stable and undergoing antiretroviral and anti-mycobacterial therapies.

Discussion

Abdominal TB is the sixth most common site for extrapulmonary TB, primarily affecting the gastrointestinal tract, peritoneum, and associated viscera. In clinical settings, these distinct forms may coexist [3,8]. Gastrointestinal TB constitutes a small percentage (1–3 %) of all TB cases globally and It can manifest either alongside pulmonary TB or independently [1]. Various segments of the intestine may be involved. The clinical presentations of gastrointestinal TB are diverse and can be quite challenging to diagnose due to their nonspecific and varied nature [[9], [10], [11]]. Symptoms often mimic other conditions, notably Crohn's disease, as well as esophageal and gastric cancers, colorectal cancer, and acute infections like appendicitis and colitis [1]. Intestinal TB may present acutely, as a chronic condition, or in an acute-on-chronic form, with symptoms such as abdominal pain, which might be relieved by defecation or vomiting, systemic symptoms like fever, night sweats, weight loss, and sometimes abdominal swelling due to fluid accumulation [12]. These protean manifestations and the diagnostic challenges underscore the importance of high clinical suspicion and comprehensive diagnostic approaches for patients with abdominal symptoms [[9], [10], [11]].

The case of the 33-year-old male described here highlights several critical aspects of gastrointestinal TB and its management. Although gastrointestinal TB seldom presents itself as an acute abdomen, our case initially presented as acute abdomen due to appendicitis. The patient reported a five-day history of abdominal pain that initially localized in the epigastric region before migrating to the right lower quadrant (RLQ). This shifting of pain is often associated with appendicitis; however, the patient's history of intermittent abdominal discomfort over the past month and the absence of nausea or vomiting suggested a more chronic process. The presence of fever and loss of appetite further supported an infectious or inflammatory etiology.

Laboratory findings revealed elevated inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP), leukocytosis with neutrophilia, and anemia with thrombocytopenia. These results typically lack specificity, often resulting in the identification of a chronic condition only. The abdominal ultrasound findings of an abnormal appendix suggested an acute appendicitis. The chest x-ray demonstrated consolidation in the right upper lobe, indicating past or present pulmonary TB. Abdominal TB can arise from the reactivation of a latent TB infection or through the ingestion of MTB. When active pulmonary TB or miliary TB is present, abdominal involvement may occur through hematogenous dissemination, direct spread from adjacent organs or via lymphatic channels [2].

During laparotomy, the identification of a severely inflamed ileocecal region with a large perforated mass and the presence of diffuse white seeding lesions and mesenteric lymphadenopathy, indicated a significant pathological process and shifted the differential diagnosis towards a more complex pathology including Crohn's disease. Perforation, which occurred in our patient, is a serious complication of abdominal TB and does not always present with the classic finding of free air under the diaphragm on radiography. The absence of this radiographic finding in some cases of perforation could be attributed to the fibrotic and adhesive changes caused by chronic inflammation in intestinal TB, which restrict the spread of luminal contents and intraperitoneal air [13]. The presence of necrotizing granulomatous colitis and granulomatous lymphadenitis on histopathological examination confirmed the suspicion of an infectious etiology, particularly TB. However, differentiating intestinal TB from Crohn's disease is notably challenging, and the classical diagnosis of abdominal TB requires microbiological and culture confirmation of MTB [14]. PCR testing for MTB was positive in our case, cementing the diagnosis.

Notably, the patient's HIV-positive status was discovered during the hospital stay. This finding is significant as immunosuppression due to HIV increases the risk of TB, including extrapulmonary forms such as abdominal TB [3]. The patient was unaware of their HIV status prior to this admission, which added complexity to the management plan. The patient underwent a right hemicolectomy due to the perforated cecal lesion and was subsequently started on a standard anti-tuberculous regimen, including isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. Additionally, the possibility of immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) should be considered when initiating antiretroviral therapy in patients with HIV and TB, as this can complicate the clinical course and requires careful management.

This case underscores the importance of considering abdominal TB in the differential diagnosis of chronic abdominal pain, especially in patients with newly discovered risk factors such as HIV. Effective recognition and appropriate surgical intervention, combined with anti-tuberculous therapy, are crucial for improving patient outcomes. Multidisciplinary care, including input from infectious disease specialists and gastroenterologists, is essential for the comprehensive management of such complex cases.

Conclusion

The presented case underscores the complexity and diagnostic challenges associated with abdominal TB, particularly when it mimics conditions like appendicitis and Crohn's disease. The patient's presentation with acute abdominal pain, coupled with nonspecific systemic symptoms, highlights the need for a high index of suspicion for TB, especially in regions with high TB prevalence or among immunocompromised individuals such as those with HIV. The diagnostic process is often complicated by the nonspecific nature of laboratory and imaging findings, necessitating comprehensive diagnostic approaches, including histopathological examination and PCR testing for MTB.

Effective recognition and appropriate management, combining surgical intervention with anti-tuberculous therapy, are crucial for improving outcomes. Additionally, the potential development of IRIS upon initiating antiretroviral therapy in HIV-positive patients must be carefully managed. This case emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary approach involving infectious disease specialists, gastroenterologists, and surgeons to effectively diagnose and treat abdominal TB. Ultimately, awareness and consideration of gastrointestinal TB in the differential diagnosis of chronic abdominal pain can lead to timely and effective treatment, significantly enhancing patient prognosis.
