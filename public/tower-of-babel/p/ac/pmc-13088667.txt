Ultrasound-assisted diagnosis and ultrasound-guided radiofrequency ablation of renal cell carcinoma in a patient with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease: a case report with two-year follow-up
Authors: Xuejiao Yu, Ruyu Liu, Zhe Sun, Liangkai Wang, Jiaojiao Ma, Shanshan Wang, Xinyi Liu, Bo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is characterized by progressive cystic enlargement and an increased risk of renal cell carcinoma. Early diagnosis and treatment are often hindered by severe distortion of renal architecture and by the limitations of contrast-enhanced computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging in patients with impaired renal function. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound offers a nephrotoxic-free alternative for lesion characterization, treatment planning, and post-ablation monitoring.Case presentationA 44-year-old woman was referred for evaluation of newly detected autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). Admission ultrasound showed markedly enlarged cystic kidneys and a 2.2-cm isoechoic lesion in the left kidney that could not be characterized due to the distorted anatomy. To avoid repeat contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), contrast-enhanced ultrasound demonstrated heterogeneous intralesional enhancement with relatively rapid washout, and positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) showed focal hypermetabolism. After cessation of dual antiplatelet therapy and correction of anemia, ultrasound-guided core biopsy confirmed renal cell carcinoma. The patient underwent percutaneous radiofrequency ablation, and post-procedural contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) showed no residual enhancement. Follow-up imaging at 6 and 24 months revealed no recurrence, and renal function remained stable.ConclusionsThis case highlights the role of CEUS in detecting and characterizing renal tumors in ADPKD, and its utility in post-ablation surveillance. Ultrasound-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is a safe, nephron-sparing treatment for localized RCC in ADPKD patients, especially when traditional contrast-enhanced imaging is limited by renal dysfunction.

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a hereditary disorder characterized by the progressive enlargement of bilateral renal cysts, often leading to chronic kidney disease and multiple complications such as hypertension, infection, and an increased risk of renal malignancy [1]. Notably, patients with ADPKD exhibit a higher incidence of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) compared with the general population [2, 3], and early detection is frequently hindered by markedly distorted renal anatomy. Conventional imaging modalities, including contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), may fail to accurately detect small solid lesions within polycystic kidneys, particularly in individuals with impaired renal function. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has emerged as a non-nephrotoxic imaging technique capable of dynamically visualizing microvascular perfusion, thereby facilitating differentiation between cystic lesions and solid tumors [4]. Beyond preoperative lesion characterization, CEUS provides real-time post-ablation assessment as well as reliable long-term surveillance.

Treatment options for RCC traditionally include surgical resection, such as partial nephrectomy or radical nephrectomy; however, these approaches may not be suitable for all patients, especially those with compromised renal function. In recent years, minimally invasive techniques have gained traction as effective alternatives for the management of localized RCC, particularly in high-risk patients, such as those with ADPKD [5]. Ablation techniques such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA), cryoablation, and microwave ablation are increasingly being used to treat small renal tumors, offering the advantage of nephron-sparing treatment. RFA delivers thermal energy to the tumor, inducing coagulative necrosis while preserving surrounding renal parenchyma. Cryoablation uses extreme cold to induce necrosis of tumor tissue. This technique allows for real-time assessment of tumor coverage and is particularly useful when precise tumor targeting is required.Microwave ablation, on the other hand, uses electromagnetic energy to generate heat and destroy the tumor tissue. While it is faster than RFA, it may have a higher risk of collateral damage due to its rapid heat distribution.Each ablation technique offers distinct advantages, and the choice of technique depends on factors such as tumor size, location, and the patient’s overall health.

However, evidence regarding the combined use of CEUS for diagnostic and follow-up purposes together with ultrasound-guided RFA for treatment in ADPKD remains limited. In this study, we report a case of a 44-year-old woman with ADPKD and RCC who underwent CEUS-assisted diagnosis, ultrasound-guided RFA, and two years of subsequent follow-up. This case provides valuable insights into the clinical feasibility, safety, and potential effectiveness of this integrated approach for managing renal cell carcinoma in ADPKD patients.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old woman presented to our hospital after being noted to have polycystic kidneys for approximately three weeks. Three weeks earlier, laboratory testing at an outside institution revealed abnormal renal function, and subsequent ultrasound and CT examinations demonstrated markedly enlarged, cystic kidneys. Based on these typical imaging features, she was clinically diagnosed with ADPKD. A contrast-enhanced CT scan reportedly suggested possible bilateral complex renal cysts, and the patient was referred to our hospital for further evaluation and management.

Her medical history included a two-year history of hypertension, with a maximum recorded blood pressure of 160/80 mmHg. She had been taking metoprolol succinate and combination of Reserpine and Hydralazine, with suboptimal blood pressure control. Approximately two weeks prior to admission, the patient experienced an acute ischemic cerebral infarction. Brain MRI demonstrated multiple ischemic lesions involving the bilateral corona radiata and the frontal–parietal cortical regions. Further evaluation with transcranial Doppler ultrasound combined with a Valsalva maneuver suggested the presence of a right-to-left shunt consistent with a patent foramen ovale. No evidence of intracranial aneurysm was identified during neurological evaluation. The patient was treated with dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel) and recovered without residual neurological deficits.

On physical examination, bilateral flank masses were palpable, without tenderness or rebound pain.Laboratory tests showed: hemoglobin 103 g/L (reference range: 115–150 g/L), hematocrit 31.90% (reference range: 35–45%), mean corpuscular hemoglobin 26.5 pg (reference range: 27–34 pg), platelet count 0.32 (reference range: 0.18–0.30). Urinary renal function tests revealed elevated urinary microalbumin at 94 mg/L (reference range: < 30 mg/L), urinary microalbumin / creatinine ratio 52.4 mg/g (reference range: < 30 mg/g), and urinary β2-microglobulin 862.22 µg/L (reference range: 0–200 µg/L), eGFR: 85.46 mL/min/1.73 m².

Imaging and diagnosis

Abdominal ultrasound was performed immediately upon admission. Renal dimensions were measured using panoramic (extended field-of-view) ultrasound imaging due to the markedly enlarged kidneys exceeding the field of view of a standard convex probe. Grayscale ultrasound demonstrated markedly distorted bilateral renal morphology, with the left kidney measuring 23.2 × 19.2 × 15.4 cm and the right kidney measuring 27.3 × 18.4 × 17.8 cm. Corticomedullary differentiation was lost, and numerous anechoic cysts of varying sizes were observed. Within this complex cystic background, an approximately 2.2 × 1.9 × 1.5 cm isoechoic area was identified in the upper–middle pole of the left kidney, with peripheral vascular signals detected on color Doppler (Fig. 1). Due to the markedly altered renal architecture and acoustic interference from adjacent cysts, conventional ultrasound could not reliably characterize the lesion. Although the patient reported having undergone a contrast-enhanced CT examination at an outside hospital 21 days prior to referral, which was interpreted as a possible complex renal cyst, the original imaging data were unavailable for review at our institution. Consequently, further differentiation among a complex cyst, focal inflammatory change (such as a renal abscess), and a small solid renal mass remained necessary. Repeat contrast-enhanced CT or MRI was recommended to the patient to further characterize the lesion. However, the patient expressed significant concern regarding potential deterioration of renal function associated with repeated contrast administration and therefore declined additional contrast-enhanced CT and MRI examinations.

In this clinical context, after detailed discussion of alternative imaging options and obtaining informed consent, CEUS — a non-nephrotoxic imaging modality—was selected as a problem-solving tool for further lesion characterization and biopsy planning. The CEUS was performed on the second day after admission. 1.0 mL of SonoVue was injected intravenously via the left cubital median vein, followed by 10 mL of saline for flush. During the arterial phase, microbubbles demonstrated a predominant peripheral-to-central enhancement, with a significantly enhanced edge, while a small, non-enhancing area was noted in the eccentric region. Venous phase wash-out occurred relatively rapidly (Fig. 2). This imaging pattern indicated that the lesion was mainly composed of solid tissue rather than cystic components, providing valuable information for further clinical decision-making.Although this rapid wash-in and wash-out pattern is more frequently observed in malignant renal tumors. Similar enhancement patterns have been reported in certain benign conditions, such as focal inflammatory or granulomatous lesions and early enhancing regions of renal abscess. Therefore, CEUS was primarily used to support suspicion of malignancy and to guide targeted biopsy.

Following consultation with the Nephrology Department, a positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) examination was performed as a complementary imaging modality in this complex clinical context. The PET-CT aimed not only to evaluate the lesion in the left kidney, but also to screen for other metabolically active lesions within the kidney and assess the possibility of metastasis. PET-CT demonstrated focal hypermetabolism in the left kidney(Fig. 3), which supported suspicion of malignancy, while no abnormal metabolic activity was detected elsewhere, thereby excluding metabolically active extrarenal lesions. Due to the patient’s recent ischemic stroke requiring dual antiplatelet therapy, together with concurrent renal anemia, percutaneous biopsy was not performed immediately.

After discontinuation of antiplatelet agents for one month and correction of anemia with iron supplementation—resulting in normalization of hemoglobin—the patient underwent an ultrasound-guided percutaneous core needle biopsy of the left renal mass in our department. The procedure was performed using an 18-gauge automated biopsy needle, and three core tissue specimens were obtained from different regions of the lesion. Immediately after the biopsy, thrombin was injected into the biopsy tract to promote hemostasis, and an intramuscular injection of thrombin was also administered to further minimize the risk of bleeding. Histopathological examination demonstrated tumor cells arranged in nests and alveolar structures with abundant clear cytoplasm and mild nuclear atypia, accompanied by focal inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4). Taken together, these histopathological and immunohistochemical findings provided definitive pathological confirmation of a renal cell carcinoma.

Treatment

The patient subsequently underwent ultrasound-guided percutaneous RFA under general anesthesia, which was selected as a definitive minimally invasive treatment strategy in light of the markedly enlarged polycystic kidneys and the increased complexity of conventional surgical management. The RFA procedure was performed using the Medtronic S-1500 RFA system (Shanghai, China), a commonly used system for percutaneous ablation of renal tumors. The RF electrode needle used in this procedure was the 10-141281 model. During the procedure, power settings of 40–50 W were applied during the main ablation phase, with a cumulative ablation time of 23 min, to achieve adequate thermal coverage of the target lesion (Fig. 5). This was followed by a brief higher-power ablation at 80 W for approximately 10 min, which was applied during a consolidation phase to ensure complete coagulative necrosis at the tumor margins. Upon electrode withdrawal, a low-power setting of 20 W was applied for track ablation to reduce the risk of post-procedural bleeding. After completing the ablation procedure, CEUS was immediately performed 3 min post-ablation to assess whether the tumor had been completely ablated, with no residual intralesional perfusion, indicating technical success (Fig. 6). The patient tolerated the procedure well, and no procedure-related complications were observed.

Follow-up

Recurrence was defined as the appearance of newly enhanced tissue within or near the previously treated area during follow-up imaging. Follow-up CEUS and conventional imaging at 6 months and 24 months demonstrated no evidence of residual or recurrent disease (Figs. 7 and 8). Tolvaptan (a Vasopressin V2-receptor antagonist) was prescribed to the patient upon admission at our hospital, at a dosage of 15 mg once daily, to manage ADPKD and reduce kidney cyst growth. During the two-year follow-up period, the patient’s renal function was monitored regularly. The eGFR showed stable function over time, with no significant deterioration observed. The eGFR measurements were as follows: baseline (4 days prior to the procedure): 70.68 mL/min/1.73 m²; day 1 post-ablation: 94.4 mL/min/1.73 m²; 3 months post-ablation: 81.29 mL/min/1.73 m²; 6 months post-ablation: 65.11 mL/min/1.73 m²; 1 year post-ablation: 83.18 mL/min/1.73 m²; 1.5 years post-ablation: 68.36 mL/min/1.73 m²; 2 years post-ablation: 63.89 mL/min/1.73 m². Although there were slight fluctuations in eGFR, the values remained within a clinically acceptable range, and the patient did not experience any symptoms suggestive of renal deterioration.

Discussion

Current research on RCC associated with ADPKD is primarily limited to case reports [6–8], and robust epidemiological data on its true incidence remain unavailable. Nevertheless, patients with ADPKD are known to have an increased risk of RCC compared with the general population, potentially attributable to chronic inflammation, cystic epithelial hyperplasia, and abnormalities in the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) signaling pathway. Because kidneys affected by ADPKD exhibit marked distortion of morphology and architecture—and because tumors are often small (< 3 cm)—solid lesions may be obscured by adjacent cysts or misinterpreted as complex cysts. Consequently, early detection and differentiation between benign and malignant lesions are particularly challenging. Conventional imaging modalities often fail to delineate the true boundaries of such lesions, and the clinical presentation of RCC in ADPKD is nonspecific, increasing the likelihood of missed diagnoses. Although CT is the standard modality for evaluating renal masses, contrast-enhanced CT is often contraindicated in ADPKD patients with impaired renal function who have not yet initiated dialysis [9].

CEUS provides real-time, dynamic visualization of microvascular perfusion and can clearly demonstrate tumor vascularity even in the presence of complex cystic backgrounds. This makes CEUS particularly advantageous for distinguishing small solid tumors from adjacent cystic structures. In the present case, CEUS revealed rapid, heterogeneous arterial-phase hyperenhancement followed by early venous washout—features highly suggestive of malignancy. This enhancement pattern is consistent with the hypervascularity and aberrant vascular architecture typically observed in RCC unrelated to polycystic kidneys [10, 11]. In a meta-analysis by Furrer et al., CEUS demonstrated diagnostic performance for renal masses that was at least comparable to contrast-enhanced CT and MRI [12].

Importantly, CEUS played a pivotal role in this case not only in preoperative diagnosis but also in immediate postoperative assessment and long-term surveillance.Immediately after ablation, CEUS enabled real-time evaluation of microbubble perfusion within the ablation zone; a “non-perfused area completely covering the lesion” confirmed complete ablation, while any residual enhancement guided supplementary ablation. During follow-up, CEUS facilitated early identification of recurrence or local tumor progression by detecting re-perfusion or neovascularization within the treated region [13]. Through the integrated use of two-dimensional ultrasound, CEUS, and PET-CT, this case was accurately diagnosed and successfully treated, underscoring the unique value of CEUS in managing complex renal pathology and its practical utility in guiding minimally invasive therapy.

For renal tumors < 3 cm in diameter, the European Association of Urology (EAU) recommends percutaneous thermal ablation as one of the curative treatment options—particularly for elderly patients, those with comorbidities, or individuals deemed unfit for surgery [14]. This approach maximizes nephron preservation and reduces intraoperative bleeding and postoperative complications. Several studies have demonstrated that percutaneous RFA provides oncologic control and safety outcomes comparable to partial nephrectomy and may serve as a viable alternative for elderly or high-risk patients [15–17]. RFA was preferred over cryoablation and microwave ablation due to its precise control over the ablation zone, which is crucial in ADPKD patients with distorted renal anatomy. While cryoablation allows real-time tumor coverage assessment, it is less effective for small or deeply located tumors. MWA, although faster, carries a higher risk of thermal injury to surrounding tissues. The literature supports that RFA offers better nephron-sparing results and greater control, especially in complex anatomical settings [18, 19].

Currently, there is no consensus regarding optimal management strategies for RCC in patients with ADPKD. In the present case, the tumor was surrounded by multiple cystic components, which provided partial thermal insulation and helped preserve normal renal parenchyma during ablation—an observation consistent with findings reported by Linfeng Xu et al. [5]. Although RFA is a minimally invasive, nephron-sparing modality with a favorable safety profile, it introduces unique technical challenges. Previous studies have shown that surrounding cysts may alter needle trajectories and cause path deviation, while the uneven thermal distribution within cystic structures may result in incomplete or excessive ablation, potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy [5, 20]. Therefore, meticulous pre-procedural planning and real-time ultrasound monitoring are essential to ensure procedural precision and safety.

Ultrasound guidance played a critical role in minimizing the risk of hemorrhagic complications during the biopsy and ablation procedures. Given the distorted renal anatomy typical of ADPKD, there is an increased risk of bleeding during biopsy due to the potential involvement of vascular abnormalities and renal cysts. By using real-time ultrasound guidance, we were able to precisely target the lesion and avoid trauma to adjacent renal tissue, thereby reducing the risk of bleeding. Additionally, the use of coaxial biopsy technique allowed for multiple tissue samples to be obtained through a single puncture, further minimizing the risk of bleeding and collateral injury to normal kidney parenchyma. Furthermore, the coaxial biopsy technique, which was used in this case, provided an additional layer of safety by allowing multiple biopsies through a single puncture site, thus further minimizing the risk of bleeding and injury to the kidney parenchyma [21, 22].

In this case, RFA was performed under real-time two-dimensional ultrasound guidance. Moderate-to-high power settings (40–50 W) were applied during the main overlapping ablation phase, followed by a brief higher-power application (80 W) during a consolidation phase, to achieve complete tumor necrosis while preserving the surrounding renal parenchyma. Track ablation at low power (20 W) was subsequently performed to minimize bleeding risk. Immediate CEUS confirmed complete coverage of the target lesion within the ablation zone with no residual enhancement. Over a two-year follow-up period, no local recurrence or distant metastasis was observed, supporting the safety and effectiveness of this therapeutic approach in selected ADPKD patients.

Our findings suggest that ultrasound-guided RFA may be a promising nephron-sparing treatment for renal cell carcinoma (RCC) in patients with ADPKD. The presence of pericystic structures (cystic structures adjacent to the lesion) may provide some degree of thermal protection to the surrounding renal parenchyma. However, these structures also add procedural complexity, requiring careful pre-procedural planning and real-time intraoperative monitoring to ensure optimal ablation outcomes. This case highlights the potential utility of CEUS and RFA as part of the management strategy for renal malignancy in high-risk patients with polycystic kidneys.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this report describes a single case with a relatively limited follow-up duration, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Larger prospective studies with longer follow-up periods and multi-center data are necessary to validate the long-term oncologic and renal functional outcomes of RFA in patients with ADPKD. Second, although CEUS provided valuable information for diagnosis, intraoperative evaluation, and follow-up, it remains operator-dependent and may not fully delineate tumor margins in deeply located or complex cystic lesions. Finally, given the markedly distorted renal anatomy in ADPKD, there remains an inherent risk of incomplete ablation or collateral injury, despite careful procedural planning. Future research integrating multimodal imaging and advanced navigation technologies may help overcome these challenges and further enhance procedural precision.

Conclusions

Ultrasound-guided percutaneous RFA is a promising nephron-sparing treatment option for small renal cell carcinoma (RCC) in patients with ADPKD. CEUS plays a crucial role in preoperative diagnosis, biopsy guidance, immediate post-ablation evaluation, and long-term surveillance, enabling accurate assessment of ablation completeness and early detection of recurrence. The presence of pericystic structures may provide partial thermal protection to the renal parenchyma, but careful procedural planning and real-time monitoring are essential to ensure efficacy and safety. This case demonstrates the value of combining CEUS with minimally invasive therapy in the management of complex renal anatomy and suggests a potential treatment approach for high-risk ADPKD patients with localized RCC. However, further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are necessary to validate these findings and refine treatment strategies.
