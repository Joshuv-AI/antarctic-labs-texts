Endocrine disorders in pediatric medulloblastoma survivors treated with X-ray or proton beam therapy: a single-institution retrospective study
Authors: Naoya Kaneko, Shuntaro Morikawa, Megumi Endo, Nozomi Hishimura, Liu Zhitong, Takeshi Yamaguchi, Hisato Segoe, Jutaro Abe, Kazumi Oura, Saori Sawai, Minako Sugiyama, Yukayo Terashita, Shinsuke Hirabayashi, Yuko Cho, Akie Nakamura, Yukitomo Ishi, Seishin Takao, Yuto Matsuo, Shigeru Yamaguchi, Takayuki Hashimoto, Atsushi Manabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Medulloblastoma, a common pediatric central nervous system disease, is managed with surgery, chemotherapy, and craniospinal irradiation (CSI) delivered with either X-rays or protons. Proton-beam CSI, introduced to protect the surrounding tissues, may reduce endocrine toxicity. In this single-institution retrospective observational study, we evaluated adverse endocrine events in 14 patients diagnosed with medulloblastoma before 13 years of age: 9 and 5 received X-ray and proton beam CSI, respectively. In the X-ray therapy group, the median age at diagnosis was 4.2 (range: 1.4–12.5) years, the median post-radiation follow-up was 19.5 (9.7–34.7) years, and the median CSI dose was 25.2 (23.4–36.0) Gy. The proton beam therapy group showed corresponding medians of 9.4 (5.3–11.7) years, 6.9 (5.4–8.1) years, and 23.4 (23.4–36.0) Gy. Five years after radiation therapy completion, the incidence of growth hormone deficiency was 44% (4/9) and 60% (3/5) in the X-ray and proton beam therapy groups, respectively. However, primary hypothyroidism and central hypothyroidism were exclusively observed in the X-ray therapy group, in 33% (3/9) and 11% (1/9) of patients. Given the short follow-up period for the proton beam therapy group, the difference in the occurrence rates of gonadal dysfunction and adrenal insufficiency is currently unknown. Our findings suggest that, at ≤5 years after the completion of radiation therapy, radiation-induced hypothyroidism was observed only in patients treated with X-ray therapy and not in those treated with proton beam therapy. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are required to clarify this possible advantage and to refine radiation therapy protocols.

Introduction

In Japan, approximately 2,000–2,500 pediatric cancer cases are diagnosed annually [1], and improvements in treatment outcomes have led to a growing population of childhood cancer survivors [2]. As survival rates increase, late adverse events, particularly endocrine disorders, have become an important clinical issue [3].

Medulloblastoma constitutes approximately 10% of pediatric central nervous system tumors, with approximately 100 new cases diagnosed annually in Japan [4]. Standard treatment includes surgical resection and chemotherapy, often combined with craniospinal irradiation (CSI), as medulloblastoma has a strong tendency to disseminate via the cerebrospinal fluid [5, 6]. Medulloblastomas predominantly arise in the fourth ventricle; hence, endocrine disorders are uncommon at diagnosis [7]. However, CSI inevitably exposes endocrine organs, including the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, thyroid, and ovaries, to radiation. This leads to a high incidence of post-treatment endocrine adverse events [7-9].

To mitigate the radiation-related adverse events associated with X-ray therapy, proton beam therapy has been increasingly used to treat medulloblastoma [10]. Compared with X-ray therapy, proton beam therapy enables a more precise delivery of radiation to the tumor while minimizing exposure of surrounding normal tissues to radiation; therefore, it reduces the incidence of late adverse events [11, 12]. Nevertheless, the longitudinal clinical course of endocrine complications in pediatric patients with medulloblastoma has not been sufficiently documented, particularly in Japanese patients.

This single-institution retrospective observational study aimed to assess the incidence of adverse endocrine events in Japanese pediatric patients with medulloblastoma who were treated with X-ray or proton beam therapy.

Materials and Methods

Patients

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Hokkaido University Hospital, one of the 15 core hospitals treating childhood cancer in Japan. The participants comprised pediatric patients with medulloblastoma who were aged <13 years at the time of treatment initiation and were consecutively treated between April 1990 and March 2021. Patients with endocrine disorders, those who died during treatment or within 5 years after treatment completion, and those who did not undergo endocrine evaluation tests (including physical measurements and blood tests) or regular follow-up within 5 years after treatment completion were excluded. Diagnosis of medulloblastoma was confirmed by histopathological examination. Although the recommended protocols evolved over the study period, patients were managed according to the standard treatment strategies recommended at the time of their treatment [13-15]. Management approaches for medulloblastomas include surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiation therapy (craniospinal and localized irradiation), and autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Chemotherapeutic agents, including cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, melphalan, busulfan, and thiotepa, are classified as alkylating agents. These agents are known to increase the risk of gonadal dysfunction [16-19]. In this study, the cumulative dose of alkylating agents was calculated as the cyclophosphamide-equivalent dose, following the method reported in the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study [20]. Although supportive therapies in cancer treatment have evolved over time, these changes were not considered to have affected the incidence of endocrine-related adverse events during either period.

Endocrinological evaluation

Patients underwent physical examination and endocrine evaluations, including thyroid palpation and blood tests. These examinations were performed by pediatric oncologists or pediatric endocrinologists, and patients were followed up at regular intervals of 3–6 months for up to 5 years after treatment completion. Thyroid ultrasonography was performed as part of the post CSI follow-up protocol in patients who had completed radiation therapy more than 5 years earlier, at intervals of 2–3 years since April 2021 [21]. Data on clinical tests, including height and weight measurements, as well as GH stimulation test results, were retrospectively extracted from medical records at each visit. Pediatric GH deficiency (GHD) was diagnosed when the peak GH level was <6 ng/mL during insulin or arginine stimulation tests or <16 ng/mL during the GH-releasing peptide-2 (GHRP-2) stimulation test. Severe adult GHD was diagnosed if the peak GH level was <1.8 ng/mL during insulin, arginine, or glucagon stimulation tests or <9 ng/mL during the GHRP-2 stimulation test [22]. Hypothyroidism was diagnosed at the time of initiation of levothyroxine sodium replacement therapy. Hypothyroidism was classified based on serum TSH and free T4 levels as follows: overt hypothyroidism, TSH ≥5 μIU/mL with free T4 <0.75 ng/dL; subclinical hypothyroidism, TSH ≥5 μIU/mL with free T4 0.75–1.50 ng/dL; and central hypothyroidism, TSH <5 μIU/mL with free T4 <0.75 ng/dL. Overt and subclinical hypothyroidism were collectively defined as primary hypothyroidism. In some patients, a TRH stimulation test was performed to further evaluate TSH secretory capacity. A blunted TSH response was defined as a peak TSH level of <10 μIU/mL, and a delayed TSH response was defined as the TSH level at 120 min that remained ≥50% of the peak value. A blunted response was considered suggestive of pituitary hypothyroidism, whereas a delayed response was considered suggestive of hypothalamic hypothyroidism.

Pubertal development was evaluated using Tanner’s method, and serum levels of LH, FSH, and estradiol or testosterone were measured. Puberty onset was defined as testicular volume >4 mL in boys and the initiation of breast development (Tanner stage 2) in girls. Primary hypogonadism was defined as elevated LH and FSH levels above the age- and Tanner stage–specific reference ranges in the presence of low sex hormone levels. Central hypogonadism was defined as normal or low LH and FSH levels with low sex hormone levels. In some patients, a GnRH stimulation test was performed. An exaggerated LH and FSH response was indicative of primary hypogonadism, a low or absent response was suggestive of pituitary hypogonadism, and a delayed response was indicative of hypothalamic hypogonadism. Treatment was considered for boys without an increase in testicular volume by 13 years of age and for girls without breast development by 12 years of age. Precocious puberty was suspected if testicular volume was >4 mL in boys by 9 years of age or if breast development was observed in girls by 7.5 years of age, and treatment was considered accordingly. Adrenal insufficiency was diagnosed when the 8:00 a.m. cortisol level was ≤4.0 μg/dL, confirmed through a CRH or GHRP-2 stimulation test. In the CRH or GHRP-2 stimulation test, low response was defined by a peak cortisol level <15 μg/dL [23]. The adverse endocrine events during the entire follow-up period were summarized in both groups, and the frequency of endocrine disorders at 5 years after completion of radiation therapy, a time point at which endocrine disorders are known to increase [24-26], was calculated. Given the very small sample size, formal hypothesis testing was not performed, as statistical comparisons between groups could lead to overinterpretation of the results. Instead, outcomes were summarized descriptively, and exact 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method are presented in the text and tables. The incidence of endocrine disorders, timing of diagnosis, and detailed clinical course over the entire follow-up period were also evaluated.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hokkaido University Hospital (approval no. 024-0133; approval date 2024/7/10). We did not use patient tissue samples in this study, and some patients had completed their medical visits. Therefore, we adopted an opt-out consent approach. Detailed information regarding this study was announced on the Hokkaido University Hospital website, offering patients or their guardians the opportunity to opt out.

Results

Patient background

The flowchart for the inclusion of participants and a summary of patient characteristics are provided in Fig. 1 and Table 1, respectively. From April 1990 to March 2016, all patients were treated with X-ray therapy, whereas from April 2016 to March 2021, all patients underwent proton beam therapy. Fourteen patients were enrolled in the study, with nine and five in the X-ray and proton beam therapy groups, respectively (Fig. 1). The X-ray therapy group consisted of five boys and four girls, while the proton beam therapy group consisted of four boys and one girl (Table 1). The median age at diagnosis was 4.2 (range: 1.4–12.5) years in the X-ray therapy group and 9.4 (5.3–11.7) years in the proton beam therapy group, indicating that patients in the proton beam therapy group were generally older at the time of diagnosis. The median follow-up duration after completion of radiation therapy was 19.5 (9.7–34.7) and 6.9 (5.4–8.1) years for the X-ray therapy and proton beam therapy groups, respectively, reflecting a shorter follow-up period for the proton beam therapy group. The proton dose was expressed in Gy (relative biological effectiveness, RBE) to account for the increased biological effectiveness of protons compared to X-rays. Thus, the median CSI dose was 25.2 (23.4–36.0) Gy and 23.4 (23.4–36.0) Gy (RBE) in the X-ray therapy and proton beam therapy groups, respectively. These were followed by local irradiation to a total dose of 50.0–55.8 Gy in each group. Chemotherapy was administered in 13 patients, excluding Patient 1 in the X-ray therapy group. Two patients (Patients 3 and 5) in the X-ray therapy group underwent an additional high-dose chemotherapy combined with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Other chemotherapeutic agents used in treatment included cisplatin, carboplatin, vincristine, and etoposide. The median cyclophosphamide-equivalent doses (CED) were 12.0 g/m2 (range: 0–35.3 g/m2) in the X-ray therapy group and 16.0 g/m2 (range: 12.0–16.0 g/m2) in the proton beam therapy group. Detailed chemotherapy information was not available for Patients 2 and 3.

Between April 1990 and March 2021, 25 patients with medulloblastoma were treated at our institution and followed up. Of these, five were excluded because they were aged ≥13 years at disease onset. Additional exclusions included two patients who died before completion of treatment, three patients who did not undergo endocrine evaluation within 5 years after treatment completion, and one patient who did not receive craniospinal irradiation (CSI). Consequently, 14 patients were enrolled in the final analysis: nine patients in the X-ray therapy group and five patients in the proton beam therapy group.

Frequency of endocrine adverse events 5 years after the completion of radiation therapy

At 5 years after radiation therapy completion, the incidence rates (95% CI) for endocrine disorders in the X-ray therapy group (n = 9) were 33.3% (3/9 patients; 95% CI, 7.5–70.1%) for primary hypothyroidism, 11.1% (1/9 patients; 95% CI, 0.3–48.2%) for central hypothyroidism, and 44.4% (4/9 patients; 95% CI, 13.7–78.8%) for pediatric GHD (Table 2). Based on serum TSH and free T4 levels, the three patients with primary hypothyroidism (Patients 4, 5, and 9) were classified as having subclinical hypothyroidism (Supplementary Table 1). Patient 8 was diagnosed with central hypothyroidism based on serum TSH and free T4 levels, together with a delayed TSH response on the TRH stimulation test, performed 4.7 years after treatment completion (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, in the proton beam therapy group (n = 5), no cases of primary or central hypothyroidism were observed at the 5-year time point (0%, 0/5 patients each; 95% CI, 0–52.2%) (Table 2). Pediatric GHD was observed in 60.0% of patients (3/5 patients; 95% CI, 14.7–94.7%).

Thyroid function

Throughout the entire follow-up period, primary hypothyroidism was observed in 56% of patients (5/9 patients; Patients 1, 4, 5, 6, and 9) in the X-ray therapy group (Fig. 2, Table 3). TSH and free T4 levels at the diagnosis of hypothyroidism, as well as the timing of diagnosis, are provided in Supplementary Table 1. In addition to Patients 4, 5, and 9, who were diagnosed with subclinical hypothyroidism at 5 years after treatment completion, Patients 1 and 6 were also diagnosed with subclinical hypothyroidism at 7.6 and 8.1 years after treatment completion, respectively (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1). The median time to onset was 4.5 years after treatment completion (range: 1.6–8.1 years). The TRH stimulation test was performed in 4 of 9 patients in the X-ray therapy group and in 4 of 5 patients in the proton beam therapy group, at a median of 4.6 years after treatment completion (range: 2.0–5.3 years) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Patient 1 in the X-ray therapy group showed a normal TSH response on the TRH stimulation test performed 2.5 years after treatment completion; however, the patient was subsequently diagnosed with subclinical hypothyroidism 7.6 years after treatment completion. Two patients in the X-ray therapy group (Patients 2, 3) and four patients in the proton beam therapy group (Patients 10, 11, 13, 14) showed no blunted or delayed TSH responses on the TRH stimulation test and were diagnosed as having normal thyroid function, with no subsequent thyroid dysfunction observed up to the most recent follow-up (Supplementary Fig. 1). The TRH stimulation test was not performed in Patient 7 in the X-ray therapy group; however, thyroid function was considered normal based on baseline serum TSH and free T4 levels.

In Patient 6, who received X-ray therapy, the total CSI dose was 36.0 Gy over 20 fractions. The thyroid was exposed to approximately 1.26 Gy per fraction, yielding an estimated total of approximately 25.2 Gy and corresponding to approximately 70% of the prescribed dose. In contrast, in Patient 11, who received proton beam therapy, the thyroid was exposed to only minimal radiation, with a maximum dose of 0.65 Gy (RBE) and a mean dose of 0.05 Gy (RBE) per fraction.

Thyroid nodules were identified in two of the nine patients in the X-ray therapy group (Patients 1 and 2). Patient 1 was diagnosed with medulloblastoma at the age of 1.4 years. Thirty-one years following radiation therapy completion, thyroid ultrasound revealed a suspected papillary thyroid carcinoma, and the patient has since been undergoing regular follow-up in the otorhinolaryngology clinic. At the time of tumor detection, the serum TSH, free T4, and thyroglobulin levels, measured during levothyroxine therapy, were 2.16 μIU/mL, 0.98 ng/dL, and 31.5 ng/mL, respectively. Patient 2 was diagnosed with medulloblastoma at the age of 11.1 years. Seventeen years after completing radiation therapy, thyroid ultrasound confirmed papillary thyroid carcinoma, and the patient underwent thyroidectomy. At the time of tumor detection, the serum levels of TSH, free T4, and thyroglobulin were 3.29 μIU/mL, 0.91 ng/dL, and 17.2 ng/mL, respectively. No cases of hyperthyroidism were observed in either group. Anti-thyroglobulin antibodies and anti–thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies were assessed in 7 of 9 patients in the X-ray therapy group and in 2 of 5 patients in the proton beam therapy group. In the X-ray therapy group, anti-thyroglobulin antibodies were negative in 6 of 7 patients and positive in only one patient (Patient 2); the patient had normal thyroid function and was diagnosed with papillary thyroid carcinoma. Anti-TPO antibodies were negative in all 7 patients. In the proton beam therapy group, both anti-thyroglobulin and anti-TPO antibodies were negative in the two patients evaluated.

GH function

During the entire follow-up period, pediatric GHD was observed in 56% of patients (5/9 patients) in the X-ray therapy group and in 60% of patients (3/5 patients) in the proton beam therapy group (Table 3). The median time to diagnosis was 4.6 years (range: 2.5–6.0 years) in the X-ray therapy group and 3.9 years (range: 2.4–4.4 years) in the proton beam therapy group (Table 3). The primary symptoms used for diagnosing pediatric GHD were a decline in the growth rate and low serum insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels. At the time of diagnosis, IGF-1 SD scoring was –1.15 SD (range: –4.97 to –0.78 SD) in the X-ray therapy group and –2.79 SD (range: –4.33 to –2.67 SD) in the proton beam therapy group. The age at diagnosis was 8.7 years (range: 4.0–15.2 years) in the X-ray therapy group and 12.0 years (range: 9.4–12.6 years) in the proton beam therapy group. In addition, the prevalence of severe adult GHD was 33% (3/9 patients) in the X-ray therapy group, with a median time to diagnosis of 16.2 years (range: 8.7–24.1 years) (Table 3). Tumor recurrence was not reported in any patient.

Gonadal function

Over the entire follow-up period, primary hypogonadism manifested in 22% of patients (2/9 patients) and central hypogonadism in 11% of patients (1/9 patients) in the X-ray therapy group (Table 3). All the patients were female. No cases of hypogonadism were observed in the proton beam therapy group. Patients 3 and 5 in the X-ray group showed elevated LH and FSH levels and were diagnosed with primary hypogonadism. Both patients had undergone high-dose chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and Patient 5 received higher cumulative doses of alkylating agents than Patient 3 (Table 1). Patient 8 in the X-ray therapy group, who received 36 Gy of CSI, exhibited delayed breast development after 12 years of age, along with low serum LH, FSH, and estradiol levels. The GnRH stimulation test demonstrated delayed LH and FSH responses, leading to a diagnosis of hypothalamic hypogonadism. Additionally, Patient 9, a male who received high-dose X-ray CSI (36.0 Gy) at 3 years of age, developed central precocious puberty (CPP) accompanied by GHD and subclinical hypothyroidism. In male patients aged ≥13 years, testicular volume was more than 10 mL in both the X-ray and proton beam therapy groups. However, no examinations were conducted to assess the presence or absence of spermatogenesis.

Adrenal function

In the X-ray therapy group, Patient 8 was diagnosed with central adrenal insufficiency 8.7 years after therapy and was started on hydrocortisone supplementation. This patient exhibited low morning serum cortisol levels, reduced cortisol response, and excessive and prolonged ACTH response in the CRH and GHRP-2 stimulation tests.

Discussion

In Japan, Mizumoto et al. reported late complications after proton beam therapy in childhood cancer survivors aged 0–19 years, and Uemura et al. described acute toxicities following proton beam CSI in pediatric patients with brain tumors [27, 28]. In the present study, we focused specifically on pediatric patients with medulloblastoma treated with either X-ray or proton beam therapy in Japan and descriptively reported the occurrence of endocrine disorders following each treatment modality. Endocrine disorders were diagnosed using a uniform endocrine evaluation protocol conducted at a single institution, enabling consistent and detailed assessment of post-treatment endocrine status in this population. As a result, at 5 years after completion of radiation therapy, hypothyroidism was observed only in the X-ray therapy group, whereas no cases of either primary or central hypothyroidism were identified in the proton beam therapy group. In contrast, pediatric GHD was observed in both treatment groups. These results show a similar trend to those reported in previous studies on hypothyroidism and GHD [24, 25, 29].

Primary hypothyroidism is a well-documented early endocrine adverse event in patients treated with CSI, typically manifesting around 3.8 years post-radiation therapy [26]. Here, thyroid dysfunction was exclusively observed in the X-ray therapy group throughout the entire follow-up period (Fig. 2, Table 3). Radiation doses of ≥10–20 Gy to the thyroid gland are considered risk factors for hypothyroidism [30]. Consistent with this, dose distribution maps at the thyroid level in this study (Fig. 3) indicated that patients in the X-ray therapy group were exposed to approximately 70% of the prescribed CSI dose (23.4–36.0 Gy), which may have contributed to the development of primary hypothyroidism. In contrast, reduced thyroid exposure during proton beam therapy may explain the lower incidence of hypothyroidism observed in that group [25]. In patients who receive cranial irradiation, mild impairment of TSH secretory capacity may occur, potentially leading to misclassification of central hypothyroidism as normal thyroid function or subclinical hypothyroidism when diagnosis is based solely on serum TSH and free T4 levels. To address this concern, we confirmed the absence of blunted or delayed TSH responses on the TRH stimulation test in patients classified as having normal thyroid function (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, in four patients, the TRH stimulation test was not performed or was not available prior to the diagnosis of hypothyroidism and initiation of treatment. In these patients, subclinical hypothyroidism could not be distinguished from central hypothyroidism.

Although proton beam therapy is expected to reduce radiation-related endocrine adverse events, some patients in the proton beam therapy group still developed GHD. Exposure of the hypothalamic–pituitary region to radiation >10–20 Gy presents a risk for GHD development [31, 32]. In this study, the CSI doses in both groups ranged from 23.4 to 36 Gy, exceeding this threshold, suggesting that both groups remained at high risk for GHD. These findings indicate that proton beam therapy may not fully prevent GHD and highlight the need for further protocol refinement to balance endocrine risk against therapeutic efficacy.

While endocrine adverse events were compared only 5 years after radiation therapy, additional late-onset events were observed during long-term follow-up in the X-ray therapy group. These included thyroid neoplasms, primary and central gonadal dysfunction, CPP, and central adrenal insufficiency.

In the present study, two patients (Patients 1 and 2) developed suspected or confirmed papillary thyroid carcinoma after receiving 25 and 24 Gy of X-ray-based CSI, respectively (Fig. 2, Table 1). Established risk factors for radiation-induced thyroid neoplasms include thyroid radiation doses below 20 Gy, exposure before the age of 5 years, combination therapy with alkylating agents, and genetic predisposition [21, 33-35], some of which were present in both cases. In both patients, thyroid tumors were detected at the time of the first thyroid ultrasonography, suggesting that tumor development may have occurred earlier than the time of detection. Therefore, regular follow-up with thyroid ultrasonography should be considered in patients who have undergone CSI.

Primary hypogonadism was observed in female patients (Patients 3 and 5) in the X-ray therapy group (Fig. 2). Ovarian follicles are highly sensitive to radiation; notably, even low-dose exposure (5–10 Gy) may cause ovarian insufficiency [36, 37]. Furthermore, the risk of ovarian insufficiency increases when exposure to alkylating agents is combined with ovarian radiation [36]. In contrast, the testes are located outside the CSI field and are, therefore, less likely to be affected by radiation-induced damage, compared with the ovaries. Additionally, Leydig cells, the testosterone-producing cells in the testes, exhibit relative resistance to radiation, with functional impairment typically occurring only at doses >12 Gy [38]. These differences in gonadal sensitivity to radiation, combined with the anatomical differences in location, may explain the observed sex differences in the incidence of gonadal dysfunction. However, both Patient 3 (with an unknown CED) and Patient 5 underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and high-dose chemotherapy, suggesting that gonadotoxicity related to alkylating agents may have substantially contributed to ovarian dysfunction. Therefore, in these patients, it was difficult to isolate the effects of radiation alone [39].

In the X-ray therapy group, Patient 8, who had central hypogonadism, and Patient 9, who had CPP, received high-dose CSI of 36 Gy (Fig. 2, Table 1). Patient 8 was diagnosed with medulloblastoma at 6.3 years of age and subsequently developed central hypogonadism accompanied by GHD and central hypothyroidism, findings that may be consistent with radiation-related hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction. Previous studies have reported that high-dose cranial irradiation ≥30–40 Gy increases the risk of gonadotropin deficiency [7]. Patient 9 was diagnosed at a young age of 3 years. Radiation exposure of the hypothalamus and pituitary gland at a young age, particularly at doses >18 Gy, has been identified as a risk factor for the development of CPP [39], which is consistent with this patient’s clinical course.

Patient 8 developed adrenal insufficiency 8.7 years after treatment. Although an ACTH stimulation test was not performed and the radiation dose delivered to the adrenal gland was unknown, excessive and prolonged ACTH responses observed during CRH and GHRP-2 stimulation tests suggested hypothalamic adrenal insufficiency. Despite the absence of overt symptoms, hydrocortisone supplementation was initiated for stress-related events, highlighting the importance of long-term endocrine surveillance in patients who receive high-dose irradiation to the hypothalamic–pituitary region [24].

This study has some limitations. The single-institution, retrospective design of the study and its small sample size precluded statistical comparisons between groups, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the retrospective nature of the study meant that TRH stimulation tests were not performed at the time of diagnosis in several patients, making it difficult to distinguish subclinical hypothyroidism from central hypothyroidism. Furthermore, even with the additional information provided by TRH stimulation tests, some degree of misclassification may remain when thyroid dysfunction is defined primarily by serum TSH levels in patients with hypothalamic–pituitary injury. In addition, the shorter follow-up period for the proton beam therapy group limited the full assessment of long-term endocrine risks. There was also a difference in the age at medulloblastoma diagnosis between the two groups (median age: 4.2 vs. 9.4 years for the X-ray and proton beam therapy groups, respectively), which may have influenced the incidence of endocrine disorders. Moreover, treatment strategies for pediatric medulloblastoma also evolved during the study period, including changes in chemotherapy protocols, which may have affected endocrine outcomes independently of radiation modality. Finally, the actual radiation doses to endocrine organs, including the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, thyroid, gonads, and adrenal glands, were not calculated. However, this study provides a detailed longitudinal description of endocrine outcomes in pediatric patients with medulloblastoma and contributes clinically well-characterized cases to the existing evidence from previous reports.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that, at 5 years following the completion of radiation therapy in 14 Japanese pediatric patients with medulloblastoma, primary hypothyroidism and central hypothyroidism were observed in 33% and 11% of patients who received X-ray therapy, respectively, whereas no such cases were reported in those who underwent proton beam therapy (Graphical Abstract). However, GHD was observed in both the proton beam and X-ray therapy groups. These findings suggest a potential trend toward a lower incidence of hypothyroidism following proton beam therapy; however, endocrine disorders were not completely eliminated, highlighting the need for further dose optimization and individualized treatment approaches. Larger prospective studies are essential to confirm these findings and to refine therapeutic strategies to minimize the risk of radiation-induced endocrine dysfunction.
