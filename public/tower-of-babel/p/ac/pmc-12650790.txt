Multivalent aptamer engineered and functionalized NK cells for enhanced adoptive immunotherapy in CD30-positive malignant lymphoma
Authors: Fang Tan, Rui Wei, Jiqing Su, Cai Sheng, Qian Li, Yang Long, Xuyang Zhao, Liang Zhao, Shengpeng Huang, Lei Zhang, Lu Yang, Hedong Zhang, Shuanghui Yang, Huan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdoptive cell therapy is a notable achievement in the treatment of various hematological malignancies. Among these cell-based therapies, chimeric antigen receptor natural killer (CAR-NK) cells have received increasing attention because, unlike CAR-T cells, they are not associated with the risk of cytokine release syndrome, immune-effector-cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, or graft-versus-host disease. CAR-NK cells have enhanced targeting properties and demonstrated therapeutic efficacy against various types of cancer. However, they face substantial challenges, including complex operational procedures, high cost associated with long-term use, and safety concerns. Aptamers are single-stranded oligonucleotides and known as “chemical antibodies” because of their highly specific binding ability to targets. In this study, we designed a multivalent aptamer oligonucleotide complex containing four strands of CD30 aptamers to engineer and functionalize NK cells using click chemistry. Under aptamer guidance, multivalent aptamer oligonucleotide complex NK cells specifically bind to CD30-positive lymphoma cells and trigger more effective antitumor effects than that of parental NK cells, both in vitro and in vivo. Therefore, the proposed approach endows NK cells with tumor-specific targeting ability and enhances their adoptive therapeutic efficiency in CD30-positive lymphoma, showing substantial potential for application in the clinical treatment of malignant lymphoma.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, aptamers, DNA structures, NK cells, adoptive cell therapy, CD30-positive lymphoma

Introduction

Lymphomas are life-threatening hematological malignancies characterized by the expression of multiple biomarkers on the cell membrane. CD30 is an important biomarker that is universally overexpressed, mainly in Hodgkin and anaplastic large cell lymphoma, and an ideal therapeutic target.1,2,3 Currently, treatments targeting CD30, such as CD30-specific monoclonal antibodies and adoptive cell therapy (ACT), have shown satisfactory efficacy in clinical lymphoma therapy.4,5 However, some patients experience fatal side effects, such as peripheral neuropathy, serious infections, or even anaphylactic reactions.6

ACT, a crucial branch of immunotherapy that has become a novel and rapidly promoting antineoplastic therapy, is a treatment method in which autologous or allogeneic immune cells from patients are separated, activated, or genetically modified in vitro to obtain a sufficient number of enhanced immune cells with antitumor activity that can directly kill tumors or stimulate the body’s immune response to kill tumor cells in patients with cancer. In the past 10 years, marked achievements have been made in the use of chimeric antigen receptor-T (CAR-T) cells for the treatment of various hematological malignancies, such as multiple myeloma,7 acute lymphoblastic leukemia,8,9,10 and lymphoma.11,12 However, its side effects, including cytokine release syndrome, immune-effector-cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, and graft-versus-host disease, substantially restrict the clinical effectiveness of CAR-T cells.13,14,15 Compared with T cells, natural killer (NK) cells recognize and eliminate targets independently of human leukocyte antigen matching and produce cytokines with fewer immune-stimulating properties; thus, NK cells are not associated with these fatal risks.16,17 Therefore, CAR-NK cells have drawn significant attention and are being explored as promising ACT substitutes to overcome conventional adoption limitations.17,18,19 NK cells are a subclass of cytotoxic lymphocytes20,21 that can impede tumor growth through immune surveillance without the need for prior immune memory and play an important role in the innate immune system.22,23 Upon activation, NK cells can eliminate target cells via the following mechanisms: (1) forming synapses with target cells, which leads to the release of perforin and granzyme B to lyse the target cells24,25 and secretion of a variety of cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interferon (IFN)-γ, to promote antigen presentation and adaptive immune responses nearby immune cells26; (2) antibody-dependent cell cytotoxicity via the binding of FcγRIIIA and/or FcγRIIC expressed on NK cells with the Fc portion of immunoglobulins27; and (3) exerting cytotoxic potential by expressing crucial death ligands, including Fas ligand and TNF-associated apoptosis-inducing ligand, which leads to target cell apoptosis after interacting with their receptors.28,29 Compared with T cells, NK cells can directly destroy multiple types of tumor cells without the need for major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-neoantigen presentation.23 Moreover, large numbers of NK cells can be generated from multiple sources, including NK92 cell lines, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, umbilical cord blood, and induced pluripotent stem cells.30 CAR-NK cells, which possess enhanced specific targeting properties, have garnered significant clinical interest due to their demonstrated therapeutic efficacy against various types of cancer.31,32,33 Notably, in the treatment of CD19-positive B cell malignancies, phase I/II clinical trials of cord-blood-derived CD19 CAR-NK cell therapy have shown an objective response rate of 48.6% and a 1-year survival rate of 68%.34 However, the generation of CAR-NK cells still faces significant challenges, including complex operational procedures and the high cost associated with long-term use.35,36,37 Safety concerns arise from the wide distribution of proviral insertion sites of a retroviral vector and the associated risk for oncogenesis due to potential insertional mutagenesis.38

Aptamers are single-stranded oligonucleotides and are also known as “chemical antibodies” because of their highly specific ability to bind to targets, including small molecules, proteins, viruses, and cells. Compared with protein antibodies, aptamers have advantages, such as greater tissue penetration, lower immunogenicity, low production cost, and ease of modification. Consequently, aptamers can overcome many of the shortcomings of protein antibodies and be widely used in clinical practice, including for tumor imaging in vitro and in vivo,39 tumor tissue staining,40 tumor-targeted therapy,41,42 biomarkers,43 and nanomedicine.44 Meanwhile, studies in the field of targeted therapy demonstrate that modified multivalent aptamers exhibit superior binding and killing abilities to tumor cells compared with those of monovalent aptamers.45,46 The CD30 aptamer, developed by Parekh et al. using Systematic Evolution of Ligands by EXponential Enrichment technology, is a single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) aptamer with the nucleotide sequence 5ʹ-ACTGGGCGAAACAAGTCTATTGACTATGAGC-3ʹ. This synthetic aptamer can specifically bind to CD30-positive cells with high affinity. Notably, it retains its binding efficacy even after an 8-h incubation in human serum, demonstrating resistance to nuclease degradation.47 Click chemistry is fundamentally characterized by rapid, highly selective, and modular reactions that enable precise molecular conjugation.48 Among its variants, copper-free click chemistry exemplified by strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC) is a pivotal tool in precision medicine due to its exceptional biocompatibility and operational simplicity.49,50 Tetraacetyl-N-azidoacetylmannosamine (Ac4ManNAz) is one of the most extensively studied metabolic cell-labeling reagents. It is metabolized in cells through sialic acid biosynthesis to azido sialic acid on the cell membrane and then labels cells through click chemistry after treatment with dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO)-bearing reagents through SPAAC reactions,51 which can increase the specificity of cell functions.52,53

In this study, we designed a multivalent aptamer oligonucleotide complex (MAOC) to facilitate the high-density anchoring of CD30-specific aptamer onto NK cell membranes via click chemistry. This aptamer-based modification approach provides distinct advantages over conventional antibody-dependent CAR technologies, including significantly lower immunogenicity, elimination of the oncogenic risks associated with genetic insertion, and more straightforward and cost-effective procedures. This strategy aimed to enhance NK-cell-mediated tumor-specific recognition and cytotoxicity, thereby offering a valid approach for targeted lymphoma therapy.

Results

Preparation and characterization of MAOC

In previous studies on tetrahedral DNA nanostructures, the scaffold adopted by Ma et al. was constructed from four ssDNA strands, providing four modifiable sites. However, this design makes it challenging to functionalize an adequate number of specific aptamers while simultaneously performing DBCO modification to facilitate insertion into the cell membrane.54 Therefore, we strategically developed an MAOC that can carry more aptamers through the rational design of six complementary ssDNA sequences (MAOC1, MAOC2, MAOC3, MAOC4, MAOC5, and MAOC6). As shown in Figure 1A, different colors were used to distinguish the specific complementary binding regions among the six ssDNA molecules. All the ssDNA samples were acquired from commercial sources. MAOC was synthesized using a precisely controlled gradient-cooling protocol (Table 1), facilitating spontaneous base-pairing interactions among the complementary sequences to form the final three-dimensional architecture. Notably, CD30 aptamer sequences are contained in the 5ʹ-ends of MAOC2, MAOC3, MAOC4, and MAOC5, which can specifically bind to CD30-positive lymphoma cells. The terminals of the MAOC1, MAOC2, and MAOC6 molecules were modified with DBCO, strategically designed for efficient membrane anchoring through click chemistry reactions. Moreover, framework oligonucleotide complex (FOC) were designed as controls by omitting the CD30 aptamer sequences. All ssDNA sequences used in this study are listed in Table 2.

To ensure successful synthesis of MAOC, we used nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. As shown in Figure 1B, MAOC was successfully prepared after the self-assembly process. Moreover, we successfully synthesized FOCs using the same methodology (Figure S1). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) were then used to determine the size (approximately 400 nm) (Figures 1C and 1D) and zeta potential (approximately −4.92 mV) of the MAOC (Figure 1E), which also implied the successful synthesis of MAOC and demonstrated its characterization.

To further analyze the stability of the MAOC in vitro, the prepared MAOC was incubated in RPMI 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C for different time intervals, and MAOC band still showed a strong signal after 48 h (Figures 1F and 1G). These findings indicated the high stability of the prepared MAOC in vitro.

Generation and characterization of MAOC-NK92 cells

To determine the optimal concentration of Ac4ManNAz, we initially evaluated its cytotoxicity in NK92 cells using the CCK-8 assay. As depicted in Figure 2A, incubation with 50 μM Ac4ManNAz for up to 48 h did not significantly compromise NK92 cell viability when compared to that with the untreated control. However, cell viability decreased with increasing concentrations or prolonged incubation periods. Notably, a concentration of 200 μM markedly decreased viability after only 24 h of incubation. Additionally, incubation with 75 μM for 48 h also resulted in a significant adverse effect. Consequently, a condition of 50 μM Ac4ManNAz with a 24-h incubation period was chosen for subsequent experiments. After incubation with Ac4ManNAz, apoptotic or dead NK92 cells were gated and quantified via flow cytometry based on cellular signals from FITC-annexin V and propidium iodide (PI), respectively. As depicted in Figure 2C, the apoptotic and dead NK92 cell percentages in our tested groups (NK92+DMSO, NK92 + 10 μM Ac4ManNAz, NK92 + 20 μM Ac4ManNAz, and NK92 + 50 μM Ac4ManNAz) were calculated to be approximately 5%. The outcomes of these experiments suggest that supplementing NK92 cells with 50 μM Ac4ManNAz during in vitro expansion is essentially non-toxic and biocompatible. CD107a surface expression serves as a well-characterized degranulation marker of NK cell activity; therefore, we subsequently conducted flow cytometry analyses to evaluate the effects of Ac4ManNAz on CD107a expression in NK92 cells. Tumor cells can activate NK92 cells; therefore, we chose Karpas-299 cells as the activating stimulus. CD107a was significantly increased in the presence of Karpas-299 cells compared with that in isolated NK92 cells, indicating the function of tumor cells as an activating stimulus. Whereas, the CD107a expression of NK92 cells incubated with 50 μM Ac4ManNAz was not affected after tumor cell stimulation (Figure 2B). These findings collectively suggest that Ac4ManNAz-mediated metabolic labeling does not interfere with or enhance the tumor-cell-induced activation of NK92 cells.

To endow NK92 cells with tumor-specific targeting properties, the surfaces of NK92 cells were modified with MAOC. First, 50 μM Ac4ManNAz was co-incubated with NK92 cells for 24 h, and then 0.5 μM DBCO-MAOC was covalently conjugated with the azide residues on the NK92 cell surface via a click reaction to generate MAOC-NK92 cells (Figure 2D). As a control, a DBCO-labeled CD30 aptamer was used to generate CD30-NK92 cells. To facilitate the tracking of MAOC-NK92 cells, we procured commercially available 5ʹ-FAM-labeled MAOC2, MAOC3, MAOC4, and MAOC5, then synthesized FAM-DBCO-MAOC with unlabeled MAOC1 and unlabeled MAOC6. We prepared a commercially available 5ʹ-FAM-labeled CD30 aptamer (FAM-DBCO-CD30). These fluorescent signals were validated using flow cytometry after co-incubation with NK92 cells. As shown in Figures 2E and S2, strong green fluorescence signals were observed on the surface of treated NK92 cells compared with that in untreated NK92 cells, indicating that all NK92 cells treated with Ac4ManNAz could sufficiently conjugate with DBCO-MAOC or DBCO-CD30. In addition, a concentration-dependent enhancement of FAM fluorescence signals was observed in NK92 cells treated with increasing concentrations of DBCO-MAOC (Figure 2E) or the DBCO-modified CD30 aptamer (DBCO-CD30) (Figure S2). This fluorescence gradient pattern clearly demonstrates the progressive anchoring of both MAOC and CD30 aptamers to the cell membrane surface following their DBCO-mediated reaction with metabolized Ac4ManNAz groups. However, when the DBCO-MAOC concentration reached 0.5 μM, subsequent increases in concentration resulted in only marginal enhancement of fluorescence intensity. This saturation phenomenon suggested that the anchoring sites on the NK92 cell membranes approached maximal occupancy at 0.5 μM DBCO-MAOC. Based on this concentration-dependent response profile, we selected 0.5 μM DBCO-MAOC as the optimal concentration for subsequent experimental applications. Notably, control experiments revealed the absence of fluorescence in NK92 cells co-incubated with MAOC without prior Ac4ManNAz treatment, conclusively demonstrating the essential role of Ac4ManNAz pretreatment in enabling specific membrane anchoring of MAOC. In our validation study, confocal microscopy confirmed that MAOC-NK92 products were morphologically intact and revealed that the fluorescence signal of MAOC overlapped with that of the cell membrane (Figure 2F). In contrast, no obvious green fluorescence was detected in NK92 cells or NK92 cells treated with Ac4ManNAz alone, indicating that DBCO-MAOC/DBCO-CD30 successfully bound to the NK92 cell membrane.

MAOC selectively binds to CD30-positive lymphoma cells

To determine the specific binding of MAOC-NK92 cells to CD30-positive lymphoma cells, we co-incubated different groups of NK92 cells (parental NK92 cells or engineered NK92 cells: A-NK92, FOC-NK92, CD30-NK92, and MAOC-NK92) with CD30-positive (Karpas-299 and HDLM2; Figure S3) and CD30-negative (U937 and JeKo-1; Figure S3) lymphoma cells at different effector/target (E/T) ratios (5:1, 2.5:1, 1:1, 1:2.5, and 1:5). Lymphoma and NK92 cells were pre-stained with calcein red-orange AM dye or calcein-AM fluorescent dye, respectively. After 0.5 h of co-incubation, flow cytometry was used to analyze mixtures of different NK92 and lymphoma cells to estimate the binding ability of effector to lymphoma cells. As shown in Figure S4, the different cell populations were separated and gated. Red tumor cells were first gated, and the proportion of NK92 cells captured by the tumor cells was analyzed. Quantitative analysis demonstrated the superior target-specific binding of MAOC-NK92 cells to CD30-positive lymphoma cells. At 5:1 E/T ratio, MAOC-NK92 mixtures showed significantly higher enrichment of target cells than that of other NK92 cells (NK92, A-NK92, FOC-NK92, and CD30-NK92 cells): 64.4% vs. 44.0%–46.9% in Karpas-299 co-cultures (Figures 3A and 3C) and 84.7% vs. 61.7%–66.5% in HDLM2 mixtures (Figures 3B and 3D). This enhancement pattern persisted across all tested ratios (5:1, 2.5:1, 1:1, 1:2.5, and 1:5). Notably, control experiments with CD30-negative U937 (Figures S5 and S6) and JeKo-1 cells (Figures S7 and S8) revealed comparable tumor cell percentages across all NK92 groups at every E/T ratio (5:1, 2.5:1, 1:1, 1:2.5, and 1:5), confirming the MAOC-NK92 selective interaction with CD30-positive targets without off-target binding.

To characterize the enhanced specific-binding ability of MAOC-NK92 cells, inspired by Chen et al.,55 a lymphoma suspension and adherent cell models were established to simulate physiological blood environments and solid tumor surface conditions, respectively. Using fluorescence microscopy, we observed the formation of E/T clusters. As shown in Figures S9 and S10, MAOC-NK92 cells formed significantly larger and more E/T clusters with CD30-positive lymphoma cells (Karpas-299 and HDLM2 cell lines) per well in the culture plates than those of the other parental NK92 cells (NK92, A-NK92, FOC-NK92, and CD30-NK92) at an E/T ratio of 1:1. However, MAOC-NK92 cells and other parental NK92 cells had very similar sizes and numbers of E/T clusters as CD30-negative cells (U937 and JeKo-1 cells) under the same conditions (Figures S11 and S12), indicating that MAOC-NK92 cells demonstrated marked cellular specificity in targeting lymphoma cells under suspension culture conditions.

To determine whether MAOC-NK92 cells target lymphoid neoplasms, 24-well plates coated with poly-L-lysine were inoculated with lymphoma cells to mimic the surface of lymphoma lesions. As shown in Figures S13 and S14, MAOC-NK92 cells exhibited significantly enhanced binding efficiency to adherent CD30-positive lymphoma cell lines (Karpas-299 and HDLM2) compared with those of the other groups. In contrast, MAOC-NK92 and other engineered NK92 cells exhibited similar binding capacities to adherent CD30-negative tumor cells (U937 and JeKo-1 cells) (Figures S15 and S16). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MAOC-NK92 cells can specifically target and bind to both suspended and adherent CD30-positive lymphoma cells.

MAOC-NK92 enhances the ability of NK92 cells to kill CD30-positive lymphoma cells

To assess the therapeutic effects of parental NK92 and MAOC-NK92 cells on CD30-positive lymphoma cells, adherent Karpas-299/HDLM2 cells were co-incubated with different NK92 cells. In Figures 3A–3D, MAOC(0.5 μM)-NK92 showed significantly enhanced binding affinity toward target cells compared with that of CD30(0.5 μM)-NK92. The results in Figure S2 showed that incubating NK92 cells with 0.5 μM DBCO-labeled CD30 aptamer did not achieve maximum membrane anchoring efficiency, which nearly reached saturation at 2 μM. Thus, we explored killing efficiency of MAOC-NK92 toward lymphoma cells compared to CD30(0.5 μM)-NK92 and CD30(2 μM)-NK92 cells. Compared with parental NK92 cells, MAOC-NK92 cells induced a significant increase in the apoptosis/necrosis rates of target lymphoma cells (Karpas-299 and HDLM2) at all tested E/T ratios (5:1, 1:1, and 1:5) (Figures 4A and 4B). As illustrated in Figure S17, 10.5% of Karpas-299 were apoptotic and necrotic cells after the incubation of parental NK92 cells, and this proportion further increased to 19.5% after incubation with CD30(2 μM)-NK92 cells or 28.4% after incubation with MAOC-NK92 cells at an E/T ratio of 1:1. For HDLM2 cells, 31.5% of cells were apoptotic or necrotic after treatment with MAOC-NK92 cells at an E/T ratio of 1:1, and this proportion was much greater than those observed after co-incubation with parental NK92 (21.5%), A-NK92 (22.5%), FOC-NK92 (22.8%), CD30(0.5 μM)-NK92 (21.6%), and CD30(2 μM)-NK92 cells (25.3%) (Figure S18). The corresponding pooled quantitative analyses with statistical evaluation of all three replicates are shown in Figures 4A and 4B. These results strongly confirmed that MAOC-NK92 cells demonstrated significantly enhanced cytotoxicity against CD30-positive lymphoma cells compared with that of the other NK92 cell groups. Notably, this enhanced killing effect showed target specificity, as no increased cytotoxicity was observed against off-target control cells (U937 and JeKo-1 cell lines) in parallel experiments (Figures S19–S22). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MAOC engineering effectively enhances the tumor-killing capacity of NK92 cells in a target-specific manner.

To further evaluate the ability of MAOC-NK92 cells to kill CD30-positive lymphoma cells, the secretion of the cytokines, perforin, granzyme B, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, was measured using ELISA. As shown in Figures 4C–4J, the concentrations of the four cytokines in the medium increased significantly compared with those in the other NK92 cell groups, irrespective of the co-incubation of Karpas-299 or HDLM2 cells with MAOC-NK92 cells. Specifically, the level of IFN-γ secretion increased the most in the MAOC-NK92 group, reaching 160.3 pg/mL when incubated with Karpas-299 cells, whereas it increased to 7.944 pg/mL (NK92), 22.69 pg/mL (A-NK92), 20.87 pg/mL (FOC-NK92), 16.19 pg/mL [CD30(0.5 μM)-NK92], and 30.99 pg/mL [CD30(2 μM)-NK92] for the other groups. For HDLM2 cells, the IFN-γ concentration reached 123.7 pg/mL, whereas it was 17.84 pg/mL (NK92), 19.4 pg/mL (A-NK92), 28.28 pg/mL (FOC-NK92), 31.30 pg/mL [CD30(0.5 μM)-NK92], and 47.3 pg/mL in other groups. However, the secretion of the cytokines, perforin, granzyme B, IFN-γ, and TNF-α did not significantly change after each NK92 group was co-incubated with CD30-negative cells (Figures S23–S26). These results were consistent with the killing effect analyzed by flow cytometry, further suggesting that MAOC-NK92 enhances the ability of NK92 cells to specifically kill lymphoma cells.

MAOC-NK92 cells enhance lymphoma killing in vivo

Prior to in vivo administration of MAOC-NK92 cells, a safety assessment was conducted. A hemolysis experiment was first conducted after the incubation of mouse red blood cells (RBCs) with different NK92 cells [NK92 cells, A-NK92 cells, FOC-NK92 cells, CD30(0.5 μM)-NK92 cells, CD30(2 μM)-NK92 cells, and MAOC-NK92 cells] for 3 h (Figure 5B). The positive control (deionized water) group showed severe hemolysis. However, the negative control group (PBS) and groups treated with different NK92 cells did not show such changes, demonstrating the safety of the engineered NK92 cells in vivo.

We further investigated the in vivo biodistribution of MAOC-NK92 cells in tumor-bearing NOD-SCID mice. NK92, CD30-NK92, and FOC-NK92 cells were used as the controls. For tracking purposes, all NK92 cells were stained with indocyanine green (ICG) prior to injection. Subsequently, they were injected intravenously into Karpas-299 tumor-bearing mice until the tumor volume reached a certain size (100 mm3). At 24 h post-injection, all mice were imaged using a live imaging system for small animals, and the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and tumors were dissected to monitor the distribution of NK92 cells using the same system. As shown in Figure 5C, the mouse tumor is located on the right side and outlined by a red dashed circle. ICG fluorescence at tumor sites was very weak in the NK92/FOC-NK92/CD30-NK92 cell group but significantly increased in the MAOC-NK92 cell group, suggesting that more NK92 cells were recruited into the tumor tissue. The imaging results of isolated mouse organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and tumors also showed comparable outcomes, with specific accumulation at the tumor site in the MAOC-NK92 cell group being significantly higher than that in the other groups (Figure 5D). The quantitative fluorescence distribution profiles of ex vivo tumors and organs are shown in Figures 5E and S27, indicating that the tumor sites in the MAOC-NK92 group exhibited the strongest fluorescence signals, whereas no significant differences in NK92 cell accumulation were observed in the hearts of all treatment groups. However, the modified NK92 cells exhibited a slight increase in accumulation within the liver, spleen, kidney, and lungs, likely because of the abundant capillary networks present in these organs. These results revealed that MAOC-NK92 cells could specifically bind to Karpas-299 cells that highly express CD30, which may further help direct NK92 cells to the tumor site.

We further evaluated whether the prepared MAOC-NK92 cells exhibited in vivo antitumor effects. The detailed treatment timeline is shown in Figure 5A. Karpas-299 tumor-bearing NOD-SCID mice were randomly divided into five groups for different treatments (PBS, NK92, FOC-NK92, CD30-NK92, and MAOC-NK92) when the tumor volume reached 100 mm3. Tail vein injections were administered during the therapeutic period at three doses, as shown in Figure 5A. As expected, tumors in all the treated groups exhibited delayed growth compared with those in the PBS group. Furthermore, the group treated with MAOC-NK92 cells showed the most marked inhibition of tumor growth (Figure 5F). At the end of the experiment, the mice were sacrificed, dissected, and their tumors were photographed (Figure 5G). Body weight did not differ significantly between the treated and control groups (Figure 5H). The mice were sacrificed on day 18 after the first injection, after which hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemical staining of the tumor tissues were performed to further investigate the antitumor effect. Immunohistochemical staining for CD56 (Figures 5J and S29) indicated that the infiltration of MAOC-NK92 cells into the tumor tissue was significantly increased, which contributed to a stronger antitumor response. A comparative quantitative analysis of CD56-positive cell density across groups is shown in Figure S30. Concurrently, H&E staining (Figure 5I) and quantitative assessment (Figure S28) demonstrated extensive regions of tumor necrosis and apoptosis in MAOC-NK92-treated mice relative to other groups. These results further proved that MAOC enhanced the ability of modified NK92 cells to kill tumor cells in vivo.

Moreover, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, stomach, and bowel) were stained with H&E to evaluate the biosafety of MAOC-NK92 cells in vivo. The morphology of the major organs did not significantly change in any of the NK92-treated mice compared with that in PBS-treated mice (Figure S31). To further assess biosafety, blood was obtained from the mice in each group for analysis via routine blood tests. As shown in Figures S32–S36, all indicators (white blood cells, number and percentage of neutrophils, number and percentage of lymphocytes, number and percentage of monocytes, RBCs, hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, RBC distribution width, platelets, mean platelet volume, and platelet distribution width) were within the normal range. These results revealed the outstanding biocompatibility and biosafety of NK92 cells in vivo.

Discussion

This study focused on precision engineering of NK92 cells via MAOC. MAOC significantly enhanced targeting efficiency and cytotoxicity both in vitro and in vivo by enabling controlled multivalent aptamer anchoring via click chemistry conjugation.

Previous studies have found that monovalent aptamer conjugation, including using double C18 chains and metabolic glycoengineering, improves NK cell targeting.56,57 However, these monovalent modification approaches may not fully exploit the optimal therapeutic potential of NK cells. These findings prompted inquiries into how multivalent strategies can further optimize therapeutic efficacy. In this study, we designed an MAOC to facilitate the high-density anchoring of CD30-specific aptamers onto NK cell membranes via click chemistry. Unlike the current chimeric antigen receptor construction, which requires complex genetic recombination, the MAOC modification strategy enables targeted engineering of NK92 cells via chemical anchoring. This approach not only avoids the risk of insertional mutagenesis associated with genomic integration but also drastically reduces the engineering timeline from several days to mere hours, owing to the high efficiency and orthogonality of the click chemistry reaction.58 This finding may enable patients with clinically acute onset to access therapeutic drugs more rapidly. In vitro stability assays demonstrated that MAOC remained stable in serum at 37°C for up to 48 h, meeting the requirements for systemic circulation and providing critical theoretical support for in vivo application. Importantly, we observed prolonged MAOC retention on the surface of NK92 cell membranes (Figure S38). This phenomenon is likely attributable to the large nanoscale size of MAOC and its robust binding to the azide groups introduced onto the NK92 cell membrane surface. This effectively prevented MAOC from being internalized and degraded by NK92 cells. This prolonged retention is beneficial for MAOC binding to target cells. In addition to improving binding affinity, we discovered that MAOC enhanced cytotoxicity, as evidenced by flow cytometry. Moreover, this observation is consistent with the findings of Shi and Guo et al., who anchored multivalent aptamers with specific targeting properties to NK cell membranes. Their research also confirmed that this multivalent aptamer modification strategy significantly outperformed monovalent strategies in terms of both target binding capacity and cytotoxic effects.45,46 This enhancement might be attributed to the increased spatial density of aptamers, strengthening effector-target interactions. For the binding experiments, we established suspension and adherent lymphoma cell culture models to simulate lymphomas in the circulatory system and localized mass states, respectively. Both models yielded consistent results, and MAOC-NK92 cells exhibited significantly enhanced target-binding efficiency compared with NK92 cells modified with traditional monovalent CD30 aptamers. This implies that for lymphoma treatment, MAOC-NK92 cells show potential effectiveness against both circulating residues (in the blood/bone marrow) and localized solid masses. Furthermore, in contrast to Wu et al.’s assessment of CD30 CAR-T cell cytotoxicity at supraphysiological E:T ratios (40:1–10:1),59 we implemented stepwise E:T titration (5:1 to 1:5) to delineate the targeted killing efficiency of MAOC-NK92 cells against lymphoma cells. The results demonstrated a significantly enhanced killing efficiency of MAOC-NK92 cells, even at the lowest E/T ratio tested (1:5). This capacity effectively overcomes the immune exclusion barrier in tumors, where sparse effector infiltration into tumor microenvironments yields subtherapeutic effector densities, thereby enhancing cytolytic activity.60,61,62 Given this characteristic, we propose that the clinical application of MAOC-NK92 may favor a relatively low-dose administration regimen. Additionally, this therapy may hold particular promise for patients with measurable residual disease positivity, those requiring consolidation or maintenance therapy, and those with local recurrence.

MAOC demonstrates significant potential; therefore, its limitations and future research directions warrant further exploration. Adjusting the length or number of DNA strands containing MAOC, along with introducing chemical modifications to these strands, could enhance MAOC stability. This improved stability may prolong the functional duration in vivo, thereby extending the administration interval. Furthermore, increasing the number of strands provides more modification sites, thereby enabling the incorporation of higher-valent aptamers. Although these adjustments may increase design complexity, they offer substantial benefits for enhancing the targeting capabilities and improving the prospects for clinical translation. Concurrently, the modular nature of our MAOC design significantly expands its applicability by simply replacing the conjugated aptamers, and the design can be precisely targeted to diverse malignant tumors.

In conclusion, we developed an MAOC capable of loading tetravalent CD30 aptamers that can anchor to NK92 cell membranes via click chemistry. This polyvalent design overcomes the limitations of monovalent aptamer anchoring, providing superior targeted binding capability and cytotoxicity, particularly at low E/T ratios, mimicking in vivo conditions.

Materials and Methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The human anaplastic large cell lymphoma cell line (Karpas-299), human Hodgkin lymphoma cell line (HDLM2), human histiocytic lymphoma cell line (U937), and human mantle cell lymphoma cell line (JeKo-1) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (NEWZERUM, Uruguay) and 1% streptomycin/penicillin (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA). In addition, NK92 cells were purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and maintained in alpha minimum essential medium (α-MEM) (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 12.5% FBS, 12.5% horse serum (Gibco, New Zealand), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.02 mM folic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), 200 units/mL recombinant interleukin-2 (IL-2) (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA), and 1% streptomycin/penicillin. All the cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere (5% CO2).

Preparation and characterization of MAOC

To prepare MAOC, six ssDNA (MAOC1, MAOC2, MAOC3, MAOC4, MAOC5, and MAOC6) were designed, and CD30 aptamer sequences, which can specifically bind to CD30-positive lymphoma cells, are linked in the 5′-ends of MAOC2, MAOC3, MAOC4, and MAOC5 through commercial chemical synthesis. These six commercially obtained ssDNAs were self-assembled through a gradient cooling protocol (Table 1). Briefly, equimolar concentrations of MAOC1 MAOC2, MAOC3, MAOC4, MAOC5, and MAOC6 were mixed in a PCR tube, achieving a final concentration of 15 μM for each ssDNA strand. The assembly process was conducted in a thermal cycler (Mastercycle nexus; Eppendorf, Germany) using the following annealing program: initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min; secondly, cooling down from 95°C to 85°C at a rate of 1°C per minute; then cooling down from 85°C to 35°C at a rate of 0.1°C per minute; and lastly, cooling down from 35°C to 4°C at a rate of 1°C per min and maintained stable at 4°C. As a control, we synthesized an FOC lacking CD30 aptamer sequences using the six ssDNAs with the same cooling protocol. All the sequences of the ssDNAs are shown in Table 2 and were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

As previously reported,63 the synthesized MAOC solution was diluted to 10 nM using DPBS supplemented with 10 mM Mg2+ for subsequent morphological characterization. Size measurements were performed through AFM and SEM following established protocols, with technical assistance provided by Mr. Xi Liu at Yige Laboratory (Changsha). For AFM analysis, the diluted MAOC solution was deposited onto a sample holder mica. After 5 min of surface adsorption at ambient temperature, residual solution was removed by gentle rinsing with ultrapure water followed by drying. Imaging was conducted in tapping mode using a silicon probe. For SEM imaging, a drop of MAOC-diluted solution was spotted onto silicon pretreated with aminopropylsilane for 5 min to allow strong adsorption. The sample was air-dried and carefully mounted on an aluminum stub using a double stick carbon tape, and the sample was then placed in the chamber of the sputter coater and imaged by SEM.

Zeta potential measurements were performed using a Malvern Panalytical zeta potential analyzer (Zetasizer v.6.12, United Kingdom) under controlled temperature conditions (25°C). The experimental procedure consisted of the following steps: first, the MAOC suspension diluted with deionized water was loaded into a rectangular electrophoresis cell. The electrodes were then properly positioned to ensure optimal contact with the sample. Subsequently, an electric field was applied across the electrophoresis cell to induce particle migration, during which the instrument automatically recorded the resulting electrophoretic mobility. Each sample underwent three measurements to obtain representative values, with the final zeta potential being calculated as the mean of these replicates using Graphpad Prism software.

Gel electrophoresis experiments

To confirm the successful preparation of MAOC, different samples (MAOC1, MAOC2, MAOC3, MAOC4, MAOC5, MAOC6, MAOC1-3, and MAOC) enriched with loading buffer were subjected to 5% native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Native-PAGE). The electrophoresis was performed at a voltage of 90 V for 30 min in 1×TBE buffer, and then the image was visualized by using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA) after staining with SYBR GOLD (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA).

MAOC stability assay

To investigate the stability of the synthesized MAOC in a complex environment, 1 μL of MAOC (5 μM) was incubated in 9 μL of fresh culture medium containing 10% FBS at 37°C for different durations (0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h), after which the integrity of the MAOC was analyzed via 5% Native-PAGE. Finally, the bands were quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ.

CCK-8 assay

Ac4ManNAz (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 900917) was dissolved in sterile DMSO to prepare a 20 mM stock solution. NK92 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well and treated with Ac4ManNAz at final concentrations ranging from 10 to 200 μM. Following incubation periods varying from 12 to 48 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Beijing Labgic Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; Catalog No. BS350C) was added to each well. After an additional 3-h incubation period, optical density (OD) values were measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm wavelengths to assess cell viability. To ensure proper blank subtraction, we set control wells containing culture medium and CCK-8 reagent without cells.

Flow cytometry analysis of Ac4ManNAz on apoptosis in NK92 cells

An evaluation of Ac4ManNAz-induced apoptosis in NK92 cells was conducted following a 24-h co-incubation period with Ac4ManNAz at concentrations of 10, 20, and 50 μM (using DMSO as the vehicle control). Cell apoptosis was assessed via Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) dual staining, followed by flow cytometry analysis. The gating strategy for apoptosis detection identified four distinct cell populations: viable cells (Annexin V−/PI−), early apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI−), late apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI+), and necrotic cells (Annexin V−/PI+).

Preparation of MAOC-NK92 cells

To generate MAOC-NK92 cells, exponentially growing NK92 cells were incubated in complete medium supplemented with 50 μM Ac4ManNAz for 24 h under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). Following the incubation period, cells were rinsed twice with DPBS to remove residual reagents before subsequent experimental procedures. Subsequently, NK92 cells were further co-incubated with 0.5 μM of synthetic DBCO-modified MAOC (DBCO-MAOC) in DPBS supplemented with 1% FBS and 1% horse serum (HS) for 2 h at 37°C, resulting in the generation of MAOC-modified NK92 cells (MAOC-NK92). Other engineered NK92 (A-NK92, FOC-NK92, and CD30-NK92) cells were obtained in the same manner described above.

Characterization of MAOC-NK92 cells

To confirm the ability of the MAOC conjugates to anchor to the surface of NK92 cells, different concentrations of FAM-labeled DBCO-MAOC (0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1 μM) were incubated with Ac4ManNAz-treated NK92 cells, after which the fluorescence signals derived from surface-anchored MAOC conjugates on the NK92 cells were analyzed via flow cytometry (BD FACSanto II Flow Cytometer, USA).

In an additional validation step, 0.5 μM FAM-labeled DBCO-MAOC was incubated with Ac4ManNAz-treated NK92 cells, and Dil dye (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and Hoechst 33342 staining solution (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) were used to stain the cell membranes (in staining buffer) and cell nuclei (in RPMI medium), respectively, at room temperature. An LSM 900 confocal microscope (Zeiss AiryScan, Oberkochen, Germany) was subsequently used to image MAOC-NK92 cells in the bright field, FAM, PE, or Hoechst channels to evaluate cell morphology and the presence and location of the FAM-labeled MAOC, the cell membranes, and the cell nuclei, respectively.

NK92 lymphoma cell cluster formation assay

To verify whether MAOC-NK92 cells could specifically target tumor cells, lymphoma cells (target cells, T) were prestained with 100 nM calcein red-orange AM dye (red fluorescence) (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and parental NK92 cells and engineered NK92 cells (A-NK92, FOC-NK92, CD30-NK92, and MAOC-NK92) (effector cells, E) were prestained with calcein-AM dye (green fluorescence) (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). For the cell-binding reaction, effector cells were mixed with a certain number of target cells (1 × 105) at different ratios (E/T = 5:1, 2.5:1, 1:1, 1:2.5, and 1:5) in 500 μL of RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS. The mixture was subsequently incubated in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube with constant gentle shaking on a shaking bed (GE Healthcare) at room temperature for 30 min. Constant shaking mimics in vivo dynamic blood flow and prevents nonspecific cell clotting due to gravity-induced precipitation. Subsequently, the percentages of double-positive cell populations within the mixtures of effector and target cells were quantified using flow cytometry (BD FACSantoTM II Flow Cytometer, USA). Specifically, as depicted in Figure S4, distinct cell populations were selectively separated and gated. The red tumor cells were gated initially, followed by the analysis of the proportion of NK92 cells captured by the tumor cells. Mixtures with a 1:1 ratio, prepared using the same method, were imaged with DMi8 fluorescence microscopy (Leica, Shanghai, China). All experiments were repeated ≥3 times.

NK92 lymphoma cell binding observation

To observe cell binding, lymphoma cells, including Karpas-299, HDLM2, U937, or JeKo-1 cells, were centrifuged and transferred to 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) after the growth medium supernatant was aspirated and replaced with PBS. The 24-well plates were subsequently left stationary for 30 min at room temperature to allow sedimentation and adhesion of the cells onto the well bottom, after which the cells were seeded for 24 h before use, as described in previous papers.64 For tracking purposes, parental NK92 cells (NK92, A-NK92, FOC-NK92, and CD30-NK92) and engineered NK92 cells (MAOC-NK92) were prestained with 5 nM calcein AM dye and then mixed with lymphoma cells at a 1:1 ratio. After incubation at 37°C for 2 h, unbound NK92 cells were removed from the wells by washing with DPBS. The cells were imaged in both the bright field and fluorescent channels via fluorescence microscopy.

Cell killing assay

To assess the cytotoxicity of MAOC-NK92 cells toward lymphoma cells, four lymphoma cell lines (Karpas-299, HDLM2, U937, and JeKo-1) were seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated in RPMI with 10% FBS at 37°C overnight. Both parental NK-92 cells and engineered NK92 cells were prestained with calcein AM dye. Various numbers of NK92 cells were added to wells with lymphoma cells to achieve different effector-to-target cell ratios (E/T = 5:1, 1:1 or 1:5). After coincubation for 2 h, the unbound NK cells were removed by gently washing the wells, and the remaining cell mixtures were incubated in fresh culture medium at 37°C for an additional 24 h. Prior to flow cytometry analysis, the cells were then collected and stained at room temperature for 30 min with APC Annexin V (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) to highlight apoptotic cells and with PI dye (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) to mark necrotic cells in Annexin V binding buffer. Tumor cells were then gated via flow cytometry by separating them from NK92 cells with a fluorescent signal derived from prestained calcein AM dye. Subsequently, apoptotic and necrotic tumor cell subpopulations were gated and quantified via flow cytometry based on the cellular signals of APC Annexin V and PI dyes, respectively. The percentages of apoptotic and necrotic tumor cells were calculated as percentages of total tumor cells (%).

Quantification of cytokine secretion by ELISA

To assess the cytokines secreted by MAOC-NK92 cells in vitro, parental NK-92 cells or engineered NK92 cells were mixed with target cells (1 × 105) at an E/T ratio of 1:1 in RPMI with 10% FBS and then incubated at 37°C for 5 h. The supernatants of the culture media were subsequently collected to determine the concentrations of perforin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA), granzyme B, TNF-α, and IFN-γ using corresponding ELISA kits (Boster Biological Technology, Valley Ave Pleasanton, CA, USA) according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Hemolysis assay

Prior to in vivo administration, the safety of different NK92 cells was assessed. For hemolysis analysis, mouse RBCs were isolated from the whole blood of healthy NOD-SCID mice and washed with DPBS three times. Then, 2% RBCs were incubated with PBS, NK92 cells, A-NK92 cells, FOC-NK92 cells, CD30 (0.5 μM)-NK92 cells, CD30 (2 μM)-NK92 cells, MAOC-NK92 cells, and deionized water (37°C, 2 h). All the samples were subsequently centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 15 min) and photographed. Finally, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured with a microplate reader (Tecan Trading AG, INFINITE F50, Switzerland) at 450 nm to assess the viability of the RBCs.

Establishment of an animal model and biodistribution of MAOC-NK92 cells in vivo

Female NOD-SCID mice (4–6 weeks old) were purchased from HUNAN SJA LABORATORY ANIMAL Co., Ltd. (Changsha, Hunan, China) to establish xenograft tumor models by subcutaneously injecting 1 × 107 cultured Karpas-299 cells (in 100 μL of PBS) according to previously published methods.63 Ethical approval for the animal studies was granted by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. To investigate the active targeting ability of transplanted NK92 cells (parental NK92 cells, FOC-NK92 cells, CD30-NK92 cells, and MAOC-NK92 cells) in vivo, tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups when the tumor volume exceeded 100 mm3: the parental NK92 group, FOC-NK92 group, CD30-NK92 group, and MAOC-NK92 group. These tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with indocyanine green (ICG)-labeled (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Taufkirchen, Germany) parental NK92 cells, FOC-NK92 cells, CD30-NK92 cells, and MAOC-NK92 cells (5 × 106 cells). Up to 24 h after injection, whole-body imaging was performed immediately using the International Veterinary Information Service (IVIS) Imaging System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). At 24 h postinjection, the treated mice were sacrificed, and the tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were excised for fluorescence analysis of the distribution of ICG-labeled NK92 cells via an IVIS imaging system. All the fluorescence images were acquired under excitation at 745 nm.

Anticancer efficacy of MAOC-NK92 cells in vivo

For the anticancer effect of MAOC-NK92 cells in vivo, 20 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into five groups (four mice per group) when the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, and equal volumes of PBS or 5 × 106 NK92 cells were injected via the tail vein every 3 days for a total of three injections.

The mice were divided into the following five groups and received the following treatments:

1.Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4);

Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4);

2.Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS containing 5 × 106 parental NK92 cells with 1,000 IU/mouse IL-2 for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4);

Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS containing 5 × 106 parental NK92 cells with 1,000 IU/mouse IL-2 for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4);

3.Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS containing 5 × 106 FOC-NK92 cells with 1,000 IU/mouse IL-2 for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4);

Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS containing 5 × 106 FOC-NK92 cells with 1,000 IU/mouse IL-2 for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4);

4.Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS containing 5 × 106 CD30-NK92 cells with 1,000 IU/mouse IL-2 for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4).

Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS containing 5 × 106 CD30-NK92 cells with 1,000 IU/mouse IL-2 for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4).

5.Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS containing 5 × 106 MAOC-NK92 cells with 1,000 IU/mouse IL-2 for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4).

Intravenous injection of 100 μL of PBS containing 5 × 106 MAOC-NK92 cells with 1,000 IU/mouse IL-2 for a total of three injections over 3 days (n = 4).

The tumor volume and body weight of the mice in each treatment group were measured with a Vernier caliper and an electronic balance every 3 days, respectively. The tumor volume was subsequently calculated via the following formula: tumor volume = (tumor length) × (tumor width)2 × 0.52. According to our animal ethics committee, the mice were euthanized when the tumors reached a volume greater than 1,500 mm3.

To further confirm the antitumor efficiency after receiving the aforementioned treatments, the treated tumor-bearing mice in each group were sacrificed on the 18th day, and the tumors were surgically collected, fixed in 4% formalin solution, embedded in paraffin, and stained with H&E and an anti-CD56 antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for histopathological analysis. Both the H&E-stained tumor tissue sections and CD56 immunohistochemical images were analyzed using ImageJ to quantify the necrotic area and the proportion of NK92 cells. Moreover, to further examine histological changes and assess possible systemic toxicity, the main organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, stomach, and bowel, were sectioned and stained with H&E. The images were obtained via an Akoya Vectra Polaris 3 (Akoya Biosciences, MA, USA). Moreover, fresh blood samples from three mice in each group were collected for routine blood tests.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± SEM. All the statistical analyses were carried out via GraphPad Prism 7.04, and data were subjected to one-way ANOVA. p values of ∗<0.05, ∗∗<0.01, ∗∗∗< 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗< 0.001 were considered statistically significant.
