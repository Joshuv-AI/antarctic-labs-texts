Patient Needs Regarding Cardiac Rehabilitation: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Ethnographic Synthesis
Authors: Niek Koenders, Roel van Oorsouw, Daniëlle Conijn, Mariëtte Verbakel, Thijs M H Eijsvogels, Philip J van der Wees
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo improve uptake and completion of cardiac rehabilitation, it is important to identify needs and tailor cardiac rehabilitation accordingly. Therefore, the aim of this study was to synthesize the needs of patients with coronary heart disease, heart failure, or thoracic aortic dissection regarding cardiac rehabilitation.MethodsWe performed a systematic review and meta‐ethnographic synthesis of qualitative studies. We synthesized lines of argument and assessed the confidence of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation Confidence in Evidence From Reviews of Qualitative Research approach. Ultimately, we integrated our findings into a conceptual model with recommendations for cardiac rehabilitation professionals.ResultsNinety‐four studies were included and provided data from 1731 patients, 185 significant others (eg, partners or family members), and 105 cardiac rehabilitation professionals. We have synthesized 5 lines of argument that explained the needs of patients: (1) Patients need safety but not overprotection (high confidence); (2) patients need significant others to be involved (high confidence); (3) patients need support from people with whom they can identify (moderate confidence); (4) patients need personalized care (moderate confidence); and (5) patients need help to redesign a meaningful future (high confidence).ConclusionsThere is a need for innovation of current cardiac rehabilitation programs. Overprotection of significant others must be addressed within a safe environment and by thorough involvement of significant others. Cardiac rehabilitation professionals can begin adapting cardiac rehabilitation by organizing peer support, providing personalized care, and helping to redesign a meaningful future. Meeting patients' needs may improve uptake and completion of cardiac rehabilitation.Keywords: cardiac rehabilitation, coronary disease, experiences, heart failure, needs, qualitative research, systematic review, thoracic aortic dissectionSubject Categories: Quality and Outcomes, Secondary Prevention, Rehabilitation

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Cardiac rehabilitation is a multidisciplinary, systematic, yet personalized approach to providing evidence‐based therapies for patients with cardiovascular disease. 1 The effect of cardiac rehabilitation on death, 2 health care costs, 3 and health‐related quality of life 4 has been extensively studied and acknowledged. Despite the benefits, challenges remain, with a large group of patients not attending, 5 dropping out, 6 or struggling to maintain healthy behaviors over the long term. 7 Inequalities in cardiac rehabilitation participation are largely attributed to social determinants of health, geographic inaccessibility of cardiac rehabilitation, physician bias in cardiac rehabilitation referral decisions, and lack of person‐centered cardiac rehabilitation core components. 8

For practical and economic reasons, cardiac rehabilitation is provided in groups. 9 In group‐based therapy, there is less opportunity to discuss needs privately, and minority interests are more likely to get lost in dealing with a majority. Personalization is often limited to individual modifications of an exercise program. 10 Nevertheless, it is possible to provide personalized rehabilitation in group‐based settings; for example, by tailoring program components on the basis of a needs assessment 11 or tailoring program contents on the basis of the user's needs. 12 For personalization, it is important to understand patient needs, discuss them, and tailor programs accordingly, although an overview of such evidence is lacking.

Therefore, we performed a systematic review and meta‐ethnographic synthesis to provide insight into the needs of patients. We selected patients with coronary artery disease and heart failure because they represent the largest groups, 13 and cardiac rehabilitation uptake has been recommended for these patient groups 14 , 15 due to the strong effect on health outcomes. 16 , 17 We also included the perspectives of patients with a thoracic aortic dissection to explore the needs of a group of patients in whom cardiac rehabilitation is offered and recommended but not yet widely practiced. 18 , 19 , 20 This resulted in the following research question: “What are the needs of patients with coronary artery disease, heart failure, or thoracic aortic dissection related to cardiac rehabilitation?” Using these insights, cardiac rehabilitation professionals can better tailor programs to patients' needs, which could accordingly increase the uptake and completion of cardiac rehabilitation.

METHODS

Design

We performed a systematic review and meta‐ethnographic synthesis of qualitative studies as outlined by Noblit and Hare. 21 We also adopted the European Society for Patient Adherence, Compliance, and Persistence Medication Adherence Reporting Guideline 22 to provide details on all 7 phases: (1) formulating the research question; (2) identifying and selecting studies; (3) reading included studies; (4) determining how studies were related; (5) translating studies into one another; (6) synthesizing translations; and (7) expressing the synthesis. In addition, we used all items of the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research statement to structure the reporting of this study, and we provided a completed checklist in Table S1. 23 Throughout all study phases, we used the following definition of needs 24 : “A need is an undesirable state of affairs; a deficit; a necessity; its presence confers responsibility to make good the deficit; and evaluative notion (or value judgement) someone has to define them as needs.” Cardiac rehabilitation was defined as “exercise training and comprehensive services including education, psychological input, and safety recommendations focusing on health and lifestyle behavior change, risk factor modification, and psychosocial wellbeing”. 25 , 26 The review protocol was a priori registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42023485993). The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. For details, see Data S1.

Search Strategy

We systematically searched the following electronic databases from inception to December 2023: Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid EMBASE, CINAHL (EBSCO), APA PsycINFO, and Web of Science (see details in Table S2). An experienced librarian (M.I.) guided the search and development of the search strings. Google Scholar (first 50 results), OpenGrey, and medRxiv were also systematically searched with “coronary artery disease” or “heart failure” or “thoracic aortic dissection” as patient terms; “cardiac rehabilitation” as intervention term; “needs” as outcome term; and “qualitative research” as study type term. The search strategy was completed with forward citation search in Web of Science of all included studies from inception to April 2024 (Table S2). In deviation from the study protocol, reference lists were not checked, and a backward citation search was not performed because these methods yielded too many new articles to process (n=2618).

Eligibility Criteria

We included studies reporting (1) needs of patients (ie, from the perspective of patients themselves, significant others, or cardiac rehabilitation professionals); with (2) coronary artery disease, heart failure, or thoracic aortic dissection; who (3) were eligible to participate in cardiac rehabilitation (ie, presurgical phase, inpatient phase, outpatient phase, or post–cardiac rehabilitation phase); of which (4) data were captured with qualitative methods (eg, interviews, observations, or open‐ended questionnaires) as part of a qualitative or mixed‐method study. Significant others were defined as “individuals who are or have been deeply influential in one's life, and in whom one is or once was emotionally invested, including members of one's family of origin and people encountered outside of family relation.” 27 Exclusion criteria were (1) about organization of cardiac rehabilitation (ie, referral or enrollment); (2) not about the content of cardiac rehabilitation (ie, financial barriers or transport issues); (3) language not English; (4) ineligible outcomes (e.g., barriers of study intervention procedures); (5) ineligible population (eg, patients after heart valve surgery); (6) ineligible publication type (ie, conference proceeding, thesis, or commentary); and (7) no full text available. By excluding studies that were not about the content of cardiac rehabilitation, because sufficient literature was already available on this topic, 28 , 29 we were able to perform a more thorough and detailed analysis of needs of patients in cardiac rehabilitation. Full‐text studies were requested by sending an e‐mail to the corresponding author; however, if no contact was set up, the study was excluded. An overview of the search strategy and eligibility criteria using the Starlite framework is provided in Table S3.

Selection of Studies

Two review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all potentially eligible studies from the systematic searches and forward citation search (N.K. and D.C.). If there was any doubt about the eligibility of a study, the article went ahead to full‐text screening. Two review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) independently screened all full‐text studies. Differences of opinion were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer (D.C.).

Data Collection

Descriptive data were extracted by the first review author (N.K.), including lead author, year of publication, country, study aim, methodological approach, sampling strategy, data collection methods, data analysis methods, population, sample size, type of cardiac rehabilitation, and type of outcomes. Data on primary outcomes were not extracted but at once independently coded by 2 review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.). The data collection was not limited to specific sections of the studies to avoid losing concepts and context. 30

Quality Appraisal

To gain insight into methodological quality, we appraised all included studies using the Critical Appraisal Skills Program for qualitative studies (www.casp‐uk.net). One reviewer (N.K.) independently appraised all studies. A second reviewer (R.v.O.) checked the appraisal and provided feedback. In a consensus meeting, the appraisal was discussed, and the 2 reviewers reached consensus.

Data Analysis and Synthesis

We started our meta‐ethnography by formulating the research question (phase 1). Subsequently, 3 authors (N.K., R.v.O., and D.C.) identified and selected all studies (phase 2). Two review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) independently read and reread the included studies to gain familiarity with the data (phase 3). To determine how findings in the studies were related (phase 4), 2 review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) independently identified and in vivo coded text sections using analysis software (Atlas.ti, version 23.1.1; Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin, Germany) (Data S1). Next, both review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) independently juxtaposed and listed the in vivo codes and looked for patterns and structures in the data. The lists were then restructured by preliminary third‐order constructs. We used the descriptions of Britten et al 31 to distinguish between first‐, second‐, and third‐order constructs. First‐order constructs were “everyday understandings of study participants (ie, patients, significant others, and cardiac rehabilitation professionals),” and second‐order constructs were “constructs of the original author(s) on the understanding of the study participants.” We considered our new interpretations of these understandings as third‐order constructs. The first‐, second‐, and third‐order constructs were used to synthesize lines of argument that provided a rigor understanding of the needs of patients regarding cardiac rehabilitation.

In multiple discussion meetings, the lists and preliminary third‐order constructs were discussed by 2 review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) until consensus about the preliminary third‐order constructs was reached. To translate the findings of the studies into one another (phase 5), 2 review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) cocreated a mind map (Data S2). This mind map was, subsequently, used to analyze all first‐ and second‐order construct studies for differences (ie, refutational translations) and overlap (ie, reciprocal translations) between studies. When organizing the data in the matrix, 2 review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) reworded and reordered the preliminary third‐order constructs. In 3 consensus meetings, all authors discussed the preliminary third‐order constructs and found consensus on final third‐order constructs. The following data were used in the consensus meetings: study characteristics, quotes, and original author(s)’ own words about patients' needs, and the preliminary third‐order constructs. In the fourth consensus meeting, all authors synthesized translations (phase 6) in line with the lines‐of‐argument meta‐ethnographic synthesis, originally described by Noblit and Hare. 21 By creating preliminary lines of argument, we constructed an overarching explanation of patients' needs regarding cardiac rehabilitation. These preliminary lines of argument were later used to structure the first draft of the Results section. Throughout the writing process, the preliminary lines of argument were reworded and reordered into final lines of argument by all authors until consensus was reached. Two review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) returned 1 last time to the original studies to retrieve illustrative quotes supporting our reinterpretations. To express the synthesis (phase 7), we ultimately integrated our findings into a conceptual model with recommendations for cardiac rehabilitation professionals to improve creating individualized treatment plans. The first draft of the conceptual model was created by 2 authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) and then critiqued, improved, and completed by all authors.

Confidence in Evidence

Confidence in the lines of argument was rated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation–Certainty of Qualitative Evidence approach (hereafter referred to as Cerqual). 32 The Cerqual provides a transparent method for rating the confidence of evidence from reviews of qualitative research, and indicating this confidence to end users, such as guideline panels or decision makers. 32 Four domains were rated: the methodological limitations of the studies, the coherence of the data, the adequacy of the data, and the relevance of the data. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 The overall confidence was rated as high (highly likely that the line of argument is a reasonable representation of the phenomenon under study), moderate (likely), low (possible), or very low (unclear). The preliminary rating was applied by 3 review authors (N.K., R.v.O., and D.C.) and finalized after consensus with all authors. Ultimately, we constructed a Summary of Qualitative Findings table and Evidence Profile with details on the lines of argument and the confidence ratings. 37

Trustworthiness

We elaborate on the trustworthiness of our study on the basis of 5 quality criteria. 38 Credibility was improved by including studies using a variety of data collection methods, such as interviews, observations, and open‐ended questionnaires. In addition, 2 review authors (N.K. and R.v.O.) led the data analysis and all authors, including a patient representative (M.V.), engaged in translating and synthesizing the lines of argument using consensus meetings. Transferability was enhanced by providing thick descriptions of the lines of argument. Dependability and confirmability were increased by providing an audit trail and publishing all databases as appendices. Reflexivity was considered throughout the data analysis by continuous reflection on our own presumptions and prejudices. We purposefully composed a multidisciplinary research group to include multiple perspectives on patient needs regarding cardiac rehabilitation. N.K. and R.v.O. are working as physical therapists in cardiac rehabilitation. D.C. is a nonpracticing exercise therapist, formally working as therapist in cardiac rehabilitation, now working as a guideline developer and policy officer. M.V. is a certified patient representative and has cardiac disease. T.E. is an exercise physiologist and senior researcher. P.v.d.W. is a nonpracticing physical therapist, employed as full professor in allied health care. The professional background of the involved authors might lead to an increased focus on patients' needs regarding exercise‐based cardiac rehabilitation needs, which was “bracketed” throughout the data analysis. Furthermore, the analysts (N.K. and R.v.O.) marked in the mind map and matrix (phase 5) which third‐order constructs were related to exercise and which were not. This provided insight into the possible bias of the review authors to only identify exercise‐related needs. Furthermore, considering the bias due to professional background, the involvement of the patient representative also created a tendency to present additional patient needs based on firsthand experiences. All authors recognized this tendency, resulting in procedures of the analysts (N.K. and R.v.O.) to critically examine the traceability of patient needs to data of included studies. All authors assume cardiac rehabilitation to be clinically effective and cost effective for patients with coronary artery diseases and heart failure. 1

Patient and Public Involvement

One patient representative (M.V.) engaged in the data analysis. During consensus meetings, M.V. helped translate and synthesize the findings by focusing primarily on staying true to the patient perspective (ie, first‐order constructs). During study reporting, M.V. helped to clearly articulate the lines of argument so patients could understand them.

We identified 5711 unique records through database searching (Figure 1). In addition, we identified 792 records through the forward citation search. Study Characteristics

Details of study characteristics of the included studies are provided in Table S5. The 94 studies included data from, in total, 2021 participants (1731 patients, 185 significant others, and 105 cardiac rehabilitation professionals). The participants represented 19 countries around the world from both high‐income countries and developing countries (Figure 2). The population consisted of patients with coronary artery disease with and without surgery, patients with acute or chronic heart failure, and patients at risk for or with surgically treated thoracic aortic dissection. We also included studies that aimed to study the perspectives of a specific underrepresented groups, such as younger people, 41 , 42 , 95 older people, 61 , 104 women,* people living in rural areas, 56 and cultural minorities.† We found a large variety in applied methodological approaches: phenomenology (n=11), hermeneutic (n=8), randomized controlled trial with a qualitative evaluation (n=5), grounded theory (n=5), mixed methods (n=4), ethnography (n=3), critical realist (n=2), narrative (n=2), naturalistic (n=2), case study (n=1), feminist (n=1), framework (n=1), generic (n=1), hybrid (n=1), intervention study (n=1), multimethod design (n=1), and pragmatist paradigm (n=1). The remaining articles (n=44) reported no specific methodological approach. Data were collected using individual, dyadic, or focus group interviews; observations; questionnaires; and chat sessions. We found a wide variety of cardiac rehabilitation programs, such as supervised and unsupervised; center based, home based, or web based; and adapted programs for specific patient populations.

Methodological Quality

Details of methodological quality appraisal of the included studies are provided in Data S3. Overall, the aims were clearly stated, and the use of qualitative methodology was appropriate. The research design was not reported or not appropriate in 24 studies (26%). Recruitment strategies were appropriate in 65 studies (69%); however, 16 studies (17%) did not report recruitment strategies, and 13 studies (14%) lacked explanation why selected participants were the most appropriate. Data collection methods were well explained and appropriate to achieve the research aims. The relationship between researcher and participants was not reported in 72 studies (77%). Lack of relationship information is not necessarily a risk; however, we did find an increased risk of methodological issues due to a specific researcher interviewing specific participants (n=8) or addressing sensitive topics (n=3) in 11 studies (12%). Ethical issues were adequately taken into consideration in 34 studies (36%); the other studies only reported approval from an ethics committee (n=59) or did not report ethical considerations at all (n=1). Data analysis was performed rigorously in 76 studies (81%) and findings were clearly stated in 84 studies (89%).

Patients Need Safety but Not Overprotection

We have found, with high confidence in evidence, that patients need safety but not overprotection. Heart disease, whether acutely started, comes with an increased awareness of death. Having a heart disease is perceived as “life‐threatening,” 40 , 41 as an “end of a vital life,” 75 or as “feeling like dying.” 87 Patients feel vulnerable and mistrust their body. They are cautious and alert to body signals that might indicate renewed heart sickness. As a result, patients feel, among other things, insecure, angry, anxious, depressed, or frustrated.

“Fear of death had diminished among the patients and was expected to fade away. But still 4 to 6 months after myocardial infarction, some patients expressed feelings of irritation, anger, impatience, restlessness, being easily moved to tears and feeling depressed.” 85

In the context of safety, patients need support to manage symptoms. Symptoms, such as chest pain or breathlessness, remind patients of their cardiac event and make them feel unsafe. Patients need access to a medical specialist to discuss their symptoms and adapt their treatment. Furthermore, patients need medication guidance by a general practitioner to “have a dialogue regarding symptoms, side effects of medication, and quality of life.” 114

“I just sat at home and I didn't move or get up and when I got a pain, I thought it was ‘goodnight.’ I would wake up with the pain and think, ‘I am definitely a goner.’ It was really terrible. Then I went to the classes and they were amazing. I realized every pain wasn't a heart attack.” 97

In addition, patients need supervision during exercise of professionals who are “able to deal with any complications of the activity, for example, chest pain or breathlessness.” 128 Interestingly, other patients feel no need for cardiac rehabilitation anymore after symptoms are alleviated. 103

Another aspect of safety is patients' need for a safe environment. Patients feel safe in an environment where medical help is present. It is a place where patients can try out themselves during exercise and where they can explore exercise limits. Supervision by professionals during exercise therapy contributes to a safe environment by monitoring progress and intervening if needed. Therefore, patients prefer supervised cardiac rehabilitation over community groups. Monitoring blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen seems to play an important role in patients' sense of safety, explained in terms such as “they keep an eye on you” 91 and “the numbers were like holding on to life.” 83

“… when we go to the exercise classes … they check your blood pressure, the heart rate monitor watch is connected, plus they check your oxygen … there are 2 bikes and 4 treadmills … there are 6 to 8 people there, and every minute the physiotherapists, rehabilitation nurse, and the other nurses check us, they are all very attentive.”

Furthermore, patients feel that cardiac rehabilitation is provided in a safe environment when the right equipment is available, such as professional ergometer or an automated external defibrillator. In short, patients need professionals, monitoring, and equipment to feel safe when pushing their exercise limits. Interestingly, patients also feel safe during supervised telerehabilitation at home. 78 However, some patients do take actions to ensure safety at home, for example, by unlocking the door before starting exercises so that emergency workers can enter quickly if necessary. 100

One more aspect of safety is patients' need for clear exercise instructions. Patients are uncertain about “safe exertion levels” 74 and “damage to the heart” 97 when exercising. Exercise and being physically active sometimes feel like a risk for patients. However, “being a couch potato” 97 and doing nothing is not an option either. Therefore, patients need clear exercise instructions for times when they are not under professional supervision, not being monitored, or have less equipment.

“They said that the workout felt safe, that they received clear instructions and had the opportunity to ask questions and receive advice.” 100

There is clearly a need for safety; however, patients also experience overprotection by significant others. Like the patients themselves, their immediate family members also fear another cardiac event and, in a worst‐case scenario, even death. Especially exertion and going out alone are often feared by significant others. Overprotection may result from concern that symptoms will return. This is understandable, although it can cause frustrations and arguments. Overprotection by significant others may lead to serious limitations for patients such as reduced freedom and independence. 116 Overprotection by significant others is a common phenomenon and stresses the need to involve significant others in cardiac rehabilitation (see next line of argument).

“There was, however, a fine line [for family members] between keeping patients safe and overprotection, which cardiac rehabilitation professionals felt may negatively impact the long‐term physical activity engagement of the patient.” 52

Patients Need Significant Others to Be Involved

We have found, with high confidence in evidence, that patients need significant others to be involved in cardiac rehabilitation. Interestingly, patients need support from their significant others, when, at the same time, patients need support for their significant others. In this case, significant others are partners, family members, caregivers, friends, neighbors, employers, or workmates. If partners are involved, there may also be a need for care for both patient and partner at the same time, such as relationship counseling or sexual health care.

First, patients need support from significant others. Their support is crucial to enable patients to spend attention, energy, and time on their recovery. Support includes various activities, for example, helping with self‐care, motivating to participate in cardiac rehabilitation, minimizing stress, and encouraging to engage in physical activities. Moreover, significant others can play a vital role in addressing the cardiac event in the sense of being understood by a loved one and having someone to speak to.

“It would have been worth everything to be able to gather the family and tell them what had happened and why you react in different ways and that you really talk based on our situation and don't suppress it … to also let the kids and family know what has happened and how you should act.” 42

Significant others are, according to patients, able to “cheer me up” 68 and “lift my spirits.” 105 The support of family is considered essential, hypothetically lifesaving: “If I didn't have a family, I would probably have jumped off the bridge or something.” 103 Also, in a practical sense, participation in cardiac rehabilitation needs to be supported by family. For instance, women in developing countries are supposed to manage the household and, therefore, cannot be missed in their daily tasks, leaving them no time to participate in cardiac rehabilitation.

In contrast, patients may explicitly wish not to involve their significant others in cardiac rehabilitation. Reasons were not to burden their family with, for example, emotions or shocking news.

“Despite a large and caring family, this individual felt she could share things with other heart attack survivors that her family did not want to hear or could not cope with hearing.” 65

Importantly, patients need cardiac rehabilitation professionals to involve partners in cardiac rehabilitation. Partners otherwise feel misunderstood, ignored, or forgotten. Relationships sometimes change and couples are often afraid to engage in sexual activities again. Therefore, there may be a need for couples therapy or sexual health care.

“Changes to patients' and partners' relationship quality should be assessed within cardiac rehabilitation programming and action taken to support patients and partners in strengthening their relationship.” 121

Second, patients need support for significant others. Significant others may feel passed over and frustrated when they are not being involved in caregiving. Significant others suggest they need to gain knowledge about the disease, receive education about what support they may provide and regularly accompany their significant other during cardiac rehabilitation. Importantly, significant others may also be emotionally affected by their significant other's disease, which sometimes requires psychological help for themselves. Significant others, in general, are eager to effectively perform their role as carers and would like to be empowered to do so.

“Well, I am completely cut off. No, I haven't really been involved in anything. Neither this nor that … I'm only a name in the case book as the next of kin to (name of the patient), except for that I don't exist. … I should have needed to talk to a physician or registered nurse to get to know how (name) prognosis is looking for the future, what you can expect, how he is going to be able to manage his work, maybe what I should be observant of.” 42

Patients Need Support From People With Whom They Can Identify

We have found, with moderate confidence in evidence, that patients need peer support from people with whom they can identify. Peer support is considered very important and regularly led to “friendship” 86 , 105 and “camaraderie.” 57 , 117 , 120 Cardiac rehabilitation is typically provided in a group setting. Connecting with peers and sharing experiences from a cardiac event is seen as greatly beneficial, particularly when patients can identify with their peers. Identification means connecting with patients of the same age, fitness level, sex, or disease. After finishing cardiac rehabilitation, patients miss their peers and wish to find a like‐minded group of peers in their community.

There is a need for peers of the same age. It is easy for patients to connect with peers who are in the same stage of life, because they are “living at the same pace” 120 and recognize family and work situations. A disparity in age of the participants sometimes leads to loss of connection. For example, younger patients have different information needs than older patients. This contributes to a feeling of not fitting into the program, for example, because education is about frailty and young patients do not feel frail.

“I just stopped going … I don't know, I didn't feel right. Don't get me wrong, I was in my 40s. But there are a lot—the people that [were] in the rehab bit—they were all older than me. And I felt ‘I'm an old woman.’ I didn't want to feel like that.” 120

Moreover, patients need peers of the same fitness level. Being with these peers is perceived as motivating, challenging, and fun. Being with peers of a much higher level is sometimes perceived as frustrating and confrontational.

“One individual expressed that his fitness level was much greater than his peers in cardiac rehabilitation and indicated that he preferred to exercise alone so as not to be limited by the abilities of a group.” 61

Furthermore, patients need peers of the same sex. Female patients appreciate a women‐only approach that “symbolized a protected space where women could share their experiences and talk about their health issues openly and receive important cardiac health information unique to women.” 117 Being with women is perceived as comfortable, relaxed, and safe. Peers of the male sex are considered as competitive, fast‐paced, and, sometimes, intimidating. In contrast, though less frequently read, “same sex did not matter to participation.” 107 The perspective of men on a men‐only approach is not explored in the included studies.

“[Exercising at the program] wasn't like being in a gym, you know, [where] you walk in and you're really worried about how you look or you're really worried about the whole getting in shape or body image thing. It was more like just people supporting each other. [It was] very comfortable and very positive that way; I think with women we were sort of more relaxed about exercise in general. I don't think anyone in the group was in particularly good shape, so it was all pretty relaxed. And there was no competition or anything like that.” 107

In addition, patients need peers with the same disease. Patients feel understood when sharing experiences, talking about challenges in daily life, and discussing symptoms with peers with the same disease. Having the same disease helps put things in perspective, for example, by seeing that “many were much worse off.” 105 For distinct patient populations, such as patients with heritable thoracic aortic dissections, 123 peers with the same disease are lacking.

“It would be nice if there was someone who had heart failure themselves, who could walk around at the hospital unit and talk about how it really is. […] I have missed a person, I have realized afterwards, a peer‐patient that could have told me about how it is to have heart failure.” 51

In contrast to the finding that patients need peer support, there is also the perspective of patients who feel resistance to engage in group training and dislike being in a group of people. Others feel shy or embarrassed, for example, because they are not comfortable using fitness equipment. Another reason for not participating in peer support is the preference to train independently and autonomously with telerehabilitation.

“This [group training] can create a discouraging environment, prompting embarrassment over feeling like a frail, elderly person, feeling physically inferior to older participants, and feeling intimidated by participants who were more comfortable using exercise machines.” 99

Patients Need Personalized Care

We have found, with moderate confidence in evidence, that patients need personalized care. A prerequisite for providing personalized care is meeting patients' need for personal attention. Patients value an individual, personal conversations in which cardiac rehabilitation professionals listen attentively. Patients picture the conversation as a face‐to‐face meeting, where dialogue and discussion are allowed. In such a conversation, patients can express their needs, and cardiac rehabilitation professionals can tailor their personal care plan. Personal contact strengthens cardiac rehabilitation professionals to encourage patients to participate in cardiac rehabilitation and to persevere with it.

“The young patients described that they needed to discuss with [the cardiac rehabilitation professional] about their personal illness experience and about what happened to them. They needed someone who would devote time to attentively listen, to show interest in their story, and to offer a tailored care plan.” 42

After providing personal attention, cardiac rehabilitation professionals can meet patients' need for personalized goals. Patients want to set meaningful goals, monitor their goals, and celebrate achieved goals together with their significant others and cardiac rehabilitation professionals. It seems important to shape personal goals together, because a cardiac rehabilitation professional who imposes goals can be perceived as a “dictator, you know, do this, do this, do this.” 91 Specific goals, such as “weight control or increased physical fitness,” are more motivating than generic goals, such as “performing a set of exercises.” 46

“A theme identified in both facilitator and patient discussions was that although the program is a standardized program, facilitators and patients felt the individualized approach to goal setting was an important component of the program.” 91

Furthermore, after providing personal attention, cardiac rehabilitation professionals should meet patients' need for aftercare. Patients experience a gap in care after discharge from the hospital or after stopping cardiac rehabilitation, in which they feel insecure and experience a lack of support. The reasons why patients need aftercare vary: Some patients feel lonely and forgotten, others want help to stay motivated, and again others seek ongoing information and support. Suggestions for aftercare are “follow‐up phone calls, assessments, or exercise programs,” 57 and “an occasional review to reassure that their condition had not deteriorated.” 70 With good aftercare, patients feel treated like a person rather than a number. It is seen as genuine commitment from cardiac rehabilitation professionals.

“I think a phone call would be really great—especially for the older people and widows like me—as we need this as you feel forgotten after rehab.” 105

Moreover, patients value the possibility of easy access to cardiac rehabilitation professionals, for instance, by a free‐call card, providing the patient with a telephone number they can always call if they feel the need. Specifically, the engagement of the general practitioner is highly valued. The general practitioner is expected to show interest in patients' recovery after discharge from the hospital and can play a vital role in motivating patients to participate in cardiac rehabilitation.

Sometimes, the provision of personalized care is challenging because of patients' need for culture‐sensitive professionals. Cardiac rehabilitation professionals are at risk for providing inappropriate or insensitive recommendations when patients are not fully understood from a cultural perspective. Patients need, among other things, personalized nutritional advice that can be integrated into family life, opportunities to wear traditional clothing during exercise, and access to information in understandable language. Patients who identify within specific cultural groups—for example, African, 90 Gujarati Hindu, 126 Moroccan and Turkish, 112 Punjabi Sikh, 73 , 74 South Asian, 44 , 47 , 89 , 124 and immigrants in Canada, 108 — consider culture‐sensitive cardiac rehabilitation professionals to be truly person centered.

“… a relief dietitian had done [an educational talk] and didn't know much about the Asian diet and suggested dhal, and that is seen as what poor people eat and a bit of a slur that she was saying that they should eat that.” 124

Cultural sensitivity also means that health care providers do not judge patients. For example, patients may feel stigmatized when they receive lifestyle advice, for instance, when cardiac rehabilitation professionals ignore that lifestyle changes are hindered by social pressure and that patients may have less influence over their lifestyle than expected.

Patients Need Help to Redesign a Meaningful Future

We have found, with high confidence in evidence, that patients need help to redesign a meaningful future. First, patients need to regain control over their life. Specifically, patients do not want to be dependent on others in performing self‐care and daily activities. Being dependent on others is considered very problematic and patients talk about being dependent as a “fate akin to death.” 66 Patients typically want to recover fast, in order to pick up their normal life. Cardiac rehabilitation helps patients to regain control, practice becoming independent of others, and get a grip on life by providing education, exercises, and psychological support.

“Sometimes I thought about skipping rehab, but I showed up every day because I need my independence, that feeling that I have control over my life.” 67

Later, patients’ need to reflect on their recovery emerges. They experience that cardiac rehabilitation is a place to reflect, together with cardiac rehabilitation professionals and peers, on uncertainty and concerns about recovery. These reflections provide comfort, relief, and an opportunity to set goals for the rest of their recovery.

“Most of the participants described the meaningfulness of rehabilitation in providing a time and place to increase awareness of one's own needs and thoughts, set goals, and evaluate and achieve them.” 43

At the same time, patients need to deal with a changed identity. For example, patients struggle to fulfill their family role as mother or father, when others feel they are failing as breadwinners now that they are no longer able to work. They feel useless and lack meaning in life. Moreover, patients can experience (social) isolation, as they are not able to take on their work, their hobbies, or other social activities. In cardiac rehabilitation, patients highly value social care supports to redesign family life and contact with friends and family, and vocational rehabilitation to redesign daily routines and work identity.

“Patients discussed how […] they had lost a lot of confidence and felt unable to do tasks and activities they had previously undertaken with no problems and were, therefore, not the same person they were previously.” 64

In contrast, other patients do not need help to redesign a meaningful future at all.

“Thoughts, what thoughts of the future? The engine runs as it runs. What else is there to think about, about the future? You just have to go with it.” 126

Conceptual Model

We have integrated the 5 lines of argument into a conceptual model with recommendations for cardiac rehabilitation professionals (Figure 4). Our conceptual model is based on the figure of Brown et al 133 that shows the process of creating individualized treatment plans based on patient assessment, delivery method, core components, and high program quality.

Conceptual model illustrating the relationship between needs and recommendations on the one hand and the process of creating an individualized treatment plan on the other.

Two examples: (1) When assessing the patient, it is recommended to involve significant others and facilitate personalized care. (2) When providing psychosocial support, it is recommended to involve significant others, facilitate personalized care, and facilitate patients in redesigning their future.

Our first recommendation is to create a safe cardiac rehabilitation environment in the context of selecting a delivery method and providing aerobic exercise training, strength training, and physical activity counseling. Cardiac rehabilitation professionals should consider selecting a delivery method that the patient perceives as safe. In addition, physical therapists should think about supporting patients in managing symptoms; providing clear exercise instructions; monitoring vital parameters such as blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation when necessary; and addressing overprotection by significant others. Second, we recommend involving significant others when assessing patients, selecting a delivery method, and providing all core components of cardiac rehabilitation. Cardiac rehabilitation professionals should consider involving significant others during patient assessment to improve shared decision making. In addition, cardiac rehabilitation professionals should think about involving significant others in selecting a delivery method that is acceptable for both patients and their significant others. Furthermore, cardiac rehabilitation professionals should take into account involving significant others in all core components, especially in nutritional and physical activity counseling, because coping with nutrition and physical activity involves lifestyle changes that affect both patients and their significant others. Moreover, cardiac rehabilitation professionals should think about supporting significant others themselves, especially for psychosocial management. Our third recommendation is to facilitate peer support in the context of providing nutritional counseling, weight management and body composition, aerobic exercise training, strength training, and physical activity counseling. Dietitians and physical therapists should consider creating groups of patients with others they can identify with, such as people of the same age, fitness level, sex, or disease. Facilitating peer support seems especially important when these core components are delivered virtually or remotely. Fourth, we recommend cardiac rehabilitation professionals to facilitate personalized care in the context of assessing patients, selecting a delivery method, and providing all core components of cardiac rehabilitation. When assessing patients, cardiac rehabilitation nurses should think about facilitating individual conversations where patients feel listened to for setting personalized goals. When providing virtual or remote cardiac rehabilitation, cardiac rehabilitation professionals should consider arranging one‐on‐one counseling with personal attention. In all core components, cardiac rehabilitation professionals should take into account ensuring proper aftercare and involving culturally sensitive professionals. Our fifth recommendation is to facilitate patients in redesigning their future in the context of providing nutritional counseling, physical activity counseling, and psychosocial management. Dietitians, physical therapists, and psychologists should consider supporting patients in regaining control over their lives, reflecting on their recovery, and dealing with a changed identity.

DISCUSSION

The current systematic review and meta‐ethnographic synthesis aimed to study the needs of patients with coronary artery disease, heart failure, or thoracic aortic dissection regarding cardiac rehabilitation. The 5 lines of argument show that patients need safety but not overprotection, need significant others to be involved, need support from people with whom they can identify, need personalized care, and need help to redesign a meaningful future. Our synthesized findings offer a thorough understanding of patients' needs and provide concrete tools for cardiac rehabilitation professionals to personalize health care and improve uptake and completion of cardiac rehabilitation.

That patients have a need for safety is no surprise. Safety is one of the fundamental human needs and has been extensively studied in the context of health care, 134 social sciences, 135 and even meta‐science. 136 Through the lens of this study, we have found that patients need symptom management in a safe place when exercising with the right professionals and equipment. Then, if symptoms occur, patients need clear exercise limits to improve or prevent symptoms. Interestingly, we have found that safety should be balanced with overprotection by significant others. Overprotective support by partners during cardiac rehabilitation is a well‐known yet understudied phenomenon, with cardiac rehabilitation professionals recommended to train partners in a more effective support strategy when uncertainty is present. 137 It is also important for significant others themselves to be supported by cardiac rehabilitation professionals. A cardiac event has a major impact on, for example, the partners of patients, and they might suffer from distress, anxiety, or depression themselves. 138 Furthermore, it is important for patients, significant others, and cardiac rehabilitation professionals to have a perspective on the future. The gap between what is physically possible at the start of cardiac rehabilitation and what will be possible in the future might feel enormous. By providing personalized goals that are both inspiring and realistic, it is possible to tailor care based future outcomes in patients with coronary artery disease or heart failure. 139 , 140

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of our systematic review with meta‐ethnographic synthesis is the inclusion of data from 94 studies representing 2021 participants. By performing each phase of the data analysis by at least 2 review authors, using 4 consensus meetings, and involving a professional patient representative, we gained a thorough understanding of patients' needs. Furthermore, we used Cerqual to rate the confidence in our lines of argument, which is considered the state‐of‐the‐art approach for a meta‐ethnographic synthesis. 141 , 142 All findings are clearly summarized, transparently documented, systematically assessed, and directly useful for decision making.

However, 4 limitations should be acknowledged. First, our search was limited to English‐language articles. This may have limited our understanding of the needs of patients in non–English‐publishing countries. However, we included articles with a specific focus on underrepresented groups such as the Punjabi Sikh population. 73 , 74 Second, we did not search for the relation between social determinants of health and specific patient needs. There is literature available on this topic 8 , 143 , 144 ; however, during our analysis, this relationship did not emerge. Third, the included articles did not thoroughly use triangulated data collection methods (eg, patient information folders or video recordings) or data analysis methods (eg, discourse analysis or metaphor analysis). This would have led to an increased understanding of how language functions and how meaning is created in the context of patient needs regarding cardiac rehabilitation. Fourth, we did not explicitly describe or analyze how perspectives of patient needs differ between groups (ie, patients, significant others, or cardiac rehabilitation professionals) or health care contexts (ie, resource‐rich, or resource‐limited contexts). Future research could focus on differences in perspectives across groups or between health care contexts to yield more targeted insights for intervention development and policy recommendations.

Future Interventions and Innovations

Findings of our meta‐ethnographic synthesis of needs provide cardiac rehabilitation professionals with opportunities to implement interventions that fulfill patients' needs. To translate these findings to clinical practice, we recommend performing a needs assessment beyond the recommended inventory of personal exercise‐related goals. 16 , 17 This needs assessment may specifically focus on, for example, peer support and aftercare. Subsequently, brainstorming sessions on solutions to practical barriers and real‐time motivational advice via social media platforms can be used for improving peer support, 145 or case managers and informal phone follow‐up for improving aftercare. 146 In addition, innovations such as e‐Health and artificial intelligence can be used to fulfill patients' needs. To illustrate, it could be promising to develop an e‐Health cardiac rehabilitation program for significant others themselves to support coping with their behavioral change, risk factors, and psychosocial well‐being. Moreover, artificial intelligence could be used to train medically safe chatbots for 24‐hour aftercare support.

CONCLUSIONS

Cardiac rehabilitation is a globally applied, thoroughly researched, effective care program for people with heart disease. However, the uptake and completion of cardiac rehabilitation can be further improved. By involving significant others, it is possible to reduce overprotection and encourage a more effective support strategy. This is important to maintain healthy behaviors even after rehabilitation sessions and in the long term. Cardiac rehabilitation professionals can begin adapting cardiac rehabilitation immediately by highlighting a safe place for both patients and significant others, providing personalized care, and helping to redesign a meaningful future. By meeting patients' needs, patients will be more comfortable and engaged throughout their cardiac rehabilitation.
