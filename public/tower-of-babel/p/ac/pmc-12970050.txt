Clinical utility and performance of anti-C1q antibodies for SLE: comparative analysis of three different assays
Authors: Mariana Gonzalez-Treviño, MeLea D Hetrick, Alain Sanchez-Rodriguez, Ali Duarte-Garcia, Anne E Tebo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDespite the reported clinical significance of anti-C1q immunoglobulin G autoantibodies (anti-C1q) for SLE and the development of anti-C1q commercial kits, their performance characteristics in routine patient evaluation remain poorly defined.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, an unselected cohort of 107 patients with suspicion of a systemic autoimmune disease and 102 controls were included and evaluated for anti-C1q autoantibodies with three kits (Bühlmann, Orgentec and Werfen). Patients in the unselected cohort were classified as having SLE or a mimicker condition. Disease activity was assessed using Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)-2K and SLE patients were subclassified based on the presence of lupus nephritis (LN) (history/active). Each kit was assessed for diagnostic performance. The degree of association between anti-C1q and SLEDAI/complement levels was assessed using the Spearman correlation coefficient (rho). Between-kit agreement was evaluated using Fleiss Ƙ.ResultsWerfen had the best performance in discriminating patients with SLE from non-SLE (area under the curve (AUC)=0.76), whereas for the SLE versus mimicker comparison Bühlmann (AUC=0.71) and Werfen (AUC=0.71) exhibited adequate discrimination. Among patients with SLE, all kits exhibited poor discrimination of patients with history of LN versus non-renal SLE, but Orgentec displayed good discrimination for patients with active LN from non-active LN (AUC=0.74). A positive correlation was observed between anti-C1q and SLEDAI scores, whereas a negative correlation was found between anti-C1q and complement C3 and C4 across all kits. Agreement for SLE between the three kits was moderate (Ƙ=0.42).ConclusionAnti-C1q can identify patients with SLE and correlate with global and renal disease activity, highlighting their potential to function as follow-up markers in SLE. Agreement with commercial anti-C1q kits is variable. Further studies are required to harmonise anti-C1q kits for optimal routine patient evaluation.

Introduction

SLE is a multisystemic autoimmune disorder that is characterised by autoantibody-mediated tissue damage.1 It is estimated to affect ~100/100 000, with approximately one fifth developing lupus nephritis (LN).2 3 The recognition of autoantibodies in SLE is of utmost importance, as these serve as biomarkers to identify individuals at risk of developing SLE, aid in monitoring disease activity in established SLE and are associated with specific clinical manifestations.4 To date, hundreds of SLE-associated autoantibodies have been identified; however, the performance characteristics of these autoantibodies in routine clinical and laboratory practice remain to be established.46

Anti-C1q immunoglobulin G (IgG) autoantibodies (anti-C1q) are directed towards C1q, the initiation molecule of the classical complement pathway.7 It is estimated that the prevalence of anti-C1q in the general population ranges between 4% and 18%,8 but its positivity significantly increases in certain infectious9 10 and autoimmune conditions,11 12 reaching a 100% positivity rate in patients with hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis.13 14 These autoantibodies, which bind to the collagen-like region of C1q,15 16 have received special attention in SLE, with an estimated prevalence of 28–60%.17 18 Additionally, there is evidence hinting that they might play a pathogenic role in LN by inducing overt renal disease when anti-C1q deposits together with C1q in the glomeruli of patients with SLE.19 Studies suggest that these autoantibodies could have clinical utility in identifying patients with proliferative LN and might function as follow-up markers in SLE, since rising titres can predict renal flares and correlate with an increase in global disease activity.2025

The main barrier to implementing anti-C1q autoantibody testing in the clinical management of patients with SLE is the lack of standardised laboratory assays.18 Several commercial assays are available for the detection of anti-C1q antibodies, most of which are based on the ELISA technology and use high-ionic strength conditions to inhibit low affinity binding immune-complexes to identify real anti-C1q rather than C1q binding to immune-complexes.7 18 26 Nonetheless, none of these kits have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) due to a lack of prospective studies that evaluate their performance and standardised immunoassays.18 27 Furthermore, studies that systematically compare anti-C1q antibody assays from different manufacturers are lacking.7 28

In this investigation, we conducted a cross-sectional study to assess the diagnostic performance of three commercial anti-C1q assays using different positivity thresholds in discriminating patients with SLE from other conditions that triggered autoimmune workup (mimickers) and healthy donors (controls). Additionally, we evaluated the association of having positive anti-C1q antibodies with LN (history and active LN), disease activity and hypocomplementaemia within patients with SLE.

Methods

Study design and participants

We performed a cross-sectional study. An unselected cohort of 107 patients with suspicion of a systemic autoimmune disease (ie, with an order for autoimmune workup (anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), C3, C4, anti-Smith, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) and/or anti-glomerular basement membrane (GBM) serology) requested by a provider within the Mayo Clinic from February 2021 to October 2021), and 102 healthy individuals with no reported history of autoimmune, infectious, renal and/or dermatologic conditions from the Mayo Clinic Biobank29 were included in this investigation. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Mayo Clinic (#21–0 13 012). The study protocol is available from the corresponding author on request.

For the unselected cohort, a physician (MGT) blinded to the anti-C1q results performed chart review to extract the demographics and clinical characteristics of the patients. Patients were considered to have an SLE diagnosis if they met the 2019 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR)/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) Classification Criteria.30 For patients with SLE, disease activity was retrospectively assessed using the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI)-2K31 at the time of the anti-C1q determination. Diagnosis ascertainment and assessment of disease activity were performed by a rheumatologist (ASR) blinded to the anti-C1q results.

Additionally, patients with SLE were classified based on the presence of LN in the following manner.

History of LN

Patients were considered to have a history of LN if they met the following criteria: (1) biopsy-proven LN in the presence of positivity for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) or anti-dsDNA antibody or (2) having documented proteinuria (≥500 mg in 24-hour urine collection or protein-to-creatinine ratio ≥0.5) that did not have a better explanation than SLE (eg, excluding patients with diabetes mellitus or other causes of proteinuria who did not have a kidney biopsy to confirm LN) at any time before anti-C1q measurement.

Non-renal SLE

Patients without LN throughout the course of the disease. If a patient with SLE had renal disease other than LN, they were included in this group.

Active LN

Patients with evidence of proteinuria, urinary casts, haematuria and/or pyuria otherwise not explained by another cause (eg, urinary tract infections, etc) at the time of the anti-C1q antibody determination.

Non-active LN

Patients (with or without history of LN) without active renal disease (proteinuria, urinary casts, haematuria and/or pyuria) at the time of the anti-C1q measurement.

LN histological class was classified according to the 2018 revised International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society classification criteria for LN.32

Anti-C1q assays

We assessed three commercially available, ELISA-based anti-C1q test kits: Bühlmann Laboratories AG (CH-4124 Schönenbuch, Switzerland), Orgentec (Mainz, Germany) and Werfen (San Diego, California, USA). All assays had different analytical procedures. In brief, serum samples were diluted in a high ionic strength diluent, and 100 µL of diluted serum was added to microplates containing human C1q coated wells. Microplates were incubated at ambient temperature (20°C–25°C) for either 30 min (Orgentec, Werfen) or 60 min (Bühlmann) and washed three times with kit-provided wash buffer. The plate for each kit was incubated with 100 µL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labelled anti-human IgG conjugate at ambient temperature for 15 min (Orgentec) or 30 min (Bühlmann, Werfen), followed by another wash cycle. The plate was then incubated with 100 µL of 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine chromogen substrate at ambient temperature in the dark for 15 min (Orgentec) or 30 min (Bühlmann, Werfen). The reaction was terminated with 100 µL of a sulfuric acid stop solution, and absorbance was then measured at 450 nm immediately (Bühlmann, Werfen) or after a 5 min incubation (Orgentec). Concentrations were calculated using the appropriate calibration curves. All analytical steps were performed using the automated Dynex DS2 ELISA platform (DYNEX Technologies, Chantilly, Virginia, USA). Each protocol was programmed to adhere to the manufacturer’s specifications. The characteristic analytical features of the different kits and manufacturer’s specific recommended cut-off values are shown in online supplemental table 1.

Statistical analysis

The manufacturers’ recommended cut-off values were verified or modified using sera from healthy individuals (healthy donor cohort). The 95%, 97.5% and 99% reference limits were calculated for each kit and results were used in conjunction with the manufacturer recommendations to identify negative, weak positive and positive interpretive ranges for each assay. Except for the Werfen assay, the cut-off values for the Bühlmann and Orgentec kits were modified to include weak positive ranges, 15–35 U/mL (Bühlmann) and 10–15 U/mL (Orgentec) (online supplemental table 1). The negative cut-off values for all assays were considered low and the upper limits for weak positive range were the high thresholds. Using these interpretive ranges, each kit was assessed for diagnostic performance for the following comparisons: SLE versus non-SLE, SLE versus mimickers, history of LN versus non-renal SLE, active LN versus non-active LN. Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentages. Numerical variables were described as mean and SD. Sensitivity, specificity, positive (PLR) and negative likelihood ratios (NLR) were calculated for each of the comparisons. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated and the area under the curve (AUC) for each assay and comparison was estimated. The reference was a physician diagnosis by medical chart review. In addition, we assessed the degree of association between anti-C1q levels and the SLEDAI score, and anti-C1q levels and the complement levels with the Spearman correlation coefficient (rho) in patients with SLE. Between-kit agreement was assessed using Fleiss Ƙ. A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with the Analyse-it software.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 209 subjects were analysed, 107 from the unselected cohort and 102 from the Mayo Clinic healthy control biobank. From the unselected cohort, 55 (51.4%) patients were classified as having an SLE diagnosis and 52 (48.6%) as mimickers. Mimicker conditions included ANCA-associated vasculitis (n=10, 19.2%), undifferentiated connective tissue disease (n=10, 19.2%), other rheumatic diseases (n=7, 13.5%), anti-GBM (n=2, 3.8%), inflammatory bowel disease (n=2, 3.8%) and other diagnoses (n=21, 40.4%). The flow of participants can be found in online supplemental figure 1.

50 (90.9%), 34 (65.4%) and 48 (47.1%) patients were female in the SLE, mimicker and control groups, respectively. Mean age (SD) was 51 years (17.8) for SLE group, 57 years (15.2) for mimicker group and 40 years (20.2) for the control group. The frequency of positive anti-C1q individuals based on the different assays and cut-off points in each of the study groups is displayed in online supplemental table 2.

Within patients with SLE (n=55), 19 (34.5%) had a history of LN and 36 (65.5%) were classified as non-renal SLE. Regarding renal disease activity, six (10.9%) patients had active LN, whereas 49 (89.1%) presented no renal activity (non-active LN) at the time of the anti-C1q measurement. Among those with active LN, histologic classes were distributed as follows: class IV (n=2), class IV+V (n=1), class III+V (n=1) and class V (n=1); one patient did not undergo kidney biopsy. Discussion

Anti-C1q autoantibody was first recognised as a biomarker in patients with SLE and other conditions associated with hypocomplementaemic states more than 50 years ago.33 Since then, several studies have demonstrated evidence of a pathological role and clinical significance for these antibodies in patients with SLE and other autoimmune diseases.111417 18 In addition, while commercial immunoassays to detect and quantify anti-C1q antibodies are available, none of these have received approval by the US FDA, probably due to lack of standardised laboratory assays demonstrating optimal analytical and clinical performance characteristics.7 18 27 This study identified kit-specific clinical attributes for detecting anti-C1q in patients at risk for SLE with implications for test implementation and interpretations. The Werfen assay displayed the best performance in identifying patients with SLE from both mimickers and the entire non-SLE cohort (mimickers+controls), whereas the Orgentec assay appeared to have the best ability to discriminate patients with LN (history and active) from those with non-renal SLE. Additionally, our findings confirm that anti-C1q levels (using all three assays) are higher in patients with hypocomplementaemia and/or increased SLE disease activity, highlighting their potential use as an adjunct laboratory biomarker for evaluating and managing patients with SLE.

ANA have virtually 100% sensitivity in identifying patients with SLE34 but are also positive in other autoimmune conditions such as systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren syndrome, autoimmune hepatitis and can be present in healthy individuals.35 This underscores the need for biomarkers that are able to discern patients with SLE from those with other autoimmune conditions. In this study, we found that the three anti-C1q commercial test kits had adequate specificity for the identification of patients with SLE when using the high cut-off values recommended by the manufacturers. Notably, discrimination was also good for discerning SLE patients from mimicker conditions, which resembled the most the real-life diagnostic dilemma. Nonetheless, agreement for SLE between kits was moderate; this suboptimal intertest agreement hinders the use of these commercial kits in the real-world clinical settings.

The gold standard for the diagnosis of LN is the kidney biopsy, which provides histopathologic information that helps guide treatment decisions. However, kidney biopsy is an invasive procedure with modest risk of complications, making it unsafe to perform in some situations (eg, unstable patients, patients on anticoagulation). Early identification and treatment are critical in LN as it has been proven that delayed treatment is associated with worse prognosis and a higher risk of progression to end stage renal disease.36 Therefore, specific biomarkers that aid in the identification of LN cases are needed to guide early management decisions. In this study, we found that the Orgentec assay displayed adequate specificity (77.8%) in the identification of individuals with active LN among patients with SLE when using the assays’ high cut-off threshold. This finding is consistent with prior studies that have found anti-C1q to have better performance in the detection of active LN than other biomarkers, including anti-dsDNA.37 However, these results should be interpreted with caution given that, similar to prior studies that have reached this conclusion,20 37 38 the population selection in our study was not representative of the LN diagnostic dilemma (ie, patients with SLE and new onset nephrotic/nephritic syndrome). A recent prospective study on patients with SLE undergoing diagnostic kidney biopsy based on proteinuria found that elevated anti-C1q titres (as determined by the QUANTA Lite ELISA-based assay) at the time of renal biopsy correlated with proliferative LN and that higher anti-C1q levels predicted complete response at 12 months.25 These findings highlight the potential clinical utility of anti-C1q in patients with LN and the importance of standardising anti-C1q kits for widespread clinical usage.

Previous studies have evaluated the performance of the commercial anti-C1q assays assessed in this investigation for the discrimination of SLE and LN. A cross-sectional study reported a Bühlmann assay AUC of 0.642 for distinguishing SLE from healthy donors, which is comparable to our findings for discrimination between the SLE and non-SLE cohorts (AUC 0.661).39 Similarly, a study assessing the performance of the Werfen assay (formerly INOVA) for identifying active LN reported an AUC of 0.62, closely aligning with our results (AUC 0.605).40 In contrast, a retrospective study by Julnuken et al reported a lower prevalence of anti-C1q antibodies in SLE than observed in our cohort when using the Bühlmann (18.2% vs 36.4%) and Orgentec (16.8% vs 23.6%) assays.41 In that same study, the diagnostic performance for identifying active LN was higher than in our analysis, with specificities of 92% versus 68.6% for Bühlmann and 92% versus 75.8% for the Orgentec kit, respectively.41 These discrepancies may be in part attributable to higher cut-off values used to define positivity, as well as differences in patient selection and LN definitions. Collectively, these findings underscore the need for greater harmonisation in study design and cut-off thresholds to support the widespread clinical use of anti-C1q testing.

Beyond differences in study design, technical factors intrinsic to the assays themselves may also contribute to variability in performance across the three anti-C1q kits. Currently available assays for anti-C1q antibody detection lack adequate harmonisation, likely reflecting both the structural complexity of the C1q molecule and insufficient standardisation of clinically relevant autoantigenic targets.42 Given the multiple functional domains of C1q, it is plausible that distinct epitopes are associated with specific disease manifestations, including LN activity and renal flares in SLE.42 43 Vanhecke et al used anti-C1q antibodies derived from patients with SLE in a microarray-based approach to map B-cell epitopes of C1q, identifying C1q A08 (C1q A15–27: GRPGRRGRPGLKG) as the dominant epitope.42 In a subsequent study, this A08 epitope was shown to play a key role in activation of the classical complement pathway and select anti-C1q A08 antibodies were able to inhibit this process.43 An ELISA incorporating the A08 epitope demonstrated improved sensitivity and specificity for active LN compared with conventional anti-C1q assays.27 Moreover, accurate detection of pathogenic anti-C1q IgG also requires high-salt assay conditions to eliminate low-avidity, non-pathogenic IgG binding to C1q; however, this requirement is inconsistently applied across commercial assays.7 These limitations highlight the need for assays that can differentiate pathogenic from non-pathogenic anti-C1q antibodies to improve clinical utility. These findings support the premise that clinically meaningful anti-C1q testing depends on epitope-specific, conformationally sensitive assay design, offering a foundation for future assay harmonisation efforts.

Our study has several limitations. Our results are based on retrospective data, which may inflate the estimates of diagnostic performance, and its cross-sectional design did not allow us to assess the temporality of anti-C1q and its association with LN, hypocomplementaemia and high SLE disease activity. Importantly, although our results suggest adequate specificity for identifying patients with active LN, the patient population in this study was not representative of the typical diagnostic setting for LN (ie, patients with SLE undergoing evaluation for suspected renal involvement). Accordingly, our LN-related findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Well-designed, adequately powered studies are needed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of different commercial anti-C1q assays in identifying active LN, including specific histologic classes. Moreover, not all of the patients included in the cohort undergo renal biopsy (patients without suspicion of LN will not get renal biopsy), which could potentially introduce bias given that the outcome was not assessed in all patients. However, we employed a clinical definition of LN (otherwise unexplained proteinuria), which ameliorated the risk of losing patients with LN that did not undergo biopsy.

This study also has notable strengths. This is the first study to provide a direct comparison of three commercial kits in a single population. We included patients in the comparative group (mimickers) with manifestations of the target condition (SLE), which resembles the real-life diagnostic dilemma (patients with suspicion of autoimmune disease). Lastly, we systematically assessed and provided a head-to-head comparison of three commercial anti-C1q kits and demonstrated that even though all kits have adequate performance in detecting patients with SLE and that higher titres correlated with hypocomplementaemia and high disease activity, the between-assay agreement for detection of SLE was moderate, underscoring the need to standardise commercial kits for widespread use in clinical settings. These findings may inform strategies for achieving greater harmonisation among clinical laboratory assays.

Conclusion

Anti-C1q can identify patients with SLE and correlate with global and renal disease activity, highlighting their potential to function as follow-up markers in SLE. Agreement with commercial anti-C1q kits is variable. Further well-designed studies are required to validate anti-C1q kits for routine patient evaluation, and specifically its association with renal disease activity and specific classes of LN.
