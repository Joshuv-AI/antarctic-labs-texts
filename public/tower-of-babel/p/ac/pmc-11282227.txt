Differences in adult nutritional requirements impact the population growth and survival of two related species of rice leaffolders to produce interspecific differentiation
Authors: Lingwen Ding, Jiawen Guo, Yajun Yang, Yanhui Lu, Xin Xie, Zhongxian Lu, Shuping Wang, Hongxing Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNutrition is a limiting feature of species evolution. The differences in nutritional requirements are the evolutionary result of differential adaptations to environmental changes, explaining differences in their ecological traits. Cnaphalocrocis medinalis and Cnaphalocrocis exigua, two related species of rice leaffolders, have similar morphology and feeding properties but different migration and overwintering behaviors. However, it is unclear whether they have evolved adult nutritional differentiation traits to coexist. To explore this issue, this study examined the effects of carbohydrates and amino acids on their reproductive and demographic parameters. The findings indicate that carbohydrate intake prolonged the longevity and population growth of two rice leaffolders, but amino acid intake promoted egg hatching only. However, nutrient deficiency made it impossible for C. medinalis to reproduce successfully and survive, but it did not affect C. exigua. The population expansion and survival of migratory C. medinalis relied on adult nutritional intake. Conversely, the nutrients necessary for C. exigua overwintering activity mostly came from the storage of larvae. The difference in nutritional requirements for population growth and survival between the two rice leaffolders partially explained their differences in migration and overwintering.Keywords: Rice leaffolders, Adult nutrition, Interspecific differentiation, Reproduction, Longevity, Population expansionSubject terms: Developmental biology, Ecology, Evolution

Introduction

During biological evolution, to survive, some species sacrifice specific properties or abilities while enhancing others, particularly in related species1. Related species of insects often have similar morphological properties, and feed on similar host plants2,3. However, they usually exhibit niche differentiation, leading to significant differences in life-history traits to limit competition (e.g. feeding preferences and oviposition selection)4,5. Changes in nutrition have substantial consequences for the expression and evolution of life-history traits and demographic and evolutionary processes6. The differences in nutrient acquisition among related species is particularly clear in the differentiation of host plants5. For example, Helicoverpa assulta larvae are oligophagous, but the relative species, Helicoverpa armigera, is a generalist7,8, as are Heortia vitessoides and Ostrinia furnacalis9,10. Moreover, while the larvae of related species have the same feeding habits, their adults usually differ considerably in diet. For example, Eucryptorrhynchus scrobiculatus adults required petioles and annual branches, and Eucryptorrhynchus brandti fed on the trunk11. Compared to larvae with limited mobility, niche differentiation of adults can limit competition for resources among species4,12. Trophic niche differentiation is crucial in the resource allocation of sympatrically related species adults13. Clarifying the differences in nutrient intake among related species contributes to understanding the mechanisms of coexistence and evolution among related species. Targeted prevention and control strategies can be developed based on nutritional needs.

Adult access to nutrients is a major cause of population outbreaks in some lepidopteran insects14,15. Most adult lepidopterans require nectar to supplement their nutrition, and insufficient adult nutrition reduces adult longevity, mating behavior, and fecundity, such as in Cydia pomonella and Grapholita molesta16,17. Sugars and free amino acids are the primary nutrients in flower nectar7,18. The majority of nectar is composed of sucrose, but the types and contents of amino acids vary by species19. Some Lepidoptera insects showed a preference for sugars or several amino acids20,21, including sucrose, glucose, fructose, maltose, inositol, and lysine, which have feeding-stimulating effects on H. armigera7. For closely related species of Lepidoptera, in resource-limited environments, adapting to adapt to coexisting environments through differentiation in nutritional requirements may be necessary. For example, H. armigera and its close relative H. assulta had nearly opposite preferences for amino acid, H. assulta favored lysine, Glutamate, arginine, trypophan, and serine, while H. armigera favored lysine, arginine, phenylalanine, isoleucine, glycine, tyrosine, and proline7,8. However, studies on the differences in nutritional requirements of lepidoptera insects mostly focus on the larval stage, and most studies only characterize one species22,23. Little is currently known about the differences in carbohydrate and amino acid requirements among related adult Lepidoptera species and their impact on the population differentiation.

Rice leaffolders are significant pests in rice fields, posing a serious threat to rice production24,25. Cnaphalocrocis medinalis (Guenée) and Cnaphalocrocis exigua (Butler), are two related species of rice leaffolders, and their morphological and damage properties are extremely similar26. C. medinalis is one of the most destructive migratory rice pests, causing extensive damage in most rice-growing areas in China27. Unlike C. medinalis, C. exigua does not have migratory habits, and they are primarily found in rice fields in southwestern China28,29. C. exigua can overwinter in its habitat, and when the C. medinalis immigrate in, the niches of the two species in the field overlap partially and produce joint damage, making their prevention and control extremely difficult25. Previous research has found that the nutritional intake of adult C. medinalis was closely associated with their longevity and fecundity30. Carbohydrate (fructose, glucose, and sucrose) intake increased the longevity and fecundity of moths, but the intake of amino acids (a mixture of alanine, arginine, glycine, lysine, proline, and serine) by moths did no impact longevity, fecundity, ovarian development, or population growth31. However, the nutritional requirements of C. exigua moths and their impact on the population are unclear, as are whether there is trophic niche differentiation between the two species of adults. As both reproduction and migration require energy consumption, nutritional supplementation during the adult stage is essential for migratory insects30. The differences in migration and overwintering traits of the two rice leaffolders may cause differences in adult nutritional requirements. It is necessary to understand the coexistence mechanism between two rice leaffolders moths and the population differentiation mechanism in species evolution by examining the differences in adult nutritional requirements. Moreover, to improve the natural pest suppression of the rice agroecosystem, planting flowering plants rich in nectar around rice fields has been promoted in many countries to enhance the number of natural enemies and reduce pesticide use32,33. When integrating flowering plants into rice fields as natural enemies and food sources, the population dynamics of the rice leaffolders may be impacted. Therefore, it is necessary to understand their nutritional requirements to select flowering plants.

To clarify the differences in nutritional requirements between C. exigua and C. medinalis moths, this study chose different carbohydrate (maltose, sucrose, fructose, glucose, and inositol) and amino acid (alanine, proline, serine, glycine, lysine, and arginine) that have feeding stimulating effects or affect reproduction and longevity in some Lepidoptera insects according to previous research to characterize the effects of this nutrient intake on the reproduction and survival of these two rice leaffolders. Further, this study compared their differences in nutritional preferences, necessities and population dynamics. These findings will help to analyze the traits of trophic niche differentiation during their adult stage, and provide a theoretical basis for improving the competitive co-evolution mechanism between two rice leaffolders and formulating targeted control strategies.

Results

Differences in mating behavior between C. exigua and C. medinalis moths feeding on different nutrients

The mating behavior of two rice leaffolders depended on species, nutrient treatment, and their interactions (Fig. 1 and Table 1). When given honey solution, there was no significant difference in mating percentage between the two leaffolders, but the mating frequency of C. medinalis was significantly higher (Figs. 1, 2). However, when given other nutrient solutions, the mating percentage (excluding maltose) and mating frequency of C. exigua were significantly higher than C. medinalis (Figs. 1, 2). The mating behavior of the two species had different nutritional requirements. Feeding on distilled water did not lower the mating percentage (χ2 = 30.368, df = 12, P = 0.002) and mating frequency (χ2 = 65.243, df = 12, P < 0.001) of C. exigua, but honey solution reduced its mating behavior (Fig. 1). The mating behavior of two rice leaffolders differed in carbohydrate and amino acid requirements. The mating percentage of C. exigua moths consuming various carbohydrates and amino acids were not significantly different, but supplies of each type of carbohydrate (except inositol) led to a higher mating frequency compared to honey solution (Fig. 1). This implies that C. exigua can complete mating even when hungry (consuming only distilled water). Unlike C. exigua, the mating percentage and mating frequency of C. medinalis feeding on distilled water was significantly reduced compared to those feeding on honey solution (Fig. 1), indicating that nutrient intake is necessary for C. medinalis to mate. The mating percentage of C. medinalis consuming maltose was maximal (76.0%), but the mating percentage of the moths consuming other solutions and distilled water was significantly reduced (H = 68.922, df = 12, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Similar to the results of the mating percentage, compared to diets containing only distilled water, the intake of honey and maltose significantly increased the mating frequency of C. medinalis (H = 72.384, df = 12, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Differences in reproductive capacity between C. exigua and C. medinalis moths feeding on different nutrients

The reproductive capacity of two rice leaffolders was influenced by the species and also depended on the intake of nutrients and their interactions (Table 1). The oviposition rate and period of C. exigua were significantly higher than C. medinalis when consuming the same nutrients (excluding honey) (Figs. 2, 3). The rice leaffolders exhibited different nutritional needs for oviposition. The oviposition rate of C. exigua fed on distilled water was 77.2%, with no significant difference compared to the treatment group fed on carbohydrate (χ2 = 37.287, df = 12, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The oviposition rate of C. exigua consuming arginine (42.9%) was the lowest among all treatment groups (Fig. 3). However, C. medinalis fed on carbohydrates exhibited a more significant increase in oviposition rate relative to the intake of amino acids (χ2 = 58.784, df = 12, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The intake of inositol solution had no significant difference in the oviposition rate of C. medinalis compared to distilled water (Fig. 3). The effects of carbohydrate and amino acid intake on the oviposition period and rate of two rice leaffolders differed. The intake of carbohydrates prolonged the oviposition period of C. exigua (H = 79.021, df = 12, P < 0.001), while the intake of amino acids did not prolong the oviposition period of the two species, even shortening the oviposition period of C. exigua (H = 67.136, df = 12, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

The intake of nutrients is essential for the oviposition of rice leaffolders, especially carbohydrates. Compared to distilled water treatment, consuming carbohydrates (excluding glucose and inositol) significantly increased the number of eggs laid per female in C. exiguas (H = 112.067, df = 12, P < 0.001), but only the intake of honey and sucrose solution significantly increased the number of eggs laid per female in C. medinalis (H = 54.763, df = 12, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The intake of amino acid solution did not significantly elevate the number of eggs laid per female of the two rice leaffolders, with the intake of arginine especially inhibiting the egg laying of C. exigua (Fig. 3). While nutrient intake increased the egg production per female of two rice leaffolders, the degree of increase in their egg production per female varied depending on the type of nutrient. The number of eggs laid per female of C. exigua was significantly higher than C. medinalis when fed on maltose, inositol, alanine, proline, glycine, lysine, and distilled water (Figs. 2, 3). However, although the egg production of C. exigua treated with distilled water was not the highest among all treatments, the egg hatching rate was significantly higher than other nutrient treatment groups (χ2 = 3377.245, df = 12, P < 0.001). This indicated that the egg hatching rate of C. exigua did not have a high demand for nutrients (Fig. 3). Conversely, the hatching of C. medinalis eggs depended on nutrient intake. Compared to the group fed on distilled water, the egg hatching rate of C. medinalis fed on honey, maltose, fructose, inositol and serine solution increased significantly (χ2 = 1265.081, df = 12, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Although the inositol intake of C. medinalis resulted in low egg laying per female, its egg hatching rate was the highest (Fig. 3). The consumption of arginine significantly lowered the egg hatching rate of C. medinalis (Fig. 3). There was a significant difference in egg hatching rates between the two species in the treatment groups outside of those feeding on honey and maltose solutions (Fig. 3). Unlike oviposition behavior, the hatching rate of C. medinalis eggs fed with inositol (χ2 = 7.040, df = 1, P = 0.007), serine (χ2 = 37.103, df = 1, P < 0.001) and glycine (χ2 = 10.646, df = 1, P = 0.001) was significantly higher than C. exigua (Fig. 2 and Fig. 3).

Fertility life tables of C. exigua and C. medinalis

The fertility life tables of the two rice leaffolders differed under different diet treatments with regard to demographic parameters. When consuming distilled water, the R0, rm and λ of C. exigua were significantly higher than C. medinalis (Table 2). However, when fed on honey solution, the rm and λ of C. medinalis were significantly higher (Table 2). The intake of carbohydrates and amino acids did not exert a consistent impact on the differences in demographic parameters between the two species. When fed on other nutrients (excluding arginine), the R0 of C. exigua was significantly higher than C. medinalis (Table 2). However, when adults ingested sucrose, the rm and λ of C. medinalis were significantly higher, without a significant difference when consuming fructose and alanine (Table 2). The population growth of the two species varied due to their nutritional composition. When C. medinalis consumed carbohydrates (excluding glucose and inositol), the R0, rm, and λ were significantly higher than those in the treatment group given distilled water (Table 2), indicating that the population growth of C. medinalis required carbohydrates. Nonetheless, no significant difference was identified in the R0, rm, and λ of C. exigua when consuming distilled water and honey solution, whereas the intake of arginine resulted in lower R0, rm, and λ (Table 2). Relatively higher values of rm and λ were found in C. exigua consuming inositol were relatively higher, although their R0 values were not significantly different from those of the honey solution and distilled water treatment groups.

Differences in longevity between C. exigua and C. medinalis moths feeding on different nutrients

The adult longevity of both females and males were significantly affected by species and treatments, as well as the interaction between species and treatments (Table 3). The female and male C. exigua fed on distilled water survived for 12.35 ± 0.45 days and 12.30 ± 0.54 days, respectively, with carbohydrate intake extended their longevity (Fig. 4). However, the intake of amino acids had a significant inhibitory effect on female longevity, but did not inhibit the survival of female moths treated with arginine intake (female: Sidak test, F12,450 = 15.120, P < 0.001; male: Sidak test, F12,442 = 30.577, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Feeding on maltose, sucrose, and fructose solution significantly prolonged the longevity of adult C. exigua, while feeding on honey and glucose solution also prolonged the longevity of male moths (Fig. 4). However, in C. medinalis, there was no significant difference in longevity between the amino acid intake and distilled water intake treatment groups, whether in female or male moths (Fig. 4). Compared to the distilled water treatment group, honey intake prolonged the longevity of female and male moths, but the longevity of female moths fed sucrose and glucose solution and male moths feeding on maltose and fructose was also significantly prolonged (Fig. 4). Notably, except for the female moths fed on honey (Z = -0.914, P = 0.361) and glucose (Z = -5.633, P = 0.157) solution, all other nutrient treatment groups exhibited significantly enhanced longevity in C. exigua than C. medinalis (Figs. 2, 4). These findings indicate that the adult survival in the two species have different nutritional requirements, with further differences in nutritional requirements between male and female moths. Nevertheless, the longevity of C. exigua moths was generally longer than C. medinalis moths.

Discussion

Differences in nutritional requirements may have evolutionary influences on the reproductive and survival strategies of diverse organisms6. In herbivorous insects with complete metamorphosis, the larval and adult stages usually differ considerably in their nutritional requirements14. For closely related insects, even with the same diet during the larval stage, they may reduce resource competition by differentiating their nutritional needs following entry into the adult stage7,8,34. This study determined that there were differences in the effects of carbohydrate and amino acid intake on mating behavior, fecundity, longevity and population dynamics between C. exigua and C. medinalis. Their nutritional requirements were completely different, and they formed specific characteristic differentiation during evolution.

Mating is a necessary reproductive behavioral process associated with the persistence of populations, but it is costly for both sexes35,36. when nutrition is limited, some insects may need to balance energy input between mating and other behaviors37,38. This study identified that C. medinalis needed to supplement nutrients to mate better, while C. exigua had a high mating percentage even without supplementary nutrition, potentially related to differentiation in migration and overwintering during evolution. C. medinalis tended to migrate more than mating during nutrient shortages30. Our results indicated that supplementing carbohydrates significantly increased the mating percentage and frequency of C. medinalis, suggesting that their mating behavior required much higher levels of carbohydrates than amino acids. Conversely, the mating behavior of C. exigua relied more on the energy stored during the larval stage, as their mating percentage was not lowest when feeding on distilled water. Insect larvae that overwinter consume a large amount of food before winter, accumulating energy reserves to promote metabolism and meet the energetic demands of development and reproduction in the spring39. Remarkably, the mating percentage of C. exigua given distilled water was much higher than when given honey solution. This may be because when nutrition is sufficient, adults must balance the energy input of reproduction and longevity to maximize population growth40,41. Moreover, supplementing carbohydrates can not only promote the synthesis of female sex pheromones, but also facilitate sperm formation42,43, which may explain why carbohydrate intake increases the mating frequency of two species.

Successful reproduction involves behaviors such as courting, and copulation, successful production, and hatching of eggs44. Although mating promote female reproductive output45, mating behavior does not imply successful reproduction of offspring. For many Lepidoptera, specific nutritional supplements are necessary to accomplish effective reproduction41,46. Our findings indicate that although mating and oviposition in C. exigua were not highly dependent on nutrition, egg development and maturation required nutrient intake by adults. C. medinalis moths urgently need nutritional supplementation to promote successful reproduction. Otherwise, they prefer to migrate and reduce energy inputs for reproduction30. However, for C. exigua that resided in suitable habitats for prolonged durations, carbohydrate intake promoted egg production and hatching. Unlike carbohydrates, amino acids only influence the egg hatching rate, which may be because essential and non-essential amino acids consumed by adults were allocated to the eggs to improve offspring quality47. There was not a high demand for inositol in oviposition, but the intake of inositol increased the egg hatching rate of the two rice leaffolders. Inositol, as a vitamin-like carbohydrate, may not be the primary energy substance required for oviposition, but it assists in maintaining normal cell volume and osmotic pressure to promote embryogenesis and embryonic development48–50. For instance, when the migratory locust Locusta migratoria was exposed to fluctuating thermal environments, its eggs accumulated inositol51. The demand for inositol during the egg hatching of the two rice leaffolders may be a physiological mechanism that evolved from adaptation to seasonal environmental changes. Notably, the egg hatching of C. medinalis required significantly higher levels of inositol than C. exigua, while their requirements for glucose and alanine, capable of providing low-temperature protection by stabilizing cell membranes and protein structures49, are divergent. This may be associated with differences in environmental adaptability mechanisms between overwintering and migratory species. C. exigua has evolved a seasonal cold tolerance approach suitable for overwintering, while C. medinalis, which cannot overwinter, has evolved a heat tolerance model adapted to high-temperature stress environments. The specific functions of these nutrients in the process of environmental adaptation require in-depth research.

Different species use different reproductive strategies to expand their populations according to their life history traits52. Although the fertility life tables show that carbohydrate intake promoted population growth of both C. exigua and C. medinalis, the influence of different nutrients on population dynamics of the two species still varied. The intake of honey and sucrose leads to the rm and λ values of C. medinalis to be significantly higher, indicating that honey and sucrose stimulate the population growth potential of C. medinalis. However, when consuming other nutrients, the population growth of C. exigua had higher potential. Importantly, what drives population dynamics is not only reproduction but also survival53. We identified that the adult longevity of C. exigua and C. medinalis was influenced by nutrient intake, consistent with previous research on the nutritional requirements of other insects31,54. Especially the intake of carbohydrates significantly prolonged rice leaffolders longevity, potentially because carbohydrates are the main energy source for maintaining longevity and other life activities55. Interestingly, in both species, male moths had increased longevity than female moths when supplemented with carbohydrates, but the opposite is true of other treatments. Due to the requirement for males to consume energy during mating, ejaculation formation and transfer and/or courtship behaviors, the trade-off between male energy input or resource allocation favors investment in reducing longevity to increase the quantity/quality of offspring before attaining nutritional optima for lifetime reproductive output56,57. More importantly, the longevity of C. exigua was higher than that of C. medinalis in most treatments. The evolution of longevity enhances the immunity of insects, and the evolution of immune functions supports the evolution of longevity58. C. exigua cannot migrate to escape harsh environments like C. medinalis, and its prolonged longevity may be associated with the evolution of immune function. As closely related species, the difference in nutritional requirements for population growth and survival may be due to differentiation in the process of achieving population coexistence to reach the optimal nutrient levels for lifetime reproductive outputs. The migratory characteristics of C. medinalis require balancing migration, reproduction, and survival in times of nutrient shortage, sacrificing their longevity to achieve the best reproductive trajectory56. In contrast, C. exigua moths relied on the nutrient reserves accumulated during larval development to maintain survival and reproductive activities, while adult nutrition supplementation prolonged longevity and promoted reproduction. This differentiation of nutritional needs satisfying migration and overwintering may be due to evolutionary trade-offs in survivability, competitive ability, and reproductive effort59. Notably, although offering alternative flowering plants in rice fields can increase the abundance and efficiency of natural enemies to reduce the application of chemical pesticides60, our results suggest that the nutritional content in these flowering plants may change the population dynamics of these two rice leaffolders. Therefore, when selecting flowering plants as the microhabitat and food source for natural enemies in rice fields, it is necessary to consider the nutritional needs of the population dynamics of rice leaffolders and other pests.

Overall, the reproduction and longevity of C. exigua had different nutritional requirements compared to C. medinalis, influencing their population dynamics. The activity of migratory C. medinalis adults relied on carbohydrate intake, while C. exigua with overwintering traits could achieve population expansion without supplementing adult nutrition. The reliance of reproduction and longevity of two rice leaffolders on different nutrients is associated with behavioral differentiation due to migration and overwintering. These findings help us further understand the competitive co-evolutionary mechanism between these two related species, providing selection criteria for filtering flowering plants as microhabitats and food source for rice field natural enemies. However, this study only analyzed their differences in nutritional requirements and their influence on population dynamics. It is unclear how nutrition acts to differentiation of migration and wintering behavior characteristics. Further research should explore the evolutionary mechanisms of how nutrition drives differentiation in migratory and overwintering behavior between these two closely related species.

Methods

Insect rearing and rice plant preparation

C. exigua and C. medinalis larvae were harvested from a paddy field in Qianwei County (Leshan, Sichuan Province, China; 104.06°E, 29.21°N) and Nanjing (Jiangsu Province, China; 118.78°E, 32.06°N) in 2022 and 2019, respectively. The larvae of these two rice leaffolders were reared under laboratory conditions as previously described25. TN1 rice (a variety sensitive to rice leaffolders, seeds provided by the China National Rice Research Institute) was used for larval rearing. These rice seedlings were cultivated in a greenhouse at the Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Hangzhou, China (30.31°N, 120.20°E), without any insect pests or pesticide treatments, and were used for feeding insects 40 days after germination. The specific planting steps for rice drew on the methods of Zhao, et al.24. After the larvae were pupated, they were collected in plastic boxes with holes in the top (16 cm in length, 24 cm in width and 22 cm in height), and covered with absorbent cotton in the bottom to maintain humidity24. After emergence, the moths were transferred to a 350 mL plastic cup (five pairs of male and female moths per cup) and fed with 5% honey solution56. All moths were reared in intelligent artificial climate chambers at 26 ± 1 °C and 80–90% relative humidity, and with a photoperiod of 14:10 Light:Dark. The collection of all insect and plant materials complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

Feeding solution for adults

The types and concentrations of carbohydrates and amino acids were characterized based on the nutrient content of common nectar in Lepidoptera insects31,61. Five carbohydrates (maltose, sucrose, fructose, glucose, and inositol) and six amino acids (alanine, proline, serine, glycine, lysine, and arginine) (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were used to investigate the nutritional requirements of the two rice leaffolders. The concentration of all carbohydrate solutions was 5% (w/w), and the solution concentration of each amino acid component was 0.167 mol/L. Honey and distilled water were utilized as positive and negative control groups, respectively.

Reproductive parameters and longevity of moths fed with different nutrients

Newly-emerged moths were fed different carbohydrate and amino acid solutions. Each replicate comprised one female moth and one male moth, kept in a 350 mL plastic cup and sealed with plastic film for eggs laying. At least 25 replicates were conducted for each nutrient solution. Adult food was supplied through absorbent cotton wool soaked with different nutrient solutions at the bottom of the plastic cups. The feeding solution was replaced daily to ensure nutrient supply to the adult moths. The number of eggs laid in each treatment group was recorded daily, and the plastic wrap for oviposition was replaced after counting. The mortality was documented daily until all moths died. The ovaries of the dead female moths were dissected to identify whether mating occurred and the mating frequency (the number of spermatophores in the bursa copulatrix)56. The oviposition rate was the proportion of successfully mated and oviposited adults among all replicates. The oviposition period and the number of eggs in each replicate were determined. The egg hatching rate was determined according to the number of hatched larvae that successfully hatch among laid eggs.

Fertility life tables

According to the biological parameters of the adult stage obtained from the above experiment, fertility life tables were developed for each nutrient solution of C. exigua and C. medinalis according to Li, et al.31. Demographic parameters like net reproductive rate (R0), intrinsic rate of increase (rm) and finite rate of increase (λ) were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Before analysis, data were examined for normality and variance homogeneity using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene test, respectively. Data that conformed to normal distribution (P > 0.05) and variance homogeneity (P > 0.05) were analyzed using parametric tests. Otherwise they were analyzed using nonparametric tests. The interaction between species and nutrition was evaluated using Scheirer-Ray-Hare analysis for reproductive parameters. Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to analyze the single-factor significance of reproductive parameters between species and among different diets, respectively. Adult longevity of different treatments underwent a Cox proportional hazard model (CPHM). Species and different nutrients and their interactions were included as categorical variables in the analysis. Longevity data for each significant categorical variable underwent a multivariate generalized linear model with a Poisson distribution and log link function. Means were segregated with a Sidak test and Mann–Whitney U-test at α = 0.0556. Differences in mating percentage, oviposition rate and egg hatching rate between species and among treatments were compared via the Chi-square test. The differences in demographic parameters between different diets and species were examined using Tukey' HSD and t-tests, respectively. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 20.0).
