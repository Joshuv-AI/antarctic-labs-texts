The impact of magnesium biotinate and arginine silicate complexes on metabolic dysfunctions, antioxidant activity, inflammation, and neuromodulation in high-fat diet-fed rats
Authors: Kazim Sahin, Emre Sahin, Cemal Orhan, Besir Er, Bayram Akoglan, Ibrahim Hanifi Ozercan, Nurhan Sahin, James R Komorowski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiotin and arginine play crucial roles in lipid metabolism and may offer promising interventions against obesity. This study examined the combined effect of magnesium biotinate (MgB) and inositol-stabilized arginine silicate complex (ASI) on obesity-related oxidative imbalance, inflammation, lipid metabolism and neuromodulation in rats on a high-fat diet (HFD). Forty rats were divided into five groups: (a) control: rats were fed a standard diet containing 12% of energy from fat; (b) HFD: rats were fed the HFD with 42% of energy from fat; (c) HFD + MgB: rats were fed the HFD and given 0.31 mg/kg body weight (BW) MgB, (d) HFD + ASI: rats were fed the HFD and were given 12.91 mg/kg BW ASI), and (e) HFD + MgB + ASI: rats were fed the HFD and given 0.31 mg/kg BW MgB and 12.91 mg/kg BW ASI). The combined administration of MgB and ASI reduced the levels of serum cholesterol, free fatty acid (FFA), and malondialdehyde (MDA), as well as liver inflammatory cytokines, sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1-c (SREBP-1c), and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGR) proteins (P < 0.001) compared to HFD rats without supplementation. Moreover, this combination increased the activities of antioxidant enzymes (P < 0.05) and boosted the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), serotonin, dopamine (P < 0.001), as well as liver insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) (P < 0.001). These findings suggest that combining MgB and ASI could deter liver fat accumulation and enhance lipid metabolism in HFD-fed rats by modulating various metabolic pathways and neuromodulators related to energy metabolism. This combination demonstrates potential in addressing obesity and its related metabolic dysfunctions.Keywords: Biotin, Arginine, High-fat diet, Neuromodulators, Energy metabolism

Introduction

Over recent years, humans and companion animals have seen a sharp rise in overconsumption of calories and inactive lifestyles [1, 2]. High-calorie diets, especially those rich in animal-based saturated fats and carbohydrates, can initiate low-level systemic inflammation and result in conditions such as obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and disorders related to neurodegeneration [3]. Consumption of a high-fat diet (HFD) may result in elevated levels of circulating free fatty acids (FFA). This elevation can stimulate mitochondrial β-oxidation of FFA, leading to an excess flow of electrons into cytochrome c oxidase and, thus, the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [4]. Excessive production of ROS can weaken antioxidant capacity and cause inflammatory responses by stimulating proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [5]. Impaired lipid metabolism following HFD feeding elevates serum cholesterol levels due to fatty liver [6], and cholesterol accumulation may worsen hepatic inflammation [7]. These metabolic abnormalities can disrupt energy homeostasis by accelerating the onset of insulin resistance [8]. Furthermore, research indicates that HFD consumption can lead to changes in behavior and cognitive deficits in rodents and humans [9]. The brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) plays an essential role in the creation of new neurons and the development of memory, and it also has a role in suppressing appetite, thus regulating the energy balance within the central nervous system [10]. BDNF-induced signaling can alter secondary neuromodulation mechanisms by increasing serotonin and dopamine transmission [11]. The interaction between serotonin and dopamine influences the regulation of central nervous system function and the activity of neurobiological networks [12]. Earlier studies have suggested that obesity induced by HFD in rodents could affect both the function of BDNF [13] and the balance of serotonin/dopamine [14, 15]. Various therapeutic strategies, such as using vitamins, amino acids, and minerals, have been deployed to mitigate the adverse impacts of HFD-induced obesity on energy metabolism and brain function [16–18].

Biotin, also referred to as vitamin B7, can normalize disrupted antioxidant enzyme activities by decreasing ROS overproduction and lipid oxidation products [19]. Also, it can promote glucose and lipid metabolism by influencing the functions of various metabolic enzymes [20]. By raising the levels of insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) proteins and reducing sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1-c (SREBP-1c) and fatty acid synthase (FAS) levels, biotin can amplify insulin signal transmission [21]. Furthermore, biotin has neuroprotective properties attributed to its function of increasing cyclic guanosine monophosphate levels associated with neuronal survival and neurogenesis [22]. Magnesium Biotinate (MgB) is a novel form of biotin composed of 86% biotin and 14% Magnesium (Mg). MgB possesses around 40 times the biotin solubility of other commercially available D-biotin products, making it more readily absorbed in the body. This superior bioavailability enhances biotin accumulation in rats’ serum, liver, and brain [23]. Therefore, compared to D-biotin, MgB is likely more effective at normalizing glucose and fat metabolism [24], and it may also provide a stronger neuroprotective effect [25].

Arginine, a semi-essential amino acid, enhances nitric oxide (NO) availability and stimulates the production of antioxidant enzymes, including serum superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and catalase (CAT), by activating the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 pathway [26]. Additionally, it can serve as a therapeutic agent in managing high blood sugar and cholesterol levels [27]. Supplementing with l-arginine can boost insulin sensitivity [17] and avert the buildup of white adipose tissue by activating the lipolytic gene PPAR-γ in HFD-fed rats [28]. Silicon (Si), an essential trace element, can regulate the postprandial state by reducing intestinal glucose and triglyceride absorption, and it also helps to mitigate non-alcoholic steatohepatitis by blocking apoptosis pathways in the liver of rats [29]. The inositol-stabilized arginine silicate complex (ASI, with 49.5% arginine, 8.2% Si, and 25% inositol) enhances the bioavailability of arginine and Si compared to their traditional forms [30], thereby potentially augmenting the metabolic functions of arginine and Si on insulin metabolism [31, 32]. Additionally, prior studies have suggested that ASI supplementation improves nutrient distribution to the brain [33] and nitric oxide availability, thereby enhancing cognitive function [34, 35]. This function may be intricately linked to neuromodulator factors such as BDNF [36] and the interaction of serotonin/dopamine [37]. However, the potential amplified effects of the MgB and ASI combination have not been extensively studied. This study aims to investigate the potential impact of combined MgB and ASI administration on HFD-induced lipid metabolism imbalance, antioxidant enzyme activity reduction, and inflammatory cytokine production, as well as to evaluate their impact on critical neuromodulators associated with cholesterol and energy metabolism in rats.

Material and methods

Animals

A total of 40 Wistar Albino male rats (8 weeks old, 210 ± 15 g BW) were used in the study. Rats were purchased from Bingol University Experimental Research Center and reared in propylene cages under standard environmental conditions (temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, humidity 55 ± 5%, and a 12/12 h light/dark cycle). Feed and drinking water were provided ad libitum. The experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee of Bingol University (protocol number: 2020/01-01/05) following EU directives. Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVAL) guidelines were followed when designing and reporting this study.

Experimental procedures and sampling

After a week of acclimatization, the rats were randomly allocated into five different groups (each containing eight rats): (a) control group, where rats consumed a regular diet comprising 12% of energy from fat; (b) HFD group, with rats on a HFD that provided 42% of energy from fat; (c) HFD + MgB group, where, in addition to a HFD, rats were administered a daily oral dose of 0.31 mg/kg body weight (BW) of MgB; (d) HFD + ASI group, with rats on a HFD and receiving a daily oral dose of 12.91 mg/kg BW of ASI; and (e) HFD + MgB + ASI group, where rats were on a HFD and received daily oral doses of both of MgB and ASI. The experiment lasted for 90 days. Details regarding the composition of the standard and HFD are provided in Table 1. The diets (AIN-93) were formulated in compliance with the dietary guidelines outlined by the American Institute of Nutrition [38]. Feed intake was evaluated throughout the study. Calorie intake was calculated according to daily feed intake.

Upon concluding the study, the rats were weighed and euthanized through cervical dislocation. Blood was collected by cardiac puncture into biochemical tubes, and the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C at 3000×g for 10 min to obtain serum samples. Liver tissues were promptly removed and preserved at − 80 °C for future lab analysis. Additionally, a section of the liver from each rat was preserved in a 10% formalin solution to prepare for histopathological assessment.

Biochemical analyses

Serum glucose, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total protein (TP), albumin, cholesterol, and triglycerides were analyzed with rat-specific kits (IVR-PT06, Samsung LABGEO PT Biochemistry Test 15) in a portable automated chemistry analyzer (Samsung LABGEO PT10, Samsung Electronics Co., Suwon, Korea).

Serum levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a UV–vis SPD-10 AVP detector and a CTO-10 AS VP column, utilizing a mobile phase composed of 30 mM KH2PO4 and methanol (82.5: 17.5, v/v, pH 3.6) at a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The serum biotin concentrations were determined using HPLC, following previously established methods with slight adjustments [40]. This method involved drying the serum samples under a nitrogen airflow before the biotin analysis, employing a reversed-phase C18-ODS-3 column, and analyzing the chromatography fractions containing biotin. Furthermore, serum arginine levels were measured by HPLC based on the method outlined by Pieper and Dondlinger [41], which entails extracting the serum samples in sulfosalicylic acid dehydrate, centrifuging them, and then preparing the supernatants with a lithium-D buffer before the analysis.

A 0.5 mL aliquot of serum was digested with 5 mL of HNO3 in a Microwave Digestion System (Berghof Microwave MWS-2, Germany), following the instructions specified in the microwave’s user manual. This mixture was then diluted with ultra-pure water at a tenfold ratio before proceeding with the analysis. Lanthanum chloride (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was incorporated as an interference suppressant to mitigate interference during Mg analysis. The Mg and Si concentrations in the serum samples were quantified using atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS, Perkin-Elmer, Analyst 800, Norwalk, CT, USA). This included using flame atomization for Mg measurement and a graphite furnace for Si, employing specific wavelengths of 285.2 nm for Mg and 251.6 nm for Si, alongside Zeeman background correction to ensure accuracy. The analysis was performed in triplicate. The method was verified with certified reference materials (bovine muscle BCR 184), and the accuracy was 97% for Mg and 95% for Si. The detection limit was 0.0023 mmol/L for Mg and 2.85 µmol/L for Si.

Serum SOD, GSH-Px, CAT (Cayman Chemical Co.), insulin, leptin (MyBioSource, CA, USA), FFAs, cortisol (Cayman Chemical Co., MI, USA), noradrenaline, serotonin, dopamine (MyBioSource, CA, USA), and BDNF (Elabscience Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) were measured with the rat-specific kits according to the manufacturers’ protocol in a microplate spectrophotometer (Elx-800, Bio-Tek Instruments Inc, Vermont, USA). The intra-assay and inter-assay variability were recorded as follows: 3.2% and 3.7% for SOD, 5.7% and 7.2% for GSH-Px, 3.8% and 9.9% for CAT, under 8.0% and under 10.0% for insulin, under 10.0% and under 12.0% for leptin, 5.9% and 5.6% for FFAs, 5.1% and 6.7% for cortisol, under 8.0% and under 10.0% for serotonin, under 10.0% and under 12.0% for dopamine, under 10.0% for both intra- and inter-assay for BDNF, and under 10.0% and under 12.0% for noradrenaline.

Western blot analysis

Liver samples were homogenized in a cold Tris-HCl buffer (10 mM, pH 7.4) with a mixture of protease inhibitors (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). After homogenization, the samples underwent centrifugation (15,000×g, 4 °C for 30 min), and the resulting supernatants were then combined with 2xLaemmli sample buffer and heated. Protein separation was achieved through 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) using a Mini-PROTEAN Tetra Cell system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA), followed by transfer onto nitrocellulose membranes via a semi-dry method (Power Blotter, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA). These membranes were then blocked with a 5% solution of bovine serum albumin at room temperature for two hours before overnight incubation at 4 °C with antibodies targeting IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IRS-1, PPAR-γ, SREBP-1c, ATP citrate lyase (ACLY), FAS, Niemann-Pick C1-Like 1 (NPC1L1), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGR), cholesterol 7-alpha-monooxygenase (CYP7A1), acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 2 (ACAT2), and microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTP, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA). Following this, membranes were treated with an HRP-linked secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. The presence of proteins was controlled using an anti-β-actin antibody (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), and the diaminobenzidine substrate technique was used to visualize antibody interactions. For densitometric evaluation, band analysis was performed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Histopathological analysis

Briefly, the liver samples were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin blocks. Next, paraffin blocks were vertically sectioned by 4 µm with a microtome. The obtained sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined by a pathologist under a light microscope (Olympus BX53, NY, USA).

Statistical analyses

The normality of data was controlled with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the equality of the variances was checked with Levene’s test. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was run for multiple group comparisons. P < 0.05 was accepted as significant. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS statistical package program (version 22.0, IBM, SPSS, Armonk, USA). The sample size was determined by power analysis in the G*Power program (Version 3.1) [42]. To achieve 85% statistical power (1−β), we calculated the total sample size as 40 (N) with 0.65 effect size (f) and 0.05 type 1 error (α).

Results

BW, feed and calorie intake, and serum biochemical parameters

As presented in Table 2, HFD-fed rats exhibited an increase in final BW compared to the control rats (P < 0.01). When treated with MgB and a combination of MgB and ASI, there was a significant reduction in final BW compared to HFD-fed rats without treatment (P < 0.05). However, no significant change in final BW was observed in rats treated with ASI alone compared to HFD-fed rats without treatment (P > 0.05). Although feed intake decreased due to HFD feeding (P < 0.001), the treatment of MgB, ASI, or Mg + ASI did not alter feed intake or calorie intake in rats (P > 0.05). Consumption of HFD resulted in elevated serum levels of glucose (34.2%), ALT (55.9%), AST (44.2%), FFA (223.3%), cholesterol (52.7%), and triglycerides (84.3%) in rats compared to standard diet consumption (P < 0.001). Administration of MgB (P < 0.001), ASI (P < 0.05), or MgB + ASI (P < 0.001) led to a reduction in serum glucose, ALT, AST, FFA, cholesterol, and triglycerides levels compared to HFD-fed rats those received no treatment. The MgB-received rats had lower serum ALT, FFA, and cholesterol levels than the ASI-received rats (P < 0.05). The combined administration of MgB and ASI reduced serum FFA levels compared to MgB (− 20.4%) or ASI (− 33.7%) administration (P < 0.01). Moreover, the combined therapy also resulted in lower serum glucose (− 8.2%), ALT (− 17.1%), cholesterol (− 19.6%), and triglyceride levels (− 18.5%) compared to the administration of ASI. Interestingly, serum ALT, AST, and cholesterol were similar between the HFD + MgB + ASI and control groups (P > 0.05). Serum BUN, creatine, TP, and ALB were similar between all treatments (P > 0.05).

Serum Mg, biotin, Si, and arginine levels

The study showed that HFD-fed rats exhibited significantly lower levels of serum Mg (− 38.9%), biotin (− 40.2%), Si (− 33.8%), and arginine (− 26.2%) compared to the control rats (P < 0.001, Table 3). As expected, the rats receiving Mg and biotin supplements, namely the HFD + MgB and HFD + MgB + ASI groups, showed the highest serum Mg and biotin levels among all the groups (P < 0.01). Likewise, the groups supplemented with Si and arginine, namely the HFD + ASI and HFD + MgB + ASI groups, showed the highest serum levels of Si and arginine compared to the other groups (P < 0.001). Serum Mg and biotin levels were similar between HFD-fed rats and those treated exclusively with ASI (P > 0.05). Also, serum Si and arginine levels were similar between HFD-fed rats and those treated exclusively with MgB (P > 0.05).

Serum MDA levels and antioxidant enzyme activities

In comparison with the standard diet, consumption of the HFD led to a significant increase in MDA (294.3%) serum levels while diminishing the activities of SOD (− 57.6%), GSH-Px (− 67.2%), and CAT (− 52.2%) (P < 0.001; Table 4). Treatment with MgB, ASI, or their combination was effective in countering the HFD-induced elevation in serum MDA levels (P < 0.05) while also enhancing the activities of serum SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT enzymes (P < 0.05). The combined administration of MgB and ASI led to a decrease in serum MDA levels by 18.9% and 26.3%, respectively, compared to the administration of either MgB and ASI alone (P < 0.001). Additionally, the combined treatment resulted in a significant increase in the activities of SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT by 20.1%, 36.0%, and 19.0%, respectively, compared to the treatment of MgB alone (P < 0.01). In comparison with the administration of ASI alone, the combined treatment also significantly enhanced the activities of SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT by 36.0%, 70.1%, and 33.0%, respectively (P < 0.001). Moreover, the rats treated with MgB had lower serum MDA levels (− 9.1%) and higher GSH-Px activity (25.1%) than those treated with ASI (P < 0.05).

Serum hormone and BDNF levels

Table 5 demonstrates that HFD consumption significantly increased the serum by 114.7% and leptin levels by 93.4% compared to consumption of the standard diet (P < 0.001). MgB and ASI administration reduced serum insulin (− 26.0% and − 16.1%, respectively) and leptin levels (− 29.5% and − 21.4%, respectively) (P < 0.05). The combined use of MgB and ASI was more effective in lowering serum insulin and leptin concentrations than using either MgB or ASI alone (P < 0.05). In HFD-fed rats, the administration of ASI had a lesser impact on insulin levels than that of MgB (− 11.9%) (P < 0.05). Compared to rats fed the standard diet, HFD-fed rats exhibited higher serum levels of cortisol (105.1%) and noradrenaline (84.0%), both of which are known to increase stress responses (P < 0.001). Contrary to the elevated markers of stress response, HFD consumption resulted in decreased serum levels of serotonin (− 31.7%), dopamine (39.6%), and BDNF (− 59.8%) in comparison with standard diet consumption (P < 0.001). The administration of MgB, ASI, and MgB + ASI reduced serum cortisol and noradrenalin levels in HFD-fed rats (P < 0.001). The combined administration of MgB with ASI was notably more effective in reducing the concentrations of cortisol (by − 17.4% and − 22.0%, respectively) and noradrenaline (by − 11.4% and − 19.2%, respectively) compared to using either substance alone (P < 0.001 for cortisol and P < 0.05 for noradrenaline). MgB administration was associated with an increase in serum serotonin levels (P < 0.05), whereas ASI did not affect serotonin levels in serum when compared to HFD-fed rats (P > 0.05). MgB administration increased the serum serotonin level compared to ASI administration by 14.7% (P < 0.05). The combined administration of MgB and ASI significantly increased serum serotonin levels by 21.2% compared to the administration of ASI alone (P < 0.001) but was not altered compared to the administration of MgB alone (P > 0.05).

Liver histopathology

As presented in Fig. 5a, the liver microstructure of control rats showed a typical hepatic lobule pattern with the central vein situated at the center of the lobules. The hepatocytes of these rats were well-formed and polygonal, with clear cell boundaries. No signs of fibrosis or fat accumulation were observed in the livers of control rats. However, after HFD consumption, lipid droplets accumulated in the hepatocyte cytoplasm, disorganizing hepatic lobule structures (Fig. 5b). This fat accumulation was partially mitigated using either MgB or ASI (Fig. 5c–d). The combined formulation (Fig. 5e) demonstrated a more pronounced effect on liver tissue recovery than either MgB or ASI alone, consistent with its ability to decrease circulating FFA, triglyceride, and cholesterol levels.

Discussion

These findings shed light on the effects of HFD intake on BW, serum biochemical indicators, inflammation markers, antioxidant enzymes, lipid metabolism, and liver pathology in rats, as well as the protective roles played by MgB, ASI, and their combination on HFD-related metabolic alterations. In line with previous research [4, 5, 17, 27, 43], our observations of increased serum levels of glucose, ALT, AST, FFA, cholesterol, and triglycerides in rats fed with the HFD underscore its contribution to metabolic imbalance. The rise in serum MDA levels, alongside the reduction in activities of antioxidant enzymes in these rats, further validates the oxidative stress triggered by such a diet. The treatment with MgB, ASI, and their combined formulation notably ameliorated the adverse effect of HFD consumption. Specifically, there was a significant decrease in serum MDA levels and an increase in the activities of enzymes such as SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT, which highlights the antioxidant capabilities of MgB and ASI interventions. Notably, the combined treatment stood out in its effectiveness, demonstrating a collaborative effect in bolstering the body’s defense against oxidative challenges presented by the HFD. Although there are limited studies on the combined administration of ASI and MgB, rat studies demonstrated that biotin may mitigate hyperlipidemia and oxidative stress [44] by reducing ROS generation that contributes to hepatic lipoperoxidation [18]. Also, biotin administration can reduce serum MDA, FFA, cholesterol, AST, and ALT levels in HFD-induced type 2 diabetes model [43]. It has been reported that insufficient l-arginine leads to the formation of ROS [26]. l-arginine supplementation can improve NO-related insulin sensitivity [45], reduce hepatic fatty acid synthesis, and increase fatty acid oxidation in the liver [46]. Otani et al. [47] demonstrated that low dietary l-arginine can cause hepatic lipid accumulation by suppressing very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion. These effects of biotin and arginine supplementation can partially explain the action mechanism of the combined administration of MgB and ASI in reversing the disorganization of hepatic lobules and lipid accumulation in hepatocytes. Similar to our results, Sahin et al. [24] showed that MgB supplementation decreased serum cholesterol and triglyceride concentration more than commercial d-biotin in healthy rats. Sahin et al. [25] demonstrated that MgB administration for two weeks decreased MDA levels in serum and brain in propionic acid-induced autistic rats. The same study indicated that MgB administration increased SOD, GSH-Px, CAT, and glutathione levels in rat brains [25]. Cicek et al. [48] reported that combined administration of ASI and MgB for ten weeks reversed the skin damage-related elevation in MDA levels and improved antioxidant capacity. The anti-inflammatory cytokine-inhibiting effects (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) of biotin and arginine likely enhanced the antioxidative effects of the combined administration of MgB and ASI in HFD-fed rats.

The overexpression of hepatic PPAR-γ levels can prevent nutritional fibrotic steatohepatitis [49], fatty liver-related metabolic syndrome, and TNF-α mediated inflammation in mice [50]. Also, PPARγ ligands enhance the effects of insulin by promoting tyrosine phosphorylation of the insulin receptor and IRS-1 and inducing the activation of Akt [51]. Consistent with present findings, a study by Sahin et al. [52] showed that HFD consumption could reduce the liver IRS-1 and PPARγ levels in rats. Turgut et al. [21] reported that biotin supplementation could increase liver IRS-1 in exercised rats. Moreover, Sahin et al. [43] revealed that 300 μg/kg biotin supplementation exerts anti-diabetic activities in rats, owing to an ameliorating effect on insulin resistance by increasing hepatic IRS-1 and adipose PPAR-γ levels. Likewise, l-arginine has been shown to enhance gene expressions associated with energy-substrate oxidation in insulin-sensitive tissues [53]. Our findings indicated that combined MgB and ASI supplementation may have improved insulin signaling by regulating PPAR-γ and IRS-1-dependent pathways. HFD-induced low-grade inflammatory status is responsible for insulin resistance and pancreatic β cell dysfunction [54]. The administration of MgB + ASI appears to have exerted a distinct anti-inflammatory effect by reducing liver IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels. This reduction likely contributed to the improvement of insulin signaling in HFD-fed rats. Additionally, insulin regulates beta cell secretory response to arginine, and arginine-stimulated insulin secretion is better preserved than glucose-stimulated [55]. Therefore, the administration of ASI alone may not have increased IRS-1 levels due to the insulin-releasing effects of arginine in HFD-fed rats.

SREBP-1c is responsible for the transcriptional regulation of fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis pathway enzymes, including FAS and ACLY [56]. Aguilera‐Méndez and Fernández‐Mejía [20] reported that biotin supplementation reduced lipid accumulation and diminished the expression of SREBP-1c and FAS in the liver. Consistent with the present result, Sahin et al. [24] found that MgB administration (1 or 100 mg/kg) reduced liver SREBP-1c and FAS activity in healthy rats. Although there is a lack of data on the effect of ASI on SREBP-1c and FAS activity, previous studies reported that l-arginine could reduce serum lipid and cholesterol concentrations by downregulating hepatic mRNA expressions of the SREBP-2 and FAS genes in rats fed an atherogenic diet [57] and normalize liver SREBP-1c and FAS in HFD-fed Nile tilapia (l-arginine supplementation, 2% of diet) [58]. In this context, the present findings suggest that the combined administration of MgB and ASI reduced liver SREBP-1c levels compared to the administration of MgB or ASI alone. However, it remains unexplained that although MgB and ASI suppressed ACLY and FAS activity, their combined administration did not lower ACLY and FAS activity compared to the administration alone.

In the current study, the administration of MgB and ASI suppressed the activity of cholesterol metabolism-related factors in the liver, which is probably consistent with reducing serum triglycerides and cholesterol and alleviating liver fat accumulation. The following molecular mechanisms may explain the suppressive effect of MgB and ASI on cholesterol metabolism. (1) The serum insulin-lowering and anti-inflammatory activity of MgB and ASI may have prevented proinflammatory cytokine-induced activation of NPC1L1 in the liver, which most likely led to lipid accumulation through decreased secretion of VLDL triglycerides [59], (2) MgB and ASI administration possibly caused HMGR down-regulation, which is a rate-limiting enzyme for the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway by stimulating PPAR-γ [60], (3) The down-regulation of HMGR may have reduced the amount of cholesterol available, and CYP7A1 activity was suppressed because the need for cholesterol conversion to bile acid was reduced, as HFD feeding may increase CYP7A1 activity to convert cholesterol produced by HMGR to bile acid [61], and (4) Insulin signaling related to the IRS-1 pathway improved by MgB and ASI administration may have decreased liver MTP levels [62], an essential protein for the forming and releasing VLDL in the liver [63]. However, we did not find any available studies investigating the effects of biotin and arginine on these cholesterol metabolism-related factors to compare the present results.

Impaired insulin signaling and increased neuro-inflammation in the brains of HFD-fed rats can reduce serum BDNF activity [9]. In the current study, the reduction in BDNF activity may have contributed to the deceleration of fatty acid synthesis in the liver [64] and the exacerbation of TNFα-induced liver inflammation [65]. BDNF participates in glucose homeostasis, directly acting on pancreatic β cells and hepatocytes. In peripheral tissues, BDNF enhances the tyrosine phosphorylation of the insulin receptor, thereby aiding in regulating insulin signaling [10]. Tsuchida et al. [66] reported that BDNF treatment, which operates through a mechanism similar to that of leptin in regulating lipid and glucose metabolism, leads to reductions in serum cholesterol, triglyceride, and FFA levels, as well as hepatic triglyceride levels, consequently preventing fatty liver in diabetic mice. Serum and brain BDNF levels are positively related because BDNF can cross the blood–brain barrier in both directions [67]. Therefore, the increased serum BDNF levels in the present study may also reflects brain BDNF levels. Antioxidant therapies can prevent decreased brain BDNF activation and restore the antioxidant enzyme levels [68]. The antioxidant enzyme-enhancing effect of MgB and ASI treatment may have inhibited the HFD-induced decrease in serum BDNF levels in rats. Subsequently, increased BDNF levels may have helped restore the impaired insulin and lipid metabolism in HFD-fed rats.

On the other hand, HFD intake can suppress dopamine related-genes and attenuate the dopaminergic system [15]. Biotin deficiency can diminish dopamine levels by impairing protein kinase A activity [69]. Sahin et al. [25] reported that MgB supplementation suppressed neuroinflammation-related markers and increased brain BDNF levels in propionic acid-induced autistic rats. High-dose MgB therapy might improve learning and memory by raising brain serotonin levels [25]. In the present study, the administration of MgB as a biotin source increased serum dopamine and serotonin levels in HFD-fed rats, which indicates that MgB may be involved in normalizing dopaminergic activity. BDNF signaling can regulate serotonin and dopamine transmission [12]. The induction of BDNF expression may be promoted by arginine [70], possibly through NO signaling [71]. Moreover, it has been reported that NO increases the release of serotonin and dopamine in the brain, and arginine, as a NO donor, can prevent the decrease in dopamine levels in rats [72]. Therefore, present results showed that MgB and ASI might cooperatively enhance BDNF/serotonin/dopamine interaction, which is essential for neuromodulation [73].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that combining MgB and ASI may improve antioxidant activity and modulate insulin and lipid-related molecular pathways, thereby exhibiting synergistic benefits in enhancing energy metabolism in HFD-fed rats. These supplements improved insulin signaling via PPAR-γ and IRS-1 and countered fat buildup by moderating SREBP-1c/FAS activity and lowering serum cholesterol by inhibiting HMGR in the liver. Additionally, the combined treatment increased serum BDNF, serotonin, and dopamine levels, further aiding in energy metabolism regulation in HFD-fed rats. These outcomes indicate that MgB and ASI, mainly when used in tandem, could significantly improve liver lipid metabolism and offer the potential to prevent and treat obesity-related diseases, such as fatty liver. Nevertheless, further research on humans is essential to confirm their effectiveness and safety, as well as to investigate the potential of MgB and ASI in managing metabolic disorders associated with obesity.
