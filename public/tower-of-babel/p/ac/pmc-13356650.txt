Relationship between structural properties and functionality of common carp myofibrillar protein under low ionic strength conditions: Effects of high-intensity ultrasound
Authors: Dewei Kong, Xiaoming Guo, Shuangjing Li, Qian Liu, Qian Chen, Haotian Liu, Baohua Kong, Chao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Common carp, Myofibrillar protein, High-intensity ultrasound, Structural property, Solubility, Emulsifying property, Low ionic strength condition

Introduction

The common carp is one of the most widely consumed freshwater fish species worldwide and serves as an important source of high-quality dietary protein [1]. Common carp contains approximately 20 % protein, of which myofibrillar protein (MP) constitutes the predominant fraction (50 %∼55 %) and is the most abundant and important protein [2]. MP is primarily composed of myosin and actin, which assemble into highly ordered filamentous structures. MP is a salt-soluble protein and can only be dissolved in a high-salt environment (> 0.3 M) [3]. Therefore, MP is a key contributor to the texture, water retention, and processing functionality of muscle-based foods [4]. Due to its filamentous architecture and strong intermolecular interactions, MP exhibits complex structure–function relationships that are highly sensitive to environmental conditions such as ionic strength, pH, and processing treatments.

The insolubility of myosin at low ionic strength is primarily attributed to the presence of periodically distributed charge clusters with different charges in the myosin tails (rods) [5]. In high-salt solutions, the presence of Na+ and Cl− effectively screens electrostatic interactions and interacts with the charge clusters in the myosin tails, thereby promoting the dissociation of myosin filaments and enhancing protein-water interactions, which increases the solubility of myosin [6]. In contrast, in low-salt solutions, the reduction of Na+ and Cl− weakens their shielding effect with the charge clusters in the myosin rods, which strengthens the hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction between the myosin molecules, thus inducing myosin self-assembly [3], [4]. Such self-assembly behavior mainly affects the physicochemical properties and processing functionality of myofibrillar proteins under low ionic strength condition, including solubility, dispersion stability, and emulsifying capacity [7], [8]. In addition, a high-salt diet was closely related to a risk of hypertension and cardiovascular diseases, which adversely affect human health [9], [10]. Consequently, reducing salt content in meat products has become an important trend in the food industry. However, the insolubility of meat proteins (especially myosin) at low ionic strength greatly hinders the use of meat in fluid products, such as meat beverages. Therefore, there is an urgent need to improve the solubility and dispersion stability of MP under low ionic conditions to expand its application in low-salt and functional food systems. In addition to solubility, the functional properties of MP, particularly emulsifying capacity and stability, are also critically important for the development of restructured and fluid meat products [11]. Protein solubility is generally considered a prerequisite for effective interfacial adsorption and the formation of stable emulsions [12]. The aggregation of MP not only reduces its solubility but also limits its ability to rapidly adsorb to oil droplet under low ionic strength conditions, thereby weakening its emulsifying performance [13]. Therefore, strategies that can simultaneously enhance solubility and interfacial functionality of MP are of significant interest for improving product quality and processing performance.

Currently, there is an increasing number of reports on improving the functional properties of MP under low-salt conditions, such as through ultrasound [14], magnetic fields [15], high-pressure homogenization [16], phosphorylation [17], and glycosylation[18]. Among them, physical modification has received increasing attention as it is simple to operate, and less likely to produce harmful substances[19]. As a promising and environmentally friendly technology, probe-type high-intensity ultrasound (HIU) has been widely used as an effective approach for physical modifications when applied at an intensity range of 10 ∼ 1000 W/cm2 and a frequency of 20 ∼ 100 kHz [20], [21]. The principle of ultrasound modification is that cavitation bubbles are formed during ultrasound transmission; when the cavitation bubbles reach a critical size, they will collapse or implode violently and trigger high shear and turbulence [22]. These extreme localized conditions can disrupt non-covalent interactions within protein molecules, leading to conformational changes such as unfolding, aggregation or disaggregation of proteins [14]. Furthermore, the strong cavitation and mechanical effects generated by ultrasound have been widely reported to induce structural modifications in proteins, thereby altering their physicochemical and functional properties [23]. Gao et al. [24] found that moderate ultrasound power induced a decrease in the α-helix content of pea isolate proteins, an unfolding of the structure, and an increase in the protein solubility. Previous studies have also found that proper ultrasound reduces the size of pork MP in water, in turn improving the protein’s solubility [25]. In addition, Yang et al. [26] reported that ultrasound could improve the MP gel properties under low-salt conditions by effectively regulating the thermal aggregation process of MP. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that high-intensity ultrasound was a feasible and effective strategy for enhancing the functional properties of MP under low-salt condition.

Currently, most existing reporters have focused on terrestrial animal proteins or plant proteins, while systematic investigations on aquatic MP under low ionic strength remain limited. However, the intrinsic properties of MP vary significantly among species, particularly between aquatic and terrestrial animals, because of differences in muscle structure, myosin isoforms, and amino acid composition. Although several studies have investigated ultrasound-assisted modification of common carp MP, their research objectives and experimental systems differ substantially from the present work. For example, previous studies mainly focused on the synergistic effects of ultrasound combined with MgCl2 in reduced-sodium systems or the protective effects of ultrasound thawing on protein denaturation during frozen storage [27], [28]. In contrast, the present study specifically focuses on the direct regulation of common carp MP by high-intensity ultrasound under low ionic strength conditions without the addition of salts or other modifiers. This experimental system provides an opportunity to investigate the dissociation behavior of myosin filaments and the corresponding structural and functional changes induced solely by ultrasound treatment. We hypothesized that appropriate high-intensity ultrasound treatment could disrupt the self-assembled myosin filament structures formed under low ionic strength conditions, thereby promoting conformational rearrangement and molecular unfolding of MP. These structural changes were expected to enhance the exposure of reactive groups, improve protein dispersion and solubility, and consequently increase the emulsifying properties of MP. Furthermore, it was hypothesized that there would be strong correlations between ultrasound-induced structural alterations and functional improvements. Therefore, the effects of high-intensity ultrasound at varying power levels on the physicochemical (particle size, zeta potential, and turbidity), structural (microstructure, secondary and tertiary structure), and functional properties (solubility, rheological behavior, and emulsifying properties) of common carp MP under low ionic strength were systematically investigated in this study. By integrating spectroscopic analysis, microstructural observation, and multivariate statistical methods (hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA)), the relationships between structural alterations and functional properties were elucidated, providing new insights into the mechanism of ultrasound-induced functional properties improvement and offering theoretical support for the development of low-salt aquatic protein products. Importantly, the novelty of this study lies in three aspects: (i) it establishes a simplified low ionic strength system that isolates ultrasound effects without interference from salts or chemical modifiers; (ii) it provides a direct structure–function linkage based on filament dissociation behavior rather than only empirical functional enhancement; and (iii) it integrates multiscale structural characterization with multivariate statistical analysis to clarify the mechanistic basis of ultrasound-induced functional transformation of MP. These features distinguish the present work from previous studies and provide new mechanistic insight into protein behavior under low-salt processing conditions.

Materials and methods

Materials

The fresh common carp was purchased from the Haoyouduo supermarket (Harbin, Heilongjiang, China). Sodium chloride (NaCl) was purchased from Kermel Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Disodium hydrogen phosphate and sodium dihydrogen phosphate were obtained from Ruijinte Chemical Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Guanidine hydrochloride, 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitro-benzoic acid) (DTNB), and 1-anilinonaphtha­lene-8-sulfonic acid (ANS) were supplied from Solabio Corp. (Beijing, China). The reagents mentioned above were of analytical-grade purity.

MP extraction

Fresh common carp (Cyprinus carpio) was an average body weight of approximately 1.0 ∼ 1.2 kg. The fish were slaughtered by the supplier according to commercial processing practices prior to purchase. After purchase, the samples were immediately transported to the laboratory under refrigerated conditions using insulated containers containing ice packs, and the temperature was maintained below 4 °C during transportation. Since the fish used in this study were commercially obtained from a local market and no live animal experiments were conducted in this study, ethical approval was not required. Upon arrival, the dorsal muscle was separated, minced, and immediately used for MP extraction.

Following the methods of Zhang et al. [29], the MP was extracted at 4 °C. Briefly, the minced fish muscle (200 g) was homogenized with a 10-fold volume of phosphate buffer (20 mM, 25 mM NaCl, 5.0 mM EDTA, pH 7.0) for 2 min and filtered through a single layer of gauze. The filtrate was then centrifuged to collect the precipitate. Subsequently, the precipitate was resuspended in a 10-fold volume of phosphate buffer. The mixture was homogenized for 1 min at 8000 rpm using a T25 ULTRA-TURRAX® high-shear homogenizer (IKA-Werke GmbH & Co., Staufen, Germany) and centrifuged at 3500 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. This washing procedure was repeated twice. After the second homogenization, the mixture was filtered and centrifuged under the same conditions. Finally, the precipitate was resuspended in a 10-fold volume of sodium phosphate buffer and centrifuged again. The protein concentration of MP was determined using the Biuret method [30].

Sample preparation

The ultrasound treatment of MP was performed based on the method of Liu et al. [25]. The MP solution (10 mg/mL) was transferred into a beaker (100 mL). A titanium probe (6.0 mm in diameter) was inserted into the solution to a depth of 2.5 cm from the bottom. Ultrasound was applied at 20 kHz with different power levels (0, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) for 10 min (with a pulse mode of 3 s on and 3 s off) in an ultrasonic generator (Scientz Biotechnology Co., ltd., Ningbo, China). All treatments were conducted under temperature-controlled conditions to prevent excessive heating.

Particle distribution and size

Particle distribution and size of samples were determined according to Chen et al. [31]. The sample was analyzed using a laser particle size analyzer (Microtrac S3500, York, PA, USA) at a refractive index of 1.45. The MP samples were adjusted to a uniform protein concentration of 10 mg/mL prior to analysis, and the same concentration was used for all treatment groups.

Zeta potential

The zeta potential of samples was measured using a Malvern zeta potential analyzer (Nano-90S, Worcestershire, UK) [2]. The sample was diluted to 0.1 mg/mL to minimize multiple scattering effects and subsequently loaded into zeta potential cuvettes for measurement.

Turbidity

Turbidity was evaluated based on the methods of Sun et al. [32]. Briefly, MP samples were adjusted to a concentration of 1 mg/mL using the buffer, and the absorbance was recorded at 600 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Tokyo, Japan) with the buffer as the blank.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM image of samples was performed based on Han et al. [33]. The samples (1 mg/mL) were stained with 0.2 % (w/v) phosphotungstic acid for 1 min. After staining, excess solution was removed, and the samples were air-dried at 25 °C for 3 h. The microstructure of the samples was then observed using a TEM (HT7800, Hitachi Co., Tokyo, Japan) at a magnification of 4000 × .

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM)

The microstructure was also observed using CLSM according to the method described by Badar et al. [34]. Briefly, twenty microliters of Nile blue solution (0.1 %, w/v) were mixed with 1 mL of MP solution (10 mg/mL), and then incubated in the dark. The stained samples (10 μL) were placed onto a glass slide and fluorescence images were then acquired using a CLSM (Leica TCS SP5, Heidelberg, Germany) equipped with a 40 × objective lens.

SDS-PAGE

SDS-PAGE analysis was conducted based on Zhang et al. [35]. Briefly, MP samples (2 mg/mL, 0.5 mL) were mixed with an equal volume of sample buffer (4 % w/v SDS, 20 % v/v glycerol, 0.125 M Tris-HCl, 0.02 % w/v bromophenol blue, with or without 10 % β-mercaptoethanol), and the mixture was heated at 100 °C. Aliquots (15 μL) were loaded onto polyacrylamide gels. Electrophoresis was carried out at 80 V and then increased to 120 V. After electrophoresis, the gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 for 15 min and then destained with a destaining solution (5 % ethanol, 7.5 % acetic acid) until clear protein bands were visible.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra were collected according to a previously described method [36]. The spectra of the different treated samples were recorded by scanning 256 cumulative scans between 4000 and 400 cm−1 with a resolution of 2.0 cm−1. The amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1) was deconvoluted and analyzed using PeakFit v4.12 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to derive the secondary structure content.

Reactive sulfhydryl content

The reactive sulfhydryl content was determined following Yu et al. [37]. Briefly, MP solution (1 mg/mL, 1 mL) was mixed with 4 mL of buffer solution (0.086 M Tris, 0.09 M glycine, 4 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and 50 μL of Ellman’s reagent. The mixture was incubated for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the reaction mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 412 nm. The reactive sulfhydryl content was calculated according to Equation (1).

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity was determined following Zhang et al. [38]. Briefly, twenty microliters of 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) solution (8 mM) were added to 4 mL of MP solutions at different concentrations (0 ∼ 0.1 mg/mL), and the mixture was incubated for 30 min in the dark. Fluorescence intensity was recorded using a F-7000 fluorescence spectrometer (Hitachi Co., Tokyo, Japan) with an excitation wavelength of 390 nm and an emission wavelength of 470 nm. The slit widths for both excitation and emission were set at 5 nm. Surface hydrophobicity was expressed as the initial slope of the fluorescence intensity versus protein concentration plot obtained by linear regression analysis.

UV absorbance spectroscopy

The UV absorption spectra were determined following Zhu et al. [39]. Briefly, samples (0.5 mg/mL) were scanned using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Tokyo, Japan) over a wavelength range of 200 ∼ 340 nm.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy was performed according to the method described by Zhang et al. [40]. Briefly, fluorescence spectra were recorded for samples (1 mg/mL) using the following settings: excitation wavelength of 283 nm, emission wavelength range of 300 ∼ 400 nm, slit widths of 5 nm for both excitation and emission.

Solubility

Protein solubility was determined following Huang et al. [41]. Briefly, samples were adjusted to a concentration of 5 mg/mL and centrifuged at 4 °C (3500 × g, 10 min). Solubility was calculated as the percentage ratio of protein content in the supernatant after centrifugation to the total protein content before centrifugation. The protein content in both the supernatant and the original MP suspension was determined using the Biuret method, as described by the standard protocol.

Rheological behavior

The rheological behavior of MP solutions were measured using a Discovery HR-1 rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) [42]. The MP solution used for rheological analysis had a protein concentration of 10 mg/mL. After equilibrating at 20 °C for 5 min, the apparent viscosity was measured over a shear rate range of 1 ∼ 100 s−1 using parallel plates with a 1 mm gap and a 25 mm diameter.

Emulsion activity index (EAI) and emulsion stability index (ESI)

The EAI and ESI were measured according to a previous study [43]. For emulsion preparation, soybean oil was used as the oil phase, and MP solutions (10 mg/mL) served as the aqueous phase. The oil-to-water volume ratio was 1:4 (v/v). The mixture was homogenized at 10,000 rpm for 3 min using a T25 ULTRA-TURRAX® high-shear homogenizer (IKA-Werke GmbH & Co., Staufen, Germany). The freshly homogenized emulsions were allowed to stand for 0 and 10 min, respectively. Aliquots (50 μL) were taken and diluted to 5 mL with 0.1 % SDS solution. The absorbance was measured at 500 nm using UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-2600, Tokyo, Japan). The EAI and ESI were calculated according to Equation (2) and (3), respectively.

Microstructure of emulsion

The emulsion microstructure was observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) [44]. Briefly, one milliliter of samples was sequentially stained with 15 μL of Nile Red and 20 μL of Nile Blue. Nile Red was used to stain the oil phase, whereas Nile Blue was used to stain the protein-rich aqueous phase. After staining, five microliters of the sample were placed onto a glass slide and then covered. Finally, the microstructure of the emulsion was then visualized using a CLSM (Leica TCS SP5, Heidelberg, Germany) equipped with a 40 × HCPL objective.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was carried out with three replications, and the determination of the indicators was repeated three times. Data were analyzed by the general linear model in Statistix 8.1 (Analytical Software, St Paul, MN, USA) and presented as means ± standard error (SE). Significant differences between samples were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons (P < 0.05). Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA) were conducted using OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, USA) based on the standardized dataset.

Results and discussion

Analysis of physicochemical properties

Particle distribution and size

The changes in particle distribution (A) and size (B) of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 1A and Fig. 1B, respectively. The particle size distribution of natural MP was primarily concentrated in the 100 ∼ 1000 μm range, exhibiting a broad distribution peak and larger particle sizes (P < 0.05). As the ultrasound power increased, intact myosin filaments were gradually disrupted, resulting in a decrease in particle size and a more uniform distribution, while the peak of the particle size distribution shifted toward smaller particles overall. This indicated that high-intensity ultrasound treatment could effectively break down protein macromolecular aggregates, converting them into smaller particles and thereby significantly reducing the particle size of the system [45]. When the ultrasound power was further increased to 500 W and 600 W, the particle size distribution curves exhibited a bimodal distribution, with peaks in the 1 ∼ 10 μm and 10 ∼ 100 μm ranges, respectively. Notably, the peak in the 1 ∼ 10 μm range was significantly higher for the 500 W treatment than for the 600 W treatment, and the corresponding mean particle size was the smallest (P < 0.05), indicating that most myosin filaments had been effectively dissociated into single molecules or small molecular aggregates. This result was consistent with the observations of microstructure (Fig. 2), namely that the MP particles in the 500 W treatment group were the smallest and had the most uniform distribution. However, under 600 W treatment conditions, the peaks in the 1 ∼ 10 μm range showed a tendency to shift toward larger particle sizes, while the peak intensity in the 10 ∼ 100 μm range increased significantly. This was likely due to the significant thermal and cavitation effects generated by excessively high ultrasonic power, which promoted hydrophobic interactions between proteins, thereby inducing their reaggregation into uneven, large-particle structures and resulting in increased particle size [33]. As shown in Fig. 1B, the untreated MP exhibited a relatively large mean particle size of 136.97 μm, indicating the presence of extensive myofibrillar protein aggregation and strong filamentous network structures under low ionic strength conditions. With increasing ultrasound power, the mean particle size decreased significantly in a power-dependent manner. Specifically, the particle size decreased slightly at 100 W (134.30 μm), followed by a more pronounced reduction at 200 W (117.67 μm), 300 W (54.67 μm), and 400 W (29.78 μm), reaching the minimum value at 500 W (14.76 μm). All treatments except 100 W showed a significant reduction compared with the control (P < 0.05), indicating that low-power ultrasound was insufficient to disrupt the highly ordered myosin filament structures, whereas moderate and high-power ultrasound effectively promoted protein disintegration. This progressive decrease in particle size suggests that HIU treatment facilitated the disruption of myosin thick filament assemblies and weakened intermolecular interactions (e.g., electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding), leading to the breakdown of large protein aggregates into smaller particles [22]. The most pronounced reduction was observed at 500 W, where the mean particle size decreased by nearly 90 % compared with the untreated sample. This result indicates that moderate HIU at 500 W achieved the most efficient balance between mechanical cavitation and energy input, promoting extensive dissociation of myofibrillar protein aggregates into smaller particles or even individual protein molecules. However, when ultrasound power was further increased to 600 W, the mean particle size increased again to 26.80 μm. This rebound effect may be attributed to excessive cavitation intensity and localized thermal effects at high ultrasound power, which can enhance hydrophobic interactions and promote protein–protein reaggregation [25]. Overall, 500 W ultrasound treatment effectively disrupted large aggregate clusters, forming smaller protein particles with a uniform size distribution. This significantly increased specific surface area of MP and enhanced the interaction between MP and water molecules, thereby improving the solubility and dispersion of MP in water.

Zeta potential

The zeta potential is an important parameter reflecting protein stability through changes in the amount of surface charge on suspended particles in a system [46]. The changes in zeta potential of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 1C. The natural MP sample exhibited the lowest absolute zeta potential value of 9.06 mV. After HIU treatment, the zeta potential values were − 9.82 mV (100 W), −11.27 mV (200 W), −13.75 mV (300 W), −15.49 mV (400 W), −17.81 mV (500 W), and − 15.74 mV (600 W), respectively. As the ultrasound power increased, the absolute zeta potential of the samples reached a maximum of 17.81 mV at 500 W (P < 0.05). This indicated that moderate ultrasound treatment could generate strong electrostatic repulsive forces between MP molecules, thereby enhancing the stability of the MP solution. The cavitation effects disrupted the originally compact filamentous myosin structure, exposing the initially buried charged residues and binding sites on the surface of the protein particles [25]. Furthermore, ultrasound treatment altered the electrostatic balance in the tail region, disrupting the self-assembly process of myosin monomers under low ionic strength condition, thereby preventing the formation of macromolecular proteins [13]. However, a further increase in ultrasound power would lead to some degree of MP aggregation, which could be attributed to the re-embedding of certain charged groups within the protein. Ma et al. [47] also found that excessive ultrasound reduced the absolute value of the zeta potential when exploring ultrasound-assisted treatment to increase soluble yeast protein content under different treatment conditions. In summary, high-intensity ultrasound treatment with an optimum at 500 W enhanced the absolute zeta potential of MP by disrupting myosin filament structure and exposing charged groups.

Turbidity

Turbidity can be used to characterize the degree of protein dispersion and aggregation in solution systems [48], [49]. The changes in turbidity of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 1D. The turbidity of the natural MP was the highest among all treatment groups, reaching 0.946, indicating that natural MP was prone to aggregation. After HIU treatment, the turbidity values decreased to 0.930, 0.901, 0.736, 0.653, 0.611, and 0.603 at ultrasound powers of 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W, respectively. Compared with the untreated sample, all HIU-treated groups exhibited significantly lower turbidity values (P < 0.05), indicating that ultrasound treatment effectively improved the dispersion state of MP under low ionic strength conditions. This could be explained by the fact that ultrasound treatment disrupted the non-covalent and covalent interactions that maintain the internal structure of the protein [50]. When the ultrasound power increased to 500 W, the turbidity of MP was lower than other treatment groups (P < 0.05). This indicated that an appropriate ultrasound power promoted the dissociation and unfolding of myosin filaments, resulting in smaller protein particles and thereby reducing solution turbidity. In summary, high-intensity ultrasound reduced the turbidity of myofibrillar protein solutions by disrupting intermolecular interactions and promoting the dissociation of myosin filaments into smaller and more uniformly distributed protein particles.

Microstructure

The changes in images of CLSM and TEM of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 2A and Fig. 2B, respectively. Natural MP exhibited significant large-sized aggregates with a loose and uneven distribution, indicating that larger aggregate structures had formed between natural MP molecules through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding. As the ultrasound power increased (100 ∼ 400 W), the microstructure of MP underwent significant changes. CLSM images showed that large particles were gradually broken down into smaller particles, and the fluorescence signal distribution became more uniform. The TEM results further confirmed that MP aggregates were effectively disaggregated into smaller units and uniformly dispersed in water, resulting in a significant reduction in particle size and improved dispersibility. This was primarily attributed to the intense shear forces and microjet effects generated by ultrasonic cavitation, which disrupted the myosin filament structure and intermolecular non-covalent interactions, thereby promoting the transformation of large aggregates into smaller particles [51]. When the ultrasound power was increased to 500 W, the CLSM images showed a dense and uniform fluorescence distribution, while the TEM images revealed small and well-dispersed particles, indicating that the ultrasound treatment achieved optimal results in disaggregating protein aggregates. These structural characteristics were favorable for increasing the specific surface area of the MP and water molecules, thereby improving its solubility. However, when the ultrasound power was further increased to 600 W, a certain degree of reaggregation occurred in the microstructure of MP. Fluorescence enhancement in localized areas of the CLSM images and an increase in particle size in the TEM images indicate that small particles have reaggregated. This might be attributed to thermal effects and protein conformational unfolding caused by high-intensity ultrasound, which exposed more hydrophobic groups and thereby promoted intermolecular reaggregation to form larger and irregular structures. In summary, moderate ultrasound treatment (particularly at 500 W) could effectively disrupt MP aggregates and significantly reduce particle size, whereas excessively high power might cause MP re-aggregation. This finding was consistent with the trends observed in particle size distribution and solubility.

Analysis of structural properties

SDS-PAGE

The changes in SDS-PAGE profiles of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 3. MP was composed of myosin heavy chains (MHC), actin, myosin, C-protein, and myosin light chains, with MHC and actin being the primary components [46]. No significant changes in MHC bands were observed for ultrasound powers of 100 and 200 W compared to the control group. The MHC bands became significantly lighter, and no new small molecule bands were generated after ultrasound powers of 300 W. This showed that the mechanical force produced by ultrasound could dissociate the MHC in the myosin tails, and the process did not involve the breaking or shortening of the polypeptide chain. It did not involve the degradation of the individual myosin. These changes increased the accessibility of water molecules to individual myosin [52]. In the absence of β-mercaptoethanol, the oligomers increased with increasing ultrasound power with concomitant loss of MHC. Specifically, the oligomeric band appeared above the myosin heavy chain (MHC) band, and the intensity gradually increased with increasing ultrasound power. When β-mercaptoethanol was present, the oligomer bands disappeared, while most of the lost MHC was recovered. The reason for this phenomenon was that the highly reactive oxygen radicals generated by ultrasound induced a certain degree of protein oxidation. The cysteine residues distributed in the tails of the MHC rods tended to form intermolecular disulfide bonds, which induced the formation of oligomers [18].

FT-IR

The changes in FT-IR spectra and deconvoluted amide Ⅰ band intensity of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 4A and Fig. 4B. The amide A band (3200 ∼ 3400 cm−1) can reflect intermolecular N-H and O-H stretching vibrations bonded by hydrogen bonds, whereas the amide I band and amide II band can correspond to C=O stretching and N-H bending vibrations. In Fig. 4A, the effect of ultrasound treatment on the overall FT-IR spectra was small, which indicated that the protein polypeptide chain backbone was not significantly altered, especially in the amide I band and amide II band. However, a slight blueshift of the amide A band occurred after ultrasound treatment. This might be because ultrasound treatment disrupted the chemical bonds maintained by N-H and O-H in the amide A band and altered the interactions between the MP molecules, thus dissociating the protein polymeric structure. The amide I band (1600 ∼ 1700 cm−1) can reflect changes in the MP secondary structure [53]. The changes in secondary content of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 4C-F. In Fig. 4C and Fig. 4D, the ultrasound treatment resulted in a significant decrease in α-helix content and a significant increase in β-sheet content compared to the control group (P < 0.05). The α-helix structure in MP was mostly present in the tail region of the myosin superhelix, and the physical forces generated by ultrasound resulted in the disruption of the hydrogen bonds that maintained the α-helix structure, which in turn caused a reduction of α-helix content with concomitant formation of β-sheet [54]. The decreased α-helix content suggested that the ability of myosin to assemble thick filaments in the post-ultrasound sample was weakened. Moreover, the reduction of the α-helix content caused the exposure of hydrophobic groups. In Fig. 4E and Fig. 4F, compared to native MP, the ultrasound treatment led to a decrease of the random coil content (P < 0.05), an insignificant change in the β-turn content at low power (0 ∼ 200 W) (P > 0.05), and a significant change in the β-turn content at high power (300 ∼ 500 W) (P < 0.05). The ultrasound cavitation disrupted peptide and hydrogen bonds, which induced protein unfolding, depolymerization, and rearrangement. Li et al. [55] also reported a similar result. Interestingly, ultrasound treatment at 600 W resulted in an increase in α-helix content compared to 500 W, which was correlated with the reaggregation between MP molecules. This phenomenon may be attributed to the excessive cavitation and localized heating generated at high ultrasound power, which promote intermolecular hydrophobic interactions and protein reaggregation. During this process, some previously unfolded polypeptide chains may undergo partial refolding, leading to the recovery of ordered α-helical structures. Therefore, the increase in α-helix content at 600 W likely reflects the formation of reaggregated protein structures rather than further protein unfolding. In summary, high-intensity ultrasound treatment induced conformational rearrangements in MP by disrupting hydrogen bonds and reducing α-helix content while increasing β-sheet structure.

Reactive sulfhydryl content

The changes in reactive sulfhydryl content of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 5A. The natural MP had the lowest content of reactive sulfhydryl groups, at 31.78 μmol/g, indicating that some of the sulfhydryl groups were originally located within the protein molecule and existed in the form of disulfide bonds. Compared to natural MP, the reactive sulfhydryl content increased significantly with increasing ultrasound power (P < 0.05). During ultrasound treatment, the ultrasound-induced cavitation effect which caused partial denaturation of the protein structure promoted the exposure of sulfhydryl groups and the breaking of disulfide bonds [56], [57]. The reactive sulfhydryl group content reached a maximum at an ultrasound power of 500 W, increasing from 31.78 μmol/g to 49.04 μmol/g. It was most likely that ultrasound treatment induced partial unfolding and conformational rearrangement of myosin molecules through cavitation and shear effects, resulting in the disruption of intramolecular interactions and the exposure of sulfhydryl groups originally buried within the protein interior. This structural loosening increased the accessibility of reactive sulfhydryl groups on the surface of MP [58]. This was in corroboration with the above results (Section 3.1.4 Microstructure), indicating that ultrasound treatment could disrupt the assembly of filamentous myosin and promote the dissociation of large protein aggregates through the weakening of intermolecular non-covalent interactions. The decrease in the reactive sulfhydryl content at 600 W was due to the highly reactive oxygen radicals generated by excessive ultrasound treatment oxidized the reactive sulfhydryl groups on the MP surface. This finding was consistent with the study of chicken actomyosin reported by Zou et al. [59], who found that moderate ultrasound treatment induced protein unfolding, decreased α-helix content, and increased reactive sulfhydryl groups by exposing buried sulfhydryl residues. These structural changes subsequently improved the functional properties of actomyosin.

Different lowercase letters indicate that the results show statistically significant differences among the different ultrasound powers.

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity reflects the exposure degree of hydrophobic amino acid residues on the protein surface and is considered an important indicator of protein conformational changes and molecular unfolding [60], [61]. Variations in surface hydrophobicity can provide valuable information regarding structural rearrangements and the accessibility of hydrophobic groups in MP molecules. As shown in Fig. 5B, the untreated MP exhibited the lowest surface hydrophobicity (P < 0.05), which can be attributed to the formation of filamentous myosin aggregates mediated by electrostatic interactions under low ionic strength conditions. The hydrophobic groups were buried within the compact structure, which limited their interaction with ANS, thus resulting in the lowest surface hydrophobicity. As the ultrasound power increased to 500 W, the surface hydrophobicity increased significantly (P < 0.05), indicating enhanced exposure of hydrophobic groups on the MP surface. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the cavitation-induced shear forces, which disrupted the dense myosin filament structure and promoted the dissociation and partial unfolding of protein assemblies (e.g., monomers, dimers, and oligomers), thereby exposing hydrophobic residues previously buried within the protein. Under low ionic strength conditions, moderate ultrasound treatment promotes the exposure of charged and hydrophobic residues and increases the absolute zeta potential of MP, thereby enhancing electrostatic repulsion between protein molecules and inhibiting myosin filament reassembly. Under these conditions, electrostatic repulsion predominates, resulting in improved protein dispersion and solubility [62]. However, when the ultrasound power was further increased to 600 W, the surface hydrophobicity decreased significantly compared with that at 500 W (P < 0.05). Excessive ultrasonication may induce extensive protein unfolding and increase intermolecular collisions, thereby strengthening hydrophobic interactions among unfolded molecules. Under these conditions, hydrophobic attraction becomes dominant, promoting protein reaggregation and leading to the re-burial of hydrophobic groups. Therefore, the balance between electrostatic repulsion and hydrophobic interactions determines the aggregation behavior of MP under different ultrasound intensities. Lan et al. [14] reported a similar trend, where surface hydrophobicity increased with ultrasound power (200 ∼ 600 W) due to protein unfolding and exposure of buried hydrophobic residues, but decreased at excessively high power (800 W), which was attributed to oxidative modification of hydrophobic amino acid residues and structural rearrangement of proteins. In summary, high-intensity ultrasound treatment increased the surface hydrophobicity of MP by exposing buried hydrophobic residues.

UV absorbance spectroscopy

The ultraviolet absorption spectra is used to analyze changes in the tertiary structure of proteins by detecting changes in the microenvironment of aromatic amino acids [63]. The changes in ultraviolet absorption spectra of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 5C. The natural MP exhibited relatively low absorbance intensity in the range of 200 ∼ 340 nm, indicating that the aromatic amino acid residues were largely buried within the compact tertiary structure. With increasing ultrasound power (100 ∼ 500 W), the absorbance intensity of MP progressively increased. This enhancement suggested a gradual exposure of chromophoric groups, which was due to the disruption of the tertiary structure induced by ultrasonic cavitation. The mechanical effects (e.g., shear forces and microstreaming) facilitated the unfolding of protein molecules, thereby exposing previously buried aromatic residues to the aqueous environment and increasing UV absorption intensity. Notably, when the ultrasound power reached 500 W, the absorbance intensity of MP approached a plateau, and no further significant increase was observed at 600 W. This indicated that the unfolding of MP molecules had reached a relatively stable state at 500 W, where most of the buried chromophores were already exposed. The absence of a continued increase at higher power might also be associated with the onset of protein re-aggregation. In summary, these results demonstrate that moderate ultrasound treatment effectively altered the tertiary structure of MP by promoting molecular unfolding and exposure of aromatic residues, whereas excessive ultrasound treatment did not further enhance structural disruption and might even induce structural rearrangement. The ultrasound treatment played a critical role in modulating the conformational changes of MP. In summary, high-intensity ultrasound treatment enhanced the ultraviolet absorption spectra of MP by promoting the unfolding of its tertiary structure and exposing aromatic residues.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

The tryptophan/tyrosine fluorescence detection can also reflect tertiary structural changes in the protein. The changes in fluorescence spectra of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 5D. The natural MP predominantly existed in aqueous solution as intact fibrillar structures, resulting in the lowest fluorescence intensity. With the increasing ultrasound power, the fibrillar structure was progressively disrupted, leading to the exposure of intrinsic fluorophores, and consequently an increase in fluorescence intensity. However, a decrease in fluorescence intensity was observed at 600 W, which could be attributed to the excessive exposure of hydrophobic regions, promoting intermolecular interactions and subsequent aggregation of MP, thereby re-burying tryptophan residues within the protein [64]. Moreover, high-intensity ultrasound treatment might induce the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) from water sonolysis, which could oxidize tryptophan residues and further contribute to the reduction in fluorescence intensity. Additionally, the maximum emission wavelength (λmax) of MP exhibited a red shift, increasing from 324.9 nm (0 W) to 330.2 nm (500 W). This red shift indicated that high-intensity ultrasound treatment disrupted the assembly of myosin filaments, thereby exposing more tryptophan residues to the aqueous environment and facilitating their transition from a nonpolar to a more polar microenvironment [37]. In summary, high-intensity ultrasound treatment increased the intrinsic fluorescence intensity and induced a red shift of λmax in MP by promoting the unfolding of its tertiary structure and exposing tryptophan residues to a more polar environment.

Analysis of functional properties

Solubility

Solubility not only reflects the dispersion state of proteins in a solution system, but is also a key factor influencing other functional properties. The changes in solubility and appearance of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 6A. The solubility of the natural MP was the lowest (1.21 %) (P < 0.05). This result supported the notion that myosin monomers tended to assemble into filamentous polymers in the tail region under low ionic strength conditions, thereby reducing their interaction with water and resulting in poor solubility. Upon ultrasound treatment (200 ∼ 600 W), the solubility of MP increased significantly, reaching an approximately 18-fold enhancement at 600 W compared with the natural MP (P < 0.05). This improvement in solubility could be primarily attributed to the mechanical effects of ultrasound, including turbulence and shear forces, which disrupted the non-covalent interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attractions) responsible for maintaining the highly ordered myosin filament structure. On the one hand, the disruption of these interactions reduced intermolecular associations among MP molecules, thereby enhancing their ability to interact with water molecules [65]. On the other hand, such structural disruption promoted the dissociation of MP aggregates into smaller particles, thereby increasing the specific surface area and facilitating protein–water interactions, which ultimately improved MP solubility [66]. This observation was highly consistent with the particle size results (Section 3.1.1), where the mean particle size continuously decreased from 136.97 μm in the untreated sample to 14.76 μm at 500 W. The smallest particle size corresponded to the highest solubility, indicating that particle size reduction played a crucial role in enhancing protein hydration and dispersion under low ionic strength conditions. Although the solubility remained relatively high at 600 W, the slight increase in particle size observed at this power level suggested partial protein reaggregation, which may have limited any further improvement in solubility. This finding further supports the close relationship between particle size and the solubility behavior of MP. Furthermore, the visual appearance of MP suspensions revealed marked differences in dispersion stability among treatments. The natural MP exhibited evident phase separation between protein and the aqueous phase, whereas samples treated at higher ultrasound power remained uniformly dispersed with minimal phase separation. These observations indicated that ultrasound treatment effectively inhibited the formation of myosin thick filaments, thereby enhancing both the solubility and dispersion stability of MP under low ionic strength conditions.

Different lowercase letters indicate that the results show statistically significant differences among the different ultrasound powers.

Rheological behavior

Rheological behavior is commonly employed to characterize protein–protein interactions, which play a crucial role in determining food functionality during processing [67]. The changes in rheological behavior of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 6B. The apparent viscosity exhibited a decreasing trend with increasing shear rate, which indicated that all samples exhibited a shear-thinning behavior. The irregular Brownian motion of MP molecules resulted in high viscosity due to aggregation at low shear rates. Myosin, with a characteristic rod-like chain structure, is easily oriented under shear. Therefore, as the shear rate increased, the applied shear force disrupted the weak interactions between myosin molecular chains, and the unfolded or elongated protein molecules became progressively aligned along the shear direction. This molecular orientation reduced intermolecular entanglement and frictional resistance, thereby leading to a decrease in the apparent viscosity [68]. Compared with the natural MP, the viscosity initially decreased and subsequently increased with increasing ultrasonic power, suggesting that moderate ultrasound enhanced the fluidity of MP systems. This observation was consistent with previous findings, such as those reported by Lee et al. [69], who demonstrated that high-pressure homogenization generated intense shear forces and rapid molecular motion, thereby disrupting protein–protein interactions and preventing aggregation, therefore leading to reduced viscosity. In general, the fluidity of protein systems was governed by the balance between protein–protein and protein-water interactions. The increased apparent viscosity observed at 600 W might therefore be attributed to strengthened protein–protein interactions, likely resulting from ultrasound-induced aggregation at excessive energy input.

Emulsifying properties

The emulsions are primarily stabilized by the formation of a compact interfacial film, which results from protein adsorption during emulsification [70]. The EAI and ESI reflect the ability of proteins to adsorb to and stabilize the oil droplet and are widely recognized as key indicators for evaluating protein emulsifying properties [71], [72]. As shown in Fig. 6C and Fig. 6D, the natural MP exhibited EAI and ESI values of 21.14 m2/g and 5.67 %, respectively, which were lower than all treated groups (P < 0.05), indicating its poor emulsifying properties. This behavior could be attributed to the highly ordered filamentous structure of myosin under low ionic strength condition, where strong intermolecular interactions promoted the formation of large aggregates. Such a compact structure limits protein solubility and conformational flexibility, thereby hindering the diffusion and adsorption of MP molecules. Moreover, the burial of hydrophobic residues within the protein interior reduces surface hydrophobicity, which weakens the affinity of MP for the oil phase and impairs the formation of a stable interfacial film. However, the MP treated at 500 W exhibited the highest EAI and ESI values (P < 0.05). Moderate ultrasound treatment (particularly at 500 W) effectively promoted the dissociation of myofibrillar structures and the release of myosin monomers, thereby enhancing protein adsorption at the oil–water interface during emulsification. In addition, the increase in surface hydrophobicity further facilitated the adsorption of MP, promoting the formation of a more cohesive and viscoelastic interfacial film [73]. Notably, the surface hydrophobicity reached its maximum at 500 W in this study, which correlates well with the highest EAI and ESI observed under this condition. This finding also confirmed that enhanced surface hydrophobicity improves both emulsifying activity and emulsion stability [64], [74]. At higher ultrasonic power (600 W), the extensive exposure of hydrophobic groups promoted intermolecular hydrophobic interactions, leading to protein reaggregation and the formation of larger aggregates. These reaggregated structures restricted molecular mobility and conformational flexibility, thereby impairing the ability of MP to effectively adsorb and rearrange at the oil–water interface [75]. Consequently, both EAI and ESI of MP decreased significantly at 600 W compared with those at 500 W (P < 0.05). In summary, high-intensity ultrasound treatment improved the emulsifying properties of MP by enhancing the solubility, surface hydrophobicity, and interfacial adsorption.

Microstructure of emulsion

The emulsion microstructure of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W) are shown in Fig. 6E. In the CLSM images, the oil phase was visualized as red fluorescent cores, while the protein phase appeared as a green fluorescent shell surrounding the droplets. In the emulsion stabilized by the natural MP, extensive flocculation and severe aggregation of large oil droplets were observed, indicating that the highly ordered myosin filament structure was less effective in adsorbing onto and stabilizing the oil–water interface. Moreover, in the continuous phase, the oil droplets were only partially coated by a limited amount of protein in the natural MP-stabilized emulsion, suggesting that myosin filaments exhibited poor interfacial affinity and tended to remain in the aqueous phase rather than adsorbing at the oil–water interface [13]. Following ultrasound treatment, the droplet size decreased significantly, and the emulsion droplets became more uniformly distributed, with a greater amount of protein adsorbed at the oil–water interface to encapsulate the oil droplets. These improvements could be attributed to ultrasound-induced unfolding of myosin, which enhanced molecular diffusion, interfacial adsorption, and structural rearrangement of MP at the oil-water interface [37]. Among the different ultrasound powers, the most pronounced effect was observed at 500 W, where the reduction in particle size was accompanied by a marked increase in surface hydrophobicity, suggesting that ultrasound-induced disintegration of myofibrillar protein aggregates facilitated the exposure of buried hydrophobic groups. This coordinated change likely enhanced protein adsorption and interfacial activity, thereby improving emulsion stability. However, at excessive ultrasound intensity (600 W), larger droplets reappeared, which may be attributed to protein re-aggregation driven by excessive exposure and interaction of hydrophobic groups, ultimately weakening interfacial stabilization.

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA)

HCA and PCA were used to investigate the relationship between different ultrasound powers and the variation of physicochemical, structural, functional properties in MP. Prior to multivariate analysis, all variables were standardized using Z-score normalization to eliminate scale effects among different parameters. Euclidean distance was used as the similarity metric, and Ward’s linkage method was applied for hierarchical clustering. The analysis was performed using OriginPro 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, USA), and the results were visualized as a dendrogram. According to the HCA result (Fig. 7A), the samples treated with different ultrasound powers could be divided into two groups. The first group (Group (1) was consisted of low ultrasound powers (0, 100, 200 and 300 W) and the second group (Group 2) was contained of high ultrasound powers (400, 500 and 600 W). The α-helix content, zeta potential, turbidity, and particle size in Group (1) were significantly higher than those in Group 2, while the surface hydrophobicity, ESI, solubility, λmax, EAI, and reactive sulfhydryl content were lower than those in Group 2, which suggested that the high-intensity ultrasound treatments significantly altered the structural, solubility and emulsifying properties of MP. Regarding Group 2, the samples treated with 400 W had similar functional properties to those treated with 600 W, while the samples treated with 500 W showed a more significant improvement in the structural, solubility and emulsifying properties. These results indicated that appropriate ultrasound power (500 W) changed the MP structure from rigid to flexible, thus increasing the contact area with water to increase the functional properties of MP under low ionic strength. In contrast, excessive ultrasound power caused MP to aggregate through the interaction of disulfide and hydrophobic bonds between exposed residues, thereby reducing the functional properties.

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and principal component analysis (PCA) of common carp MP under high-intensity ultrasound treatment (HIU) at different powers (0, 100, 200, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 W).

The loading and scored plots of PCA were shown in Fig. 7 B-C, respectively. PCA was performed on the same standardized dataset, and principal components were extracted based on the correlation matrix. Components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were retained for interpretation. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were used to construct score and loading plots. Fig. 7B effectively visualized the relationship between ultrasound powers and the structural and functional properties of the MP. Fig. 7C inferred that the treatments located in quadrants 1, 2, 3, and 4 are correlated with the variables in the corresponding quadrants. The former two principal components explained 94.9 % of the total variance (87.9 % PC1 and 7.0 % PC2). The variables including solubility, reactive sulfhydryl content, λmax, surface hydrophobicity, and EAI exhibited a close correlation with each other. In contrast, the α-helix content, zeta potential, turbidity, and particle size were negatively correlated with the indicators mentioned above. In combination with Fig. 7 B-C, the samples treated with low powers were in a different quadrant than those treated with high powers. The samples treated with high powers (400 ∼ 600 W) possessed similar functional properties. Solubility, reactive sulfhydryl groups, surface hydrophobicity, λmax, EAI, and ESI contributed most to the similarity. Furthermore, a significant change in separation was observed with high ultrasound powers compared to low ultrasound powers, especially at 500 W. In summary, the structural and functional properties of the MP changed continuously with different ultrasonic powers, of which the best improvement was achieved with 500 W.

Conclusions

The effects of different ultrasound powers on physicochemical, structural, and functional properties of common carp myofibrillar protein (MP) under low ionic strength conditions were systematically investigated in this work. The moderate high-intensity ultrasound treatment (particularly at 500 W) significantly reduced particle size, turbidity, and aggregation of MP while increasing zeta potential, indicating enhanced the dispersion stability of MP. According to the results of FT-IR, fluorescence, and UV spectroscopy., these changes were closely associated with structural rearrangements of MP, including disruption of myosin filament assembly, decreased α-helix content, increased β-sheet structure, and enhanced exposure of reactive sulfhydryl and hydrophobic groups. Consequently, these structural changes promoted the improvement of functional properties (notably solubility and emulsifying properties) by facilitating protein unfolding, increasing molecular flexibility, and enhancing interfacial adsorption. Multivariate analyses (HCA and PCA) further revealed clear clustering between low-power ultrasound treatments and high-power ultrasound treatments and demonstrated strong correlations between MP structure (e.g., α-helix, sulfhydryl content, surface hydrophobicity) and functional properties (e.g., solubility and emulsification). In conclusion, high-intensity ultrasound treatment at an optimal power (500 W) effectively transformed MP from a rigid and aggregated state into a more flexible and functional conformation, providing a promising strategy for improving MP functionality in low-salt food systems. However, this work focused solely on the individual effects of high-intensity ultrasound on common carp myofibrillar protein under low ionic strength conditions, without considering potential synergistic effects with other modification strategies. Future research could explore combined approaches, such as the integration of ultrasound with functional additives (e.g., alkaline amino acids), to further enhance protein structural modulation and functional properties in low-salt food systems.
