Dynamic evolution of bed separation volume and strata migration in coal seam groups under repeated mining
Authors: Bin Li, Hongsheng Wang, Lei Li, Weibin Guo, Jiahao Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To enhance mining safety in coal seam groups underlying ultra-thick sandstone aquifers, this study investigates the Yanjiahe Mine’s geological conditions through physical simulation, numerical modeling, and in-situ monitoring. We analyze the evolution patterns of bed-separation space in No. 5− 1, 5− 2, and 8 coal, revealing volume expansion characteristics across mining methods and strata migration mechanisms. Firstly, through physical simulation of upward mining of No. 8 and 5− 1 coal and downward mining of No. 5− 2 coal, it is determined that the bed-separation volume of coal seam group mining has the expansion characteristics of Fracture Type (FT) → Cavity Type (CT) → Cavity + Fracture Type (CFT)→ Fracture Type (FT). Secondly, through numerical simulation of upward mining of No. 8 and 5− 1 coal and downward mining of No. 5− 2 coal, it is determined that the bed-separation strata have the migration laws of Lower Type (LT) → Higher Type (HT) → Lower Type (LT). Finally, by observing the overlying strata of 8105, 5105, and 5205 working faces through borehole peeping, the evolution laws of bed-separation are obtained as Lower Fracture Type (LFT) → Lower Cavity Type (LCT) → Lower Cavity + Fracture Type (LCFT) → Higher Cavity Type (HCT)→ Higher Fracture Type (HFT)→ Lower Fracture Type (LFT).

Introduction

Increasing mining depth and thickness, combined with variable hydrogeological conditions, lead to frequent roof bed separation water hazards in coal mines. These hazards feature unobvious inrush precursors, large instantaneous water inflow, and periodic occurrences. Compared to overburden failure and bed separation evolution during single-seam mining, these processes under repeated multi-seam group mining are significantly more complex.

Numerous studies have investigated longwall-induced overburden fracture characteristics and bed separation development. Refs1,2 used physical testing to analyze separation horizon evolution and formation timing, establishing a ratio for maximum fractured zone height relative to seam thickness. Refs3–5 employed APSO algorithms and BP neural networks for dynamic, comprehensive prediction of water-conducting fracture zone height. Ref6 combined an improved stepwise comparison method with elastic thin plate theory to accurately predict separation location and volume. Ref7 proposed a reliability model considering spatial variability and strength uncertainty, deriving a function to assess water-conducting fracture zone spatial scale via overburden failure probability. Ref8 analyzed key influencing factors for separation water inrush and mapped regional risk levels. Refs9–12 used physical simulations to study fracture distribution/evolution in multi-seam mining overburden, analyzing strata movement effects on fracture propagation. Ref13 examined water-conducting fracture zone evolution and aquifuge integrity during repeated mining of shallow, closely-spaced seams in Northwest China. Ref14 investigated narrow gob-side coal pillars’ influence on water-conducting fracture zone height and morphology. Refs15,16 proposed a theoretical method quantifying overburden movement and separation evolution, defining a four-stage process. Refs17–19 identified key water-conducting fracture zone height controls: seam thickness, hard rock proportion, panel length, mining depth/dip, and inter-seam spacing. Ref20 developed a separation water inrush risk assessment method using statistics and simulations. Ref21 analyzed roof fracturing and water-conducting fracture zone height via SEM and simulations. Refs22–25 applied mechanical analysis to study separation mechanisms, proposing identification/assessment models. Ref26 found Cretaceous aquifer hydraulics controlled by four-stage separation space development using fiber-optic/magnetic data. Ref27–31 modeled repeated mining overburden to study separation evolution across mechanism, horizon, form, and space, the stress transfer and damage mechanism of rock strata have been studied. Refs32–34 analyzed water-conducting fracture zone spatiotemporal evolution and proposed a height prediction method for closely-spaced seams. Refs35,36 employs a variety of research methods to analyze the evolution of mining-induced fractures, derives the three-stage law of fracture evolution, and this law has been verified through the dual approaches of numerical simulation and on-site monitoring. Research on bed separation evolution under repeated multi-seam mining remains limited compared to single-seam mining. Compared to previous studies on single-seam mining, this research systematically reveals the dynamic evolution patterns of bed separation volume and horizon under repeated mining of coal seam groups, integrating physical simulation, numerical modeling, and field validation for the first time.

The overlying Luohe-Yijun Formation (127–243 m thick) at Yanjiahe Mine, characterized by thick bedding, high water content, and permeability, poses a severe threat to safe mining. This study investigates volumetric expansion and horizon migration of bed separation spaces during repeated mining of the coal seam group beneath this thick sandstone aquifer.

Project overview

Yanjiabe Coal Mine, located in the eastern part of the Binchang Mining Area within the Huanglong Jurassic Coalfield, contains three minable coal seams in ascending order: No. 5− 1 coal, No. 5− 2 coal, and No. 8 coal. The No. 5− 1 coal has an average thickness of 1.38 m. Its immediate roof primarily consists of mudstone, sandy mudstone, and sandstone. The No. 5− 2 coal seam has an average thickness of 1.07 m. Its immediate roof is primarily composed of mudstone, sandy mudstone, and sandstone.The No. 8 coal seam has an average thickness of 4.78 m. Its immediate roof is primarily formed by carbonaceous mudstone, sandy mudstone, and sandstone.

The vertical separation between the No. 5− 1 and 5− 2 coal seams ranges from 0.55 to 7.63 m, averaging 3.03 m. The No. 8 coal seam lies 10.31–49.64 m below the No. 5− 2 coal seam, averaging 30.60 m, indicating a coal seam group. Formation and expansion mechanisms of fracture-cavity bed-separation

Volume expansion behavior of bed-separation in upward mining

Based on the columnar section of Borehole X4 in Yanjiahe Coal Mine, a plane stress test stand was utilized to simulate the working face retreat mining process. The physical similarity model dimensions were length × height × width = 2.0 m × 1.5 m × 0.2 m. The model employed the following similarity ratios: geometric similarity ratio (α1) = 200, unit weight similarity ratio (αγ) = 1.67, time similarity ratio (αt)= 14.14, and strength similarity ratio (αr) = 334. The similarity ratios were determined based on the physical and mechanical properties of the rock strata in the study area, ensuring that the model accurately represents the field conditions. Overlying strata loading was simulated using physical counterweights. The mining simulation involved extracting multiple coal seams: upward mining sequences included No. 8 coal and No. 5− 1 coal, while downward sequences included No. 5− 1 coal and No. 5− 2 coal. Each excavation step measured 15 m, totaling 16 excavation steps with a cumulative excavation length of 240 m. The physical model is shown in Fig. 3.

Following the caving and compaction of the overlying strata, the bed separation in the middle section of the working face gradually tended to close. The roofs of the overlying No. 5− 1 coal and No. 5− 2 coal subsided uniformly as an integrated unit. The siltstone key stratum did not fracture. Volume expansion behavior of bed-separation in downward mining

Following completion of the No. 5− 1 coal extraction and subsequent overburden collapse-compaction, the No. 5− 2 coal was mined downward. Figure 6 illustrates the evolutionary characteristics of mining-induced separation in the overburden during No. 5− 2 coal extraction. The separation development exhibited distinct zonal features: At 60 m advancement: FT separation developed within the overlying roof strata following cave-in. At 120 m advancement: Separation primarily localized near the open-off cut and panel extremities, while the central zone compacted with persistent FT separation. At 240 m advancement (final stage): FT separation prevailed, demonstrating significantly greater height at panel boundaries compared to the No. 5− 1 coal case. The mid-gob separation transitioned to a compacted state, whereas boundary separation at the open-off cut and headgate reached 62.5 m height below the key siltstone stratum overlying the seam.

Progressive failure analysis revealed: As face advancement proceeded, roof caving generated predominantly fractured-type separation. Upon reaching the stopping line, although the separation zone did not extend to the roof of the Zhiluo Formation aquifer, partial vertical fractures connected the separation space to the aquifer. This connectivity initiated continuous drainage of the Zhiluo Formation groundwater, with the separation zone ultimately functioning as preferential flow pathways.

Panel extraction extended 240 m along the strike direction, with the surrounding rock represented by the Mohr-Coulomb constitutive model. Employing an upward mining sequence, we sequentially extracted the No. 8 coal followed by the No. 5− 1 coal. During the 240-m advancement of the No. 8 coal, the evolutionary behavior of mining-induced separation was simulated as illustrated in Fig. 8.

Fig. 8Evolution law of bed separation fractures after No. 8 coal mining.

Evolution law of bed separation fractures after No. 8 coal mining.

At 60 m advancement, extensive overburden fracture-induced caving occurred with first weighting of the main roof, forming LCT separation. At 120 m advancement, separation primarily localized at panel extremities, developing 5–10 m ahead of the open-off cut and 10 m behind the face as LCFT. At 180 m advancement, overburden rupture generated LCT composite separation. At 240 m advancement, separation developed 30 m ahead of the open-off cut and 20 m behind the face (height: 38 m), presenting as LFT within the siltstone key stratum beneath the No. 5− 1 coal roof.

Mechanical analysis (Fig. 8): Post-mining separation exhibited stratigraphic continuity due to progressive caving. The separation zone concentrated above the face and open-off cut side, with two distinct mechanisms: Higher space: Separation development in mid-face strata. Lower space: Dominant vertical fractures in floor strata. These zones remained mechanically disconnected except for partial linkages at panel boundaries. Crucially, the coal abutment near the open-off cut provided sufficient support to prevent subsidence, resulting in concentrated tensile-shear failure and significant downward deformation in adjacent strata.

Following stabilization of the No. 8 coal goaf, upward mining of the No. 5− 1 coal commenced. Figure 9 documents the separation evolution: At 60 m advancement: Key stratum flexural deformation induced synchronous subsidence, generating HFT separation 5 m ahead of the open-off cut. At 120 m advancement: HFT separation developed 10 m ahead of the open-off cut and above the face centerline. At 180 m advancement: Tensile-dominated propagation extended separation upward, forming HFT concentrations above the face center. At 240 m advancement: Separation expanded to 30 m ahead of the open-off cut and 20 m behind the face (height: 83 m) as HFT.

Comparative mechanics: Final separation elevation at 240 m advancement showed 18.2% vertical elevation increase compared to the No. 8 coal. Tensile-shear coupling effects enhanced both separation height (+ 118%) and width (+ 67%), with dominant fractured-type morphology concentrated along panel flanks.

Fig. 9Evolution law of bed separation fractures after No. 5− 1 coal mining.

Evolution law of bed separation fractures after No. 5− 1 coal mining.

During upward mining, partial failure of the siltstone key stratum facilitated upward propagation of the separation space. While the central overburden compacted with closed separation spaces lacking hydrogeological storage potential, the primary evolution space concentrated within the 30-m ahead of the open-off cut and 20-m behind the working face. This HT separation exhibited FT morphology with limited water storage capacity. Crucially, the combined effects of extraction thickness (5.2 m) and upward mining method resulted in a 118% increase in separation height compared to the underlying No. 8 coal, with FT separation dominating 89% of the total volume.

Strata evolution laws of bed-separation in downward mining

Following extraction and overburden compaction of the No. 5− 1 coal, downward mining of the No. 5− 2 coal commenced. Figure 10 delineates the separation evolution:

At 120 m advancement: Undercompacted fractured overburden concentrated above the open-off cut and face centerline, exhibiting HFT morphology at relatively LFT compared to the No. 5− 1 coal separation horizon.

At 240 m advancement: Progressive gradual compaction of central overburden occurred with face advance, shifting separation development toward panel flanks as HFT, predominantly within the 20-m zone ahead of the open-off cut and behind the working face.

Fig. 10Evolution law of bed separation fractures after No. 5− 2 coal mining.

Evolution law of bed separation fractures after No. 5− 2 coal mining.

After the completion of downward mining, the key silty sandstone stratum was damaged by tension, resulting in local subsidence. Bed separation developed in partial areas above the key stratum, which belonged to HT bed separation. The bed separation in the middle overlying strata was compacted and closed, failing to form water storage conditions. Affected by coal thickness, coal seam spacing, and mining methods, the evolution height of bed separation after downward mining of No. 5− 2 coal did not increase significantly compared with that after mining of No. 5− 1 coal, and even showed a decreasing trend in some areas due to the compaction and closure of overlying strata. The bed separation was mainly of the FT, with weak water storage capacity.

During the mining of coal seam groups, the volume expansion and positional migration of separation cavities exhibit distinct evolutionary patterns due to factors such as working face dimensions, inter-seam spacing, mining sequence, mining parameters, and overlying rock strength. The spacing between No. 8 coal and No. 5− 1 coal is greater than that between No. 5− 1 coal and No. 5− 2 coal, and the key stratum is located above No. 5− 1 coal. As a result, the separation cavities are more developed when mining is conducted in an ascending sequence (No. 8 coal followed by No. 5− 1 coal) compared to a descending sequence (No. 5− 2 coal).

Based on the analysis of the volume expansion of the bed separation space and the evolution law of the strata, the mining sequence should be adjusted and the mining parameters should be optimized during the extraction of close-distance coal seams. For Cavity Type separated beds, prediction of the strata position and volume of the bed separated, as well as advanced water exploration and drainage, should be carried out.

Conclusion

Physical simulation demonstrates that during upward mining of the No. 8 coal, the bed separation volume evolution exhibits an expansion pattern characterized by Fracture Type (FT) → Cavity Type (CT) → Cavity + Fracture Type (CFT)→ Fracture Type (FT). During upward mining of the No. 5− 1 coal, the evolution follows Cavity Type (CT) → Fracture Type (FT). During downward mining of the No. 5− 2 coal, the evolution persistently maintains a Fracture Type (FT) → Fracture Type (FT) pattern.Numerical simulation indicates that during upward mining of No. 8 coal, the bed separation is of Lower Type (LT); during mining of the No. 5− 1 coal, the bed separation is of Higher Type (HT); during downward mining of the No. 5− 2 coal, the bed separation is of Lower Type (LT).
