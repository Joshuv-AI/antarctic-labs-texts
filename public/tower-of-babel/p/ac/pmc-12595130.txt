Unraveling virus-host interactions: Current advances in viral lipidomics
Authors: Belen Requena, Coral Barbas, Carolina Gonzalez-Riano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryViruses significantly alter host lipid metabolism to facilitate their replication, assembly, and immune evasion. Lipidomics, a mass spectrometry-driven field, enables the comprehensive profiling of virus-induced lipid remodeling and provides crucial insights into host–pathogen interactions. This review provides a comprehensive overview of cutting-edge lipidomic research in viral infections, focusing on studies published from January 2023 onwards. Emphasis is placed on recent advancements in understanding key respiratory viruses (e.g., SARS-CoV-2), bloodborne pathogens (HIV, HCV), and emerging viral threats such as West Nile or the Dengue viruses. We examine the latest analytical platforms, annotation techniques, and biological findings, highlighting how specific alterations in glycerophospholipid, sphingolipid, and sterol pathways reveal novel diagnostic and therapeutic opportunities. While challenges in standardization, isomer annotation, and clinical translation persist, emerging MS technologies and computational strategies promise to overcome these limitations. Integrating lipidomics with systems biology approaches will be crucial for advancing precision virology and developing next-generation antiviral therapies.Subject areas: biological sciences, biotechnology, omics

Introduction

Viruses are acellular pathogens that critically depend on host cellular processes for replication, assembly, and dissemination. Historically responsible for devastating pandemics, their ability to strategically rewire host metabolism is central to their success. Among these, host lipid metabolism, especially lipid biosynthesis and trafficking, plays a pivotal role in supporting viral envelopes’ composition and replication, evading immune surveillance, and modulating inflammatory pathways.1,2 Recent advances in mass spectrometry (MS)-based lipidomics have revealed that viral infections induce profound and selective remodeling of the host lipidome, often in virus- and tissue-specific patterns. These perturbations are far from passive cellular responses. They represent active viral strategies to control membrane dynamics, signaling, trafficking, and modulate host signaling pathways.

The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the urgent need for system-level approaches to understanding host-virus interactions and identifying reliable biomarkers of disease progression, severity, and therapeutic response.3 Lipidomics, the large-scale study of lipids using MS-based techniques,4 has emerged as a powerful tool to characterize the dynamic lipid remodeling that takes place during viral infections. Viruses reprogram host lipid metabolism to generate replication organelles, evade immune surveillance, and control membrane curvature and trafficking.5 Both untargeted and targeted lipidomic studies have uncovered dysregulation in glycerophospholipids (GP), sphingolipids (SP), and cholesterol metabolism in infections such as SARS-CoV-2, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), West Nile virus (WNV), and others.6,7,8 Furthermore, integrating lipidomics with other omics approaches, such as transcriptomics and proteomics, in multi-omics studies is proving increasingly valuable for gaining a more complete understanding of the complex molecular mechanisms underlying viral infections.9,10 These insights pave the way for novel lipid-based biomarkers and host-directed therapeutic strategies. To provide a current perspective, this review critically examines recent developments in lipidomics applied to viral infections, focusing primarily on peer-reviewed articles published from January 2023 onwards in Q1-ranked journals according to the Journal Citation Reports (JCR), identified through searches in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. Additionally, only peer-reviewed articles in English, carried out in a pathogen agent, human cell lines, and biological human-derived samples, such as biofluids and tissues, were considered for this review. To provide a complete state of the art, relevant research and key findings from the preceding years, dating back to 2018, have also been reviewed and integrated. We concentrate on technological advances, mechanistic insights, and clinical applications, highlighting how lipidomic profiling is improving our understanding of viral pathogenesis and proposing future directions toward its integration in precision virology.

Analytical frontiers and emerging technologies in lipidomics for virology research

The rapid evolution of lipidomic technologies has significantly enhanced our capacity to dissect host-virus interactions at the molecular level. Continuous improvements in MS resolution, the integration of ion mobility separation, and refined sample preparation now permit the identification and quantification of thousands of lipid species, including structural isomers and low-abundance signaling molecules, from minimal biological samples.4 Viral infections frequently induce the formation of structurally similar lipid species, such as isobaric or isomeric lipids, including sn-positional or double-bond variants, which are functionally distinct and highly relevant to membrane dynamics, viral entry, viral replication and immune evasion.11,12 However, most conventional workflows fail to resolve these isomers, overlooking finer structural details such as sn-positions or cis/trans configurations, and often collapsing them into a single identification. Applying advanced lipidomics techniques to the complex and dynamic environment of viral infection presents several unique analytical challenges that must be addressed to fully harness the potential of this approach in deciphering virus–host interactions. One of the most persistent hurdles is the limited sample availability, particularly from clinical cohorts or specific tissues affected by infection, or in those involving primary cells or subcellular fractions. Viral lipidomics studies often lack standardized protocols suited for small-volume or clinical samples, and this constraint restricts not only the global analysis of broad lipid classes but also the detection of low-abundance signaling lipids, which are often critical mediators of immune modulation and viral replication.13 The limited availability and high cost of class-specific internal standards also compromises the accuracy of quantification and the reproducibility of the analysis, especially for rare or infection-induced lipid subclasses such as oxidized lipids or ether-linked species.14 Although direct infusion strategies and nano- or microflow MS techniques can enhance detection sensitivity to the sub-femtomole level, they often introduce increased technical complexity, longer analysis times, and reduced throughput, making them less suitable for high-volume clinical studies.15 Traditional MS platforms such as gas chromatography-MS (GC-MS) and liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem MS (LC-ESI-MS/MS) remain pivotal for characterizing lipid diversity.16 While GC-MS effectively identifies fatty acids (FA) and sterol lipids,17 it struggles with non-volatile and complex lipids, which play crucial roles in disease pathophysiology, and can suffer information loss during necessary derivatization steps.18,19,20,21 However, GC-MS is one of the most robust separation techniques, offering high sensitivity and reproducibility. It has been a key tool in the chemotaxonomic identification of numerous pathogenic microorganisms, thanks to its ability to resolve the structural complexity of fatty acids (FA).17 Despite that, for viral lipidomics the limited capacity for analyzing intact lipids can be a drawback when the comprehensive profiling of complex lipid classes beyond FA is required to understand the full scope of metabolic reprogramming.19,20,22 In contrast, LC-ESI-MS/MS has been instrumental in broadening our understanding of the lipidome’s complexity.23 Within LC-based methods, reversed-phase liquid chromatography (RPLC) is dominant in viral lipidomics studies (e.g., COVID-19,6,24,25,26 HCV27,28,29 and WNV30), offering detailed separation based on fatty acyl chain length and unsaturation crucial for comprehensive profiling and identifying disease-specific alterations. The application of this separation technique prior to the analysis reduces the ion-suppression effect because of decreasing the number of analytes reaching the ion source at the same time. Furthermore, this technique provides more reliable annotation of individual lipid species, allows the separation of isomers and isobars, reduces the matrix effect, and enables the detection of thousands of molecules according to their physicochemical properties during a single run, also facilitating the reliable quantification of lipid species.12,23,31 Complementarily, hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC) is often preferred for its precision in separating polar lipid classes such as GP12 (e.g., phosphatidylserines (PS) and phosphatidylethanolamines (PE)), which are frequently implicated in viral replication and immune modulation, finding application in studies on conditions such as HCV-associated HCC29 and COVID-19.24 When coupled with appropriate mass spectrometry platforms, HILIC enables the separation of highly hydrophilic and amphiphilic analytes in complex matrices and does not require extreme pH conditions. This also implies the use of shorter microbore columns (100–150 nm) and less hydrophobic solvents, with a minimum percentage of water present in the mobile phase (5–40%). In addition, while it supports accurate lipid quantification, due to the fact that each lipid class elutes in a very narrow time window, this could increase the difficulty of lipid annotation and identification.12,20 While less common, normal phase liquid chromatography (NPLC) has also been used to study lipid alterations in diseases such as HIV.7 NJPLC separates non-polar lipids based on adsorption and RT, focusing on the differences between the polarity of the phospholipid parts, and using long columns (100–250 nm) and highly non-polar solvents, with low ionization capacity.12,19,32 The reduced used of NPLC in viral lipidomics studies is due to its worse reproducibility and robustness, and the presence several problems in the coupling with the MS.33 Although techniques such as thin-layer chromatography (TLC) offer a cost-effective solution, their low resolution typically limits their use in comprehensive high-throughput lipidomic studies, particularly when detailed the characterization of a wide range of lipid species is required.27,34

Building on these separation techniques, both untargeted and targeted lipidomics strategies are widely employed in virology. Untargeted approaches, typically using high-resolution Orbitrap or time-of-flight (TOF) analyzers, facilitate global lipid profiling and hypothesis generation, proving particularly valuable in uncovering dysregulation patterns linked to disease severity in infections such as SARS-CoV-2 and HIV.9,10,35 Untargeted lipidomics typically relies on high-resolution full-scan MS and data-dependent (DDA) or data-independent (DIA) MS/MS. DDA has long been used for broad lipid coverage; however, it often suffers from limited coverage in complex samples due to stochastic precursor ion selection and limited reproducibility. More recently, data-independent acquisition (DIA) techniques, including SWATH-MS and DIA-PASEF (parallel accumulation serial fragmentation), have emerged as powerful tools that offer improved reproducibility, deeper coverage, and better quantification, especially from low input lipid sample volumes, which is often a limiting factor in clinical viral studies.36,37 While an untargeted approach provides broad lipidome coverage, it suffers from several limitations. Lipid extracts from infected cells can yield tens of thousands of spectral features, many of which include overlapping isobars or poorly characterized lipid classes. The vast complexity of untargeted data requires sophisticated bioinformatics and faces challenges in definitively identifying and annotating all altered lipid species, particularly isomers. Conventional software tools often annotate lipids based on MS/MS spectra matched to library entries, yet many biologically relevant lipid isomers produce indistinguishable fragmentation patterns. This leads to the underrepresentation of key functional lipid species, which is crucial for pinpointing specific lipids involved in virus-host interactions.

Targeted methods, such as multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) on sensitive triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP) instruments, provide enhanced sensitivity and reproducibility for quantifying specific lipid classes (e.g., ceramides (Cer), lysophospholipids (lyso-GP)) with greater potential for clinical translation.24,38,39 While powerful for quantifying known lipids, targeted approaches are limited to pre-defined lists and may miss unexpected or novel lipid changes induced by viral infection.

Recent technological strides are further pushing the boundaries. Ion mobility separation (IM-MS), especially platforms incorporating differential mobility spectrometry (DMS), represents a transformative advance for resolving structurally similar lipid isomers39,40 adding a powerful third dimension to lipidomics, separating ions based on their shape, size and charge with milliseconds-scaledrift time resolution, successfully resolved isomeric phosphatidylcholine and ceramide species in complex mixtures, with studies reporting the clear separation of positional isomers and cis/trans variants in biological samples.41 The Lipid IM Atlas created by Leaptrot et al. (2019) provides collisional cross-section (CCS) values for thousands of lipids, enabling improved annotation confidence.42 IM-MS remains technically demanding and not yet routinely implemented in viral lipidomics workflows, despite it enables the confident distinction of biologically critical features such as sn-positional isomers or double-bond locations, particularly when coupled with trapped ion mobility (TIMS) or PASEF.43 A recent study by Rudt et al. (2023) evaluated DDA-, DIA-, and PRM-based PASEF modes on a TIMS TOF MS combined with HILIC separation. DIA PASEF produced the greatest MS/MS sensitivity but suffered from precursor assignment ambiguity in regions of overlapping retention time and mobility. DDA PASEF offered more reliable identification for unknown lipids, while PRM PASEF achieved the highest selectivity and reproducibility, highlighting its suitability for clinical and targeted viral lipidomics.44 In this line, the ZenoTOF (SCIEX) platform exemplifies this progress, having been used to characterize hundreds of lipids in SARS-CoV-2 plasma studies, discriminating disease severity via the differential quantification of key lipid classes.24 Despite these advances, the comprehensive separation and identification of all biologically relevant isomers in complex biological matrices infected with viruses remains an ongoing challenge. Beyond bulk analysis, imaging mass spectrometry (IMS), particularly using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI), is gaining traction for spatially resolved lipidomics in virology. IMS allows the direct visualization of lipid distributions within tissue sections, offering insights into localized metabolic changes during infection, as demonstrated by mapping lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC) enrichment and PC depletion in HCV-infected liver regions.8 While providing invaluable spatial context, IMS currently faces limitations in sensitivity and lipid coverage compared to LC-MS, and distinguishing whether localized lipid changes are a direct result of viral presence in specific cells or a broader tissue response requires careful experimental design.

Lipidomics strategy and the art of lipid annotation: Enhancing viral infections research

Complementing these experimental advances is the critical task of accurate lipid annotation. Precise identification of lipid species is not merely a technical step but is paramount for unraveling the complex and dynamic lipid remodeling that occurs during viral infections and accurately linking specific lipid alterations to their biological functions and roles in pathogenesis. The annotation process in lipidomics is highly variable, with a range of tools and strategies tailored to different research objectives, as collected in Figure 1 and Tables 1, 2, 3, and 4. The variety of software tools and annotation strategies used can be classified into three main categories: lipid-oriented databases, specialized software and in-house tools, and hybrid approaches that combine software and manual efforts.45

Comprehensive lipid-oriented databases such as LIPID MAPS,46 HMDB,47 ChemSpider,48 METLIN,49 KEGG,50 and CEU Mass Mediator51 are commonly used to cross-reference lipid species, offering insights into lipid structures. LIPID MAPS, for instance, offers curated classification schemes and access to MS data repositories and tools for lipid identification, quantification, and data visualization. However, these resources generally lack sufficient coverage of viral infection–induced lipid modifications, such as oxidized lipids or rare phospholipid species observed during SARS-CoV-2 or HCV infections. Specialized software and in-house tools are crucial in lipid annotation, improving the accuracy and efficiency of lipidomics research. Open-source software such as MS-DIAL52 offers robust general capabilities widely used in untargeted COVID-196,25 and HCV research,29 due to its user-friendly interface, and support for data-independent acquisition (DIA) workflows.53 MS-DIAL’s performance in isomer differentiation and confident annotation of overlapping lipids is limited by its reliance on library-matching and generic fragmentation patterns, which may lead to ambiguous identification, especially in the context of novel viral lipids.54 For targeted workflows, vendor-specific software such as TraceFinder,55 PeakView,7,56 and LipidSearch8 facilitate high-confidence identification. LipidSearch offers accurate mass filtering, in-silico fragment prediction, and database-driven scoring, but its limited support for custom adducts and poor performance with unusual viral lipids (e.g., plasmalogens) may restrict its utility in viral infection studies. TraceFinder and PeakView offer robust precision and throughput in targeted viral lipidomics, but often lack the depth and flexibility required for discovery-oriented annotation or structural elucidation of unknown lipids. Increasingly, studies integrate multiple tools, such as combining MassHunter Lipid Annotator57 with LipidHunter26 or Lipostar,26 to resolve structural ambiguities. These platforms offer extended annotation pipelines, allowing users to process both high-resolution data and isomeric features with custom scoring systems. Despite this, annotation reproducibility remains an issue due to the very low overlapping in lipid identifications between different software across complex biological samples.14 Other software utilized in lipidomics studies of viral infections include Micromass MassLynx,30 LipidXplorer,58 and LipidLynxX,58 which have been applied in semi-targeted annotation strategies. LipidLynxX also facilitates ontology alignment, which is essential for integrating lipidomics with systems biology approaches to study host–virus interactions. However, these tools typically require manual curation and may lack direct support for isomer-resolving (ion mobility or derivatized) lipid data. Pipelines such as LipidMatch Flow10 support customizable large-scale annotation, but its accuracy depends on the quality of in-silico fragmentation libraries and may still miss infection-specific structural variants; while tools such as LION/web and BioPAN58 aid in pathway analysis, which are crucial in viral lipidomics for understanding how individual lipid changes collectively impact broader metabolic networks and biological processes exploited by viruses. However, their output critically depends on upstream annotation quality—and tools such as Lipostar and MS-DIAL can produce inconsistent features that hinder pathway integration. Despite ongoing automation, manual annotation remains essential, particularly for novel or unexpected lipid features that emerge in viral lipidomics. While software such as PeakView or Lipid Annotator automates certain aspects, manual annotation remains essential for discovering novel viral lipids and ensuring the accuracy of resolved isomers or distinguished co-eluting species.57,59 Semi-automated processes, though efficient, still require human intervention due to lipids’ unique characteristics, such as multiple adduct formation and consistent retention behavior across chromatographic modes.60

These software tools and strategies are indispensable for processing complex MS data generated from infected biological samples, enabling researchers to identify potential lipid biomarkers or track changes in specific lipid classes relevant to viral replication or immune response. However, despite this progress, significant challenges persist in harmonizing identification criteria across platforms, particularly for complex species such as ether lipids, oxidized lipids, and low abundance signaling molecules. Accurately annotating the lipid species is critical in viral infections because they often play key roles in dynamic processes such as membrane remodeling, inflammation, and immune signaling that are directly impacted by viral activity. Overcoming the variability in annotation workflows and achieving higher confidence in isomer-specific identification is, therefore, essential for advancing our understanding of the precise lipid-mediated mechanisms governing virus-host interactions.

Lipidomic profiling of respiratory viral infections: Host responses and disease severity

The COVID-19 pandemic dramatically accelerated research into virus-host interactions, with lipidomics emerging as a crucial tool revealing substantial metabolic disruptions induced by SARS-CoV-2. Initial findings from studies conducted since 2019 demonstrated that COVID-19 infection induces significant metabolite and lipid alterations, indicating a disruption of whole-body metabolism. Notably, differences in lipid levels were observed across varying severities of infection and in fatal cases compared to samples from healthy volunteers.

Early lipidomic investigations detected measurable disruptions even in mild to moderate infections. Studies characterizing lipid alterations in various biological matrices, including serum, identified specific phosphatidylcholines (PC), triglycerides (TG), and sphingomyelins (SM) as being depleted or enriched in recovered patients compared to healthy individuals.61 Building upon these foundational insights, it can be stated that there exists a strong correlation between host lipid profiles and COVID-19 severity. Further comparisons between mild, severe, and control samples highlighted increased lysophospholipids (lyso-GP), ether-linked GPs, and SMs during infection, with the most significant elevations seen in severe patients. A total of 196 lipids were identified, and specific species such as PC 16:0/18:1, PC O-16:1/18:2, LPC 16:0, and LPE 18:1, were notably abundant, while others, such as LPC 22:5, LPE 16:0, and LPE 18:0, were among the least abundant. Most detected GPs were unsaturated, although LPC and LPI subclasses exhibited a higher proportion of saturated fatty acyl chains.25

Several studies showed that levels of TG, diglycerides (DG), and free FA (FFA), highlighting DG 16:0/20:2 and TG 14:0/22:1/22:3, were found to increase with disease progression, particularly in severe and fatal cases. Conversely, phosphatidylcholines (PC) levels often showed a corresponding decline over the course of infection.62 This accumulation of TGs in severe cases is notably linked to liver dysfunction, suggesting a direct impact of altered lipid metabolism on organ pathology. These findings correlate with those obtained by Wei et al. using untargeted lipidomics across multiple human cell lines. They identified conserved lipid dependencies across SARS-CoV-2 variants, with significant alterations in phospholipids, ether lipids, neutral lipids, and sphingolipids, establishing potential host-targeted antiviral strategies that remain effective across viral evolution63 (Table 1).

Lipidomics studies also identified variations in apolipoproteins, steroids, SP, GP, and fatty acids such as FA 20:4 as potential biomarkers for severity assessment. Specifically, more than 100 lipid species from SP, GP, and choline derivatives were found to be downregulated in association with severe SARS-CoV-2 infection.64 Specifically, a significant decrease in plasma levels of GP, particularly phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), PC, and phosphatidylserine (PS) species, was observed in deceased patients compared to recovered and healthy individuals. These early disruptions in lipid metabolism were linked to oxidative and vascular stress, highlighting their critical prognostic value.65

Targeted lipidomic analyses refined these observations, identifying specific lipid signatures distinguishing severe from non-severe illness. Elevated ceramides (Cer), certain GP, DG, TG, short-chain SM, and ether-linked PE (PE-O) species frequently mark severe cases, contrasting with reduced levels of specific phosphatidylinositols (PI), HexCer, SMs, and cholesteryl esters (CE). While general trends are apparent, some research discrepancies, especially regarding GPs, might stem from confounding factors such as treatments potentially reversing infection-driven lipid changes.24 Consistent with these systemic changes, the lipid composition of platelets also shifts significantly in severe COVID-19, exhibiting decreased ether lipids (PC O-, LPC O-, PE O-) and phyto-ceramides, but increased ganglioside GM3.26 The reduction in ether lipids in platelets could contribute to the thrombotic complications frequently observed in patients with severe COVID-19.

Beyond the acute phase, lipid disturbances persist in individuals experiencing post-COVID conditions (PCC). These patients may show elevated lysophosphatidylglycerols (LPG) and PEs alongside lower LPC levels compared to those without PCC, correlating with the severity of their initial infection. These lipids were also more abundant in symptomatic individuals than those asymptomatic individuals during their initial infection.55

Intriguingly, lipid remodeling in Long-COVID appears linked to mitochondrial dysfunction. Affected individuals often exhibit decreased plasma levels of TGs, DGs, LPE, LPI, PE, hexosylceramides (HexCer), and Cer (especially long-chain species), coupled with increased FA and FAHFA. This specific pattern of decreased long-chain Cer and increased FA suggests that altered ceramide trafficking and mitochondrial accumulation could impair cellular respiration and increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, contributing to the persistent symptoms characteristic of Long-COVID (Figure 2; Table 1).6

While SARS-CoV-2 has been a primary focus, it is clear that respiratory viral infections more broadly disrupt lipid metabolism, often impacting inflammatory pathways, as evidenced by studies identifying alterations in over a thousand lipid species, including PEs, PE-O, PI, LPC, and TAG.40 These lipid changes likely play a role in modulating the host inflammatory response, a key component of respiratory viral pathogenesis.

Structural analysis of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein revealed a conserved binding pocket for the free fatty acid linoleic acid (LA), which stabilizes the spike in a locked conformation, potentially reducing ACE2 receptor interaction and offering a novel therapeutic target based on host lipid interactions.66

To understand the patients’ disease status and support non-invasive diagnostics, Misato Kida et al. comprehensively analyzed human non-treated urinary lipids to identify non-invasive biomarkers in COVID-19. Their investigation reflected 21 species of FA altered levels detected, where docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) increased compared to healthy subjects. Moreover, arachidonic acid-derived metabolites of major proinflammatory lipid mediators (PGE2, TXA2, and PGF2α) were also increased, reinforcing the link between systemic inflammation and lipidomic alterations in patients with COVID-19.67

Host lipidome remodeling in HIV: From viral persistence to therapy response

HIV targets white blood cells, leading to immunodeficiency (AIDS). Managed with antiretroviral treatment (ART), there is no cure, and approximately 36.9 million people are living with HIV globally.68,69 Studies conducted prior to 2023 with plasma samples from patients with long-term viral and immunological HIV control began to reveal differences in their metabolic and lipidomic profiles, identifying lipid species that could differentiate these groups and potentially be linked to viral replication.70 Understanding the potential link between plasma lipids and HIV latency, persistence, and viral rebound after ART became a crucial area of research. Analysis of lipidomic changes during therapies before 2023 showed that increased plasma levels of lyso-GPs (LPC and LPA) before treatment interruption were associated with faster viral rebound. Additionally, higher levels of PCs before treatment cessation were related to higher levels of HIV-DNA presence in T-cells, proposing GPs as possible contributors to HIV persistence and rebound infection.71 The prolonged treatment and long-term viral infection were also linked to other diseases, such as metabolic syndrome (MetS) comorbidity. Plasma metabolic profiling differences between patients with HIV with and without MetS in studies before 2023 revealed an increase of Cer, DG, Cer-NDS, LPE, MG, PE, and TG, while HexCer decreased in patients with HIV-MetS, with DG and TG showing significantly differential abundance.72 Moreover, the lipid metabolism disruption resulting from HIV infection was related to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in studies conducted prior to 2023, with specific lipid species such as CE 16:1, Cer 16:0;2O, and several others being associated with increased risk of plaque formation.73 The diabetes risk was also evaluated in both HIV-infected and HIV-uninfected plasma samples before 2023, with prolonged ART inducing disruption of lipid and glucose metabolism linked to diabetes risk, and specific lipid species associated with either decreased or increased risk.74 The metabolic effects of HIV infection were also shown to be transmissible to newborns during pregnancy, affecting their lipidome profile, with studies identifying lipid species in cord blood that could discriminate between HIV-exposed uninfected and HIV-unexposed uninfected infants.75

Recent studies published from January 2023 onwards (Table 1) have continued to build upon these observations, providing more detailed insights into the lipidomic alterations associated with HIV infection across different patient populations and treatment responses. For instance, the study conducted by Lin-Yu Wan et al. revealed higher plasma levels of TG, FFA, Cer, acylcarnitines (CAR), and GM3 in treatment-naive, immunological responders and immunological non-responders compared to healthy controls. These alterations were positively correlated with inflammation and could have an impact on immune recovery.7 Different treatment strategies, such as triple-drug or two-drug regimen (2DR) showed differences in the levels of specific metabolites, including TG(48:0). The increasing level of this TG after 2DR indicated metabolic changes affecting lipid metabolism with a link between the inflammatory and immune responses to the therapy switch; however, the long-term clinical implications remain uncertain.9 Different treatment studies also revealed changes in DG, TG, sphingolipids (SP), and GP increased levels, depending on the treatment applied. Specifically, some DG, TG, and one LPC were associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk35 (Figure 3; Table 1). HIV infection can affect newborns during pregnancy, altering their lipidome profiles. Youth with perinatally acquired HIV (YPHIV) showed higher concentrations of Cer, DG, FFA, LPC, and PC compared to HIV-negative youth. Saturated PEs (16:0 and 18:0) were strongly associated with makers of immune activation, inflammation, and CVD, indicating their potential involvement in the chronic inflammatory state and increased CVD risk observed in YPHIV; whereas LC-TGs were inversely associated with CVD markers, indicating a potential protective role.38 Recent multi-omics analyses in virologically suppressed people with HIV (PWH) have shown that plasma lipid profiles correlate strongly with cardiometabolic risk. These studies, which integrated lipidomics with host genetic data, transcriptomics (specifically adipose tissue gene expression), and clinical parameters, revealed that TGs and DGs were positively associated with insulin resistance, visceral adiposity, and liver fat, while PCs and LPCs correlated with more favorable metabolic outcomes.38 These findings highlight how multi-omics approaches can link systemic lipid alterations to underlying metabolic dysfunction and provide a more holistic view of disease pathogenesis in the context of viral infections. Together, these findings highlight the potential of plasma lipidomics for assessing metabolic health in PWH under antiretroviral therapy.10

Lipidomic signatures in hepatitis C virus infection and liver disease progression

The Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is responsible for the Hepatitis C infection, affecting the normal functioning of the liver. There is no vaccine for HCV, but it can be treated with ART.76 Globally, HCV affects approximately 60 million people, most of them with a clinical picture of persistent infection, causing the death of about 300,000 people each year.77

Before 2023, researchers began to shed light on the pathological lipidomic alterations occurring during HCV infection. Several studies were conducted to understand the crucial role that FA plays during viral replication and virion production, with enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis and elongation essential for successful replication.78 Additionally, it has been described that the cholesterol and PC accumulation in the microsomal section during HCV infection, along with SMs, were enriched in the replication sites. Additionally, cholesterol metabolism alteration and steatosis induction during HCV infection were found to be associated with the viral genotype, with studies illustrating differences in lipid metabolism between HCV-genotype 1 and HCV-genotype 3 chronically infected patients, including lower levels of LDL cholesterol and increased hepatic steatosis in HCV-G3.79 Furthermore, acquired immunodeficiency due to impaired immune function associated with HIV can lead to co-infection with HCV, and studies before 2023 determined the plasma metabolic fingerprint of patients with cirrhosis, comparing HIV/HCV-coinfected patients with HCV-infected patients and identifying lipid-increased levels of FA, bile acids, and oxidized phospholipids in advanced cirrhosis.80 Research also assessed the evolution of plasma metabolome after direct-acting antivirals in HIV/HCV-coinfected patients and those with cirrhosis, showing changes in plasma metabolic profile related to improvements in cirrhosis and inflammatory status.81

Building on these earlier findings, recent lipidomic studies published from January 2023 onwards have continued to identify specific lipid alterations and their implications in HCV infection and liver disease progression. Lipidomics studies identify increased levels of fatty acid amides and SP metabolites in patients with HCV, with decreased levels of GPs and TGs. Specifically, the levels of LPC 18:2 increase and PC 16:1/16:1 and PC 16:0/16:1 decrease in infected liver regions over the course of the infection. This alteration in phospholipid metabolism and lipid droplet formation is a consequence of the overexpression of phospholipase A2 group 4C (PLA2G4C), an enzyme induced by NF-κB and c-Myc transcription factors, which plays a direct role in facilitating viral replication.8 Regarding the new advances in biomarker discovery associated with HCV infection, some researchers were able to shed light on the pathological lipidomic alterations occurring during HCV infection. Lipidomic investigations performed in human plasma samples identified increased levels of eight FA amides, several GPs, three sphingosine 1-phosphate (d16:1, d18:1, d19:1), and one sphinganine 1-phosphate (d18:0). Counterparts, 25 GPs, and 3 TG decreased, where LPS 17:1 and LPI 22:6 showed the greatest decline. Specifically, it was shown that decreased levels of LPIs were implicated in the HCV replication by activating the synthesis of PI 4-phosphate pools, which are essential for forming viral replication factories.28 HCV infection and DGAT2 overexpression both increased phospholipids with long-chain PUFAs, leading to neutral lipid accumulation and changing membrane lipid composition, modulating lipid droplet biogenesis and HCV replication organelle formation.39 This reprogramming of lipid metabolism by HCV, partly through DGAT2, directly supports the formation of the viral replication organelle. The infection derived from HCV could also lead to the generation of several other pathologies. This is the case of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most frequent type of hepatic cancer, whose early diagnosis is essential for patient survival. To date, there are useful tools and biomarkers associated with HCC; however, they still have limitations. The elaboration of a lipidomic plasma profile of patients with HCV could be used to uncover the potential HCC signatures. In this sense, a total of 280 compounds were annotated, of which it can be elucidated that HCC was characterized by increased levels of 6 CAR and a global decrease in 9 LPCs.29 Long-term HCV infection contributes to liver steatosis by altering host lipid metabolism. In cell culture models, HCV infection induced phosphatidylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PEMT), which promotes viral replication, lipid accumulation, and steatosis. This upregulation led to increased levels of PCs, PEs, free cholesterol, CE, and neutral lipids, particularly within the endoplasmic reticulum. Several GPs were also elevated. Notably, silencing PEMT impaired viral replication and mitigated lipid alterations. This suggests that PEMT-mediated lipid alterations are not only a consequence of infection but actively support viral replication and contribute to the development of steatosis, positioning PEMT as a potential therapeutic target in HCV-associated liver disease27 (Figure 4).

Lipidomic approaches to emerging viral threats

Beyond the well-studied respiratory and chronic bloodborne viruses, lipidomic approaches have also been applied to understand emerging and re-emerging viral threats. Prior to 2023 (Figure 5), research into Dengue virus (DENV) highlighted the critical role of endoplasmic reticulum-associated proteins, such as TMEM41B and VMP1, as host factors involved in lipid metabolism and essential for DENV infection. Studies demonstrated that the deficiency of these proteins led to diminished DENV replication and revealed that TMEM41B-deficient cells accumulated several lipid species, while VMP1-deficient cells exhibited the depletion of FA and TG but increased SP.82 The DENV shares epidemiological and ecological characteristics with members of the Flaviviridae family, including the Zika virus (ZIKV). Metabolomic analysis in human serum samples showed that ZIKV infection induces the remodeling of the intracellular host membrane, particularly affecting sphingolipids, to form replication sites. Studies also revealed increased phosphatidylinositol phosphate (PI-P) and plasmalogens (PE-P) levels in serum samples from infected patients with ZIKV.56,83,84 The severe symptomatology related to ZIKV in newborns due to its targeting of the placenta and interference with transplacental lipid transport was also investigated before 2023, with plasma lipidome comparisons revealing alterations in circulating lipids, including higher concentrations of hydroxyoctadecadienoic acid (HODE) and LPC in affected newborns.85 The Ebola virus disease (EVD) also garnered attention, and plasma lipidome analysis of EVD survivors and fatalities conducted before 2023 provided insights into lipidomic changes associated with disease states, critical illness, and recovery, identifying significantly altered lipids such as PCs containing LC-PUFA in survivors and increased TGs, PS, PE, Cer, PG, and DG in the fatality group.86 Furthermore, lipidomic profiling of Enterovirus-infected cells, specifically with Enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) and coxsackievirus A16 (CV-A16), conducted before 2023, revealed significant perturbations in intracellular lipid homeostasis, with increased levels of certain PUFAs upon infection, indicating a specific modulation of host lipid pathways essential for efficient viral replication.87

More recent lipidomics analyses have continued to expand our understanding of emerging viral threats (Figure 5). In a recent lipidomic analysis, Hehner et al. (2024) delineated virus-specific and shared lipid remodeling events during infection with five orthoflaviviruses (ZIKV, West Nile (WNV), tick-borne encephalitis (TBEV), DENV, and yellow fever (YFV-17D) viruses) in Huh7 cells. Remarkable changes were observed in Cer metabolism, with significant accumulation of Cer species in WNV, DENV, and YFV-17D infections, whereas TBEV induced minimal Cer elevation. This was paralleled by a consistent decrease in SM, suggesting enhanced sphingomyelinase activity. ZIKV induced early accumulation of neutral lipids, particularly TGs, which later decreased across all viruses except TBEV. DENV was characterized by a unique increase in HexCer and distinct reductions in PC, DG, and TGs. Lyso-GP, including LPC and LPE, was transiently elevated early during infection, especially in ZIKV, WNV, and YFV-17D. A consistent pattern of GP remodeling was also identified, with the notable depletion of PE and PS in WNV, DENV, and TBEV, while ZIKV showed a slight PS elevation. Additionally, orthoflavivirus infections led to shifts in FA composition, marked by increased monounsaturated FA content (e.g., FA(16:1) and FA(18:1)) and elevated arachidonic acid (FA(20:4)) in PE and PS. Functional studies revealed that disrupting Cer synthesis and glycerophospholipid remodeling pathways impacted viral titers and cytopathic effects, underscoring their essential roles in viral replication. These findings establish lipid metabolism, especially Cer and GP dynamics, as critical determinants in orthoflavivirus infectivity and pathogenesis.58 The severe form of WNV can also cause meningitis, encephalitis, or acute flaccid paralysis. Patricia Mingo-Casas et al. addressed a combined analysis of plasma and serum samples from infected mice and patients. The data provided, where 192 molecular species were identified, explained specific fingerprints of different stages of the infection. The results were characterized by a significant increase in circulating sphingolipids (Cer, Cer-NDS, and SM-NDS), PEs, and TGs at advanced stages. Also, specifically, HexCer and monoacylglycerols (MG) showed increased levels in human samples.30 These specific lipid signatures, such as elevated Cer and HexCer, may serve as novel peripheral biomarkers for tracking disease progression in WNV infection.

Outlook and future directions

Lipidomics has proven to be a powerful tool in elucidating the complex interplay between viruses and host lipid metabolism. Across studies on SARS-CoV-2, HIV, and HCV, several recurring themes emerge: (I) viral replication is closely tied to host lipid remodeling, especially in pathways involving glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and cholesterol; (II) distinct lipid signatures have been identified in infection contexts, such as elevated ceramides and reduced LPC in severe COVID-19, or increased polyunsaturated phosphatidylethanolamines in HIV latency models; (III) lipid-based immune modulation is evident, with lipids influencing cytokine signaling, inflammasome activation, and membrane trafficking; and (IV) integration of MS-based untargeted lipidomics with bioinformatics tools has enabled high-throughput lipid identification and quantification, but annotation challenges, particularly with isomers, remain a barrier. These findings support the concept that lipidomics is not only a tool for biomarker discovery but also a window into the molecular mechanisms of viral pathogenesis.

Building upon recent breakthroughs, the field of lipidomics is rapidly advancing from fundamental discovery toward clinical applications in virology, promising new avenues for diagnostics and therapeutics. However, translating these insights into tangible clinical advancements requires navigating several critical frontiers.

A key imperative is the harmonization of methodologies. Addressing current inconsistencies in sample handling, data acquisition, and lipid annotation is paramount for enhancing reproducibility and enabling robust cross-study comparisons. Initiatives such as LIPID MAPS and the Lipidomics Standards Initiative (LSI) provide crucial frameworks for establishing standardized workflows and reference materials. Such standardization is vital not only for basic research but especially for accelerating the rigorous analytical and clinical validation of lipid biomarkers, moving promising candidates identified in discovery phases (e.g., for COVID-19 severity or WNV progression) through the pipeline toward reliable clinical tools. Lipidomic profiling holds increasing clinical relevance in virology, offering potential applications in early diagnosis, biomarker discovery, and precision therapy. However, while numerous studies have reported specific lipid species or classes that correlate with viral infection, disease severity, or treatment response, none of these candidates have yet been validated for clinical use. Translating these findings into clinical practice will require large, well-designed validation studies, ideally across diverse cohorts and with standardized platforms.88

Moreover, robust statistical modeling, longitudinal sampling, and integration with other omics and clinical parameters are essential to define lipid signatures with true diagnostic or prognostic power. At present, the clinical utility of viral lipidomics remains mainly exploratory. However, its potential for applications such as early diagnosis, patient stratification, infection typing, and treatment monitoring is significant—particularly in areas where conventional diagnostics are limited. As research advances, priority should be given to identifying lipids with reproducible patterns across studies, evaluating them in prospective clinical trials, and establishing reference ranges and thresholds that can be adopted in healthcare settings. While stage-specific lipidomic alterations have been reported in HCV-associated liver disease progression, similar investigations in other oncogenic viruses, such as HBV, or those causing organ-specific lesions, remain largely unexplored in the current literature. Expanding lipidomic research to these viral infections could help determine whether such lipid remodeling patterns are universal or virus-specific, representing an important direction for future studies. Furthermore, overcoming the analytical challenge of resolving and accurately quantifying structurally similar lipid isomers, which often possess distinct biological roles, remains a significant limitation requiring dedicated innovation in high-throughput methods.89

The future also lies in the synergistic integration of cutting-edge technologies. Advanced MS platforms, including innovations such as ZenoTOF, IM-MS, and IMS, offer exciting possibilities for deepening lipidome coverage, achieving spatial resolution within tissues, and uncovering localized metabolic events crucial to pathogenesis (e.g., localized remodeling in HCV-infected liver). Combining these powerful analytical tools with sophisticated computational approaches and machine learning algorithms will be essential for navigating the complexity of lipidomic datasets and extracting meaningful biological knowledge. Exploring the integration of lipidomics with other high-resolution techniques, such as single-cell transcriptomics or advanced microscopy, could provide unprecedented insights into cellular heterogeneity and the precise roles of specific lipids within defined cell populations during infection.

Moving forward, embedding lipidomics within broader systems biological frameworks is essential to unravel the intricate interplay between host and virus. This integration goes beyond simply identifying altered lipid levels; it aims to build comprehensive, dynamic models of cellular processes by combining diverse datasets. A key direction for the field is the robust integration of lipidomics with other high-resolution techniques through multi-omics approaches.90 Correlating lipid profiles with transcriptomic, proteomic, clinical, and even host genetic data holds immense promise for deciphering the complex interplay between the viral manipulation of host lipids and disease outcomes.9,10 Such integrated approaches are crucial for building comprehensive network models that map lipid alterations to upstream signaling pathways and can predict disease trajectories, metabolic shifts, revealing fundamental mechanisms of viral replication and immune evasion (e.g., involving enzymes such as PEMTor PLA2G4C), and highlight effective host-directed intervention points, thereby providing mechanistic insights that cannot be gleaned from lipidomics alone. However, successful implementation requires harmonized data acquisition protocols, interoperable data formats, and centralized repositories to support cross-omic analyses. Tools such as multi-omic pathway enrichment platforms and integrative network modeling (e.g., through Cytoscape or MetaboAnalyst) are increasingly vital for generating testable hypotheses and identifying key regulatory nodes with clinical relevance.

While significant progress has been made in high-profile infections such as SARS-CoV-2, HIV, and HCV, extending these powerful lipidomic investigations to encompass a broader range of viral threats, including neglected and emerging pathogens such as mpox,91 is crucial. Furthermore, comparative lipidomic analyses across different viral families, strains, and even within longitudinal studies tracking individual patient responses, will be invaluable for identifying both conserved lipid dependencies exploitable by broad-spectrum antivirals and virus-specific vulnerabilities.

Ultimately, the convergence of analytical precision, robust validation pipelines, system-level integration, deeper mechanistic understanding, and a broadened scope will solidify lipidomics as an indispensable pillar of precision virology. This trajectory promises to drive the development of next-generation diagnostics and novel host-targeted therapies, offering new strategies to combat viral diseases globally.
