Diabetes mellitus is a global health challenge, with > 800 million people worldwide suffering from the disease.1 The prevalence of type 2 diabetes has significantly increased throughout the world, especially in low- and middle-income countries, where treatment is most limited. Regardless of where they might practice, every nephrologist caring for adult patients will be familiar with these trends, and the looming specter of concomitant increases in diabetic kidney disease, end-stage kidney disease, and the need for renal transplantation. Given the above, it is no surprise that on-call transplant clinicians are often faced with kidney offers from deceased donors with diabetes, and that there is substantial interest in the outcomes of organs transplanted into recipients with diabetic kidney disease in an era of ongoing organ scarcity; because kidney transplantation—even from donors considered to be of lower quality—might provide superior survival compared with remaining on the waitlist.2

In this issue, Orsillo et al.3 use the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry to compare and contrast posttransplant graft and patient outcomes of kidneys from diabetic donors and nondiabetic deceased donors implanted into 11,343 adult diabetic and nondiabetic recipients between 2003 and 2022. After risk-adjustment for other variables known to affect outcomes, Orsillo et al. demonstrated that only diabetic donor–diabetic recipient patients had significantly reduced death-censored graft survival, though diabetic-donor kidneys had relatively minor reductions in posttransplant estimated glomerular filtration rate regardless of recipient diabetes status. As expected, patient survival was significantly lower in diabetic recipient groups, regardless of donor diabetes status, reflecting the additional overall mortality risk associated with diabetes.4 Of note, the diabetic donor–nondiabetic recipient group did not experience reduced patient survival.

This is a timely addition to the evidence-base, with a relatively contemporaneous cohort using a high-quality registry. Importantly, this analysis included recipient comorbidity data such as diabetes, coronary artery disease, and peripheral vascular disease. Recipient diabetes status was defined using these data, not just those with diabetic kidney disease as their primary cause of end-stage kidney disease. Approximately 6% of donors in this study had diabetes, similar to US and French cohorts.5 Although the effect of donor and recipient diabetes status on posttransplant outcomes in deceased donor kidney transplantation has been examined by other groups, this is one of the first analyses from outside the USA. This is important, because US deceased donors tend to be younger than in many other countries and global organ acceptance practices differ widely.5,6

Orsillo et al. acknowledge the limitations of their study in their paper. Transplant clinicians crave the data granularity that retrospective registry analyses are usually unable to provide. Information on duration of donor and recipient diabetes are missing, along with markers of severity (e.g., glycated hemoglobin, albuminuria, and biopsy findings). The broad study period, which was needed to provide reasonable statistical power to identify differential outcomes from the relatively rare diabetic donor group meant that the impact of modern diabetes management (insulin pumps, glucose sensors, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, etc.) was unable to be assessed. Furthermore, the development of diabetes in the recipient posttransplantation could not be accounted for by this study.

The findings from this study provide some reassurance to transplant clinicians but add a further layer of complexity. This analysis confirms the widespread understanding that recipient diabetes is associated with worse posttransplant patient survival, when compared with those without diabetes. However, a history of donor diabetes does not appear to be associated with worse graft outcomes in all recipients, in all countries. The widely used US Kidney Donor Risk (Profile) Index calculator identifies donor diabetes as a strong poor prognostic factor7; Orsilla et al.’s study from Australia and New Zealand suggests otherwise, as does an online risk calculator from the UK8 (Figure 1). It is therefore essential that clinicians have knowledge of outcome data from the country in which they practice and can appreciate the reasonably subtle distinctions between analyses from other national registries. Fundamentally, what is needed is an understanding of the potential survival benefit of earlier transplantation of diabetic donor organs versus remaining on the waitlist for another (nondiabetic donor) organ. So, where does this leave the on-call transplant clinician, contemplating an organ offer from a deceased donor with diabetes? Evidence from the study by Orsillo et al. can assuage some anxieties and provide data for a nuanced discussion with potential recipients. An individualized risk assessment is still required, taking into account donor diabetes duration and severity, if that information is available. Recipient risk factors for mortality on the waitlist and their likelihood of receiving another (perhaps better) offer must also be considered, along with the estimated survival benefit that transplantation often brings. Local waitlist factors, most importantly, organ supply and demand, should be taken considered. Orsillo et al.’s study adds to our evidence-base, while reminding us that there are very few straightforward questions (or answers) in deceased donor kidney transplantation.
