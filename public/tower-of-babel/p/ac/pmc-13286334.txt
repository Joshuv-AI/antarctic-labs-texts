Therapeutic drug monitoring of digoxin, Serum levels and associated biochemical markers in a tertiary care laboratory
Authors: Zeynep Gunes Ozunal, Begum Dokuzagac, Okan Dikker, Belkiz Ongen Ipek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study focuses on evaluating digoxin requests and their relationship with other laboratory tests, such as renal and liver function, and electrolytes in a city hospital. The study retrospectively analyzed therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) requests for digoxin between February 1, 2024, and September 30, 2025. Parameters such as the age and sex of patients, requesting departments, and measurement results of digoxin, urea, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase, sodium, potassium, chloride, calcium (Ca), magnesium, albumin, and total protein were assessed. Digoxin levels were classified into 3 groups: subtherapeutic (<0.8 ng/mL), therapeutic (0.8–2.0 ng/mL), and supratherapeutic (>2.0 ng/mL). A total of 237 digoxin TDM requests were retrieved. Of these, 72.8% were female. Internal Medicine accounted for the highest proportion of TDM requests (35.3%), followed by other departments (27.6%) and intensive care units (12.5%). According to digoxin concentration levels, subtherapeutic values were lower than therapeutic and supratherapeutic groups for age, urea, and creatinine (P ≤ .001). Subtherapeutic group values were higher than therapeutic and supratherapeutic groups for ALT (P = .01 for subtherapeutic-therapeutic and P = .029 for therapeutic-supratherapeutic groups), albumin (P ≤ .001 for subtherapeutic-therapeutic and P = .004 for subtherapeutic-supratherapeutic), and total protein (P = .002 for subtherapeutic-therapeutic and P = .015 for subtherapeutic-supratherapeutic). There was a statistical difference in the subtherapeutic-supratherapeutic group (P = .01) and the therapeutic-supratherapeutic group (P = .001) for Ca. Our study shows that treatment with digoxin needs to be evaluated with comprehensive laboratory tests, including urea, creatinine, ALT, albumin, total protein, and Ca.

1. Introduction

Derived from the foxglove plant (Digitalis spp.), digoxin is a cardiac glycoside that has been used in medicine for centuries.[1] Following oral administration, approximately 70% of the dose is absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. About 25% of circulating digoxin is bound to serum albumin. Digoxin has a large volume of distribution due to its extensive binding to skeletal and cardiac muscle, whereas binding to adipose tissue is negligible. The drug penetrates the cerebrospinal fluid, crosses the placental barrier, and is excreted into breast milk. Digoxin cannot be effectively removed from the body by dialysis.[2] Digoxin exhibits low plasma protein binding (approximately 20–30%), which limits clinically significant displacement interactions. However, changes in body composition – particularly in elderly patients with reduced lean body mass – may influence the apparent volume of distribution and increase sensitivity to digoxin, even at therapeutic serum concentrations.

Digoxin pharmacokinetics are strongly influenced by renal function, as the drug is primarily eliminated unchanged by the kidneys.[3] In patients with renal impairment – particularly in the elderly – digoxin clearance is reduced, leading to a prolonged half-life and an increased risk of drug accumulation and toxicity. Age-related physiological changes, including decreased glomerular filtration rate and altered body composition, further contribute to variability in digoxin exposure. Although digoxin exhibits relatively low plasma protein binding, changes in serum albumin may still modestly affect free drug concentrations in frail or elderly patients. In addition, electrolyte disturbances, especially hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and hypercalcemia, significantly enhance myocardial sensitivity to digoxin, increasing the risk of toxicity even at serum concentrations within the traditionally defined therapeutic range.

Digoxin is a drug that requires careful monitoring in clinical use because it has a narrow therapeutic range, and toxic doses can be reached at levels very close to therapeutic doses. These pharmacological characteristics have rendered digoxin a prototypical agent for which therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) has been considered in clinical practice since the late 1960s. Measurement of digoxin serum levels was associated with a reduction in toxicity in 1974.[4] The reliable measurement of serum digoxin concentrations became feasible following the development of radioimmunoassay techniques, allowing a more accurate assessment of the relationship between digoxin pharmacokinetics and its clinical effects. Currently, digoxin concentrations in serum and other biological fluids are most commonly determined in clinical laboratories using immunoassay-based methods, including particle-enhanced turbidimetric immunoassays and chemiluminescence immunoassays. However, in situations where immunoassay- related cross-reactivity or analytical interference may compromise result interpretation, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry is regarded as a reference analytical method owing to its high specificity and analytical accuracy.[5] Traditionally, TDM involves measuring drug concentrations in various biological fluids and interpreting these concentrations in terms of relevant clinical parameters.

While digoxin TDM is well established, the relationship between serum digoxin levels and routinely measured biochemical parameters, such as renal or liver functions and electrolyte balance, has not been fully elucidated in datasets. The aim of this study was to assess digoxin measurements and evaluate the factors influencing drug levels, as well as their relationship with other laboratory parameters, such as renal and liver function and electrolytes.

2. Materials and methods

This retrospective study included samples from digoxin TDM requests received between February 1, 2024 and September 30, 2025 at Prof Dr Cemil Taşcioğlu City Hospital, a reference laboratory receiving specimens from home care services, outpatient clinics, and inpatient departments, including intensive care units. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Prof Dr Cemil Taşcioğlu City Hospital (approval number: 387/2025).

For each sample, patient age, sex, and the department submitting the test request were documented. Available laboratory parameters, including urea, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), sodium (Na), potassium (K), chloride (Cl), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), albumin, and total protein levels, were recorded. Urea, creatinine, ALT, and AST levels were analyzed using enzymatic spectrophotometric methods, whereas albumin, total protein, Ca, and Mg levels were measured by colorimetric spectrophotometric methods. Sodium, K, and Cl levels were analyzed using the ion-selective electrode method. All routine biochemical analyses were performed on the Beckman Coulter AU5800 autoanalyzer (Beckman-Coulter, Fullerton) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Serum digoxin samples were analyzed using a turbidimetric immunoassay method (Beckman-Coulter, Digoxin, OSR6404) with the Beckman Coulter AU/DxC AU 480 autoanalyzer (Beckman-Coulter, Fullerton) in the Medical Biochemistry Laboratory, and the results were expressed in ng/mL. The laboratory reference range for digoxin was defined as 0.8 to 2.0 ng/mL. According to digoxin concentration levels, samples were classified into 3 groups: subtherapeutic (<0.8 ng/mL), therapeutic (0.8–2.0 ng/mL), and supratherapeutic (>2.0 ng/mL). The detection limit for digoxin was 0.3 ng/mL, and samples with digoxin concentrations below the detection limit were included in the subtherapeutic group.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk). Repeated samples obtained from the same patients were retained and included in the analysis. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using visual (histograms) and analytical methods, and non-parametric tests were applied owing to the non-normal distribution of the data. Continuous variables were expressed as the median and interquartile range, while categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Associations between continuous variables, including serum digoxin levels and laboratory parameters, were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation coefficient. For group-wise comparisons, patients were categorized into 3 groups according to serum digoxin concentrations (subtherapeutic, therapeutic, and supratherapeutic). Differences in continuous variables among the 3 digoxin groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Analyses were performed on available cases for each parameter. When a statistically significant difference was detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn test with Bonferroni correction. Comparisons of categorical variables, including sex distribution, across digoxin groups were conducted using the chi-square test. A two-tailed P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

A total of 237 digoxin TDM requests were identified. Measured and reportable results were obtained for 232 samples. Of these, 51 were repeat samples, and 18 patients contributed more than one sample. Among total samples, 114 samples yielded detectable digoxin concentrations, whereas 118 samples were below the assay’s limit of detection (<0.3 ng/mL). The median age of the study population was 60 years, with females comprising 72.8% (Table 1). Among the laboratory parameters evaluated alongside digoxin TDM, K, Na, and creatinine were the most commonly requested tests (Table 1).

4. Discussion

TDM of digoxin plays a critical role in clinical practice, given its narrow therapeutic index and the substantial interindividual variability in pharmacokinetics and toxicity risk. The objective of the study was to evaluate digoxin measurements and factors affecting serum digoxin levels, and their relationship with various laboratory parameters, including AST, ALT, albumin, total protein, urea, creatinine, Na, K, Cl, Mg, and Ca. There was a lack of information about sampling time of digoxin, dosing, dosing interval, timing of sampling relative to dose, indication, and concomitant medications. Concomitant test results were received for eighteen patients, and fifty-one test results were repeated test values. The number of requested tests varied across parameters, since laboratory tests were not consistently ordered for all digoxin level requests. According to digoxin concentration levels, samples were classified into 3 groups: subtherapeutic (<0.8 ng/mL), therapeutic (0.8–2.0 ng/mL), and supratherapeutic (>2.0 ng/mL). The detection limit for digoxin was 0.3 ng/mL, and samples with digoxin concentrations below the detection limit were included in the subtherapeutic group. Subtherapeutic digoxin levels were associated with significantly lower urea and creatinine levels compared with therapeutic and supratherapeutic groups (P < .001), highlighting the importance of renal function monitoring during digoxin therapy. In addition, albumin and total protein levels were significantly higher in the digoxin subtherapeutic group than in the therapeutic and supratherapeutic groups, suggesting a potential role of protein metabolism in influencing digoxin levels.

The median age in our study was 60. A study from Sweden involving more than 35,000 digoxin results reported median ages of 77 and 83 years for males and females, respectively.[6] Consequently, the patients in our cohort were younger. It has been reported that females have twice the rate of emergency visits for digoxin toxicity when compared with males[7]; consistent with previous studies focusing on emergency department visits, our analysis demonstrated a higher proportion of female patients (72.8%), even though the study population was not restricted to emergency cases. Pediatric patients accounted for 4.7% of the samples. While digoxin is approved for pediatric use and is available in injectable dosage forms, its use has declined with the availability of alternative therapies.

Older adults with heart failure may also be at particular risk of developing digoxin toxicity due to concomitant comorbidities such as hypokalemia (often resulting from diuretic use) or renal impairment. In Türkiye, established home care services enable blood sampling at patients’ homes with centralized laboratory analysis for digoxin TDM; this particularly benefits elderly and immobile patients. Digoxin is not usually measured by point-of-care devices in the home setting.

Grześk et al reported significant sex-related differences in serum digoxin concentrations, with higher levels observed in women (P = .00002).[8] In our study, although supratherapeutic digoxin levels were numerically more frequent among women, this difference did not reach statistical significance. However, the inclusion of repeated patient samples without statistical adjustment may still compromise the independence of observations.

Despite its role in toxicity assessment, digoxin TDM was infrequently requested from the emergency department. The absence of point-of-care digoxin testing may explain its limited utility in guiding acute toxicity treatment. See et al evaluated emergency department visits and hospitalizations, concluding that digoxin use and the burden of digoxin toxicity – as measured by emergency visits and hospitalizations – did not decline from 2005 through 2010. The clinical approach toward requesting digoxin levels may vary between countries.[7] In another study from Türkiye, digoxin measurements were primarily requested by intensive care units (37.5%) and by cardiology departments (25%).[9]

Furthermore, the measurement of serum digoxin levels in patients evaluated at the neurosurgery outpatient clinic may reflect comorbid cardiovascular disease or the need for a differential diagnosis of neurocognitive symptoms potentially related to digoxin toxicity. Smaller proportions of samples were requested by cardiology clinics. This may be because digoxin is currently classified as a second-line, adjunctive drug in cardiology guidelines, used mainly for symptom and rate control rather than mortality benefit. Furthermore, current guidelines often lack specific recommendations for digoxin monitoring during pharmacotherapy.

Our results supported impaired renal function in the supratherapeutic group. Digoxin is predominantly eliminated via renal excretion; therefore, patients with reduced kidney function require lower maintenance doses than those with normal renal function. Inadequate dose reduction in this population may increase the risk of digoxin toxicity, and toxic effects may persist for a longer period compared with patients with preserved renal function. A population pharmacokinetic model of digoxin was developed for older patients with heart failure and chronic kidney disease, and simulation-based dosing recommendations were proposed to enhance therapeutic safety in this high-risk population.[10]

The frequent assessment of K, Na, and creatinine alongside digoxin TDM reflects the well-established influence of electrolyte imbalances and renal function on digoxin pharmacokinetics and toxicity risk, as hypokalemia and impaired renal clearance are key determinants of digoxin-related adverse effects. In the presence of electrolyte disturbances, particularly hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia, digoxin toxicity may occur even at serum concentrations below 2.0 ng/mL. The depletion of K or Mg enhances myocardial sensitivity to digoxin, thereby increasing the likelihood of toxic effects. In our study, K levels were not significantly different across the digoxin groups. Young et al[11] reported that patients with digoxin toxicity had lower serum Mg levels. In our study, although the supratherapeutic digoxin group exhibited lower Mg concentrations, this difference did not reach statistical significance.

Lower albumin and total protein levels may lead to an underestimation of total digoxin concentrations. Albumin and total protein levels were significantly higher in the subtherapeutic digoxin group compared with the therapeutic and supratherapeutic groups in our study. This finding may be explained by the influence of hypoalbuminemia on measured total digoxin concentrations, as digoxin is partially protein bound and routine assays quantify total rather than free drug levels. Samples obtained from the same patients at multiple time points should be taken into account when interpreting causal relationships.

More than half of the samples had drug concentrations below the assay’s limit of detection. Biochemical methods with lower detection limits may improve the interpretation of TDM results and support individualized dose adjustment. Multiple meta-analyses on digoxin therapy consistently emphasize the need for regular monitoring to maintain serum concentrations within the range of 0.5 to ≤1.0 ng/mL, as higher levels are associated with increased morbidity and mortality without additional clinical benefit.[12] TDM requires detailed medication-related information for accurate interpretation, including dosage, dosing intervals, sampling timing, and patient adherence to therapy. Digoxin necessitates rigorous monitoring in clinical practice because of its narrow therapeutic range. In current American and European heart failure guidelines, digoxin has a limited role and is recommended only for patients who remain symptomatic despite optimized guideline-directed medical therapy.[13] Despite its limited clinical role, novel mechanisms of digoxin have been proposed based on preliminary cellular and animal studies, raising the possibility of renewed therapeutic interest in the future.[14] Therefore, clinical cutoff levels should be reevaluated.

Limitations of the study include the lack of information regarding the appropriateness of sampling time, dosing, dosing interval, timing of sampling relative to dose, indication, and concomitant medications. Retrospective studies have certain limitations; all tests were not ordered for each digoxin level request. Therefore, statistical analyses were performed on available cases for each test parameter. Additionally, repeated samples obtained from the same patients were retained and included in the analysis, which may have introduced intra-individual variability.

5. Conclusions

Our study suggests that digoxin therapy should be evaluated together with comprehensive laboratory parameters, including urea, creatinine, ALT, albumin, total protein, and Ca. However, due to missing clinical variables outlined in the study limitations, a causal relationship could not be established. These results suggest the need for individualized evaluation in clinical practice. Nevertheless, the lack of key clinical variables limits causal interpretation. Future prospective studies with detailed clinical data are essential to confirm these findings and to refine digoxin monitoring strategies.
