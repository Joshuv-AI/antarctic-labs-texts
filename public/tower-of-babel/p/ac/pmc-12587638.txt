Prevalence of advanced fibrosis risk using FIB-4 in patients with type 2 diabetes and its associated factors: a cross-sectional study in a primary care unit of a university hospital in Southern Thailand
Authors: Thareerat Ananchaisarp, Panya Chamroonkiadtikun, Kan Bunchote, Jiraphat Thedpun, Chonnikan Srisamoot, Chuda Rujiworanat, Vissut Jayasakul, Natsurang Chaiphom, Thanaphum Kittivarapong, Nuthprawee Chuaysong, Pimsiri Sripongpun

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a prevalent noncommunicable disease that is strongly associated with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, a leading cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. The Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index, a noninvasive tool for liver fibrosis assessment, is recommended for all patients with T2DM, yet its use in routine clinical practice remains unclear. We aimed to determine the prevalence of intermediate- and high-risk of advanced liver fibrosis using FIB-4 in patients with T2DM and to identify associated factors. We also assessed documentation of FIB-4 scores and referrals to gastroenterologists in primary care.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among patients with T2DM receiving continuous follow-up care in a primary care setting at a university hospital in southern Thailand during 2024. The FIB-4 index was calculated and categorized into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups. Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with intermediate-to-high FIB-4 risk level.ResultsAmong 829 patients, 46.9% were classified as the intermediate-risk and 6.2% as the high-risk groups for having advanced fibrosis. A diabetes duration of ≥ 10 years was significantly associated with intermediate-to-high risk of advanced liver fibrosis [adjusted OR (95% CI) = 2.06 (1.51–2.81)]. Conversely, elevated triglyceride levels, low HDL cholesterol, and obesity were significantly associated with low risk of advanced liver fibrosis [adjusted OR (95% CI) = 0.65 (0.46–0.93), 0.68 (0.51–0.91) and 0.65 (0.49–0.88), respectively]. Imaging in the high-risk group revealed fatty liver in 61.1% and cirrhosis in 22.2%. However, FIB-4 scores were documented in only 1.6% of records, and only 4.1% of intermediate- to high-risk patients were referred to gastroenterology specialists.ConclusionMore than half of the patients with T2DM had a FIB-4 index suggesting increased risk of advanced liver fibrosis, especially those with longer disease duration. Background

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a global health concern, affecting an estimated 537 million individuals in 2021, with projections rising to 783 million by 2045 [1]. While T2DM is widely recognized for its cardiovascular and renal complications, its strong association with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is increasingly acknowledged [2]. The prevalence of MASLD varies depending on the study population and diagnostic criteria used. For example, rates of up to 70% have been reported in patients with T2DM or obesity in Europe [3], 87% in T2DM patients in Indonesia [4], and 82.8% in T2DM patients with normal liver enzymes in Thailand [5]. MASLD could lead to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with fibrosis being the strongest predictor of liver-related mortality [6, 7]. Conversely, T2DM is a major risk factor for advanced fibrosis among MASLD patients [8, 9].

MASLD comprises various stages, ranging from simple steatosis, steatohepatitis, and liver fibrosis to cirrhosis. Liver fibrosis is characterized by scarring of the liver tissue due to ongoing inflammation from chronic liver disease. It is staged from F0 to F4, with F0 indicating no fibrosis and F4 representing cirrhosis. Advanced liver fibrosis, defined as fibrosis stage of at least F3, is significantly associated with an increased risk of liver-related complications in patients with MASLD [10, 11].

Despite its clinical importance, liver fibrosis frequently remains undiagnosed in patients with T2DM because of its asymptomatic presentation and the invasiveness of liver biopsy, the reference standard for fibrosis assessment [12]. To address this challenge, the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index has emerged as a validated, noninvasive tool for estimating liver fibrosis risk. FIB-4 is calculated using routinely available biochemical parameters, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), platelet count, and age, to stratify patients into low-, intermediate-, or high-risk groups for having advanced liver fibrosis. Its ease of use, cost-effectiveness, and high negative predictive value make it particularly suitable for use in primary care [13, 14]. Accordingly, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) has recommended annual FIB-4 screening for all patients with T2DM since 2023 [15].

The prevalence of intermediate-to-high FIB-4 index scores in T2DM patients varies across studies and settings. For example, prevalence rates of 59.6% have been reported in university hospitals in Japan [16], 28.2% in hospitals in Greece [17], and 43.0% in university hospitals in the United States [10]. In a population with T2DM, diabetes duration over 10 years, pioglitazone or statin use, increased waist circumference, microalbuminuria, LDL-C above 100 mg/dL, and low platelet count were associated with increased odds of advanced liver fibrosis. Conversely, underlying hypertension and triglycerides over 150 mg/dL were negatively associated with advanced liver fibrosis [18–20].

However, few studies have evaluated the prevalence of intermediate-to-high FIB-4 risk and its associated factors specifically in primary care settings. Furthermore, the extent to which FIB-4 is documented or used to guide referrals in routine practice remains largely unexplored, despite clinical guideline recommendations. To address this gap, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the prevalence of intermediate-to-high-risk FIB-4 index among patients with T2DM. Secondary objectives included identifying associated clinical factors, assessing documentation of the FIB-4 index in medical records, and evaluating referral rates to gastroenterologists.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cross-sectional study included patients with T2DM who were followed up at the Primary Care Unit (PCU) and General Practice (GP) clinic of Songklanagarind Hospital, a tertiary care university hospital and referral center in southern Thailand, during 2024.

Study sample and sampling

All patients with T2DM, identified by ICD-10 codes in electronic health records, who received continuous care (defined as ≥ 2 follow-up visits) at PCU and GP clinics during January 1 to December 31, 2024, were considered eligible for inclusion. Patients were excluded if they had a history of hepatitis B (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, as indicated by positive HBsAg or anti-HCV serology, or if they lacked the laboratory data necessary for FIB-4 index calculation. For patients with multiple eligible visits during the study period, data from the most recent visit were used for the analysis.

The sample size calculation was guided by the primary objective of estimating the prevalence of intermediate-to-high FIB-4 index among patients with T2DM. Using the population proportion formula, n = [(Z1-α/2)²p(1-p)]/d², the minimum required sample size was determined to be 370. This calculation was based on a previously reported prevalence of FIB-4 >1.3 in 59.6% of T2DM patients [16], with a desired margin of error (d) of 0.05. The number of eligible patients during the study period was adequate to meet the calculated sample size; however, to maximize representativeness, all eligible patients during the study period were included in the analysis without the use of a sampling technique.

Variables

The dependent variable for the primary objective was the FIB-4 categories, calculated as [21]:

The results were categorized into three risk groups for having advanced liver fibrosis: low, intermediate, and high-risk. The FIB-4 index strata in the present study were analyzed using two methods [22–24]. The first method applied a fixed-cutoff for all ages, as advised by the ADA [25]: <1.3 (low-risk), 1.3 to < 2.67 (intermediate-risk), and ≥ 2.67 (high-risk). The second method used age-adjusted cut-offs recommended by the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) [22]: for participants < 65 years old, the same fixed-cutoff were used, while for participants ≥ 65 years old, the cut-offs were low-risk (< 2.0), intermediate-risk (2.0 to < 2.67), and high-risk (≥ 2.67). The dependent variables for the secondary objective were documentation of the FIB-4 index in the medical records and referral to a gastroenterologist.

Independent variables, selected based on a literature review, included sex, age, waist circumference, AST, ALT, platelet count, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), triglycerides, high density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, duration of diabetes mellitus, and presence of comorbidities (hypertension, dyslipidemia, and obesity). Laboratory values were chosen from the most recent measurement within one year prior to the study visit. The duration of T2DM was based on physician documentation; if the date of diagnosis was unavailable, the date of the first recorded hospital visit for diabetes treatment was used. Comorbidities were identified either through physician-documented diagnoses or by evidence of pharmacologic treatment for the condition.

Data collection

Eligible patients and laboratory data were identified according to the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria and extracted from the Digital Innovation and Data Analytics (DIDA) Unit of the institute. Subsequently, medical records were reviewed using the Hospital Information System to obtain baseline characteristics, documentation of the FIB-4 index, and records of referrals to gastroenterology specialists. All researchers received training on the data collection process and the interpretation of each variable prior to initiating data extraction to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Data management and analysis

All statistical analyses were done using R software (R Core Team, 2022, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, FIB-4 index documentation, abdominal imaging orders (ultrasound, CT, or MRI), and gastroenterology referrals. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3), owing to their non-normal distributions. To compare differences among the three advanced fibrosis risk groups, we applied the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. Factors included in the FIB-4 score will be described but not compared using statistical tests.

In primary care setting, those with intermediate- or high-risk FIB-4 should be further investigated, while patients with low-risk FIB-4 could be managed safely with primary care doctors, we then combined the intermediate- and high-risk FIB-4 groups for further analysis. We subsequently performed a binary logistic regression to identify factors associated with intermediate-to-high-risk FIB-4 strata. An initial multivariable model included variables with p-values < 0.2 from univariable analysis: duration of T2DM (categorized as < 10 years and ≥ 10 years), obesity, hypertension, triglycerides (categorized as < 150 mg/dL and ≥ 150 mg/dL per established thresholds [26]), HDL cholesterol (categorized as low (defined as < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women) or high according to standard definitions [26]). The factors which have previously been known to be associated with liver fibrosis risk were also included in the model: sex, LDL, and HbA1c (categorized as < 7.0% and ≥ 7.0%, reflecting T2DM glycemic control status [27]). Variables already incorporated in the FIB-4 formula (age, AST, ALT, and platelet count) were excluded from the prediction model. Likewise, systolic and diastolic blood pressure were omitted to prevent multicollinearity with the hypertension variable. Backward stepwise elimination was applied to identify statistically significant predictors. And finally, we performed sensitivity analyses to compare the risk of advanced fibrosis when applying a single fixed FIB-4 index cut-off to all participants against using an age-adjusted cut-off. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Of the 1,503 medical records of patients with T2DM who received continuous follow-up at the PCU and GP clinic in 2024, 13 were excluded due to a history of hepatitis B or C infection, and 657 (43.7%) lacked the laboratory data required to calculate the FIB-4 index. This resulted in a final sample size of 829 eligible patients (Fig. 1). Approximately 58.1% of patients were female, and the median age was 65.0 years (interquartile range 59.0–72.0) (Table 1). Dyslipidemia was a common comorbidity, and 39.1% of patients met the criteria for metabolic syndrome.

Table 1 presents patients’ characteristics stratified by the risk strata of having advanced fibrosis categorized by fixed-cutoff FIB-4 index. Of 829 eligible diabetes patients, 389 (46.9%) were classified as low-risk, 389 patients (46.9%) as intermediate-risk, and 51 (6.2%) as high-risk. Obesity and metabolic syndrome were significantly more prevalent in the low-risk group compared to the higher-risk categories. Diabetes duration increased progressively with higher FIB-4 index risk (p < 0.001).

The prevalence of FIB-4 index documentation, requests for hepatic imaging, and gastroenterology referrals are presented in Table 2. FIB-4 index documentation was present in only 1.6% of medical records, with no significant differences across FIB-4 risk groups. Hepatic imaging was requested in 213 patients (25.7%), nearly half of which were prompted by suspected fatty liver. The distribution of imaging findings—normal liver, fatty liver, and cirrhosis—differed significantly across risk groups (p = 0.022). Fatty liver was detected in over 75% of patients who underwent imaging. Cirrhosis was observed in 22.2% of high-risk patients but was uncommon in low- and intermediate-risk groups. Gastroenterology consultation was documented in 11.3% of patients overall, primarily for evaluation and management of MASLD or cirrhosis. Notably, only 4.1% (18 out of 440) of patients in the intermediate-to-high-risk categories, those for whom referral would generally be indicated, were referred for gastroenterology consultation.

We conducted a sensitivity analysis in which participants were categorized using an age-adjusted FIB-4 index rather than a fixed-cutoff model. As shown in Table 4, this approach classified 579 patients (69.8%) as low risk, exceeding the number classified by the fixed-cutoff FIB-4 (46.9%). Similar to the fixed-cutoff FIB-4, diabetes duration progressively increased with higher FIB-4 index risk. Table 5 demonstrates that two factors remained significantly associated with an intermediate-to-high risk of liver fibrosis categorized by age-adjusted FIB-4 index: diabetes duration of ≥ 10 years [adjusted OR (95% CI) = 1.37 (1.01–1.87)] and high triglyceride levels [adjusted OR (95% CI) = 0.68 (0.46–0.99)].

Discussion

A substantial burden of potentially advanced liver fibrosis, as indicated by an intermediate-to-high-risk FIB-4 index, was observed in a large proportion of the patients with T2DM, particularly among those with longer diabetes duration. This finding contrasts sharply with the exceptionally low utilization of the FIB-4 index as a routine screening tool in clinical practice.

Among the 829 T2DM patients included in our study, 53.1% had an intermediate-to-high-risk FIB-4 index (≥ 1.3) using a fixed-cutoff, a proportion consistent with findings from a Japanese study reporting a prevalence of 59.6% [16].

The ADA recommends annual FIB-4 screening for adults with T2DM, even if they have normal liver enzyme levels [15]. The FIB-4 tool comprises four readily obtainable variables in a primary care setting (age, AST, ALT, and platelets), and prior studies have highlighted its benefit as a screening tool to improve detection of liver disease in the general population [28, 29]. However, our study found that only 1.6% of eligible patients had the FIB-4 index documented in their medical records, a notably low rate. We acknowledge the possibility that physicians caring for these patients might have calculated the FIB-4 score but did not record it in the medical records. Nonetheless, among all T2DM patients screened, nearly 50% were excluded due to lacking the necessary laboratory data for calculation. These findings underscore a significant gap in current clinical practice and highlight the urgent need to integrate FIB-4 screening into routine primary care to improve early detection and management of liver fibrosis in patients with T2DM [28, 29].

We also identified factors associated with intermediate-to-high risk of advanced fibrosis, as categorized by fixed-cutoff FIB-4 index. Patients with a diabetes duration of 10 years or more had higher FIB-4 index values compared to those with a shorter disease duration. This finding is consistent with the previous study [18]. And it may be attributed to the contribution of dysfunctional insulin-resistant adipose tissue to hepatic fibrosis in T2DM, independent of BMI or steatosis. Chronic hyperglycemia and prolonged insulin resistance promote hepatic fat accumulation and disease progression [30]. In contrast, elevated triglyceride levels and low HDL cholesterol were significantly associated with lower FIB-4 index values in this study. These results are aligned with the previous study as well [18]. Fasting triglyceride levels have been shown to inversely correlate with liver fibrosis, particularly in advanced cases, potentially due to impaired hepatic lipoprotein synthesis in patients with chronic liver disease [31, 32]. Interestingly, obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) was not associated with elevated FIB-4 index in our study, in contrast to previous studies reporting a positive association between higher BMI and more advanced liver fibrosis [8, 33]. The underlying reasons for this counterintuitive finding remain unclear. Similar patterns have been observed in studies of Asian populations [34–36], whereas Western cohorts more consistently demonstrate a relationship between higher BMI and increased fibrosis risk. Advanced fibrosis may alter metabolic function, potentially leading to weight loss and explaining the lower BMI observed in the high-risk group [37].

Our study possesses several notable strengths. Specifically, we focused on the documentation and utilization of the FIB-4 index in routine diabetes care, an area that has received limited attention in literature. The use of the hospital’s electronic information system improved data reliability by providing access to comprehensive comorbidity data, laboratory and imaging results, and reducing transcription errors related to handwritten records. Nonetheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design led to missing some clinical data, including waist circumference and HbA1c levels, which may have affected the analysis of associated factors. Furthermore, excluding 657 participants due to missing laboratory data required for FIB-4 calculation may have introduced selection bias. To assess potential bias, we compared included and excluded participants (Supplementary Table 1). The two groups were similar in sex distribution, obesity prevalence, and waist circumference; however, excluded participants were significantly younger than those included. This age difference may have contributed to a lower mean FIB-4 index among excluded participants. Using age-adjusted cut-off analysis likely mitigates this potential selection bias. Second, under-documentation of the FIB-4 index may not fully reflect clinical underutilization, since some physicians may have calculated the index without recording it, potentially overestimating the degree of underutilization. However, nearly half of all T2DM patients followed up at our center lacked the laboratory data necessary to calculate the FIB-4 index, as mentioned earlier. This supports the conclusion that FIB-4 was underutilized in our study. Third, the limited number of patients who underwent imaging studies may have reduced our ability to fully assess the association between FIB-4 index levels and imaging-confirmed liver disease severity. Finally, this study was conducted at a single tertiary care center in southern Thailand, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings with different patient demographics and clinical practices.

In summary, given the substantial proportion of patients classified as intermediate-to-high risk of advanced liver fibrosis and the very few referred to gastroenterologists, the FIB-4 index should be incorporated as a routine screening tool for patients with T2DM in primary care to enable early identification of at-risk individuals and facilitate timely intervention. In high-volume clinical settings, prioritizing FIB-4 screening for patients with established risk factors, such as prolonged diabetes duration and comorbid hypertension, may offer a practical approach. Future studies should employ prospective designs to improve the completeness and reliability of data related to predictors of elevated FIB-4 scores. Additionally, research assessing primary care physicians’ awareness, attitudes, and practices regarding liver fibrosis screening and the use of the FIB-4 index is warranted.

Conclusion

More than half of patients with T2DM in this study had an intermediate-to-high-risk FIB-4 index using the fixed cutoff, reflecting a substantial burden of potential liver fibrosis, particularly among those with longer diabetes duration. Despite this burden, the FIB-4 index remains markedly underutilized as a routine screening tool in clinical practice. These findings underscore the need to routinely incorporate FIB-4 screening into diabetes care pathways, especially in primary care, to facilitate early intervention and potentially prevent progression to advanced liver disease.
