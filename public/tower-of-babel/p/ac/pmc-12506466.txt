The efficacy of high-protein nutritional support on mortality, clinical outcomes, and nutritional adequacy in critically ill patients: a double‑center randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mohaddeseh Badpeyma, Alireza Sedaghat, Ahmad Bagheri Moghaddam, Majid Khadem-Rezaiyan, Fatemeh Sistanian, Mohammad Bagherniya, Golnaz Ranjbar, Farzaneh Fazeli, Abdolreza Norouzy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough nutritional support is crucial in intensive care, the impact of protein intake remains unclear, emphasizing the need for further randomized controlled trials. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of high-protein versus conventional-protein nutritional support on clinical outcomes in critically ill patients, with 60-day mortality as the primary endpoint.MethodIn this double-blind, two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial, 56 adult patients admitted to the intensive care unit [1] were enrolled. Participants received either high-protein support (2.2 g/kg/day, actual body weight [ABW]) or conventional-protein support (1.0 g/kg/day, ABW) for 12 days. Both groups targeted 25 kcal/kg/day energy intake. Patients and data analysts were blinded. Mortality was assessed at ICU discharge, on days 28 and 60, and at hospital discharge. Hospital mortality was defined as any death occurring during the hospital stay, including both the ICU and post-ICU periods. Mid-arm circumference (MAC) was measured as an indicator of muscle attenuation.ResultsMean protein intake was 1.67 ± 0.33 vs. 0.93 ± 0.10 g/kg/day in high- vs. conventional-protein groups (P < 0.05). In-hospital mortality was significantly lower in the high-protein group (8 patients [28.6%]) compared to the conventional-protein group (16 patients [57.1%]; adjusted P = 0.049). Although 60-day mortality was also lower in the high-protein group (28.6% vs. 53.6%), the difference did not reach statistical significance (adjusted P = 0.07). A significant reduction in MAC attenuation was observed in the high-protein group (P < 0.001).ConclusionHigh-protein intake (1.67 g/kg/day) significantly reduced in-hospital mortality and improved preservation of muscle mass. Although 60-day mortality reduction was not significant, the trend suggests a meaningful benefit warranting further study.IRCT registration IDIRCT20180619040151N4.

Introduction

The optimal nutritional strategy for critically ill patients remains an area of active investigation, with current evidence offering limited consensus on best practices [2, 3]. Protein provision plays a central role in intensive care unit (ICU) management, as it helps preserve lean body mass, supports immune function, and reduces complications such as infections and prolonged mechanical ventilation [4, 5]. These benefits are mediated through well-established physiological mechanisms, including modulation of inflammation, maintenance of nitrogen balance, and attenuation of muscle catabolism during critical illness [6, 7]. However, the acute phase of critical illness is characterized by severe metabolic stress, systemic inflammation, and frequent enteral feeding intolerance, which collectively complicate nutritional management. Furthermore, the optimal energy and protein targets during this phase remain uncertain, as current evidence is inconclusive [8–10]. To address this clinical and evidentiary gap, we conducted a rigorously designed randomized controlled trial to compare the effectiveness of high-protein versus conventional-protein nutritional strategies in critically ill ICU patients.

Clinical nutrition effectively alleviates and manages morbidity and has a crucial effect on critically ill patients [11–13]. Although energy targets have been extensively studied [14–16], protein intake has increasingly been recognized as a more critical determinant of clinical outcomes, particularly in preserving muscle mass, supporting immune function, and counteracting the effects of hypercatabolism [17–20]. Observational studies have reported a non-linear, U-shaped relationship between macronutrient delivery and mortality, suggesting that both underfeeding and overfeeding, especially when protein provision is suboptimal, may adversely affect patient outcomes [19, 21]. These findings underscore the importance of individualized, precision-based nutritional strategies in the ICU setting.

Current international guidelines endorse moderate-to-high protein delivery for critically ill patients. The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) recommends a minimum intake of 1.3 g/kg/day of protein equivalents, adjusted to actual or ideal body weight, to attenuate muscle catabolism and support recovery [22]. This recommendation is primarily based on expert consensus and clinical experience, given the inconclusive and inconsistent findings across existing randomized trials. Similarly, the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN), in collaboration with the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM), advises a protein intake of 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day for the general ICU population, with higher doses—up to 2.5 g/kg/day—suggested for hypercatabolic conditions such as trauma, burns, or sepsis. These targets aim to optimize nitrogen balance and preserve lean body mass in the context of acute metabolic stress [20, 23]. However, despite these recommendations, clinical equipoise persists, as high-quality studies have yielded divergent outcomes and no definitive consensus has yet emerged regarding the optimal protein dosing strategy in critical care.

Multicenter studies, such as the International Nutrition Survey, have shown that critically ill patients receive, on average, only 55% of their prescribed protein intake, with substantial variability across institutions [21]. However, beyond issues of feasibility, a more fundamental challenge lies in the limited understanding of whether higher protein delivery translates into improved clinical outcomes in this population. Observational studies have consistently shown that higher protein intake has anabolic and potentially protective effects in critically ill patients, contributing to improved nitrogen balance and the preservation of lean body mass [24]. However, findings from recent large-scale randomized controlled trials—including PRECISE and NUTRIREA-3—have been heterogeneous, with several failing to demonstrate consistent improvements in survival or functional recovery. Notably, emerging evidence has also raised concerns about potential adverse effects, including reduced health-related quality of life during long-term follow-up [25, 26]. These conflicting findings highlight the persistent uncertainty surrounding the efficacy, optimal dosing, and safety of high-protein nutrition in critically ill patients, reinforcing the need to elucidate its clinical utility within the context of ICU care.

A double-blind, two-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial was conducted in critically ill patients to address existing knowledge gaps by comparing two isocaloric feeding strategies. Within 48 h of ICU admission, patients were randomized to receive either high-protein (2.2 g/kg/day, based on ABW) or conventional-protein (1.0 g/kg/day, ABW) nutritional support, delivered via a combined enteral and parenteral route. Energy intake was standardized at 25 kcal/kg/day across both groups. The primary outcome was 60-day all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality (defined as death before hospital discharge), ICU and hospital length of stay (LOS), duration of mechanical ventilation, nutritional adequacy, gastrointestinal tolerance, and selected biochemical markers. Given the potential for high protein intake to influence metabolic tolerance, via mechanisms such as increased ureagenesis and gastrointestinal burden, these secondary outcomes were chosen to enable a comprehensive safety evaluation. This study was designed to generate clinically meaningful evidence to inform individualized protein delivery strategies in the management of critically ill patients.

Materials and methods

This parallel, randomized, double-blind controlled trial was conducted in the general intensive care unit of Imam Reza Hospital, Mashhad, Iran, between November 2019 and May 2020. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: I.R.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1397.543) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) under registration number IRCT20180619040151N4 on November 7, 2019. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, or, when patients were incapacitated, from their legally authorized representatives, by the ethical standards for research involving critically ill individuals.

Patients

All patients were admitted to the Edalatian Intensive Care Unit of Imam Reza Hospital in Mashhad, Iran.

The primary inclusion criteria were: age between 18 and 80 years; ICU admission; hemodynamic and metabolic stability within the first 24–48 h (as assessed by the attending intensivist based on stable vital signs, adequate organ perfusion, and readiness to initiate nutritional support); requirement for enteral nutrition (EN); and an expected duration of invasive mechanical ventilation exceeding 72 h. Exclusion criteria included: inability to tolerate EN due to anatomical or mechanical barriers (e.g., bowel obstruction); exclusive indication for parenteral nutrition (PN); pregnancy or lactation; current or prior history of acute or chronic renal failure; hepatic encephalopathy; receipt of the study intervention for fewer than four days; inability to obtain written informed consent; concurrent enrollment in other clinical trials; frequent need for blood transfusions; or physician discretion deeming the patient unsuitable for participation.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the control or intervention group. Following screening and informed consent, stratified block randomization was performed based on age and sex. Male and female participants were stratified using permuted blocks of size four. Allocation sequences were generated by an independent statistician using a random number table and sealed in opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. These envelopes were opened only after eligibility was confirmed to ensure allocation concealment. Blenderized tube feeding (BTF) formulas—high-protein (2.2 g/kg/day, ABW) and conventional-protein (1.0 g/kg/day, ABW)—were prepared and labeled as Formula A and Formula B by the hospital kitchen in identical containers. The two formulas were visually indistinguishable in appearance, color, and smell. While treating clinicians were not blinded due to their role in prescribing and administering the nutritional intervention, patients and outcome assessors remained blinded to group assignments throughout the study.

Nutrition protocol

Patients were assigned to either the high-protein or conventional-protein group, and interventions were initiated with patients blinded to group allocation. EN was started immediately after randomization whenever clinically feasible. Energy and protein requirements were calculated by trained clinical nutritionists using ESPEN guidelines, with actual pre-ICU body weight or ideal body weight (based on a body mass index [BMI] of 25 kg/m²) for patients with obesity (BMI > 30). In the absence of indirect calorimetry, an estimated energy target of 25 kcal/kg/day was applied, as recommended by ESPEN [10]. To minimize the risk of overfeeding, clinicians closely monitored energy provision in both groups. From day 1 to day 12, total daily caloric and protein intake from both enteral and parenteral sources was recorded to assess nutritional adequacy. Parenteral energy included contributions from intravenous dextrose and, where applicable, propofol infusions. Parenteral protein intake was calculated based on the amount of intravenous amino acids administered. EN was delivered via a nasogastric tube within 24 to 48 h of ICU admission. Nutritional intake was initiated at a low rate and gradually increased every 6 h based on patient tolerance, as defined in the feeding protocol. In the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms, such as diarrhea, regurgitation, abdominal distension, or gastric residual volume (GRV) exceeding 500 mL, the feeding rate was titrated upward. When EN was insufficient due to intolerance, PN was initiated. Supplemental PN was provided as needed, with early initiation permitted under the supervision of the attending intensivist. The intervention (high-protein) group aimed for a protein intake of 2.2 g/kg/day, while the control (conventional-protein) group targeted 1.0 g/kg/day.

Nutritional support was administered seven times per day using the bolus method. In both groups, BTF was administered via bolus feeding, with an energy density of 82.26 kcal/100 mL (composition per 100 mL: 8.14 g carbohydrates, 5 g protein, and 3.3 g fat). To supplement protein intake, ISO Whey Protein (Karen Company, Iran) was provided as an additional protein source (per 28 g: 101 kcal, 24 g protein, 0.5 g lactose, 0.3 g dietary fiber, and 0.3 g total fat). Enteral nutrition was continued until the patient either died or it was discontinued by the attending physician under the following conditions: (i) the patient was discharged from the ICU, or (ii) the continuation of enteral nutrition was deemed clinically unnecessary. These nutritional interventions were maintained for up to 12 days, a duration selected based on the median ICU length of stay reported in comparable randomized trials involving critically ill patients. The nutritional goal was to deliver between 80% and 100% of the estimated energy and protein requirements, in accordance with ESPEN and ASPEN guidelines. Clinicians were advised to avoid overfeeding, particularly during the acute phase of illness. PN was withheld in patients with hemodynamic instability, based on clinical judgment. All patients received standard ICU care, including protocol-based treatments as prescribed by the attending physician. Routine medications and adjuvant therapies were continued, as withholding them would have been ethically inappropriate.

Data collection and measurements

A specialized nutritionist recorded daily energy and protein intake throughout the intervention period. Demographic and baseline anthropometric data—including age, sex, weight, height (estimated using ulna length), BMI, comorbidities, primary diagnosis, and relevant medical history—were collected at enrollment.

Mid-arm circumference (MAC) was measured twice—once at baseline (Day 1) and again on Day 12—to assess changes in muscle mass over time. MAC was used as an indicator of nutritional status and served as a secondary outcome measure, reflecting muscle preservation over the intervention period. Measurements were taken using a non-elastic measuring tape on either the left or right arm. The midpoint between the acromion and olecranon processes was identified by flexing the elbow to 90°, then extending the arm along the torso for measurement [27].

Nutritional status was evaluated using a combination of anthropometric measures (BMI and MAC), biochemical markers (serum albumin and total protein), and validated screening tools. These included the modified Nutrition Risk in the Critically Ill (NUTRIC) score (excluding IL-6), the Strength, Assistance with walking, Rise from a chair, Climb stairs, and Falls (SARC-F) questionnaire for sarcopenia, and the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS). Illness severity and organ dysfunction were assessed using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores [28]. APACHE II, SARC-F, NUTRIC, and CFS scores were recorded at baseline, while SOFA scores were recorded every other day throughout the intervention period.

The primary outcome was 60-day all-cause mortality, chosen to capture both short- and medium-term effects of the nutritional intervention. Secondary outcomes included 28-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, ICU and hospital LOS, duration of mechanical ventilation, nutritional adequacy, gastrointestinal tolerance, and changes in MAC. In-hospital mortality was determined from hospital records, while 28-day and 60-day mortality were confirmed using hospital data or post-discharge follow-up via telephone contact with patients’ families or caregivers.

Biochemical outcomes were assessed at baseline and on Day 12, including serum urea, creatinine (Cr), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), albumin, total protein, liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), and C-reactive protein (CRP). The urea-to-creatinine ratio (UCR) was calculated to evaluate potential metabolic effects associated with high-protein intake, particularly in the context of protein overfeeding.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software, version 20 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables, and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using independent t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the data’s normality. Within-group comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To adjust for baseline differences and potential confounders, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed for post-intervention outcome comparisons. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate associations with hospital mortality. Normality of data distribution was assessed using skewness statistics and Q–Q plots. Missing data for quantitative variables were addressed according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, and all randomized patients were included in the final analysis. Missing outcome values were handled using multiple imputation techniques [29].

Results

Study population

Eligibility was assessed in 261 patients in the ICU. Of these, 194 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 11 declined to participate. A total of 56 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned equally to the high-protein and conventional-protein groups (n = 28 per group) (Fig. 1). In the high-protein group, two participants were unable to achieve adequate protein intake due to persistent feeding intolerance and were deemed clinically unsuitable for parenteral nutrition. In the control group, two participants discontinued the intervention before Day 4. Despite these deviations, all randomized patients were included in the final analysis according to the ITT principle. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups in terms of age, sex, height, weight, BMI, APACHE II score, SOFA score, SARC-F score, CFS, or relevant past medical history. The distribution of ICU admission diagnoses did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.50; Fisher’s exact test).

Total energy and protein delivery increased progressively in both groups over the intervention period. The mean daily energy intake was 1385.33 ± 245 kcal in the high-protein group and 1248.08 ± 250 kcal in the conventional-protein group. Although the high-protein group received, on average, 137.25 kcal/day more, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.061). Daily energy intake and adequacy are illustrated in Fig. 2. Protein intake differed significantly between the groups. The high-protein group received a mean of 1.67 ± 0.33 g/kg/day, compared to 0.93 ± 0.10 g/kg/day in the conventional-protein group (P < 0.05 for all intervention days; Fig. 3). Protein adequacy over the 12 days was 76.21% in the high-protein group and 93.29% in the conventional-protein group, with this difference reaching statistical significance (P < 0.001; Table 2).

Primary and secondary outcomes

Although 60-day mortality was lower in the high-protein group (28.6%) compared to the conventional-protein group (53.6%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (unadjusted P = 0.057, Pearson chi-square test; adjusted P = 0.07, logistic regression controlling for age, BMI, and ICU admission diagnosis; Table 3). The number needed to treat (NNT) to prevent one additional 60-day mortality was calculated to be 4, suggesting a potentially meaningful clinical effect despite the lack of statistical significance. ICU mortality was lower in the high-protein group (8 patients [28.6%]) compared to the conventional-protein group (15 patients [53.6%]; unadjusted P = 0.057; adjusted P = 0.09). In-hospital mortality was significantly lower in the high-protein group (8 patients [28.6%]) compared to the conventional-protein group (16 patients [57.1%]; unadjusted P = 0.029, Pearson chi-square test; adjusted P = 0.049, logistic regression controlling for age, BMI, and ICU admission; Table 3). ICU length of stay was 27.16 ± 18.57 days in the high-protein group and 22.38 ± 15.24 days in the conventional-protein group (P = 0.32). Hospital length of stay was 29.25 ± 20.12 days and 26.21 ± 17.01 days, respectively (P = 0.57). The duration of mechanical ventilation was 9.96 ± 2.93 days in the high-protein group versus 10.39 ± 2.58 days in the conventional group (P = 0.56). Gastrointestinal intolerance occurred in 4 patients (14.3%) in the high-protein group and 8 patients (28.6%) in the conventional-protein group (P = 0.32). No statistically significant differences were observed in any of these secondary outcomes.

Nutritional and biochemical markers

Higher protein delivery was associated with a significantly reduced loss of MAC, suggesting improved preservation of lean body mass (P < 0.001; Table 4).

SOFA scores remained relatively stable in the high-protein group, while a modest increase was observed in the control group; however, the post-intervention difference was not statistically significant. No significant between-group differences were observed in biochemical parameters, including serum albumin, total protein, liver enzymes (ALT, AST), and renal function markers (creatinine, urea, eGFR). Similarly, no significant difference was found between groups in the trajectory of the UCR during the intervention period (mean difference = 1.74; 95% CI: − 13.39 to 16.88; P = 0.883), suggesting that high-protein feeding did not result in disproportionate urea production or impaired renal clearance. These findings indicate that higher protein intake was not associated with adverse hepatic or renal effects.

Discussion

Impact on mortality and clinical outcomes

In this single-center, randomized controlled trial, high-protein nutritional support (mean: 1.67 g/kg/day) was associated with a clinically meaningful reduction in ICU and 60-day mortality compared to conventional-protein intake (0.93 g/kg/day). In-hospital mortality was significantly lower in the high-protein group (28.6% vs. 57.1%), and a 25% absolute reduction in 60-day mortality was observed, suggesting a potentially beneficial trend despite not reaching statistical significance (unadjusted P = 0.057; adjusted P = 0.07 after controlling for age, BMI, and ICU admission diagnosis).

These findings are consistent with previous observational and interventional studies suggesting that increased protein delivery is associated with improved clinical outcomes in critically ill patients [30–32]. However, conflicting results from several large randomized controlled trials—including those by Doig et al., Ferrie et al., Fetterplace et al., van Zanten et al., De Waele et al., and Allingstrup—have reported neutral or inconsistent effects of high-protein strategies [33–38], underscoring the persistent uncertainty and complexity in determining the optimal protein dose for this population.

Muscle preservation and MAC

Preservation of skeletal muscle mass is a critical objective in the management of critically ill patients, as muscle loss can exceed 10% within the first 10 days of ICU stay when protein delivery is suboptimal [2]. In our study, patients in the high-protein group exhibited a significantly smaller reduction in MAC, which may suggest attenuated muscle wasting. However, as MAC is an indirect and nonspecific marker—potentially influenced by factors such as fat redistribution or fluid shifts—this finding should be interpreted with caution and regarded as a proxy rather than a definitive indicator of muscle mass preservation.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports supporting the anabolic benefits of higher protein delivery in the ICU setting. Notably, one study demonstrated increased diaphragmatic thickness in mechanically ventilated patients receiving 2 g/kg/day of protein [39]. A weekly reduction in muscle loss of 3.4% with increased protein provision has also been reported [40]. Additionally, prior studies have emphasized the synergistic effect of higher protein intake combined with early resistance exercise in attenuating muscle wasting [39, 41]. Collectively, these results support the role of moderate-to-high protein feeding in preserving muscle integrity during critical illness. Nonetheless, our interpretation remains cautious, given the limitations of MAC as a surrogate marker of actual muscle mass in ICU patients.

Mechanistic considerations

From a mechanistic perspective, protein metabolism during critical illness is governed by complex interactions, including increased proteolysis, hormonal dysregulation (e.g., elevated cortisol and insulin resistance), and suppression of anabolic signaling pathways such as mTOR. These disruptions underscore the need for higher protein delivery to counteract catabolic stress and support muscle protein synthesis [42]. Mechanistic studies have shown that intravenously administered amino acids (IVAAs) stimulate de novo protein synthesis and improve nitrogen balance [21(Liebau, 2021 #250, 43]. Sustained protein provision throughout the ICU stay may therefore contribute to the preservation of lean body mass and facilitate improved clinical recovery.

Metabolic tolerance and UCR safety

The metabolic safety of higher protein intake in critically ill patients has been the focus of ongoing investigation, primarily due to concerns about nitrogen burden, renal stress, and hepatic dysfunction. In our study, high-protein feeding, with a mean intake of 1.67 g/kg/day, was not associated with significant differences in renal or hepatic biomarkers between the groups. Serum levels of urea, creatinine, GFR, ALT, and AST remained stable throughout the intervention, and no evidence of metabolic intolerance was observed. Notably, the urea-to-creatinine ratio (UCR)—an emerging marker of protein overfeeding—also remained stable. In contrast to the findings from the EFFORT trial, where elevated UCR was associated with poorer clinical outcomes [44], our results showed no significant changes in UCR over time in either group. These findings support the metabolic safety of moderate to high-protein nutritional support in the ICU setting.

Additionally, although high-protein strategies could theoretically prolong ICU or hospital LOS due to increased survival among more critically ill patients, no significant differences in LOS were observed in our study. These findings are consistent with previous neutral trials, which reported no impact of protein dose on ICU or hospital LOS [1, 45]. Furthermore, no clinically meaningful changes in metabolic parameters were observed, indicating acceptable tolerance of the protein intervention. Nevertheless, tolerance profiles and the adequacy of energy and protein delivery remain critical considerations in enterally fed ICU patients. A recent randomized controlled trial evaluating a standardized BTF formula in ICU patients demonstrated improved gastrointestinal tolerance and enhanced nutritional delivery compared to a commercial formula [46]. Complementary nutritional strategies—including micronutrient-based interventions (e.g., folate and curcumin) [47, 48], sensory stimulation, and behaviorally influenced modifiers such as physical activity and sleep—have also demonstrated promise in improving nutritional tolerance, inflammatory profiles, and clinical recovery in vulnerable populations [49, 50]. Given the heterogeneous and dynamic metabolic responses in critical illness, future research should investigate individualized nutrition strategies that go beyond protein dosing alone, such as personalized feeding approaches informed by metabolic biomarkers, body composition analysis, or functional recovery outcomes.

Comparison with published trials

Recent large-scale randomized controlled trials—including PRECISE [25], EFFORT [44], and NUTRIREA-3 [26] —have challenged the assumption that higher protein provision leads to linear improvements in outcomes for critically ill patients. Emerging evidence from these studies suggests a potential U-shaped relationship between protein dose and clinical benefit, in which both inadequate (< 1.0 g/kg/day) and excessive (> 2.0 g/kg/day) protein intake may be associated with adverse outcomes. These include increased gastrointestinal intolerance, diminished functional recovery, and possible harm in vulnerable subgroups.

In contrast, the achieved protein intake in our study, 1.67 ± 0.33 g/kg/day, falls within the hypothesized optimal range. Notably, this moderate-protein strategy was well tolerated, with no significant elevations in hepatic or renal biomarkers and no increase in gastrointestinal complications or overall morbidity. These findings support the emerging perspective that moderate protein targets may offer the most favorable balance between anabolic efficacy and metabolic tolerance in ICU nutrition therapy.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. However, the protocol targeted a protein intake of 2.2 g/kg/day; real-world factors, including hemodynamic instability, gastrointestinal intolerance, and fluid restrictions, limited actual delivery to a mean of 1.67 ± 0.33 g/kg/day. Additionally, due to the unavailability of indirect calorimetry, energy requirements were estimated rather than directly measured. While computed tomography (CT) and ultrasound are the preferred modalities for assessing muscle mass in critically ill patients [51], we used MAC as a practical alternative. However, MAC is not fully validated in ICU populations and may be affected by fluid shifts and fat redistribution [52]. As such, conclusions regarding muscle preservation should be interpreted with caution. A further limitation of this study is the absence of a formal sample size calculation. Although an a priori power analysis based on 60-day mortality was initially considered, the projected sample size was not feasible within the ethical and logistical constraints of a single-center ICU trial. As a result, we adopted a pragmatic approach, determining the sample size based on anticipated patient flow and institutional capacity—an approach consistent with previous ICU nutrition trials that employed feasibility-based enrollment strategies [38]. Future research should explore more individualized approaches to protein dosing, assess feeding responsiveness, incorporate biomarker-guided strategies, and evaluate long-term outcomes such as quality of life and functional recovery.

Conclusions

Compared to conventional-protein nutritional support, high-protein delivery at 1.67 g/kg/day was associated with a significantly lower in-hospital mortality rate among critically ill ICU patients. A trend toward reduced 60-day mortality was also observed, although this did not reach statistical significance. Higher protein intake resulted in significantly less reduction in MAC, potentially indicating attenuated muscle wasting; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as MAC is an indirect and nonspecific marker of muscle mass. No significant differences were observed in ICU or 28-day mortality, length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation, or gastrointestinal tolerance. Given the single-center design of this trial, the generalizability of these findings is limited and should be confirmed in larger, multicenter studies.

Future studies should investigate optimal protein dosing strategies, including the timing of administration, patient-specific responsiveness to feeding, and personalized approaches guided by body composition or metabolic biomarkers. Additionally, research should prioritize long-term outcomes such as functional recovery and health-related quality of life.
