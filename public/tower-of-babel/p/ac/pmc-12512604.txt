Shaping tomorrow’s professionals: King Saud University’s transformative course on medical volunteerism, empowering students to develop proposals for impactful initiatives
Authors: Sulaiman A Alshammari, Nada A Alyousefi, Aljohara Almeneessier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVolunteer healthcare is crucial in medical education, promoting empathy, leadership, and civic responsibility. King Saud University’s Health Volunteer Project integrates volunteerism into coursework and helps students create structured proposals to address healthcare challenges.ObjectivesThe primary objective was to evaluate the effectiveness of the Health Volunteer Project by Medical Students in developing professional skills and addressing healthcare service gaps. The secondary objectives were to assess the program’s broader impact, identify areas for improvement, and explore its potential for replication in other educational and healthcare settings.MethodsThis initiative was embedded in the professionalism course and combined lectures, interactive group work, and poster presentations. Students were divided into groups of approximately ten members and asked to design health volunteer proposals using a standardized template provided through the university’s learning management system. Each proposal included objectives, target population, methods, anticipated outcomes, resources, and potential barriers. Proposals were summarized into posters, exhibited at an annual event, and evaluated using a structured scoring tool by faculty reviewers. Visitors (students and faculty) were invited to provide feedback through QR codes. This study followed a descriptive cross-sectional design. Target audiences expanded from the public to vulnerable groups, families, and healthcare students. Strategies progressed from educational campaigns to digital platforms, mobile applications, wearable technologies, and virtual platforms. Notable projects included colorectal cancer awareness, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) education, and AI-based health solutions.ConclusionIntegrating volunteerism into medical education fosters professional development while addressing societal health needs. Student-led proposals demonstrate adaptability, innovation, and alignment with Vision 2030 priorities. Introduction

Healthcare volunteering is vital to modern medical education, fostering empathy, leadership, and civic responsibility among healthcare professionals [1, 2]. Globally, it has addressed service gaps, particularly in disaster-stricken and underserved communities [3], complementing existing services and helping identify systemic gaps to enhance healthcare delivery [4]. Such initiatives improve access to essential care and foster healthier communities [5] while providing students opportunities to enhance clinical skills and bridge theory with practice through structured programs [6]. Defined as the provision of time, skills, and effort without financial reward, healthcare volunteerism enables students to engage in community service and public health activities that strengthen professional competencies and societal well-being [7]. Worldwide, medical schools have implemented volunteerism through service-learning programs, student-run clinics, disaster-response training, and health campaigns, offering experiential learning that complements formal curricula [7, 8].

Healthcare volunteerism, while related to social accountability and university social responsibility (USR), remains distinct. Social accountability refers to the obligation of medical schools to address priority community health concerns [9], whereas USR represents broader commitments to sustainability, knowledge-sharing, and societal partnership beyond healthcare [10]. Within this framework, volunteerism serves as a practical expression of USR, aligning institutional values with community needs. Universities enhance this role by integrating USR into policies that promote collaboration, sustainability, and knowledge sharing, strengthening reputation and stakeholder engagement [11, 12]. Evidence shows that USR fosters collaboration and entrepreneurial orientation, supporting sustainable knowledge sharing among students [13]. Strategies include embedding service-learning in curricula, building social capital, and fostering partnerships between institutions and communities [2].

Medical volunteerism in Saudi Arabia is a critical component of Vision 2030, which aims to mobilize one million volunteers by 2030 This alignment is evident in creating digital platforms, such as the National Volunteer Portal, which streamlines volunteer recruitment, training, and tracking. Programs such as Numuw (Growth) focus on skill development and prepare volunteers to address their immediate priorities and long-term societal needs [14, 15]. In Saudi Arabia, where healthcare systems are undergoing rapid transformation as part of Vision 2030, the integration of volunteerism into medical training has become increasingly significant [2, 16].

The Health Volunteer Project conducted by medical students at King Saud University represents a novel approach to embedding volunteerism within the educational framework of professionalism. Through structured courses that combine didactic lectures, interactive sessions, and hands-on proposal development, this initiative equips medical students with the skills to identify health challenges, design innovative solutions, and effectively engage with their communities. Over the past three years (2022–2024), the program has evolved, providing valuable insights into the potential of student-led volunteerism to impact healthcare services positively.

This study is significant in its ability to bridge the gap between academia and community services. By encouraging medical students to design proposals for volunteering projects, the Health Volunteer Project fosters experiential learning while simultaneously promoting health advocacy and community engagement. Previous studies have highlighted the dual benefits of such initiatives: students gain clinical and professional skills, while communities benefit from increased health awareness and services [17–19]. Building on these findings, our study evaluates three years of student-led proposals at King Saud University, identifying thematic trends, methodological innovations, and challenges to inform the future development of educationally embedded volunteer programs.

The findings can inform healthcare policies, enhance patient care, and inspire the development of sustainable volunteer-based health services in Saudi Arabia. This study contributes to the growing body of evidence that supports the integration of volunteerism into medical education and its role in advancing healthcare delivery in Saudi Arabia. The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of the Health Volunteer Project by Medical Students in fostering professional skills and addressing gaps in healthcare services through innovative, student-led initiatives.

Methods

The Health Volunteer Project by Medical Students is a structured initiative integrated into the professionalism course, emphasizing volunteerism as a core value, which is a credit-bearing course delivered to second-year medical students in the College of Medicine of King Saud University (KSU). The course is coordinated and supervised by the Medical Education Department, with teaching and mentorship provided by faculty members from all departments of the College of Medicine, representing different academic ranks (from Assistant Professor to Full Professor). The target learners are second-year medical students. The content is delivered through lectures. Faculty provide close guidance throughout the project, ensuring that students apply the concepts of professionalism in community engagement. Importantly, the Health Volunteer Project is an assessed component of the Professionalism course.

Study design

This study followed a descriptive cross-sectional design, analyzing health volunteer project proposals developed by undergraduate second year medical students. The design was chosen to allow systematic documentation and comparison of proposals over three consecutive years (2022–2024).

Enrollment and participants

Participation was compulsory for all second-year medical students enrolled in the professionalism course at King Saud University. As such, no sampling was applied—all enrolled students (n = 832 across three years) contributed to group proposals, resulting in the inclusion of all 88 proposals submitted during the study period. This approach avoided selection bias in participation.

Group formation and bias control

Students were allowed to self-select groups of approximately ten members, a practice that could introduce self-selection bias. To mitigate potential imbalances (e.g., uneven distribution of skills or reduced inclusivity), course instructors and supervisors monitored group formation. They also reviewed group dynamics during the proposal process to encourage equitable contribution from all members.

Proposal development

Each group developed its proposal using a standardized template, structured around nine evidence-informed components: title, background, objectives, target population, methods/design, resources, outcomes, obstacles, and supervisor. These elements reflect best practices in service-learning and student project-based education, which emphasize structured planning, measurable outcomes, and reflective learning [20, 21].

Poster preparation and evaluation

Following proposal submission, groups summarized their projects into posters presented at the annual exhibition. Instructions for poster production and event details were communicated to the students in advance. In December, the posters are exhibited during an event held under the auspices of the dean of the College of Medicine, coinciding with the celebration of World Volunteering Day on the fifth of December or around that date annually.

Evaluators

Only faculty members from the Department of Family and Community Medicine and related disciplines were eligible to serve as evaluators. The course is coordinated and supervised by the Medical Education Department, with teaching and mentorship provided by faculty members from all departments of the College of Medicine, representing different academic ranks (from Assistant Professor to Full Professor). Importantly, the Health Volunteer Project is an assessed component of the Professionalism course. which is a credit-bearing course delivered to second-year medical students in the College of Medicine of King Saud University (KSU).

Evaluation criteria

Each proposal was independently assessed by two faculty evaluators using an online structured scoring rubric. It was developed using Microsoft Office 365 Forms (King Saud University licensed platform) to ensure that all data were collected and stored within the institution’s secure system. Access was restricted to the research team only. The form was designed to collect responses electronically in a secure manner. Visitors (students and faculty) were invited to provide feedback through QR codes, but only faculty evaluations contributed to official scoring. Visitors included students and faculty members from all departments of the College of Medicine, representing different academic levels. They were invited to observe the event, share their insights, and provide constructive feedback to support participants’ professional development. While their input was valued as a means of enriching the experience, their scores were not incorporated into the final evaluations. Scores from all evaluations were aggregated, and the three highest-scoring proposals received the Best Volunteering Project Proposal award.

Scoring rubric development

The scoring rubric was developed collaboratively by the course committee, comprising senior faculty from the Department of Family and Community Medicine. The rubric was piloted during the first year (2022) and refined for clarity and reliability in subsequent years. Assessment domains included:

Data collection and analysis

All proposals submitted between 2022 and 2024 were collected in electronic form through the King Saud university’s Blackboard platform. Data from the proposals were extracted and coded based on themes, target populations, methodologies, outcomes, and challenges. Descriptive statistical analysis was applied to quantify distributions across years, while qualitative thematic analysis was conducted to identify key trends.

Discussion

This study analyzed 88 health-volunteer proposals submitted by medical students between 2022 and 2024, revealing the dynamic evolution of their creativity and responsiveness in addressing public health challenges. Over the three years, proposals shifted from traditional awareness and preventive care initiatives toward technology-driven solutions and targeted community engagement. This reflects students’ adaptability to changing societal needs and aligns with global evidence that volunteerism strengthens empathy, professional growth, and resilience among healthcare professionals [6, 17, 22]. Integrating volunteerism into medical curricula, as in this initiative, highlights its dual role in fostering civic responsibility and enhancing professional competencies [12, 13, 23].

Motivation and engagement

Sustained student participation in volunteerism is shaped by motivation. The Volunteer Functions Inventory identifies altruistic values and opportunities for learning as the strongest drivers [24], consistent with regional studies that emphasize cultural values, national duty, and professional aspirations as key determinants [12, 25, 26]. However, barriers remain. Safety concerns [25, 27], family obligations [26], and logistical difficulties [27] have been reported as deterrents. Embedding recognition, mentorship, and alignment with national health priorities into curricular design can strengthen engagement. Moreover, volunteering provides well-documented individual health benefits—including stress reduction, improved mental health, healthier aging, and reduced mortality risk [28, 29] further justifying its institutionalization in medical training.

Expansion of themes and methodologies

The proposals demonstrate a clear thematic progression. In 2022, students focused on noncommunicable disease (NCD) awareness, mental health, lifestyle modification, and first aid. By 2023, emphasis expanded to smoking cessation, blood donation, preventive health, and technology integration. In 2024, innovative projects such as AI-powered speech therapy tools and wearable safety devices highlighted a decisive move toward technology-enabled volunteerism [30, 31]. These innovations mirror global digital health trends [32, 33] and resonate with Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 priorities [16].

Methodologies also evolved. Early projects relied on traditional campaigns, workshops, and health screenings, while later initiatives integrated apps, wearable sensors, and virtual platforms [32, 34–36]. Outcomes consistently aimed to improve awareness and behavior, but later proposals emphasized patient outcomes, sustainable practices, and adoption of digital health tools. Nonetheless, persistent challenges—including funding constraints, cultural sensitivities, and technical barriers—were repeatedly cited, underscoring the importance of institutional and external support [37–39].

Target audiences and equity

The scope of target audiences broadened substantially over time. In 2022, initiatives were directed mainly at the general public, children, the elderly, and healthcare workers. By 2023, students engaged adolescents, patients, and families. In 2024, attention shifted to vulnerable groups and healthcare students, reflecting growing sensitivity to equity and healthcare access disparities [40, 41]. This evolution aligns with international calls for medical education to emphasize inclusivity, equity, and community resilience through structured volunteerism [14, 15, 42].

Institutional and policy implications

The ingenuity and diversity of proposals provide insights for policymakers and educators. Expanding exhibitions to public venues, such as malls and parks, could amplify visibility and engagement. Involving diverse evaluators—including healthcare professionals, NGOs, and the public—would enhance feedback and sustainability [41, 43]. Regular scientific conferences dedicated to medical student volunteerism could further facilitate cross-institutional collaboration. Partnerships with established structures such as the Saudi Red Crescent Authority [44], the Ministry of Health Volunteer Program [15], and global humanitarian actors such as KSRelief [45, 46] could provide essential infrastructure and resources. Tailoring programs to consider cultural sensitivities, gender dynamics, and disaster-response readiness would further enhance effectiveness [47–49].

National and regional context

Volunteerism in Saudi Arabia is strongly influenced by religious, cultural, and professional values, with national pride and altruism cited as major motivators [50, 51]. Studies from various universities report willingness rates among medical students ranging from 23.6 to 84.1%, reflecting both enthusiasm and variability depending on context. Yet, barriers such as training gaps, financial limitations, and safety concerns persist [23, 25, 26, 47, 52]. Addressing these requires targeted training programs, financial support, safety protocols, and regional volunteer databases, which would ensure greater preparedness and sustained engagement.

Broader significance

Embedding structured volunteerism into medical curricula has benefits beyond individual student development. It contributes to workforce sustainability by fostering innovation, adaptability, and civic responsibility [53]. Institutions that support structured volunteerism can enhance healthcare system resilience, particularly in the face of emerging challenges. Taken together, this study and the broader literature indicate that medical student volunteerism—particularly when linked with digital innovation and supported by institutional and policy frameworks—represents a powerful mechanism to advance medical education, promote healthcare equity, and strengthen public health outcomes in Saudi Arabia and globally [6].

Limitations and future directions

This study is limited by its single-institution scope, which restricts generalizability, and by its focus on proposal content rather than real-world implementation or long-term outcomes. Subjective evaluations by faculty and feedback by visitors may have introduced bias despite the use of structured rubrics, and the self-selection of student groups could have affected project performance. The three-year timeframe and limited access to detailed proposal content further constrain the depth of analysis. Despite these limitations, the study offers novel insights into integrating structured volunteerism into medical education, demonstrating its potential to foster professional skills, enhance community engagement, and address healthcare service gaps. Future research should validate these findings across institutions, use standardized evaluation frameworks, and assess longitudinal impacts to strengthen the evidence base for scalable, evidence-informed volunteerism models in medical training.

Conclusion

This study highlights the evolving role of healthcare volunteerism in medical education, with student-led initiatives shifting from traditional awareness campaigns to technology-driven, equity-focused projects aligned with global healthcare transformation and Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030. Structured volunteerism fosters empathy, teamwork, and leadership while addressing health service gaps, particularly for vulnerable populations. Despite limitations of a single-institution scope and proposal-based analysis, the findings offer actionable insights for scaling volunteerism through cross-institutional frameworks, disaster-response training, and partnerships with NGOs and digital health platforms. Embedding such models can prepare socially accountable healthcare professionals and strengthen community health systems.
