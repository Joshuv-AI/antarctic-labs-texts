Neu1 deficiency is associated with reduced nicotine responsiveness and dopamine D3 receptor downregulation in zebrafish
Authors: Sumomo Tsuji, Haruka Ego, Ryota Shimizu, Yuko Kamada-Futagami, Toshiki Hyodo, Masaharu Komatsu, Kazuhiro Shiozaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nicotine induces pronounced neural and behavioral activation through dopaminergic signaling. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms that regulate nicotine sensitivity remain poorly understood. Here, we investigated the roles of lysosomal sialidase Neu1 and neural glycan polysialic acid (PSA) in nicotine-induced responses in zebrafish, a vertebrate model. Western blot analyses revealed reduced PSA levels and significantly upregulated neu1 expression in the zebrafish brain following acute nicotine exposure. Behavioral assays revealed increased tolerance to nicotine-induced lethality and attenuated nicotine-evoked swimming excitation in neu1-knockout (neu1-KO) zebrafish compared with those in wild-type fish. Nicotine-induced neuronal activation, assessed based on c-Fos expression, was markedly reduced in neu1-KO zebrafish. Gene expression analyses revealed altered dopaminergic signaling, including reduced drd3 expression, in neu1-KO zebrafish. Pharmacological experiments demonstrated that blocking dopamine D2/D3 receptors suppressed nicotine-induced swimming excitation in wild-type zebrafish, whereas treatment with a selective dopamine D3 receptor agonist partially rescued the attenuated nicotine responsiveness observed in neu1-KO zebrafish. Nicotine-induced c-Fos activation occurred predominantly in PSA-positive neurons within the hypothalamus, a brain region implicated in dopaminergic signaling. These findings suggest that Neu1-mediated PSA degradation may modulate dopamine D3 receptor-dependent nicotine sensitivity. We propose a conceptual model wherein nicotine-induced Neu1 activation may contribute to reduced PSA levels and altered dopamine D3 receptor-related signaling, thereby influencing the threshold for nicotine-induced neural and behavioral activation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10695-026-01735-8.

Introduction

Fish behavior is an integrated indicator of physiological state and environmental responsiveness. It is closely linked to survival strategies, such as growth, feeding, avoidance, and social interactions. Accordingly, behavioral analysis has been widely employed in fish physiology as a non-invasive and effective approach to evaluate changes in internal physiological conditions. As behavior emerges from the integration of multiple physiological systems, including metabolism, immunity, and sensory responsiveness, it is particularly useful for assessing whole-organism responses to genetic and environmental perturbations. Behavioral responses to chemical substances present in aquatic environments are considered important physiological indicators of chemical sensitivity and overall health status of fish. Such responses have been extensively used in toxicological, ecological, and aquaculture-related studies as a sensitive readout of fish conditions.

Sialoglycans are glycoconjugates of sialic acids located at non-reducing ends. Sialoglycans are fundamental molecular components present in various fish tissues that play essential roles in maintaining cell-surface environment and regulating the responsiveness to external stimuli (Jin et al. 2015; Yamakawa et al. 2018). Through their structural diversity, sialoglycans contribute to cell–cell interactions, molecular recognition, and modulation of receptor accessibility at the cell surface. Among neural glycans, polysialic acid (PSA)—a linear polymer of α2,8-linked sialic acid residues primarily conjugated to the neural cell adhesion molecule (NCAM)—is known to play a critical role in regulating neuronal plasticity, cell–cell interactions, and neurotransmitter responsiveness in the nervous system (Acheson et al. 1991; Hayakawa et al. 2023). Alterations in PSA levels impact synaptic function and neural excitability, highlighting its importance in neural physiology (Muller et al. 1996; Bonfanti and Theodosis 2009).

Sialidases are glycan-degrading enzymes that cleave sialic acid residues from the non-reducing termini of sialoglycans. Four sialidases (Neu1, Neu2, Neu3, and Neu4) have been identified in vertebrates, each differing in their subcellular localization and substrate specificity (Shiozaki et al. 2019). Neu1 is primarily localized in the lysosomes and is involved in the degradation of glycoproteins and oligosaccharides (Shiozaki et al. 2019). It can be translocated to the cell surface via lysosomal exocytosis, where it contributes to the desialylation of extracellular glycoconjugates, including PSA (Uemura et al. 2009; Annunziata et al. 2013; Tsuji et al. 2025). Through these intracellular and extracellular activities, Neu1 is believed to impact tissue-level physiological functions rather than acting solely as a lysosomal degradative enzyme. In fish, Neu1 has been identified and characterized in multiple species, indicating a high degree of evolutionary conservation (Manzoni et al. 2007; Ryuzono et al. 2016; Honda et al. 2020). This conservation suggests that Neu1 fulfills fundamental physiological roles that have been maintained during vertebrate evolution.

In an earlier study, we generated Neu1-deficient zebrafish using genome-editing technology and demonstrated that the loss of Neu1 leads to distinct alterations in physiological and behavioral traits at an individual level (Okada et al. 2020). Moreover, in neu1-knockout (neu1-KO) zebrafish, Neu1 deficiency alters the composition of complex glycoconjugates in multiple tissues, as well as affecting diverse aspects of fish physiology. These changes provide a valuable in vivo model for examining how the disruption of glycan metabolism affects organism-level phenotypes. Notably, neu1-KO zebrafish exhibits reduced susceptibility to infection by pathogenic bacteria, suggesting altered host–pathogen interactions and immune-related physiological responses (Sahashi et al. 2022; Ishii et al. 2025). In addition, neu1-KO zebrafish display enhanced exploratory interest in heterospecific fish, which is associated with increased dopaminergic activation mediated by dopamine D1 receptors (Ikeda et al. 2021; Tsuji et al. 2025). These findings suggest that Neu1 deficiency broadly affects dopaminergic signaling and motivational- or stimulus-driven behavioral outputs. Furthermore, the glial cell activation observed in the brains of neu1-KO zebrafish indicates changes in their neural and physiological microenvironments (Ikeda et al. 2022). Taken together, these observations indicate that Neu1 plays a broad role in maintaining physiological homeostasis and behavioral regulation in fish.

Despite these advances, it remains unclear how Neu1 deficiency-induced alterations in sialoglycan metabolism impact behavioral responsiveness to external chemical substances. In aquatic ecosystems, fish are continuously exposed to a wide variety of natural and anthropogenic chemicals, and their ability to respond appropriately to these substances is essential for maintaining normal physiological functions. Low-molecular-weight compounds present in aquatic environments can be absorbed through the gills and body surface, leading to changes in locomotor activity and behavioral patterns (Erickson et al. 2006; Dutra Costa et al. 2020). As such, behavioral changes reflect physiological conditions, stimulus sensitivity, and overall health status. Chemical exposure assays based on behavioral responses are widely regarded as important evaluation systems in fish physiology and environmental biology. Therefore, assessing the chemical sensitivity of genetically modified fish provides insights into how molecular-level alterations in glycan metabolism translate into organism-level physiological outcomes.

Nicotine (Nco) is a plant-derived, low-molecular-weight alkaloid to which fish may be exposed in aquatic environments. In addition to their natural occurrence, Nco and Nco-related compounds can enter aquatic systems through agricultural runoff and anthropogenic activities (Espíndola et al. 2024). Previous studies have reported that Nco exposure induces dose-dependent changes in swimming activity and exploratory behavior in fish; therefore, Nco has been widely used as a standard test substance for evaluating chemical sensitivity and behavioral responsiveness (Singer et al. 2016; Wronikowska et al. 2020). Notably, Nco elicits reproducible and quantifiable behavioral responses over a wide concentration range, making it suitable for comparative analyses of different genetic backgrounds. In particular, zebrafish are well-suited for such analyses because Nco can be readily administered via waterborne exposure, and behavioral changes can be quantitatively assessed, making this species a widely used model in fish behavioral physiology.

In the present study, we investigated behavioral responsiveness to waterborne chemical stimuli in Neu1-deficient zebrafish. Behavioral analysis was employed as an indicator reflecting the integrated physiological state, and Nco was used as a representative chemical stimulus to compare the chemical sensitivity and behavioral responses of Neu1-deficient and wild-type (WT) fish. By focusing on behavioral outputs, this approach enables the evaluation of whole-organism physiological responses without relying on invasive measurements. This study aimed to clarify how Neu1 deficiency and its consequent alterations in PSA dynamics impact neural signaling and behavioral responses to environmental chemical stimuli.

Materials and methods

Fish

The Neu1-deficient (neu1-KO) zebrafish were generated in our previous study using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing of the RIKEN WT (RW) strain, in which a five-nucleotide deletion was introduced into the neu1 gene (Okada et al. 2020). The RW strain was maintained and used as the WT control throughout the experiments. The neu1-KO zebrafish line was maintained by repeated backcrossing with the RIKEN WT strain to minimize genetic background differences between WT and KO fish. Fish were housed in 2-L tanks under controlled laboratory conditions with a 14-h light/10-h dark photoperiod and were fed a commercial diet (Otohime B2; Marubeni Nissin Feed Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) twice daily. Experiments were performed using young adult zebrafish aged 5 months, including both males and females. All experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Animal Experiment Committee of Kagoshima University and were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines and relevant regulations.

Analysis of changes in swimming behavior following Nco exposure

Swimming behavior under Nco exposure was evaluated using a custom-designed transparent acrylic tank for side-view behavioral recordings. Behavioral assays were conducted in a tank with an asymmetric trapezoidal cross-section, in which one lateral wall was inclined, whereas the opposite wall remained vertical. The tank measured 28.5 cm in length, 14.5 cm in height, and 7.5 cm in depth (front to back). Its horizontal width differed between its upper and lower edges, measuring 28.5 cm and 24 cm at the top and bottom, respectively, owing to its trapezoidal geometry. For the spatial analysis of swimming behavior, the water column was divided into three equal vertical zones, and the uppermost zone was defined as the “upper layer.” Individual zebrafish were gently transferred to the experimental tank filled with water and allowed to acclimate briefly before exposure. Nco was added directly to the experimental tank to obtain final concentrations of 0, 2.5, 7.5, or 10 mg/L. These concentrations were determined based on previous reports and empirical observations of zebrafish responses (Ponzoni et al. 2021). The fish were continuously exposed to Nco throughout the behavioral recording period (10 min). Each fish was tested only once and not reused in subsequent experiments.

Swimming behavior was recorded from the lateral side of the tank using a digital video camera (HDR-CX430, Sony, Tokyo, Japan) positioned perpendicular to the long axis of the experimental tank. Video recording was initiated immediately after the fish were transferred to the tank. Behavioral parameters, including total swimming distance, swimming distance in the upper layer, time spent in the upper layer, and duration of excited swimming behavior, were quantified from the recorded videos. In the present study, Nco-induced behavioral responsiveness was primarily evaluated based on two characteristic behavioral responses: increased upper-layer swimming and excited swimming behavior. Upper-layer swimming was assessed by swimming distance and time spent in the upper layer. In zebrafish novel tank behavioral assays, increased swimming activity in the upper layer is commonly observed following nicotine exposure and is generally associated with reduced anxiety-like behavior and heightened behavioral arousal. Excited swimming behavior was operationally defined as a stereotyped swimming pattern observed at the water surface, in which fish oriented their head upward and repeatedly opened and closed their mouth while swimming. The spatial distribution of their swimming activity was visualized using heatmap representations generated from individual swimming trajectories.

Swimming trajectories and behavioral parameters were analyzed using behavioral tracking ANY-maze software (ANY-maze, IL, USA). Survival following Nco exposure was monitored, and survival rates were calculated for each Nco concentration and genotype.

To evaluate the effects of pharmacological agents on zebrafish behavior, haloperidol (a D2/D3 receptor antagonist), risperidone (a D2/D3 receptor antagonist), and PD128907 (a D3 receptor agonist) were used. These reagents were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and haloperidol (2.5 µM), risperidone (300 µg/L), or PD128907 (10 µM) was added to the water. The exposure conditions were determined based on previous studies (Geng et al. 2023; Tsuji et al. 2025). The final concentration of DMSO was 0.001% in waterborne exposure experiments. Corresponding vehicle controls containing the same concentration of DMSO were included in each experiment. Drug exposure was conducted in a separate tank measuring 10 cm × 10 cm, with a water depth of 5 cm. The fish were exposed to each pharmacological agent for 30 min under these conditions. After 30 min of exposure, the fish were transferred to the behavioral test tank and their behavior was recorded as described above.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction analyses

Real-time PCR analysis was performed as previously described (Tsuji et al. 2025). Total RNA was isolated from zebrafish brain tissue using Sepasol-RNA I Super G (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was subsequently reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (TOYOBO, Tokyo, Japan) to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. Real-time PCR analysis was performed using a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Amplification reactions were performed using KOD SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO). Gene-specific primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The expression levels of the target genes were normalized to those of elongation factor 1α (ef1α) or actb mRNA, which was used as an internal reference to correct for variations in RNA quantity and reverse transcription efficiency between the samples. PCR specificity was confirmed using melting curve analysis at the end of each run. Reactions performed without the cDNA template were included as negative controls to verify the absence of nonspecific amplification or contamination.

Immunohistochemical analyses

Immunohistological analysis was performed as described previously (Tsuji et al. 2025). For histological examination, zebrafish brains were collected and fixed in 4.0% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. After fixation, tissues were cryoprotected by sequential incubation in 10, 20, and 30% sucrose solutions prepared in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.02% sodium azide. The brains were then embedded in 5.0% agarose (Agarose XP; NIPPON GENE, Tokyo, Japan), rapidly frozen using a dry ice/hexane bath (− 60 °C), and sectioned at a thickness of 20 µm using a CryoStar NX50 cryostat (PHC, Tokyo, Japan). Frozen sections were mounted onto glass slides and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval by microwave treatment for 1 min in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The autofluorescence was subsequently reduced using an illuminator system (TiYO; NEPPAGENE, Chiba, Japan). After blocking with goat serum, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-Fos monoclonal antibody (1:50, E-8; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA), anti-TH polyclonal antibody (1:100 diluted, GTX113016, GeneTex), and anti-PSA monoclonal antibodies (1:200; clone 2-2B, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; 1.0 µg/mL). Following primary antibody incubation, the sections were treated with appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies. Immunostained sections were visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Apotome; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany, and BZ-X1000; KEYENCE, Osaka, Japan). Fluorescence signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, MD, USA).

Lectin and Western blot analyses

Lectin and Western blotting were performed as previously described (Ikeda et al. 2021). Zebrafish were anesthetized by immersion in ice-cold water, after which their brains were rapidly excised. Brain tissues were homogenized in 300 µL of PBS containing 1.0% Triton X-100, supplemented with leupeptin (10 µg/mL), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (1.0 mM), and phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (1.0 mM). Homogenization was performed using BioMasher (Nippi, Tokyo, Japan). Homogenates were clarified by centrifugation at 13,200 × g for 15 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected as protein lysates. Protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 1.0% bovine serum albumin before probing. For lectin blot analysis, membranes were incubated with biotinylated lectin from Maackia amurensis (MAM, 1 µg/mL, J-CHEMICAL, Tokyo, Japan), which recognizes α2,3-linked sialic acid, and biotinylated lectin from Sambucus sieboldiana (SSA, 1 µg/mL, J-CHEMICAL), which recognizes α2,6-linked sialic acid. Lectin binding was visualized using horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin.

For immunoblotting, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against PSA (1:500, monoclonal antibody, clone 735; Abnova, Taipei, Taiwan) and β-Actin (1:500, monoclonal antibody, 66,009–1-g; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA). Following incubation with the primary antibody, the membranes were treated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using the ChemiDoc Touch Plus imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) in combination with EzWestLumi Plus chemiluminescence reagent (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analyses

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, values were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test.

Results

Changes in sialoglycoproteins in the zebrafish brain following Nco exposure

To investigate the effects of Nco exposure on the composition of sialylated glycoproteins in the zebrafish brain, zebrafish were exposed to Nco (2.5 mg/L) for 15 min, after which their brains were excised and analyzed by lectin blotting. The compositions of α2,3-linked sialoglycoproteins, detected using MAM lectin, and α2,6-linked sialoglycoproteins, detected using SSA lectin, were not altered by Nco exposure (Fig. 1A, B). Next, changes in PSA-containing proteins were assessed by western blotting using an anti-PSA antibody. Nco exposure significantly reduced PSA levels in the brains of zebrafish (p < 0.05; Fig. 1C).

In the zebrafish brain, PSA is cleaved by Neu1 sialidase (Tsuji et al. 2025). Therefore, we examined the expression of the neu1 gene following Nco exposure. Nco exposure significantly increased neu1 mRNA expression (p < 0.05; Fig. 1D). In contrast, the mRNA levels of the PSA-synthesizing enzyme St8sia2 were unchanged following Nco treatment, whereas the expression of another PSA-synthesizing enzyme, St8sia4, was significantly decreased (p < 0.05; Fig. 1E). Moreover, the expression of genes encoding the core polysialylated protein NCAM, including ncam1a and ncam1b, remained unchanged (Fig. 1E). Taken together, these results suggest that Nco exposure induces a reduction in PSA levels in the zebrafish brain, which may be driven by increased PSA degradation via Neu1 upregulation and reduced PSA synthesis via St8sia4 downregulation.

Effects of Nco exposure on swimming behavior in neu1-KO zebrafish

Because Nco exposure altered neu1 expression and PSA levels, Nco responsiveness was further examined in neu1-KO zebrafish (Fig. 2A). WT and neu1-KO zebrafish were exposed to Nco at the concentrations of 0, 2.5, 7.5, and 10 mg/L, and the survival rates during the 10-min behavioral recording period are shown in Fig. 2B. The WT zebrafish exhibited a marked decrease in survival at higher Nco concentrations, with complete mortality observed at 7.5 and 10 mg/L. In contrast, neu1-KO zebrafish displayed a higher tolerance to Nco, maintaining 100% survival at 7.5 mg/L and approximately 57% survival at 10 mg/L. These results indicate that Neu1 deficiency reduces the susceptibility to Nco-induced lethality.

Swimming behavior was subsequently analyzed using side-view recordings. Nco responsiveness was evaluated primarily by two characteristic behavioral responses, namely increased upper-layer swimming and excited swimming behavior. The representative heatmaps of the swimming trajectories are shown in Fig. 2C. Under control conditions (0 mg/L Nco), both the WT and neu1-KO zebrafish predominantly occupied the lower region of the tank. Upon exposure to 2.5 mg/L Nco, WT zebrafish exhibited increased swimming activity in the upper region, whereas neu1-KO zebrafish remained largely confined to the lower region. At higher Nco concentrations (7.5 and 10 mg/L), WT zebrafish did not display measurable swimming trajectories owing to Nco-induced mortality. In contrast, neu1-KO zebrafish remained viable and exhibited a concentration-dependent increase in swimming activity in the upper layer. The results of the quantitative analysis of the total swimming distance are presented in Fig. 2D. In WT zebrafish, the total swimming distance modestly decreased at higher Nco concentrations. neu1-KO zebrafish exhibited a significant reduction in their total swimming distance at 10 mg/L Nco concentration compared with that in the non-Nco-treated fish (p < 0.05) (F = 4.139, p = 0.02, one-way ANOVA). Swimming distance in the upper layer is shown in Fig. 2E. WT zebrafish exposed to 2.5 mg/L Nco concentration demonstrated a significant increase in the upper-layer swimming distance compared with that in the non-Nco-treated fish (p < 0.01) (F = 9.843, p = 0.0005, one-way ANOVA). This increase in upper-layer swimming distance may reflect Nco-induced excitatory behavior and/or reduced anxiety-like behavior. In contrast, neu1-KO zebrafish did not exhibit a comparable increase at this concentration, indicating an attenuated responsiveness to Nco. At 10 mg/L Nco concentration, neu1-KO zebrafish exhibited an increase in the upper-layer swimming distance. Consistent with these findings, the time spent swimming in the upper layer was significantly increased in WT zebrafish at 2.5 mg/L Nco concentration compared with that in the non-Nco-treated fish (p < 0.01) (F = 18.038, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA), whereas neu1-KO zebrafish exhibited a blunted response at lower concentrations (Fig. 2F). At 10 mg/L Nco concentration, neu1-KO zebrafish displayed prolonged swimming times in the upper layer (p < 0.01; Fig. 2F). The duration of the excited swimming behavior is shown in Fig. 2G. WT zebrafish exhibited a pronounced increase in excited swimming at 2.5 mg/L Nco concentration (p < 0.01) (F = 21.489, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA), whereas neu1-KO zebrafish showed minimal excited swimming at this concentration. At 10 mg/L Nco concentration, neu1-KO zebrafish displayed a significant increase in excited swimming duration (p < 0.01; Fig. 2G), indicating a shift in the concentration threshold required to elicit Nco-induced behavioral activation. No apparent abnormalities in swimming posture, buoyancy control, or swim bladder function were observed in surviving neu1-KO zebrafish following nicotine exposure, and behavioral tracking could be performed normally throughout the observation period.

Nco-induced neuronal activation assessment based on c-Fos expression

To confirm neuronal activation in response to Nco exposure, the expression of c-Fos, an immediate-early gene widely used as a marker of neuronal activity (Hoffman et al. 1993), was examined in the brains of WT and neu1-KO zebrafish (Fig. 3A). WT and neu1-KO zebrafish were exposed to Nco (0 or 2.5 mg/L) for 15 min before brain collection. Under basal conditions without Nco stimulation, WT and neu1-KO zebrafish exhibited comparable c-Fos signal intensities and distributions, with low-level expression observed across major brain regions, including the telencephalon, olfactory bulb, diencephalon, hypothalamus, optic tectum, cerebellum, and medulla (Fig. 3B, C). No apparent differences in signal intensity or spatial distribution were detected between genotypes. Following Nco exposure (2.5 mg/L), WT zebrafish showed robust induction of c-Fos expression throughout the brain (Fig. 3D). In contrast, neu1-KO zebrafish exhibited markedly attenuated c-Fos expression compared with that in the Nco-treated WT zebrafish, with reduced signal intensity and a more restricted spatial distribution (Fig. 3E). Quantification of fluorescence signal intensity revealed that Nco treatment significantly increased c-Fos immunoreactivity in WT zebrafish (p < 0.01) (hypothalamus: F = 87.371, p < 0.0001; telencephalon: F = 14.096, p = 0.0015; diencephalon: F = 370.099, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA), whereas no significant increase was observed in neu1-KO zebrafish (Fig. 3F–H). Consistent with these findings, real-time PCR analysis demonstrated that c-fos mRNA expression was significantly elevated following Nco exposure in WT zebrafish but not in neu1-KO zebrafish (p < 0.01) (F = 32.723, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA; Fig. 3I). These results indicate that basal neuronal activity is comparable between WT and neu1-KO zebrafish, whereas Nco-induced neuronal activation is significantly lower in neu1-KO fish than in WT fish, which is consistent with their reduced behavioral sensitivity to Nco.

Nco-induced c-Fos activation in PSA-positive neurons

To further investigate the relationship between Nco exposure and PSA levels, we examined the distribution of PSA in the brains of WT and neu1-KO zebrafish. Consistent with our previous findings (Tsuji et al. 2025), PSA immunoreactivity in the hypothalamus was significantly higher in neu1-KO zebrafish than in WT fish (p < 0.05; Fig. 4A). Considering that Nco exposure reduced PSA levels, as shown in Fig. 1C, PSA immunostaining was performed on the brains of Nco-exposed zebrafish (2.5 mg/L, 15 min). Colocalization analysis revealed that c-Fos signals overlapped with those of PSA-positive neurons in the hypothalamus (Fig. 4B). These findings indicate that Nco-induced c-Fos activation occurred predominantly within PSA-positive neuronal populations in the hypothalamus and suggest that altered PSA dynamics may contribute to the reduced Nco sensitivity observed in neu1-KO zebrafish.

Expression of nicotinic acetylcholine receptor and dopamine receptor genes following Nco

To determine whether altered Nco sensitivity in neu1-KO zebrafish is associated with changes in receptor gene expression, the mRNA levels of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) and dopamine receptors were examined. The basal expression levels of the nAChR subunit genes (chrna4b, chrna6, and chrna7a) did not differ significantly between WT and neu1-KO zebrafish (Fig. 5A), indicating that Neu1 deficiency did not affect baseline nAChR expression.

Next, we focused on the dopaminergic system, considering its established involvement in Nco-induced excitation and the known role of PSA in regulating dopaminergic signaling. Analysis of the dopamine receptor gene expression revealed genotype-dependent differences (Fig. 5B). Although the expression of drd1a, drd1b, drd2b, drd4a, drd4b, and drd5 remained unchanged, drd2a expression was slightly but significantly reduced, and drd3 expression was markedly reduced in neu1-KO zebrafish compared with WT (p < 0.01; Fig. 5B).

The effects of Nco exposure on dopamine receptor expression were examined in WT zebrafish to identify drd2a and drd3 dopamine receptor genes responsive to Nco stimulation (Fig. 5C). Nco treatment (2.5 mg/L, 15 min) significantly increased drd2a and drd3 mRNA levels (p < 0.05), indicating that drd2a and drd3 are Nco-responsive genes in zebrafish. Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunoreactivity labels the dopaminergic neurons in the hypothalamus (Tsuji et al. 2025). When the hypothalamus of Nco-stimulated WT zebrafish was immunostained, a partial overlap between c-Fos-positive cells and TH signals was observed (Fig. 5D). Additionally, TH-positive signals were frequently detected in proximity to c-Fos-positive cells. These findings raised the possibility that altered drd2a and, in particular, drd3 expression may be involved in the altered Nco responsiveness observed in neu1-KO zebrafish.

Effects of D2/D3 receptor antagonism on Nco-induced behavior in WT zebrafish

To further evaluate the contribution of dopamine receptor subtypes to Nco-induced behavioral responses, WT zebrafish were treated with haloperidol and risperidone, dopamine D2/D3 receptor antagonists with higher affinity for D2 receptors (Tateno et al. 2018), and their responses to Nco exposure (2.5 mg/L) were analyzed. Pretreatment with haloperidol did not significantly affect the Nco-treated total swimming distance, swimming distance in the upper layer, or time spent in the upper layer compared with Nco alone in WT and neu1-KO zebrafish (Fig. 6A–D). In contrast, the duration of excited swimming behavior was significantly reduced by haloperidol co-treatment compared with Nco treatment alone in WT zebrafish (p < 0.01) (F = 32.649, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA; Fig. 6E), but not in neu1-KO zebrafish. Risperidone treatment produced similar suppressive effects on excited swimming behavior only in WT zebrafish (p < 0.01) (F = 728.126, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA; Fig. 6J), whereas swimming distance in the upper layer and time spent in the upper layer remained unchanged (Fig. 6F–I). These results indicate that D2/D3 receptor antagonism reduces behavioral sensitivity to Nco, as evidenced by the attenuation of Nco-induced excited swimming behavior, without markedly affecting overall locomotor activity or vertical positioning.

Effects of dopamine D3 receptor activation on nicotine responsiveness in neu1-KO zebrafish

Based on these findings, we investigated the involvement of dopamine D3 receptors in the reduced Nco sensitivity of neu1-KO zebrafish. To determine whether diminished D3 receptor signaling contributes to the attenuated Nco responsiveness, the effects of the selective D3 receptor agonist PD128907 on Nco-treated WT and neu1-KO zebrafish (2.5 mg/L) were examined (Fig. 7A). Treatment with PD128907 did not significantly affect total swimming distance (Fig. 7B). In contrast, both the swimming distance (p < 0.01) (F = 47.742, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA; Fig. 7C) and time spent (p < 0.01) (F = 84.054, p < 0.0001, one-way ANOVA; Fig. 7D) in the upper layer increased in neu1-KO zebrafish treated with Nco plus PD128907 compared with those in zebrafish treated with Nco alone. PD128907 treatment did not significantly affect the behavioral responses of WT zebrafish. Excited swimming behavior was also analyzed; however, PD128907 treatment did not significantly increase the duration of excited swimming behavior in WT and neu1-KO zebrafish under the present experimental conditions (Fig. 7E). These findings suggest that activation of dopamine D3 receptors partially rescues the attenuated Nco-induced behavioral responses observed in neu1-KO zebrafish, supporting the involvement of dopamine D3 receptor-related signaling in the altered behavioral responsiveness to Nco.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the roles of PSA and lysosomal sialidase Neu1 in regulating Nco sensitivity in zebrafish, as well as the underlying molecular and behavioral mechanisms. We demonstrated that Nco exposure selectively reduced PSA levels accompanied by upregulation of neu1 expression. Using neu1-KO zebrafish, we further showed that Neu1 deficiency attenuated Nco-induced lethality, behavioral excitation, and neuronal activation, as assessed by c-Fos expression. Importantly, we identified dopamine D3 receptor-related signaling as a potential downstream pathway involved in Nco responsiveness and found that pharmacological activation of D3 receptors partially rescued specific Nco-responsive behaviors in neu1-KO zebrafish. Together, these findings support a model in which Neu1-mediated PSA dynamics may influence the threshold for Nco-induced neural and behavioral activation through dopamine D3 receptor-related signaling.

Although the relationship between PSA and Nco exposure has not been previously reported, PSA is known to be conjugated to NCAM, and its strong negative charge modulates cell–cell interactions, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal circuit dynamics (Acheson et al. 1991; Bonfanti and Theodosis 2009). PSA also functionally interacts with several neuromodulatory systems, including dopaminergic and BDNF signaling, thereby impacting receptor activation and downstream signal transduction (Yamada et al. 2020). From this perspective, the Nco-induced reduction in PSA levels observed in the present study may represent a mechanism through which Nco reshapes neuronal excitability and behavioral output by modulating the extracellular glycan environment.

Neu1 is classically regarded as a lysosomal sialidase. However, accumulating evidence in both mammals and fish indicates that Neu1 can translocate to the cell surface via lysosomal exocytosis (Uemura et al. 2009), where it cleaves extracellular sialylated glycoconjugates, including PSA. Although fish Neu1 has been reported to cleave multiple sialic acid linkages, including α2,3-linked sialoglycoproteins (Ryuzono et al. 2016; Okada et al. 2020), our lectin blot analyses revealed no detectable changes in α2,3- or α2,6-linked sialylation profiles in the zebrafish brain following Nco exposure. This selective effect strongly suggests that PSA is the major candidate substrate for Neu1 involved in Nco-induced neural responses under the present experimental conditions. PSA plays a crucial role in higher brain functions, including learning and memory, and dysregulation of PSA homeostasis has been implicated in human neuropsychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia (Sato and Kitajima 2013). Altered NEU1 expression has been reported in mood disorders and autism spectrum disorders (Zhang et al. 2022). Consistent with these findings, our previous studies revealed that neu1-KO zebrafish exhibit altered anxiety-like and social behaviors (Ikeda et al. 2021), indicating that Neu1-mediated regulation of neural glycans is crucial for vertebrate brain function. The present study extends this concept by identifying Nco sensitivity as another behavioral domain that is influenced by Neu1–PSA signaling.

Our previous study revealed that PSA regulates dopamine availability and modulates dopamine D1 receptor-dependent social behavior in neu1-KO zebrafish (Tsuji et al. 2025). In contrast, the present study suggests that altered PSA dynamics may influence dopamine D3 receptor-mediated signaling in the context of Nco-induced excitation. Dopamine D3 receptors are characterized by high affinity for dopamine and are thought to sense tonic dopamine levels (Kiss et al. 2021), thereby acting as threshold regulators for dopaminergic signaling. The observed shift in Nco responsiveness—where neu1-KO zebrafish required higher Nco concentrations to exhibit behavioral activation—suggests that PSA-dependent modulation of D3 receptor signaling contributes to the regulation of Nco-responsive behavioral thresholds. Consistent with this possibility, activation of D3 receptors by PD128907 partially enhanced Nco-induced behavioral responses in neu1-KO zebrafish. However, the rescue was incomplete, as nicotine responsiveness was not restored to WT levels and excited swimming behavior was not significantly affected. Therefore, reduced D3 receptor signaling may contribute to the attenuated Nco sensitivity observed in neu1-KO zebrafish, although additional molecular and neural mechanisms are likely involved. To the best of our knowledge, these findings provide initial evidence that PSA may differentially influence dopamine receptor subtype-related signaling in a context-dependent manner.

Our data further indicated that the hypothalamus may serve as a key anatomical site for Neu1–PSA–dopamine interactions. Nco-induced c-Fos activation was prominently observed in this region, and c-Fos signals colocalized with PSA-positive neurons. Given that dopamine D3 receptors are expressed in the vertebrate hypothalamus (Pagano et al. 2016), these findings support a model in which PSA-dependent modulation of D3 receptor signaling in hypothalamic circuits contributes to Nco-induced behavioral activation.

The mechanisms underlying the reduced expression of drd3 in neu1-KO zebrafish remain unclear. However, our previous studies have demonstrated that neu1-KO zebrafish possess significantly elevated brain dopamine levels compared with WT fish (Tsuji et al. 2025). In substance use disorders, chronic dopamine elevation induces the compensatory downregulation or desensitization of D2-like receptors, including D3 receptors (Volkow et al. 2019), resulting in reduced dopamine sensitivity. Thus, reduced drd3 expression observed in neu1-KO zebrafish may reflect a compensatory adaptation to persistently elevated dopamine levels. From this perspective, neu1-KO zebrafish may represent a genetically fixed dopamine hyposensitivity- or tolerance-like state, resembling the neural adaptations observed in addiction. Although drd2a expression was slightly reduced in the present study, whereas drd5 showed a decreasing trend in our previous report (Tsuji et al. 2025), the most consistent dopaminergic alteration observed across studies was the marked reduction in drd3 expression.

Reduced dopamine sensitivity is a hallmark of Nco dependence and other addictive behaviors, including drug abuse and gambling disorders. Although no previous studies have linked Neu1 or PSA directly to addiction-related phenotypes, our findings raise the possibility that alterations in Neu1 expression or PSA dynamics may impact the vulnerability to or progression of addictive behaviors. However, this hypothesis warrants further investigation using fish and mammalian models.

This study has several limitations. First, although we demonstrated a strong association between a reduction in PSA levels and altered Nco sensitivity, direct causal evidence remains to be established. Although the present study focused on Neu1-dependent alterations in PSA-associated neuronal signaling, PSA synthesis and turnover are likely regulated by multiple mechanisms. Future studies will be required to determine how nicotine exposure influences PSA dynamics in both WT and neu1-KO zebrafish. Second, although dopaminergic regulation of Nco sensitivity is likely conserved across vertebrates, brain structure and circuit organization vary substantially among species, necessitating the validation of these findings in other animal models. Third, Neu1 participates in broader lysosomal glycan catabolism. Although no evidence of lysosomal dysfunction was observed in the present study, the potential contribution of lysosome-related pathways to Nco sensitivity must be explored in future studies. Fourth, the present study focused exclusively on acute nicotine exposure. Future studies using chronic low-dose nicotine exposure are needed to determine whether Neu1 also contributes to long-term nicotine responsiveness.

In conclusion, we propose a conceptual model in which Nco exposure induces Neu1 upregulation and alters PSA dynamics, thereby influencing dopamine D3 receptor-related signaling and shifting the threshold for Nco-induced neural and behavioral activation. This study suggests that neural glycans may modulate dopamine receptor-specific signaling and highlights the Neu1–PSA axis as a potential regulatory system governing Nco sensitivity. Our findings provide new insights into the molecular basis of dopaminergic threshold regulation and have broader implications for understanding addiction-related behaviors across vertebrates.
