Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) encompasses a spectrum of pathological processes affecting arterioles, capillaries, and venules in the brain [1], and is a major contributor to lacunar stroke, vascular cognitive impairment, gait disturbance, and late-life disability. Conventional MRI markers—white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, and enlarged perivascular spaces—have been widely adopted as surrogate markers of CSVD burden [2]. However, these structural markers largely reflect accumulated damage and provide limited information about ongoing microvascular dysfunction or treatment-responsive physiology.

Perfusion imaging offers an attractive complementary approach. Among available techniques, arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI is particularly appealing because it uses magnetically labeled arterial blood water as an endogenous tracer, avoiding exogenous contrast agents, and can be repeated safely in longitudinal studies [3]. ASL permits quantitative measurement of cerebral blood flow (CBF) and, with appropriate acquisition schemes, can also provide estimates of arterial transit time (ATT) and related hemodynamic parameters.

In sporadic CSVD, however, etiologic heterogeneity (hypertension, diabetes, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, and other comorbid pathologies) complicates the interpretation of perfusion abnormalities. In this context, monogenic CSVDs offer unique opportunities. They are defined by a single, well-characterized genetic cause, reducing etiologic noise and allowing clearer links between imaging markers and underlying microvascular pathology [[4], [5], [6]].

Among monogenic CSVDs, CADASIL (cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy) is the best characterized and most prevalent. In this review, we focus on CADASIL as a prototypical, low-heterogeneity form of CSVD and examine how ASL, including advanced variants such as multi-delay ASL and diffusion-prepared pseudo-continuous ASL (DP-pCASL), can be used to interrogate microvascular function and to monitor therapeutic response. We integrate data from CADASIL, other hereditary CSVDs, and sporadic CSVD, and we outline a roadmap for incorporating ASL into future clinical trials.

CADASIL as a prototypical monogenic human model of CSVD

CADASIL is caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in NOTCH3, typically affecting cysteine residues in the epidermal growth factor–like repeats of the receptor. The disease is characterized by progressive degeneration of vascular smooth muscle cells, granular osmiophilic material (GOM) deposition in vessel walls, and widespread arteriopathy predominantly affecting small penetrating arteries [1]. Clinically, CADASIL presents with migraine with aura, recurrent subcortical ischemic events, mood disturbances, and gradual cognitive decline, often leading to subcortical vascular dementia in mid-to-late adulthood [1,7,8].

On MRI, CADASIL shows a characteristic pattern of WMH, lacunes, and microbleeds. WMH often involve the anterior temporal poles and external capsule, in addition to periventricular and deep white matter [2]. Lacunes and microbleeds cluster in the basal ganglia, thalamus, and brainstem. The pathologic substrate is relatively well delineated, and there is minimal confounding from large-artery atherosclerosis or Alzheimer-type amyloid pathology in many patients, particularly at younger ages.

i)Genetic homogeneity: A single-gene cause (NOTCH3) reduces etiologic heterogeneity relative to sporadic CSVD, where mixed vascular and neurodegenerative pathologies are common.

For these reasons, CADASIL provides a unique platform in which to evaluate ASL-derived measures as biomarkers of microvascular dysfunction and potential surrogates for therapeutic response.

Principles and technical considerations of ASL in small vessel disease

Basic principles of ASL

In pseudo-continuous ASL (pCASL), a train of RF pulses and gradient fields is applied at the level of the feeding arteries in the neck to invert the longitudinal magnetization of arterial blood. After a post-labeling delay (PLD) to allow labeled spins to travel from the labeling plane to the imaging volume, perfusion-weighted images are obtained by subtracting labeled and control acquisitions. Quantitative CBF maps can then be derived using a kinetic model that incorporates labeling efficiency, T1 relaxation, PLD, and other factors.

Compared with dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC) MRI, ASL offers several advantages [3,9]: it is non-invasive, does not require gadolinium-based contrast agents, can be repeated frequently, and is particularly suitable for patients with renal impairment or those requiring serial examinations. However, ASL has lower signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), is sensitive to arterial transit effects, and requires careful parameter selection.

Specific challenges in CSVD and CADASIL

Applying ASL to CSVD, and particularly to white matter, poses distinct technical challenges:

i)Low perfusion and SNR: White matter CBF is inherently low, resulting in a small perfusion signal and increased vulnerability to noise. This is accentuated in CSVD where perfusion may be further reduced.

Low perfusion and SNR: White matter CBF is inherently low, resulting in a small perfusion signal and increased vulnerability to noise. This is accentuated in CSVD where perfusion may be further reduced.

ii)Prolonged ATT: CSVD often involves arteriolar stenosis and reduced vascular compliance, leading to delayed arrival of labeled blood, and reduced cardiac output or impaired cardiac function may further prolong ATT. If PLD is too short, CBF will be underestimated due to incomplete arrival of labeled blood.

iii)Partial volume effects: WMH often abut ventricles and cortical gray matter. Partial volume contamination can distort CBF estimates if not accounted for. Multi-delay ASL, in which images are acquired at several PLDs, addresses some of these challenges by enabling joint estimation of CBF and ATT, and by reducing sensitivity to arbitrary PLD choices. Nevertheless, scan time and SNR remain constraints, particularly in clinical settings.

In practice, however, single-delay 3D pCASL with a sufficiently long PLD, as recommended in current consensus guidelines for older and clinically ill adults, remains the workhorse sequence in routine clinical protocols and multi-center studies [3]. Most published CADASIL and hereditary CSVD studies to date have used single-delay pCASL to derive reliable global and regional CBF measures in gray and white matter [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Multi-delay ASL should therefore be viewed as a valuable extension for more detailed hemodynamic modeling, rather than a prerequisite for extracting clinically meaningful ASL biomarkers in CSVD.

Standardization, quality control, and reproducibility of ASL

The clinical adoption of ASL has been supported by consensus recommendations on sequence implementation, parameter selection, and post-processing [3]. These guidelines emphasize the use of pCASL with background suppression, careful labeling plane placement, and standardized PLDs appropriate for the target population.

Building on these recommendations, dedicated image-processing frameworks such as ExploreASL have been developed to harmonize single- and multi-delay ASL data across scanners and centers, providing standardized steps for motion correction, registration, partial-volume correction, and CBF quantification [14]. Such pipelines are particularly relevant for CSVD and CADASIL studies, where subtle between-group differences and longitudinal changes in CBF need to be distinguished from site- and sequence-related variability.

Quality control (QC) is critical. Visual QC scales have been developed to assess labeling efficiency, motion, vascular artifacts, and overall image quality [15]. Test–retest studies in older adults and patients with CSVD have demonstrated acceptable intraclass correlation coefficients for global and regional CBF when standardized protocols are used, supporting the use of ASL as a longitudinal biomarker.

In the context of CADASIL and other hereditary CSVDs, adherence to standardized ASL protocols and systematic QC will be essential if ASL-derived metrics are to be used as endpoints in multi-center trials. When these elements are in place, single-delay 3D pCASL can provide robust, reproducible CBF measures that are suitable for use as primary pharmacodynamic endpoints in clinical trials.

Cross-sectional evidence of ASL in CADASIL

ASL and cognition in CADASIL

One of the key questions in CADASIL is how microvascular dysfunction translates into cognitive impairment. ASL provides a direct window on tissue perfusion that can be correlated with neuropsychological performance.

In a study of CADASIL patients using 3D ASL combined with diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), reduced white matter CBF—particularly in regions affected by WMH—was associated with worse performance on global cognitive measures such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [10]. Importantly, regression analyses suggested that perfusion measures contributed independently to cognitive impairment beyond structural markers such as WMH volume and DTI indices. These findings support the notion that hypoperfusion is not merely a consequence of tissue damage but may play a direct role in cognitive decline.

In CADASIL, reduced perfusion in frontal white matter and subcortical structures such as the thalamus is thought to contribute to the characteristic dysexecutive and processing-speed profile, consistent with observations from ASL and positron emission tomography (PET)/single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) studies reporting frontal cortical and subcortical hypoperfusion or hypometabolism in CADASIL [[8], [10], [16], [17]]. Although most published CADASIL ASL studies have emphasized global cognition, the regional pattern is biologically compatible with domain-specific impairment: lower perfusion in frontal white matter would be expected to track tests of processing speed and executive control such as the Trail Making Test, Digit Symbol Coding, or set-shifting paradigms, whereas thalamic and other deep gray matter hypoperfusion may relate more strongly to attentional control and working-memory performance. These regional hypotheses remain to be tested systematically in larger cohorts, but they provide a useful framework for aligning ASL-derived metrics with the cognition-and-behavior focus of future CADASIL studies and early-phase trials.

ASL and lesion burden in CADASIL

ASL has also been used to investigate the relationship between perfusion and structural lesion burden in CADASIL. In a small but illustrative study, regional CBF was quantified in WMH, normal-appearing white matter (NAWM), gray matter, and whole brain [11]. CADASIL patients exhibited lower CBF in WMH compared to NAWM and gray matter, and CBF in WMH was inversely associated with total WMH volume.

These findings suggest that reduced perfusion is closely linked to lesion burden and may contribute to lesion expansion. More broadly, ASL can capture diffuse hypoperfusion in NAWM, which may represent an at-risk tissue state preceding overt WMH formation [2]. Supporting this concept, a two-year longitudinal 3D pCASL study in CADASIL found that lower baseline CBF and greater CBF decline were associated with subsequent progression of subcortical hyperintensity volume, and that lower baseline CBF predicted decline in attention and working memory [18]. These data highlight CBF as a candidate imaging biomarker of disease progression in CADASIL [2].

ASL in acute and subacute CADASIL episodes

Beyond chronic changes, ASL may be informative in acute or subacute episodes such as CADASIL encephalopathy. In a single-case report of CADASIL-related acute encephalopathy (abrupt global aphasia with reduced responsiveness), ASL performed during the acute episode and repeated after recovery demonstrated dynamic regional perfusion abnormalities (including thalamic and temporo-parieto-occipital cortical regions) that evolved in parallel with clinical improvement; combined with EEG findings, these serial ASL changes were discussed as helping to delineate migrainous versus ictal pathophysiology rather than indicating an ischemic event [19].

Although these observations are preliminary, they highlight potential roles for ASL in the acute diagnostic work-up of CADASIL, including differentiating encephalopathic episodes from stroke and guiding management. They also illustrate the dynamic nature of perfusion changes in this disorder.

Multi-delay ASL and arterial transit time in CADASIL

Given the microvascular involvement in CADASIL, alterations in ATT are expected and must be taken into account even when using single-delay ASL. Consensus recommendations therefore advocate relatively long PLDs in older adults and patients with cerebrovascular disease to reduce underestimation of CBF due to delayed arterial arrival, and standardized processing pipelines can further mitigate transit-related confounds at the analysis stage [3,14]. Beyond these single-delay strategies, multi-delay pCASL studies have begun to explicitly characterize ATT in CADASIL by acquiring data at multiple PLDs and fitting kinetic models that estimate both CBF and ATT. Preliminary multi-delay pCASL work in CADASIL has provided direct evidence of ATT prolongation. In an ISMRM 2022 abstract reporting a case–control study including 27 genetically confirmed CADASIL patients and 28 healthy controls, ATT derived from five-delay pCASL was significantly longer in all predefined regions of interest, including cortical lobes, subcortical nuclei and white matter, whereas CBF was largely preserved except for reduced CBF in white matter. The authors interpreted this pattern of uniformly prolonged ATT with relatively preserved CBF as indicating hemodynamic compensation and collateral circulation in the setting of small-vessel pathology, rather than a simple global reduction in flow [20]. Building on this work, Yang et al. applied five-delay 3D pCASL in combination with high-resolution black-blood MRI to 29 CADASIL patients and 33 healthy controls [21]. In addition to confirming widespread ATT prolongation, they quantified small-vessel density and demonstrated concurrent reductions in small-vessel density and CBF across cortical and subcortical regions in CADASIL, with these hemodynamic and structural alterations correlating with poorer cognitive performance. This integrated approach highlights the potential of combining multi-delay ASL with vessel-wall imaging to obtain a more complete picture of microvascular remodeling and its cognitive consequences in CADASIL.

These findings underscore that ATT is not simply a nuisance variable but may carry biologically meaningful information. Incorporating multi-delay ASL into CADASIL studies can therefore enhance sensitivity to microvascular dysfunction and improve interpretation of CBF differences.

Beyond perfusion: BBB function and microvascular physiology with diffusion-prepared pCASL

Diffusion-prepared pCASL and BBB water exchange

Conventional ASL primarily measures perfusion—the delivery of blood to tissue per unit time. However, microvascular pathology in CSVD also involves alterations in the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and capillary exchange properties. Diffusion-prepared pCASL (DP-pCASL) extends ASL by incorporating diffusion-sensitizing gradients that differentially attenuate intravascular and extravascular labeled water. By acquiring data with and without diffusion preparation and fitting an appropriate model, one can estimate the water exchange rate across the BBB (kw), which reflects combined effects of capillary surface area, permeability, and hemodynamics [22,23].

DP-pCASL is therefore well suited to probe BBB and microvascular function non-invasively, without the need for gadolinium-based contrast agents or dynamic susceptibility sequences. At the same time, because diffusion preparation further attenuates the already small ASL signal, DP-pCASL has lower signal-to-noise ratio than standard pCASL and is consequently more sensitive to scanner-specific implementation, motion, and acquisition-time constraints.

DP-pCASL evidence in CADASIL and HTRA1-related CSVD

Two complementary lines of evidence support the use of DP-pCASL to interrogate BBB function in hereditary CSVD. First, a dedicated DP-pCASL study in CADASIL demonstrated that kw is significantly reduced in patients compared with age- and sex-matched controls, both globally and in regions typically affected by CSVD, such as deep gray matter and normal-appearing white matter [12]. Lower kw was associated with greater MRI lesion burden and worse functional status, as indexed by disability scales, even after accounting for conventional perfusion metrics. These data indicate that DP-pCASL is sensitive to BBB/microvascular dysfunction in CADASIL and that kw carries information that is not redundant with CBF.

Second, Li et al. extended this paradigm to a mixed cohort of hereditary CSVD, including CADASIL and heterozygous HTRA1-related CSVD [13]. Using DP-pCASL, they showed that kw is reduced in both genetic subtypes relative to healthy controls, with broadly similar spatial patterns across cortical and subcortical regions. Importantly, decreased kw correlated with higher overall disease burden and poorer global cognitive performance. In multivariable analyses, the associations between kw and clinical measures persisted after adjustment for age, vascular risk factors, and conventional imaging markers, suggesting that BBB water exchange abnormalities represent a core component of hereditary CSVD pathophysiology rather than a mere epiphenomenon of structural damage or reduced flow [13].

Together, these studies highlight several key points. (i) BBB dysfunction, as captured by reduced kw, is a shared feature of CADASIL and HTRA1-related CSVD, consistent with convergent microvascular mechanisms despite distinct genetic etiologies. (ii) kw provides complementary information to CBF and ATT: regions with relatively preserved perfusion may still exhibit reduced water exchange, implying that microvascular and BBB abnormalities can precede or outlast overt hypoperfusion. (iii) Associations between kw, lesion burden, and global cognitive impairment support the biological and clinical relevance of DP-pCASL-derived metrics, although the directionality and regional specificity of relationships with particular cognitive domains (e.g., executive function, processing speed) require further study. Within CADASIL specifically, cross-sectional patterns of kw reduction align with the known predilection of the disease for frontal–subcortical networks, reinforcing the rationale for incorporating DP-pCASL into mechanistic studies and early-phase trials.

Beyond hereditary CSVD, recent work in patients with sporadic SVD has combined DP-pCASL and dynamic-contrast enhanced MRI to interrogate BBB function. In a Mild Stroke Study 3 substudy, Stringer et al. reported that higher kw was associated with greater baseline WMH burden and tended to predict 1-year WMH progression, whereas kw showed only limited correlations with Patlak-derived permeability-surface area product, plasma volume, or kGad [24]. These findings support DP-pCASL-derived kw as a sensitive and partly non-redundant marker of BBB dysfunction in sporadic SVD, reinforcing the rationale for incorporating DP-pCASL metrics alongside or instead of gadolinium-based permeability imaging in CADASIL and other hereditary CSVD.

More recently, Ying et al. combined DP-pCASL with gadolinium-based dynamic contrast–enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) in a mixed cohort of CADASIL, heterozygous HTRA1-related CSVD, sporadic CSVD, and healthy controls [25]. They demonstrated that CADASIL was characterized predominantly by globally reduced kw with largely preserved gadolinium leakage rate (Ktrans), whereas sporadic CSVD showed increased Ktrans with relatively preserved kw and HTRA1-related CSVD exhibited both increased Ktrans and reduced kw. Across all three patient groups, altered kw and Ktrans measures correlated with lesion burden and clinical severity. These findings underscore that BBB dysfunction in CSVD is heterogeneous across genetic and sporadic subtypes and that kw and Ktrans provide complementary, non-redundant information about BBB pathology.

DP-pCASL in clinical trials and ongoing studies

DP-pCASL has begun to appear in clinical trial protocols targeting CADASIL and related conditions. For example, ongoing studies registered on clinical trial platforms use DP-pCASL-derived kw as a primary or secondary outcome to assess BBB function. In the MRI-CADASIL-BBB study (NCT05902039) [26], the primary objective is to determine whether kw, quantified with DP-pCASL, is altered in patients with CADASIL and how it relates to MRI markers and clinical features. This trial illustrates how DP-pCASL-derived kw can be positioned as a central physiological endpoint in hereditary SVD. These trials are expected to provide critical data on the feasibility, reliability, and responsiveness of kw to intervention.

If DP-pCASL can demonstrate sensitivity to treatment-induced changes in BBB/microvascular function, it could become a powerful tool for phase 2 trials, enabling smaller sample sizes and shorter durations by focusing on proximal physiological endpoints rather than distal clinical outcomes alone.

Comparison with other monogenic and sporadic SVD

ASL in Fabry disease: a different perfusion pattern

Fabry disease, an X-linked lysosomal storage disorder caused by α-galactosidase A deficiency, is not a classic arteriolar CSVD but often presents with white matter lesions and small-vessel ischemic changes. ASL studies in adult Fabry patients have reported increased resting CBF in cortical and subcortical regions compared with controls, with associations between CBF, WMH burden, and disease biomarkers such as lyso-Gb3 [27].

This pattern contrasts with the predominantly hypoperfused white matter seen in CADASIL. The divergence likely reflects distinct underlying pathophysiology-globally increased flow related to endothelial dysfunction and nitric oxide (NO) pathway alterations in Fabry disease, versus chronic hypoperfusion and structural arteriopathy in CADASIL. From a review perspective, Fabry disease provides a useful counterexample: even within the broad umbrella of “monogenic conditions with small vessel involvement,” perfusion patterns are disease-specific. This reinforces the value of CADASIL as a model for “pure ischemic” CSVD and underscores the need to interpret ASL findings in light of disease-specific biology.

ASL in HTRA1-related and other hereditary CSVD

HTRA1-related CSVD, encompassing CARASIL and autosomal dominant forms with heterozygous HTRA1 variants, shares clinical and imaging features with CADASIL, including WMH, lacunes, and early-onset stroke [5]. As discussed above, DP-pCASL studies in HTRA1-related CSVD have revealed reduced kw similar to CADASIL, suggesting convergent microvascular and BBB dysfunction [13].

Other monogenic CSVDs linked to COL4A1/2, TREX1 (RVCL-S), and additional genes are less well studied with ASL to date [5,6]. Conventional MRI and, in some cases, contrast-based permeability imaging have been reported, but systematic ASL or DP-pCASL studies are scarce. This represents a gap and an opportunity: extending ASL-based paradigms from CADASIL to these disorders may elucidate shared and distinct microvascular phenotypes.

ASL in sporadic small vessel disease and vascular cognitive impairment

Sporadic CSVD is vastly more common than hereditary forms and constitutes the bulk of vascular cognitive impairment. Numerous ASL studies have examined CBF in patients with WMH, lacunes, and related imaging markers [9,28]. Overall, these studies report reduced CBF in white matter and subcortical gray matter, as well as increased spatial variability of CBF (e.g., higher spatial coefficient of variation) associated with higher CSVD burden and worse cognition. DP-pCASL studies in sporadic SVD have suggested that kw is sensitive to SVD burden and short-term WMH progression, while showing only modest overlap with gadolinium-based leakage metrics [24]. Together with DP-pCASL data in CADASIL and HTRA1-related CSVD [25], these observations indicate that BBB water-exchange measures may capture a core, treatment-responsive aspect of microvascular dysfunction that is shared across hereditary and sporadic forms of CSVD.

ASL as a biomarker for therapeutic trials in CADASIL

Why CADASIL is well suited for ASL-based therapeutic trials

The unmet therapeutic need is substantial in CADASIL: no disease-modifying therapy has been proven to prevent stroke recurrence or cognitive decline, and standard antithrombotic strategies appear insufficient for long-term protection in many patients.

Small physiological and pharmacological studies suggest that cerebral hemodynamics in CADASIL are modifiable, providing a mechanistic rationale for perfusion- and BBB-based imaging endpoints. In an ASL-based hypercapnia study, reduced cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) was closely linked to lacune burden, brain atrophy, disability and slower processing speed, and ASL-derived CVR correlated with peripheral flow-mediated dilatation, underscoring the systemic nature of vascular dysfunction in CADASIL [29]. Acetazolamide challenge studies using MRI bolus tracking have shown markedly reduced cerebrovascular reactivity in CADASIL, yet preserved capacity for vasodilatory augmentation in at least part of the microvascular bed [30]. Chronic administration of acetazolamide over several months has been reported to increase CBF and cerebrovascular reserve without major safety concerns in small open-label series [31], although these data are preliminary and require confirmation in controlled trials. Likewise, lomerizine—an antimigraine calcium-channel blocker that selectively dilates cerebral arteries—has been associated with improved cerebral perfusion and a reduction in recurrent ischemic events in open-label studies of CADASIL patients, prompting the LOMCAD trial to formally evaluate its stroke-preventive efficacy in a prospective, multicenter setting [32,33].

Taken together, these observations support a view of CADASIL as a disorder in which microvascular tone, CVR, and possibly BBB water exchange remain at least partially responsive to pharmacological modulation, even on a background of structural arteriopathy. ASL and DP-pCASL are therefore well positioned to serve as pharmacodynamic readouts in CADASIL trials: they can quantify changes in regional CBF, ATT, and kw over relatively short time scales, are repeatable and non-invasive, and directly target the physiological domains that emerging therapies aim to influence. In early-phase studies, modest but consistent improvements in these ASL-derived metrics—particularly in normal-appearing white matter and strategic frontal–subcortical regions—could provide critical evidence of biological activity before long-term structural or cognitive benefits become apparent.

Choice of endpoints should be informed by prior observational data, test–retest reliability, and expected treatment mechanisms. For example, a therapy targeting endothelial function might be hypothesized to increase kw and normalize ATT before producing measurable changes in lesion burden.

Beyond observational studies, ASL has already been implemented as a primary endpoint in interventional trials targeting small vessel disease. In the PASTIS cross-over trial of tadalafil in older patients with symptomatic sporadic SVD, the primary outcome was change in subcortical CBF measured by ASL, demonstrating the feasibility of using ASL-derived perfusion metrics as a sensitive pharmacodynamic marker in vascular cognitive impairment. Although the trial did not show a large acute CBF increase at the group level, it provided robust data on effect sizes, variability, and test–retest characteristics that can inform future study design.

A similar concept is now being extended to monogenic SVD. In the AMCAD phase II study of adrenomedullin for CADASIL, the primary endpoint is the change rate of frontal cortical CBF from baseline to day 28, quantified by ASL. This trial exemplifies how ASL can be positioned as the main imaging outcome in a disease-modifying study for CADASIL, bridging mechanistic hypotheses on microvascular function and clinical efficacy. For future CADASIL or hereditary SVD trials, these experiences support the use of standardized multi-delay pCASL protocols, careful test–retest characterization, and rigorous quality control as essential prerequisites when ASL is chosen as a primary or key secondary endpoint.

Notably, both PASTIS and AMCAD relied on single-delay 3D pCASL with standardized PLDs and processing pipelines to quantify regional CBF, and used these CBF measures as prespecified primary perfusion endpoints. This experience illustrates that robust, trial-feasible perfusion endpoints can be implemented without multi-delay acquisitions, while multi-delay ASL and DP-pCASL can be reserved for substudies where detailed hemodynamic or BBB modeling is a primary objective [9,14,34].

The effect sizes for kw and ATT are less well defined and will need to be refined in pilot studies. For DP-pCASL specifically, the lower signal-to-noise ratio imposed by diffusion preparation may increase between-site variability, so phantom work, site qualification, harmonized reconstruction, and centralized quality control will be especially important if kw is to be used in multi-center phase II studies.

i)Improve endothelial function and nitric oxide bioavailability.

ASL-based trials could, for instance, enroll patients in mid-stage disease with measurable cognitive and MRI burden, randomize them to active treatment versus placebo, and assess changes in CBF, ATT, and kw at 6–12 months, alongside clinical and neuropsychological outcomes. In early-phase trials, ASL readouts may serve as primary endpoints to establish biological activity, with later-phase studies focusing on clinical benefit [35].

By establishing robust ASL biomarkers in CADASIL, investigators can build a translational bridge to sporadic CSVD and vascular cognitive impairment, where similar trial designs could be adapted with appropriate adjustments for heterogeneity [34].

Collectively, PASTIS and AMCAD illustrate that ASL-based CBF measures are not only biologically informative but also operationally feasible as primary endpoints in both sporadic and monogenic SVD, providing a strong precedent for future CADASIL-focused therapeutic trials.

Future directions and research gaps

Despite encouraging progress, the literature on ASL in CADASIL remains limited in sample size and scope. Most studies are cross-sectional, involve relatively small cohorts, and use heterogeneous acquisition and post-processing approaches. A more critical reading of the field therefore points to several unresolved issues, including center-to-center protocol variability, incomplete longitudinal validation, and uncertainty about how directly perfusion and BBB water-exchange metrics track clinical progression. Key gaps include:

i)Longitudinal data: There is a paucity of longitudinal ASL studies assessing how CBF, ATT, and kw evolve over time and how they relate to progression of WMH, lacunes, microbleeds, and cognitive decline.

Longitudinal data: There is a paucity of longitudinal ASL studies assessing how CBF, ATT, and kw evolve over time and how they relate to progression of WMH, lacunes, microbleeds, and cognitive decline.

ii)Standardized multi-center protocols: Harmonized, multi-center ASL protocols have not yet been widely implemented in CADASIL or hereditary CSVD cohorts, limiting generalizability.

Standardized multi-center protocols: Harmonized, multi-center ASL protocols have not yet been widely implemented in CADASIL or hereditary CSVD cohorts, limiting generalizability.

iii)Integration with other modalities: Combining ASL/DP-pCASL with diffusion imaging, susceptibility-weighted imaging, and molecular imaging (e.g., PET) could provide a more comprehensive view of microvascular and neurodegenerative processes.

Integration with other modalities: Combining ASL/DP-pCASL with diffusion imaging, susceptibility-weighted imaging, and molecular imaging (e.g., PET) could provide a more comprehensive view of microvascular and neurodegenerative processes.

iv)Extension to other monogenic CSVDs: Systematic ASL studies in COL4A1/2-related CSVD, TREX1-associated vasculopathy, and other monogenic entities are needed to define disease-specific perfusion signatures and shared pathophysiological themes.

Extension to other monogenic CSVDs: Systematic ASL studies in COL4A1/2-related CSVD, TREX1-associated vasculopathy, and other monogenic entities are needed to define disease-specific perfusion signatures and shared pathophysiological themes.

v)Validation as surrogate endpoints: Ultimately, ASL-derived metrics must be shown to predict clinically meaningful outcomes and to respond to effective therapies in order to be accepted as surrogate endpoints by regulators and clinicians.

Validation as surrogate endpoints: Ultimately, ASL-derived metrics must be shown to predict clinically meaningful outcomes and to respond to effective therapies in order to be accepted as surrogate endpoints by regulators and clinicians.

Addressing these gaps will require coordinated efforts across centers, standardized imaging protocols, and integration of hereditary and sporadic CSVD cohorts. At the same time, several practical barriers to broader clinical implementation should be acknowledged. Compared with contrast-based perfusion imaging, ASL generally has a lower signal-to-noise ratio and is more susceptible to motion artifacts. In addition, the availability of ASL and DP-pCASL remains uneven across institutions because of differences in scanner platforms, software support, acquisition protocols, and local expertise. These limitations, together with cost and accessibility, continue to hinder broader adoption in routine clinical practice. CADASIL, with its well-defined genetic etiology and characteristic phenotype, is uniquely positioned to serve as a testbed for these advances.

In summary, ASL MRI—using standardized single-delay 3D pCASL as a practical foundation, and extended where appropriate with multi-delay acquisitions and DP-pCASL—offers powerful tools to interrogate microvascular function in CADASIL. The accumulated methodological and clinical experience indicates that pCASL-derived regional CBF is sufficiently mature to serve as a primary perfusion endpoint in phase II CADASIL trials. ASL acquisition with several post-labeling delays, enabling joint estimation of cerebral blood flow and arterial transit time by fitting a kinetic model.

An ASL variant that combines pCASL with diffusion-sensitizing gradients to distinguish intravascular from extravascular labeled water and estimate BBB water exchange.

The volume of blood delivered to a given amount of brain tissue per unit time, usually expressed in mL/100 g/min.

The time required for labeled arterial blood water to travel from the labeling plane to the tissue microvasculature.

A group of disorders affecting small arteries, arterioles, capillaries, and venules, leading to lacunar infarcts, white matter lesions, microbleeds, and cognitive decline.

CSVD arising from acquired risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and aging, without a single defined genetic cause.

Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy; a monogenic CSVD caused by NOTCH3 variants, characterized by migraine, strokes, and vascular cognitive impairment.

Hereditary CSVD caused by pathogenic variants in HTRA1, including autosomal dominant and recessive forms (CARASIL), with early-onset strokes and white matter lesions.

An X-linked lysosomal storage disorder due to α-galactosidase A deficiency, often associated with white matter lesions and small-vessel involvement but a distinct perfusion pattern from ischemic CSVD.

A selective barrier formed by endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes that regulates the exchange of substances between blood and brain tissue.

A quantitative DP-pCASL-derived parameter describing the rate at which water molecules cross the BBB between the vascular and tissue compartments.

Areas of increased signal on T2-weighted and FLAIR MRI, reflecting chronic small vessel disease–related white matter damage.

White matter that appears normal on conventional MRI but may show microstructural or perfusion abnormalities on advanced imaging.

Brain regions rich in neuronal cell bodies, including cerebral cortex and deep nuclei, with higher baseline perfusion than white matter.

The capacity of cerebral blood vessels to change caliber and blood flow in response to a vasoactive stimulus, such as acetazolamide or CO₂.
