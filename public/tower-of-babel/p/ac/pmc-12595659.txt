TP53 c.730G > A pathogenic variant as a plausible candidate biomarker in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients from Karachi, pakistan: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: SM Adnan Ali, Yumna Adnan, Saleema Mehboob Ali, Zubair Ahmad, Iqbal Azam, Tabish Chawla, Hasnain Ahmed Farooqui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancers is known to stand among the most aggressive cancers with low survival rate. Research indicates that investigating target biomarkers can provide scientific grounds for tailored treatments in cancers. However, biomarker expression is observed to vary among different populations. Therefore, investigating the population biomarkers is a pre-requisite to understand the disease patterns and the selection of precision therapy. The biomarker spectrum of Pakistani pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients remains unmapped.MethodsWe enrolled a total of 109 patients in the study and obtained FFPE tumor samples. After confirmation of diagnosis and appropriate tumor content, DNA extraction was performed. Based on the results from a pilot investigation by our group, we selected specific regions of KRAS, TP53, BRCA1 and APC genes for the current study. Extracted DNA samples were subjected to primer specific PCR for the selected gene regions followed by Sanger sequencing.ResultsA total of 59 genetic variants were found in the four selected genes, out of which 22% were pathogenic. TP53 pathogenic mutations were most frequently detected (75.2%) with all these patients carrying one consistent mutation c.730G > A. The occurrence of genetic alteration in BRCA1 was significantly associated with occurrence of alterations in KRAS (p = 0.001), TP53 (p = 0.008) and APC (p = 0.000). Moreover, the independent occurrence of mutations in TP53 (p = 0.001) and KRAS (p = 0.001) were found to significantly associated with the overall survival of the patients.ConclusionThe detection of pathogenic mutation TP53 c.730G > A is exceptionally high in our patients. This finding shows that TP53 c. 730G > A may be a plausible biomarker in the Pakistani PDAC population. It also indicates unique characteristics of the population in this specific geographical region as compared to global data. This is the first study that investigates genetic biomarkers in a large cohort of Pakistani pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients. The findings from the study can provide directions about the candidate biomarkers and targeted therapies for these patients.Keywords: Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, Prognosis, Pathogenic variant, KRAS, TP53, BRCA1, APC

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer ranks among the most aggressive forms of malignancies worldwide, being the sixth leading cause of global cancer-related deaths [1]. For early-stage Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma (PDAC), surgical resection is the preferred treatment option with positive impact on the patient’s survival. However, in approximately 80% of patients, pancreatic malignancy is diagnosed at the advanced stages rendering it an unresectable disease which is only treated with chemotherapy. Despite developments in surgical procedures and chemotherapy regimens, the 5-year overall survival rate stands at 12.5% [2]. Extensive molecular genetic analyses have demonstrated various molecular alterations linked to PDAC. Global studies reveal that the most common alteration is the somatic variant of the proto-oncogene KRAS, present in over 90% of pancreatic tumors. Other frequently mutated genes include TP53, CDKN2A, and SMAD4. Identifying these alterations not only serves in understanding tumorigenesis but also holds significance for developing personalized therapies for these patients. In PDAC, although actionable mutations are less common compared to other solid tumors, advances in precision oncology have enabled a subset of patients to benefit from genetically tailored therapies. The PARP inhibitor olaparib has been FDA-approved as a maintenance therapy for metastatic PDAC patients harboring germline BRCA1/2 mutations, based on evidence from the POLO trial [3]. Similarly, immune checkpoint inhibitor pembrolizumab has shown clinical benefit in PDAC cases with mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR) or high microsatellite instability (MSI-H). In addition, targeted therapies such as sotorasib and adagrasib, which are approved for KRAS G12C-mutated NSCLC, have entered clinical trials for PDAC patients with the same mutation. Though these targeted therapies were originally developed and validated in other tumor types, they are now being explored or repurposed for pancreatic cancer based on overlapping molecular profiles [4]. However, identification of these genetic biomarkers and development of targeted therapies are based on the studies that predominantly focused on European and Caucasian populations [5].

Investigating genetic variants within local populations is crucial for advancing personalized medicine, particularly in oncology. The presence of significant pharmacogenomic (PGx) variants that differ widely across ethnicities underscores the need for targeted research in diverse populations [6]. For example, studies have shown that certain variants, such as those in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, exhibit markedly higher prevalence in specific populations, with a Nigerian study reporting mutation rate of 7.9% for BRCA1 and 3.1% for BRCA2. This is significantly higher than the rates observed in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [7]. Further research has shown PTEN mutational signature in Caucasian and Asian descent endometrial cancer patients while TP53 variants were more prevalent in cases with African American descent. This suggests that cancer genetic landscapes can vary dramatically across different ethnic groups, highlighting the importance of local investigations to uncover these differences and improve diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Moreover, the implications of genetic diversity extend beyond the prevalence of specific variants to encompass variations in treatment response and disease severity. Ethnic differences in pharmacokinetics, safety, and efficacy have been reported for more than 20% of FDA-approved drugs, emphasizing the need for tailored treatment protocols. A meta-analysis of advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients indicated significant disparities in survival rates based on ethnicity, with evidence suggesting that certain subgroups, such as the Chinese population, may experience better outcomes under specific treatment regimens [8]. Further, genetic polymorphisms can modify disease severity in specific populations. For instance, the GSTT1-absent genotype has been significantly associated with overall survival among Pakistani breast cancer patients [9]. These findings illustrate the necessity of conducting localized genetic research to develop effective, population-specific interventions, ultimately improving health outcomes and reducing disparities in cancer care across diverse populations.

Recognizing the significance of population genetics, it becomes imperative to investigate genetic profiles of understudied populations. The Pakistani population is among the subsets that has been highly underrepresented in the global pancreatic cancer studies over the years. According to International Agency for Research on Cancers (IARC), the current mortality rate of pancreatic cancer in Pakistan stands at 97% [1]. Despite the alarming statistics, there is a general lack of research on this population at the global as well as local levels. In a recent pilot study conducted by our research group, we evaluated a panel consisting of 88 cancer associated genes in the Pakistani PDAC population [10]. The results revealed 29 different alterations in the coding region of various genes, with five pathogenic variants found in KRAS, TP53, BRCA1 and APC genes. Interestingly, none of the detected genetic variants corresponded to the two commonly reported genes globally, CDKN2A and SMAD4. Instead, genetic variants including pathogenic variants were found in BRCA1 and APC which are until now highlighted to be among the least frequently mutated genes in PDAC, globally [11]. Building upon these pilot findings, in the current study, we designed a large-scale investigation aimed at evaluating the targeted regions of KRAS, TP53, BRCA1, and APC genes in Pakistani PDAC patients.

The selection of specific variants for this study was guided by findings from our pilot investigation, in which these variants were detected among Pakistani PDAC patients, as well as by their established or potential roles in pancreatic tumorigenesis. The KRAS c.35G >A and c.35G >T variants are among the most frequent oncogenic alterations in PDAC globally, known to constitutively activate downstream signaling pathways such as MAPK and PI3K, thereby promoting uncontrolled cellular proliferation [12]. The TP53 c.713G >T variant lies within the DNA-binding domain critical for transcriptional activation of target genes involved in cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and its dysfunction is linked to poor prognosis in various cancers [13]. The BRCA1 c.5509T >C variant, located in the BRCT domain responsible for DNA repair fidelity, has been implicated in impaired homologous recombination repair [14]. The APC c.1100_1101delCT frameshift variant results in a truncated protein likely to disrupt β-catenin regulation and Wnt signaling, which are key in tumor suppression [15]. By focusing on these genetic variants, the study aims to investigate clinically relevant, potentially pathogenic alterations that may have population-specific implications in PDAC pathogenesis and patient outcomes.

Methodology

Patient enrollment and sample collection

A retrospective cohort study was conducted on a total of 109 PDAC patient samples. Initially, the study began with a cohort of 167 pancreatic cancer patients. However, 58 patients were excluded for various reasons: the unavailability of biopsy material, a diagnosis of pancreatic cancer subtypes other than PDAC, or refusal to provide written informed consent. For 109 patients included in the study, Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) tumor samples from the Department of Histopathology at Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH), Karachi, Pakistan. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the AKUH. Sample size was determined using STATA version 15.1. A total of 108 subjects were needed to achieve 80% power with an anticipated hazard ratio of 2.8 or more, standard error of the log of hazard ratio of 0.3 or higher, percentage of censoring of 15% and withdrawal of 10% and level of significance of 5%.

The inclusion criteria were set as PDAC patients aged 18 years and above, who underwent biopsy or surgery at AKUH between January 2014 to December 2022. Informed consent was obtained from all the patients. Patients failing to meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. The enrollment process did not prioritize any specific gender. All patients included in the study were treatment-naive at the time of diagnosis. Moreover, individuals with other pancreatic conditions, such as pancreatitis and Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency (EPI), were excluded from the study during the initial screening process to ensure a more homogenous cohort of PDAC patients. Clinical data were gathered by reviewing patients’ medical records, with follow-up calls conducted with six-month intervals to assess their health status. The last follow-up call was done in November 2023.

DNA extraction and quantification

A senior histopathologist (ZA) verified sample histology and tumor content using Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining. Tissues with over 20% tumor cells were subjected to DNA extraction. Tumor DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat No. 564040), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extraction process involved deparaffinization of tissue sections using xylene, followed by ethanol washes to remove residual xylene. Proteinase K digestion was performed at 56 °C for two hours to release DNA from the tissue. After lysis, DNA was purified using spin columns, with multiple wash steps to remove contaminants, and eluted in buffer AE (as provided in the kit). DNA quantification was carried out using spectrophotometry DS-11vs (DeNovix, USA) which measures DNA concentration based on absorbance at 260 nm. The 260/280 nm ratio was also evaluated to assess the purity of the DNA. Samples with a ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered to have acceptable purity for downstream applications. For both DNA extraction and quantification, the manufacturer’s recommended protocol was followed.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and gel electrophoresis

Primers were designed to evaluate DNA regions of the targeted variants KRAS c.35G > A, KRAS c.35G > T, TP53 c.713G > T, BRCA1 c.5509 T > C and APC c.1100_1101delCT. PCR conditions were optimized for individual genes using specific primers for target regions of selected genes. For each reaction, DreamTaq Green PCR 2X master mix (Thermo Scientific, Cat No. K1081) was diluted to 1X using nuclease free water. Further, 1 µl of 10µM primer and 50ng/µl template was added. Final reaction volume was set at 25 µl. Details for primers, cycling conditions and amplified products are shown in Table 1.

Subsequently, agarose gel electrophoresis technique was performed to evaluate PCR amplification. We prepared 2% agarose gel using molecular biology grade UltraPure™ agarose (Invitrogen™ ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat No. 16500500), UltraPure 10X TAE buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat No. 15558026) and UltraPure 10 mg/mL Ethidium Bromide (Invitrogen™ ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat No. 15585011). The gel was run at 100 V for 45 min in the multiSUB Mini, Mini Horizontal Electrophoresis System (Cleaver Scientific, Cat No. MSMINI7) was used. Amplified product was observed under Gel Doc XR + Gel Documentation System (BioRad) using 50 base pairs DNA ladder (Thermo Scientific™, Cat No. SM0371).

Sanger sequencing and clinical interpretation of genetic variants

Aliquots of 20 µl of the amplified product and 10 µl of both the forward and reverse primers were individually prepared in microfuge tubes. The primers utilized for sequencing were identical to those employed for PCR, as indicated in Table 1. Each tube was labelled, sealed and packed for shipping. Each amplified product sample was subject to purification and Sanger sequencing by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) https://dna.macrogen.com/quotation/retrieveCesQuotation.do. An electropherogram was generated against each sample which were subsequently analyzed and interpreted by our research team.

To assess the pathogenicity of the identified genetic variants in our study, we utilized a multi-step approach. Initially, the clinical significance of the variants was evaluated using the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/) and the Catalogue of Somatic Variants in Cancers (COSMIC) database (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic) for somatic variants. In instances where a variant was not listed in ClinVar, we referred to COSMIC. If the variant was absent in both databases, we conducted an extensive literature search to identify any relevant studies or reports. Variants that remained unreported after this thorough investigation were considered novel genetic variants. This approach ensured that our classification of novel variants was robust and based on comprehensive evidence [21].

Insilico functional analysis

To evaluate the potential functional impact of the TP53 c.730G > A (p.Gly244Asp) variant, we conducted in silico analysis using PolyPhen-2 (http://genetics.bwh.harvard.edu/pph2/index.shtml) and MutationTaster (https://www.mutationtaster.org/), two widely used predictive algorithms for assessing the pathogenicity of genetic alterations.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS® version 23. The Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was employed (as appropriate) to assess the association between categorical variables. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test. Further, Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to assess the prognostic impact of genetic variants and clinical factors on survival outcomes. A 95% Confidence Interval (CI) was used for all analyses.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics

From the total of 109 patients, 55% were males and 89.9% belonged to > 40 years of age group. Mean age of patients was 56.23 ± 12.280 (19–82 years). All the patients had confirmed diagnosis of PDAC. Among all, 73.4% patients were diagnosed with tumors of the head region, 7.3% had tumor in the body/tail region, while specific tumor site in the pancreas was unknown for remaining patients. Lymphovascular invasion and perineural invasion were detected in 14.7% and 32.1% tumors, respectively. A total of 33 (30.2%) tumor samples were found to depict organ invasion and metastasis. Among these 60.6% had tumor invasion in duodenum, 15.2% in liver and while remaining depicted invasion and metastasis in multiple organs. Complete data for T stage, N stage, AJCC stage and histological differentiation was unavailable as indicated in Table 2. Consequently, these four variables were excluded from the chi-square test analysis, while survival analysis was conducted using the subset of patients with available data. According to the available data of T staging, the highest frequency was that of T2 stage (31.5%) followed by T1 (16.5%) and T3 stage (11.9%). None of the patients were reported in T4 stage. For N stage, the highest frequency was observed in N0 (22.0%) followed by N1 (20.1%) and N2 stages (10%). In AJCC staging, the majority patients were diagnosed with stage II tumors (39.4%) followed by stage I (9.17%) and stage III (5.50%). Moderately differentiated tumors were the most common in our cohort (56.8%), followed by well differentiated (19.2%) and poorly differentiated tumors (13.7%).

Identification of genetic alterations

A total of 59 genetic variants were found in the four selected genes, out of which 14 (23.7%) were pathogenic variants. A total of 22 (37.2%) Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS) were found. Highest number of VUS (n = 11) were identified in BRCA1, followed by APC (n = 7), KRAS (n = 3) and TP53 (n = 1).

KRAS alterations were observed in 53 patients (48.6%). Among these patients, 39 patients (73.5%) were found to have pathogenic variants. The remaining were non-pathogenic variants that included benign, likely benign, likely pathogenic and VUS. The highest number of different pathogenic variants (n = 8) were detected in KRAS as compared to any other gene in the current study. The most frequently detected was KRAS c.35G > A (p. Gly12Asp) in 20 patients, followed by c.35G > T (p. Gly12Val) and c.38G > A (p. Gly13Asp) detected in eight and three patients respectively. Other pathogenic variants detected were KRAS c.15 A > C (p. Lys5Asn), c.34G > C (p. Gly12Arg), c.35G > C (p. Gly12Arg), c.40G > A (p. Val14Ile) and c.65 A > T (p. Gln22Leu).

TP53 genetic alterations were the most prevalent, detected in 84 patients (77.1%). Among these, 82 patients (97.6%) were identified with pathogenic variants. Another interesting finding was that all these 82 patients were found to carry TP53 c.730G > A pathogenic variant (p. Gly244Ser). Other TP53 pathogenic variants detected were c.731G > A (p. Gly244Asp) and c.742 C > T (p. Arg248Trp) in two patients. Interestingly, both c.731G > A and c.742 C > T were detected together in both the patients.

BRCA1 alterations were identified in 42 patients (38.5%). It was found to be highly altered gene with 23 different alterations being identified. Pathogenic variants found in BRCA1 were c.5509T > C (p. Trp1837Arg) and c.5509T > A (p. Trp1837Arg) detected in three patients each. Moreover, BRCA1 also carried the highest number of VUS (n = 11) and likely benign variants (n = 6).

The least frequently altered was APC gene (36 patients, 33%). Interestingly, it was the only gene bearing frameshift variant. Furthermore, only one type of pathogenic variant APC c.1100_1101delCT (p. Ser367fs) was detected in four patients. The second highest number VUS was identified in APC (n = 7).

Association analyses for co-occurrence of genetic biomarkers, demographics and clinicopathological characteristics

For these analyses, only pathogenic variants were considered to assess associations of clinical relevance (Table 5). The analysis revealed several key associations. The presence of KRAS pathogenic variants showed a marginally significant association with TP53 pathogenic variants (p = 0.090, OR: 2.357, 95% CI: 0.859–6.465). No associations were observed between pathogenic variants of any other genes in the panel. KRAS pathogenic variants showed another marginal significant association with > 40 years’ age group (p = 0.093, OR: 6.333, 95% CI: 0.779–51.482), which might indicate age-related pathogenic effects. Further, KRAS pathogenic variants demonstrated a significant association with detection of perineural invasion (p = 0.018, OR: 3.048, 95% CI: 1.182–7.859), highlighting its potential role in the progression of malignancy. TP53 pathogenic variants were found to possess marginal association with the male gender (p = 0.065, OR: 2.375, 95% CI: 0.935–6.035), indicating a possible gender-specific variation in its pathogenic expression. BRCA1 pathogenic variants were significantly associated with ≤ 40 years’ age group (p = 0.013, OR: 11.875, 95% CI: 2.052–68.709), suggesting age as a relevant factor in BRCA1-driven tumorigenesis.

Mean survival of the patients was 13.63 ± 15.576 (1–81 months). During the study period, 79.8% patients died while remaining were reported to be alive till the last follow-up (November 2023). One-year survival rate of the cohort was found to be 36.6%.

T3 stage (p = 0.015, median survival: 21 months for T1 vs. 11 months for T2 vs. 5 months for T3), N2 stage (p = 0.019, median survival: 20 months for N0 vs. 11 months for N1 vs. 7 months for N2) and poor differentiation of the tumor (p = 0.024, median survival: 11 months for well vs. 9 months for moderate vs. 4 months for poor differentiation) were found to be significantly associated with the shorter overall survival. Patients were categorized into groups based on: (i) the number of genetic alterations, (ii) the number of pathogenic variants, (iii) all possible two-, three-, and four-gene combinations of genetic alterations among the four studied genes (with representative examples such as TP53 and KRAS, TP53 and APC, TP53–APC–BRCA1, KRAS–TP53–APC–BRCA1 and etc.), and (iv) all possible combinations of pathogenic variants. Among these analyses, we observed a highly significant association of overall survival with combined TP53 and KRAS genetic alterations (p = 0.000, median survival: 4 vs. 13 months). Interestingly, when TP53 and KRAS alterations were evaluated individually, neither showed a significant association with overall survival; however, in combination, their effect was highly significant. Moreover, we also found a significant association of overall survival with genetic alterations in more than one gene (p = 0.004, median survival: 8 vs. 13 months).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the molecular biomarkers expression spectrum in the Pakistani PDAC population. Biomarkers selected were based on the findings from the pilot project conducted by our research group [10] therefore, the current investigation also serves as the validation study for the prevalence of the selected genetic biomarkers in the Pakistani population.

Our investigation aligns with patient demographics reported in the previous studies, demonstrating a higher incidence of PDAC detection among males compared to females. In our study, males comprised 55% of the cases, aligning closely with the range of 57–60% documented in other consistent studies [22, 23]. However, it’s noteworthy that a smaller subset of investigations has also reported a higher frequency of PDAC in females, with findings such as 53% females versus 46% males [24]. Moreover, our study reinforces the observation that PDAC is more prevalent in individuals older than 40 years, consistent with existing reports in the literature [22–24]. In the analysis of clinicopathological characteristics, our study supports prevailing trends while also revealing some variations. Consistent with literature [22–24], pancreatic tumors were primarily localized in the head region, representing 73.4% of our study cohort. In literature, poorly differentiated pancreatic tumors are frequently documented as the most prevalent histological differentiation [22, 24]. Conversely, our research revealed that moderately differentiated tumors constituted the highest frequency, accounting for 56.8% of the cases. Furthermore, for pancreatic tumor staging, varying findings are being reported by previous studies. While some studies identify stage II as the predominant stage [1], others indicate stage IV being detected with the highest frequency [23]. Our investigation found stage II to be the most prevalent, detected in 39.4% of patients. Moreover, our cohort diverges from global trends regarding lymphovascular and perineural invasion in PDAC. In contrast to international studies where these characteristics are detected in high frequencies [22], we observed lymphovascular and perineural invasion only in 14.7% and 32.1% of our patients respectively. In comparing the clinicopathological characteristics of pancreatic tumors between our study and previous investigations from Pakistan [25, 26], several similarities and differences are observed. Both studies show a predominance of tumors in the head of the pancreas (73.4% vs. 72%) and a higher percentage of male patients, though the previous study reported more males (70.7% vs. 55%). The mean age at diagnosis was in the similar range, with 56.23 years in our cohort and 55.2 years in the previous cohort. However, significant differences were observed in disease stage and invasiveness. In our cohort, most patients (39.4%) were diagnosed at stage II, while the earlier study reported a higher incidence of advanced-stage disease (66.7% at stage III or IV), suggesting a more severe disease burden. Further, while our study reported lymphovascular and perineural invasion in 14.7% and 32.1% of cases respectively, these factors were not reported in the previous study. It is noteworthy that the current study included PDAC patients from Karachi region (Sindh) while the previous study involved participants from Lahore (Punjab) and Peshawar (KPK). These differences may reflect regional variations in disease presentation, progression, and access to care within Pakistan. These findings also highlight the importance of context-specific analyses in understanding the heterogeneity of PDAC manifestations across geographically distinct populations. Pilot study conducted by our group on Pakistani PDAC population had reported pathogenic variants in KRAS (c.35G >A and c.35G >T), TP53 (c.713G >T), BRCA1 (c.5509 T >C) and APC (c.1100_1101delCT) [10]. Among these, KRAS c.35G >A and c.35G >T, BRCA1 c.5509 T >C and APC c.1100_1101delCT pathogenic variants were detected in the current cohort. While none of the patients were found to possess TP53 c.713G >T variant. However, a range of genetic variants with different clinical significance were found in the targeted regions of all four genes. A total of 59 different genetic variants were detected, out of which 13 (22%) were pathogenic variants. Identified genetic biomarkers were found to have strong association with the demographics, tumor clinicopathological characteristics, co-occurrence of alterations in other genes in the panel and overall survival of the patients.

Globally, the most frequently found KRAS variants in pancreatic cancers are Gly12Asp (G12D) followed by Gly12Val (G12V), Gly12Ala (G12A) and Gly12Ser (G12S). However, among these, G12V is known to be associated with worse patient outcome as compared to any other three variants [27–29]. Consistent with the global findings, the current study also reports c.35G >A (G12D) as the most frequently detected (18.3%) KRAS variant, followed by c.35G >T (G12V) which was detected in 7.3% patients. None of the patients in our cohort were detected with either G12A or G12S variants. Moreover, c.35G >T (G12V) was found to be significantly associated with shorter patient survival (p = 0.022). For KRAS codon 13 variants, Gly13Asp (G13D) is most frequently detected worldwide, followed by Gly13Cys (G13C) [29, 30]. Again, consistent with these reports, c.38G >A (G13D) was detected in 2.7% patients, no other codon 13 variants were found in our cohort. All the mentioned variants are known to constitutively activate the KRAS protein resulting in the oncogenic signaling [31]. Sotorasib and adagrasib are the first KRAS G12C variant targeted drugs to receive US FDA approval for clinical use in NSCLC [31]. Several drugs targeting G12D variants such as HRS-4642 and ASP3082 are currently in phase 1 clinical trials [32]. Identification of target drugs for G12V variant remains a challenge. Recently, a molecule called TUS-007 has shown to simultaneously target G12D and G12V variants through in vitro and in vivo experiments [33]. Furthermore, KRAS variants in pancreatic cancers have also shown positive association with the gemcitabine chemotherapy resistance [11, 34]. The identification of specific KRAS variants in pancreatic cancer has important clinical implications, particularly for prognosis and treatment strategies. Variants such as G12D and G12V are associated with poor patient outcomes, with G12V correlating significantly with shorter survival. The presence of these mutations may also indicate resistance to standard therapies, such as gemcitabine, necessitating alternative treatment approaches. While targeted therapies for G12C variants are now available, the development of effective treatments for G12D and G12V variants remains a critical area of research, with compounds like TUS-007 showing potential in preclinical studies.

All the three TP53 pathogenic variants (c. 730G >A, c. 731G >A and c. 742 C >T) detected in the current study are located in the gene region that codes for DNA binding domain of the p53 protein. As the p53 protein is a transcription factor and plays a vital role in the regulation of several other tumor suppressor genes by interacting with their promoter region therefore its DNA binding domain is very crucial for the activity. Databases show that till date approximately 70,054 variants have been observed in the TP53 DNA binding domain and therefore it is known as the hotspot region of this gene [35]. All the three TP53 pathogenic variants have been previously reported in the literature for various cancers; c. 730G >A has been detected in breast carcinoma [35], Li Fraumeni syndrome [36] and various sarcoma [37], c. 731G >A in oral cancers [38] and c. 742 C >T in breast cancers [35] and Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC) [38]. Studies based on investigation of TP53 gene in Canadian and Chinese pancreatic cancers population are found in the literature, however, none of them have reported any of these pathogenic variants [39, 40].

To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to report TP53 c. 730G >A, c. 731G >A and c. 742 C >T variants in PDAC, globally. We also report TP53 c.730G >A variant in a staggering high percentage of our patients (75.2%, n = 82). These findings render TP53 c. 730G >A, a plausible genetic biomarker in the Pakistani PDAC population. Moreover, we report a significant association between TP53 c.730G >A pathogenic variant and the overall survival (p = 0.001). The longest survival duration with the TP53 c.730G >A variant was 81 months for a 44-year-old female patient. Interestingly this is also the longest survival duration of the overall study cohort. Further, multivariate analyses revealed TP53 c. 730G >A as an independent prognostic marker in the cohort. For the other two pathogenic variants TP53 (c. 731G >A and c. 742 C >T), the overall survival analysis could not be performed since each variant was detected in two patients only. There is limited literature available for survival analysis for these specific TP53 variants in other cancers. Batta N et al., 2019 reports median overall survival and disease-free survival of OSCC patients with TP53 c. 742 C >T to be 47.4 vs. 63 months and 52.6 vs. 77.8 months respectively. However, both survival variables did not show any significant association [38]. Targeted therapies to restore wild type p53 function in pancreatic cancers are currently in clinical trials: SGT-53 and GEM/nab-paclitaxel in clinical trial phase 2 [41]. The high prevalence of the TP53 c.730G >A variant in the Pakistani PDAC population indicates its potential as a valuable genetic biomarker for prognosis and personalized treatment strategies. Its significant association with overall survival underscores the importance of incorporating TP53 mutation profiling into clinical practice.

In addition to our sequencing findings, in silico predictions provided further evidence for the pathogenicity of TP53 c.730G > A (p.Gly244Asp). Both the computational tools (PolyPhen-2 and MutationTaster) consistently classified the variant as disease-causing with high confidence scores. The mutation occurs within the DNA-binding domain of p53, a region essential for its tumor suppressor activity. The substitution of glycine, a small and flexible residue, with aspartic acid, a bulkier and negatively charged residue, is likely to disturb local conformation and weaken p53–DNA interactions. These converging lines of evidence strongly support the functional relevance of this variant and reinforce its potential role as a population-specific biomarker in Pakistani PDAC patients. The ongoing clinical trials aimed at restoring wild type p53 function may provide new therapeutic options for patients harboring TP53 mutations, potentially improving treatment outcomes and survival in this challenging cancer type.

Pancreatic cancers are known to be the third most common cancer to possess germline BRCA1/2 variants [42]. In contrast, the frequency of BRCA1/2 variants in sporadic pancreatic cancer cases is much lower – less than 10% [41]. The BRCA1 c.5509T >C and c.5509T >A pathogenic variants detected in our cohort have been frequently reported in breast and ovarian cancers [43–46]. To the best of knowledge this is the first report of their detection in PDAC, globally. Both the variants lead to replacement of the amino acid tryptophan (neutral and slightly polar in nature) to arginine (basic and polar in nature) on the 1837 position in the BRCA1 protein. This amino acid position lies in the BRCT region which is a functional domain of BRCA1. As a result, the protein loses its structural stability to perform the physiological function [10, 47]. In our cohort, BRCA1 reveals the highest number of VUS; 11 different VUS in 23 (21%) patients. As per the literature, BRCA1 gene is one of those known to be frequently detected with VUS which complicates clinical management. This is because without the proven pathogenicity/susceptibility of the variant, the treatment/prophylactic measures cannot be planned [44]. The Know Your Tumor program conducted investigation to determine the impact of pathogenic variants in homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) such as BRCA1 and other DNA-damage response (DDR) genes on patient survival. The findings revealed that patients with advanced PDAC carrying HR/DDR mutations experienced improved survival, only when treated with platinum chemotherapy (PtCh). In contrast, in patients who had not received PtCh, there was no survival benefit observed [48]. The HRD phenotype associated with BRCA-mutated cancers appears to enhance the sensitivity of PDAC cells to DNA-damaging chemotherapies such as PtCh [49]. Targeted therapies for BRCA germline variants are also widely known. Clinical trials for BRCA1 somatic variants in pancreatic cancers have also started: (a) Maintenance therapy with Rucaparib (PARPi) for advanced stage pancreatic cancers with a known deleterious germline or somatic BRCA1/2 or PALB2 variant and no progression on PtCh and (b) Niraparib (PARPi), with Dostarlimab (immunotherapy) for metastatic pancreatic cancers with germline or somatic BRCA1/2 and PALB2 variants. Both the trials are currently in phase 2 [42].

APC variants are most frequently known to occur in colon cancers, Colorectal Carcinoma (CRC) and Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP). Research studies have indicated either the absence or extremely low occurrence of APC variants in PDAC. These variants are primarily identified when individuals are affected by FAP associated PDAC [50–52]. Nevertheless, literature shows that the occurrence of pancreatic cancer in individuals with FAP is relatively rare throughout their lifetime, estimated at approximately 2%. Two studies published in the 1990 s reported APC variants in sporadic pancreatic cancers [53, 54]. However, the study findings could not be validated by subsequent investigations [11, 55]. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to report APC c.1100_1101delCT pathogenic variant in PDAC, globally. Various drugs targeting Wnt signaling (not specifically APC) are currently under pre-clinical testing. Vanticumab (OMP-18R5) is a mAb tested in gastric cancer mouse model, targets human frizzled receptor (FZD) 1, 2, 5, 7 and 8. Ipafricept (OMP-54F28) targeting FZD-8 has shown tumor inhibitory response in pancreatic cancer mouse model when used in combination with gemcitabine. Tnks inhibitors (XAV939, IWR-1, and G007-LK) have shown positive results in CRC patient derived cell lines possessing APC truncating variants [56]. The clinical implications of the APC c.1100_1101delCT pathogenic variant in PDAC are significant, as this is the first report of its occurrence in this context. While APC variants are primarily associated with CRC and FAP, the current finding suggests a potential role for APC in PDAC tumorigenesis. Investigation of the germline presence of this variant could aid in genetic counseling and risk stratification for patients with familial PDAC. Further, the ongoing development of therapeutic agents targeting the Wnt signaling pathway, particularly in combination with gemcitabine, offers a promising approach for personalized treatment in this subset of patients.

For co-variant status, literature shows that wild type TP53 tumors display a higher variant rate for tumor suppressor genes like BRCA1 [41]. The current cohort showed the co-existence of wild-type TP53 with BRCA1 variants patterns in 3.6% tumors. However, no association was observed with overall survival. Previous investigations in literature also report that the KRAS variants frequently co-exist with TP53 variants in PDAC [31]. In our study, 39% tumors displayed co-existing KRAS/TP53 variants. The co-mutation/haplotype analyses revealed that TP53 and KRAS genetic alterations, when occurring together, were significantly associated with reduced overall survival (p = 0.000), despite neither gene showing prognostic significance individually. This observation underscores a potential synergistic interaction between TP53 and KRAS in driving disease aggressiveness and influencing patient outcomes in PDAC. Beyond this specific pair, we also found that alterations spanning more than one gene were significantly associated with overall survival (p = 0.004), indicating that cumulative or cooperative effects of multiple variants, pathogenic or otherwise; may modulate prognosis. These findings highlight the importance of multi-gene variant assessments and support their clinical utility in refining prognostic stratification and potentially guiding therapeutic decision-making in PDAC.

Overall, this study provides an extensive insight on the genomic biomarker spectrum of PDAC patients in the Pakistani population. However, it is important to highlight study limitations. First, using publicly available databases such as ClinVar and COSMIC, though well-established, may introduce potential bias by overlooking underrepresented or population-specific variants. Incorporating additional variant databases and functional assays in future research would also strengthen the reliability and clinical relevance of the results. Further, the retrospective design was used in the study. To address this limitation, future prospective and multicentric studies are recommended to validate the findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of population-specific biomarkers in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. A key limitation of this study is the absence of matched normal controls, either adjacent non-tumorous tissue or blood from healthy individuals. This was mainly due to the use of biopsy specimens and diffuse tumor involvement in surgical cases. Although TP53 c.730G > A is an established pathogenic variant, the lack of normal controls may limit the ability to comprehensively validate findings in the local population and to clearly distinguish between somatic and germline events. Nevertheless, this study provides the first evidence of the exceptionally high frequency and clinical association of TP53 c.730G > A in a large local cohort, offering valuable insights into its potential as a population-specific biomarker. Future studies incorporating normal controls will be important to further strengthen and extend these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study offers a comprehensive analysis of the genomic biomarker landscape in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients within the Pakistani population. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to report TP53 (c. 730G > A, c. 731G > A and c. 742 C > T), BRCA1 (c.5509T > C and c.5509T > A) and APC c.1100_1101delCT pathogenic variants in PDAC, globally. Moreover, the detection of pathogenic variant TP53 c.730G > A is exceptionally high in our patients. Further, TP53 c. 730G > A was detected as an independent prognostic marker in the cohort. These findings demonstrate that TP53 c. 730G > A may be a plausible genetic biomarker in the Pakistani PDAC population. It also indicates unique characteristics of the population in this specific geographical region as compared to global data. However, further larger and multi-center studies are required to validate TP53 c. 730G > A as a genetic biomarker in the Pakistani PDAC population. Such investigations hold significant potential for identification of clinically relevant target biomarkers for prognosis prediction and selection of tailored molecular therapies for these patients.
