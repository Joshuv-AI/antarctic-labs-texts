Dear Editor,

Oral cavity cancers are usually preceded by oral premalignant lesions (OPL), mainly being oral leukoplakia and erythroplakia1. It is challenging to manage the OPL progression to oral cancer, which is a multistep malignant process. From a clinician perspective, the primary outcome measure of all the interventions for OPL should be to prevent the malignant transformation, in order to reduce the cancer morbidity and mortality and to reduce burden on healthcare expenditures. Secondary outcome measures include safety data including intraoperative and postoperative parameters (e.g. bleeding, pain, swelling, healing time, and postoperative fibrosis and scars). Depending on lesion nature and risk factors, the current treatments for OPL can range from careful observation to surgical intervention. A wide range of nonsurgical and surgical treatments have been reported, but there is no consensus on which is the best option1.

Cryosurgery is a method that uses extremely low temperature (up to minus 196°C) to freeze the tissue rapidly, which destroys the original cell structure of lesion tissues in situ by either a ‘closed’ or an ‘open’ system2. Tissues destruction contain disruption of the cell membrane, cell swelling and rupture, cellular dehydration, enzyme and protein damage, thermal shock injury to cells, damage to the vasculature. As a simple, safe, time-saving, and cost-effective surgical treatment, cryosurgery also has several advantages including low bleeding, a very low incidence of secondary infections, and a relative absence of pain and scars2. The presence of edema and swelling in patients received cryosurgery are evaluated to be more serious than other surgical interventions3. Generally, secondary outcome measures of cryosurgery for OPL are superior to other surgical interventions. However, there are sparse data on the primary outcome measure of cryosurgery for OPL malignant transformation.

In 2014, a review article by Yu et al.4 described the mechanisms of tissue destruction by cryosurgery including tissue and histological changes after cryosurgery, and summarized the studies evaluating the efficacies of close- and open-system cryosurgery treatments on oral precancers. In 2015, delightedly, Chen and colleagues reported a retrospective study on cryosurgery for 72 oral leukoplakia and adjacent smoking-induced melanosis lesions on the buccal mucosa, and found that there was no malignant transformation of the treated lesions during 3–41 (mean, 18) months of follow-up5. The studies2,5–10 on cryosurgery for OPL with follow-up data are summarized in Table 1. In 2016, a Cochrane updated systematic review reported that surgical interventions, including cryosurgery, have never been studied by means of a randomized controlled trial that included a no treatment or placebo arm, and concluded that there was no evidence of an effective treatment to prevent malignant transformation of oral leukoplakia11.

Malignant transformation occurs in a fraction of patients with OPL, and if it occurs, it often can take many years. As such, a well-designed interventional study using malignant transformation as an endpoint must be of adequate sample size and follow-up data to capture the malignant events (typically>5 years)1. One strategy to reduce the sample size and follow-up is to enrich the study subjects at higher risk for malignant development. Risk stratification including subjects with high-grade dysplasia, nonhomogeneous lesions, and lateral/ventral tongue is one such strategy, owing to the higher risk for malignant development in these patients. After all, the retrospective study by Chen and colleagues only recruited 8 (11.1%) high-grade (7 moderate, 1 severe) dysplasia of 72 buccal lesions possibly without a sufficient follow-up period and final clinical endpoints5. Notably, Saito et al. 7 earlier reported a retrospective study on cryosurgery for oral leukoplakia of 16 cases (7 non, 8 mild-, 1 moderate- dysplasia), and found that 4 (25%) developed into oral cancer within 3–16 (mean, 9) years. Meanwhile, Saito et al. 7 found that only 1 (1.3%) of 75 oral leukoplakia patients treated by surgical excision developed into oral cancer at 8 years of follow-up, and concluded that surgical excision but not cryosurgery may reduce the risk of malignant development.

It is increasingly accepted that high-risk OPL, for example, severe dysplasia, received surgery should obtain the safety surgical margins, which theoretically reduces the risk of the recurrence and malignant transformation12. Cryosurgery destroys tissues by freeze thawing them, which cause that no adequate sample leaves for further accurate histology and the margins are ruptured1. It is actually unknown whether the width and depth of the margin, because this method often lacks the margin control. To our dismay, Pospisil and MacDonald earlier conducted an animal experiment involving cryosurgery for OPL part of a carcinogen 7,12-dimethlybenz(a)anthracene treated hamster cheek pouch13. The results of this experiment suggested that cryosurgery for dysplastic lesions might potentiate the subsequent development of further dysplastic lesions or of neoplasms13. Subsequently, they demonstrated that the tumor potentiating effect of cryosurgery in hamster cheek pouch may be due to the regenerative stimulus where this involved premalignant epithelium rather than being a specific complication of the cryosurgery per se 14. These reflect from one aspect the importance of safety surgical margins.

Collectively, the effectiveness of cryosurgery for OPL in reducing the risk of malignant transformation is questionable due to the lack of adequate clinical trials. On the basis of the above observations, it would appear that although cryosurgery for OPL gives very satisfactory results initially. Whether it is possible that it may render the tissue adjacent to treated areas susceptible to the development of further dysplastic lesions or to malignant transformation is not addressed. In order to determine whether or not this is a particular disadvantage of cryosurgery, well-designed prospective studies with prolonged follow-up of clinical cases will be required.
