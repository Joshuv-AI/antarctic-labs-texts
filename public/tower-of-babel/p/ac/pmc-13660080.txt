Effects of hypoxic high-intensity interval training on cardiorespiratory fitness, body composition, and cardiometabolic outcomes in non-clinical populations: a systematic review and meta-analysis with an age-related evidence distribution assessment
Authors: Wantong Gao, Pengwang Zhao, Zhaotong Ma, Jizu Shi, Yi Pan, Hao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypoxic high-intensity interval training (HHIIT) has been proposed as a time-efficient strategy for enhancing physiological adaptations. However, its efficacy in non-clinical populations remains inconsistent. This systematic review and three-level meta-analysis followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase were searched from database inception to 20 December 2025. Fourteen randomized controlled trials, including 534 participants, met the eligibility criteria. HHIIT produced a significant, small positive effect on overall cardiorespiratory fitness outcomes (Hedges’ g = 0.369, 95% CI [0.186, 0.552], P < 0.001), and the results remained robust following CR2 small-sample correction. For relative oxygen uptake, HHIIT showed a moderate beneficial effect (g = 0.553, 95% CI [0.308, 0.797], P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses indicated that the effects of HHIIT were larger when compared with non-exercise controls than with exercise controls. n contrast, HHIIT had no significant overall effect on body composition or cardiometabolic outcomes (g = − 0.018, 95% CI [− 0.133, 0.097]) or on hematological outcomes (g = 0.122, 95% CI [− 0.292, 0.535]). The current evidence base is concentrated mainly in young adults, with limited representation of females, middle-aged adults, and older adults. In conclusion, low-to-moderate certainty evidence suggests that HHIIT may provide limited, modality-specific improvements in cardiopulmonary adaptations in non-clinical populations, particularly in relative oxygen uptake. However, substantial intervention heterogeneity warrants cautious interpretation of these findings. Its effects on body composition, cardiometabolic health, and hematological parameters remain unclear. Future trials should standardize hypoxic dose and training prescriptions, while addressing existing age- and sex-related evidence gaps. These efforts are needed to clarify the generalizability and practical value of HHIIT across broader populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01844-0.

Introduction

Oxygen (O₂) is a fundamental substrate for sustaining biological function [1]. A reduction in environmental or systemic oxygen availability, termed “hypoxia”, imposes substantial physiological stress. When sustained or uncontrolled, hypoxia is strongly associated with metabolic dysregulation and diverse pathological processes [2, 3]. In contrast, controlled intermittent hypoxia can elicit adaptive physiological responses. Within structured training programmes, the combined stimuli of hypoxia and exercise may promote systemic adaptations and enhance exercise performance. In sports science, hypoxia is typically defined as reduced oxygen availability caused by a decrease in barometric pressure (PB), the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂), or both [4, 5]. This reduction disrupts the oxygen cascade and triggers compensatory increases in ventilation and adjustments in circulatory regulation [6, 7]. At the cellular level, hypoxia induces adaptive changes in energy metabolism and oxygen transport. These changes involve processes such as erythropoiesis and metabolic reprogramming [8–10]. Collectively, these systemic and cellular adaptations provide the physiological rationale for using hypoxic exposure as a training intervention [1].

For decades, the integration of exercise training and hypoxic exposure has established several classical paradigms in training practice, including live high–train high (LHTH), live high–train low (LHTL), and live low–train high (LLTH) [11]. With the evolution of training paradigms, high-intensity interval training (HIIT) has offered enhanced time efficiency and physiological stimuli compared to traditional continuous training; when combined with hypoxia, this composite stimulus is postulated to further augment training adaptations [12, 13]. In this review, HIIT is broadly defined to encompass both traditional high-intensity interval training and sprint interval training (SIT), which is characterized by “all-out” efforts [14]. When applied in hypoxic environments, this training paradigm includes interval hypoxic training (IHT) and repeated sprint training in hypoxia (RSH). In the present review, these modalities are integrated into a unified analytical framework and collectively termed hypoxic high-intensity interval training (HHIIT). However, although both IHT and RSH are forms of intermittent training, they differ systematically in intensity anchoring, work-interval duration, and recovery structure. These differences may contribute to inter-study heterogeneity [15]. Therefore, while the present study pooled these modalities in the primary analysis, subgroup analyses or meta-regressions were pre-specified to examine the potential impact of variations in training prescriptions [16].

Driven by advancements in hypoxic equipment and expanding training paradigms, HHIIT is increasingly applied in non-clinical and health-promotion settings as a non-pharmacological intervention for overweight, obesity, and related metabolic abnormalities [17–19]. Despite growing interest in hypoxic interval trainingand previous research on its cardiopulmonary and metabolic benefits [20–22], the true efficacy of HHIIT in non-athletic and non-clinical populations remains highly controversial. Current evidence shows substantial heterogeneity in hypoxic dose quantification, training prescriptions, and control conditions [19], Consequently, interpreting overall effects is complicated by protocol discrepancies and baseline participant characteristics. Furthermore, the age distribution in current randomized controlled trials is narrow, predominantly featuring young to middle-aged adults [23]. This limited demographic representation indicates that the applicability of HHIIT, particularly to older non-clinical populations, warrants further validation [24].

By applying stringent inclusion criteria, this study aims to evaluate the effects of HHIIT on cardiorespiratory fitness, body composition, and cardiometabolic and hematological outcomes in non-clinical populations. Additionally, we explored whether intervention characteristics and participant factors modulate these effect sizes, while mapping the age distribution of the current evidence base [25].

Materials and methods

Study design and registration

This meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement (Page et al., 2021) [16].Furthermore, this systematic review was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration number: CRD420251273810).

Search strategy

A systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase was conducted from database inception to December 20, 2025. To identify relevant literature on the cardiometabolic and body composition effects of HHIIT in non-clinical populations, the following search terms were combined using Boolean operators: (“High-Intensity Interval Training” OR “HIIT” OR “Interval Training” OR “Sprint Interval Training” OR “SIT” OR “Repeated-sprint training” OR “RSH”) AND (“Hypoxia” OR “Hypoxic” OR “Altitude” OR “Simulated altitude” OR “Normobaric” OR “Hypobaric” OR “FiO2”) AND (“Body composition” OR “BMI” OR “waist circumference” OR “body fat” OR “fat mass” OR “fat-free mass” OR “cardiorespiratory fitness” OR “VO2max” OR “VO2peak” OR “blood pressure” OR “glucose” OR “insulin” OR “HOMA-IR” OR “triglycerides” OR “cholesterol” OR “metabolic syndrome” OR “MetS”). The search was restricted to English-language, peer-reviewed articles involving human subjects. To identify additional eligible studies, a snowballing strategy was applied by screening the reference lists of included articles, tracking forward citations, and identifying related articles.

Study selection

Study eligibility was determined a priori according to the PICOS framework.

Population (P)

Non-clinical populations of any age, encompassing healthy active, sedentary, and overweight or obese individuals. Overweight and obesity were defined using WHO criteria (overweight: BMI 25–29.9; obesity: BMI > 30) or age- and sex-specific percentiles for adolescents [26].

Intervention (I)

(HHIIT) conducted in either artificial normobaric hypoxic chambers or natural high-altitude environments. Hypoxia was defined as an altitude > 2000 m or a fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) < 16.4% [15, 27]. Eligible exercise intensities included: peak/maximal heart rate (HRpeak/HRmax) ≥ 80% [17, 28], peak/maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂peak/VO₂max) ≥ 80%, a rating of perceived exertion (RPE) ≥ 14, or explicitly described “all-out” efforts. The protocol required repeated high-intensity bouts interspersed with passive rest or active recovery, with a minimum intervention duration of 4 weeks.

Comparison (C)

(1) Non-exercising controls maintaining habitual lifestyles; or (2) normoxic exercise training, including either moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT; ~55–75% HRmax) or normoxic HIIT.

Outcome (O)

Eligible outcomes encompassed: (1) Cardiorespiratory fitness: relative and absolute VO₂, VO₂max/VO₂peak, ventilatory threshold indices, HRmax, HRmean, and resting HR (HRrest); (2) Body composition: BMI, waist circumference, body fat percentage, fat mass, and fat-free mass; (3) Cardiometabolic markers: SBP, DBP, fasting glucose, insulin, HOMA-IR, TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDL-C; and (4) Hematological markers: Hb, Hct, RBC, EPO, and other erythropoietic indices.

Study design (S)

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they involved: (1) Clinical populations with chronic metabolic diseases requiring pharmacotherapy (e.g., type 2 diabetes), cardiovascular diseases, cancer, or severe musculoskeletal injuries; (2) Isolated hypoxic exposure without concurrent exercise; (3) Blood flow restriction (BFR) training; (4) Multi-modal interventions precluding the isolation of HHIIT effects; (5) Non-randomized, pre-post, or crossover designs lacking extractable first-phase data; or (6) Non-original research (e.g., reviews, abstracts, editorials, animal studies).

Literature screening

Duplicate records were removed using EndNote X9. Two investigators (GWT and MZT) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the retrieved literature based on the pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria, utilizing Zotero software (version 8.0.x; Corporation for Digital Scholarship, Vienna, VA, USA). Any discrepancies during the screening process were resolved through consensus discussion. If an agreement could not be reached, a third investigator (YP) was consulted for final adjudication regarding study inclusion. Subsequently, a full-text review was conducted following the identical procedure.

Terminology and intervention categorization

This review adopts “HHIIT” as an umbrella term for all high-intensity interval training interventions conducted under hypoxia. Specific modalities included under this term are traditional hypoxic high-intensity interval training (HHIIT), hypoxic sprint interval training (SIH), and hypoxic repeated-sprint training (RSH). Although these modalities share intermittent hypoxic exposure, they elicit structurally distinct physiological stimuli. To avoid confounding interpretations across different comparative frameworks, primary data were pooled by control group type (i.e., HHIIT vs. normoxic training, and HHIIT vs. non-exercising controls). Additionally, training modality was defined a priori as a variable for exploratory subgroup analysis to assess its impact on outcomes, contingent upon sufficient study availability and adequate heterogeneity.

Data extraction and transformation

Two investigators (GWT and YP) independently extracted data using a customized Excel form developed a priori. Extracted data encompassed fundamental study details (e.g., first author, publication year, study design), participant demographics, sample sizes, and control conditions. Intervention protocols (training frequency, intensity, duration, interval structure) and relevant outcomes (BMI, body fat percentage, VO₂max/VO₂peak, SBP, DBP) were also recorded. A third investigator (ZPW) cross-checked the data. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus, with ZPW adjudicating any remaining disagreements.

For missing or incomplete data, the original authors were contacted via email. If no response was received but the necessary data were presented graphically, numerical values were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer version 4.1. Studies for which data were extracted graphically were explicitly noted in the text.

When studies provided pre- and post-intervention means and dispersion measures rather than exact change scores, the standard deviation of the change score (SDchange) was calculated according to the Cochrane Handbook.

Risk of bias and methodological quality

Two reviewers (GWT and MZT) independently evaluated the methodological quality of the included trials using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [30]. A third reviewer (YP) resolved any discrepancies. The RoB 2 framework assesses bias across five domains: (1) the randomization process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) outcome measurement, and (5) selection of the reported result. Based on the tool’s signalling questions, each domain and the overall risk of bias were rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk.” For multiple publications originating from a single trial, assessments were conducted at the trial-cluster level for outcomes included in the meta-analysis.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The certainty of evidence for each primary outcome was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Evidence from randomized controlled trials initially received a “high” certainty rating, which was downgraded, if applicable, based on five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Final certainty was classified as “high,” “moderate,” “low,” or “very low” [31]. Two reviewers (GWT and YP) independently conducted the GRADE assessments, with a third reviewer (MZT) verifying the results.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.6.0) using the metafor, meta, and clubSandwich packages. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P < 0.05. Continuous outcomes were evaluated using the standardized mean difference (Hedges’ g) to correct for small-sample bias. Effect sizes were interpreted as trivial (< 0.20), small (0.20–0.49), moderate (0.50–0.79), or large (≥ 0.80) [32]. To account for effect size dependency arising from identical or overlapping samples [33], a three-level random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation was employed [34]. Cluster-robust variance estimation with CR2 small-sample correction was applied at the study level to compute robust standard errors [35, 36].

In the overall model, outcomes were categorized into broader domains (e.g., “cardiorespiratory fitness”) to assess macro-level trends. To prevent these aggregated estimates from obscuring specific physiological responses, clinical directionality was strictly evaluated at the individual-outcome level [37]. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (P < 0.05) and the I² statistic. I² values were classified as low (< 25%), low-to-moderate (25%–50%), moderate-to-substantial (50%–75%), or substantial (> 75%). The overall I², variance distribution across levels, and 95% prediction intervals were reported where appropriate.

Moderator analyses (subgroup comparisons and meta-regression) were strictly exploratory to prevent overinterpretation. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate the robustness of the pooled results [38]. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test (requiring ≥ 10 studies). For outcomes with fewer studies, funnel plots served solely as visual aids [29]. If bias was detected, the trim-and-fill method was applied as a sensitivity analysis [38].

Results

Search results and overview

As shown in Fig. 1, this meta-analysis systematically searched four databases to ensure comprehensive coverage, yielding 10,922 records. After three stages of screening, 14 articles were included in the final review [11, 39–51]. Several reports related to Camacho-Cardenosa showed substantial overlap in research team, sample source, intervention protocol, and baseline characteristics; therefore, they were treated as the same study cluster in the quantitative analysis to avoid double-counting participants [41, 52–54]. One additional report primarily focused on follow-up outcomes after detraining and was not included in the primary analysis of immediate post-intervention effects [52].

Risk-of-bias assessment

The risk of bias in the included randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool (Fig. 2). Overall, most studies were judged as having “some concerns,” whereas a smaller number were rated as being at “high risk” of bias. At the domain level, risk of bias was mainly concentrated in the randomization process (D1), deviations from the intended interventions (D2), and selection of the reported result (D5). In contrast, the proportions of studies judged as having a low risk of bias were relatively higher for missing outcome data (D3) and measurement of the outcome (D4). These findings indicate that the included studies had an overall degree of methodological concern, and the subsequent results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Results of the systematic review and meta-analysis

Effects of HHIIT on cardiorespiratory fitness outcomes

The overall analysis included 12 studies and 67 effect sizes (Fig. 3). HHIIT was associated with a small positive effect on cardiorespiratory fitness-related outcomes (g = 0.369, 95% CI [0.186, 0.552], p < 0.001). This result remained statistically significant after CR2 small-sample robust correction (95% CI [0.164, 0.574], p = 0.0027). Heterogeneity was low to moderate, Q(66) = 87.91, p = 0.037, with a total I² of 29.54%. The proportions of variance attributable to within-study effect-size-level heterogeneity and between-study heterogeneity were 14.85% and 14.69%, respectively. The 95% prediction interval ranged from − 0.240 to 0.978. At the individual outcome level, VO₂rel and several ventilatory-threshold-related outcomes generally exhibited positive effects, whereas heart-rate-related outcomes showed relatively inconsistent directions of change (Fig. 3).

Subgroup analyses indicated that the effect of HHIIT on cardiorespiratory fitness was greater when compared with non-exercise controls (g = 0.57, 95% CI [0.34, 0.80], p < 0.001). When compared with exercise controls, the effect remained statistically significant but was smaller in magnitude (g = 0.22, 95% CI [0.08, 0.37], p = 0.004). Control condition was significantly associated with differences in effect size, F(1,65) = 6.36, p = 0.014 (Supplementary Appendix S4, Fig. 2). By contrast, between-group differences according to training modality, training status, sex, and hypoxic exposure mode were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05). Within individual subgroups, significant positive effects were observed for HIIT (g = 0.50, 95% CI [0.15, 0.86], p = 0.006), Tier 0 participants (g = 0.45, 95% CI [0.19, 0.71], p = 0.001), male participants (g = 0.47, 95% CI [0.20, 0.74], p < 0.001), and normobaric hypoxic interventions (g = 0.39, 95% CI [0.19, 0.59], p < 0.001) (Supplementary Appendix S4, Fig. 3). Given that the corresponding between-group differences were not statistically significant, these within-subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Exploratory meta-regression analyses indicated that age, intervention duration, and weekly training frequency were nominally associated with the effects of HHIIT on cardiorespiratory fitness. Age and intervention duration were negatively associated with effect size (age: β = −0.014, p = 0.018; intervention duration: β = −0.047, p = 0.022), whereas weekly training frequency was positively associated with effect size (β = 0.194, p = 0.031). Weekly training duration and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) showed no nominally significant associations. Given the limited number of independent studies and the absence of adjustment for multiple testing, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating (Supplementary Appendix S4, Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analyses showed that the pooled effect size ranged from 0.313 to 0.410 after sequentially excluding each study, and the results remained statistically significant in all cases (all p < 0.01). The I² values ranged from 0% to 16.3%. Egger’s regression test did not indicate significant asymmetry in the distribution of effect sizes (p = 0.178; Supplementary Appendix S4, Figures. 5 and 6).

A separate model for the primary outcome VO₂rel included 11 studies and 25 effect sizes (Fig. 4). HHIIT was associated with a moderate improvement in VO₂rel (g = 0.553, 95% CI [0.308, 0.797], p < 0.001). The result remained consistent after CR2 small-sample robust correction (95% CI [0.284, 0.822], p = 0.00125). Heterogeneity was low, Q(24) = 22.34, p = 0.559, with a total I² of 18.99%. The 95% prediction interval ranged from 0.029 to 1.077. Owing to the limited number of included studies, no further exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted for this outcome.

Effects of HHIIT on body composition and cardiometabolic outcomes

A total of 11 studies comprising 78 effect sizes were included in this analysis (Fig. 5). The overall analysis showed no statistically significant effect of HHIIT on body composition and cardiometabolic outcomes (g = − 0.018, 95% CI [− 0.133, 0.097], p = 0.756). This result remained unchanged after CR2 small-sample robust correction (g = − 0.018, 95% CI [− 0.163, 0.127], p = 0.771). Heterogeneity was low, Q(77) = 40.03, p = 0.9998, with a total I² of 3.25%. The 95% PI [− 0.212 − 0.176].For individual outcomes, SBP showed a decreasing trend (g = − 0.25, 95% CI [− 0.53, 0.03], p = 0.083). WC and body fat percentage also showed slight decreasing trends, although these effects did not reach statistical significance. FBG, BMI, DBP, LDL-C, TG, TC, and HDL-C showed no clear statistical effects.

Publication bias

Following Cochrane Handbook guidelines, visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression tests were conducted for eligible core outcomes. These included overall cardiorespiratory fitness (k = 12), relative maximal/peak oxygen uptake (VO₂rel; k = 11), and body composition and metabolic outcomes (k = 11) (Fig. 8). Neither visual inspection nor Egger’s test revealed significant asymmetry in effect size distributions across these primary outcomes (P > 0.05), indicating no clear evidence of small-study effects or publication bias. However, given the small number of included studies, the possibility of undetected publication bias or small-study effects cannot be entirely ruled out. For secondary domains with fewer than 10 studies (e.g., blood-related outcomes), funnel plots and bias tests were deemed strictly exploratory. Consequently, they served as descriptive references rather than definitive evidence against publication bias.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for primary outcomes was evaluated using the GRADE approach (Table 2). Moderate-certainty evidence supported the beneficial effects of HHIIT on overall cardiorespiratory fitness and VO₂rel. Similarly, moderate-certainty evidence indicated no significant overall effect of HHIIT on body composition and cardiometabolic outcomes. While this finding remained robust across sensitivity analyses, pooling diverse parameters (e.g., blood pressure, lipids, glucose, and adiposity indices) within this domain introduced inherent clinical heterogeneity. For hematological markers, evidence certainty was downgraded to low due to limited study inclusion and wide confidence intervals, revealing no significant treatment effect. In summary, current evidence substantiates the efficacy of HHIIT in enhancing cardiorespiratory fitness, specifically VO₂rel. Conversely, its impacts on body composition, cardiometabolic health, and hematological profiles remain inconclusive and warrant further high-quality trials.

Discussion

Evidence summary

This systematic review and three-level meta-analysis suggests that the intervention effects of HHIIT may be outcome-specific.Low-to-moderate certainty evidence supports improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness, particularly relative oxygen uptake (VO₂ or VO₂rel). However, consistent benefits were not observed for body composition, cardiometabolic profiles, or hematological markers. he external validity of these findings remains limited. Most included studies focused primarily on young adults and showed substantial heterogeneity in training prescriptions, hypoxic dose, and control conditions. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence within the HHIIT framework rather than as definitive evidence supporting a standardized training prescription.

Effects of HHIIT on cardiorespiratory fitness in non-clinical populations

Studies have shown that cardiorespiratory fitness is closely associated with exercise performance and is an important predictor of cardiovascular disease risk and all-cause mortality. Oxygen uptake is widely regarded as a core indicator of cardiorespiratory fitness. Ventilatory threshold reflects the transition between aerobic and anaerobic metabolism and indicates sustained exercise capacity during submaximal exercise. Heart rate-related indicators reflect cardiovascular load and regulatory responses during exercise [56]. Therefore, in this review, VO₂rel, VO₂abs, ventilatory threshold, and heart rate-related indicators were classified as cardiopulmonary function-related outcomes to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the effects of HHIIT on cardiorespiratory fitness.

Compared with control conditions, HHIIT showed a significant pooled effect on overall cardiorespiratory fitness outcomes (g = 0.369, 95% CI [0.186, 0.552], P < 0.001). This finding suggests a small-to-moderate positive effect on composite indicators of cardiorespiratory fitness in non-clinical populations. At the individual-outcome level, the positive effect on VO₂rel appeared consistent. However, findings for outcomes such as ventilatory threshold should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of studies. In contrast, heart rate-related outcomes, including HRmax, HRmean, and HRrest, did not show favourable changes. These results suggest that cardiorespiratory fitness following HHIIT may be more readily reflected by oxygen uptake-related outcomes than by heart rate-related outcomes [20]. However, the current evidence is insufficient to determine whether these observed improvements are primarily driven by central cardiovascular adaptations, enhanced peripheral oxygen extraction, or improved metabolic efficiency.

Exploratory subgroup analyses showed that HHIIT produced more pronounced improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness when compared with non-exercise controls. However, when compared with exercise controls, the effect was only small despite reaching nominal statistical significance (g = 0.22). The beneficial effects of HHIIT should not be interpreted solely on the basis of p values. Instead, they should be evaluated in relation to effect size, comparator type, and practical relevance [57]. Therefore, HHIIT may offer advantages over no structured exercise intervention, but its additional benefits over conventional exercise training appear limited.

A recent meta-analysis suggested that HHIIT may be superior to normoxic high-intensity interval training in improving VO₂max [20]. However, previous studies have largely used VO₂max as the primary outcome for evaluating HHIIT [58, 59]. Comprehensive assessments of ventilatory threshold, heart rate responses, and other oxygen uptake-related outcomes remain limited. Nevertheless, VO₂-related outcomes, ventilatory threshold, and heart rate-related outcomes reflect different aspects of cardiopulmonary adaptation. Pooling these outcomes may therefore obscure response differences across outcome domains.Traditional HHIIT typically involves longer submaximal intervals, which may impose greater stress on central cardiopulmonary responses [15]. By contrast, SIH and RSH involve very high-intensity or “all-out” efforts of short duration and may be more strongly influenced by peripheral skeletal muscle adaptations, including phosphocreatine resynthesis, muscle buffering capacity, oxidative capacity, and neuromuscular factors [60].

Interpreting pooled cardiopulmonary adaptations without distinguishing between central and peripheral mechanisms may reduce the physiological clarity of the current evidence. Overall, HHIIT may provide clearer cardiorespiratory fitness benefits compared with non-exercise interventions. However, its additional advantage over normoxic or conventional exercise training appears small and should be interpreted cautiously in light of intervention modality, outcome selection, and certainty of evidence.

Exploratory meta-regression analyses identified nominal associations between the estimated effect sizes for cardiorespiratory fitness and age (β = −0.014, p = 0.018), weekly training frequency (β = 0.194, p = 0.031), and intervention duration (β = −0.047, p = 0.022). Weekly training duration was not associated with effect size (β = 0.002, p = 0.279). FiO₂ (β = −0.072, p = 0.159), providing no clear support for a hypoxic dose–response relationship. However, these exploratory findings were based on a limited number of independent study clusters, and the nominal p values were not adjusted for multiple testing. Therefore, they should not be interpreted as robust moderator effects or mechanistic evidence, but rather as hypothesis-generating signals for future research.

From a mechanistic perspective, the potential effects of HHIIT on cardiorespiratory fitness may involve several processes. These include hypoxia-related molecular pathways, peripheral vascular adaptations, and improved oxygen utilization efficiency. The hypoxic stimulus superimposed on exercise may increase the mismatch between tissue oxygen supply and demand. This mismatch may activate physiological responses related to hypoxic adaptation, angiogenesis, and mitochondrial regulation [61–64]. High-intensity interval exercise can also promote cardiorespiratory adaptations by imposing substantial cardiopulmonary load and stimulating skeletal muscle metabolism [15]. Therefore, the cardiorespiratory benefits observed after HHIIT may reflect the combined effects of exercise intensity and hypoxic stimulation rather than a single underlying mechanism.

The synthesis of multiple cardiorespiratory fitness outcomes suggests that HHIIT may represent a promising but not yet established training strategy for improving cardiorespiratory health in non-clinical populations. The observed benefits should not be attributed solely to hypoxic exposure. These improvements may partly reflect the general effects of high-intensity interval training, with hypoxic exposure providing only a modest additional adaptive stimulus. Given the heterogeneity in training modality, hypoxic dose, training load, and outcome selection, rigorously controlled studies are needed to determine whether HHIIT provides meaningful incremental benefits over normoxic high-intensity interval training.

Effects of HHIIT on body composition and cardiometabolic outcomes in non-clinical populations

The overall analysis showed that HHIIT did not produce statistically significant additional improvements in body composition or cardiometabolic indicators, and the pooled effect size was close to zero (g = − 0.018, 95% CI [− 0.133, 0.097], p = 0.756). In this study, body composition, blood glucose, blood lipids, and blood pressure were grouped into the same outcome domain based primarily on the cardiometabolic risk framework. These indicators are commonly used to characterize metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular health risk and can help assess whether HHIIT produces consistent improvements across different health risk indicators [65, 66]. However, these indicators reflect partly distinct physiological dimensions. Therefore, pooling these indicators in a combined analysis may obscure outcome-specific differences in response. At the level of individual indicators, the directions of effect for blood glucose, blood lipids, blood pressure, and body composition-related outcomes were inconsistent, and most confidence intervals crossed the line of no effect. These findings suggest that current evidence is insufficient to support a stable and consistent beneficial effect of HHIIT on body composition and cardiometabolic outcomes.

This finding may be partly explained by the baseline characteristics of the participants included in the studies. In the included studies, most participants were young adults aged 20–30 years. These populations generally have relatively low baseline metabolic risk, with BMI, blood glucose, blood lipids, and blood pressure mostly within the normal range. Therefore, the scope for detectable improvement after relatively short-term training may be limited. Although some studies included overweight or obese participants, both the number of relevant studies and their sample sizes were limited. This limitation makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions regarding populations with high metabolic risk A meta-analysis by Guo et al. in overweight and obese populations showed that normobaric hypoxic training did not confer significant additional benefits over normoxic training for blood glucose, triglycerides, BMI, or waist-to-hip ratio [67]. Liu et al., after including more randomized controlled trials, also found that although hypoxic training may reduce body fat percentage, its overall effects on glucose and lipid metabolism indicators were similar to those of normoxic training [68]. However, these studies examined hypoxic exercise training in a broader sense, including continuous or moderate-intensity interventions, and therefore cannot be directly equated with HHIIT. Accordingly, the findings of the present study should be interpreted as follows: within the current evidence base for HHIIT, consistent signals of improvement remain lacking for composite outcomes related to body composition, glucose and lipid metabolism, and blood pressure.

Across the explored subgroups, there was no clear evidence that the effects of HHIIT on body composition or metabolism-related outcomes differed consistently. From a physiological perspective, hypoxic exposure may influence body composition and metabolism-related phenotypes through changes in fluid balance, energy metabolism, and appetite regulation [69–73]. Hypoxic training may also affect body composition and glucose and lipid metabolism through pathways involving inflammatory responses, glucose homeostasis, HIF-1α-related signalling, and substrate oxidation [74].

However, within the current evidence base, these potential mechanisms have not yet been translated into consistent improvements in body composition, glycaemic control, lipid metabolism, or blood pressure. the current findings do not provide clear evidence that HHIIT confers additional benefits for body composition or cardiometabolic outcomes. A recent network meta-analysis in overweight or obese populations suggested that the potential advantage of HHIIT may be more evident for VO₂max, whereas combined hypoxic aerobic and resistance training may be more favourable for improving BMI, fat mass, LDL-C, and blood pressure [75]. These findings suggest that different hypoxic exposure mode may be associated with distinct outcome profiles. Accordingly, although incorporating HIIT, SIH, RSH, and IHT into a unified HHIIT framework facilitates overall evidence synthesis, it may also attenuate the ability to detect modality-specific effects on body composition and cardiometabolic outcomes. Future studies should include participants with higher baseline metabolic risk, apply more standardized prescriptions for hypoxic dose and training load, and extend follow-up periods to determine whether specific subgroups may benefit from HHIIT.

Effects of HHIIT on hematological parameters in non-clinical populations

The present study did not observe a significant overall effect of HHIIT on hematological indicators (g = 0.122, 95% CI [− 0.292, 0.535], P = 0.547), and this inference remained largely unchanged after CR2 small-sample robust correction. Effect sizes for red blood cell-related indicators, including Hct, RBC, and Hb, were small, and their confidence intervals crossed the line of no effect. These findings suggest that, under the current intervention conditions, HHIIT has not demonstrated stable hematological adaptations. Although the point estimate for EPO was relatively large, this finding was derived from only one study and therefore should not be interpreted as reliable evidence of an overall hematological effect.

Exploratory subgroup analyses did not detect statistically significant effects on hematological indicators when HHIIT was compared with either non-exercise controls or exercise controls. Because only a small number of studies contributed data to these outcomes, more detailed subgroup analyses were not performed. The effects of hypoxic training on hematological indicators remain uncertain and have been widely debated [76–78]. Evidence from altitude training camps suggests that, under a sufficient hypoxic dose, 24 days of live high–train low may increase Hbmass and red blood cell volume and may be accompanied by changes in EPO, reticulocytes, and Hct, with potential implications for exercise performance [79]. A previous meta-analysis reported a dose-dependent association between hypoxic exposure and hemoglobin mass, with Hbmass increasing by an average of approximately 1.1% per 100 h of hypoxic exposure [79]. This evidence should be interpreted in the context of protocols with relatively high cumulative hypoxic doses, such as traditional altitude training or live high–train low [80]. From a mechanistic perspective, hematological adaptations to hypoxic training may require a sufficiently high cumulative hypoxic dose and relatively sustained hypoxic stimulation. Continuous or prolonged hypoxic exposure may stabilize HIF-related pathways, promote EPO secretion, and support erythropoiesis in the bone marrow [80, 81]. In contrast, hypoxic exposure during HHIIT is mostly short-duration and intermittent. Although high-intensity interval training may induce marked fatigue, metabolic disturbance, and acute changes in the internal environment, the short and intermittent nature of hypoxic exposure in HHIIT may be insufficient to sustain erythropoietic signaling after recovery to normoxia. This may limit its capacity to induce stable changes in Hb, Hct, or RBC.

Therefore, the absence of a clear hematological effect in the present study may be plausible. The cardiorespiratory benefits of HHIIT may be more closely related to adaptations in peripheral oxygen utilization, hemodynamics, and skeletal muscle metabolism than to substantial enhancement of erythropoiesis or blood oxygen-carrying capacity. In addition, markers such as Hb and Hct are influenced by plasma volume, hydration status, and training-induced hemodilution. Therefore, they may not sensitively reflect true changes in erythropoiesis. In contrast, parameters such as Hb mass, red blood cell volume, and reticulocyte count may be more appropriate for evaluating hematopoietic adaptations associated with hypoxic training. Therefore, relying solely on Hb, Hct, or routine peripheral blood markers in HHIIT studies may lead to underestimation or misinterpretation of hypoxia-related hematological responses [82]. Future studies should prioritize reporting changes in hemoglobin mass, red blood cell volume, reticulocyte count, and plasma volume, and should integrate these hematological markers with body composition outcomes in comprehensive analyses.

Age stratification

Across the life course, this review shows that HHIIT studies lasting more than four weeks have predominantly recruited young adults aged 20–30 years, highlighting the limited age coverage of the current evidence base. This limitation is clinically relevant because aging is associated with reduced vascular compliance, attenuated baroreflex sensitivity, and a progressive decline in maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max), which together increase cardiometabolic risk in middle-aged and older adults [24]. Evidence from normoxic HIIT indicates that high-intensity interval training can improve cardiorespiratory fitness and selected functional outcomes in middle-aged and older adults [83, 84]. Meanwhile, meta-analyses of normoxic HIIT in children and adolescents show improvements in VO₂max and some cardiometabolic markers, whereas effects on body composition and specific lipid profiles remain inconsistent. However, this evidence is derived primarily from normoxic HIIT and cannot be directly extrapolated to HHIIT [85]. Therefore, evidence on HHIIT interventions lasting more than four weeks in children, adolescents, and older adults remains insufficient, and future exploratory studies should be conducted with strict safety monitoring and ethical oversight. It also remains unclear whether adding a hypoxic stimulus provides additional benefits in these populations or increases adverse-event risk because of reduced cardiovascular reserve. Accordingly, high-quality randomized controlled trials in specific age groups are needed, with rigorous safety monitoring, to clarify the applicability and risk boundaries of HHIIT across the life course.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Although relatively strict eligibility criteria were applied, the interventions included under the HHIIT framework remained heterogeneous. HIIT, SIH, RSH, and IHT all involve intermittent exercise exposure under hypoxic conditions, but they differ in intensity anchoring, work-to-rest structure, metabolic stress, and expected adaptive pathways. Therefore, pooling these modalities may have diluted modality-specific effects and reduced the mechanistic interpretability of the pooled estimates. Similarly, combining physiologically distinct outcomes—such as VO₂-related variables, ventilatory thresholds, and heart rate indices—within a single “cardiorespiratory fitness” domain may have obscured outcome-specific physiological responses. Second, the limited number of eligible studies and independent study clusters reduced the statistical power of subgroup analyses and meta-regression. Although three-level random-effects models with cluster-robust variance estimation were used to account for effect-size dependency, moderator findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, particularly because nominal P values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Third, outcome-domain aggregation may have introduced additional interpretative uncertainty. For example, the cardiorespiratory adaptation domain included VO₂-related outcomes, ventilatory threshold variables, and heart rate-based indices, which may not reflect the same physiological processes. Therefore, domain-level pooled estimates should be regarded as broad signals of adaptation rather than definitive evidence of specific mechanisms or changes in individual markers. Finally, the included populations were predominantly young adults, with an underrepresentation of female participants and insufficient reporting of several metabolic, haematological, and hypoxic-dose indicators. These factors limit the external validity of the findings and constrain the ability to examine sex differences, insulin-related metabolic regulation, haematological adaptation, and hypoxic dose–response relationships.

Conclusion

This systematic review and three-level meta-analysis indicate that HHIIT-related interventions may improve cardiorespiratory fitness in non-clinical populations, with the clearest evidence observed for VO₂rel. However, the current evidence does not demonstrate consistent or statistically significant effects on body composition, cardiometabolic outcomes, or haematological markers. Compared with normoxic exercise controls, HHIIT may provide a small additional stimulus for cardiopulmonary adaptation, although the overall magnitude of this effect appears modest. Furthermore, because the existing evidence is derived primarily from young adults, findings should be extrapolated with caution to female participants, middle-aged and older adults, and individuals with cardiometabolic risk. Future studies should standardize training load, hypoxic dose, and outcome-reporting frameworks to clarify the optimal target populations and practical benefits of HHIIT.

Future research recommendations

On the basis of the main findings and limitations of this review, future HHIIT research could be strengthened in several key areas. First, because the current evidence is concentrated mainly in young non-clinical adults, future studies should include more female participants, middle-aged and older adults, and individuals with diverse training backgrounds. For female participants, menstrual-cycle phase and relevant hormonal status should be recorded or controlled where feasible to reduce potential variability in hypoxic adaptation, haemodynamic responses, and metabolic outcomes. Second, because this review focused on non-clinical populations, its findings cannot be directly generalized to individuals with obesity, hypertension, impaired glucose or lipid metabolism, or other cardiometabolic risk factors. Randomized controlled trials with appropriate safety monitoring are needed to determine whether HHIIT provides additional cardiopulmonary, metabolic, or haemodynamic benefits over conventional normoxic high-intensity interval training (normoxic HIIT) in these populations. Reporting of hypoxic exposure and training load should be more standardized and transparent, including FiO₂, simulated altitude, exposure duration per session, intervention duration, training intensity, and total training volume. More refined stratified designs or dose–response frameworks are also needed to identify the optimal combination of hypoxic dose and high-intensity interval training load. With regard to blinding, future trials should consider normoxic placebo-gas conditions, single-blind designs, or highly standardized control protocols to minimize expectation effects and performance bias. These methodological improvements would help clarify whether HHIIT offers meaningful advantages over normoxic high-intensity interval training in specific practical or clinical settings.
