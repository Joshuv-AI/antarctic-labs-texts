Efficient Shift-and-Invert Preconditioning for Multi-GPU Accelerated Density Functional Calculations
Authors: Jeheon Woo, Woo Youn Kim, Sunghwan Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo accelerate the iterative diagonalization of electronic structure calculations, we propose a new inexact shift-and-invert (ISI) preconditioning method. The key idea is to improve shift values in the ISI preconditioning to be closer to the exact eigenvalues, leading to a significant boost in the convergence speed of the iterative diagonalization. Furthermore, we adopted a preconditioned conjugate gradient solver to rapidly evaluate an inversion process. Finally, we accelerated overall processes, including the proposed modification, with state-of-the-art graphical processing units (GPUs) and assessed its parallel efficiency with real-space density functional calculations of 1D, 2D, and 3D periodic systems. Our method attains both fast diagonalization convergence and high multi-GPU parallel efficiency. This is evident from the fact that single-point density functional calculations for hundreds of atom systems can be done in approximately 10 s using 8 GPUs. The proposed method can be generally applied to any electronic structure calculation methods involving large-scale diagonalizations.

Introduction

Efficient iterative diagonalization is a key to expanding the applicable domain of electronic structure calculations. As the size of chemical systems increases, the computation and memory costs associated with calculating electronic structures become prohibitively high. To mitigate or to avoid the high computational cost of diagonalization, there are many alternative methods (e.g., density purification,1 density minimization,2,3 and quadratically convergent self-consistent field (SCF) methods4−10) have been actively developed. However, because many quantum chemical methods require only few lowest eigenpairs, an iterative diagonalization method that effectively finds few extreme eigenpairs is still widely used.11−14 One prominent example of iterative diagonalization is the Davidson algorithm,11 initially proposed for diagonalizing a configuration interaction matrix but subsequently applied across various contexts in quantum chemistry.

A significant application of large-scale diagonalization is in real-space calculations, a numerical strategy that describes electronic structures by discretizing real-space instead of utilizing atom-centered or planewave-like basis functions. There has been a strong increase in the development of real-space methods to calculate the electronic structure of molecular and solid systems, due to their high flexibility and computational efficiency in representing electronic structures.15,16 However, the large number of grid points required to cover the space occupied by a molecule or solid leads to a high dimensionality of the Hamiltonian matrix.17,18 Despite the high sparsity of the real-space Hamiltonian matrix, the diagonalization of the high-dimensional matrix needs iterative procedures. Therefore, most practical real-space calculations rely on an iterative diagonalization.

Recent studies on real-space methods focus on efficiently utilizing graphical processing units (GPUs).18−26 These studies involve rewriting existing electronic structure programs and modifying algorithms because GPUs intrinsically provide a large number of slow but parallelly working computing units. Therefore, for efficient utilization of GPUs, algorithms need to avoid operations that cause idle status of computing units such as data transfer and global memory access. Sometimes, even if additional computations are introduced, overall performance may be improved by reducing idle computing units.

The performance enhancement by introducing an additional process is a longstanding approach. Preconditioning, a technique that accelerates iterative algorithms by transforming an original problem into an easier-to-solve one, falls within this category. Although preconditioning introduces extra computations, it reduces the number of iterations needed for convergence, thereby enhancing overall speed. Numerous preconditioning methods, leveraging prior knowledge of chemical systems and computing hardware, have been proposed to optimize the iterative diagonalizations used in electronic structure calculations.27−29

Inexact shift-and-invert (ISI) preconditioning is a widely adopted method.30−35 The ISI preconditioner is constructed by shifting and inverting the target diagonalization matrix. This preconditioning reduces the contributions of unwanted eigenvectors from residual vectors and thus accelerates the convergence of target eigenvectors. The power of the ISI preconditioning is particularly pronounced when the shift value is close to the target eigenvalue. Unfortunately, in many practical applications, eigenspectrum information is unavailable. Another problem of the ISI preconditioner is the heavy computational cost of the inversion operation. To tackle this drawback, iterative inversion methods have been widely applied, yet the inversion process is still the main bottleneck of the ISI preconditioning.30−35

In this work, we propose modifying the ISI preconditioning method to enhance its performance and validate this enhancement using state-of-the-art multi-GPU machines. By introducing a correction in the approximated eigenvalues, we enhance the accuracy of the shift value and thus observe significant improvement in the convergence speed of the iterative diagonalization. Additionally, we reduce the cost of matrix inversion through efficient preconditioned inverse iterations and parallelization using multiple GPUs. Our implementation’s performance is validated in real-space density functional calculations of various chemical systems. Although our work demonstrates the benefits through real-space density functional calculations, the proposed modification of the ISI preconditioning can apply to a wide range of electronic structure calculations.

The block Davidson method can be decomposed into six types of operations. The first one is “Hamiltonian operation”, HΨ. This is a typical multiplication of a matrix and a block of vectors. Since a Hamiltonian matrix is treated in a sparse format in most large-scale electronic structure calculations, the sparse matrix-vector multiplication is performed using the sparse module of PyTorch. The second operation is “Projection”. In general, the projection encompasses the entirety of Ψ* H Ψ and H̃ = (Ψ ⊕ P)* H(Ψ ⊕ P). However, the “Projection” in this case denotes only the matrix multiplication operations between Ψ* and HΨ and between (Ψ ⊕ P)* and H(Ψ ⊕ P). The first and 12th lines in Algorithm 1 contain the “Hamiltonian operation” and the “Projection”. The third operation is the “Subspace diagonalization”, eigh(H̃) (the second and 13th lines in Algorithm 1). This operation diagonalizes the projected Hamiltonian, H̃. The fourth operation is “Rotation”, which is used to construct Ritz vectors for the next iteration. The third and 14th lines correspond to the “Rotation”. The fifth operation is “Orthonormalization”. Depending on algorithms, several different implementations are allowed for both operations. We have implemented “Orthonormalization” using the Cholesky decomposition method like other works.25 The sixth operation is “Preconditioning”, corresponding to the 9th line. This entails transforming a matrix into a more favorable form before applying diagonalization techniques, thereby improving the convergence and stability of the iterative process. In this context, it includes applying a preconditioner, T, to the residuals to enhance the overall efficiency of the process.

Modifications of Inexact Shift-and-Invert Preconditioning: Shift Value

The ISI preconditioning method is based on operations that transform the eigenvalues of a matrix without changing the eigenvectors. It uses (H – ε̃iI)−1 as the preconditioner T, where ε̃i is the approximate eigenvalue of the target matrix H, and I is an identity matrix. Through this transformation of the residual vector, the contribution of a specific eigenvector associated with a given eigenvalue close to ε̃i becomes more significant than the other eigenvectors. Hence, the quality of ε̃i is highly related to the performance of the ISI preconditioner. Since the exact eigenvalue is typically unknown during iterations, most practical diagonalization solvers replace ε̃i with μi, which represents the eigenvalue of the target matrix projected onto Ritz vectors obtained from the previous iteration.43,44

One known issue with the ISI method arises from the fact that this approximate shift value causes unstable initial convergence.45 As the diagonalization iteration converges, the difference between the Ritz values and the actual eigenvalues becomes smaller, making the ISI preconditioner work more efficiently. However, when the Ritz values are substantially different from the eigenvalues, the ISI preconditioner may filter out the target eigenvectors, hindering the convergence behavior. To resolve this issue, Cai et al. proposed a hybrid preconditioning method that uses the ISI preconditioning with Ritz values only when the residual is small enough. They combined two types of shift-and-invert preconditioners, one with a Ritz value shift and the other with a constant shift, referred to as local and global preconditioners, respectively. The local preconditioner is constructed by solving (H – μiI) pi = ri as in a typical ISI method, where ri denotes a residual vector and pi is a preconditioned one. On the other hand, the global preconditioner is formulated as (H – ε̅I)−1, where ε̅ denotes a predefined global shift value. For the iteration having large residual vectors, the local preconditioner may perform suboptimally compared to the global one due to the large deviation of μi from the target eigenvalue. Therefore, when preconditioning residual vectors, the global preconditioner is first applied to all residual vectors, with local preconditioning carried out only for those whose norm is sufficiently small. The local preconditioner is constructed at each SCF step, while the global preconditioner using the predefined shift value is constructed once at the first step of the SCF calculation and then used in subsequent SCF iterations. Thus, any change in H during the SCF cycles is ignored.

Inspired by this hybrid preconditioning method, we propose a new ISI preconditioner that adopts two different formulations depending on the size of residual vectors. It should be noted that our method does not rely on an arbitrary predetermined global shift value nor on memory consumption to store the results of the explicit inversion. This modification avoids the system dependency that comes from the choice of the global preconditioning value. In every SCF iteration, we check out the L2 norm of R, and if the norm is greater than 10, ε̃i is set to 0 for all systems. When the norm is less than 10, ε̃i is set to the Ritz value with a correction proportional to the norm of R. This correction makes the ε̃i value closer to the corresponding actual eigenvalue. This is supported by Cauchy’s interlace theorem, which states that the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix within a subspace are interlaced with the eigenvalues of its original matrix. Even after accelerating the convergence of the inversion iterations, the ISI preconditioning still demands a high computational cost due to a large number of matrix-vector multiplications in the inversion process. To fu rther speed up the process, we introduce column-wise distribution of block vectors (Ψ, HΨ, R, and P in Algorithm 1) and independently perform the time-consuming matrix-vector operations on multi-GPUs. Because modern GPUs provide sufficient memory space to store the entire Hamiltonian matrix, the column-wise distributed block vectors parallelize the “Hamiltonian operation” without a communication among GPUs (see Figure 1a). This column-wise distribution of block vectors entails data communication in the “Projection”, “Rotation”, and “Orthonormalization” operations (see Figure 1c–e). For effective data communication, we introduce “Redistribution” steps that convert the column-wise distributed block vectors into row-wise ones or vice versa (see Figure 1b). As shown in Figure 1e, “Orthonormalization” includes both “Projection” and “Rotation”. Though the “Projection” and “Rotation” operations within “Orthonormalization” are carried out in the same way as other instances of “Projection” and “Rotation”, the time spent by both processes within “Orthonormalization” is specifically categorized under “Orthonormalization” rather than “Projection” or “Rotation” in the performance analysis presented in the next section.

Schematic illustrations for the parallelization of major operations in the block Davidson method: (a) Hamiltonian operation and preconditioning, (b) redistribution, (c) projection, (d) rotation, and (e) orthonormalization. Block vectors are distributed in either columns or rows, depending on the type of operation.

For parallel computing, before the “Projection” and “Orthonormalization” operations, we redistribute block vectors, HΨ and P, and GPUs independently perform the products of two block vectors, Ψ* (HΨ) and P* P. The final results of “Projection” and “Orthonormalization” are obtained from the summation of the product results. These summations are executed by all_reduce function. The row-wise distributed block vectors are converted back to the column-wise ones after the following “Rotation”. To parallelize “Subspace diagonalization”, parallel packages for dense matrix diagonalization can be adopted. In our experiments, however, we decided to perform the diagonalization serially on all GPUs because this operation does not cause a time bottleneck. Therefore, all GPUs compute the same eigenpairs of the projected Hamiltonian matrix.

Details of Numerical Experiments

All computations were performed on KISTI Neuron’s compute nodes, which have 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs with 80 GB of memory each and two AMD central processing units (EPYC 7543). All density functional calculations were performed using GOSPEL,17 which is written in Python. GOSPEL provides highly flexible and well-structured electronic structure calculation modules with high efficiency. Its numerical operations, including iterative diagonalization and preconditioning, are implemented based on PyTorch48 which covers various types of processors. All calculations were conducted with PyTorch (version 2.0.1) installed with CUDA (version 11.8).

The performance of ISI preconditioning was evaluated using density functional calculations at the Γ-point of three systems: (4, 2) CNT with 1D periodic boundary condition (PBC), hBN with 2D PBC, and Si with 3D PBC. These systems consist of 196, 240, and 512 atoms with corresponding valence electron numbers of 784, 960, and 2048, respectively. The geometry data of the systems is available in the Supporting Information. We employed a uniform grid and a 7-point finite-difference method to represent the Laplacian operator. All density functional calculations were performed with spin-unpolarized and the PBE49 exchange-correlation functional. In addition, we employed SG optimized norm-conserving pseudopotentials50 for B, C, and N and a local pseudopotential51 for Si.

To accurately measure the performance of the ISI preconditioning, we consider two different cases: the diagonalization of a fixed Hamiltonian and SCF calculations. In the former case, we compute the eigenpairs of a converged Hamiltonian matrix obtained from typical SCF calculations. Randomly generated vectors are used as initial trial eigenvectors after orthonormalization. In the latter case, initial vectors are chosen as the eigenvectors of the previous SCF step, except for the first step. The initial vectors for the first SCF step are constructed in the same manner as the fixed Hamiltonian diagonalization case. The SCF calculations start with the density obtained from the atomic density superposition. The Pulay mixing52 technique is used, which speeds up the convergence of the SCF process by improving the stability and efficiency of the iterative procedure. The total energy tolerance for the SCF convergence check is set to 0.1 mHartree/atom. We choose the grid spacing for both cases to achieve convergence to a threshold of 1.0 mHartree/atom, yielding 0.15, 0.14, and 0.3 Å for CNT, hBN, and Si, respectively. The number of orbitals to find is set to the number of occupied orbitals plus its 5% as virtual states in all experiments. For the CNT system, this corresponds to 411 orbitals and 1,179,871 grid points; for the hBN system, 504 orbitals and 943,578 grid points; and for the Si system, 1075 orbitals and 389,017 grid points.

In the case of the fixed Hamiltonian diagonalization, the Hamiltonian operator is constructed using the corresponding SCF-converged electron density with the following setting. A convergence threshold of 0.001 is used for the L2 norm of the residual. The number of the maximum iterations of the ISI preconditioner is set to 4.

Results and Discussion

Comparison of Convergence Rate with Respect to Shift Value Types

To evaluate the effectiveness of our correction method, we compared the convergence behaviors of diagonalization with the ISI preconditioning according to the five types of shifts: (1) zero (no shift), (2) Ritz values, (3) Ritz values or zero (depending on the size of the residual norms), (4) corrected Ritz values in eq 3, and (5) true eigenvalues. The Ritz value shift is the most common, as mentioned in the previous section, while the zero shift is also used in many problems due to the instability of the Ritz value in the early stages of the iteration.27,31,46,53 The third method chooses either zero or the Ritz value as the shift value depending on the norm of the residual vectors. As described in the Methods section, the zero shift value is used when the norm is greater than 10, while the Ritz shift value is used otherwise. In comparison, the fourth method differs in adding a correction term to the Ritz value (see eq 3). The fifth method requires true eigenvalues, which serve as the ideal shift values but are unavailable in practical cases.

The change in the residual norm during the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian matrix for the hBN system is visualized in Figure 2. When Ritz values are employed as shift values, the convergence of the residuals is relatively faster in the middle and late stages of the iteration but slower in the early stages compared to the results without shifting (see Figure 2b). This is because the error in the Ritz values is particularly large at the beginning when the residual is large, which is confirmed by Figure 2c, where the initial convergence speed is significantly improved by applying zero shifts at the early steps. Figure 2d shows that the results of the corrected Ritz values exhibit similar behavior to that of using no shifts at the initial steps since the initial iterations are primarily performed without a shift. However, this fast convergence rate is maintained throughout the entire iteration. As a result, the corrected shift values give the closest results to the ideal case with the true eigenvalues.

The proposed correction of the Ritz shift is designed based on the assumption that the effectiveness of the ISI preconditioning increases as the shift values approach the true eigenvalues. To examine the effect of the correction on improving the shift values, we visualize the Ritz and corrected Ritz values corresponding to both the highest and lowest occupied states of the hBN system at each iteration of the Davidson method (see Figure 3a). Additionally, Figure 3b presents the following two distributions: one representing the differences between the Ritz values and the corresponding true eigenvalues and the other illustrating the differences between the corrected Ritz values and the corresponding true eigenvalues for all target eigenvalues. In both subplots, it is observed that the Ritz values have a significant discrepancy from the corresponding true eigenvalues at a few initial iterations, whereas the corrected Ritz values show less significant errors. For early iterations, Ritz values record large positive values. Therefore, they show more deviations from the corresponding true eigenvalues than the corrected Ritz values, which are set to zero due to the large norm of the residual vectors. This effect mitigates the slow convergence pattern shown in the first few iterations of Figure 2b. At most mid and late iterations, the corrected Ritz values also have smaller differences from the true eigenvalues. It indicates that the correction based on the residual norm, , reduces the differences of the Ritz values from the true eigenvalue, leading to the fast convergence observed in Figure 2d. Consequently, we verified that our correction on Ritz values successfully refines the shift value of the ISI preconditioning, accelerating the convergence of the iterative diagonalization.

Performance Optimization of the PCG Method in the ISI Preconditioning

In addition to reducing diagonalization iterations by modifying the shift value of the ISI method, we further optimize the ISI preconditioning speed. The most time-consuming part of the ISI preconditioning is PCG iterations to solve eq 4. To reduce the computational cost of the PCG method, we select the type of TPCG in Algorithm 2 and simultaneously optimize the maximum iteration of the PCG solver . Because the inverse of the shifted matrix is numerically unstable, the PCG method in the ISI preconditioner is designed not to target a highly accurate solution. In most implementations of the ISI preconditioning, the PCG solver stops after a small number of iterations, which critically affects the performance of the PCG solver. Therefore, we investigate the diagonalization performance with no preconditioner, filter preconditioner, and Gaussian-approximated Poisson preconditioner (GAPP) combined with the PCG solver on a wide range of . GAPP, as proposed in our previous work, aims to reproduce the inverse of the major component of a Hamiltonian matrix using Gaussian convolution.27 Since GAPP comprises a couple of convolution operations, it is well-optimized in modern processors.

SCF Calculations

As a proof-of-concept for the proposed preconditioner, we measured the overall elapsed time of the SCF calculation which is a typical application example of diagonalization. At each SCF step, the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian is performed to evaluate molecular orbitals. One thing that differs from a typical diagonalization is that relatively accurate starting vectors are available from the previous SCF step, except for the first SCF step. In the ISI preconditioning method, these vectors are used for the initial guess vectors of the iterative diagonalization (Ψ) and for estimating the Ritz values (μi). Especially, this estimation of μi can improve the quality of ISI preconditioning, and thus our ISI preconditioner can effectively reduce the number of diagonalization iterations to reach the convergence.

To investigate the optimal SCF speed using our ISI preconditioner, we assessed the elapsed time of SCF calculations with the proposed preconditioner by changing the maximum number of diagonalization iterations. Because the precise convergence of diagonalization is required only at the last step, the optimal selection of the maximum iteration numbers can achieve high speed without losing accuracy. Figure 6a–c shows the total elapsed time and SCF iterations as a function of the maximum iterations required to reach convergence. Increasing the maximum iteration of the diagonalization algorithm results in fewer SCF steps but with increased total elapsed time. This is because the larger number of maximum iterations evaluates eigenvectors more accurately at each SCF step. As a result, the proposed ISI method yielded the optimal number of diagonalization iterations, which was one, with the shortest total elapsed time in all tested systems (CNT, hBN, and Si).

Conclusions

Here, we propose a new inexact shift-and-invert (ISI) preconditioner to accelerate density functional computations for large systems with multiple GPUs. We introduced two key modifications to the ISI preconditioning to overcome the computational bottleneck from the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian matrix. First, we devised a corrected Ritz value to approximate the shift value more closely to the true eigenvalue, which significantly accelerates the convergence of the iterative diagonalization with the ISI preconditioner. Second, we improved the performance of the preconditioned conjugate gradient method used in the inversion process by utilizing the Gaussian-approximated Poisson preconditioner (GAPP). As a result, the proposed ISI preconditioning efficiently reduces the number of iterations required in the iterative diagonalization process.

In addition, the overall iterative diagonalization, including the ISI preconditioning, has been efficiently parallelized with multiple GPUs by using a column-wise distribution of block vectors. Though the distribution entails communication between GPUs, the performance gain from the parallelization overcomes the communication overhead. Thus, a high parallel efficiency of 50–80% is achieved for the diagonalization and overall SCF calculations on up to 8 GPUs.

This study contributes significantly to accelerating electronic structure calculations on multiple GPUs. We anticipate that our method can be applied to any electronic structure calculation method involving large-scale diagonalizations.
