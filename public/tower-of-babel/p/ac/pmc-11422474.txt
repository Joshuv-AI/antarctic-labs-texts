Beekeeping breakthrough: unveiling hive health with a portable membrane inlet mass spectrometry detection method
Authors: Daria Ilić, Đorđe Vujić, Maja Buljovčić, Jelena Živančev, Branko Šikoparija, Boris Brkić
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSupporting bee populations is essential considering threats posed by human activities like pesticide usage and habitat destruction. However, the current methods for monitoring and analyzing beehives and their surrounding environments are invasive, complex, and time-consuming. These methods often rely heavily on laboratory analyses, making them difficult to implement independently in the field. This study explores the application of portable membrane inlet mass spectrometer (MIMS) for noninvasive hive analysis, demonstrating its ability to detect various compounds indicative of hive conditions and environmental stressors. In addition to the expected compounds found in beehives, such as α-bergamotene, hexadecanoic acid, heptadecane, hexadecanamide, α-bisabolol-, 9-octadecenamide, (Z) − , and benzaldehyde, unexpected compounds, pollutants, like indane (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon) and carbofuran (pesticide), were also detected. The MIMS detection method provides rapid, accurate, and real-time results, making it suitable for preventive measures against bee diseases and integral to environmental biomonitoring. This integration of technology represents a significant advancement in bee conservation efforts, offering hope for the future of both bees and ecosystems.Keywords: Honeybee, MIMS, Biomonitoring, Portable mass spectrometry method, Pheromones, Environment impact

Introduction

Monitoring and evaluating the health of bee colonies

Bees represent a remarkable example of evolutionary adaptation and play a crucial role in ecosystems, serving as an indispensable component of biodiversity essential for the survival of plants but also humans and other animals. They offer invaluable ecosystem services by pollinating agricultural crops, which are vital for human well-being. The interconnection between the health of bee colonies and human well-being underscores the need for a responsible and committed approach to ensuring a healthy environment. According to Hung et al. (2018) approximately 87% of flowering plants and, consequently, 34.5% of global food products for human consumption rely on effective pollination. Recognizing their significance, efforts to understand and support bees have long been, and continue to be, of utmost importance. Enhancing our understanding of bees through research and active monitoring of their behavior and health, both in natural habitats and managed hives, is fundamental. This becomes particularly crucial in the face of significant anthropogenic challenges, such as pesticide use, agricultural intensification, habitat loss, climate change, and extreme weather events, which pose serious threats to native and wild pollinator insects (Goulson et al. 2015; Gill et al. 2012).

The gaseous mixture found inside a hive exhibits high temporal fluctuation and is also unique and complex from the chemical perspective (Abd El‑Wahed et al. 2021). A honeybee colony’s surrounding atmosphere typically contains a combination of gases produced by the bees themselves (such as pheromones, other chemicals released to ward off pests and predators, metabolites), as well as emissions from hive pests and their metabolites (e.g., fungi, acarine, beetles…), emissions generated by the stores kept in the hive (such as honey, nectar, beeswax, pollen, and propolis), and gases produced by the materials used to construct hives (wood, paint, plastic, etc.) (Devillers et al. 2002). The air around the hive is also filled with pollutants that come from nearby houses, businesses, farms, and cars. A honeybee colony’s indoor environment might occasionally be affected by bee illnesses. For instance, colony suffering from American foulbrood (a bacterial disease of honeybee brood) causes a specific odor, because rotting larvae produce a mixture of valerian, isocaproic, and caproic acids which can even be detected by the human nose. Furthermore, it is well known that the infamous Varroa mites can alter the chemicals on their surface to match the developmental stage of their hosts. The premise for detecting varroosis could be a probable alteration in the chemical composition of the indoor air (Szczurek et al. 2019). Apitherapy is a science of human well-being that focuses on the use of honeybee products such as royal jelly, honey, propolis, pollen, bee bread, and bee venom. The aim is to address common human ailments and boost health and well-being (Gokulakrishnaa and Thirunavukkarasu 2020). Other descriptions of apitherapy are described as an art and science of treatment and holistic healing with the use of honeybees and its products to benefit humans and other animals. The use of beehive air as a treatment option was developed by Heinrich Huttner, and it is confirmed that beehive air helps reduce asthma and eliminates respiratory allergies, and that the air inhaled from the beehive carries with it beneficial products that are produced from the honeybee body (Weis et al. 2022; Guardia and Stângaciu 2022). Beehive air contains natural substances such as propolis, cytochrome protein, the smell of honey, wax, and other 1000-plus substances which have a direct impact on the improvement of a person's health condition (Kopała et al. 2019).

Examining and overseeing the health and status of bee brood within hives hold significant importance both on a broad scale and in finer detail. The condition of bees, as vital pollinators, serves as an indicator of environmental pollution, contributes to product yields, and has a direct impact on human health.

Beekeeping typical inspection processes include opening the beehives for a visual assessment of the colony, with the aim of identifying any potential issues with the colonies or the general health status of the colonies. Inspections can interfere with the nest bees’ work and the microclimate balance within the hive if they are not done correctly (Rafael Braga et al. 2020) (the workers responsible for the internal work of the nest).

This study introduces an innovative, noninvasive method for beehive inspection and monitoring using a portable membrane inlet mass spectrometer (MIMS). This method enables the rapid, efficient, and accurate detection of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in beehive air samples. Given the natural variability in VOC content within the beehive environment, our approach focuses on a qualitative, nontarget analysis rather than quantifying specific compounds. By utilizing both the conventional GC–MS technique and the novel MIMS for real-time screening, we aim to identify a broad range of VOCs. Among these, we will focus on significant compounds and monitor their occurrence and relative changes in relation to specific conditions like beehive illnesses. Through extensive analysis, we hope to identify VOCs that could serve as selective biomarkers for future environmental biomonitoring and beehive health assessment.

Researchers may use a combination of active and passive sampling methods to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the factors affecting bee health and hive conditions. The choice between active and passive sampling depends on the specific research objectives and the resources available.

To collect volatiles, sample odors are trapped on exposed inert polymer matrix (adsorbent packing materials), which differ in terms of their adsorbent characteristics and technique of analysis. The trapped volatiles are subsequently expelled by either thermal desorption or solvent desorption, which involves removing the chemicals from the packing material. Desorbing the collected volatiles can be performed in two ways: either straight into the analytical device or into a solution that will be examined later. According to the information generated, the simplicity of use, and the cost to the researcher, each strategy has specific benefits and drawbacks (Torto et al. 2013).

The air inside a honey bee hive has previously been sampled for specific volatile chemical mixtures (VOCs and SVOCs) using a syringe, and the chemical contents of the sample have been analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS). However, this method does not permit the storage of samples prior to analysis (Torto et al. 2013). Different methods have coupled adsorbent packing materials with vacuum/air tube systems, such as activated charcoal, Tenax TA, Super Q, Hayesep Q, and Porapak Q. These approaches are constrained by the difficulty of building vacuum/air tube systems and the possibility that their presence could result in vibrations that stress the bees and imbalance the chemical profiles observed (Murcia‑Morales et al. 2020; Carroll and Duehl 2012; Torto et al. 2005).

Materials and methods

Portable membrane inlet mass spectrometry system

The equipment utilized in this study encompassed a portable mass spectrometry setup comprising a membrane sample inlet, a singular quadrupole mass spectrometer (QMS), and a vacuum system inclusive of diaphragm and turbo pumps, along with a laptop PC designated for data acquisition and subsequent processing of the findings. Specifically, the QMS utilized was a PrismaPlus® compact mass spectrometer manufactured by Pfeiffer Vacuum GmBH, featuring a sealed electron impact (EI) HS-gas tight ion source furnished with yttriated iridium filaments. Additionally, it comprised a single quadrupole mass analyzer QMS 200 with a length of 100 mm and containing round rods with a diameter of 6 mm. The instrument was equipped with a dual-detector system consisting of a Faraday cup and a secondary electron multiplier, complemented by an electronic control unit (ECU) denoted as QME 220 M3, ensuring efficient management of operations. This mass spectrometer is designed for partial pressure analysis below 10−4 mbar. Throughout its entire working mass range, it provides unit resolution and quick response times (≤ 0.5 s) at low concentration levels (parts per billion). Considering its analytical mass range up to 300 mass-to-charge ratios (m/z), this device is appropriate for identifying low-molecular-weight volatile compounds. The time required for a complete scan spanning the entire mass range is estimated to be around 3 min, with each mass point being observed for approximately 1 s. The system dimensions measure 616 × 220 × 433 mm (length × height × width), and it has a weight of 23 kg. With its minimal energy consumption (averaging about 200 W/h at maximum operation), ability to be powered by 8-h battery, to perform real-time analyses, and swift scanning speed, it is perfectly equipped for field applications.

This system was coupled with the sheet membrane probe. The stainless-steel probe was constructed “in-house” according to a previously reported design (Giannoukos et al. 2014). It was combined with a SIL-TEC A class VI USP medical-grade polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) sheet membrane for selective introduction of VOCs to the system. Due to its high durability, this membrane can be used for a very long time before needing to be replaced. There is no risk of a carry-over impact since it is cleaned using warmth and vacuum suction 18. The thickness of the membrane was 0.127 mm, and the active membrane area was 32 mm2.

GC–MS analysis benchmarking

Analysis of the air from the hive was performed by lab-based GC–MS (Agilent 7890B) with an electron-ionization mass-selective detector (Agilent Technologies 5977A). A sample was injected (2 µL) into a split/splitless inlet at 220 °C. The carrier gas, helium (purity 99.999%), was used with a constant flow of 1.0 mL/min. The compounds were separated on an Agilent Technologies HP-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 µm), using the following temperature program: start at 60 °C, 3 °C/min to 246 °C. Total run time was 62 min. Eluate was delivered to the mass spectrometer via a transfer line held at 240 °C. Ion source temperature was maintained at 230 °C, electron energy at 70 eV, and quadrupole temperature at 150 °C. Data were acquired in scan mode (m/z range 40–400). The compounds were identified by mass spectra comparison with libraries (NIST/EPA/NIH Mass Spectral Library 05 and Mass Hunter workstation Software, Unknown Analysis). The n-alkane mixture, consisting of a mixture of C9-C28 n-alkanes corresponding to 820–2800 KI, was used as a standard for the determination of retention indices. Relative amounts of components, expressed in percentages (%), were calculated by normalization measurement according to peak area in the total ion chromatogram.

Air pump

The collecting process of samples for GC–MS analysis involved placing an air flow pump Apex2 Flow Detective from CASELLA, equipped with ORBO 100 sorbents in close proximity to the beehive. The pump operated continuously at a flow rate of 0.8 L/min over a 24-h period. ORBO 100 sorbents were selected for their efficiency in adsorbing and ensuring a comprehensive collection of VOCs from inside beehive atmosphere and VOCs from outside the hive.

Experimental section

Motivation and concept

The motivation behind this study is to validate in-field analytical method for beehive air matrix investigation, by direct chemical analysis of volatile organic compounds in the gas phase using a lightweight portable MS system. The in-field use of a portable MIMS can provide a complete user-friendly analytical solution that will enable the measurement (detection, monitoring, alarming, etc.) of potential bee disease outbreak or environmental pollutants and toxic industrial compounds. Selected chemicals for analysis are presented in Table 3. The targeted compounds, including bee pheromones crucial for colony communication, possible volatile disease markers, and harmful pollutants, can provide a holistic assessment of the hive’s environmental conditions. Traditional methods for air analysis often lack the portability and real-time capabilities necessary for on-site biomonitoring.

Experimental setup

Experiments were done using a hand-portable membrane inlet mass spectrometer. The MIMS system is based on quadrupole mass spectrometry, and its technical characteristics have been previously described in detail. The system consists of a quadrupole mass spectrometer (QMS) with a triple filter analyzer inside a vacuum chamber connected to the vacuum system and the electronic control unit (ECU). Samples were introduced through the inlet based on a thin hydrophobic flat sheet polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membrane. The PDMS material allows passage of nonpolar volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from individual substances and mixtures using the pervaporation process. The system also contains a membrane sample inlet that allows near real-time monitoring of VOCs and semi-VOCs in air and water. It is mounted inside a Swagelok vacuum fitting union as described by Giannoukos et al. (2014). The length of the membrane sampling tube was kept at minimum with approximately 15-cm separation between the membrane and the EI source (Fig. 1).

The portable in-field setup system: MIMS device, membrane probe, and laptop

The experimental apparatus was positioned at the site of the investigated beehives within the scope of this research. The geographic coordinates of the site hosting the examined beehives are specified as 45°15′10.1″ N latitude and 19°45′21.3″ E longitude, situated in Veternik, Vojvodina, Novi Sad, Serbia. The specimens derived from a total of four beehives underwent real-time, in-field analysis utilizing the MIMS instrument, as depicted in Fig. 2. The hive configuration in this apicultural setting comprised a two-stage Langstroth hive structure. It is noteworthy that the practice of beekeeping was uniformly prevalent throughout the entirety of the apiary situated at the same location. This investigative process unfolded throughout the months spanning from July to September, with examinations being conducted bimonthly.

Air sampling from three distinct vertical sections of the honey bee hives

In the preliminary stage of our investigation, specific location within the honey bee hive conducive to obtaining a representative air sample with minimal disruption to the resident bee colony was identified. This selection was made under the premise that the distribution of certain volatile compounds within the hive atmosphere is not homogeneous across all regions (Peters et al. 2019; Jarimi et al. 2020). Consequently, air samples were extracted from three distinct vertical sections of the hives with an air flow pump Apex2 Flow Detective equipped with ORBO 100. The pump operated continuously at a flow rate of 0.8 L/min over a 24-h period for sample intake. Subsequently obtained through gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis of these vertical section samples (Fig. 3) revealed that the apex of the hive, particularly within the upper region of the frames, exhibited the highest concentration of volatile compounds.

Membrane probe equipped with a 5 cm-long tube made of PTFE material

Comparative assessment of the results involved overlaying chromatograms derived from three separate samples. Notably, the data indicates that sample point 1, corresponding to the uppermost section of the hive, demonstrates a significantly elevated intensity of volatile compounds relative to the mid and lower sections. Following the identification of the sampling point harboring the highest concentration of volatile compounds within the beehive, the subsequent phase of the experiment entails in-field sampling utilizing MIMS technique.

The in-field setup system comprises a portable MIMS device, a membrane probe equipped with a 5-cm-long tube made of PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) material and a laptop for acquisition and processing the results (Fig. 2). Positioned proximate to the beehive, the system is configured to minimize the response time for detecting VOCs. At the designated location on the hive (previously mentioned in the text), a perforation was made through which a tube connected to the membrane probe was directly inserted. The sampling point was located in the upper segment, specifically within the honey super box, wherein removable comb frames with an 8-mm air gap between them were employed. Additionally, the hive was equipped with a queen excluder installed between the segments.

The examination of the gaseous composition within the bee hives was executed utilizing a MIMS in real time, whereby each assessment persisted for a duration of 30 min per hive. This encompassed a total of 10 discrete cycles recorded in full-scan mode, facilitating the scanning of up to 300 atomic mass units (amu). Operational pressure during analysis was 1 × 106 torr.

Results and discussion

In total, 240 air samples were taken from the beehives for a period of 3 months. Air samples were collected from both inside and outside the bee hives. The outside samples served as a reference point representing typical environmental conditions or background levels of VOCs in the surrounding area. External factors such as pollution, weather conditions, and seasonal variations can influence the composition of air samples. Control samples collected from outside the beehive enable the mitigation of external factors, thus facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of the specific influences exerted by the hive environment on air quality. These baseline measurements help in assessing the impact of factors unique to the hive environment, such as bee activity, hive materials, emissions from honey bee products, or the influence of pathogens on air quality.

The purpose of this study is to confirm the application of field methods as valid analyses of VOCs in the atmosphere of bee hives. The detection rationale can have various objectives, such as defining the health status of the hive, environmental biomonitoring, and analysis of bee products. A selection of VOCs of interest according to the following groups was conducted to confirm the comprehensive application of the detection method using a portable MIMS device (see Table 3). These include compounds from different chemical family, compounds (semiochemicals) involved in communication in the bee colonies, compounds that can be indicators of bee colony health/diseases, compounds characteristic for the presence of bee products, pollutants inside the beehive and from the surrounding environment, and compounds (semiochemical) profile of different phases of bee development. The aim of this presentation is to highlight a classification based on the chemical structure of various compounds and the broad spectrum of detection achievable through the MIMS technique, rather than solely focusing on their behavioral effects.

In this study, innovative hive analysis was conducted using the MIMS device, where approximately 120 volatile organic compounds were identified per sample obtained from bee hives through this method. Concurrently, the application of gas chromatography-mass spectrometry, serving as a benchmarking technique, confirmed the presence of an average of 230 VOCs per sample derived from bee hives.

In Fig. 4, the results of the mass spectrum obtained using the MIMS method from a sample of air collected from a single beehive are depicted. The mass spectrum illustrates some of the detected compounds (the most dominant fragments of the detected compounds), along with their relative values calculated through the intensity of the ion current for each detected mass. The separation and detection process begins with spectrum recordings capable of detecting up to 300 amu. Following the initial detection, singular ion monitoring (SIM) recording was utilized to identify compounds of interest with greater specificity. For precise identification, ions that are characteristic and dominant for each target compound are monitored. This approach ensures that even compounds that may overlap or have similar mass-to-charge ratios are accurately identified. By carefully observing these characteristic ions, along with those that could potentially interfere or overlap with the target compounds, the MIMS device facilitates a detailed and accurate analysis of the sample (Fig. 5). This methodology enhances the reliability and specificity of compound detection. Additionally, a prominent peak at 18 m/z can be observed on the spectrum, indicative of the presence of water or moisture within the beehive. The humidity level within the hive typically ranged between 76.5 and 85.6% owing to the moisture content of the honey stored within the compartment (Oluwaseyi et al. 2022).

In line with the concept of establishing the wide spectrum limits of various compounds and their roles within the bee colony, analysis was conducted on 13 significant compounds found in samples from beehives (see Table 3). Based on the obtained results, a wide range of different VOCs that can be detected in real time using in-field MIMS technique was identified. When comparing the obtained results with the control results derived from air samples collected from the surrounding environment, it was concluded that the analyzed compounds listed in Table 2 are solely confined to the gaseous matrix within the hive. From Group 1, five significant VOCs were isolated: hexadecanoic acid, hexadecanamide, 9-octadecenamide (Z) − , cis-β-farnesene, and benzoic acid methyl ester. From Group 2, two compounds were isolated: 9,12-octadecadienoic acid, methyl ester, and benzoic acid methyl ester. From Group 3, four compounds were isolated: α-bergamotene, α-bisabolol − , heneicosane, and benzaldehyde. Identified pollutants belong to Group 4, namely: carbofuran and indane, and from Group 5, an indicator of a certain bee developmental phase was isolated: heptadecane.

Communication within a bee colony represents a critically important aspect of its healthy functioning. Any sudden change, absence, or excessive presence of specific semiochemicals or pheromones indicates activities, phases, types of organization, and the condition in which the bees find themselves. Based on these facts, real-time detection of VOCs from Group 1 holds significant importance. Hexadecanoic acid, hexadecanamide (palmitamide), young queen pheromone, and cis-β-farnesene were all identified as components of the queen mandibular gland pheromone (QMP). The QMP plays a crucial role in regulating the behavior and physiology of honeybee colonies. When a honeybee queen is present and actively laying eggs, she produces QMP, which is distributed throughout the hive. This pheromone serves several important functions: Suppression of worker ovarian development — QMP inhibits the development of worker bee ovaries, preventing them from laying eggs. This helps maintain the reproductive dominance of the queen within the colony. Furthermore, worker retinue behavior — worker bees exhibit specific behaviors in response to QMP, such as tending to the queen, feeding her, and grooming her. This behavior ensures the well-being and comfort of the queen, as well as the overall health of the colony. Colony recognition — QMP aids in colony recognition, helping bees identify members of their own colony and distinguish them from bees of other colonies. This is crucial for maintaining colony cohesion and preventing conflicts with neighboring hives.

9-Octadecenamide, (Z) − , commonly known as Z9-18:Ac, is a compound found in the pheromones of honeybees. In beehives, Z9-18:Ac plays a crucial role in communication among colony members. It is specifically involved in the regulation of worker bee behavior, particularly in tasks related to foraging and food distribution within the hive.

Benzoic acid methyl ester — also known as methyl benzoate plays a role in communication and defense, as alarm pheromone. Methyl benzoate is one of the components of the alarm pheromone released by worker bees when the hive is threatened. This alarm pheromone alerts other bees to potential danger and triggers a defensive response, such as increased aggression toward the perceived threat.

The overlapping of groups can be observed, where, for example, benzoic acid methyl ester, based on the role of alarm pheromones, whose concentration increases in an unbalanced hive state, which may occur in the presence of bacteria, viruses, molds, and disease development. Furthermore, 9,12-octadecadienoic acid, methyl ester, also from Group 2, has antibacterial and antifungal properties, especially against American and European foulbrood, and it can be considered as disease index.

Analysis and monitoring of hive conditions through real-time VOCs could enhance and ensure a greater and higher-quality yield of bee products. Therefore, the detection of compounds characteristic of bee products is an essential group that should be monitored. From Group 3, α-bergamotene and α-bisabolol − are compounds characteristic of propolis, and benzaldehyde is VOCs characteristic for honey. On the other hand, heneicosane is a long-chain hydrocarbon found in honeycomb wax, which constitutes a vital component of beehives. Within beehives, heneicosane serves several critical functions: it is recognized as a component of QMP and contributes to the structural integrity of beeswax, utilized by honeybees in constructing honeycomb cells. Beeswax forms a stable framework for storing honey and pollen and developing brood within the hive. The presence of heneicosane aids in waterproofing the wax and maintaining its stability, thereby ensuring the structural integrity of the hive.

The mass spectrum of VOCs from beehive air samples, recorded by the MIMS device, indicates the presence of pollutants such as PAH (indane) and pesticide (carbofuran). A more detailed analysis of pollutant presence in the hives using the same MIMS technique is described in Ilić et al. (2024).

Throughout the entire brood developmental cycle (21 days, from the egg stage to adult bee emergence), temporal changes in the presence and abundance of compounds characterizing the brood can be monitored in real time using the MIMS technique. From Group 5, the VOC heptadecane was detected and isolated, which takes part in the odor blend of newly laid eggs. Semiochemical compounds emitted by the brood, which may play a role in communication between the brood and workers, are described in detail in Noël et al. (2023).

After processing the obtained results, an analysis of the relative abundance of VOCs in the atmosphere of the beehive was conducted. This analysis was based on the intensity of ion current during sampling with the MIMS device and true GC–MS benchmarking. The relative percentage representation was calculated using model peak deconvoluted area data (Fig. 6). For the need of data processing, an “in-house” Python application was developed. Experimentally obtained maximal ion current (IC) values in amperes (A) for selected m/z values from the scan of the air inside and outside the hive on the same day were multiplied by a factor of 1 × 1015 to facilitate graphical representation. Additionally, when multiple cycles of the same sample were scanned, the mean value of the data for a specific m/z value was calculated. From summarized results of relative abundance, it can be concluded that 9-octadecenamide, (Z), is the most dominant compound in all samples.

Conclusions

Research efforts aimed at comprehensively understanding and supporting bee populations have never been more imperative. Anthropogenic factors such as pesticide usage, habitat degradation, and climate change pose existential threats to bee colonies, highlighting the urgency for proactive conservation measures. In this regard, innovative technologies offer promising avenues for advancing bee health monitoring and management. One such groundbreaking advancement is the application of MIMS technology for noninvasive hive analysis which is described in this study.

Creating a novel bioanalytical technique for detecting VOCs in beehive air presents a formidable challenge. The composition of air within beehives is inherently volatile and influenced by numerous variables, including biological and environmental factors, making it an unstable analytical matrix. The versatility of MIMS extends beyond mere detection, encompassing a diverse array of applications ranging from health diagnostics to environmental monitoring. Through real-time analysis of VOC profiles, researchers can discern subtle changes indicative of hive conditions, disease outbreaks, or environmental stressors. Central to the efficacy of MIMS is its ability to detect a wide spectrum of compounds, which is demonstrated in the experiment from this study, each with distinct implications for bee health and hive integrity. From queen pheromones regulating colony behavior to alarm cues signaling threats, the identified VOCs provide a nuanced understanding of hive dynamics. Moreover, the detection of pollutants (indane and carbofuran) and disease indicators (9,12-octadecadienoic acid, methyl ester) underscores the broader ecological context within which bees operate, highlighting the intricate interplay between natural and anthropogenic factors.

By monitoring VOC profiles within beehives, MIMS aids in gauging ecosystem integrity and identifying potential threats to bee populations and broader biodiversity.

The integration of innovative technologies like MIMS represents a paradigm shift in bee conservation and management efforts. By harnessing the power of advanced analytics and real-time monitoring, we can empower stakeholders to make informed decisions and enact targeted interventions to safeguard bee populations and the ecosystems they inhabit. As we navigate the complex challenges facing bee populations in the twenty-first century, technological innovations offer a beacon of hope in our collective quest to ensure a sustainable future for both bees and humanity.

The next step in this research involves large-scale pilot sampling from beehives located in both nonurban and urban areas as well as the creation of a database with the classification of identified hive air profiles with certain compounds. Furthermore, to achieve more precise determination of fragmented compounds, a change in ionization energy will be introduced during sample analysis. By reducing electron energies for ionization to 20 eV (and below), most predominant fragments for the target compounds may be more distinguishable. This has already been shown for tryptophan by (Abdoul‑Carime et al. 2005). The data conceived in this way will help to form a system of preventive response to the possible consequences of the interaction of specific compounds and stressors according to established patterns of certain air profiles. This will be a significant step toward fast, reliable, and practical methods for identifying the health status of beehives in the field and, consequently sustainable, profitable, and healthy beekeeping.
