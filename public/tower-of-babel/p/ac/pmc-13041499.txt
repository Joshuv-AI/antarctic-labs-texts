Therapeutic rewiring of ceRNA networks: a computational pipeline for drug repurposing in acute kidney injury via circRNA–miRNA–mRNA axis disruption
Authors: Ming Wang, Feng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review synthesizes current evidence on competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulation in acute kidney injury (AKI) and proposes a practical, reproducible framework for computationally guided drug repurposing that aims to rewire pathogenic circRNA–miRNA–mRNA axes. We summarize the ceRNA concept and its biological rationale in renal injury, anchored to foundational descriptions of the ceRNA hypothesis. Building on curated circRNA resources and drug–gene mapping platforms, we present and critically evaluate a five-stage computational framework: (1) multi-omics data mining and ceRNA triplet inference, (2) network pharmacology mapping to drug–gene resources, (3) RNA-aware molecular docking to candidate structured RNA motifs, (4) molecular dynamics (MD) refinement and rescoring, and (5) integrative prioritization with explicit experimental filters for renal safety and tractability. We synthesize methodological best practices and limitations for each stage, with particular attention to the emerging field of RNA-targeted small molecules and their experimental validation. Three illustrative case studies demonstrate how distinct repurposing logics (direct RNA engagement, network/miRNA modulation, pathway-level modulation) can be mapped onto this pipeline and what orthogonal assays are required to progress candidates from in silico nomination to biochemical and in vivo testing. The review concludes with a pragmatic experimental roadmap, clear caveats (RNA structural dynamics, cellular context dependency, miRNA redundancy), and proposals to integrate single-cell and systems-pharmacology approaches to improve replicability and translational potential. By collating the literature and proposing a transparent, stepwise framework with validation benchmarks, this review aims to generate testable, prioritized in-silico hypotheses for RNA-aware repurposing in AKI that require rigorous biochemical and preclinical validation before any consideration of clinical testing. Because AKI arises from multiple, biologically distinct etiologies (for example ischemia–reperfusion injury, sepsis, nephrotoxic injury such as cisplatin exposure, and obstructive causes), candidate drugs and circRNA/miRNA targets must be evaluated in etiology-matched cellular and animal models; the pipeline therefore explicitly recommends etiology-aware filtering and model selection to avoid overgeneralizing findings across all AKI types.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, ceRNA networks, Drug repurposing, circRNA–miRNA–mRNA axis, Computational pharmacology

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common, clinically consequential syndrome associated with substantial short-term mortality, persistent risk of chronic kidney disease and high health-care utilization worldwide. The global AKI burden has been repeatedly documented across settings and criteria, and despite improved recognition and supportive care there remain limited mechanism-directed therapies that change long-term outcomes. This persistent therapeutic gap motivates approaches that rapidly convert mechanistic molecular insights into clinically actionable interventions [1, 2]. AKI is an etiologically heterogeneous syndrome. Major causes include ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI), sepsis-associated AKI, nephrotoxins (e.g., cisplatin), and obstructive causes, each of which produces distinct pathophysiologic and transcriptomic signatures in the kidney. This heterogeneity implies that a repurposed agent predicted to rewire a circRNA–miRNA–mRNA axis in one AKI context (for example sepsis) may not be effective, or could be harmful, in another (for example cisplatin nephrotoxicity). Accordingly, our pipeline emphasizes etiology-aware candidate triage and recommends testing nominated agents in etiology-matched in vitro and in vivo models before translational claims are advanced [3–5].

One promising conceptual framework that has emerged in the last decade is the competing endogenous RNA hypothesis, which reframes post-transcriptional regulation as a networked language in which messenger RNAs, long noncoding RNAs and circular RNAs communicate via shared microRNA response elements to modulate gene expression [6]. The ceRNA model offers an integrative view of how noncoding elements can titrate miRNA availability and thereby exert broad, coordinated control over protein coding networks that are relevant to injury, inflammation and repair. This perspective is particularly germane to AKI, which is a multifactorial syndrome where convergent dysregulation of stress response, inflammation, fibrosis and metabolism determines outcomes, and where network-based approaches can reveal disease-specific regulatory patterns [7]. Importantly, the ceRNA concept has moved beyond purely theoretical proposals to experimental demonstrations in multiple tissues, for example, the neuronal circRNA ciRS-7/CDR1as acts as an in vivo sponge for miR-7 [8, 9], and transcriptome-scale analyses show extensive tissue-specific ceRNA interaction networks during development [10], while quantitative studies have also shown that measurable stoichiometry and miRNA/target concentrations govern when ceRNA effects are detectable [11, 12].

Within the ceRNA landscape, circular RNAs have emerged as attractive therapeutic nodes because they are stable, often tissue-specific, and can potently modulate miRNA activity; primary experimental work has shown both loss-of-function (antisense-/gapmer-mediated circRNA knockdown) and gain-of-function (engineered circRNA ‘sponges’) can alter phenotype in vivo and in vitro [13, 14]. At the same time, small-molecule strategies for RNA targeting (e.g., RIBOTAC/RNase-recruiting chimeras) provide orthogonal ways to perturb regulatory RNAs [15–18]. Circular transcripts are inherently resistant to exonucleolytic decay, frequently conserved across species, and in many cases display cell type and developmental stage specificity, characteristics that make them more stable and potentially more specific molecular sponges than linear transcripts [19, 20]. These properties support two complementary therapeutic paradigms: targeting pathogenic circRNAs to release deleterious miRNA repression or leveraging engineered circRNA sponges to sequester injurious miRNAs and restore protective mRNA programs. Nonetheless, translating circRNA biology into therapy requires rigorous validation of target engagement and network impacts, because ceRNA effects are sensitive to expression levels, subcellular localization and miRNA abundance [12].

Drug repurposing provides a strategic shortcut to clinic because it pairs mechanistic insight with compounds that already have characterized pharmacokinetics and safety, thereby shortening timelines and reducing development risk [21, 22]. Historically repurposing has produced high impact clinical successes across diverse diseases, and recent advances in computational, omics and artificial intelligence methods enable systematic matching of existing drugs to complex, multifactorial pathologies for which single target approaches have failed [23, 24]. Given the networked, multi-nodal architecture of ceRNA regulation, repurposing approaches that integrate transcriptome-wide ceRNA perturbation signatures with drug-induced gene expression profiles offer a pragmatic path to prioritize candidates that can modulate the circRNA–miRNA–mRNA axis and thereby rewire pathogenic programs in AKI.

Accordingly, the present work aims to develop and validate a computational pipeline that converts high-resolution ceRNA network maps from injured kidney into ranked, repurposable pharmacologic interventions, with experimental prioritization of agents predicted to disrupt pathogenic circRNA–miRNA–mRNA axes. Our pipeline combines network-level ceRNA inference and circRNA-specific tractability filters with RNA-aware docking and MD validation, yielding repurposing candidates supported by both network evidence and atomistic models of RNA–ligand engagement. This two-tiered (network → structure → dynamics) approach differentiates our pipeline from reviews that treat ceRNA analysis and RNA small-molecule discovery separately [25, 26]. This approach seeks to move beyond single molecule discovery to a translationally oriented systems pharmaco-modulation of injury and repair in AKI.

Conceptual overview: ceRNA biology in AKI

Mining multi-omics data for ceRNA network construction

The first critical step in decoding the acute kidney injury competitive endogenous RNA landscape is systematic mining of public and specialized repositories to assemble complementary transcriptomic layers, and standard sources include the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus for bulk and small RNA expression data, which remains the canonical repository for mining case and control cohorts [27]. Complementary datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) can be useful as a tissue-level reference or for benchmarking analysis pipelines and methods (for example, assessing tissue-specific expression patterns and tool performance), but TCGA is a tumor-derived resource and does not represent acute injury biology. Therefore, TCGA data may be helpful for technical benchmarking and for comparing renal tissue regulatory architecture in the steady-state or tumor contexts, but it should not be used as direct biological evidence for acute kidney injury mechanisms without independent AKI-specific experimental validation [28]. ArrayExpress provides an independently curated corpus of microarray and sequencing experiments that is useful for replication and meta-analysis across platforms [29].

Because this pipeline is intended to be applied across public cohorts from different laboratories and measurement platforms, explicit, reproducible rules for dataset inclusion, preprocessing, normalization and batch-effect correction are required. Below we (i) justify combining datasets from distinct platforms and (ii) specify recommended, well-validated correction algorithms the user should apply.

Inclusion and platform-compatibility criteria. We recommend combining datasets only after verifying (a) species and genome build consistency, (b) comparable tissue/cell type and sample preparation (e.g., bulk kidney tissue vs. tubular-enriched microdissections), (c) presence of raw data or count-level quantification, and (d) availability of sufficient sample- and batch-level metadata (study ID, platform, library protocol, read length, library depth, RNA extraction method). For microarray vs. RNA-seq combinations we recommend avoiding direct merge of normalized intensities and counts, instead treat technology classes separately (e.g., perform parallel analyses and meta-analysis or transform to comparable summary statistics such as effect sizes) unless a clear harmonization strategy is available.

Recommended preprocessing and normalization. For bulk RNA-seq use raw integer counts as input to downstream normalization workflows (e.g., edgeR/DESeq2 normalization or voom for limma), and for circRNA backsplice junction counts use the raw junction counts normalized by library size prior to modelling. For microarray data retain the raw probe intensities and apply robust background correction and quantile normalization before platform-level adjustment. For single-cell datasets, we recommend SCTransform or log-normalization plus selection of highly variable genes before integration.

Batch-effect correction algorithms. For known batch variables in microarray or normalized expression matrices use ComBat (empirical Bayes adjustment) to remove additive and multiplicative batch effects [30]. For bulk RNA-seq count matrices we recommend ComBat-seq (negative-binomial based ComBat for counts) which preserves integer counts and maintains compatibility with standard differential expression tools [31]. When latent/unobserved confounders are suspected use sva (Surrogate Variable Analysis) to estimate and adjust for hidden sources of variation [32]. For single-cell RNA-seq integration use established single-cell integration methods such as Seurat v3/v4 anchors or Harmony, depending on dataset size and integration goals; Harmony performs well for large cell numbers and provides LISI/kBET compatible embeddings for validation [33, 34].

Rationale and caveats. We emphasize that batch correction can remove both unwanted technical variation and, when confounded, true biological signal. Thus, whenever possible, preserve the biological covariate of interest in the design matrix supplied to ComBat/ComBat-seq/sva (i.e., do not pool cases and controls across batches without modelling condition). When batch and biological condition are fully confounded (e.g., cases = study A only and controls = study B only), avoid cross-study merging and instead use meta-analytic approaches or seek additional data. The pipeline therefore treats batch-correction as a structured, reported step with objective validation rather than an implicit black box [30–32].

circRNA-specific atlases and repositories such as circBase and the recently expanded circAtlas 3.0 enable reliable annotation of backsplice junctions and isoform sequences and are indispensable for mapping candidate circRNAs to genomic coordinates and expression evidence [35, 36]. For miRNA target discovery and hypothesis generation, established prediction engines including TargetScan and miRanda deliver complementary seed- and energy-based predictions that should be intersected rather than relied upon in isolation [37]. An effective mining strategy explicitly documents the source and file types used: we recommend (i) identifying case–control gene expression and small-RNA studies in NCBI GEO (search Series and DataSet records; download Series Matrix files and raw FASTQ where available) or in EMBL-EBI BioStudies (ArrayExpress-migrated) (download raw/processed data and sample metadata), (ii) annotating circRNAs using circBase identifiers and circAtlas 3.0 coordinates to confirm backsplice junctions and cross-sample evidence, and (iii) performing parallel small-RNA read processing (adapter trimming → alignment → miRNA quantification) and intersecting expressed miRNAs with in-silico miRNA–mRNA predictions (e.g., TargetScan, miRanda) and expression anticorrelation to produce a reproducible candidate list for ceRNA modeling [27, 36]. Identification of dysregulated elements must then proceed with rigorous differential expression and co-expression filtering to nominate AKI-associated circRNAs. Recent patient and model studies have reported multiple AKI-associated circRNAs, for example, circHIPK3 has been repeatedly implicated in sepsis-AKI and cisplatin models where knockdown reduces oxidative stress and apoptosis, and hsa_circ_0005519 was identified in patient RNA-seq and validated as an AKI-relevant sponge, reinforcing the clinical and mechanistic relevance of these nodes for ceRNA analysis [16, 38, 39]. By contrast, circRNAs like hsa_circ_0002137 and other catalogued identifiers have stronger evidence in nonrenal contexts but nevertheless represent mechanistic exemplars for circRNA–miRNA–mRNA axes because hsa_circ_0002137 modulates BCL2 expression in orthogonal systems and so can serve as a biochemical template for candidate-driven interrogation in AKI samples [40, 41].

Recommended DE thresholds, rationale, and sensitivity testing. When performing primary differential expression for pipeline applications we recommend reporting results from both (A) statistical and (B) effect-size perspectives rather than applying a single, fixed cutoff. Statistically, use an FDR-controlling procedure (e.g., Benjamini–Hochberg) and report adjusted p-values (FDR); a commonly accepted reporting threshold is FDR < 0.05, but FDR ≤ 0.10 can be reported when justified by low sample size or exploratory aims. From an effect-size (biological) perspective, report fold change as log2FC and avoid arbitrarily excluding modest but consistent changes; useful practice is to provide results at multiple |log2FC| cutoffs (for example ≥ 0.5 and ≥ 1.0) and to annotate findings that are both statistically significant and exceed a biologically meaningful effect size. Also report minimum expression/abundance filters (for example baseMean or CPM thresholds) so that low-count artefacts are excluded from fold change summaries. Finally, perform and report a sensitivity analysis that shows how the key nominated circRNA/miRNA/mRNA triplets change across at least two FDR thresholds (e.g., 0.05 vs. 0.10) and two log2FC cutoffs (e.g., 0.5 vs. 1.0) [42].

When combining expression datasets from different platforms (bulk RNA-seq, small RNA-seq, microarray) or from independent studies, explicit assessment of platform compatibility and a documented normalization pipeline are essential to avoid spurious associations driven by technical artifacts. We therefore recommend the following reproducible decision workflow: (i) metadata harmonization and QC: download raw counts or CEL files and harmonize sample/experimental metadata; perform initial quality control and exploratory PCA/hierarchical clustering to detect strong study or batch effects; (ii) platform-specific preprocessing: for RNA-seq use established count models (DESeq2 or edgeR) for differential testing and apply variance-stabilizing or TMM normalization as appropriate; for microarrays apply robust multi-array averaging (RMA) and quantile normalization [43–45]. (iii) cross-platform integration rules: combine datasets only when metadata and experimental conditions are sufficiently comparable (similar sample type, preservation, library protocol); if study heterogeneity is low, transform counts to log2-CPM/voom and use limma frameworks to jointly model data across studies; when platform differences are large (e.g., microarray vs. RNA-seq with different probe sets) prefer meta-analysis (study-level effect aggregation) rather than naive pooling to avoid bias [46]. (iv) batch effect detection and correction: assess residual technical structure with PCA and surrogate variable analysis; use empirical-Bayes methods (ComBat) or the sva framework to remove known/latent batch effects while preserving biological signal. If batch covaries with the variable of interest, avoid aggressive correction and prefer study-level meta-analysis [30, 32]. (v) reporting and reproducibility: state clearly which raw inputs were used, which normalization/transformations were applied, and provide software versions and parameters in Supplementary Methods or a pipeline repository. When aggregating across platforms we recommend providing sensitivity analyses that compare pooled results to study-level meta-analysis to demonstrate robustness. These steps justify and constrain any dataset combination used in ceRNA construction, and they reduce the risk of platform-driven false correlations that would confound circRNA–miRNA–mRNA triplet nomination [30, 44–47].

Similarly, some circ identifiers such as hsa_circ_0008899 appear in large-scale circRNA inventories and therefore are reasonable exploratory candidates even if direct AKI validation is pending [48]. Downstream miRNA selection should be informed by both expression anticorrelation and predicted binding; for AKI the literature repeatedly implicates miR-16-5p as proapoptotic via BCL2 targeting, miR-144-3p in oxidative and mitochondrial responses, and miR-150-5p in modulating inflammatory and fibrotic programs, making these miRNAs high-priority mediators to test within ceRNA models [49–51]. Finally, mRNA targets to include in an AKI ceRNA network should be chosen for mechanistic plausibility and therapeutic relevance, for example canonical apoptosis regulators such as BCL2, central inflammatory effectors including NFKB1, ferroptosis executors exemplified by GPX4, and canonical profibrotic receptors such as TGFBR1 [52–55]. This narrative review did not perform primary re-analysis of raw transcriptomic data. Where we reference differential-expression (DE) outcomes reported in the literature we have quoted the original authors’ reported thresholds. For clarity, when the pipeline described here is applied to raw transcriptomic inputs we recommend the following principled approach for choosing and reporting DE thresholds (statistical and biological justification) and performing sensitivity analyses. We identified and summarised published human and animal RNA studies that reported circRNA/miRNA/mRNA interactions in AKI using the following search strategy (databases searched: PubMed, GEO, ArrayExpress; search terms: ‘acute kidney injury’, ‘circRNA’, ‘circular RNA’, ‘ceRNA’, ‘miRNA’; date range: Jan 2000 – Dec 2025). Inclusion criteria for cited studies were: (1) primary human or relevant animal model of AKI with reported circRNA/miRNA data, (2) sufficient methods detail to extract AKI definition and sample characteristics, and (3) peer-reviewed publication. Exclusion criteria: (1) reviews, (2) case reports lacking molecular data, and (3) studies without clear AKI case/control definitions.

Constructing and validating the pathogenic ceRNA network

Constructing a pathogenic ceRNA network for AKI requires a reproducible bioinformatics pipeline that integrates differential expression, pairwise correlation, and target prediction to nominate circRNA–miRNA–mRNA triplets with consistent expression patterns and predicted binding sites [57–59]. Because correlation alone is insufficient, we additionally require explicit, reportable tests of core ceRNA assumptions (relative abundance/stoichiometry; shared miRNA competition; and inverse/positive correlation patterns) [11, 12]. To avoid over-interpreting correlative triplets, any ceRNA reconstruction must explicitly test the three core assumptions that underpin ceRNA theory: (1) relative abundance/stoichiometry (miRNA molecules vs. total target sites), (2) shared-miRNA competition (evidence that candidate RNAs share the same functional miRNA binding in vivo), and (3) inverse/positive correlation patterns consistent with competition (miRNA vs. target inverse correlation; sponge RNA vs. mRNA positive correlation). Practically, we recommend the following minimal, reportable checks whenever a circRNA–miRNA–mRNA triplet is proposed: (A) absolute/stoichiometric quantification: measure miRNA and target RNA copy numbers per cell (qPCR/ddPCR with synthetic spike-in standards or single-molecule FISH to obtain copies per cell) and calculate total MRE (miRNA response element) site load per cell to assess whether the system lies in the titration-sensitive range described in quantitative analyses of ceRNA competition [11, 12]. (B) shared-site validation: demonstrate shared functional binding using orthogonal biochemical maps such as AGO-CLIP/PAR-CLIP or reporter assays with wildtype vs. MRE-mutant sequences and targeted mutagenesis of predicted sites to show loss/gain of regulation [60, 61]. (C) directional/causal perturbations: perform controlled perturbations (titrated overexpression or knockdown of the circRNA and miRNA mimic/antagomir experiments, plus rescue experiments) and show dose-dependent effects predicted by molecular titration models (e.g., threshold behaviour), rather than relying solely on passive correlations across cohorts [12, 62]. (D) single-cell or cell-type resolved analyses: where possible, use single-cell RNA-seq or smFISH to verify that the candidate RNAs and miRNAs are co-expressed in the same cell type/subcellular compartment (a necessary precondition for meaningful competition) [63]. Finally, report these checks transparently (copy numbers per cell, MRE site counts, AGO-CLIP evidence, reporter assay p-values, and perturbation dose–response curves) in either the main text or the Supplementary Methods so readers can judge whether the proposed ceRNA interactions are mechanistically plausible or likely to be passive correlations.

Practically, this workflow begins with explicit, platform-aware preprocessing and normalization rather than a single generic normal notice, we recommend: (1) preprocess each dataset using platform-appropriate methods (RNA-seq: count-based normalization and variance stabilization using DESeq2 or edgeR; microarray: RMA and quantile normalization), (2) evaluate platform and batch effects with PCA and hierarchical clustering, (3) if technical effects are present and not confounded with biological groups, apply empirical-Bayes batch correction (ComBat) or surrogate variable adjustment (sva), (4) if combining RNA-seq and microarray data consider transformation to log2-CPM and voom to permit joint linear modeling with limma, and (5) where platform heterogeneity remains high prefer study-level meta-analysis (random-effects aggregation) instead of naive pooling. These explicit steps reduce false positive circRNA–miRNA–mRNA correlations that arise from technical artifacts and provide a documented justification for any merged dataset used for ceRNA inference [30, 43–47]. When human cohorts with outcome metadata are available, integrating survival or recovery analyses provides orthogonal evidence of clinical relevance, but in AKI many clinical cohorts lack long-term survival endpoints, so networks are frequently prioritized by enrichment for injury-relevant pathways rather than prognostic statistics alone [57, 64].

Functional enrichment using Gene Ontology and KEGG contextualizes candidate mRNA sets and has repeatedly shown that AKI-associated ceRNA modules converge on TNF signaling, hypoxia inducible factor biology, and p53-mediated cell death programs, thereby linking transcriptomic perturbations to the known pathophysiology of tubular injury and inflammation [54, 65]. Network topology analysis then identifies hubs by degree and betweenness centrality, and experimental prioritization should focus on high-centrality circRNAs or miRNAs that connect multiple pathogenic mRNAs because perturbing these nodes has the greatest potential to rewire pathogenic outputs [66]. Mechanistically, this axis can be understood as a stoichiometry-dependent titration: elevated circ_0008899 could sequester miR-16-5p in the cytoplasm, reducing miR-16-5p loading into Argonaute complexes and thereby releasing translational repression of the anti-apoptotic transcript BCL2. If the circRNA: miRNA ratio crosses the functional threshold described for ceRNA interactions, small changes in circRNA abundance translate into disproportionate derepression of BCL2 and measurable reductions in tubular apoptosis. Conversely, modest miR-16-5p elevation can overcome sponge capacity and re-impose repression, a dynamic consistent with stoichiometric modeling and quantitative measurements of miRNA/target abundance [12, 41, 48, 49].

At a mechanistic level, circHIPK3 appears to act as a cytoplasmic reservoir that reduces the free concentration of specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-338), thereby permitting higher translation of targets such as FOXA1 and upstream regulators of NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling; this pattern maps onto observed increases in pro-inflammatory transcripts and cytokine release in LPS models. Critically, demonstrating functional ceRNA activity for circHIPK3 requires three orthogonal checks: (i) demonstration of physical association (RNA pulldown or AGO-CLIP showing miRNA occupancy), (ii) evidence of stoichiometric sufficiency (quantitative qPCR/absolute copy-number showing circRNA: miRNA ratios consistent with titration), and (iii) functional rescue (mir-mimic or antimiR rescue of the phenotype). The circHIPK3 sepsis-AKI study provides experimental precedence for this chain of evidence [66–68]. Mechanistically, increased miR-144-3p levels may reduce expression of GPX4, lowering cellular capacity to detoxify lipid peroxides and thereby sensitizing tubular epithelial cells to ferroptotic death during AKI. A circRNA that sequesters miR-144-3p can therefore act upstream to preserve GPX4 levels and delay ferroptotic execution, but only if circRNA abundance and subcellular localization permit effective cytoplasmic sequestration. Interrogating this axis requires lipid-peroxidation assays, GPX4 protein quantification, and rescue experiments with antagomiRs or circRNA knockdown in cisplatin/IRI AKI models. This interpretation connects transcriptomic triplets to a defined cell-death mechanism (ferroptosis) recently reviewed in AKI [50, 53, 69]. From networks to druggable targets

Identification of hub RNAs with high network centrality and tractability is a natural prioritization strategy in network analysis. However, computational prioritization alone does not establish therapeutic tractability or biomarker validity, such nominations remain putative until confirmed by orthogonal biochemical and in-vivo experiments. We therefore recommend explicit wording throughout the manuscript that nominated targets are computationally prioritized hypotheses for experimental follow-up rather than validated therapeutic targets [66]. Intervention points can be conceptualized along three axes: first, direct disruption of circRNA–miRNA interactions using antisense oligonucleotides or gapmers to degrade or sterically block the circRNA, approaches that have proven feasible for selective circRNA knockdown in vitro and in vivo [14, 72].

Second, antimiR strategies such as antagomirs or locked nucleic acid inhibitors can neutralize pathogenic miRNAs and thereby restore expression of protective mRNAs; these chemistries have mature preclinical pedigrees and are amenable to renal delivery strategies [73, 74].

Third, modulation of RNA binding proteins and editing enzymes that control circRNA biogenesis offers an upstream entry point, for example targeting QKI to alter backsplicing or manipulating ADAR to change A-to-I editing landscapes, and these approaches have been shown to remodel the circular transcriptome in experimental systems [75, 76]. Each of these strategies carries mechanistic and safety trade-offs and therefore should be triaged by integrated evidence including expression specificity, conserved binding sites across species, and proof-of-concept perturbation in AKI models [57, 77]. In summary, rigorous multi-omics mining, statistically principled ceRNA assembly, and prioritization of hub nodes combined with established RNA-targeting modalities create a coherent path from descriptive networks to therapeutically tractable hypotheses in AKI, but success will demand careful experimental validation in the tissue and temporal contexts that define injury and repair [14, 27, 58].

Current computational tools and best-practice summary

Before proceeding we note that this manuscript is a narrative review that describes and evaluates a purely computational (in silico) pipeline for ceRNA-centered drug repurposing. All analytic workflows, illustrative case studies and methodological recommendations presented here were derived from literature synthesis and computational approaches (network inference, database mapping, RNA-aware docking and molecular dynamics) rather than from new wet-lab or animal experiments. Experimental assays and in vivo models are proposed as a recommended validation roadmap for future work but were not performed as part of this study. For guidance on transparent reporting and credibility assessment of in-silico studies we refer readers to recent recommendations and methodological reviews.

Stage 1: network pharmacology-based screening

The first stage of a computational repurposing pipeline for ceRNA-mediated acute kidney injury places the reconstructed circRNA–miRNA–mRNA subnetwork at the center and treats the proteins translated from network mRNAs as the immediate points of pharmacological intervention. Public, curated drug–gene interaction resources such as DGIdb, ChEMBL and DrugBank provide complementary coverage of approved drugs, clinical candidates, and annotated bioactivities and are therefore natural starting points for systematic mapping of compound–target edges onto the ceRNA-derived proteome [56, 71, 78]. In practice, a reproducible pipeline projects each mRNA node to its UniProt/Entrez identifier, queries DGIdb for experimentally or clinically supported interactions, retrieves bioactivity and binding-annotation records from ChEMBL, and augments these with DrugBank’s mechanism and clinical-status metadata to identify licensed agents that directly or indirectly perturb the mapped proteins [56, 71].

Beyond a forward mapping from network to drug, a reverse or hypothesis-driven strategy starts from classes of agents already implicated in nephroprotection and asks which nodes in the ceRNA network they are likely to modulate. Clinically validated renal protectants such as SGLT2 inhibitors and, increasingly, GLP-1 receptor agonists constitute high-value seeds for this reverse analysis because their systemic and intrarenal effects converge on processes relevant to AKI pathogenesis including tubular metabolism, inflammation and cell stress pathways [79–81]. A reverse workflow thus (i) assembles a seed list of nephroprotective drugs, (ii) retrieves their known protein targets and pathway annotations from DrugBank and ChEMBL, and (iii) evaluates overlap or shortest-path proximity between those targets and ceRNA-mRNA products in protein–protein interaction space. This combined forward and reverse strategy both broadens mechanistic hypotheses and prioritizes repurposing candidates that are biomedically plausible and clinically actionable [71, 82].

Stage 2: molecular docking to RNA targets, a cutting-edge frontier

Contemporary small-molecule pharmacology has moved beyond proteins to recognize that structured RNA motifs are druggable functional sites. Well characterized examples include splicing modulators and RIBOTACs that recruit cellular nucleases to degrade specific precursors, demonstrating that small molecules can bind pre-miRNA hairpins, bulges and junctions with selectivity sufficient for biological effect []. Recent work has shown these approaches are rapidly maturing into practical therapeutic modalities. Small-molecule degraders (RIBOTAC/RiboTAC/RNATAC families) can recruit endogenous ribonucleases to catalytically degrade disease-linked RNAs, converting a simple RNA binder into a sub-stoichiometric degrader with demonstrable in-cell and in-vivo activity. Foundational experimental demonstrations (for example Costales et al., PNAS 2020) established that selective recruitment of RNase activity to a pre-miRNA can reduce mature miRNA levels and blunt pathological programs, and subsequent methodological and review studies (e.g., Bonet-Aleta 2024; RNATAC literature 2024) have fleshed out design principles and emerging caveats (off-target nuclease recruitment, activation context dependency). These small-molecule degraders complement oligonucleotide modalities by providing non-oligonucleotide routes to RNA depletion, but they pose specific challenges for delivery and selectivity that must be considered in any repurposing pipeline [18, 86, 87].

This biological precedent establishes the rationale for docking small molecules not only to protein products of ceRNA networks but also to the RNA elements that sustain the ceRNA interactions themselves, most notably seed-complementary segments in miRNA precursors and predicted structured regions within hub circRNAs.

Methodologically, RNA-targeted docking begins with realistic three dimensional models of the RNA targets. Tertiary structure generators and folding simulators such as RNAComposer and SimRNA are widely used to produce atomistic or coarse-grained starting models of pre-miRNAs, miRNA-mRNA duplexes and structured circRNA fragments when experimental structures are unavailable [88, 89]. Libraries of repurposing candidates or FDA-approved molecules are prepared in 3D from ZINC15, DrugBank and ChEMBL to ensure comprehensive chemical coverage during virtual screening [71, 90]. For docking, tools that have been validated or adapted for nucleic acid targets such as rDock, HDOCK and recent versions of AutoDock Vina provide practical options; however, benchmarking studies show that RNA-specialized scoring and pose-generation protocols tend to outperform protein-centric engines and that combined use of orthogonal programs improves hit recovery [91–93].

Critically, the RNA docking stage must recognize limitations and avoid overinterpretation. RNA structural heterogeneity, ion and water-mediated interactions, and the frequently shallow, solvent-exposed nature of RNA binding pockets reduce the predictive power of single docking scores; consequently, docking should be used as a prioritization filter to select chemically plausible binders for higher fidelity evaluation rather than as definitive proof of targeting. The field’s recent methodological advances and successful case studies nevertheless justify inclusion of RNA docking as a core stage when the ceRNA hypothesis explicitly implicates discrete and structurally constrained RNA motifs [26, 83].

Stage 3: molecular dynamics simulations for validation

Top-scoring docking poses require dynamic validation because static docking cannot capture conformational adaptation, solvent effects or the kinetic stability of RNA–ligand complexes. All-atom molecular dynamics simulations using established engines such as AMBER or GROMACS and RNA-appropriate force field parametrizations provide the standard route to refine docked complexes and to measure stability metrics including RMSD, hydrogen-bond occupancy and contact lifetimes [94, 95]. For RNA systems, use of revised Amber torsion parameters and RNA-optimized corrections has been shown to materially improve fidelity of backbone dynamics and hairpin behavior, reducing artifactual fraying that would otherwise confound binding analyses [96].

Free energy rescoring by end-point methods such as MM/GBSA is a pragmatic compromise between accuracy and throughput for post-MD ranking of candidates. Systematic assessments indicate MM/GBSA can meaningfully re-rank docking hits and correlate with experimental affinities for nucleic acid systems when protocols account for explicit solvent minimization and appropriate generalized Born settings [97, 98]. Beyond thermodynamic metrics, MD trajectories allow mechanistic interrogation of whether a small molecule sterically or electrostatically perturbs the native miRNA–mRNA pairing or the circRNA structural element that scaffolds ceRNA interactions. Quantifying disruption of the native RNA–RNA interaction through contact-mapping, free-energy decomposition and perturbation of predicted Dicer or RISC recognition motifs yields mechanistic plausibility that strengthens repurposing hypotheses [83, 99]. While Table 3 lists commonly used programs and parameters, users should be aware of practical trade-offs in sensitivity, specificity and RNA-adaptation. Briefly, circRNA detection tools show large differences in sensitivity (false negatives) though precision is often comparable between callers; docking engines originally developed for proteins (e.g., AutoDock Vina) are usable for RNA but typically underperform on pose ranking versus RNA-aware tools (rDock) or workflows that re-score with RNA-specific functions (AnnapuRNA). Hybrid servers (HDOCK) are convenient for protein–RNA modeling but may require template guidance and careful preprocessing for single-stranded or highly flexible RNA motifs. In practice, combining multiple docking engines, validating RNA secondary structure prior to 3D modelling, and post-hoc re-scoring or MD refinement improves hit reliability [26, 48, 92, 100]. Stage 4: integrative prioritization

The final stage integrates orthogonal evidence streams into a single ranked list suitable for experimental follow-up. A defensible scoring schema weights network metrics and physicochemical tractability alongside clinical feasibility, importantly, most clinically successful nucleic acid therapeutics to date use liver-directed delivery platforms (GalNAc conjugates, LNPs) or systemic formulations that do not naturally accumulate in kidney; therefore, a prioritized candidate must be evaluated not only for target engagement but also for renal deliverability (conjugation/vehicle availability, receptor-mediated uptake potential, and existing clinical PK/safety data). The current clinical landscape of approved oligonucleotide/siRNA agents underscores both the promise of RNA therapeutics and the delivery gap that must be bridged for kidney indications [71, 82, 102–104]. Multi-criterion decision algorithms may combine standardized z-scores or probabilistic rankings from each domain and then apply clinical filters to prioritize agents with acceptable renal safety profiles for acute intervention in AKI. Importantly, integrative prioritization exposes tensions that merit critical review: high network centrality does not guarantee tractable RNA binding, and a chemically plausible RNA binder may have an unfavorable safety signal that precludes clinical testing in acutely ill kidney patients [92, 105].

In summary, a reproducible computational pipeline for ceRNA-centered drug repurposing in AKI orchestrates curated drug–gene databases, targeted RNA docking and rigorous MD validation, and closes with a transparent, multi-criteria prioritization that maps in silico hypotheses to feasible translational paths. The approach is not yet prescriptive. It is a hypothesis generation engine that reduces the experimental search space and provides mechanistic rationales that can be tested in focused biochemical and renal cell models before animal or clinical evaluation [56, 82, 83]. Translational drug-repurposing potential and case studies

Candidate 1: repurposing an anticancer drug (e.g., mitoxantrone)

Mechanistically, mitoxantrone’s nucleic-acid affinity could stabilize or distort pre-miRNA hairpin conformations, interfere with Dicer processing, and thereby reduce mature miRNA abundance (for example miR-17 family members). However, because mitoxantrone intercalates nucleic acids broadly and has cytotoxic activity, any putative benefit via miRNA processing must be weighed against widespread transcriptional/RNA processing perturbations and direct toxicity. The computational prediction therefore generates two testable mechanistic hypotheses: (A) mitoxantrone selectively stabilizes a pre-miRNA conformation that reduces Dicer cleavage efficiency (measure via in vitro Dicer assays and pre- vs. mature miRNA qPCR), or (B) mitoxantrone causes nonselective RNA binding that indirectly alters miRNA biogenesis (assessable by global small RNA-seq and toxicity readouts). These assays distinguish specific RNA-targeted activity from general nucleic-acid reactivity and clarify translational feasibility [12, 83, 84, 106]. To reduce systemic toxicity while retaining potential RNA-targeting activity, mitoxantrone (or related ligands) could be evaluated in kidney-targeted formulations: receptor-directed conjugates (e.g., carbohydrate/peptide RENTAC-like moieties that exploit megalin/cubilin uptake), nanoparticle formulations engineered for renal tubule accrual, or locally administered formulations in model systems. Early preclinical work should therefore compare systemic vs. kidney-targeted delivery in biodistribution and toxicity assays described above to determine whether a therapeutic window exists for RNA-engagement without unacceptable off-target cytotoxicity. Evidence that kidney-directed conjugates can drive proximal tubule uptake in vivo (RENTAC) supports the feasibility of this strategy but also highlights the need for receptor-dependency and injury-state testing [85, 107, 108].

In the context of acute kidney injury, the miR-17 ~ 92 cluster and in particular miR-17-5p have been implicated in renal ischemia–reperfusion biology where modulation of miR-17 family members alters PTEN expression and downstream Akt/Beclin1 signaling, a pathway that governs apoptotic and autophagic responses critical for tubular cell survival and functional recovery [109, 110]. Taken together, these two independent observations motivate the hypothesis that mitoxantrone, by physically binding to a pre-miR hairpin within miR-17 ~ 92 or to flanking structural elements that regulate its processing, could reduce pathological miR-17 activity and thereby de-repress PTEN to recalibrate AKI recovery dynamics [111, 112]. The mechanistic rationale for mitoxantrone (perturbation of pre-miRNA processing) is most plausibly tested in ischemia–reperfusion and related IRI models where the miR-17 family has documented roles; mitoxantrone’s broad nucleic-acid reactivity and systemic toxicity also make it a poor candidate for immediate clinical repurposing in sepsis-AKI without targeted delivery or formulation changes [106].

From a computational perspective, structure-based virtual screening and RNA-aware docking workflows that have recently matured provide a tractable pipeline to evaluate mitoxantrone–pre-miR interactions in silico; these methods have been successfully applied to pre-miRNA elements of the oncomiR-1 cluster and other disease-relevant hairpins and can prioritize binding modes for biochemical validation [113, 114]. However, several critical caveats must be emphasized. First, RNA conformational heterogeneity and protein cofactor effects can radically alter small molecule access and affinity so predicted binding does not guarantee functional inhibition of miRNA biogenesis [115]. Second, mitoxantrone’s cytotoxic pharmacology and global nucleic acid reactivity raise substantial safety and off-target liabilities in nononcologic indications; any repurposing strategy must therefore consider formulation, dosing, and targeted delivery to the injured kidney to avoid unacceptable systemic toxicity [116]. Finally, because the miR-17 ~ 92 cluster exhibits context dependent, sometimes protective roles in renal pathophysiology, simple inhibition could be deleterious in certain injury stages; accordingly, in silico docking should be complemented with staged in vitro assays of miRNA processing, Argonaute loading, and functional readouts in tubular cells prior to animal experiments [117, 118]. Candidate 2: repurposing a metabolic drug (e.g., metformin)

Metformin is a pleiotropic biguanide whose canonical actions through AMPK and mitochondrial complex I inhibition are well described but whose transcriptome and noncoding RNA effects have attracted increasing attention as potential mediators of organ protection [119]. Notably, multiple studies report that metformin modulates the miR-34a–SIRT1–p53 regulatory loop, with metformin treatment associated with induction of miR-34a and consequent downregulation of SIRT1 in several cellular contexts, thereby amplifying p53 dependent transcriptional programs [120, 121]. This canonical miR-34a/SIRT1/p53 axis is highly relevant to kidney stress responses because SIRT1 activity mitigates cellular senescence and oxidative injury and preserves tubular epithelial homeostasis, while p53 governs injury-induced apoptosis and regeneration [119, 122].

Network level analyses performed on drug–miRNA interaction databases and transcriptional perturbation signatures further suggest that metformin exerts indirect modulation across miRNA families beyond miR-34a, and therefore could provoke functional rewiring of ceRNA networks that converge on SIRT1 and p53 nodes; such rewiring may occur without direct small molecule binding to RNA but rather through altered transcription factor activity, epigenetic modulation, or changes in miRNA processing [123, 124]. The therapeutic attractiveness of metformin for repurposing in AKI is that its safety profile is well characterized and kidney-targeted dosing strategies have precedent, but important limitations exist because the net effect of increasing miR-34a can be either protective or harmful depending on cellular context and the state of p53 [119, 125]. Operationally, the most informative proof of concept experiments will combine network inference from patient or animal AKI transcriptomes with perturbational assays that measure how metformin shifts ceRNA titration between SIRT1 mRNA, competing circRNAs or lncRNAs, and miR-34a, and whether those shifts translate into altered SIRT1 protein activity, p53 acetylation status, and renal repair outcomes [124, 126]. Metformin’s transcriptional/miRNA effects are most relevant in metabolic or ischemic stress contexts and may interact with p53 status; therefore testing should prioritize metabolic/ischemia models and measure p53/SIRT1 endpoints, since effects may differ in sepsis-associated or cisplatin AKI [120].

Candidate 3: a neurological agent (e.g., Propofol)

Propofol is an intravenous anesthetic whose pleiotropic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties have been repeatedly linked to organ protection, including renal protection in ischemia–reperfusion models where propofol pretreatment enhances nuclear localization of Nrf2 and upregulates downstream antioxidant responses [127, 128]. Mechanistically, miR-144-3p has emerged as a negative regulator of Nrf2 in multiple tissue settings and increased renal miR-144-3p is associated with suppressed NRF2 axis activity and enhanced oxidative stress in experimental kidney disease, which makes the miR-144/Nrf2 node an attractive target for ceRNA-centered manipulation [129, 130]. Separately, circ_0000284 has been validated as a cytoplasmic competing endogenous RNA in oncologic systems and therefore represents a plausible scaffold that, if expressed in injured renal epithelium, could sequester miRNAs and modulate NRF2 translation indirectly [129, 131].

Bringing these strands together suggests a testable model in which propofol, by mechanisms that include augmentation of Nrf2 signaling and regulation of miRNA expression, could favorably rewire a putative circ_0000284/miR-144-3p/NRF2 ceRNA axis to reduce oxidative damage during AKI [130, 132]. To explore this computationally, in silico docking of propofol or propofol-like fragments to accessible structural motifs in the NRF2 3′ untranslated region or to miRNA precursor hairpins could be used as a hypothesis generator because small molecules have been shown to bind functional RNA motifs and thereby alter miRNA maturation or mRNA stability [83, 112]. Nevertheless, such docking predictions should be considered exploratory because propofol is a low molecular weight, highly lipophilic anesthetic whose in vivo effects are more plausibly mediated by protein targets and redox modulation than by high affinity RNA binding; therefore empirical validation using RNA pulldown, reporter assays for 3′UTR occupancy and loss-of-function studies for circ_0000284 will be essential to demonstrate bona fide ceRNA rewiring [128, 129]. Propofol’s antioxidant/Nrf2 effects are best evaluated in ischemia–reperfusion and oxidative-stress models (e.g., IRI, remote I/R paradigms) rather than sepsis models; thus its nomination should be treated as etiology-specific until cross-etiology efficacy is demonstrated [127].

In summary, each candidate exemplifies a distinct repurposing logic: direct RNA binding and miRNA processing interference for mitoxantrone, network-level epigenetic and miRNA modulation for metformin, and antioxidant-linked miRNA/ceRNA modulation for propofol [106, 121, 128]. All three approaches are scientifically plausible yet carry specific mechanistic uncertainties and safety considerations that mandate a rigorous sequence of in silico docking or network inference, biochemical validation, cellular functional assays, and kidney-targeted in vivo studies before clinical translation [111, 113]. Challenges, validation, and future directions

Technical and biological challenges

Computational pipelines that propose therapeutics to rewire circRNA–miRNA–mRNA competitive endogenous RNA networks face fundamental in silico limitations because RNA structural ensembles are highly dynamic and popular molecular mechanics force fields still struggle to reproduce experimentally observed conformational populations for many RNA motifs [133, 134]. An other, practical challenge for translating RNA-targeted hypotheses in AKI is renal delivery. Unlike liver-directed oligonucleotide programs (e.g., GalNAc conjugates), the kidney requires different uptake routes and is heterogenous in endocytic competence. Proximal tubule epithelial cells express high levels of endocytic receptors (megalin and cubilin) that mediate uptake of many macromolecules and can be exploited for receptor-mediated delivery, but receptor distribution and injury-dependent changes alter uptake efficiency and safety. Recent work has demonstrated chemically-defined kidney-targeting conjugates (for example the RENTAC carbohydrate conjugates) that achieve selective proximal tubule uptake in vivo (megalin/cubilin-dependent), and several groups are advancing peptide, aptamer, and nanoparticle surface-modification strategies to bias accumulation to renal tubules or to glomerular cells. These approaches show promise for delivering antisense oligonucleotides, peptide-nucleic acids or small-molecule payloads to the kidney, but they also raise new preclinical requirements (quantitative kidney biodistribution, receptor-dependency tests, and injury-state pharmacokinetics) that should be built into any repurposing and validation pipeline [107, 108, 135].

Even state of the art atomistic simulations remain sensitive to force field choice and sampling protocols, which directly affects predicted ligand binding modes and predicted energetics for small molecules that target structured RNA [136, 137]. Consequently, binding hypotheses produced by docking or short MD trajectories can be misleading when used alone to prioritize repurposed drugs for experimental testing [138, 139]. Predicting cellular permeability and intracellular bioavailability for RNA-binding compounds adds another major computational barrier because existing permeability models were developed for protein targets and lipophilic small molecules rather than polar RNA binders or heterobifunctional modalities [25, 140]. From a biological standpoint, ceRNA interactions are deeply context dependent and cell-type specific, so bulk RNA-seq ceRNA reconstructions can obscure the true regulatory wiring active in proximal tubular cells, endothelial cells, or infiltrating immune cells during acute kidney injury [16, 141]. Importantly, the kidney is highly heterogeneous, multiple epithelial subtypes (proximal tubule segments S1–S3, thick ascending limb, collecting duct), diverse endothelial beds, interstitial fibroblasts, and immune infiltrates each have distinct miRNA/circRNA repertoires and stoichiometries. As a result, bulk tissue ceRNA correlations can reflect changing cell composition rather than bona fide regulatory crosstalk within a single cell type. To reduce this confounding we recommend (i) generating or leveraging single-cell/nucleus RNA-seq or spatial transcriptomics when possible to resolve cell-type specific ceRNA wiring; (ii) applying bulk deconvolution methods, for example CIBERSORTx for cell fraction estimation and digital cell-expression profiles, MuSiC (and MuSiC2) for multi-subject deconvolution, or Bayesian methods such as BayesPrism, to estimate and, where feasible, correct for compositional shifts prior to co-expression or ceRNA inference; and (iii) validating prioritized triplets in sorted or microdissected cell populations, or with single-cell/single-nucleus assays and spatial validation to confirm localization and stoichiometry. Employing these orthogonal strategies helps distinguish artifactual bulk correlations from cell-intrinsic ceRNA regulation and provides a stronger basis for targeting hub nodes in translational studies [141–145].

Notably, recent single-cell and single-nucleus transcriptomic studies have begun to resolve cell-type specific ceRNA wiring in AKI and to identify epithelial, immune and endothelial subpopulations that differentially express circRNAs, miRNAs and target mRNAs. Single-cell atlases of human and murine AKI show conserved epithelial injury programs and reveal how infiltrating immune subsets and stressed tubular compartments contribute distinct noncoding-RNA signatures that are invisible in bulk profiles. Incorporating single-cell (and spatial) data into ceRNA inference reduces cell-composition confounding and enables nomination of cell-restricted therapeutic targets and delivery strategies [146, 147]. Moreover, the expression and stoichiometry of circRNAs, miRNAs, and target mRNAs change dynamically across injury and repair phases of AKI, which means therapeutic interventions that appear promising at a single time point could be ineffective or even detrimental at another stage [148, 149]. Functional redundancy within miRNA families and network-level compensatory mechanisms make single-node perturbations frequently buffered, creating a high false positive rate for single-target repurposing predictions [150, 151]. Finally, many tools for ceRNA inference rely on correlative metrics that cannot by themselves establish causality, so computationally nominated drugs must be scrutinized for off-target network effects and for their capacity to enact durable topological shifts in the ceRNA interactome rather than transient expression changes [152, 153].

The essential experimental roadmap for validation

A rigorous experimental validation pipeline must begin in vitro with targeted, orthogonal perturbations that address both specificity and causality; CRISPR–Cas13 platforms permit direct degradation of circRNA species and can be optimized to minimize collateral activity while providing rapid functional readouts of ceRNA axis disruption [154, 155]. Concomitantly, investigators should (i) perform absolute copy-number measurements (qPCR/ddPCR or smFISH) to assess miRNA and target stoichiometry, (ii) map functional miRNA binding by AGO-CLIP/PAR-CLIP or reporter assays with MRE-mutants, and (iii) perform dose-response perturbations (circRNA titration ± miRNA mimic/antagomir with rescue) to demonstrate competition consistent with molecular titration models before claiming functional ceRNA activity [12, 60, 61]. Complementary experiments using miRNA mimics and inhibitors are necessary to confirm the directionality of interactions predicted in silico, because rescue or phenocopy experiments reveal whether a drug’s effect is mediated through the miRNA node or through unrelated downstream pathways [16, 156].

We recommend a hierarchy of mechanistic readouts: (i) biochemical validation (RNA pulldown/AGO-CLIP/SHAPE-MaP) to demonstrate direct occupancy or structural perturbation, (ii) quantitative stoichiometry (absolute copy-number of circRNA vs. miRNA) to test the feasibility of titration as described by quantitative ceRNA frameworks, and (iii) functional reporters (dual-luciferase with native 3′-UTR or circRNA MREs) combined with rescue experiments (miRNA mimics/antimiRs and circRNA knockdown) to prove causality. This order ensures that luciferase readouts are interpreted in the context of physical binding and stoichiometric sufficiency, reducing false positives from correlative expression changes [12, 25, 67, 157]. For lead candidates, biochemical validation of direct RNA binding using orthogonal assays such as SHAPE-MaP, selective 2 hydroxyl acylation followed by primer extension, or crosslinking immunoprecipitation adapted to small molecule–RNA complexes should be incorporated to link computational docking to measurable structural change [136, 138].

In vivo validation requires disease-relevant AKI models where temporal dynamics matter; cisplatin nephrotoxicity and ischemia reperfusion injury models capture distinct pathophysiologies and should be used in tandem to assess whether candidate repurposed drugs promote regeneration, attenuate tubular cell death, and rewire ceRNA networks toward adaptive repair [141, 149]. To capture temporal dynamics of RNA regulation, we recommend longitudinal sampling coupled with time-resolved approaches (e.g., metabolic RNA labelling and time-course single-cell RNA-seq) that can discriminate newly transcribed versus pre-existing RNAs and resolve kinetics of circRNA/miRNA/mRNA changes during injury and repair. Recent methodological advances (metabolic labelling adapted for in vivo, time-resolved single-cell RNA-seq) make it feasible to map transient miRNA bursts, circRNA induction/clearance and stage-specific ceRNA interactions that would otherwise be missed by single time-point assays. These temporal datasets are essential to avoid stage-specific misinterpretation of protective vs. detrimental ceRNA effects [158, 159].

High depth RNA sequencing after treatment in these models must be complemented by phenotypic outcomes including serum creatinine, histologic scoring, and cellular composition analysis so that molecular rewiring can be directly correlated with clinically meaningful kidney function improvement [148, 160]. Finally, longitudinal sampling and single cell or single nucleus sequencing of treated kidneys are essential to verify that network rewiring is cell-type specific, durable across repair phases, and not associated with maladaptive fibrotic programs or immune dysregulation [141, 149]. Beyond single targets: toward systems pharmacology

Translating ceRNA rewiring into clinically translatable regimens will require a paradigm shift from single target selection to systems pharmacology where causal inference frameworks and machine learning are used to predict rational multi drug combinations that collectively shift network topology toward a pro regenerative attractor [152, 153]. Causal discovery algorithms that integrate perturbation data, temporal expression trajectories, and prior mechanistic knowledge can prioritize combinatorial interventions predicted to overcome miRNA redundancy and compensatory feedback, thereby reducing the number of empirically tested combinations [150, 161].

Machine learning models should be trained on multimodal datasets that include structural RNA features, single cell expression matrices, and phenotypic readouts to propose combinations that are both mechanistically coherent and pharmacologically feasible [136, 162]. Integrating single cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics resolves cell-type specific ceRNA wiring and enables precision targeting of drug delivery systems or oligonucleotide modalities to the most vulnerable or regenerative cell compartments in AKI [149, 163]. Ultimately, success will depend on iterative cycles of high fidelity computational prediction followed by tightly controlled experimental validation to ensure network level resilience, safety, and translational relevance for human AKI [139, 160].

Future computational directions and AI integration

Rapid developments in machine learning and large language models (LLMs) are creating concrete opportunities to accelerate ceRNA-centered repurposing pipelines at multiple stages, from improved RNA structure prediction to integrative target prioritization and hypothesis generation. First, advances in deep learning for 3D biomolecular modeling now extend beyond proteins and are improving RNA tertiary structure prediction and RNA–small molecule interaction modeling; these methods reduce a key bottleneck for reliable RNA-aware docking and enable higher-confidence selection of structured RNA motifs as therapeutic sites [155, 164, 165].

Second, generative and discriminative AI models (including graph neural networks and LLMs tuned on chemical and biological corpora) can be used to propose and triage RNA-binding chemotypes, predict ADME/toxicity profiles, and prioritize drug combinations that are predicted to shift network topology toward regenerative attractors. These approaches complement classical docking + MD by learning patterns from large panels of annotated RNA binders, RIBOTACs and transcriptome perturbations, thereby improving recall of plausible leads and reducing false positives prior to biochemical screening [86, 166–168].

Third, LLMs and multimodal models can assist with integrative data synthesis: they can summarize heterogeneous omics cohorts, extract mechanistic hypotheses from literature and annotate candidate circRNAs/miRNAs with functional context and prior experimental evidence, accelerating human curation and revealing non-obvious target–drug linkages. Recent proof-of-concept studies demonstrate LLMs’ utility for target–disease linkage and trial optimization, provided outputs are validated with orthogonal experimental or database evidence [166, 169].

Fourth, combining causal discovery frameworks with active-learning experimental designs will be essential. Causal inference and perturbation-aware ML can prioritize perturbations (CRISPR, small molecule, oligonucleotide) that maximally reduce uncertainty about network causality, which in turn reduces experimental combinatorics when testing multi-drug or multi-modal interventions [170].

Finally, the rapidly evolving ecosystem of AI-for-biology companies and open models is producing practical toolchains (structure prediction, enhanced sampling, ADME predictors, and molecular generators) that can be integrated into a reproducible pipeline: (i) single-cell/spatial ceRNA mapping → (ii) AI-guided RNA structure and motif identification → (iii) AI-assisted ligand generation/triage → (iv) RNA-aware docking and MD refinement → (v) causal ranking and experimental validation. While AI promises to speed candidate discovery, it must be deployed with principled validation (benchmarking, orthogonal biochemical assays, and prospective experiments) to avoid over-reliance on uncalibrated predictions [139, 171].

Conclusion

The ceRNA hypothesis provides a powerful lens through which to view the complexity of acute kidney injury, revealing networked RNA interactions that can be therapeutically targeted. In this work, we have outlined a computational pipeline that translates ceRNA network maps into prioritized drug repurposing candidates, integrating multi-omics mining, network analysis, RNA-aware docking, and dynamic validation. Through illustrative case studies, we demonstrate the feasibility of targeting the circRNA–miRNA–mRNA axis with existing drugs, such as mitoxantrone, metformin, and propofol, each representing a distinct mechanistic approach. However, significant challenges remain, including RNA structural dynamics, cellular context dependency, and validation in disease-relevant models. Moving forward, iterative computational and experimental efforts, supported by single-cell technologies and systems pharmacology, will be essential to transform these hypotheses into clinically viable therapies. Crucially, the candidate drugs discussed here are illustrative computational nominations, their efficacy and safety must be established in etiology-matched experimental systems (e.g., IRI, sepsis, nephrotoxin models) before claims of general utility across AKI etiologies can be made.

Unlike prior reviews that separately summarize ceRNA network biology or survey RNA-targeted ligands, this work explicitly integrates RNA-aware structure-based screening (docking) and dynamic validation (molecular dynamics and MM/GBSA rescoring) into a ceRNA-centric drug-repurposing pipeline. By linking network-level prioritization of circRNA–miRNA–mRNA triplets to atomistic models of structured RNA motifs (and then validating top docking poses via MD), the framework narrows computational false positives and provides mechanistic, structural hypotheses that are directly testable in biochemical and cellular assays, bridging a gap between transcriptome network inference and RNA-targeted medicinal chemistry. This integrative strategy draws on methodological advances in RNA-ligand docking and MD refinement to produce mechanistically grounded, prioritized computational hypotheses for ceRNA-based repurposing. However, the outputs should be regarded as starting points for a staged experimental program (biochemical binding assays → cellular functional assays → disease-relevant in-vivo testing) before any translational or clinical claims can be made; many promising computational leads fail to translate without rigorous validation.
