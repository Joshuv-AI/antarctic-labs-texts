Health-related quality of life and financial inclusion in Ghana's Savannah region: examining the moderating role of health literacy
Authors: Mbanba Ziblilla Yakubu, Mustapha Immurana, Mohammed Ridwan Saani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAlthough financial inclusion in promoting health and well-being is well acknowledged, empirical evidence supporting its link with health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is still evolving, with the moderating role of health literacy often overlooked. Therefore, this study offers empirical evidence on the moderating role of health literacy in the link between financial inclusion and HRQoL.MethodsData were collected from August to October 2024 through a survey administered to residents aged 18 years and above in Ghana's Savannah region. HRQoL was assessed using the 36-item Short Form Health Survey, and health literacy using the All-Aspects Health Literacy Scale. A tailored financial inclusion index was developed and refined through factor analysis.ResultsA positive, significant link between financial inclusion and HRQoL was observed across physical and mental domains. Health literacy enhanced the relationship between financial inclusion and physical HRQoL (β = 0.19, p < 0.01).ConclusionsOur findings underscore a significant association between financial inclusion and HRQoL, with health literacy strengthening this connection. The study advocates deepening financial inclusion in Ghana's rural areas by improving access to and use of high-quality financial products. More importantly, the study suggests a synergistic policy approach that integrates health literacy into financial inclusion programs.

Introduction

Health is a cornerstone of workforce productivity, social stability, and economic growth (Occhipinti et al., 2023). Therefore, protecting and improving health are key goals of both national policies and international efforts. Health is described as a state of complete physical, mental, and emotional well-being, with normal bodily functions and no pain, injury, or illness (Pressman et al., 2020). The concept of health underpins health-related quality of life (HRQoL), which assesses an individual's perceived well-being across physical, mental, and social health domains (Killewo et al., 2010). Health, a multidimensional construct, is shaped by multiple factors, including access to healthcare, lifestyle choices, socioeconomic status, and the environment (Shetty et al., 2024).

Recently, there has been a growing focus on structural factors influencing individuals' hierarchical power. Notably, financial inclusion has become a crucial element affecting access to healthcare worldwide. As such, it is recommended as a policy approach for underserved areas like Ghana's Savannah region, which has the country's highest multidimensional poverty rate at 76.6% (Ghana Statistical Service, 2024). Financial inclusion refers to the extent to which individuals can access affordable, high-quality financial services and products timely (World Bank, 2022). According to Grossman's (1972) model, financial inclusion enables individuals to better manage health shocks by using credit and savings to pay for medical expenses, thereby avoiding cost-related delays in necessary care.

Despite global efforts to enhance financial inclusion for health and social progress, the scientific evidence linking it to HRQoL remains limited and continues to evolve, with interconnected factors often overlooked. One such factor is health literacy, defined as a person's ability to access, understand, and use healthcare information to make informed health decisions (Liu et al., 2020). This encompasses reading and writing healthcare information (functional literacy), communicating and sharing health information (communicative literacy), and critically analyzing information to better manage health issues (critical literacy). Enhancing functional, critical, and communicative health literacy can boost a person's autonomy and empowerment (Nutbeam, 2000).

Earlier research has primarily explored the relationship between financial inclusion and health. For instance, Gyasi et al. (2019) in Ghana found a positive association between financial inclusion and self-rated health as well as psychological distress. Building upon this, our study explores the association between financial inclusion and HRQoL in Ghana's Savannah region, with particular attention to the moderating role of health literacy. This focus is important because, while financial inclusion enables access to healthcare, health literacy serves as a cognitive and behavioural filter ensuring that the benefits are used effectively to enhance physical, mental, and social well-being. In doing so, our study contributes to an emerging body of evidence by highlighting the moderating role of health literacy in the nexus between financial inclusion and HRQoL. This research offers valuable insights for policymakers and practitioners, potentially broadening existing knowledge and encouraging further discourse on preventive health.

Methods

Study design and population

The study used a cross-sectional survey of residents in the Savannah region by selecting participants from four districts (Bole, West Gonja, Central Gonja and North-East Gonja) of the seven districts through a simple random. A convenience sampling method was then employed to recruit participants because there was no comprehensive list of individuals for the various communities. Four trained research assistants, fluent in English and two local languages, collected data mainly face-to-face, reading and translating questions and consent forms. Where respondents could read and write, a self-administered technique was employed. Participants had to be at least 18 years old and have clear communication skills in English or a local language. Before the main survey, a pre-test verified the questions' validity, accuracy, and clarity to enhance understanding. The survey was administered via Google Forms. Between August and October 2024, 500 subjects participated, but 489 responses were valid after excluding 11 for missing data and outliers. Before starting fieldwork, ethical approval was granted by Yonsei University's Institutional Review Board, South Korea, confirming that participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw anytime without repercussions. Additionally, no personal information was collected to ensure respondents' anonymity.

Measures

The study's dependent variable is HRQoL, measured using the 36-item Short Form (SF-36) health survey, chosen for its broad and comprehensive evaluation compared to other scales that target specific age groups, treatments, or diseases (Ware, 2002). The scale evaluates HRQoL across eight dimensions grouped into physical and mental components. The scoring metrics range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better HRQoL. In Ghana, previous research shows that the scale's psychometric properties, including Cronbach's alpha, exceed 0.70 for most items (Frempong-Ainguah and Hill, 2014).

This study's main independent variable is financial inclusion. Measures from existing literature were carefully adapted to assess financial inclusion using a composite score of its access, usage, and quality domains (see OECD, 2022; Ravikumar, 2017). Because no validated multidimensional instrument suited our specific context, we created a custom scale. The scale included twelve items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). To ensure methodological rigour, the scale underwent factor analysis, reliability and validity tests to assess its construct structure and internal consistency.

Health literacy was assessed using the All Aspects of Health Literacy Scale (AAHLS) (Chinn and McCarthy, 2013). For functional, communicative, and critical literacy, responses were categorized as “often,” “sometimes,” or “rarely”. For empowerment, participants chose between “Yes” and “No,” with the last item offering two options: “Information & Encouragement to lead healthy lifestyles” and “Good Housing, Education, Decent Jobs & Good Local Facilities.” The analysis controlled for demographic, socio-economic, and behavioural factors, including gender, educational status, marital status, place of residence, employment status, monthly income level, regular exercise, alcohol intake, smoking of substances, active health insurance and age. To simplify results interpretation, marital and educational statuses were regrouped into binary categories.

Statistical analysis

The study used descriptive statistics, such as percentages, means, and standard deviations, to summarize the sample characteristics. The financial inclusion scale has not been validated, unlike the SF-36 and AAHLS scales, prompting the use of Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA). To satisfy the preconditions of EFA, Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity were examined. The Bartlett's Test of Sphericity examines whether variables are unrelated, whereas KMO assesses the suitability of data for factor analysis (Shrestha, 2021); a KMO score of at least 0.5 (Kaiser, 1974). The EFA used principal components analysis with an orthogonal rotation method based on varimax, retaining factors with eigenvalues ≥1 per the eigenvalue criterion (Kaiser, 1970). Factor loadings above 0.4 were kept (Kaiser, 1970; Shrestha, 2021). Subsequently, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) verified the structure, reporting indices such as relative chi-square (χ2/df), p-value (p), Standardized Root Mean Squared Residual (SRMR), Root Mean Square of Error Approximation (RMSEA), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) within a Structural Equation Modeling framework. Diagnostic tests, including internal consistency reliability, were evaluated using Cronbach's alpha. The study also measured average variance extracted (AVE > 0.5) and composite reliability (CR > 0.6) to evaluate convergent validity. Since the dependent variable is continuous, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression was used to estimate the study's models. Before the estimations, we checked for multicollinearity using the variance inflation factor (VIF < 5.0), residual normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis, and heteroskedasticity was tested using the Breusch–Pagan/Cook–Weisberg test. To assess robustness, the models were re-estimated with a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) using robust standard errors. The analyses were conducted using Stata version 19.5.

Results

Our study outlined participants' demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioural characteristics. The results indicated that 39.7% were female, while 58.9% were married. Most participants had low levels of education, with 28.2% having no formal schooling. Additionally, 74% identified as Muslims, and 26.8% came from Bole District. Rural residents accounted for 61.3%, with 50.5% unemployed. Regarding income, 27.2% earned less than GHC 500, while 52.3% regularly exercised. Most participants, 89.8% abstained from alcohol, and 92.2% do not smoke substances. At the time of the survey, 58.7% lacked active health insurance. Descriptive statistics of health literacy and HRQoL

Table 3 shows a low to moderate level of functional literacy among participants, with nearly 49.5% and 31.3% often or sometimes needing help with reading. About half receive assistance with reading often (44.2%) or sometimes (46%), while 9.8% rarely do. Importantly, 54.6% do not require help filling out healthcare documents. Regarding communicative literacy, responses vary: over a third rarely or sometimes provide information to seek healthcare, whereas 22.1% can supply such information. The ability to ask questions is high at 57%, but only 10.6% often seek clarification. Most show strong critical literacy: 63% are interested in health information, and 61.7% reflect carefully on it, yet only 4.3% often identify trusted sources. Additionally, 65% frequently question healthcare advice, about 69.7% have multiple ways to express opinions on health, 64.6% prioritize their health, and 37.4% take actions based on their health concerns. Factor analysis and diagnostics test

The results of Bartlett's Test of Sphericity for the financial inclusion instrument are significant with a chi-square of 3629.9 and 66 degrees of freedom, indicating sufficient correlation among variables for factor analysis. The KMO value of 0.9 suggests sample adequacy. EFA revealed three factors with eigenvalues of 6.0, 1.4, and 1.0, explaining 69.9% of the variance. Factor loadings were high, mostly over 0.4, but two items loaded onto unintended factors with loadings of 0.5 and 0.6 and were removed before CFA. CFA showed high loadings above 0.5 and acceptable model fit. For the HRQoL, CFA refined the construct, retaining factors with loadings above 0.5. Items with low loadings and minimal contribution to consistency were removed based on statistical and theoretical criteria (Kaiser, 1974). For health literacy, the entire empowerment dimension was removed due to low factor loadings, minimal contribution to internal consistency, and existing studies (see Chinn and McCarthy, 2013) that conceptualize health literacy as functional, communicative, and critical as measured by the AAHLS. This suggests that the empowerment domain results from health literacy, but not a core component of it.

Regarding diagnostics, the results show that the Cronbach's alpha for financial inclusion ranges from 0.7 to 0.9, demonstrating acceptable reliability across all constructs. For HRQoL, general health has the lowest Cronbach's alpha (0.6), while physical functioning has the highest (0.9). The Cronbach's alpha for the health literacy construct ranges from 0.7 to 0.8, showing good internal consistency. Results reveal that only the general health, access, and critical literacy dimensions have AVEs below 0.5. However, the CR values range from 0.6 to 0.9. Measures with AVEs under 0.5 are acceptable only if CR exceeds 0.6 (Fornell and Larcker, 1981). Each of the three constructs with AVE below 0.5 has CR above 0.6, so they are valid. Regarding OLS assumptions, the independent variables have VIFs below 5.0, indicating no multicollinearity in the models. The skewness–kurtosis test showed a deviation from normality (Adjusted χ2 = 29.0, p = 0.000), and the Breusch–Pagan test demonstrated non-constant error variance (χ2 = 49.4, p < 0.000). To address these issues, a logarithmic transformation reduced skewness and stabilized the variance of the outcome variable, and robust standard errors were estimated to ensure reliable inference.

Baseline regression and robustness checks

Table 5, Table 6 demonstrate that financial inclusion is positively and significantly associated with physical (β = 0.07, p < 0.001) and mental (β = 0.04, p < 0.05) HRQoL, meaning a one-unit increase in financial inclusion corresponds to a 7% and 4% increase in physical and mental HRQoL, respectively. The interaction between financial inclusion and health literacy is positive and significant for physical HRQoL in model 4 (β = 0.19, p < 0.01), showing that health literacy strengthens this association. Conversely, the interaction was not significant for mental HRQoL in model 8, likely due to the cross-sectional design, which limits observing long-term psychological and behavioural processes through which health literacy might affect mental HRQoL. Age correlates negatively with HRQoL, with each additional year reducing physical HRQoL by 0.9% and mental HRQoL by 0.4%. Conversely, being married is associated with a 9% increase in physical and mental HRQoL. Formal education is associated with an 8% increase in physical HRQoL. Discussion

The study explored how health literacy moderates the relationship between financial inclusion and HRQoL. The results affirm the expected positive link between financial inclusion and both physical and mental HRQoL. The findings support Grossman's model and United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 3, which emphasizes that financial inclusion is vital for good health and well-being by allowing households to cover medical costs and mitigate health emergencies. These results also reinforce earlier findings linking financial inclusion with health outcomes or HRQoL (Abdurrahman and Haryanto, 2023; Ajefu et al., 2020; Banerjee et al., 2023; Gyasi et al., 2019; Xiao and Tao, 2022). However, unlike the current study, Aguila et al. (2016) observed that financial inclusion is linked to mental health but not with physical health.

The positive link between financial inclusion and physical HRQoL aligns with the idea that physical HRQoL depends on the ability to afford improved nutrition, as many health issues are linked to poor nutrition and deficiencies (Zavitsanou and Drigas, 2021). Access to financial resources enables individuals to afford healthier foods and mitigate issues caused by malnutrition. Studies show that greater financial inclusion correlates with lower financial stress, potentially benefiting mental health and decreasing stress-related illnesses (Ajefu et al., 2020). Thus, improved mental HRQoL helps people handle stress, reach their potential, learn, work efficiently, and engage actively in their communities (Gautam et al., 2024). Consequently, financial inclusion empowers people by providing access to education and social services, strengthening social bonds, and enhancing overall well-being (Mishra et al., 2024).

Research consistently shows that individuals with higher health literacy are better at understanding medical information and navigating healthcare systems than those with limited health literacy (Coughlin et al., 2020). The moderating role of health literacy on physical HRQoL suggests that those with higher health literacy are better able to convert financial resources into health-promoting actions and to use healthcare more effectively. However, the anticipated connection with mental HRQoL was not observed, likely because mental well-being is affected by a wide range of psychosocial and environmental factors, such as stress, social support, and emotional resilience. These factors cannot be fully accounted for by cross-sectional data, which limits observing long-term psychological and behavioural processes through which health literacy might strengthen the association with mental HRQoL. Therefore, mental HRQoL may be less responsive to health literacy moderation than physical HRQoL.

With respect to demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioural factors, a decline in HRQoL with increasing age is associated with reduced physical capacity and increased risk of chronic diseases (Grimmer et al., 2019). Being married is positively linked to physical and mental HRQoL, as marital quality provides social support and positive interactions between spouses, offers security, and coping strategies (Gyasi et al., 2019). Employment status and income are positively associated with HRQoL, reflecting the beneficial association of financial resources with healthcare access. Regular exercise and active health insurance are also linked to better HRQoL, while alcohol consumption and smoking are associated with poorer HRQoL.

Limitations include convenience sampling, which is common but limits the generalizability of findings due to its non-random nature. Cross-sectional data also hinders establishing causality or directionality. Additionally, endogeneity issues, reverse causality, and unobserved heterogeneity may affect the estimated relationships. Furthermore, lack of data on participants with insignificant diagnoses (Hsin-Hung et al., 2024) or who underwent unnecessary diagnostic procedures. Consequently, some HRQoL responses may reflect diagnostic experiences rather than actual HRQoL. Future research could use longitudinal data to establish causality and address endogeneity concerns, explore mediating factors linking financial inclusion to HRQoL outcomes, replicate in other contexts, and address concerns related to overdiagnosis and false-positive outcomes.

Conclusion

Although many initiatives aim to improve financial inclusion for health and social benefits, the scientific evidence linking it to HRQoL is still developing, with key factors often overlooked. This study offered empirical insights into the moderating role of health literacy in the association between financial inclusion and HRQoL. The results show that health literacy positively moderates the link between financial inclusion and physical HRQoL but not mental HRQoL. The study, therefore, recommends deepening financial inclusion in Ghana's rural areas by improving access to and use of quality financial products. Furthermore, the study recommends a synergistic policy approach that integrates health literacy into financial inclusion programs and encourages community-based health education initiatives.
