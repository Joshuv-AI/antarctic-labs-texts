Anchor‐Based Double‐Row Suture‐Bridge Repair for Stener‐Like Distal Medial Collateral Ligament Lesions of the Knee
Authors: Logan D Moews, Napatpong Thamrongskulsiri, Jacob T Morgan, Felipe Casanova, Cesar Alejandro Jiménez‐Aroche, Tanner Nishioka, Jorge Chahla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStener‐like distal injuries of the superficial medial collateral ligament (MCL) are characterized by displacement of the distal ligament stump superficial to the pes anserinus fascia, preventing anatomic healing and resulting in persistent valgus and anteromedial rotatory instability. This technical note describes a reproducible anchor‐based double‐row, suture‐bridge repair for Stener‐like MCL lesions that restores the native tibial footprint and provides a low‐profile augmentation. Following medial exposure, the distal superficial MCL is mobilized, interposed pes tissue is released, and the tibial footprint is prepared to a bleeding cancellous bed. A proximal all‐suture anchor is placed approximately 12 mm distal to the joint line, a second all‐suture anchor is inserted 6 cm distal to the joint line, and suture limbs are incorporated into a 4.75 mm knotless anchor to create a suture‐bridge construct that compresses the ligament over a broad contact area. This configuration is designed to replicate the native flat tibial insertion, restore valgus restraint, and enhance resistance to anteromedial rotatory loads without graft harvest or multiple tunnels. The described technique provides a simplified, anatomic, and low‐profile option for treating Stener‐like distal MCL injuries and may improve biomechanical restoration and clinical outcomes.

Medial collateral ligament (MCL) injuries represent one of the most common ligamentous pathologies of the knee, and although most cases respond to conservative treatment, high‐grade tears or those associated with persistent instability remain a clinically relevant challenge. 1 , 2 A specific subset of injuries, Stener‐like MCL lesions, pose an even greater challenge. In these cases, the distal MCL stump becomes displaced and trapped superficial to the pes anserinus fascia or medial retinaculum, mechanically preventing anatomic healing. 3 Similar to the classic thumb Stener lesion, this interposition leads to persistent medial instability despite appropriate immobilization. 3 , 4 Therefore, surgical repair is recommended.

Several surgical techniques have been described for repair of Stener‐like distal MCL lesions. Most authors have emphasized open anatomic repair of the distal stump after freeing the interposed pes anserinus or medial retinacular tissue, followed by reattachment to the tibial footprint using suture anchors or transosseous fixation. 5 , 6 , 7 Anchor‐based repairs with the addition of internal brace augmentation or suture tape constructs have also been reported to protect against early valgus loading and to support rehabilitation. 7 , 8 Rechter et al. described a repair construct using washer‐post or suspensory fixation techniques to enhance stability at the tibial insertion. 9 Although these approaches generally restore valgus stability, they may require additional hardware or multiple fixation points, and most focus primarily on valgus restraint without specifically addressing the combined valgus and anteromedial rotatory instability (AMRI) associated with these injuries.

Unlike previously described repairs for Stener‐like distal MCL lesions, which typically rely on a single anchor fixation point with or without suture augmentation, our technique uses a proximal‐distal double‐row configuration with a knotless suture‐bridge construct. This approach increases footprint compression, enhances contact area for healing, and recreates the native flat geometry of the superficial MCL without the morbidity of graft harvesting or multiple tibial tunnels. By establishing two spaced fixation points across the ligament's tibial insertion and linking them with a compression bridge, this technique is designed to provide robust valgus restraint with improved resistance to AMRI compared to conventional single‐point repair constructs.

The purpose of this technical note is to describe a simplified anchor‐based repair for Stener‐like distal MCL lesions using a proximal‐distal double‐row construct with a low‐profile, knotless augmentation. This method re‐establishes the native tibial footprint of the superficial MCL while providing a short, isometric restraint to valgus and anteromedial rotatory forces. By combining anatomic reattachment with a compression‐bridge configuration, this construct aims to replicate the flat physiologic morphology of the superficial MCL and enhance fixation strength without the need for graft harvest, multiple tibial tunnels, or extensive reconstruction techniques.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Indications

This technique is indicated for acute or subacute distal MCL tears with clinical or imaging findings consistent with a Stener‐like displacement pattern, in which the distal superficial MCL stump is displaced superficial to the pes anserinus fascia or medial retinaculum, preventing anatomic healing (Figure 1). In the acute setting, the most reliable indications include grade III injuries that fail to stabilize after a brief period of bracing, tibial‐sided “Stener‐like” lesions in which the distal MCL is displaced superficial to the pes anserinus and cannot heal in an anatomic position, and MCL entrapment or interposition that prevents the ligament from reapproximating its native footprint. Additional indications include symptomatic instability in active patients, high‐demand athletes, or those undergoing concomitant ligamentous reconstruction, in whom medial‐side stability is necessary to protect cruciate grafts. This construct is appropriate when the tissue quality is adequate for primary repair and when restoration of the superficial MCL tibial footprint is desired without graft harvest or multiple tunnels.

Contraindications

Contraindications include chronic MCL insufficiency with retracted or poor‐quality tissue that cannot be reliably secured and therefore requires formal reconstruction. Additional contraindications include multiligament injuries involving the posteromedial corner, prior failed MCL repair, or active infection.

Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned supine on the operating table. After induction of general anesthesia, a thorough knee examination is performed to assess ligamentous stability and range of motion. A well‐padded thigh tourniquet is applied to the operative limb. The operative leg is secured in a leg holder (Mizuho OSI, Union City, CA), and the contralateral leg is placed in an abduction stirrup (Birkova Product, Gothenburg, NE). A Spider leg positioner (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is then used to stabilize the operative leg throughout the procedure.

Dissection

A standard midline incision, similar to that used for a bone–patellar tendon–bone autograft harvest, is performed (Figure 2A). A medial soft‐tissue flap is elevated to expose the pes anserinus bursa and the distal insertion of the superficial MCL (Figure 2B). The deep fascia covering the pes anserinus is opened in an inverted‐T configuration (Figure 2C), while taking care to avoid injury to the pes anserinus tendons. The distal portion of the superficial MCL is identified. The MCL was fully ruptured and displaced over the pes tendons (Figure 2D). Fibrotic tissue over the medial proximal tibia is debrided to create a fresh bleeding bed for repair (Video 1).

Step‐by‐step demonstration of open medial dissection with exposure of the pes anserinus and identification of the displaced distal superficial medial collateral ligament. Following preparation of a bleeding tibial footprint, a proximal all‐suture anchor is placed 12 mm distal to the joint line and the ligament is secured with locking sutures. A second distal anchor is inserted 6 cm distal to the joint line, and running sutures are used to complete bicortical fixation of the ligament. A knotless 4.75 mm anchor is then deployed distal to the second anchor to create a low‐profile suture‐bridge construct that compresses the ligament to bone and re‐establishes the broad native tibial footprint of the superficial medial collateral ligament. This configuration provides anatomic reduction and augmentation designed to address valgus instability and anteromedial rotatory instability. Proximal Row Fixation

A 2.8 mm Q‐Fix all‐suture, double‐loaded anchor (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is placed approximately 12 mm distal to the tibiofemoral joint line to establish a proximal short‐row construct for reattaching the superficial MCL (Figure 3A). The distal end of the ligament is tensioned using a Kocher clamp, and a free needle is used to pass each suture limb from the anchor through the ligament in three locking loops, repeated twice for the double‐loaded anchor (Figure 3B; Video 1).

Distal Row Fixation

The distal row fixation point is located 6 cm distal to the tibiofemoral joint line. A second 2.8 mm Q‐Fix all‐suture, double‐loaded anchor (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is then inserted at this location (Figure 4A). With the ligament again tensioned distally, running locking stitches are placed using a free needle to further secure the superficial MCL (Figure 4B; Video 1).

Suture‐Bridge Fixation

A pilot hole is then created approximately 10 to 12 mm distal to the distal anchor, just anterior to the posteromedial tibial flare, and tapped to 4.75 mm. A 4.75 mm SwiveLock knotless anchor (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is loaded with suture limbs from both the proximal and distal rows (Figure 5A). The anchor is inserted into the pilot hole, creating a suture‐bridge construct that enhances compression and maximizes the contact area of the repair, thereby increasing overall fixation strength (Figure 5B; Video 1).

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The knee is placed in a hinged brace locked in full extension for ambulation and sleeping during the first 6 weeks. Patients remain nonweight‐bearing with crutches for 6 weeks, avoiding valgus stress and tibial rotation. Passive range of motion from 0° to 90° begins immediately and progresses as tolerated, with the goal of achieving 0° to 90° by 2 weeks and advancing toward full flexion thereafter. At 6 weeks, patients transition to progressive partial and then full weight‐bearing as quadriceps control improves, with the brace gradually unlocked once a nonantalgic gait is achieved. Most patients discontinue the brace between 8 and 12 weeks, depending on strength and stability. Return to running typically begins at approximately 16 to 20 weeks, and sport‐specific activity resumes between 6 and 9 months postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

The presented technique describes a double‐row, suture‐bridge repair for Stener‐like distal MCL injuries designed to restore the anatomic footprint of the superficial MCL while providing a low‐profile augmentation that enhances both valgus and rotational stability. Although nonoperative management remains standard for many MCL injuries, persistent valgus laxity, AMRI, and soft‐tissue interposition resembling Stener‐type displacement frequently necessitate surgical management. 2 , 10 These scenarios demand strategies that re‐establish not only linear valgus restraint but also the multidirectional tensioning characteristics of the medial knee structures.

Biomechanical studies have highlighted the functional interplay between the superficial MCL, deep MCL, and associated anteromedial structures in guiding medial knee stability across knee flexion angles. 11 , 12 , 13 Shatrov et al. showed that long, isolated superficial MCL graft reconstructions may leave residual external rotation laxity, whereas multi‐strand or short isometric constructs more effectively restore the rotational component of medial stability. 13 These findings reinforce the importance of recreating the broad, flattened tibial insertion and the angle‐dependent tensioning pattern of the native superficial MCL.

The current technique parallels these principles through a double‐row anchor fixation and a knotless compression‐bridge configuration intended to recreate the broad tibial footprint of the superficial MCL. Addressing the Stener‐like displacement pattern is particularly advantageous as displacement of the distal MCL stump superficial to the pes anserinus fascia may prevent spontaneous healing despite immobilization. 4 The open dissection allows direct visualization, mobilization, and reduction of the displaced superficial MCL, and preparation of a bleeding bony bed facilitates biologic incorporation.

The use of an anchor‐based short‐construct augmentation represents a conceptual bridge between pure repair and formal reconstruction. Whereas full reconstructions require multiple tunnels, graft harvest, and more extensive dissection, the low‐profile suture‐bridge construct offers robust reinforcement without additional morbidity. 14 This philosophy parallels that of short isometric reconstructions, which prioritize restoring native ligament kinematics and minimizing graft length changes through knee range of motion. 15 Such constructs have shown promising rotational control and may reduce the risk of over‐constraint often seen with longer, anisometric grafts. 15

Rotational stability remains a critical but often underrecognized component of medial knee biomechanics. Recent work by Behrendt et al. has shown that flatter, more anatomic superficial MCL configurations significantly improve AMRI control, particularly in near‐extension ranges where instability is most symptomatic. 16 By restoring a broad insertion and maintaining compression across the tibial footprint, the presented construct seeks to address these rotational demands in addition to valgus stability.

Despite these benefits, several limitations merit attention. The quality of tissue and chronicity of injury may influence the repair construct durability. Cases with extensive degeneration or chronic insufficiency may still require formal reconstruction. Additionally, although biomechanical principles strongly support the construct, direct comparative biomechanical or clinical outcome studies are required to validate superiority over traditional suture repair methods. Future work assessing valgus and rotational laxity patterns following this technique, along with return‐to‐sport and long‐term functional outcomes, will be essential.

Key pearls include thorough release of the distal superficial MCL from the pes anserinus fascia to correct the Stener‐like displacement, precise identification of the tibial footprint, and preparation of a bleeding bony bed to optimize healing. Accurate anchor placement at the proximal (12° mm distal to joint line) and distal (6° cm distal) sites, along with proper ligament tensioning, helps restore native length and footprint compression with the suture‐bridge construct. Common pitfalls include incomplete release of interposed tissue, malpositioned anchors leading to improper length‐tension, and overtensioning that may overconstrain the medial knee (Tables 1 and 2).

In summary, this double‐row, suture‐bridge repair provides an anatomic restoration of the superficial MCL footprint and incorporates a low‐profile augmentation designed to improve valgus and rotational stability. By combining the biologic advantages of primary repair with characteristics of short isometric constructs, this technique may offer a treatment for patients with Stener‐like distal MCL lesions.
