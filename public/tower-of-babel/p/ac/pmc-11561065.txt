Chronic dizziness in the elderly: small vessel disease and cortical atrophy
Authors: Adolfo M. Bronstein, Diego N. Kaski, Toby J. Ellmers, Qadeer Arshad, Patricia B. Castro, Louisa Murdin
Published: 2024

Dear Sirs,

We were pleased to read the article by Felfela et al. [1] on a novel multimodal diagnostic algorithm for predicting cognitive impairment in elderly patients with dizziness. From the opening, the authors rightly state that “current classifications of vestibular disorders almost exclusively list peripheral neuro-otological disorders”. Indeed, for older individuals with dizziness in particular, both neurologists and geriatricians remain frustrated by the normal neuro-vestibular examination in their patients. Consequently, dizziness remains ‘unexplained’ in 30% patients beyond 60 years of age and 50% beyond 85 years [2]. The article addresses the impression that many clinicians have: that in a sizeable proportion of dizzy older patients cognitive and balance symptoms seem to co-relate. Felfela and colleagues explore whether cognitive impairment in dizzy patients can be predicted from the results of routine vestibular, gait and imaging assessments. It turns out that their model does a good job at such prediction, accounting for nearly 50% of the variance in cognition. Patients with increased vascular risk factors, normal vestibular function, impaired gait and worse age-related neuro-imaging (atrophy and small vessel disease) are more likely to be cognitively impaired (MOCA < 25). A possible label of ‘Dizziness in Cognitive Impairment’ for this syndrome has been proposed by Felfela et al. [1].

These results point at a fascinating area of convergence with our own research. Frustrated by the lack of definitive diagnosis in many elderly dizzy patients, 10 years ago we reviewed the clinical notes and imaging of 120 older patients [3]. We found that approximately 50% of patients had a definitive explanation for their dizziness (e.g. vestibular, cerebellar, haemodynamic) but the other 50% did not. When we compared the neuro-imaging in these two groups, it was clear that the frequency of severe small vessel disease was significantly higher in the patients without a vestibular diagnosis [3]. This implied that the small vessel disease itself may be causative, or at least contributory, to the emergence of the dizziness. Following from that, we conducted prospective studies which confirmed that white matter changes in frontal deep white matter, the genu of the corpus callosum and the right inferior longitudinal fasciculus [4] interfere with posture-relevant neuro-physiological cerebral loops [5]. The interpretation was that the subclinical disruption of such postural loops is reported as dizziness by the elderly population.

Whilst our imaging and electrophysiological findings support the notion that small vessel disease plays a substantial part in the emergence of dizziness in these patients, we and Felfela et al. both show that older patients with dizziness have increased vascular risk factors, sub-clinical gait, postural and, of particular interest, cognitive dysfunction. Our data suggested that this syndrome was best explained by the underlying microangiopathy, giving rise to the term MicroAngiopathy-Induced Dizziness (MAID) [6, 7]. Admittedly, we did not specifically assess cortical anatomy in detail so we may have missed the cortical atrophy reported by Felfela et al. Equally, Felfela et al. did not analyse white matter structure as deeply as we did. Beyond such technical details, the important conclusion is that two research groups have independently reached similar shores. Patients who otherwise may have been labelled with a dubious peripheral vestibular condition (“presbyvestibulopathy”) likely suffer from a balance syndrome underpinned by vascular risk factors, central hemispheric pathology, gait and postural disorder, normal vestibular function and cognitive dysfunction. The underlying pathology will be established definitely with further work, bearing in mind, however, that white matter disease and cortical atrophy are well correlated (8).
