Patient and care setting characteristics related to adverse events of self-harm, suicide attempt and suicide: a Danish nationwide study
Authors: Katrine Ulrikke Madsen, Mette Reilev, Christina Petrea Larsen, Elsebeth Stenager, Erik Christiansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients dying by suicide while receiving healthcare services contradicts patient safety and is considered an adverse event (AE) - an unintended event during any part of the patient’s care process, not limited to e.g., adverse drug reactions, and results in preventable harm. This study examined potential relationships between patient and care setting characteristics and AE reports of self-harm, suicide attempts and suicide, overall and by sex.MethodWe used Chi-square Independence Test to analyse relationships between patient and care setting characteristics and AEs of self-harm and suicidal behaviour reported to the Danish Patient Safety Database from January 1st, 2022, to December 31st, 2022.ResultsAEs of self-harm were reported more from assisted living facilities, and suicide attempts and suicide were reported more from hospitals. Distinct patterns were found between males and females. AEs of self-harm was overrepresented among females in the age group 10–25 and males in the age group 26–50, while suicide was more frequent in the age group 51–90 for both sexes. Suicide attempts among females, and suicide among males and females occurred more often away from the reporting facility. The information and level of details in the AEs varied greatly, with a large lack of sufficient information from the free-text fields describing the AE.ConclusionFuture initiatives should be targeted at improving patient safety at assisted living facilities, as well as during agreed leave, absconding and discharge from hospitals. Lastly, a more thoroughly reporting of AEs, e.g., by adding checkboxes tailored to the specific types of AEs, might generate knowledge that could improve patient safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14365-w.Keywords: Self-injurious behaviour, Suicide attempted, Suicide completed, Patient safety, Health services research

Introduction

Patient safety is a major concern of health care services. Since the end of the 20th century many health care providers around the world have launched programs aiming at improving patient safety by investigating unintended and potentially harmful events occurring during the delivery of healthcare services [1]. Such events are broadly defined as adverse events (AEs) by WHO and refer broadly to unintended events happening in relation to any part of the patient’s care process, not limited to e.g., adverse drug reactions, and results in preventable harm to a patient [1]. The use of the term “adverse events” has been adopted by the Danish Patient Safety Authority [2] and will in this paper be referred to as such. In the Danish context, AEs - in Danish utilsigtede hændelser are defined as events that cause death, or temporary, permanent or severe harm to the patient e.g., fall from hospital bed, patient mix-up, or failure in drug dispensing. Additionally, events that potentially would have caused harm if not averted (near misses) e.g., delayed initiation of rehabilitation, are also referred to as AEs [3]. Suicides, suicide attempts and self-harm events occurring while receiving healthcare services are also considered AEs [4].

Healthcare staff in public hospitals have been obligated to report AEs in Denmark since 2004. This reporting system was expanded in 2010 to include the primary care sector, the pharmacy sector, prehospital services, and the municipal healthcare sector. Patients and relatives have been able to submit reports on a voluntary basis since 2011 [3]. The reporting obligation for AEs was revised to ensure uniform reporting requirements across all sectors of the healthcare system, including private hospitals in 2023 [5].

The goal of reporting these AEs aims to improve patient safety and foster safety culture by learning from mistakes and reducing the number of AEs. To facilitate this, the reporting of AEs is anonymous at the individual level and not governed by sanctions [6]. In 2023, AEs of self-harm, suicide attempt and suicide together were the fifth most reported severe or deadly AEs in Denmark [7]. Of note, these AEs are not restricted to events happening during e.g., stay at hospital or assisted living facilities, but should in theory cover all individuals who have received healthcare services, broadly defined.

To our knowledge, few studies have used AEs for research on suicidal behaviour, and none have used AEs to investigate self-harm. Most of the studies investigate AEs on suicide [8–10], with one study investigating attempted suicides and how they were prevented [11]. Most of the studies only include psychiatric or mental health services, except from a Swedish study, which includes all healthcare services [9]. To improve preventative efforts among patients with self-harm and suicidal behaviour, an investigation of patterns and challenges related to AEs of self-harm, suicide attempts and suicide in all areas of the healthcare sector seems of importance. Data sources covering AEs provide unique sources of information to reveal such patterns and challenges.

By investigating all reported adverse events (AEs) in 2022 of self-harm, suicide attempts and suicide, this study aimed to examine potential relationships between patient and care setting characteristics and AE reports of self-harm, suicide attempts and suicide, overall and across males and females.

Methods

In this study all reported AEs on self-harm, suicide attempt and suicide across Denmark from January 1st, 2022, to December 31st, 2022 were analysed to describe potential relationships between characteristics related to the patient and care setting and the type of AE.

Data source

Data on AEs were extracted from the Danish Patient Safety Database (DPSD). The database covers all reported AEs not only by hospitals but also by other healthcare institutions e.g. primary care sector, the pharmacy sector, and the municipal healthcare sector in accordance with reporting obligations introduced progressively from 2004 to 2011 [3]. AEs are reported electronically to the database through a Danish webpage (https://uth.dpsd.dk/Form/) using both mandatory and voluntary structured fields, as well as free-text fields. It is not possible to link these data to other Danish healthcare registries due to the reporting of AEs being anonymous and not governed by sanctions, when stored in DPSD.

The DPSD is governed by the Danish Patient Safety Authority (DPSA), who is responsible for monitoring trends and underlying patterns in the AEs at the national level [3]. For more information on the Danish reporting system please see supplementary, additional file 1. At the time of data collection (2022), DPSA handled AEs; however, this responsibility was transferred to another authority as of May 1, 2025.

Study material

This study focuses on nationwide reported AEs. An individual could have several reported AEs, each describing a single event. All reported AEs were included in the study, irrespective of patient-related factors like age and sex. The level of details provided in the free-text field of the AEs varied greatly, ranging from a few sentences to over 700 words.

Type of adverse events

The AE reports were categorised as AEs of self-harm, AEs of suicide attempts, and AEs of suicide, as this is how the events are categorized in the DPSD. The categorization was done at the facility where the AE occurred. To do so, the reporters at the facility used the official definitions provided by the DPSA, which we have translated into English for presentation [4]:

Self-harm: Often a habitual action, performed strictly privately, with the purpose of a form of self-help.

Self-harm: Often a habitual action, performed strictly privately, with the purpose of a form of self-help.

Suicide attempt: Not a habitual action, without a deadly outcome, where the purpose is to achieve a desired change, e.g., to die, to get help, etc.

Suicide attempt: Not a habitual action, without a deadly outcome, where the purpose is to achieve a desired change, e.g., to die, to get help, etc.

Suicide: Self-initiated and self-completed action, where the deceased desired, and was aware of the deadly consequences of the action.

Suicide: Self-initiated and self-completed action, where the deceased desired, and was aware of the deadly consequences of the action.

Study variables

A range of study variables related to the patient and care setting characteristics were examined, some defined by the DPSA (structured fields in the DPSD) and some defined by the authors (free-text field in DPSD) (see supplementary, additional file 2). To structure the data extracted from the free-text field, we developed variables based on knowledge achieved from a previous review of a subsample of AEs, as well as previous studies investigating AEs on self-harm and suicidal behaviour. We further categorised some variables from the structured fields e.g. age was categorised into three groups, facility into three groups and methods into four groups.

The first author (KUM) and co-author (CPL) read every reported AE thoroughly at the office of the DPSA. If a study variable was not mentioned in the free-text field, if the reporter did not find it relevant to mention, or if it was not a part of the circumstances of the AE, it was coded as missing.

Statistical analyses

For the three types of AE, we reported frequencies of study variables. Relationships between each study variable and the three types of AEs were estimated by χ² Independence Test or Fisher’s Exact Test. These determined whether the observed frequencies of each study variable were significantly different from the frequencies that would have been expected if the study variable was unrelated with the type of AE. A Bonferroni correction p-value (0.0026) was used as alfa-value, but p-values below 0.05 and 0.0001 was also reported.

All analyses were conducted for the overall study population for the type of AE, separately, and repeated stratifying by sex. All analyses were made in RStudio using the R version 4.2.3 or Stata BE 18.

Results

A total of 750 adverse events (AEs) of self-harm, suicide attempt and suicide were reported to the Danish Patient Safety Database in 2022. After removal of duplicates by the Danish Patient Safety Authority, and events not involving self-harm or suicidal behaviour by the authors, a total of 681 AEs remained: 288 related to self-harm, 275 to suicide attempts, and 118 to suicide. Of the 572 suicides registered in Denmark in 2022, these 118 (21%) were reported to the Danish Patient Safety Authority as they occurred while individuals were receiving healthcare services. The remaining suicides happened outside healthcare settings and there therefore not subject to AE reporting.

For each variable, observed counts and percentages are compared with expected counts under the assumption of independence. Sex was significantly associated with the type of AE (χ² = 76.7, p < 0.001). Males accounted for 76% of suicides, markedly higher than expected (47.6), while females were overrepresented in self-harm and suicide attempts. Age showed a strong association with type of AE (χ² = 119.2, p < 0.001). The age group 10–25 years were overrepresented in self-harm (56%) and underrepresented in suicide (17%). In contrast, the age group 51–90 years accounted for 46% of suicides, far exceeding the expected count (18.6). Time of day was also significantly associated (χ² = 78.7, p < 0.001). AEs of self-harm and suicide attempts were more frequent in the evening, while suicides were more likely to occur during the day. Facility type had the strongest association with type of AE (χ² = 241.8, p < 0.001). AEs of suicides predominantly occurred in hospitals (89%), while self-harm was most reported in assisted living facilities (68%). Location of the AE was significantly associated with type of AE (χ² = 146.8, p < 0.001). AEs of suicides were more likely to occur off-site (79%), while self-harm events were mostly on-site (93%). The choice of method showed a pronounced association (χ² = 294.2, p < 0.001). Cutting was the predominant method in AEs on self-harm (63%), poisoning in suicide attempts (50%), and hanging in suicides (31%). All suicide events were classified as deadly, while self-harm and suicide attempts were more often mild or moderate.

Among males, AEs related to self-harm were heavily concentrated in the 26–50 age group, with 73 observed events (78%) compared to 53.4 expected events, indicating a substantial overrepresentation. In contrast, the age group 51–90 was underrepresented in self-harm (9 observed (10%) compared to 23.2 expected) but overrepresented in suicide (43 observed (48%) and 22.3 expected). Among females, self-harm was most frequent in the 10–25 age group (147 observed (78%) and 114.9 expected), also showing a strong overrepresentation. Suicides were notably overrepresented in the 51–90 age group (11 observed (39%) and 2.7 expected), following a similar pattern to males, though with a greater deviation from expected values.

For males, AEs of self-harm was most common during the day (47 observed (59%) and 42.7 expected) and suicide attempts during the evening (31 observed (47%) and 24.6 expected). Suicides were more frequent during the day (27 observed (57%) and 25.1 expected). Among females, AEs of self-harm and suicide attempts was highest in the evening (55% and 50%, respectively). AEs related to suicide were more evenly distributed during the time of the day, with suicides occurring at night (4 observed (22%) and 2.8 expected) being slightly overrepresented.

A high number of AEs related to self-harm among males were reported from assisted living facilities (80 observed (85%) and 32.3 expected), while AEs of suicides were mostly reported from hospitals (77 observed (87%) and 54.8 expected). Among females, AEs related to self-harm was also primarily reported from assisted living facilities (115 observed (60%) and 67.9 expected), though to a lesser extent than in males. AEs of suicide among females were highly concentrated in hospitals (27 observed (96%) and 17.1 expected), showing a larger deviation from expected values compared to males.

In terms of the location, AEs related to self-harm among males was mostly reported to occur on-site (<40 observed (<92%) and 20.9 expected) while suicide showed a strong tendency to occur off-site the facility (57 observed (78%) and 35.7 expected). Among females a similar pattern was observed, with self-harm mostly occurring on-site (134 observed (92%) and 103.8 expected) and suicides predominantly occurring off-site (20 observed (80%) and 7.1 expected). The deviations from expected counts were more pronounced among females.

Among males, cutting was the dominant method for self-harm (70 observed (77%) and 29.8 expected) and hanging for suicide attempts (24 observed (29%) and 16.5 expected) and suicides (25 observed (33%) and 14.7 expected). Among females, cutting was also the most dominant method for self-harm (105 observed (56%) and 55.7 expected), but poisoning was more common for suicide attempts (100 observed (55%) and 62.1 expected). And similar to the pattern of males, hanging was the most dominant method for suicide (6 observed (25%) and 2.2 expected).

All AEs of suicide were classified as deadly for both sexes. AEs of self-harm among males was predominantly mild (74 observed (82%) and 28.0 expected), while among females more often moderate (89 observed (55%) and 76.4 expected) or severe (<25 observed (<13%) and 34.5 expected). Suicide attempts among males were mostly moderate (49 observed (66%) and 17.8 expected), while females had a broader distribution across moderate (77 observed (48%) and 76.4 expected) and severe (56 observed (35%) and 34.5 expected.

Discussion

Key findings

This nationwide study found distinct patterns in adverse events (AEs) of self-harm, suicide attempts, and suicides across patient and care settings characteristics, with some important differences between males and females. Males were significantly overrepresented in AEs of suicides, especially among older adults and off-site from the facility, while females were more likely presented in AEs related to self-harm and suicide attempts, particularly in younger age groups and within assisted living facilities. In terms of method used and severity, males and females predominantly used cutting for self-harm. AEs of self-harm among males were more often classified as mild in severity, while self-harm among females were more moderate or severe. Suicide attempts were more classified as moderate among males, while females had a broader distribution across moderate and severe. Among both sexes, hanging was the dominant method used for suicide.

Strengths and limitations

The Danish Patient Safety Database (DPSD) is a unique data source covering AEs of self-harm, suicide attempts, and suicides among the entire Danish population irrespective of e.g., sex and income. Also, it covers information on AEs reported by health care staff in different health care sectors in Denmark as well as by patients and relatives. To this end, self-harm and suicidal behaviour recorded in the DPSD is not restricted to AEs that lead to hospital admissions and emergency room contacts. Thereby, AEs reported to the DPSD cover self-harm and suicidal behaviour as it occurs among the general population receiving healthcare services not only severe cases requiring medical attention. However, suicides occurring after discharge or termination of care may not be appropriately represented in the AEs. The reporting to the DPSD is based on actual occurrences of events of suicidal behaviour. The risk of misclassifying events related to other issues as being related to suicidal behaviour is, as such, expected to be limited.

The definitions on self-harm and suicide attempt applied in this study follow the Danish Patient Safety Reporting System, as these form the basis for the AE reports analysed. In the Danish healthcare system, “self-harm” is more closely associated with non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and often involves habitual behaviour intended to cope with psychological distress. In contrast, “suicide attempt” refers to a non-habitual act aimed at changing one’s situation and aligns more closely with the broader global concept of self-harm, which includes acts both with and without intent to die. Our findings may not be directly comparable to studies using broader definitions, as international definition differ – particularly regarding the role of intent, motives and habitual behaviour.

It is worth noting, that it is not possible to determine whether the proportion of AEs reported from e.g., hospitals vs. assisted living facilities reflects the true distribution of suicidal events occurring at each facility. It could be that the propensity to report AEs varies between health care staff and staff at e.g., assisted living facilities due to differences in work culture and education of staff. However, we find it likely that AEs with an identified learning opportunity have been reported to enhance patient safety with the understanding that the reporter will not be sanctioned. As such, some patients with repeated behaviour may be represented with multiple events in the AEs. All this could potentially exaggerate some of the observed patterns, and might not represent the real-world patterns among AEs and suicidal behaviour across patient and care setting characteristics.

For some study variables, the level of missing information turned out to be remarkably high, preventing us from performing analyses on potential relationships. This might suggest that our definition of study variables was too specific or narrow or not relevant to the AE. For instance, sector communication is irrelevant if the patient was not being transferred at the time of the AE. It may also reflect that categorization of free text is not straight forward. Furthermore, the high level of missing information may indicate that if we aim to extract meaningful learning opportunities from AE reports of self-harm, suicide attempts and suicide, the current reliance on free-text fields may be insufficient. A more structured reporting format – potentially with additional mandatory fields or checkboxes, could help ensure that critical information is consistency documented.

Comparison with existing literature

An overview of inpatient suicides showed that hanging and drowning were the most used method for suicide [12], which aligns with our findings of AEs of suicide. Regarding suicide attempts, we found that poisoning was the most used method, whereas a study from the UK investigating patient safety reports of attempted suicide among psychiatric inpatients found strangulation to be the most used method [11]. Besides psychiatric inpatients, our study also covers patients receiving in-and outpatient care, individuals living at assisted living facilities, nursing homes etc. which may explain the observed differences. Reasons behind the more extensive use of poisoning in our dataset are speculative but may indicate that procedures related to drug administration should be investigated.

We found that most AEs of self-harm were reported from assisted living facilities, suggesting that individuals living in these facilities are at risk of self-harm. This is in line with the results of a Danish report from 2015 [13]. Despite this, there seems to be no governmental initiatives targeted at preventing self-harm at assisted living facilities. The set-up of the assisted living facilities with independent housing makes it difficult to limit access to means. Preventative efforts are further complicated by the different organisational structures with some facilities being private entities while others are run by municipalities or the Danish regions. Also, the composition of staff varies across entities along with the availability of health care support. The primary staff members are pedagogues and nursing assistants, but also social workers, occupational therapists, physiotherapists and nurses may be present depending on the entity [13].

Nearly two thirds of suicide reported as an AE occurred away from the premises of the facilities (65%). This is similar to findings from two studies, where 70–80% of suicides occurred off the premises, e.g., if the patient absconded or were on agreed leave [14, 15]. This indicates potential issues with procedures related to agreed leave or absconding e.g., lack of suicide risk assessments or low clinical value of these assessments [16]. In 83% of the AEs, a suicide risk assessment was not documented. This seems puzzling, as it is – according to regional Danish guidelines, mandatory to conduct suicide risk assessments at different times of psychiatric in- and outpatient contacts and document the results in the patient journal [17–20]. The high range of missing of suicide risk assessment in the AEs, does not equal that suicide risk assessments have not been conducted and documented in the patient journal. Nonetheless, whether a suicide risk assessment had been conducted was not described in many of the AEs. Further, regarding self-harm it is not always relevant to conduct a suicide risk assessment, which might also contribute to the high level of missing. However, reasons behind the low rate of reporting suicide risk assessment should be investigated.

The information and details from the free-text fields in the AE reports varied greatly. This questions whether they can be used to foster knowledge on preventative initiatives in their current form. The reporting form for AEs are currently uniform irrespective of type of AE, whether it being a suicide attempt, patient mix-up, patient fall etc. To obtain more information on the circumstances of AEs of self-harm and suicidal behaviour, mandatory checkboxes for these specific types of AE would be useful e.g., on completed suicide risk assessment and safety plans.

Many of our findings align with broader epidemiological patterns: males are more likely to die by suicide, while females are more likely to engage in non-lethal self-harm and suicide attempts. The choice of method reflects gender differences in both lethality and intent. The gender paradox in suicide has been explained by factors such as method choice, help-seeking behavior, socialization expectations, substance abuse, and aggression - all of which are more strongly associated with male gender [21]. Among young people, self-harm is primarily undertaken for intrapersonal (automatic) reasons such as emotion regulation, anti-dissociation, and self-punishment. These motivations are more strongly associated with the female gender but are also linked to greater severity of non-suicidal self-harm and to current, repetitive, and persistent self-harm [22]. We found a higher degree of severity for self-harm and suicide attempts among females, compared to males.

We also found that AEs of self-harm, suicide attempt and suicide among females showed a higher frequency in the evening and night, suggesting a potential pattern of emotional vulnerability during these time periods. Furthermore, the location for self-harm and suicide attempt among females tended to be on-site which may indicate that females may engage in self-harm in more usual settings, and the behavior is a way to seek help, expressing or coping with distress. The off-site nature among females in terms of suicides could suggest planning and intent.

Conclusion

Future initiatives should be targeted at improving patient safety at assisted living facilities, as well as during agreed leave, absconding and discharge in relation to patient contacts. Also, a more thoroughly reporting of AEs, e.g., by adding checkboxes tailored to the specific types of AEs, might generate knowledge that could improve patient safety.
