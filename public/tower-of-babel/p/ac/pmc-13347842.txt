Adaption of a posttraumatic stress disorder intervention for patients who use stimulants and opioids in the opioid treatment programme setting
Authors: Amanda M. Bunting, Hunter Molloy, Brittany Griffin, Ana Ventuneac, Tanya Renn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Polysubstance use of stimulants and opioids is prevalent among patients receiving methadone in opioid treatment programmes (OTPs) and has been linked with complex clinical profiles and poor outcomes, including psychiatric comorbidity, overdose death, and early attrition from treatment.Objective: The study goal was to adapt the evidence-based intervention Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation with Narrative Therapy (STAIR-NT) for polysubstance populations informed by the ADAPT-ITT framework to gain preliminary information regarding intervention acceptability and implementation barriers.Method: To obtain feedback on adapting the intervention and approaches to integrate it, focus groups with key stakeholders (i.e. counsellors, clinical leadership) were conducted, as well as individual interviews with patients receiving treatment at OTPs in New York City, USA. Following decisions regarding intervention adaption, two protocol versions were tested using an open pilot.Results: Patients and key stakeholders were enthusiastic about the intervention and its potential to meet patient treatment needs, bridge gaps in mental health care, and improve outcomes. Specific strategies were identified to adapt the intervention related to session length, treatment delivery format, and choice of interventionist. This qualitative work informed the creation of a 6-week protocol that was tested in an open pilot. Patients were randomized to receive four STAIR sessions with two 90-minute NT sessions (one session per week) or four STAIR sessions with four 60-minute NT sessions delivered in a massed schedule over two weeks. Participants who completed treatment generally preferred the four-session NT format; however, substantial implementation barriers impacted retention. Barriers related to scheduling and patients’ complex vulnerabilities, including housing instability and illness.Conclusions: Adapting and testing the STAIR-NT intervention within the OTP setting provided an opportunity to align the therapeutic format, content, and implementation with lived experiences and clinical needs of patients. The selected protocol will be tested in a pilot randomized controlled trial.

Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating psychiatric condition characterized by intrusive reexperiencing of trauma, persistent avoidance, negative alterations in mood and cognition, and marked arousal and reactivity changes. Lifetime PTSD prevalence among adults in the United States is estimated between 4.8% and 6.1% (Goldstein et al., 2016; Kilpatrick et al., 2013); yet rates are considerably higher among individuals with substance use disorders (SUDs) (Ecker & Hundt, 2018). One such space where this elevation is marked is within opioid treatment programmes (OTPs), where patients with an opioid use disorder (OUD) receive methadone maintenance treatment (MMT). In the United States, OTPs are highly regulated, historically requiring daily supervised dosing with take-home dosing only recently expanding (Krawczyk et al., 2023). Among this population, trauma exposure is nearly universal (Mills et al., 2005), yet largely untreated. Many OTP patients continue to engage in the use of stimulants and other illicit substances (Jackson et al., 2025), particularly cocaine. Patients who engage in polysubstance use (i.e. use of other substances with an opioid) demonstrate a higher incidence of PTSD diagnoses, more severe PTSD symptomatology across all symptom clusters, and elevated rates of childhood trauma exposure compared to those using opioids alone (Martinotti et al., 2009; Meier et al., 2014; Salgado et al., 2007). The association between PTSD severity and polysubstance use may indicate that the pursuit of enhanced effects achieved when combining substances serves as an avoidant coping strategy in response to extreme psychological distress and dysregulation (Bedard-Gilligan et al., 2011; Hien et al., 2010; Mills et al., 2006).

PTSD can complicate SUD treatment through multiple mechanisms. Individuals frequently use substances to avoid trauma-related experiences and self-medicate symptoms including hyperarousal and intrusive memories (Hien et al., 2010; Norman et al., 2007). When PTSD remains unaddressed, symptoms commonly persist or worsen following substance use stabilization, attributed to removal of substances used to suppress distress without provision of alternative coping strategies (Flanagan et al., 2016; Reynolds et al., 2005). Standard SUD treatments, largely centred on pharmacological management and relapse prevention, rarely address the underlying trauma-related emotional regulation deficits and relational disruptions that may sustain substance use (Messman-Moore & Bhuptani, 2017). Integrated, trauma-focused interventions are therefore essential for this population. Addressing PTSD among individuals engaged in MMT represents a critical opportunity to provide integrated, evidence-based care to reduce PSU severity and associated overdose risk (Hien et al., 2000; Lister et al., 2022). Evidence demonstrates that concurrent, integrated treatment of PTSD and substance use is safe and efficacious for patients (Back et al., 2024; Schumm & Gore, 2016). Concurrent treatment can improve treatment retention rates, reduce substance use severity, and enhance overall psychological wellbeing and functional outcomes for patients with these co-occurring conditions (Hien et al., 2000; Najavits et al., 2020; Roberts et al., 2015). Concurrent treatment models include trauma-focused integrated treatment (i.e. exposure-based) and non-trauma-focused integrated treatment (i.e. non-exposure). Trauma-focused treatments, which directly address the processing of traumatic memories, are the most highly recommended and empirically supported treatments for PTSD (Hamblen et al., 2019; Institute of Medicine, 2008).

One such trauma-focused integrated treatment with a robust evidence base is the concurrent treatment for PTSD and substance use disorders using prolonged exposure (COPE), which combines prolonged exposure for PTSD with cognitive behavioural therapy for SUD (Back et al., 2019; Mills et al., 2012). In a prospective randomized clinical trial, COPE was more efficacious for PTSD than a non-trauma-focused integrated intervention among individuals with alcohol use disorder, while both treatments had comparable reductions in alcohol use outcomes (Norman et al., 2019). Trauma-focused integrated treatments, such as COPE, have demonstrated efficacy at improving PTSD symptoms, but evidence is more limited for SUD-related outcomes (Roberts et al., 2015). Despite the development of empirically supported trauma-focused interventions adapted for individuals with SUD over the past two decades, treatment selection remains guided primarily by clinical judgment, patient preference, and setting-specific feasibility and implementation considerations rather than definitive comparative effectiveness data (Mills et al., 2012; Najavits et al., 2020). Treatment approaches may vary substantially across different SUD treatment settings, given the heterogeneity in treatment structure, organizational resources, and constraints that characterize the SUD treatment landscape (Flanagan et al., 2016; Simpson et al., 2021).

The competing demands and intersecting vulnerabilities of patients in OTP require a shorter intervention duration than many effective PTSD treatments (Simpson et al., 2021). For example, COPE involves twelve 90-minute sessions. Massed treatment models, or condensed treatment schedules, can overcome limitations of traditional PTSD treatments by consolidating evidence-based interventions into shorter timeframes, reducing barriers of treatment duration and scheduling complexity experienced by patients (Wachen et al., 2019). These models adapt evidence-based trauma interventions originally designed for delivery over 12 to 18 weeks into concentrated formats, with accumulating evidence demonstrating comparable treatment outcomes to standard-length protocols (Galovski et al., 2022). A massed treatment model for PTSD may be particularly feasible and efficacious in OTPs, where prolonged treatment protocols can compete with other clinical demands, including medication management, case coordination, and crisis intervention, and consequently increase the risk of treatment disengagement and premature dropout (Jeske & O’Byrne, 2019; Kleinman et al., 2023; Reisinger et al., 2009). OTPs have distinct structural and clinical features, including high staff and patient turnover, mandatory daily or near-daily medication dosing schedules, co-occurring polysubstance use disorders, housing instability, legal system involvement, and competing urgent psychosocial needs that demand immediate clinical attention (McLellan et al., 2003; Reisinger et al., 2009). The goal of the current study was to adapt a PTSD intervention with a trauma-focused component, Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation with Narrative Therapy (STAIR-NT), for the OTP setting. The manuscript details the phased process by which adaption occurred and reflects on the implementation lessons learned.

Methods

This study followed the ADAPT-ITT framework (Wingood & DiClemente, 2008) to adapt STAIR-NT for individuals with opioid-stimulant polysubstance use receiving methadone in OTPs. This formative process followed linear steps for adapting evidence-based interventions (EBIs) in new settings or with new populations. The ADAPT-ITT framework has been widely used to design and implement trauma interventions in diverse settings (Cavanaugh & Wismar, 2023) and has also been used to lead EBI adaptation for populations with a history of substance use (Cavanaugh & Wismar, 2023; Magidson et al., 2020; Satinsky et al., 2020). The eight phases of ADAPT-ITT are generally linear. The present manuscript reports on the Assessment (Phase I), Decision (Phase II), and Administration (Phase III) phases. The primary goals of these first three phases were to assess feasibility, acceptability, and potential for a future powered pilot randomized controlled trial. Subsequent ADAPT-ITT phases (Production, Expert Review, Training, Testing) are underway and will be reported separately. All study procedures were approved by the New York University Langone Health and community partner institutional review boards (IRBs).

Study setting

The current study was conducted in collaboration with an established community-based organization operating six OTPs in New York City, USA from 2022–2024. Of the 3430 patients, 52% were 56 years of age or older, 71% were male, and 82% were African-American, Hispanic, or mixed/other. Almost all of the patients had an income well below the federal poverty level and had Medicaid (76%), Medicare (10%), or both (13%) as their health coverage.

Although the community partner organization operated six OTPs, participation varied across phases of the adaptation process. The study purposively did not enrol clinics in the borough of Brooklyn due to study personnel's location, and focused study activities on four OTP sites in Manhattan. All four OTP sites participated in Phase I assessment activities, while two sites (Clinic A and Clinic B) participated in the Phase III open pilot. These sites were chosen due to their availability of the required interventionist role and the close geographic proximity of the clinics to each other to support the research staff's ability to travel between clinics. During the open pilot phase, Clinic A experienced substantial operational barriers related to interventionist availability, scheduling constraints within clinic workflow, and difficulties sustaining delivery of extended psychotherapy sessions alongside routine clinical responsibilities. As a result, enrolment and intervention delivery continued exclusively at Clinic B for the remainder of the open pilot. Overall, these sites represent demographically and socioeconomically diverse areas with high levels of opioid-related morbidity and mortality.

STAIR-NT intervention

Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation with Narrative Therapy (STAIR-NT) is a modular, phase-based intervention that integrates emotion regulation skill development with trauma narrative processing to address PTSD (Cloitre et al., 2010; Cloitre et al., 2020). The theoretical foundation of STAIR-NT is grounded in emotion regulation theory and attachment-based conceptualizations of complex trauma, which posit that chronic interpersonal trauma disrupts the development of core emotional and relational competencies, thereby creating enduring vulnerabilities to affect dysregulation, maladaptive coping strategies including substance use, and interpersonal dysfunction (Cloitre et al., 2002; Cloitre et al., 2020).

STAIR-NT is structured as a three-module treatment programme delivered across 18 sessions. The first treatment module comprises five sessions dedicated to the systematic development of core emotional competencies, including emotional awareness and identification, emotional modulation and regulation strategies, and emotionally engaged and values-consistent living. The second module consists of five sessions focused on cultivating interpersonal and social competencies, including assertiveness, boundary-setting, relationship evaluation, and healthy relationship maintenance. Together, modules one and two constitute the STAIR (Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation) component, which provides patients with foundational self-regulatory and relational capacities that chronic trauma exposure may disrupt or prevent them from developing.

The third module implements the Narrative Therapy (NT) component across eight sessions, which involves systematic confrontation and emotional processing of traumatic experiences through constructing a coherent trauma narrative that integrates these experiences within the broader context of one's life story and identity. This sequential, phase-based structure is predicated on the clinical principle that emotional processing of traumatic memories becomes therapeutically tolerable and productive only after patients have acquired improved self-regulation capacities, distress tolerance skills, and self-compassion through the preparatory STAIR sessions.

Flexibility for module individualization is built into the STAIR-NT protocol, allowing clinicians to adjust session pacing, skill emphasis, and content selection within the modular framework. An adaption of STAIR for primary care has been completed into a 5-session version, with findings that demonstrated efficacy that the session numbers and content can be adapted to meet client needs or other settings (Jain et al., 2020). The 5-session STAIR-PC is premised on 30-minute sessions, without an NT component. In a 2023 systematic review, fifteen studies of STAIR/STAIR-NT/adapted STAIR/NT were identified (n = 10 STAIR/adapted STAIR, n = 5 STAIR-NT/adapted STAIR-NT) (Lorbeer et al., 2023). Previous populations studied include adolescents and adults including veterans, persons with histories of childhood sexual violence, and female prisoners. Overall, small to large effects were found for STAIR and adaptions reducing PTSD symptoms, affective dysregulation, and interpersonal problems (Lorbeer et al., 2023). One prior study integrated STAIR-NT into community mental health settings following a terrorist attack, and individuals experienced improved emotional regulation and reduced use of substances to cope (Levitt et al., 2007). However, the intervention has not previously been adapted for SUD populations.

Phase I assessment

The assessment phase typically includes generating data to understand the priorities and needs of the target population (Sullivan et al., 2014; Wingood & DiClemente, 2008). In the current study, elicitation interviews with patients, surveys of community-based agency leaders, and focus groups with the community partner were completed.

Elicitation interviews

Individual elicitation interviews (n = 7) with patients at the OTP who engaged in polysubstance use were conducted. Interviews were audio-recorded, and a semi-structured guide assessed: (1) the priorities and needs of the population around trauma intervention delivery, (2) the feasibility of taking part in STAIR-NT within the context of their PSU, (3) the acceptability of potential intervention components and related content to support STAIR-NT uptake, adherence, and retention, and (4) strategies to promote retention in STAIR-NT. Informed consent was explained by the interviewer, and an Informed Consent Form was signed by each participant before data collection. Interviews were transcribed by a professional transcription service and reviewed by a member of the research team for accuracy. Participants received a $50 ClinCard (debit/credit card) for participation.

Inclusion criteria for individuals who engage in polysubstance use included: (1) must be 18 years or older, (2) be a patient at the enrolling clinic receiving methadone for treatment of opioid use disorder, (3) self-report 10 + days of co-use of cocaine and illicit opioids in the past 30 days, (4) meet the criteria for stimulant use disorder (cocaine type; mild, moderate, or severe), and (5) a score of 3 ≥ on the PC-PTSD-5. Exclusion criteria included (1) cognitive impairment that would interfere with their ability to understand study participation as assessed by the researcher, (2) does not speak/understand English at a conversational level, (3) plans to leave the enrolling clinic in the next 60 days, (4) patients who missed methadone doses (inactive) for 30 days or more.

Focus groups

We intended to host two focus groups. The first focus group included clinic leadership (n = 7). However, the second group consisting of counsellors only had three participants and did not meet focus group size criteria. This group session occurred after the clinical leadership focus group, and findings for the small group can be treated as supportive, exploratory qualitative data. The sessions were conducted remotely via WebEx. These interviews assessed the (1) feasibility of delivering a trauma intervention within the context of MMT, specifically the number of sessions that seem appropriate, (2) barriers and facilitators to STAIR-NT adherence and retention in the OTP, and (3) acceptability of potential intervention format and structure, skills required to support STAIR-NT adherence. The groups were recorded using WebEx recording features and subsequently reviewed for accuracy and de-identification. The focus group/group session lasted approximately 45 min. All participants provided verbal consent and received a copy of the Key Information Sheet via email. Participants received a $50 e-gift card for their time.

Inclusion criteria for the OTP staff focus group/group discussion included: (1) 18 years or older and (2) meet the definition of counsellor or behavioural counsellor leadership; with counsellor defined as providing direct patient care to persons with a substance use disorder one or more days per week at the community partner clinic and leadership defined as holding the title of Clinic Director or Assistant Clinic Director. Exclusion criteria included (1) cognitive impairment that would interfere with their ability to understand study participation as assessed by the researcher, and (2) does not speak/understand English at a conversational level.

Community leader survey

While the intention was to complete a focus group of community-based agency leaders, after multiple failed scheduling attempts related to varying availability across agencies, the focus group questions were adapted to a REDCap survey that included open-ended fields. The survey opened with a copy of the Key Information Sheet, and consent was obtained by checking ‘agree' via the REDCap survey. The survey was sent to 14 agencies and five completed within the requested timeframe. Participants (n = 5) received a $50 e-gift card for completing the survey.

Inclusion criteria for the community-based agency leader survey included: (1) 18 years or older, (2) meet the definition of a stakeholder, and (3) work for a nonprofit or government (city, state). Stakeholder was defined as having been in a leadership role in an organization that serves vulnerable population (e.g., persons who inject drugs, persons with a substance use disorder) for a minimum of two years. Exclusion criteria included: (1) cognitive impairment that would interfere with their ability to understand study participation as assessed by the researcher, and (2) does not speak/understand English at a conversational level.

Phase I recruitment

All participants were recruited through the participating community partner. For the elicitation interviews, a trained research assistant utilized electronic health records on previously collected urine drug screen results and initiated contact with individuals who tested positive for cocaine and opioid use, either via phone or in person. Study staff explained the study opportunity and referred interested individuals to screening for eligibility. For the focus group/group discussion and community-based agency survey, an email invitation was sent to individuals who met the inclusion definition based on a list of potential participants identified in collaboration with the community OTP.

Phase I analysis

A directed content analysis approach was used to identify themes related to intervention fit, feasibility, and implementation barriers. Unlike traditional line-by-line coding, transcripts were reviewed holistically to extract adaptation-relevant domains. Rapid analytic mind-mapping techniques were used during stakeholder discussions to cluster themes in real time (Burgess-Allen & Owen-Smith, 2010). Given the small sample, thematic saturation was not formally assessed. Because the goal of the interviews was to inform intervention adaption decisions within the current study context, the analysis focused on code saturation, operationalized as the absence of new adaptation-relevant categories in the final two patient interviews. This approach is consistent with methodological work distinguishing code saturation from meaning saturation, relevant to specific study aims (Hennink et al., 2017; Saunders et al., 2018).

Because this study was designed to inform intervention adaption rather than generate theory, reflexivity was addressed primarily through transparency about team perspectives and structured decision-making procedures. The analytic team consisted of investigators with backgrounds in social work, trauma-focused intervention research, and substance use treatment research. Investigators had prior experience adapting evidence-based interventions for vulnerable populations and held perspectives supportive of integrated PTSD-SUD treatment models. To reduce the influence of individual bias, adaptation decisions were documented prospectively and discussed collaboratively across investigators and the partner OTP.

Phase II decision phase (structured adaptation process)

Based on information gathered from the Assessment phase, the Decision phase included a structured process to make adaptations to the STAIR-NT protocol. Specific adaptations to make included the number of sessions, removal or addition of content, and decisions on format and structure of the adapted STAIR-NT. This process resulted in a working provisional protocol to test in the Administration phase.

Phase II procedures and analysis

The Principal Investigators (n = 2) held a series of meetings to review each STAIR-NT session from the 18-version protocol. For each original STAIR-NT session, the conceptual theory/module, premise/goal of session, core activities, worksheets, and feedback were noted using a structured form. A collaborative process was used by the Principal Investigators to determine which items to retain and cut, using findings from Phase I and additionally reviewing and comparing to the 5 session STAIR-PC content. To reduce bias, adaptation decisions were documented prior to Phase III testing. Prospectively documenting adaptation decisions enhanced transparency and reduced the likelihood that modifications would be made post hoc in response to pilot outcomes. During Phase III, participant feedback and implementation observations were systematically reviewed to assess whether the pre-specified adaptations functioned as intended or required modification. Where pilot feedback indicated feasibility concerns or unmet needs, additional refinements were made.

Phase III administration (open pilot)

The third phase involved administering, or pilot testing, the adapted intervention with the target population to gather feedback on the content, format, and attitudes about the intervention. An open pilot was completed using two provisional STAIR-NT protocols. An open pilot is a small pilot that includes substantial qualitative feedback from participants during the intervention to make adaptations before entering a randomized controlled trial. A sample size of 10 was set a priori, and once the open pilot started and retention barriers were noted, the protocol was amended to allow for over-enrolment to achieve five treatment completers. Two versions of the proposed protocol were tested, and participants were randomized 1:1 to receive the intervention protocol version. Participants completed a baseline assessment before beginning the intervention, pre-session PCL-5, weekly qualitative interview check-ins after their session, and the first five participants to finish the intervention completed an end-of-intervention assessment and six-week follow-up PCL-5. Participants provided written consent. Participants received $40 for the baseline assessment, $20 for weekly qualitative interviews, a $20 bonus for completing all interviews, and $40 for the end-of-intervention assessment. Payments occurred via reloadable ClinCards. Participants were not compensated for completing PCL-5s or for attending intervention sessions. All sessions were audio recorded, and a random 25% of sessions were selected to review for protocol fidelity.

Phase III participants

Inclusion criteria were the same as the elicitation interviews. Based on feedback in Phase I, an additional exclusion criterion for the open pilot (Phase III) patients was added: (5) having received clinical care from the interventionist(s) in the past 30 days.

Phase III recruitment

All participants were recruited through the community partner. The trained site research assistant utilized clinic data on previously collected urine drug screen results and initiated contact with individuals who tested positive for cocaine and opioid use in the past three months. The RA explained the study opportunity and referred interested individuals to screening for eligibility if interested. Additionally, flyers detailing a study overview and contact information of the research staff were placed near the check-in desk, dispensing window, and counsellors’ desk. Individuals interested in participating were able to approach the research assistant for the screening process.

Baseline assessment

The research assistant completed baseline assessments with participants in-person at the OTP using REDCap software (Harris et al., 2009; Harris et al., 2019). The baseline assessment covered demographics, overdose risk behaviours, brief inventory of psychological symptoms, difficulties in emotional regulation, social adaptation, impulse control, physical and mental health assessment, emotional distress, posttraumatic stress symptoms, adverse childhood experiences, interpersonal problems, measure of HIV risk behaviours, measure of effective coping, past 30 d substance use, polysubstance use behaviours, and service utilization.

Weekly PCL-5

Standard clinical practice for PTSD includes the administration of a PCL-5 before each treatment session. PCL-5 assesses the past week's PTSD symptoms experienced. Participants could complete the PCL-5 on their own from a pre-session text, or via paper copy when they arrived at the clinic.

Qualitative interviews

After each session, but no later than the start of the subsequent session, the research assistant contacted participants to conduct a brief qualitative check-in. Qualitative check-ins served to gather participant feedback about their experience with intervention content that week and, as applicable, understand why participants cancelled or did not show to their appointment(s).

End-of-intervention assessment

The first five participants who completed the intervention completed end-of-intervention assessments with the research assistant. The end-of-intervention assessments repeated baseline measures. Additionally, the Working Alliance Inventory (Horvath & Greenberg, 1989) assessed the relationship between the client and interventionist, and the Client Satisfaction Inventory (Attkisson & Zwick, 1982) assessed patient satisfaction with the intervention. Four to six weeks after the end of the intervention, participants received an additional PCL-5 that was optional for them to complete. This PCL-5 helped the study team understand if the participants experienced any resurgence of PTSD symptoms after the intervention had ended.

Phase III analysis

Qualitative interviews were analysed using a structured form. A research team member listened to each interview and completed a form that captured (1) what the patient liked, (2) what the patient disliked or improvements suggested, (3) a multiple-choice question regarding session timing, (4) barriers mentioned, and (5) other important notes for adaption. Quantitative data, including key measures from the baseline assessment, PCL-5 scores, and end-of-intervention assessments, were summarized with descriptive statistics. Given the small sample size, bivariate associations were not conducted.

Participant protections and confidentiality

Participation in all study activities was voluntary and independent from participants’ OTP clinical care and methadone treatment services. Participants were informed during the consent process that declining participation or withdrawing from the study would not affect their access to methadone treatment, counselling services, dosing status, or other clinic services. Clinical providers were not informed about individuals’ decisions regarding study participation unless participants independently disclosed their involvement. Compensation was provided for completion of research assessments and interviews as detailed in each section above. To protect confidentiality, qualitative interviews and assessments were conducted by trained research staff with no clinical responsibilities in a private research room or via phone rather than with participants’ clinical providers or in clinical space, and all study data were de-identified prior to analysis. A Data Safety Monitoring Board was in place for the duration of the open pilot and a Data & Safety Monitoring Plan was approved by the university and community partner IRBs.

For focus groups, all invitations to participate came from a member of the study team (as opposed to the clinical research team) using an IRB-approved e-mail. The e-mail emphasized that participation was voluntary and had no effect on employment or relationship with the community partner. Community leaders also received an IRB-approved recruitment e-mail stating that their participation was voluntary.

Adaption decisions for STAIR-NT for patients engaged in polysubstance use in an opioid treatment programme.

Phase I

Seven patients were interviewed, 7 attended the behavioural counselling leadership focus group, 3 attended the counsellor group discussion, and 5 community stakeholders completed a survey. Although community stakeholder and counsellor participation were more limited than originally intended, findings from the assessment phase converged across patients and stakeholders and directly informed intervention adaptation decisions while considering clinical feasibility. Three primary domains emerged to inform the adaption decisions: delivery format, treatment structure, and interventionist role.

Related to delivery format, participants emphasized the need for individual, in-person sessions. Patients expressed discomfort with group-based trauma discussions within the OTP setting, expressing a lack of comfort in addressing trauma in a similar group setting. Although telehealth was viewed as acceptable by some patients, stakeholders expressed concern regarding privacy, technology access, and therapeutic depth. Community stakeholders were not asked about the session format. There was support across patients and focus group participants for a condensed treatment schedule, including the four to six sessions proposed by the research team. Community stakeholders erred towards longer protocols, ranging from 9 to 18 sessions. Across all groups, there was support for sixty-minute sessions. The focus groups and two community stakeholders expressed that the treatment should consider a contingency management component. While some felt strongly that contingency management components assist in improving adherence, others had some ambiguity.

A key theme across all groups was related to the staff providing the treatment. Patients articulated concerns about receiving trauma-focused treatment from their assigned OTP counsellor. Many described their counsellor as part of the regulatory structure governing methadone dosing and toxicology monitoring, which created hesitation about disclosing trauma-related material or substance use lapses. Focusing on the study’s specific OTP setting, every patient is assigned a counsellor for treatment. Counsellors have backgrounds as Credentialed Alcoholism and Substance Abuse Counsellors (CASACs). This training includes how to complete assessments for substance use disorder, intervention, referral, and counselling specific to substance use disorder. While it was the research team who initially suggested the use of the counsellors, patients and focus group participants highlighted that the counsellors may not be the most appropriate fit for intervention delivery.

More broadly, even among community stakeholders, there was an emphasis on ensuring staff had sufficient clinical background and expertise in trauma-related treatment. Participants consistently noted the value of purposeful staff selection and strong rapport-building, recognizing that burnout is a common reality in this type of clinical work. These findings, combined with the structural realities of OTP settings, supported systematic review and abbreviation of the original 18-session STAIR-NT protocol.

Phase II

Results of Phase I data were shared in a presentation to the clinic’s research team. Decisions were made regarding the treatment delivery to align with patient preferences, while considering clinical feasibility. In collaboration with the clinic, it was determined that 60-minute, in person, one-on-one sessions were ideal. A goal was to identify which clinical role would be most appropriate to deliver the intervention, to adhere to patient preference that their treatment counsellor remain for their methadone treatment and that trauma-related components would be independent of that relationship, and to determine if there were alternative staff roles with aligned backgrounds appropriate to the level of intensive trauma-focused intervention as opposed to the CASAC role. In addition to the counsellor role, each clinic also has one behavioural health specialist (BHS). BHSs are licensed as Licensed Clinical Social Worker or Licensed Master Social Worker. They maintain a smaller caseload, and often patients may not be assigned to a BHS for their OTP counselling sessions. Considering these factors, the study exclusion criteria were modified such that patients who were on the BHS caseload in the past 30 days were ineligible to participate. In doing so, the study aimed to provide patients and the interventionist with a ‘blank slate’ relationship where the interventionist could work to establish rapport within the confines of the adapted treatment.

Next, a structured review of each of the 18 original STAIR-NT sessions was completed. For each session, investigators (n = 2) documented the theoretical mechanism, core therapeutic tasks, and essential clinical elements. Stakeholder feedback and feasibility constraints were mapped onto each session to determine which components were essential to retain and which could be consolidated without compromising theoretical integrity. The investigators also drew from the STAIR-PC condensed protocol to review which core elements were retained.

Four STAIR sessions were retained, focusing on emotional awareness, emotional regulation strategies, interpersonal effectiveness, and boundary setting. These domains represent the core stabilization mechanisms underlying STAIR and were considered the minimum necessary foundation prior to trauma narrative work. The decision to retain four sessions was informed by both stakeholder feedback favouring brevity and prior evidence from abbreviated STAIR-PC models demonstrating flexibility in session number while maintaining skill acquisition.

Narrative Therapy (NT) was operationalized as a structured, imaginal exposure-based intervention. During NT sessions, participants were guided to recount their selected traumatic memory in detail in the present tense, including sensory, emotional, and cognitive elements of the experience. Narratives were recorded by the interventionist, who facilitated repeated, prolonged engagement with the memory within the session, encouraged emotional activation, and supported processing of trauma-related beliefs and meanings. Between-session assignments included continued reflection on the narrative and identification of trauma-related triggers. For the NT component, two formats were developed for pilot testing: (1) two 90-minute sessions delivered weekly, and (2) four 60-minute sessions delivered in a more massed format. It was determined that patients would be limited to processing their most salient traumatic memory to preserve exposure integrity within the condensed structure. These decisions were guided by literature supporting massed PTSD treatment formats, feasibility for total intervention length, and early topical expert review of adaptations. These two protocol versions were tested in the phase III, open pilot protocol. Patients were given a two-week window to move forward with sessions (i.e. after more than two weeks of not showing to their appointment, they would be considered lost to follow-up) related to the desire to have patients accumulate skills and also considering STAIR-NT’s delivery of weekly sessions. Phase III

Two BHS clinicians were trained at two clinics (referred to as A and B) to deliver the adapted intervention. 60 patients were screened, and 16 were eligible. One patient abandoned their screening, and 43 were ineligible due to not meeting study criteria. Patients could be ineligible for more than one reason, and 23 patients did not have more than 10 days of opioid use, 30 did not have more than 10 days of cocaine use; 15 did not meet the threshold score for stimulant use disorder; and 16 did not meet the PC-PTSD-V threshold for probable PTSD.

Of the eligible patients, 16 consented and 15 completed the baseline. Patients varied in age from 19 to 72 (median age 50.6). Both men (47.4%) and women (52.6%) enrolled. Patients were demographically diverse: Hispanic (47.4%), White (21.2%), Black (31.6%), and had significant socioeconomic vulnerabilities: 63.2% had a stable house (75.0% of which received government assistance for their housing), and 36.8% had varying degrees of housing instability including staying on the street, in a shelter, or with family/friends. Additionally, 47.4% reported running out of money in the past month to cover basic needs like food and housing.

An overview of patient barriers and session completeness is provided in Table 3. Patients were randomly allocated 1:1 to the 90-minute NT protocol and the 60-minute protocol. Twelve patients completed their first session, while 3 did not receive any sessions (excluding the one participant who consented but did not complete baseline). Reasons for not beginning sessions included participant facing instability (unhoused, no phone access, no show to clinic/session) and scheduling conflicts. Seven patients fell out of their two-week session window and were lost to follow-up. Those who completed at least one session but did not complete the intervention faced similar challenges and barriers. These barriers included patient medical issues or obligations, patient instability leading to no show to session or clinic, and interventionist(s) being unavailable or out of office. Related to these barriers, the decision was made after twelve patients had enrolled to end enrolment at clinic A, and only enrol at clinic B. Despite reduced implementation barriers at clinic B, patient barriers also prevented patients from completing all study sessions. Five patients completed all sessions (1 from clinic A, 4 from clinic B). All sessions (n = 68) were recorded and 25% were reviewed for fidelity (n = 17). These 17 sessions represented 10 of the 12 open pilot patients who initiated treatment. Fidelity monitoring suggested adherence to core STAIR and NT components. Qualitative Feedback During the Pilot. Of the 16 patients, four did not complete any sessions and thus, no qualitative interviews were conducted. Twelve participants completed a total of 43 structured post-session qualitative interviews, with participants completing between one and six interviews depending on treatment attendance (Table 3). Analysis of these interviews indicated high satisfaction with emotion regulation and interpersonal skills content. Participants frequently described the STAIR sessions as helpful for understanding the relationship between trauma and substance use. Preferences regarding session length varied both across and within participants, often depending on daily circumstances such as sleep quality or timing of methadone dosing. Patients could decide if they wanted to receive their methadone dosing before or after treatment, and some patients experienced drowsiness when choosing to receive their dose beforehand, which made them feel 60-minute sessions were a bit long. Participants assigned to the two 90-minute NT sessions reported that additional NT sessions may have been beneficial, which informed the selection of the four-session NT format for future testing. Qualitative interviews also served a safety-monitoring function, allowing the research team to assess emotional distress following exposure sessions, and no safety events were reported.

Preliminary Clinical Observations. The PCL-5 was administered at baseline, prior to each intervention session, at the end of the intervention, and one month after the end of the intervention. The study utilized the PC-PTSD-V (Prins et al., 2016) as a probable PTSD signal to enrol patients rather than diagnostic assessment. Four patients had a baseline PCL-5 score less than the probable clinical cutoff (31–33), and one patient had a score of 32. Three of these five patients did not continue treatment after one session. Patients who did not complete any sessions or those who completed only one did not have additional PCL-5 scores besides baseline. Three patients completed two or more sessions; two of these patients experienced decreases in PCL-5 scores even with partial treatment. Among the five patients who completed all of their assigned sessions, one patient remained stable (no symptom change), one saw a slight decrease at the one month post-intervention assessment, and two patients had clinically meaningful decreases (decreased scores by 20 and 37). One patient had an increase in score. Notably, this patient had a non-probable PCL-5 score at baseline. Although the patient’s end-of-intervention score was still below the probable PTSD threshold, at the one month follow-up, the patient had an increased score and stated that day they had encountered a triggering event the day of assessment.

Given the small number of treatment completers (n = 5), quantitative outcomes are presented descriptively and should be interpreted as exploratory findings related to feasibility rather than evidence of efficacy. Among the completers, pre-to-post clinical outcomes were examined. Patients' psychological distress, as assessed by the Brief Symptom Inventory (Derogatis, 1982), decreased (154.8 to 126.2), and Negative Mood Regulation (Catanzaro & Mearns, 1990) increased (88.6 to 109). There were no changes in scores on the Inventory of Interpersonal Problems (Horowitz et al., 1988) (2.76 to 2.80). Substance use outcomes were limited in interpretation due to overlap between intervention participation and the 30-day recall window. While some reduction in days of heroin use (25.4 to 22) and polysubstance use (19.6 to 18.4) were noted, cocaine use remained stable (21.8 to 22). Post-intervention, patients expressed high intervention satisfaction (149.6 (R: 23–184)) and a strong therapeutic alliance with their therapist (166.6 (R:36–180)). While participants who engaged in treatment generally reported positive experiences with the intervention content, implementation feasibility at the organizational and patient level was substantially constrained by staffing limitations, scheduling barriers, and patient instability.

Implementation barriers. Implementation barriers varied across the two participating OTP sites and ultimately affected the ability to sustain intervention delivery throughout the open pilot. Although two clinics (Clinic A and Clinic B) initially participated in Phase III, early implementation challenges at Clinic A resulted in the decision to discontinue enrolment at that site and continue implementation exclusively at Clinic B. Key barriers at Clinic A included limited interventionist availability, scheduling conflicts within the existing clinic workflow, and difficulty coordinating extended psychotherapy appointments within the structure of routine OTP services. Because each clinic had only one Behavioural Health Specialist available to deliver the intervention while maintaining existing clinical responsibilities, disruptions related to clinician scheduling, leave, or competing demands substantially affected continuity of care.

Participant-level instability further complicated implementation across sites. Many participants experienced housing instability, inconsistent phone access, medical issues, transportation barriers, and competing psychosocial needs that interfered with treatment attendance and follow-up. These challenges contributed to missed appointments, difficulty maintaining contact with participants, and disruptions in session continuity. Recruitment and retention were additionally affected by the study’s requirement that sessions be completed within a two-week timeframe, which may have been difficult for some participants to sustain given the broader structural and socioeconomic vulnerabilities present within the sample.

Although implementation barriers were reduced after enrolment shifted exclusively to Clinic B, challenges related to patient retention and scheduling persisted throughout the pilot. Collectively, these findings highlight the complexity of implementing trauma-focused interventions within resource-constrained OTP settings and underscore the importance of considering staffing capacity, protected clinical time, workflow integration, and patient-level instability during future implementation planning.

Discussion

Adapting and testing the STAIR-NT intervention within the OTP clinical setting was an important opportunity to ensure that intervention format, session structure, therapeutic content, and implementation procedures authentically reflect the lived experiences and clinical needs of patient populations while simultaneously addressing practical considerations related to long-term sustainability within resource-constrained OTP settings. The systematic adaptation utilized in the current study was informed by the ADAPT-ITT framework and included stakeholder input to ensure that the intervention would maintain both theoretical adherence and contextual fit. At the end of the assessment, decision, and administration phases, the four 60-minute NT session protocol version was selected for the next steps (Renn et al., 2024). This decision was informed by patient preference for shorter sessions and desire among patients in the two 90-minute NT protocol for additional NT sessions.

The study is an example of how bi-directional partnerships are helpful for intervention adaptation. The study team had originally considered that the OTP counsellors would provide the intervention to patients; however, the identified BHS role had a strong, relevant clinical background and provided more opportunity for rapport building and the establishment of a therapeutic alliance. The suggestion for the use of the BHS came from the clinical research team, underscoring the importance of flexibility and bi-directional engagement from research, community, and clinical partners. Moreover, the decision to select the BHS role was the best for this adaption, within this system of clinics. Other OTP settings may be limited in their resources and not have an appropriate or alternative interventionist role, which further highlights the need to work collaboratively with community and clinical partners to assess appropriateness of an intervention. The decision to use the BHS as the interventionist came with implementation challenges, including that there was a single clinician available (one BHS per clinic), which was a barrier for many patients related to scheduling and the interventionist’s availability. Findings from the open pilot suggest that implementation of exposure-based PTSD interventions within OTP settings requires clinicians with specialized trauma expertise, protected clinical time, and organizational support, particularly when working with highly vulnerable patient populations. Rather than demonstrating readiness for broad dissemination, the current study identified critical barriers and implementation considerations necessary to inform future implementation-focused refinement and testing.

Although participants who completed treatment generally described the intervention positively and found the opportunity to process trauma meaningful, the study identified substantial implementation barriers within OTP settings. These barriers included interventionist availability, competing organizational demands, participant instability, scheduling difficulties, and challenges sustaining intervention delivery consistently across sites. While evidence increasingly supports the safety and efficacy of concurrent trauma-focused treatment among individuals with co-occurring SUD and PTSD, the current findings suggest that implementing exposure-based interventions within OTP settings may require considerable organizational support and clinicians with specialized trauma expertise, flexibility, and protected clinical time. Delivering trauma-focused work among highly vulnerable populations also appeared particularly sensitive to staffing continuity, therapeutic alliance, and patients’ broader psychosocial instability, including housing insecurity, medical issues, and competing survival needs. While OTPs, in general, enrol a subpopulation of persons with significant socioeconomic vulnerability (Gaeta et al., 2020; Tran et al., 2016), the individuals enrolled in this study were purposefully those with active polysubstance use who may experience exacerbated social vulnerabilities. While PTSD treatment, in general, has high rates of drop-out (Imel et al., 2013), scheduling and patient-level barriers were acute. Collectively, these findings suggest that although trauma-focused interventions such as STAIR-NT may be clinically meaningful for patients receiving methadone treatment, additional implementation-focused refinement is needed to improve scalability, retention, and sustainability in resource-constrained OTP settings.

Subsequent steps in the ADAPT-ITT process include production of the proposed protocol, topical expert review, training, and testing of the adapted intervention. The implementation barriers identified in the preliminary phases of the adaption process can further inform subsequent phases. Specifically, adaptions can be considered to improve patient retention and reduce scheduling barriers. One such consideration from the research team includes revising patient-facing materials, specifically patient-centred methods to introduce patients to what to expect for treatment and how it may differ from their normal clinic interaction. Future studies that build off the current adaption lessons could also consider expanded eligibility to assess if patient-level barriers persist among a patient population that may be more stable in their treatment. At the clinic level, the research and clinical team will explore ways to increase interventionist availability, including blocking off schedules and advanced coordination with the research team.

The ADAPT-ITT model was instrumental in tailoring STAIR-NT to the OTP setting and for patients with co-occurring opioid and stimulant polysubstance use. Some investigators have utilized theatre testing as part of the ADAPT-ITT process (Crooks et al., 2023; Freeman et al., 2024), while the current study opted for an open pilot process. The results suggest that this method is feasible in situations where there may be significant implementation barriers to consider, but major intervention modifications are clear from the assessment phase. Resource availability may also assist researchers in determining if theatre testing versus an open pilot is feasible and appropriate for their adaption process. Part of our rationale in utilizing an open pilot process was related to the ability to conduct qualitative check-ins after each intervention session. This process allowed us to maintain clinical contact and assess patient well-being promptly after each session, including the NT components, which had the potential to be more stressful for patients. Despite evidence that patients with comorbid SUD and PTSD can be concurrently treated (Najavits et al., 2017), decades of historical exclusion related to beliefs that SUD patients cannot handle treatment continue to keep patients excluded from treatment. The open pilot was a strength of our study, as it assisted the study team in advocating that vulnerable patients can receive treatment for their PTSD and can experience improvements in several key domains.

Limitations

The current study was specific to the selected OTP in New York City. While the intervention content may be appropriate for patients with SUD in different settings, adaption processes to account for implementation and clinic differences would be needed to consider the adapted intervention in a new setting. Notably, the US OTP clinical regulations and requirements are unique. While the ADAPT-ITT process delineates clear steps in intervention decision-making, as highlighted, some researchers apply the steps in a different method. It is entirely plausible that our findings would have revealed different findings if alternative methods, such as theatre testing, were used in the assessment phase. The study used post-session qualitative interviews to assess patient experience, with a secondary goal of ensuring patient safety with a massed treatment schedule. Repeated contact can serve as a source of reactivity and should not be components of future studies that would assess efficacy or attempt to isolate treatment effects. The study assessed outcomes pre- to post-intervention among a small sample of open pilot participants (n = 5), which did not allow for meaningful assessment of the impact of the intervention on clinical outcomes. The small open pilot sample and substantial retention challenges, limits conclusions regarding scalability and broader implementation feasibility. Additionally, community stakeholder participation was more limited than intended, particularly among external community leaders, where scheduling barriers prevented focus group completion and necessitated transition to a survey format. Rather than demonstrating readiness for dissemination, the current study identifies organizational and patient-level barriers that should inform future implementation-focused refinement and testing.

The current study utilized a funding mechanism that explicitly accounts for the adaption process, and there has been advocacy for the funding of developmental stages and capacity building (Minkler et al., 2003). Even within the current study, certain decisions could not be made related to pre-specified funding decisions. Specifically, the use of contingency management was brought up in focus groups and community surveys. However, there was no budget for contingency management to consider this suggestion. The use of incentivized qualitative interviews after each intervention session introduced a quasi-contingency management component. However, the interview incentive did not appear to be enough to overcome external barriers or intrinsic motivation to attend sessions. Additionally, the study opted to pursue a real-world implementation, embedding it in the community partner clinic with existing staff who continued to maintain their additional responsibilities. Budgeting for a dedicated study clinician may have provided an opportunity to overcome some of the scheduling barriers faced.

Findings from the current study suggest that implementation of the adapted intervention may require clinicians with specialized trauma-focused expertise and protected clinical time, potentially limiting scalability within under-resourced OTP settings. Future implementation efforts should examine whether additional workforce training, supervision, or alternative delivery models can improve sustainability and dissemination potential. Lastly, repeated qualitative interviews may have influenced participant perceptions of the intervention or increased engagement through repeated contact with study staff.

Conclusion

Untreated PTSD continues to undermine recovery efforts for patients with polysubstance use in OTPs, yet the traditional structure of SUD treatment settings has seldom accommodated PTSD interventions with a trauma-focused component. The systematic adaption of EBIs for co-occurring PTSD and SUD is critical to ensure interventions align with the lived experiences and clinical needs of populations within resource-constrained settings. Through the ADAPT-ITT framework, the study identified both the promise and substantial implementation challenges associated with adapting STAIR-NT within OTP settings and those who have historically been excluded from trauma treatment. The use of the ADAPT-ITT framework allowed for the integration of key voices to refine and establish a provisional protocol. Early pilot data suggest that condensed, skills-based trauma interventions such as STAIR-NT may be both acceptable and beneficial for this population when delivered by clinicians with appropriate clinical expertise in psychotherapy to account for the intensity of the intervention. Further hybrid testing that includes efficacy and implementation needs will be essential to determine its efficacy, scalability, and sustainability before broader dissemination. This study underscores the essential role of community-academic partnerships in intervention adaption, further emphasizing the need for funding mechanisms to support developmental stages to enhance feasibility, acceptability, and sustainability of interventions in real-world settings and improve clinical outcomes for patients.
