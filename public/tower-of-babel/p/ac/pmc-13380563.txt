Attenuated aortic–cerebral pulsatile transmission with posture-related increases in stroke volume in healthy young adults
Authors: Rintaro Sakamoto, Yuka Ninomiya, Marina Fukuie, Koki Takahashi, Jun Sugawara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcessive pulsatile hemodynamics increased under higher stroke volume (SV) impose mechanical stress on the cerebrovasculature, requiring attenuation before reaching the brain. SV is greater in the supine than upright, yet the characteristics of aortic-to-cerebral pulsatile transmission under such conditions are unclear. To address this, we compared the transfer function gain from aortic pressure (AoP) to middle cerebral artery blood velocity (MCAv, measured by transcranial Doppler) across postures that altered SV. Fifteen young adults underwent four postures: semi-sitting at 60° and 30° backrest, supine, and supine with leg elevation. AoP was estimated from the radial arterial pressure through the general transfer function. AoP and MCAv spectra were analyzed by heart rate harmonic analysis to exclude the effect of postural difference in heart rate. SV (via the Modelflow method) was significantly greater in the supine than in the semi-recumbent positions (60° vs. supine, P < 0.05 for both). There was a significant difference in AoP spectra among postures (main effect of condition, P < 0.01). However, transfer function gain was significantly attenuated in the supine positions (60° vs. supine positions P < 0.05 for both), resulting in no significant increase in MCAv spectra across postures (main effect of condition P > 0.05). Transfer function gain decreased with decreasing systemic vascular resistance (r(rm) = 0.67, P < 0.01). Therefore, cerebral pulsatility may not increase despite greater SV, owing to an attenuation in aortic–cerebral pulsatile transmission associated with changes in systemic vascular resistance in healthy young adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00421-026-06202-z.Keywords: Aortic-cerebral pulsatile transmission, Transfer function analysis, Stroke volume, Systemic vascular resistance, Posture

Introduction

Cardiac ejection generates pulsatile blood flow and pressure that propagate from the heart to peripheral organs with each heartbeat. When pulsatile hemodynamic fluctuations in higher-frequency bands (> heart rate [HR]) are amplified, cyclic mechanical stress on the microvasculature, particularly in the cerebral circulation, increases (Thorin-Trescases et al. 2018). Normally, central arteries such as the proximal aorta buffer these oscillations through their Windkessel (reservoir) function, thereby attenuating pulsatile transmission to the brain (O'Rourke and Safar 2005; Palta et al. 2019). Given the brain’s critical role in cognitive and physiological regulation, protecting the cerebral microvasculature from excessive pulsatility in both pressure and flow before it reaches the intracranial arteries is essential for preventing microvascular injury, such as white-matter damage (Palta et al. 2019; Lefferts et al. 2020).

Because pulsatile pressure generated by cardiac ejection is formed in the proximal aorta before propagating toward the cerebral circulation, aortic pressure (AoP) represents the most physiologically relevant driving signal for assessing heart-to-brain pulsatile transmission. Intra-beat pressure fluctuation is amplified in the aorta as well as peripheral arteries by increases in stroke volume (SV) (Sugawara et al. 2023). As arterial pressure waves travel distally, pulse pressure (PP) progressively increases due to PP amplification, resulting in higher waveform amplitude at peripheral measurement sites (Nichols et al. 2022). Consequently, peripheral arterial pressure waveforms increasingly reflect amplification imposed by distal vascular properties rather than the central pulsatile forcing generated by the heart and are therefore less suitable as input signals for assessing heart-to-brain pulsatile hemodynamic transmission. Using this physiological framework, we previously demonstrated that the transfer function gain between AoP and middle cerebral artery blood velocity (MCAv) was reduced during aerobic exercise despite an increase in SV (Sugawara et al. 2023). This finding suggests the presence of a protective mechanism that dampens both pulsatile pressure and flow transmission to the brain during periods of elevated cardiac ejection (Sugawara et al. 2023).

SV is also known to increase in the supine posture compared to the seated position, due to enhanced venous return to the heart (Teixeira et al. 2018). Notably, a substantial portion of daily life, approximately one-third, is spent in a lying posture, particularly during sleep (Cabanas-Sánchez et al. 2019). Epidemiological studies have reported associations between long sleep duration and an increased risk of cerebrovascular disease (Ikehara et al. 2009). Although causality has not been established, these observations raise a potential concern that prolonged exposure to supine-related alterations in central and cerebral hemodynamics may influence cerebrovascular health. Accordingly, it is important to characterize arterial and cerebral hemodynamics under prolonged resting conditions such as the supine posture. Physiologically, the supine-induced increase in central blood volume leads to a Frank–Starling–mediated increase in SV and activates cardiopulmonary baroreceptors, resulting in reduced systemic vascular resistance (Ogoh et al. 2015). In contrast, the brain can constrict its cerebral vessels via myogenic mechanisms in response to enhanced pressure and flow pulsatility to prevent overperfusion and microvascular damage (Stauss 2007; Tzeng and Ainslie 2014). However, whether these regulations affect beat-by-beat pulsation, observed in the higher-frequency harmonics of the cardiac cycle, has yet to be fully elucidated.

To determine whether systemic and cerebral regulatory mechanisms influence beat-by-beat pulsatile hemodynamics, dynamic hemodynamic fluctuation with heartbeat can be characterized by frequency-domain analysis. In this analysis, the difference in HR should be noted when comparing frequency domain data (Fukuie et al. 2024). Indeed, resting HR was altered by the difference in postures such as seated and supine positions (Teixeira et al. 2018). Thus, HR harmonic analysis, obtaining the spectra data associated with cardiac frequencies, can characterize the amplification of frequency domain values with exclusion of the effect of postural difference in HR.

Based on this background, as a first step toward elucidating age-related changes in vascular regulation that buffer pulsatile hemodynamic transmission to the cerebrovasculature, this study aimed to determine whether the posture-related increase in SV influences hemodynamic transition from the heart to the brain through transfer function analyses in healthy young adults. We hypothesized that pulsatile AoP fluctuation would be augmented by the posture-related increase in SV. However, the transfer function gain between AoP and MCAv would decrease with increased SV, resulting in unchanged MCAv pulsatility even with changes in posture. The decrease in the transition would be associated with changes in systemic and/or cerebral vascular resistance.

Methods

Participants

Fifteen healthy adults (8 women; age, 24 ± 3 years; body mass index, 22 ± 2 kg/m2; mean ± standard deviation) participated in this study. All subjects were non‐smokers, were free of any known neurological, cardiovascular or pulmonary disorders, and were not taking any prescribed and over the counter medication known to influence systemic or cerebrovascular function. All experimental procedures and protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Tokyo Ariake University of medical and health sciences (No. 0241) and performed according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and Belmont Report. All participants gave written informed consent before participation.

Experimental protocols

Before the measurements, participants fasted, abstained from caffeinated beverages for 3 h, abstained from alcohol intake and strenuous physical activity for at least 24 h. Cerebral and systemic hemodynamics were recorded for 6 min under four postural conditions in a randomized order (Fig. 1). Each measurement was separated by more than 5 min between postures. All measurements were conducted at a reclining bed in an environmentally controlled laboratory with a quiet and air-conditioned room (24–25 °C). Participant’s postures were set using a reclining bed at semi-sitting positions of 60 degrees (60° condition) and 30 degrees (30° condition) of backrest, supine (0° condition), and supine with leg elevation (Leg condition). In leg condition, participants rested their legs on a platform with the hip and knee joints flexed at approximately 90 degrees.

Measurement and data analysis

From the start of the measurement in the first condition, radial arterial pressure waveforms were recorded at the right wrist by an applanation tonometry-based blood pressure (BP) measurement device (HEM-9000AI, Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan and JENTOW; Nihon Colin Co., Komaki, Japan). The height of the right wrist was set at the same level of the heart. for each participant, radial BP measurements were consistently obtained by the same operator to ensure within‑subject measurement consistency. Oscillometry-derived systolic and diastolic BP (SBP and DBP, respectively) of the right brachial artery was obtained at 3 min and 6 min during measurement in each posture (HEM-9000AI and Form/ABI, Colin Company, Ltd., Komaki, Japan). After data collection, radial arterial pressure was analyzed off-line by an analysis software (LabChart 8, AD Instrument Inc., Colorado Springs, CO, United States) and was calibrated by the averaged brachial BP values in each condition.

Electrocardiography was recorded using a three-lead system for calculation of HR (ML 132 Bio Amp, AD Instruments Inc., Colorado Springs, CO). MCAv was recorded over the right temporal window ipsilaterally using 2-MHz transcranial Doppler probe (EZ Dop; DWL, Sipplingen, Germany and Doppler Box-X; DWL, Sipplingen, Germany). As well as radial BP, MCAv was measured by the same operator to ensure within‑subject measurement consistency. The sampling depth was set from 47 to 56 mm, and the angle of the Doppler probe and the sampling depth were adjusted to optimize the signal quality for each subject according to the previous studies (Tomoto et al. 2015, 2018; Sugawara et al. 2017; Ninomiya et al. 2019; Alexandrov et al. 2012). During the measurement, subjects were instructed to breathe normally. Partial pressure of end-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2) was monitored by a metabolic cart equipped with a respiratory gas-analyzing system (AE280S; Minato Medical Science, Tokyo, Japan) or capnography (OLG-3800; Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan). Analog signals of electrocardiogram, the spectral envelope of MCAv, and PETCO2 were stored at 1000 Hz into a computer using a data acquisition system (PowerLab, AD Instrument Inc., Colorado Springs, CO) throughout the experiment, and analyzed by off-line analysis software (LabChart 8).

For each posture, one minute of artifact-free radial arterial pressure waveforms and MCAv were analyzed after more than 5 min of rest following the posture change. SV and cardiac output (CO) were computed from radial arterial pressure waveforms via the Modelflow method (BeatScope 1.1a, Finapres Medical System BV, Amsterdam, Netherlands) (Sugawara et al. 2003; Wesseling et al. 1993). Modelflow technique is well validated approach to provide reliable information of relative changes in SV across postural conditions rather than absolute values (van Lieshout et al. 2003; Karaki et al. 2025). Systemic vascular resistance (SVR) was calculated from mean arterial pressure (MAP) divided by CO. Time-averaged (mean), peak (systolic), end-diastolic (diastolic), and pulsatile (= peak – end-diastolic) MCAv, cerebrovascular resistance index (CVRi = brachial MAP/mean MCAv), and pulsatility index (PI = pulsatile MCAv/mean MCAv) were calculated.

Radial arterial pressure waveforms were recorded for 1 min, and three representative single-beat aortic pressure (AoP) waveforms were estimated using a validated generalized transfer function technique (SCOR-Mx, SphygmoCor, AtCor Medical, Sydney, Australia) (Sugawara et al. 2023, 2017). This method has been validated for non-invasive estimation of aortic pressure waveforms (Sugawara et al. 2010), and radial tonometry provides stable and reliable input signals (Sugawara et al. 2017; Karaki et al. 2025). Radial arterial pressure waveform was calibrated to brachial SBP and DBP. Only AoP waveforms with a quality index ≥ 75% were analyzed. Aortic reservoir and excess pressure amplitudes and integrals were calculated according to established methods (Davies et al. 2010; Schultz et al. 2014; Sugawara et al. 2018). Aortic SBP, DBP, PP, and mean AoP were averaged from three accepted AoP waveforms. MCAv was recorded simultaneously and processed in parallel. Three averaged AoP and corresponding MCAv waveforms were obtained from three non-overlapping 15-s segments within each 1-min recording (Sugawara et al. 2023, 2017). Because perfectly synchronized acquisition was not feasible, MCAv pulse length was adjusted to match AoP, and both signals were repeated to generate 10-s segments of identical length, yielding three paired datasets per posture.

Waveforms were converted to the frequency domain using fast Fourier transform (DADiSP 2002, DSP Development Co.) (Sugawara et al. 2017, 2023; Fukuie et al. 2024) and resampled at 100 Hz. Auto-spectra of AoP and MCAv were calculated from the 1st–8th HR harmonics. HR harmonic analysis allows comparison across individuals with different HRs and postures (Teixeira et al. 2018; Fukuie et al. 2024). The range of 0.78–8 Hz, corresponding to the 1st–8th harmonics at 60 beats/min, encompasses most cardiac-related pulsatile energy (Nichols et al. 1977; Fukuie et al. 2024), whereas signal-to-noise ratio declines beyond the 8th harmonic (Dujardin and Stone 1981). For transfer function analysis, changes in AoP were used as the ‘input’ signal and changes in CBV as the ‘output’ signal according to previous studies (Sugawara et al. 2023, 2017). Resampled data were subdivided into 256-point segments (2.56 s), with 50% overlap for spectral estimation using the Welch algorithm in accordance with previous studies (Zhu et al. 2011; Sugawara et al. 2023, 2017). This process yielded a spectral resolution of 0.39 Hz, which enables identification of intra‑beat temporal coupling between pulsatile signals (Zhu et al. 2011; Sugawara et al. 2021, 2023, 2017). From these analysis, three data of each frequency domain value (i.e. spectra of AoP and MCAv and gain) were calculated in each condition. Finally, in each parameter, the average value in each condition was determined. For statistical analysis, transfer function gain was averaged from 0.78 – 7.80 Hz in each condition.

To validate the application of waveform-based data fitting for estimating transfer function gain, we compared the gain values derived from the present analytical approach with those obtained using previously published methods (Sugawara et al. 2017, 2023). Briefly, both analyses utilized simultaneously recorded AoP and MCAv waveform data from a pilot dataset collected in our laboratory (n = 5). The duration of each recording was 15 s. One-beat waveforms were constructed in line with current analysis approach. In contrast, in the prior methods, transfer function gain between AoP and MCAv was computed directly from the continuous waveform data without constructing averaged one-beat waveform. Agreement between the two approaches was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis, and associations were examined using Pearson’s correlation and repeated-measures correlation. Bland–Altman analysis showed a mean difference of 0.04, with 95% limits of agreement of –0.294 to + 0.368, and more than 90% of all data points lay within these limits (Supplemental data). A strong correlation was observed between the average transfer function gain calculated by the current and previous methods (r = 0.99, P < 0.01). Similarly, a high correlation in transfer function gain was also found when comparing values across each frequency (0.78 to 7.8 Hz) within subjects using repeated-measures correlation analysis (r(rm) = 0.96, P < 0.01). From these, we concluded that the analytical approach employed in the present study provides a valid means of extending the conventional method to asynchronous waveform data.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome of this study was the transfer function gain; however, this study conducted under the assumption that SV increases in the supine position from seated posture. From this concept, sample size was estimated from the previous study comparing SV between seated and supine postures, using the same equipment as in the present study (Karaki et al. 2025). Our calculation showed that 8 participants would provide 95% confidence to detect the differences in SV with an effect size of 0.6 and an α level of < 0.05. However, considering the technical difficulty of measuring MCAv, including instances where derivation of an adequate temporal window was not possible, as well as missing data, we recruited 15 participants. All statistical analyses were performed by using the SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY) except for repeated measure correlation analysis. The normality of continuous variables was examined by the Shapiro–Wilk test. To detect the differences in time domain systemic and cerebral vascular parameters, PETCO2, and average value of transfer function gain among postural conditions, data were analyzed using generalized linear mixed model analysis (factor, condition). Spectra of AoP and MCAv and transfer function gain were also analyzed by generalized linear mixed model analysis to detect postural differences in the spectra at each HR harmonic (factors, condition and HR harmonic) and differences in the transfer function gain at each frequency (factors, condition and frequency). In the generalized linear mixed model analysis, the factors of condition or condition and HR harmonics are set as fixed factors. Compound symmetry was selected as the covariance structure. Parametric data were modelled with an assumption of normal distribution. Nonparametric data were conducted by the model of gamma distribution or normal distribution (Robinson et al. 2006). In case of a significant interaction, the post hoc test was performed with the Bonferroni correction for multiple pairwise comparisons. A repeated-measures correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationship between intra-individual changes in SV, SVR, or CVRi and average gain using the R package (Bakdash and Marusich 2017). Data are presented as means ± standard deviation before the statistical analysis. The significance level was set to P < 0.05.

Results

Resting durations before obtaining the artificial free BP and MCAv waveforms from the start of physiological data recording in each condition were 13.7 ± 4.2 min, 14.1 ± 4.6 min, 13.4 ± 7.2 min, and 10.8 ± 0.8 min in 60°, 30°, 0°, and Leg conditions, respectively.

SV was higher in the supine with and without leg elevation than in the seated positions (Fig. 2, 60° vs. 0° and Leg, and 30° vs. Leg P < 0.05 for all). However, there was no significant difference of SV between the 0° and Leg conditions (P > 0.05 for both). CO showed no significant differences among postures, although a main effect of condition was detected (Table 1, P > 0.05 for all post hoc tests). By contrast, SVR decreased in the 0° and Leg conditions compared with the 60° and 30° conditions (P < 0.05 for all). HR was lower in Leg condition than in the 60° and 30° conditions (P < 0.01 for both).

From the 1st to 8th harmonic, the spectra of AoP and MCAv gradually decreased in all postures (Fig. 3, main effect of harmonic, P < 0.01 for both). When transfer function gain was averaged from 0.78 to 7.8 Hz, lower value was observed in 0° and Leg conditions compared with 60° condition (P < 0.05 for both). In contrast, the change in average gain was positively associated with SVR alteration (r(rm) = 0.67, 95% CI [0.48, 0.81], P < 0.01). However, there is no relationship between changes in CVRi and gain (r(rm) = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.26, 0.32], P = 0.81).

Discussion

The buffering mechanism of pulsatile hemodynamic transmission to the cerebrovasculature needs to be investigated in relation to age‑related vascular adaptations. As a preliminary study elucidating this buffering mechanism without vascular aging effects, the present study examined the impact of posture on heart‑to‑cerebral pulsatile transmission and its association with systemic and cerebral vascular resistance in young adults. The main findings were as follows: 1) SV increased from the seated to the supine position, and the AoP spectra also differed among postures. However, 2) the transfer function gain between AoP and MCAv decreased with the increase in SV, resulting in no significant increase in the MCAv spectra among postures. 3) The decrease in transfer function gain was associated with a reduction in SVR but was not correlated with changes in CVRi. These results suggest that cerebral pulsatile hemodynamic fluctuations may not be augmented despite increased cardiac ejection, owing to attenuation of aortic–cerebral pulsatile transmission in young adults. This buffering mechanism could involve changes in the systemic vascular resistance rather than in the cerebrovascular resistance.

Pulsatile hemodynamic fluctuations, a potential contributor to cerebral microvascular dysfunction (Thorin-Trescases et al. 2018; Palta et al. 2019), are augmented by increased cardiac ejection. Thus, it is crucial to modulate the pulsatile transition from the heart to the cerebrovasculature when SV increases. A previous study reported that the pulsatile transition from the aorta to the MCA is attenuated during aerobic exercise, a condition accompanied by marked metabolic, ventilatory, and autonomic perturbations (Sugawara et al. 2023). Under such circumstances, attenuation of pulsatile transmission has been attributed to heterogeneity in vascular regulation between systemic and cerebral circulations, which may enhance damping of pulsatile energy upstream of the cerebral vasculature rather than facilitate pulsatile flow within cerebral arteries (Ogoh et al. 2010, 2015; Willie et al. 2014; Sugawara et al. 2023).

In contrast to exercise, the present study demonstrates that a reduction in aortic–cerebral transfer function gain also occurs when SV is increased under resting supine conditions, in the absence of substantial metabolic or ventilatory activation. Importantly, the decrease in transfer function gain was associated with a reduction in systemic vascular resistance but not with changes in cerebrovascular resistance. These findings indicate that attenuation of pulsatile transmission to the cerebral circulation can be achieved through upstream systemic vascular adjustments alone, without requiring active changes in cerebrovascular tone. Collectively, the present results suggest that protective buffering of cerebral pulsatile hemodynamics is not restricted to exercise-specific conditions but may also operate during resting states when cardiac ejection is elevated.

A key finding of the present study was that changes in the average transfer function gain between AoP and MCAv were associated with alterations in SVR but not CVRi. This dissociation suggests that modulation of pulsatile transmission from the heart to the brain predominantly occurs upstream of the cerebral circulation, rather than through active changes in cerebrovascular tone. One plausible mechanism underlying this upstream modulation is a posture-related reduction in systemic vascular tone mediated by cardiopulmonary baroreflex activation. In the supine position, increased venous return stimulates cardiopulmonary receptors, leading to a decrease in sympathetic outflow and systemic vascular resistance (Katayama et al. 2024). Such systemic vasodilation may reduce arterial impedance and enhance damping of pulsatile energy generated by increased cardiac ejection, thereby limiting the transmission of pulsatile fluctuations of pressure and flow to the cerebral circulation. Consistent with this interpretation, the present findings demonstrate that reduced SVR was accompanied by a decrease in aortic–cerebral transfer function gain, leading to no elevation in MCAv pulsatility despite posture-related increases in SV.

From aortic pressure waveform analysis, excess pressure indices increased in supine postures compared to seated postures, in line with posture‑related changes in stroke volume. Aortic excess pressure reflects increased forward hemodynamic energy derived from cardiac ejection (Davies et al. 2010). However, aortic reservoir pressure indices did not differ among postures. Reservoir pressure reflects the blood‑volume reservoir function of central arteries (Schultz et al. 2014), which would be associated with aortic stiffness (Sugawara et al. 2018). Considering the results for reservoir pressure and the relationship between decreased systemic vascular resistance and attenuated transfer‑function gain, the posture‑related increase in pulsatile hemodynamic transmission to the cerebrovasculature may be attenuated not only by central arterial compliance but also by integrated vascular regulatory mechanisms operating throughout the systemic arterial tree.

Experimental considerations

The present study has several limitations. First, transcranial Doppler measures blood velocity and does not assess vessel diameter or volumetric flow. This technique allows for high time-resolution evaluation of cerebral circulation, enabling the examination of relative changes in pulsatile transmission (Sugawara et al. 2023, 2017). However, previous studies indicate that MCAv assessed by TCD was not associated with MCA flow assessed by magnetic resonance imaging (Fico et al. 2023). Therefore, future studies using advanced imaging modalities that enable direct quantification of intracranial blood flow, such as magnetic resonance imaging, may provide more complementary evidence regarding pulsatile mechanical stress on the cerebral vasculature. Second, AoP was not directly measured at the aortic root by catheterization. Instead, this study used a validated noninvasive estimation method (Sugawara et al. 2010, 2013), which is advantageous for assessing posture-related changes in AoP and allows for comparison with previous studies (Hughes and Casey 2017; Karaki et al. 2025). Third, the transfer function analysis was performed using unsynchronized AoP and MCAv waveforms; therefore, phase and coherence estimates could not be derived in the present study. These parameters would have provided additional insight into the interpretation of the transfer function outcomes in future studies (Sugawara et al. 2023, 2017). Fourth, SV was estimated using the Modelflow method. The current study focused on the postural change in SV within subjects rather than on absolute SV values. The Modelflow estimates of SV are associated with echocardiography measurement when evaluating relative changes in response of orthostatic stress (van Lieshout et al. 2003). While absolute SV values derived from Modelflow have inherent limitations, this method has been validated for detecting within-subject changes in SV across postural conditions and was therefore appropriate for the purposes of the present study. Fifth, brachial MAP was used in the calculation of CVRi to maintain consistency with prior literature (Sugawara et al. 2017, 2023), despite the central focus on AoP in the transfer function analysis. Considering the pressure changes from central to peripheral arteries (Nichols et al. 2022), future studies should note whether central or peripheral pressure is used for the calculation of cerebrovascular resistance. Sixth, this study did not control for menstrual phase or hormonal contraceptive use among all female participants. Future studies are expected to elucidate sex-specific and hormone-dependent mechanisms influencing pulsatile hemodynamic transmission to the cerebral vasculature. Lastly, all participants abstained from caffeine for at least 3 h. Blood pressure may be affected by only 3 h of abstaining from caffeine (Mesas et al. 2011). However, this study used a repeated-measures design and focused on within-subject changes in all parameters; thus, the influence of caffeine on our findings may be limited.

Future direction

The present study specifically focused on cardiac-frequency pulsatile transmission to isolate beat-by-beat hemodynamic interactions between the heart and the cerebral circulation in young adults. Future studies extending this framework to include lower-frequency oscillations (< 0.15 Hz), which predominantly reflect cerebrovascular autoregulatory mechanisms buffering cerebral perfusion against slow arterial pressure fluctuations (Claassen et al. 2016), may provide a more comprehensive understanding of multiscale cardiovascular–cerebrovascular regulation. Importantly, such integrative frequency-domain approaches may be particularly informative in the context of aging, in which arterial stiffening, elevated systemic vascular resistance, and altered autonomic regulation can impair both pulsatile damping and dynamic cerebrovascular control (Sugawara and Tanaka 2025). Investigations in older populations incorporating broader frequency ranges and direct indices of arterial stiffness are therefore warranted to clarify how age-related vascular adaptations influence the interaction between systemic and cerebral circulations under conditions of increased cardiac ejection. Such studies may help identify age-related vulnerabilities in cerebral protection against pulsatile mechanical stress and inform strategies to preserve cerebrovascular health.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effect of the posture-related increase in SV on aortic-cerebral pulsatile transmission in healthy young adults. Our results indicated that the transfer function gain between AoP and MCAv, which reflects the magnitude of pulsatile hemodynamic transmission, was attenuated as SV increased. In addition, this attenuation was associated with a decrease in systemic vascular resistance but did not correlate with the change in cerebrovascular resistance. Consequently, cerebral hemodynamic pulsatility may not be augmented despite an increase in pulsatile AoP fluctuation with higher SV in the supine position, owing to attenuated pulsatile transmission from the aorta to the cerebral circulation compared with the seated position. This buffering mechanism may be partly governed by systemic regulation of vascular tone rather than by local (e.g., cerebral) control.
