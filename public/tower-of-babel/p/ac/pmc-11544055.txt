Dietary supplementary with ellagic acid improves the intestinal barrier function and flora structure of broiler chicken challenged with E. coli K88☆
Authors: Peng Li, Shengnan Zhao, Yi Teng, Shaochen Han, Yuzhu Yang, Mengjun Wu, Shuangshuang Guo, Bingying Ding, Lei Xiao, Dan Yi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEllagic acid (EA) contributes to the immunity and anti-oxidant function of body, whereas there are few reports about its effect on the intestinal health and growth performance of broiler chickens. Hence, the present study was arranged to investigate the effect of dietary supplementary with EA on the intestinal barrier function and flora structure of broiler chickens challenged with Escherichia coli K88 (E. coli K88). A total of 216 healthy 1-day-old, Ross 308 broilers with uniform weight were randomly assigned into three treatment groups, six replicates in each group and twelve birds in each replicate. Broilers in the control (CTR) group and E. coli K88 infected group (ETEC) were fed with the basic diet, and 200 mg/kg EA was supplemented into the diet of the E. coli K88 infected group treated with EA (EAETEC). The animal trial had lasted for 42 days, and the outcomes showed that the ADG and ADFI during the animal trial, the jejunal villi height (VH) and the ratio of VH to crypt depth (CD) tended to be decreased with E. coli K88 treated (P< 0.05). Additionally, the level of serum diamine oxidase (DAO) and intestinal malondialdehyde (MDA) were elevated, the activity of intestinal total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), the mRNA levels in jejunal claudin-1 and occludin were down-regulated with E. coli K88 treated as well as the transcription levels of ileal Mucin-2, aquaporin-3 (AQP-3) and Na+/H+ exchanger proteins-3 (NHE-3) (P< 0.05). In addition, E. coli K88 down-regulated the α-diversity index of cecal flora, the ratio of Bacteroidota to Firmicutes and the relative abundance of Barnesiella were up-regulated and it of Alistipes was descended with E. coli K88 treated (P< 0.05). Beyond that, the content of propionic acid in the cecal chyme was decreased and the amino acid metabolic pathways were inhibited with E. coli K88 challenged (P< 0.05). Additionally, there was a significant positive correlation between the relative abundance of Alistipes and the levels of butyric acid in the caecal chyme and the activity of GSH-Px in the intestine (P< 0.05). Interestingly, dietary supplementary with EA could reshape the intestinal flora structure and alleviate the above negative effects of E. coli K88 on broiler chickens. In conclusion, dietary supplementary with ellagic acid improved the intestinal barrier function and flora structure of broiler chickens challenged with E. coli K88.Keywords: Ellagic acid, Broiler chicken, Barrier function, Intestinal flora, E. coli K88

Introduction

The world's population is increasing, and so is the demand for protein. Among them, chicken is regarded as one of the main sources of protein food supply, therefore, ensuring the healthy breeding of broiler chickens is related to the national economy and people's livelihood (Mckenna et al., 2020). As is known to us, intestine is the main site of nutrient absorption and the largest immune organ. Hence, intestinal health is the key to the healthy growth of broilers. However, there are a number of factors that adversely affect the intestinal health of broiler chickens, such as harmful bacteria and virus, anti-nutritional factors in feed, poor feeding and management conditions or others (Sommer et al., 2017). Among them, Escherichia coli, one of the Gram-negative bacterium, which is the original bacteria existing in human and animal intestines. Most of Escherichia coli is regarded as a conditioned pathogen, its abnormal abundance change will bring headaches to the farmers. Some other Escherichia coli, such as enterotoxic Escherichia coli K88, are troublemakers for intestinal health in broilers due to their secretion of endotoxins or their cell-wall components that cause immune stress (Ekim et al., 2020). In addition, the ban on antibiotics in feed makes the situation even worse. Therefore, the development of natural substitutes for feeding antibiotics is one of the key research topics at present.

Ellagic acid (EA) is widely found in various fruits, nuts and plants, by way of example, pomegranates, grapes and walnuts. There are many biological functions such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, anticancer, and regulating intestinal flora are demonstrated in EA (Gupta et al., 2021). Studies have also found that EA was able to inhibit the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, for instance, Escherichia coli and Salmonella. Interestingly, EA was not harmful to the proliferation of probiotics such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium (Qin et al., 2022). Although EA was proven with so many benefits, its application in broiler production alone has not been fully characterized.

More than ever, we want to know the effects of EA on intestinal health in broilers and investigate its potential as an alternative to antibiotics. In the present study, E.coli K88 was used to model intestinal damage in broilers to study the effects of dietary supplementary with ellagic acid on the intestinal barrier function and flora structure of broiler chicken challenged with E. coli K88. The aim of this experiment was to provide a theoretical basis for the application of ellagic acid in broiler production.

Materials and methods

Animal and diet

The animal study was carried out in the Wuhan Polytechnic University (Hubei, China), and the number named as WPU202307006 of the experimental design scheme was approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the unit. A total of 216 healthy male 1-day-old, Ross 308 broilers with uniform weight were randomly assigned into three treatment groups, six replicates in each group and twelve birds in each replicate. Broilers in the control (CTR) group and E. coli K88 infected group (ETEC) were fed with the basic diet, and 200 mg/kg EA was supplemented into the diet of the E. coli K88 infected group treated with EA (EAETEC). It should be noted that the E. coli K88 was presented by Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China. And the optimal dose of EA was 200 mg/kg from the perspective of growth performance and immunity in birds by setting different EA dose gradients in our previous study. This section has not yet been published, details about it could be obtained by email with the corresponding author. The feeding standard of China (Ministry of Agriculture of the People's Republic of China, 2004) was used to design the basal diet and nutrient levels, and the formula of basal diet was shown in Table 1. The purity of EA was greater than 90 % and purchased from Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., LTD. On day 10 of the trial, broilers in ETEC and EAETEC groups were orally given 1 ml of E. coli K88 fresh bacterial suspension (1×108 CFU/mL), and birds in the CTR group were treated with an equal volume of normal saline. A 42-day trial was performed, and on day 12 and 21, two birds in each replicate were selected to collect blood from the wing vein, and slaughtered for harvesting the samples. The average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion efficiency (FCR) were calculated from day 1 to day 21 as well as from day 1 to day 42. all birds raised in wire cages got free access to water and feed, and a 24 h light regime was performed during the animal trial. From day 1 to day 3, the room temperature was controlled at 35 °C, and then it was reduced by 1 °C per day until it was maintained at 25 °C.

Intestinal morphological structure and the level of serum diamine oxidase

The jejunum and ileum were removed separately and folded in half, then a 1 cm length in the middle was harvested and stored in a pre-cooled 4 % polyformaldehyde solution. Immediately after that, the intestine was coated with wax blocks, and then made slices and treated with HE staining. The villi height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) was measured according to the method described in the previous study (Hou et al., 2012). Briefly, in each intestinal section, 10 morphologically intact intestinal villi were selected to investigate the VH and CD with the help of a thousand-screen high-definition color pathology graphic analysis system in the Olymps BX-41TF microscope, and the ratio of VH to CD was calculated. The blood on day 12 and 21 was harvested from the wing vein, and then it was centrifuged at 4 °C, 3 500 r/min for 10 min to separate the supernatant and obtain the serum. The kit of diamine oxidase (DAO) purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Biological Engineering, Jiangsu, China was used to measure the level of DAO.

Intestinal antioxidant enzymes and peroxide products

On day 12 and 21 of the trial, jejunum and ileum were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen for grinding. Following the principle that the ratio of weight to volume is equal to 1:9, intestine and pre-cooled normal saline were well mixed evenly and centrifuged at 4 °C, 3 500 r/min for 10 min to separate the supernatant. The kits purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Biological Engineering, Jiangsu, China were used to investigate the levels of H2O2 and malondialdehyde (MDA), the enzyme activity of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and catalase (CAT) in the jejunum and ileum.

Transcription level of genes in the intestine

On day 12 and 21 of the trial, jejunum and ileum were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen before transferred to -80 °C refrigerator. According to the method described in the previous study (Wu et al., 2021), the total RNA was extracted for cDNA preparation, and real-time PCR were performed subsequently. Briefly, the total RNA was obtained with the Trizol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China), the following conditions were required in order to ensure RNA purity, with the ratio of OD260 to OD 280 approximately 2.0. Then, cDNA was prepared following the protocol of the gDNA Eraser (Takara, Dalian, China). Finally, an applied biosystems 7500 fast Real-time PCR system (Foster City, CA) was used to perform the real-time PCR. To be clear here, the real-time PCR reaction program should be that an initial denaturation step set at 95 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s, and an annealing and extension temperature at 60 °C for 34 s. The relative expression of each gene was analyzed by the way of 2−ΔΔCt method described in the previous study (Fu et al., 2010), among them, β-actin was used as the reference gene. 16S sequencing and analysis of specific intestinal bacteria

The cecal chyme was collected on day 21. QIAamp fast DNA stool mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract the genomic DNA of cecal microflora. A Nano Drop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to measure the concentration of DNA. Sequencing and analysis according to the method described by previous study (Zhang et al., 2018). Brifly, 16S rDNA genes V3-V4 region universal primers 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) were used to amplify bacterial DNA, and then a sequencing library was established after the PCR products were purified, quantified and homogenized. HiSeq2500 PE250 was used for on-machine sequencing. Sequencing analysis was completed by Beijing Nuohe Zhiyuan Bio-Information Technology Co., Ltd. Qiime software (Qiime2-2019.7, Nature Biotechnology) was used to generate species abundance tables of different taxonomic levels. R (Version 3.0.3) was used to get the Venn diagram and PCoA, and LEfSe software (Version 1.0) was used to obtain the LEFse results, among them LDA scores greater than 4. PICRUSt and R (Version 2.15.3) were used for functional prediction.

The chyme of jejunum, ileum and cecum were mixed, and then the DNA of bacteria was collected and its concentration was measured according to the protocol described above. The steps of real-time PCR and the calculation method of genes were the same as described above. The primers of Escherichia coli and Lactobacillus were as follows, Escherichia coli: F-GTTAATACCTTTGCTCATTGA, R-ACCAGGGTATCTAATCCTGT. Lactobacillus: F-AGCAGTAGGGAATCTTCCA, R-CACCGCTACACATGGAG. The reference gene was the total bacteria (16s rRNA), which primer was F- ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAGT, R- GTATTACCGCGGCTGCTGGCAC.

Contents of short-chain fatty acids in the cecal chyme

The cecal chyme was collected on day 21 of the trial, 0.5 g chyme was weighed and placed in a 2 ml EP tube containing 1.5 ml of ultra-pure water, mixed and stationary for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 4 °C, 15 000 r/min for 20 min to separate 1 ml supernatant into a new 2 ml EP tube containing 0.2 ml a 25 % metaphosphate solution, mixed and stationary for 30 min at 4°C, then the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C, 15 000 r/min for 20 min to separate supernatant, and a 0.22 micron filter membrane was used for supernatant filtration, and the filtered solution was collected. The gas chromatography analyzers (Agilent GC-MS 7890B) were used to analyze the contents of acetic acid, propionic acid and butyric acid. The chromatographic conditions were as follows, the parameter of chromatographic column named Agilent DB-FFAP was 30 m× 0.25 mm× 0.25 μm, the helium with purity greater than 99.99 % was used as carrier gas. The temperature conditions were as follows, 50 °C was held for 1 min, then heated up to 200 °C at the rate of 10 °C per min, the temperature of the inlet was 250 °C, the flow rate was 1.0 ml/min, and the injection volume was 1 μL. The shunting ratio was 2:1, EI was used as the ionization mode, in which the electron energy was -70 eV, the electron multiplier voltage was 0.954 kV, the ion source temperature was 280 °C, the four-stage rod temperature was 150 °C, the transmission line temperature was 280 °C, and the scanning range was 33-200 m/z. Agilent Mass Hunter was used for data analysis.

Statistical analysis

The One-way ANOVA and Duncan's multiple comparisons were used in SPSS 23.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) to analyze the data, and the independent sample T-test was used to analyze the differences between CTR and ETEC group, between ETEC and EAETEC group. The correlation between the measured indicators and the relative abundance of some bacteria was performed by the Pearson correlation coefficient in SPSS 23.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). In our outcomes, P< 0.05 was considered to be significant, 0.05< P< 0.1 indicated a trend of significant differences between groups additionally, and the Graphpad Prism 8.0 software was used to graph the data.

Results

Growth performance and intestinal morphological structure

Compared with the CTR group, E.coli K88 tended to decrease the ADG (P= 0.051), and ADFI (P= 0.054) during the animal trial, and down-regulated the ADFI from day 21 to day 42 of the trial (P< 0.05). Dietary supplementary with EA did not alleviate the negative effect of E.coli K88 on the ADG and ADFI (Table 3). The intestinal morphological structure was not affected by E.coli K88 and EA on day 12 of the trial, it should be mentioned that the jejunal villi height (P= 0.094) and the ratio of villi height to crypt depth (P= 0.01) were descended with E.coli K88 challenged on day 21 of the trial, dietary supplementary with EA tended to raise the villi height (P= 0.081) and elevated the ratio of villi height to crypt depth in the jejunum on day 21 of the trial (Table 4).

Genes expression in the intestine

The mRNA levels of claudin-1, occludin, Mucin-2 and AQP-3 in the jejunum, Mucin-2 and AQP-3 in the ileum were down-regulated with E.coli K88 challenged on day 12 of the trial (P< 0.05). Compared with ETEC group, dietary supplementary with EA up-regulated the transcription levels of jejunal claudin-1, occludin, ZO-1 and AQP-3, ileal ZO-1, Mucin-2, AQP-3 and NHE-3 (P< 0.05) (Fig. 2A, 2B). Compared with ETEC group, the mRNA levels of claudin-1, occludin, ZO-1, AQP-3 and NHE-3 in the jejunum, ileal claudin-1 and NHE-3 were up-regulated with EA supplemented in the diet on day 21 of the trial (P< 0.05) (Fig. 2C, 2D).

Intestinal flora structure and the levels of short-chain fatty acids in the cecal chyme

The α-diversity indexes such as shannon, simpson, ACE and Chao-1 were down-regulated with E.coli K88 challenged (Fig. 3A), dietary supplementary with EA alleviated the down-regulation of the indexes about shannon, ACE and Chao-1. Venn diagram showed that there was 1556 unique OTU in the CTR group, 424 in the ETEC group, 1243 in the EAETEC group (Fig. 3B). The outcomes about LEFse analysis indicated that Barnesiella was the dominant bacteria in the ETEC group, Bacteroides eggerthii was the dominant bacteria in the EAETEC group (Fig. 3D). On the level of phylum, the relative abundance of Bacteroidota and the ratio of Bacteroidota to Firmicutes were up-regulated with E.coli K88 challenged, dietary supplementary with EA tended to alleviate it (Fig. 4A). On the level of genus, E.coli K88 up-regulated the relative abundance of Barnesiella and down-regulated it of Alistipes, dietary supplementary with EA also could alleviate it (Fig. 4B).

Interestingly, the relative abundance of Alistipes was positively correlated with the activities of intestinal GSH-Px and CAT, the mRNA level of AQP-3, and the level of butyric acid in the cecal chyme (P< 0.05) (Fig. 4D).

Function prediction results showed that the metabolism of amino acids in bacteria such as glycine, serine, threnine and β-alanine were weakened, however the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins were enhanced with E.coli K88 challenged (Fig. 5A). Compared with ETEC group, dietary supplementary with EA up-regulated the metabolism of amino acids in bacteria such as glycine, serine, threnine and β-alanine, and also activated the PPAR signaling pathway (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

It is well established that maintaining intestinal health is one of the important factors to ensure efficient production of broiler chickens. A healthy intestine requires not only well-equipped absorption, barrier, and immune functions, but also a stable microbial community (Kim et al., 2019). The infection of pathogenic bacteria will cause a structural homeostasis imbalance of intestinal flora, which will negatively affect intestinal absorption, barrier and immune function, and ultimately impair the growth performance of broiler chickens (Lu et al., 2021). In the present study, E. coli K88 infection raised the abundance of E. coli in the intestinal cavity of broiler chickens, we further identify it disturbed the homeostasis of intestinal flora. We characterized the physiology of the intestine that might be affected by it. Indeed, we further observed the intestinal morphological structure and found that the integrity of intestinal villi was destroyed, the ratio of villi height to crypt depth was reduced and the level of serum DAO was elevated. Notably, the more complete the intestinal villi, or the higher the villi height, the better the intestinal absorption and barrier function, in addition, the higher the level of serum DAO, the more severe the degree of intestinal injury (Wang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2014). These outcomes might be used as evidence for the deterioration of growth performance of broiler chickens in the ETEC group. Study found that dietary supplementary with EA contributed to the intestinal villi and improved the growth performance of broiler chickens (Wang et al., 2022) . In the present study, dietary supplementary with EA was able to alleviate the negative effects of E. coli K88 on the intestinal villi and descended the up-regulation of serum DAO in broiler chickens challenged with E. coli K88. It illuminated us that EA was helpful for the intestinal health, however, it did not mitigate the negative effects of E. coli K88 on the growth performance of broiler chickens in the present study. This was not entirely consistent with the outcomes of previous study (Wang et al., 2022). To explain the observed activity, we might consider the age, diet composition and the dose of EA were not consistent with them.

To gain more insight into the effects of EA on the intestinal barrier function, we proposed that dietary supplementary with EA up-regulated the mRNA levels of claudin-1, occludin and ZO-1 in the intestine of birds challenged with E. coli K88. Those tight-junction proteins bind intestinal epithelial cells together, preventing pathogens or their harmful metabolites from penetrating the intestinal barrier and entering the bloodstream, where they could trigger a systemic inflammatory response (Suzuki, 2020). The absorption and transport of water by intestinal cells are closely related to diarrhea. AQP-3, a protein located on the cell membrane, regulates the flow of water in and out of the cell (Zhao et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2019). NHE-3 is involved in the regulation of osmotic pressure of intestinal cells and is necessary to maintain the balance of water and sodium ions (Dominguez and Rieg, 2024). In the present study, the transcription levels of intestinal AQP-3 and NHE-3 were down-regulated in the birds challenged with E. coli K88. This might be one of the reasons why E. coli causes diarrhea in livestock and poultry. Unfortunately, we did not investigate the diarrhea rate in this study. Interestingly, dietary supplementary with EA alleviated the down-regulation of intestinal AQP-3 and NHE-3 in the birds challenged with E. coli K88. Although a detailed investigation of it was beyond the scope of this work, we acknowledged that EA offered exciting opportunities to the intestinal barrier function and avoided the occurrence of diarrhea.

We generally accept that a healthy intestine should be able to maintain a balance of oxidation and antioxidation. Oxygen radicals or superoxide anions are elevated challenged by abnormal production environments and pathogens, intestinal enzymes involved in antioxidants are unable to remove oxygen free radicals, ultimately causing intestinal oxidative stress injury (Hussain et al., 2021). Malondialdehyde is commonly used as an indicator of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation. It is one of the products of lipid peroxidation and closely associated with cell injury and disease development (Miletic et al., 2018). In the present study, the level of malondialdehyde in the intestine was elevated, we further identify the contents of intestinal antioxidant related enzymes, for instance, T-SOD, CAT and GSH-Px were descended with E. coli K88 challenged. Our outcomes likely revealed E. coli K88 induced intestinal oxidative stress in broiler chickens, which might be a evidence of its negative effects on intestinal morphology and growth performance. A multitude of study characterized that EA contributed to improving the antioxidant capacity of broiler chickens. To illustrate, Yang et. al found that dietary supplementary with EA raised the levels of T-SOD, CAT and GSH-Px and alleviated the negative effects of heat stress on broiler chickens (Yang et al., 2022). Tang et. al also observed EA was able to up-regulate the levels of T-SOD and the villi height (Tang et al., 2022). In the present study, dietary supplementary with EA elevated the levels of T-SOD, CAT and GSH-Px in the intestine and down-regulated the contents of malondialdehyde, these results were consistent with previous studies (Tang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). Although the mechanism has not been fully characterized, we would like to believe that EA does improve the antioxidant capacity of the intestine.

The effect of natural plant active ingredients on improving intestinal health might be related to the regulation of intestinal flora structure (Xu et al., 2017). By way of example, Tang et. al (Tang et al., 2022) demonstrated that dietary supplementary with EA elevated the shannon index and the relative abundance of Firmicutes in the cecal chyme, which contributed to alleviating the negative effects of Clostridium perfringens on the intestine. In the present study, the α-diversity index such as shannon, ACE and Chao-1 were descended with E. coli K88 challenged, dietary supplementary with EA alleviated it. Additionally, dietary supplementary with EA tended to down-regulate the relative abundance of Bacteroidota in the cecal chyme of birds challenged with E. coli K88. It illustrated that EA had great potential to shape the structure of the intestinal flora. In order to deeply investigate it, we further characterized that dietary supplementary with EA alleviated the up-regulation of Barnesiella and the down-regulation of Alistipes in the cecal chyme of birds challenged with E. coli K88. Studies suggested that Barnesiella could stimulate the proliferation of CD4+T and CD8+T cells and participate in the regulation of immune homeostasis (Daillere et al., 2016; Shuai et al., 2024). Alistipes were able to metabolize and produce SCFAs such as acetic acid, propionate and butyric acid, thereby improving the intestinal health of host (Cai et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). In the present study, we also found the levels of acetic acid, propionate and butyric acid in the cecal chyme were elevated. Hence, we hold that the beneficial effect of EA on intestinal health might be related to its promotion of the proliferation of Alistipes.

To further explore this concept an association analysis was performed, and found that the relative abundance of Alistipes was positively correlated with the levels of intestinal CAT and GSH-Px, as well as the mRNA level of AQP-3 and the level of butyric acid in the cecal chyme. The results of functional prediction showed that EA reshaped the metabolic capacity of the intestinal flora in the birds challenged with E.coli K88, for instance, the metabolic capacity of glycine, serine, threnine and β-alanine. On the basis of above outcomes, we concluded that EA improved the intestinal health likely dur to the involvement of Alistipes. Another interesting finding was that dietary supplementary with EA might activated the PPAR signaling pathway in our outcomes, PPAR signaling pathway was involved in many biological processes such as immune regulation and lipid metabolism (Korbecki et al., 2019). We suggested it would be of great significance to further study the role and mechanism of Alistipes and PPAR signaling pathway in EA regulation of intestinal health in broiler chickens.

Conclusion

Dietary supplementary with EA contributed to alleviating the negative effects of E.coli K88 on the intestinal barrier and immune function, which might be related to EA improving the structure of intestinal flora, especially promoting the proliferation of Alistipes.
