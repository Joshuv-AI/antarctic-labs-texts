Advancing global precision in dementia research: Examining Normative Heterogeneity of Aging and Neurodegeneration in Chinese Elders (ENHANCE)
Authors: Boon Lead Tee, Jessica Spat‐Lemus, Spencer Xinyi Zhang, Shreya Upendra, Xinge Zhao, GuanXue Chen, Yu Chen, Xin Wen, Anna Yao, FangDa Leng, Clara Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Examining Normative Heterogeneity of Aging and Neurodegeneration in Chinese Elders (ENHANCE) project addresses gaps in Alzheimer's disease and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) research by prioritizing Chinese‐speaking communities—an underrepresented but rapidly growing population in the United States (US) and globally. ENHANCE uses a transadaptation approach to develop cognitive assessments that reflect the language and culture of Cantonese and Mandarin speakers, making tools more relevant and appropriate. The project brings together data from older Chinese American participants at University of California San Francisco (UCSF) and Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (ISMMS), guided by community input and pilot testing results of research instruments. By combining data from ISMMS, UCSF, and the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC), ENHANCE explores meaningful cross‐cultural differences in AD/ADRD risk and progression. The goal is to improve diagnostic accuracy and representation in AD/ADRD research. ENHANCE helps make AD/ADRD research more responsive to language and cultural backgrounds in the older Chinese American community, supporting the goal of precision medicine in diverse population.

WHY IS ENGAGING CHINESE‐SPEAKING COMMUNITIES IN AD/ADRD RESEARCH IMPORTANT?

With the aging global population, the incidence of Alzheimer's disease and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) is increasing. 1 Yet, significant gaps remain in the inclusion of racial/ethnic groups in clinical research overall, including Chinese‐speaking communities. Given that these communities represent one of the largest and fastest‐growing ethnoracial groups 2 in the United States (US) and globally—and that Chinese is one of the most widely spoken native languages worldwide and the third most spoken language in the US 3 —this continued disparity in research highlights the urgent need for more inclusive scientific efforts. Studies have shown that older Asian Americans, including Chinese Americans, often experience delays in diagnosis and/or their dementia syndromes go undetected. 4 , 5 These disparities are driven in part by social determinants of health such as, but not limited to, gaps in health‐care literacy and reduced access to culturally and linguistically competent care. Increasing the participation of Chinese‐speaking individuals in dementia research not only enhances equitable practices and but also advances scientific understanding. For example, the Examining Normative Heterogeneity of Aging and Neurodegeneration in Chinese Elders (ENHANCE) study will contribute to greater insight of the unique cognitive profiles of older Chinese Americans on neuropsychological measures in order to inform the development of more culture‐considered assessment batteries. Such efforts are also essential for advancing precision medicine and reinforcing health‐care services. Overlooking Chinese‐speaking communities means we may miss key differences that could offer valuable insights into how AD/ADRD develops and affects people across different backgrounds in the US.

WHAT INSIGHTS INTO AD/ADRD RESEARCH HAVE EMERGED FROM STUDIES INVOLVING CHINESE POPULATIONS?

Research involving Chinese populations has started to shed light on how aging and dementia risk factors differ across cultural and linguistic contexts. A growing body of studies have highlighted the critical role of culture and language in shaping cognitive aging patterns, dementia symptomatology, and biomarker profiles. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Studies have revealed cultural influences across a wide range of cognitive domains, including visual perception, 15 attention control, 13 spatial reasoning, 16 sense of self, 17 and motivation. 18 Furthermore, numerous neuroimaging studies have found that individuals from different cultural backgrounds may engage distinct neural pathways when performing similar cognitive tasks. 12 , 13 , 19

Language, as both a conduit of culture and a reflection of unique linguistic structures, plays a pivotal role in shaping the heterogeneity of clinical manifestations in neurodegenerative diseases. For example, language‐specific symptomatology has been reported in aphasic patients across diverse linguistic backgrounds, including German, 20 Italian, 21 Japanese, 22 , 23 , 24 French, 25 , 26 Spanish, 27 and Chinese 9 languages. In particular, Chinese‐speaking individuals with primary progressive aphasia (PPA) exhibit distinct patterns of dysgraphia that differ from those documented in English‐speaking cohorts. 28 Moreover, Chinese‐speaking PPA patients present with additional language‐specific impairments, such as lexical tone errors and deficits in noun classifier usage. 29 , 30 These findings underscore the critical need to develop culturally and linguistically appropriate assessment tools and highlight the importance of including diverse populations in dementia research to ensure accurate diagnosis and equitable care. Factors such as education level, literacy, and language proficiency have a significant influence on the interpretation of cognitive testing and dementia diagnosis. When assessment tools are not properly adapted and interpreted, these factors can result in underdiagnoses or misclassification for Chinese‐speaking individuals.

When enrolling Chinese‐speaking participants into research studies in the US, it is imperative to address a range of enrollment barriers. Cultural stigma surrounding AD/ADRD, limited access to linguistically and culturally concordant health‐care providers, and concerns about privacy can all hinder individuals from seeking early evaluation or participate in research. These challenges affect not only who receives a timely diagnosis but also influence the representativeness of study populations—specifically, whether they reflect the diversity of the US population—and compromises the consistency of data collection across Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs).

WHAT MAKES YOUR CURRENT TRANSLATION WORK TIMELY AND IMPACTFUL?

The ENHANCE project aims to address disparities in dementia research by undertaking transadaptation—a process that combines translation and cultural adaptations—to develop cognitive assessments and study materials that are both linguistically and culturally appropriate for Chinese‐speaking populations. The materials focus on the two most widely spoken Chinese languages, Cantonese and Mandarin, and produces materials in both Simplified and Traditional Chinese scripts, resulting in four harmonized versions of the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center Uniform Data Set 4.0 (NACC UDS 4.0) packets. This initiative comes at a pivotal moment, as research institutions across the US work to fulfill the demand for inclusive, population‐representative data under the National Alzheimer's Project Act and related initiatives. Such work is critical not only for generating clinically relevant information, but also for laying the groundwork for future studies and clinical trials that aim to enroll AD/ADRD populations reflective of the diverse communities served in real‐world clinical settings.

Developing and adapting study materials that are culturally and linguistically sensitive to the target population leads to research studies that are more inclusive and better balanced, ultimately leading to findings that more accurately reflect the full spectrum of those affected by AD/ADRD. Moreover, by engaging diverse populations with distinct biological and environmental backgrounds, we can deepen our understanding of the neurobiology of aging and advance toward a truly individualized precision medicine approach to dementia prevention and treatment.

WHAT IS THE APPROACH OF THE ENHANCE STUDY?

The ENHANCE project integrates psychometric rigor with community‐centered cultural adaptation to develop linguistically valid and culturally sensitive versions of the NACC UDS 4.0 for Chinese‐speaking populations. Our translation and adaptation process combined the World Health Organization (WHO) framework, 31 which emphasizes conceptual and cultural equivalence, with the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) recommendations 32 focusing on measurement equivalence and cross‐regional harmonization.

According to ISPOR, when a single language is shared across multiple regions with geographic and cultural proximity, researchers may adopt either a universal approach, which is creating one version acceptable across all regions, or a same‐language adaptation approach, in which an existing translation is modified for neighboring contexts, particularly when test items reference education, health‐care systems, or culturally specific terminology. Our study population primarily includes Chinese American older adults who emigrated from China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and, to a lesser extent, from other regions with Chinese‐speaking communities, including Southeast Asian countries such as Vietnam, Malaysia, and Singapore. Mandarin is the predominant spoken language in China, Taiwan, and many Chinese‐speaking communities in Southeast Asia, whereas Cantonese is primarily spoken in Hong Kong and the Guangdong province. While Simplified Chinese is the standard written script in China and much of Southeast Asia, Traditional Chinese is used in Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau. Given the linguistic and geographic proximity of these regions, and the shared linguistic foundations, which includes both Traditional and Simplified Chinese scripts, our process represents a hybrid of ISPOR's universal and same‐language adaptation approaches. Specifically, we created two spoken‐language versions (Mandarin and Cantonese) using a same‐language adaptation framework and implemented a universal adaptation approach to harmonize written materials across traditional and simplified Chinese scripts. This hybrid framework ensures that the Mandarin and Cantonese versions with the two script formats, while linguistically distinct, achieve broad acceptability, cultural appropriateness, and functional equivalence across Chinese‐speaking populations of diverse origins. Accordingly, our approach aligns with WHO guidance while also adhering to ISPOR's best practices, tailored to the linguistic and cultural diversity of Chinese‐speaking populations in the US.

For non‐cognitive study materials (e.g., questionnaires), we began with independent forward translations into Mandarin and Cantonese, followed by independent back‐translations into English. Differences between forward and back versions were reconciled in consensus meetings. When disagreements persisted, further verifications were conducted by bilingual or multilingual stakeholders proficient in Mandarin–English or Cantonese–English. Once consensus was achieved, our Community Advisory Board (CAB) members participated in reviews, ensuring that the materials reflected not only linguistic accuracy but also cultural relevance, appropriateness of idioms, and sensitivity to health beliefs as well as stigma surrounding dementia. The CAB primarily comprised bilingual or multilingual clinical experts, including neuropsychologists, physicians, postdoctoral scholar, outreach experts from the Alzheimer's Association, and community leaders from senior service organizations (e.g., Caring Kind, and Chinese American Coalition for Compassionate Care). Following this, pilot testing with senior volunteers was conducted to evaluate both cultural and linguistic appropriateness across the Mandarin, Cantonese, and English versions. Once the Traditional Chinese version was finalized, it was converted into a Simplified Chinese version.

In addition to the staged translation process described above, the transadaptation of neurocognitive assessments also leveraged data from Chinese American participants at the University of California San Francisco (UCSF) Memory and Aging Center and the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (ISMMS), the study team has applied psychometric and statistical methods to identify items within the Chinese version of the NACC UDS 3.0 that may elicit different responses in older Chinese American participants. Specifically, score distributions from cognitively normal Chinese American participants tested in Mandarin or Cantonese has been compared to those from English‐speaking non‐Hispanic White participants, adjusting for age and education. Item response theory and measurement invariance analysis were also employed to detect differential item functioning. This approach aims to ensure that the resulting cognitive assessment materials were not only linguistically and culturally appropriate, but also there was measurement equivalence for older Chinese Americans with diverse linguistic and education backgrounds. We are currently finalizing the transadaptation analyses for the remaining measures in the cognitive test battery and anticipate that this process will be completed prior to the second half of 2026. Following this, we will initiate data collection using the revised versions to validate the instruments and establish normative data to support accurate interpretation for Chinese‐speaking individuals in the US. The transadapted NACC UDS 4.0 battery by ENHANCE study are nearing completion, and we anticipate that finalization will occur before the second half of 2026. Thereafter, data collection using the revised instruments will commence to empirically validate their psychometric performance and to generate normative reference data, thereby facilitating accurate interpretation of cognitive outcomes among Chinese‐speaking populations in the US.

WHAT OPPORTUNITIES LIE AHEAD FOR CONTINUED DATA COLLECTION?

We are planning a stepwise validation process of our transadapted materials across several US sites, starting with those that serve larger Chinese‐speaking communities. Ensuring the success and sustainability of this effort will require continued investment in bilingual team members, culturally and linguistically appropriate training materials, and robust community outreach. In addition to regional sites, we are also in the early stages of exploring international partnerships to better coordinate the collection of Chinese‐language data across studies in Canada and Asia countries. By creating more consistency in data collection, we will be able to make meaningful comparisons across cultural groups, identify larger public health patterns, and contribute to more globally informed approaches in precision health.

The implementation of the ENHANCE project presents a number of strategic opportunities to advance equity and scientific rigor in dementia research. First, ENHANCE presents an opportunity to harmonize cognitive assessment protocols across ADRCs and align them with international initiatives such as the NACC, the ADNI, and the Asian Cohort for Alzheimer's Disease (ACAD). Standardizing data collection across sites will strengthen our ability to conduct cross‐cultural comparisons and contribute to globally informed models of dementia risk and resilience. Furthermore, it provides an opportunity for longitudinal follow‐up of Chinese‐speaking participants in AD/ADRD research. This will deepen our understanding of disease progression and support the identification of early, population‐specific markers of decline.

Additionally, the project paves the way for linguistic expansion beyond Cantonese and Mandarin, with the potential to adapt this model for other languages. Such efforts would broaden the inclusivity and applicability of our tools, enabling us to better serve a wider range of non–English‐speaking communities both within the US and internationally. The ENHANCE infrastructure also facilitates integration of clinical and neurocognitive data with neuroimaging. This model supports more precise phenotypic characterization and aids in disentangling the complex interplay between linguistic–cultural factors and underlying neurobiological mechanisms. The project further enhances community engagement within the research infrastructure and strengthens partnership with local communities.

HOW MIGHT THIS WORK HELP ADVANCE THE AD/ADRD FIELD?

The work discussed above helps establish a research framework that more accurately reflects the linguistic and cultural heterogeneity of the older Chinese population in the US. By including Chinese‐speaking individuals, who have often been underrepresented in AD/ADRD research, we generate data that is more representative and relevant for researchers, clinicians, and policymakers alike. Further, this project not only enhances the generalizability of findings but also opens new avenues for understanding dementia risk, disease progression, and treatment response through the lens of language and culture.
