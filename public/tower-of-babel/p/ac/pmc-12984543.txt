Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Disease Burden in Children and Adults Following Heart Transplantation

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDHeart transplantation (HT) is the definitive therapy for end-stage heart failure. However, with improving post-HT survival in the modern era, recipients are increasingly cumulatively exposed to unique risk factors for cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) dysfunction. An expanded understanding of the incidence and prevalence of CKM dysfunction post-HT may inform screening and therapeutic strategies to mitigate adverse events.OBJECTIVESThe aim of this study was to characterize the incidence and prevalence of CKM risk factors in adult and pediatric HT recipients and to define their association with cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) and mortality.METHODSA single-center retrospective observational study was conducted in adults and children who underwent HT between January 1, 2015, and June 30, 2024. Longitudinal clinical and laboratory data were extracted from the electronic health record. Incidence rates (IRs) of type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM2), overweight or obesity, hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and dyslipidemia were calculated. longitudinal trajectories of CKM dysfunction were constructed, and the impact of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP1RAs) on CKD and body mass index, respectively, was evaluated. Finally, immunologic and CKM comorbidities were linked with clinical outcomes by using time-varying Cox regression models.RESULTSDuring the study period, 860 adults and 84 children underwent HT. Among adults, the IRs (reported as cases per 100 person-years) of DM2, overweight or obesity, dyslipidemia, and CKD were 28.6, 77.3, 139.1, and 69.7, respectively. Among children, the IRs of DM2, overweight or obesity, dyslipidemia, and CKD were 2.8, 26.9, 5.5, and 3.6. Within 12 months post-HT, 99% of adults developed stage 1 or 2 hypertension, and 22.1% of all adults developed hemoglobin A1c levels ≥6.5%, regardless of a preexisting diagnosis of DM2. Similarly, 37.5% of adults developed moderate to severe hypertriglyceridemia and 31.1% manifested worsened low-density lipoprotein cholesterol control. Among adults with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥45 mL/min/1.73 m2 pre-HT, 86.2% displayed worsening renal function (eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2) within 12 months post-HT. In adults initiated on SGLT2 inhibitor post-HT (n = 242), there was a nonlinear improvement in eGFR during the ensuing 12 months; for individuals initiated on GLP1RAs (n = 168), there was a predominantly linear reduction in body mass index. Among CKM comorbidities, none was significantly associated with CAV, whereas DM2 was associated with increased post-HT mortality (HR: 1.84; 95% CI: 1.04–3.25).CONCLUSIONSA significant proportion of HT recipients experience new-onset or worsening CKM dysfunction after HT. Furthermore, CKM comorbidities are associated with post-HT mortality. These results indicate that SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP1RAs may mitigate the burden of CKM disease.

Survival after heart transplantation (HT) has improved steadily over the past few decades.1 Although risk factors and clinical outcomes, including acute rejection, cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV), and graft survival, have been prioritized for study, less studied or understood is the burden of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) disease as HT recipients live increasingly longer. Cardiometabolic disease encompasses type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM2), obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and the metabolic syndrome, and it has been rising in prevalence in the United States, leading to increased risk of stroke, cardiovascular disease, and death.2 Indeed, in recognition of the significant contribution of poor CKM health to morbidity and mortality, the AHA (American Heart Association) has called for improved understanding of major gaps in knowledge regarding CKM dysfunction.3,4

Even preceding transplantation, individuals with end-stage organ disease exhibit a high prevalence of DM2 (ranging from 15%-30%)5 and obesity.6 After HT, recipients are cumulatively exposed to unique risk factors such as immunosuppressive therapies that directly modulate metabolic pathways and may accelerate metabolic and kidney disease.7–9 Estimates suggest that within 1 year post-transplant, up to 71% of recipients develop hypertension and 60% develop dyslipidemia.10 Because allograft outcomes are thought to be dependent in part on metabolic risk,11 the ISHLT (International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation) recommends frequent screening for both dyslipidemia and DM2.12 However, granular data on incidence and prevalence of CKM outcomes after HT in adults and children are limited. Furthermore, it remains unclear how novel therapeutic agents such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP1RAs) may mitigate CKM dysfunction in this population.

Here, we leverage the extensive clinical data available at our high-volume transplant center to characterize the burden of CKM dysfunction in adult and pediatric HT recipients and link these comorbidities with clinical outcomes. Furthermore, in a subset of individuals, we evaluate the impact of SGLT2 inhibitors on kidney function and GLP1RAs on body mass index (BMI). These data inform opportunities to mitigate CKM morbidity after HT.

METHODS

STUDY POPULATION.

This study was a retrospective review of all individuals aged ≥2 years who underwent HT at VUMC (Vanderbilt University Medical Center) between January 1, 2015, and June 30, 2024. Individuals who underwent transplant elsewhere who transferred their care to VUMC post-HT were included. Individuals referred by the Veterans Affairs system and who underwent HT at VUMC were excluded, as these individuals are not managed at VUMC beyond their index admission.

As is the case for all adult HT recipients at our center, maintenance immunosuppression during the first year post-HT consisted of calcineurin inhibitors, antiproliferative agents, and steroids; patients were progressively weaned off of steroids during the first 4 to 6 months post-HT with the exception of multiorgan recipients. Sensitized individuals with panel reactive antibodies >25% were treated with basiliximab or thymoglobulin induction at the supervising transplant attending’s discretion. Pediatric HT recipients at VUMC undergo thymoglobulin induction followed by a 5-day steroid taper. Subsequently, maintenance therapy in children constitutes a calcineurin inhibitor and antiproliferative agent for 3 months, followed by replacement of the antiproliferative agent with a mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor.

This study was approved by the VUMC Institutional Review Board (IRB #200551).

DATA COLLECTION AND OUTCOMES.

Donor data were abstracted from the UNOS (United Network for Organ Sharing) database, and recipient data were extracted from VUMC’s electronic health record. Data collected included longitudinal laboratory values for BMI, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). In addition, diagnoses (based on International Classification of Diseases–Ninth Revision [ICD-9] or International Classification of Diseases–Tenth Revision [ICD-10] codes) and their respective dates were abstracted as follows: 1) chronic kidney disease (CKD): ICD-9 codes 585 and 585.* and ICD-10 codes N18 and N18.*; 2) dyslipidemia: ICD-9 code 272.* and ICD-10 codes E78.* and E88.*; and 3) hypertension: ICD-9 codes 401, 401.*, 402, 402.*, 403, 403.*, 404, 404.*, 405, and 405.* and ICD-10 codes I10, I10.*, I11, I11.*, I12, I12.*, I13, I13.*, I16, and I16.*.

Hypertension was also extracted from the electronic health record when an individual’s problem list included “hypertension” excluding cases in which the language also included “portal/pulmonary/rv/intracranial hypertension.” DM2 was defined according to the eMERGE/Phenotype KnowledgeBase definition. Longitudinal laboratory data were extracted from the time of HT until 36 months post-HT, and laboratory data were averaged in 3-month increments. We allowed for data collected within ±30 days of each pre-designated timepoint post-HT to be included in analyses specific to that timepoint.

Among adults, BMI was categorized as underweight or healthy weight (BMI <25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25 to <30 kg/m2), class I obesity (BMI 30 to <35 kg/m2), class II obesity (BMI 35 to <40 kg/m2), and class III obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2) as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. For children, height and weight were converted to z-scores, followed by calculation of BMI z-scores that were age- and sex-adjusted (CDCAnthro).13–15 BMIs were then categorized as normal weight (BMI z-score <1.04), overweight (BMI z-score of 1.04 to <1.645), and obese (BMI z-score ≥1.645).14–16

In accordance with the ACC (American College of Cardiology) and AHA guidelines,17 blood pressure (BP) in adults was categorized as normal (SBP <120 mm Hg and DBP <80 mm Hg), elevated (SBP 120–129 mm Hg and DBP <80 mm Hg), stage 1 hypertension (SBP 130–139 mm Hg or DBP 80–89 mm Hg), and stage 2 hypertension (SBP ≥140 mm Hg or DBP ≥90 mm Hg). For children, the American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines were used,18 and 50th percentile for height assumed for simplicity. A normal BP for children aged 2 to 12 years was defined as <90th percentile for SBP and DBP. Elevated BP was defined as ≥90th percentile to <95th percentile or 120/80 mm Hg to <95th percentile (whichever was lower). Stage 1 hypertension was defined as ≥95th percentile to <95th percentile + 12 mm Hg or 130/80 to 139/89 mm Hg (whichever was lower). Finally, stage 2 hypertension was defined as ≥95th percentile + 12 mm Hg or ≥140/90 mm Hg, whichever was lower. For children aged ≥13 years, normal BP was defined as <120/80 mm Hg, elevated BP as 120/<80 to 129/<80 mm Hg, stage 1 hypertension as 130/80 to 139/89 mm Hg, and stage 2 hypertension as ≥140/90 mm Hg. If individuals had multiple BP checks within 1 day, the average SBP and DBP were used.

Outcomes included incident overweight or obesity, DM2, dyslipidemia, and hypertension after HT, as well as prevalence of these conditions post-HT. In addition, trends in eGFR, HbA1c, SBP, DBP, and lipid profiles across the first 36 months post-HT were examined. For eGFR analyses, individuals who underwent simultaneous multiorgan transplantation were excluded, as were those with a history of dialysis pre-HT to minimize any confounding on post-HT eGFR trajectory. For individuals who needed renal replacement therapy after HT, we assigned their eGFR value to 0 after their first renal replacement therapy episode. In a subset of individuals who were prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP1RAs, we evaluated the impact of these therapies on eGFR and BMI trajectory, respectively, in the 12 months following prescription. Furthermore, we captured cellular rejection (acute cellular rejection [ACR]), defined as grade 2R or 3R,19 and antibody-mediated rejection (AMR), defined as pathologic AMR 2 or pathologic AMR 3,20 along with coronary angiography and mortality. CAV was considered significant with CAV grade ≥1, per ISHLT (International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation) consensus.21

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS.

Continuous variables are described as mean ± SD or median (Q1-Q3). Categorical variables are described as n (%). Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted for incident DM2, worsening HbA1c to ≥6.5% (irrespective of previous diagnosis of DM2), dyslipidemia, worsening triglycerides and LDL-C, worsening eGFR, and ≥stage 1 hypertension. Incident rates (IRs) of disease per 100 person-years were calculated for each variable as follows: [number of new cases/(total population at risk follow-up time)] × 100. Cumulative incidence of disease burden, estimated by using the Kaplan-Meier method, after transplantation is reported in percentages.

To examine the trajectories of laboratory values over time, trend plots are presented. In addition, to mitigate bias from unequal sample sizes at different timepoints, a generalized least squares (GLS) model with a first-order autoregressive (AR[1]) correlation was used to construct models of trajectories over 36 months post-HT for the following: HbA1c, triglycerides, LDL-C, cholesterol, HDL, BMI, eGFR, SBP, and DBP. The GLS models include months since HT (0–36), and the laboratory values were analyzed over time without assuming a linear relationship. A flexible nonlinear model using a restricted cubic spline with 3 knots was utilized. The model output provided 2 coefficients: one for time (representing the linear effect) and another for timeʹ (representing the nonlinear effect). The results of these GLS models should be interpreted as model-estimated changes over time. Similarly, GLS models with AR(1) correlation were developed to characterize the trajectories of eGFR and BMI following initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP1RA, respectively. Details, including sample sizes at each timepoint for all CKM variables assessed, are outlined explicitly in Supplemental Tables 1 to 9.

Time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models were fit to examine risk factors for CAV after HT. Within the model, the following risk factors were treated as time-varying variables: development of donor-specific antibodies (DSAs), biopsy-proven ACR, biopsy-proven AMR, DM2, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CKD. The model also adjusted for donor age, recipient age at transplant, and recipient sex. Similarly, Cox models were fitted for post-HT mortality, including DSAs, CAV, DM2, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CKD as time-varying covariates.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by repeating all the aforementioned analyses but excluding individuals who died or were lost to follow-up within 30 days of HT. We also performed sensitivity analyses excluding individuals who underwent simultaneous multiorgan transplants. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and RStudio 2024.12.1 (Posit PBC).

RESULTS

INDIVIDUAL CHARACTERISTICS.

During the study period, 860 adults (mean age 52.9 ± 12.8 years at HT) and 84 children (mean age 10.0 ± 4.5 years at HT) underwent HT. Female individuals accounted for 30.1% and 44% of the adult and pediatric cohorts, respectively. Recipient and donor demographic characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The baseline prevalence of DM2, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and CKD in adults was 49%, 76%, 36%, and 78%, respectively.

TRENDS IN DM2 AFTER HT IN ADULTS.

Among 700 adults whose HbA1c was captured at time of HT, mean HbA1c was 5.87% ± 0.86%, and this steadily increased to a peak of 7.46% ± 2.04% by 30 months post-HT (Table 2). Among 434 adults without DM2 prior to HT, based on the Kaplan-Meier plot, the cumulative incidence of developing diabetes was 38.8% and 43.8% within 6 months and 1 year post-HT, respectively (Figure 1A, Supplemental Figure 1). Median DM2-free survival was 1.98 years. The IR of DM2 was 28.6 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 24.8–32.4 per 100 person-years). In 507 adults with HbA1c <6.5% prior to HT irrespective of prior DM2 diagnosis, the cumulative incidence of developing worsening HbA1c to ≥6.5% at 6 months post-HT and 1 year post-HT were 12.6% and 22.1%, respectively (Figure 1B). The median survival without worsening of HbA1c was 4.52 years. The IR of worsening HbA1c after HT was 17.2 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 14.6–19.8 per 100 person-years). A GLS model of HbA1c trajectory post-HT suggested that time after HT was significantly associated with HbA1c values (Pchunk < 0.001) (Supplemental Figure 2). We found significant linear (β = 0.049; P < 0.001) and nonlinear (β = −0.047; P = 0.043) time effects to HbA1c levels after HT. Overall, HbA1c values followed a steady increase after transplantation (model-estimated difference between 0 and 36 months was 0.98%; P < 0.001).

TRENDS IN BMI AFTER HT IN ADULTS.

Among 826 adults with available BMI data at HT, mean BMI was 29.2 ± 5.0 kg/m2. Of these, 21.4% had underweight or healthy weight, 35.7% had overweight, 29.7% had class I obesity, 11.5% had class II obesity, and 1.7% had class III obesity (Table 2). The prevalence of post-HT underweight/healthy weight, overweight, and obesity is depicted in Figure 2A. Among 178 adults with BMI <25 kg/m2 at time of HT, the cumulative incidences of developing a BMI ≥25 kg/m2 within 6 months and 1 year post-HT were 56.6% and 66.9%, respectively (Figure 2B). The median post-HT survival free of overweight or obesity was 0.29 year. The IR of overweight or obesity was 77.3 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 63.8–90.7 per 100 person-years). GLS modeling revealed that time after HT was significantly associated with BMI trajectory (Pchunk = 0.001) (Supplemental Figure 3), showing significant linear (β = −0.017; P < 0.001) and nonlinear (β = 0.052; P = 0.001) time effects. After HT, there was an initial decline in BMI in all adults, followed by a steady increase in BMI thereafter (model-estimated increase between 12 and 36 months post-HT of 0.85 kg/m2; P < 0.001). Among 168 individuals started on a GLP1RA (semaglutide, exenatide, dulaglutide, or lixisenatide) after HT, there was an overall significant change in BMI in the 12 months after prescription (Pchunk = 0.006) (Figure 2C), with a predominantly linear decrease (linear β = −0.080; P = 0.10). The model-estimated reduction in BMI between 0 and 36 months after GLP1RA use was −2.94 kg/m2 (P = 0.023).

TRENDS IN LIPID PROFILES AFTER HT IN ADULTS.

Most adults (n = 653) had a diagnosis of dyslipidemia before HT. At the time of HT, the mean triglyceride level was 129.6 ± 84.0 mg/dL, mean LDL-C level was 70.5 ± 31.0 mg/dL, mean total cholesterol level was 135.7 ± 41.3 mg/dL, and mean HDL level was 39.9 ± 13.9 mg/dL. LDL-C, total cholesterol, and HDL levels all uptrended after transplant, peaking at 3 months at 81.4 ± 30.7 mg/dL, 164.2 ± 41.5 mg/dL, and 51.2 ± 16.5 mg/dL, respectively (Table 2). Triglyceride levels also uptrended steadily but did not peak until 21 months post-HT (192.8 ± 134.9 mg/dL). Among 207 adults without dyslipidemia prior to HT, the IR of dyslipidemia was 139.1 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 118.7–159.5 per 100 person-years), with a cumulative incidence of 77.7% of individuals developing dyslipidemia by 6 months post-HT (Figure 3, Supplemental Figure 4). The median dyslipidemia-free survival was 0.12 year.

In 572 adults with triglyceride levels <200 mg/dL at time of HT, 37.5% developed levels ≥200 mg/dL by 1 year post HT. Similarly, in 470 adults with LDL-C levels <100 mg/dL at the time of HT, 31.1% developed levels ≥100 mg/dL by 1 year post HT. This translated into an IR of 29.4 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 26.0–32.8 per 100 person-years) for worsening triglyceridemia post-HT and 21.7 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 18.6–24.8 per 100 person-years) for worsening LDL-C control post-HT. GLS modeling revealed significant linear and nonlinear time effects after HT to increasing triglycerides (Pchunk <0.001) and cholesterol (Pchunk = 0.007), with a predominantly linear time effect on LDL-C (Pchunk = 0.029). In our modeling, triglycerides were estimated to peak at 20 months post-HT (linear β = 2.782; P < 0.001; nonlinear β = −3.231; P < 0.001) (Supplemental Figure 5), representing a model-estimated increase by 30 mg/dL between 0 and 20 months post-HT; total cholesterol levels were estimated to peak between 12 and 15 months post-HT (linear β = 0.560; P = 0.007; nonlinear β = −0.866; P = 0.002) (Supplemental Figure 6). Conversely, LDL-C levels were estimated to steadily decline after HT (linear β = −0.172; P = 0.029; nonlinear β = 0.035; P = 0.871) (Supplemental Figure 7). The model-estimated reduction in LDL-C levels between 0 and 36 months post-HT was 5.3 mg/dL. There were no significant changes in HDL levels post-HT (Pchunk = 0.227).

TRENDS IN HYPERTENSION AFTER HT IN ADULTS.

Before HT, 67.7% of adults had normal BP, 25.5% had elevated BP, 6.7% had stage 1 hypertension, and 0.1% had stage 2 hypertension (Table 2). Mean pre-HT SBP was 115.3 ± 8.9 mm Hg, and mean pre-HT DBP was 69.0 ± 6.2 mm Hg (Figure 4A). Among adults with normal or elevated BP measurements preceding HT, the cumulative incidence of developing stage 1 or more hypertension by 6 months post-HT was 98.1%. GLS models revealed significant linear and nonlinear contributions of time from HT (Pchunk < 0.001) to increasing SBP and DBP (Supplemental Figures 8 and 9). Both SBP (linear β = 0.945; nonlinear β = −0.980; P < 0.001 for both) and DBP (linear β = 1.072; nonlinear β = −1.141; P < 0.001 for both) increased post-HT and were estimated to peak between 18 and 21 months; the model-estimated increases between baseline and 20 months post-HT were 11.1 mm Hg (P < 0.001) and 12.4 mm Hg (P < 0.001) for SBP and DBP, respectively.

TRENDS IN RENAL FUNCTION AFTER HT IN ADULTS.

After excluding individuals who required dialysis pre-HT or who underwent multiorgan transplantation, mean eGFR at the time of HT was 53.7 ± 25.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 and steadily decreased thereafter, reaching a nadir of 45.3 ± 26.5 mL/min/1.73 m2 at 27 months post-HT (Table 2). Among 583 adults with eGFR ≥45 mL/min/1.73 m2 pre-HT, the cumulative incidence of experiencing worsening eGFR to <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 within 6 months post-HT was 81.8%, translating to an IR of 186.8 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 170.8–202.8 per 100 person-years). Among 181 adults without CKD pre-HT, 51.7% and 63.8% developed CKD within 6 months and 1 year post-HT, respectively (Figure 4B, Supplemental Figure 10). This translated to an IR of a new CKD diagnosis of 69.7 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 58.2–81.3 per 100 person-years). GLS modeling showed significant linear and nonlinear time effects from HT (Pchunk < 0.001) to declining eGFR (Supplemental Figure 11). In 242 individuals who were prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors post-HT, there were significant linear and nonlinear time effects on eGFR during the ensuing 12 months (linear β = −0.781; P = 0.0384; nonlinear β = 1.014; P = 0.016) (Figure 4C).

TRENDS IN PEDIATRIC CARDIOMETABOLIC DISEASE AFTER HT.

In children, the IR of post-HT DM2 was 2.8 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 1.0–4.6 per 100 person-years) (Supplemental Figure 12). Only 14 children had HbA1c values measured prior to HT; among these, the mean HbA1c was 5.7% ± 0.5% (Table 3). The mean BMI z-score among 74 children who had BMI measured prior to HT was 0.41 ± 1.36 at time of HT, with 66.2% of children having normal weight, 14.9% having overweight, and 18.9% having obesity (Figure 5A). Of 49 children who were underweight or normal weight, the cumulative incidence of developing overweight or obesity (BMI z-score ≥1.04) by 6 months post-HT was 43% (Figure 5B); this translated to an IR of 26.9 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 16.1–37.7 per 100 person-years). The median post-HT survival free of overweight or obesity was 3.81 years. The IR of dyslipidemia was 5.5 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 2.7–8.2 per 100 person-years) (Figure 5C).

Mean LDL-C among children (n = 13) was 69.9 ± 60.4 mg/dL preceding HT, initially peaking at 79.5 ± 21.7 mg/dL at 3 months post-HT, followed by a downtrend and another peak of 80.0 ± 13.0 mg/dL at 27 months post-HT. Similar trends were seen with total cholesterol (113.0 ± 40.5 mg/dL pre-HT, 154.7 ± 31.5 mg/dL at 3 months post-HT, and 177.1 ± 33.9 mg/dL at 33 months post-HT) and triglycerides (144.6 ± 86.9 mg/dL pre-HT, 176.7 ± 109.8 mg/dL at 3 months post-HT, and 491.9 ± 294.4 mg/dL at 33 months post-HT). Of 23 children with LDL-C levels <100 mg/dL preceding HT, the cumulative incidence of worsening LDL-C to ≥100 mg/dL by 1 year post-HT was 9.5%, translating to an IR of 12.7 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 3.9–21.5 per 100 person-years) for worsening LDL-C control. Among 33 children with triglyceride levels <200 mg/dL before HT, the cumulative incidence of developing worsening triglycerides to ≥200 mg/dL by 1 year post-HT was 21.6%, translating to an IR for worsening hypertriglyceridemia of 13.1 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 6.0–20.2 per 100 person-years).

Only 2 children developed stage 1 or greater hypertension. In GLS models for BP, SBP (linear β = 0.413; P = 0.009; nonlinear β = −0.514; P = 0.003) and DBP (linear β = 0.271; P = 0.007; nonlinear β = −0.284; P = 0.016) significantly changed over time, estimated to peak at 15 to 21 months post-HT. The model-estimated increase between baseline and 20 months post-HT was 4.6 mm Hg (P = 0.005) and 3.4 mm Hg (P = 0.002) for SBP and DBP, respectively. In evaluation of renal function, the IR for CKD diagnosis was 3.6 per 100 person-years (95% CI: 1.5–5.8 per 100 person-years) (Figure 5D, Supplemental Figure 13). None of the pediatric individuals included in the study was prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP1RAs after HT.

ASSOCIATION OF CARDIOMETABOLIC-KIDNEY-IMMUNOLOGIC PHENOMENA WITH CLINICAL OUTCOMES.

Finally, we tested the association between key cardiometabolic-kidney-immunologic phenomena with CAV and mortality in 659 individuals with complete data, including coronary angiography (Figure 6). Of these, 196 (30%) developed CAV grade ≥1. Using DSAs, ACR, AMR, and CKM comorbidities as time-varying covariates, we identified donor age (HR: 2.05; 95% CI: 1.57–2.68), recipient female sex (HR: 0.57; 95% CI: 0.40–0.80), the presence of DSAs (HR: 2.04; 95% CI: 1.47–2.82), and ACR (HR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.14–2.11) as significantly associated with CAV development over a median time of 998 days. Of 659 individuals, 99 (15%) died over a median time of 1,296 days. Using presence of DSAs, CAV, and CKM comorbidities as time-varying covariates, we identified DM2 (HR: 1.84; 95% CI: 1.04–3.25), presence of DSAs (HR: 3.30; 95% CI: 2.19–4.98), and presence of CAV (HR: 1.67; 95% CI: 1.06–2.63) as significantly associated with mortality. Of 68 children who underwent coronary angiography, 7 (10%) had evidence of CAV. Similarly, only 5 (7%) of 68 children died over the duration of this study.

SENSITIVITY ANALYSES.

In sensitivity analyses excluding adults who died or were lost to follow-up within 30 days’ post-HT (n = 810 adults), the IRs of DM2, overweight or obesity, dyslipidemia, and CKD were 28.5, 75.6, 138.1, and 70.4 per 100 person-years, respectively. Similarly, when excluding adults who received simultaneous multiorgan transplants (n = 761 adults), the IRs of DM2, overweight or obesity, and dyslipidemia were 29.3, 74.8, and 129.6 per 100 case-years.

DISCUSSION

In a comprehensive analysis of CKM dysfunction at a high-volume transplant center, we found a high incidence and prevalence of post-HT DM2, hypertension, dyslipidemia, CKD, and overweight and obesity among both adults and children (Central Illustration). Importantly, these comorbidities developed early, within 1 year or less after HT, in a large proportion of individuals, with specific laboratory values over time estimated using GLS models. In a subset of individuals prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP1RAs post-HT, significant improvements were observed in renal function and BMI, respectively, suggesting an opportunity for early post-HT intervention in modifying the burden of CKM disease trajectory. Finally, we linked CKM dysfunction with important clinical outcomes, observing that post-HT DM2 is associated with increased mortality.

Transplant recipients are at increased risk for CKM disease due to a combination of diseases present pre-HT along with exposure to immunosuppressive agents after HT. For the majority of individuals at our center and elsewhere, maintenance immunosuppression consists of calcineurin inhibitors (eg, tacrolimus, cyclosporine), antiproliferative agents (eg, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine), and some duration of steroids, with some individuals receiving a mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor (eg, everolimus, sirolimus), usually in place of antiproliferative agents, to attenuate development or progression of graft vasculopathy.7,22 Although detailed mechanistic insight into immunosuppression-mediated metabolic dysregulation is outside the purview of the current study, each of these therapies has the potential to contribute to CKM dysfunction. Calcineurin inhibitors, for example, promote gut uptake of glucose via increased expression of SGLT1 and result in pancreatic β-cell dysfunction as well as nephrotoxicity that may lead to impaired glycemic control, hypertension, and worsening renal function.23–25 Steroid-induced hyperglycemia occurs through numerous mechanisms and is a major contributor to the development of post-HT DM28,9,22,26 as well as to weight gain and lipid dysregulation. Finally, mammalian target of rapamycin inhibition can result in β-cell failure, impaired insulin sensitivity, reduced glycogen synthesis, and increased 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase activity, all of which may contribute to DM2 and hypertriglyceridemia.24,27–29 Collectively, these unique exposures in the transplant population likely contribute significantly to the high incidence and prevalence of CKM dysfunction observed in the current study.

In addition to the impacts of immunosuppressive therapies, trends in some markers of cardiometabolic kidney disease may be explained by other post-HT factors. Model-estimated post-HT BMI trajectory based on GLS modeling, eg, was characterized by an initial decline in BMI that may reflect early loss of extravascular fluid as cardiac output normalizes or the loss of muscle mass during the days and weeks immediately after surgery when some individuals remain relatively inactive and may be acutely malnourished. Thereafter, BMI values steadily increased. In the case of post-HT dyslipidemia, the peak in LDL-C around 3 months post-HT may be explained in part by early statin initiation at most centers. Our findings that levels of HbA1c, triglyceride, and cholesterol are estimated to continue increasing with time after HT may have several potential etiologies. Although immunosuppressive agents may drive early incident or worsening of CKM disease, the development of CKM derangement may sustain or worsen disease further in a vicious cycle of feedback.3,4,30,31 Furthermore, as cardiac output normalizes after HT, individuals become accustomed to immunosuppressive regimens; in addition, reduction of target immunosuppressive troughs over time, which in turn may lead to reduced side effects, can result in the recrudescence of a sedentary lifestyle and poor nutrition that contribute to ongoing CKM dysfunction.32–34 These data support the notion that early intervention may attenuate this cycle and reduce cumulative CKM disease burden.

The improvements in renal function and BMI observed in a subset of individuals prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP1RAs have important implications for post-HT management that may mitigate the burden of CKM disease. In recent years, the advent of these drugs has made significant inroads toward addressing CKM disease in the nontransplant population (both adult and pediatric),35–43 and several smaller transplant studies have suggested a role for these drugs after transplantation.44–46 In the current study, among 242 adults prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors and 168 adults prescribed GLP1RAs, we observed significant improvements in eGFR and reductions in BMI, respectively. The post-SGLT2 inhibitors eGFR trajectory in this study exhibited the characteristic curve observed in other studies, with an initial reduction in eGFR until ~5 months post-initiation, followed by a steady increase in eGFR thereafter.35,36,38 This initial “dip” in eGFR, followed by eGFR stabilization, is thought to be secondary to the effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on tubuloglomerular feedback. Although we did not explore the effect of GLP1RAs on other CKM endpoints, weight loss has been associated in large-scale studies with significant improvements in cardiovascular disease, kidney dysfunction, and psychiatric illness.47 Although ideal timing of initiation of these therapies post-HT remains unclear, the significant burden of CKM dysfunction observed within 1 year post-HT in our study supports early intervention.

Interestingly, the current findings depart from historical reports that linked CKM dysfunction to increased CAV risk.1,9,12,48 This discrepancy may be explained by 2 phenomena: 1) we treated CKM dysfunction as time-varying rather than using static timepoints (eg, the presence of DM2 or dyslipidemia at 1 year post-HT), thereby crediting “at-risk” time only after the specific CKM dysfunction develops; and 2) in the contemporary era, aggressive risk modification of CKM comorbidities as they develop (practices informed by earlier studies) likely attenuates cumulative contribution to endothelial injury. In addition, the median follow-up time of 998 days (~2.7 years) may be insufficient to fully capture the long-term risk and evolution of CAV, and some relevant risk factors may only emerge with extended follow-up. Furthermore, because we only included individuals who underwent CAV assessment with invasive angiography while use of noninvasive screening approaches rises, there may be individuals with CAV who were not captured in our analysis. Notably, the strongest risk factor associated with CAV development in our cohort was donor age, consistent with a large body of published reports.1,48 Development of DSAs and immunologic events also remained robustly associated with increased risk of CAV. Of the CKM comorbidities, we do note a significantly increased risk of death among individuals who develop DM2, similar to that of the nontransplant population, while the seemingly protective association for hypertension likely reflects treatment and survivor bias.

Although the overall burden of CKM disease in pediatric HT recipients was lower in our study, it remains higher than in the non–HT population.49–51 Similar to adults, it is likely that immunosuppressive regimens after solid-organ transplantation contribute to this phenomenon. In addition, the same nontransplant (“traditional”) risk factors contributing to the childhood obesity epidemic may be influential as well.49,50 These risks may be compounded by data suggesting that nearly two-thirds of pediatric HT recipients may not be receiving statin therapy.52,53 The dyslipidemia burden may be particularly relevant in light of increasing understanding that cumulative exposure to LDL-C and other lipids may affect future cardiovascular risk.54 Importantly, despite the overall lower incidence and prevalence of CKM burden in children compared with adults in the current study, prior work has shown that cardiometabolic disease increases the risk of graft vasculopathy and graft failure in pediatric HT recipients.51 As SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP1RAs, and other novel therapeutics are increasingly being studied in the pediatric population, early intervention in children who undergo HT may attenuate long-term consequences of CKM dysfunction.

STUDY LIMITATIONS.

First, not all 944 adults and children studied had complete CKM data due to: 1) a small portion of individuals who underwent transplantation elsewhere and transferred their post-HT care to VUMC later; and 2) laboratory protocols that evolved over time such that not all relevant CKM values were available at all timepoints of interest for all individuals. Our use of GLS models with AR(1) helps to mitigate these issues by extrapolating missing data using data from surrounding timepoints to construct laboratory trajectories over time. Second, because IRs were calculated based on the time post-HT at which individuals first met the outcome of interest (eg, eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2), we did not account for subsequent improvements in laboratory values that may have occurred thereafter. Third, as with all electronic health record–based studies, it is possible that miscoding of ICD diagnoses codes may have affected the accuracy of our results. To minimize this possibility, we prioritized study of captured laboratory values related to diagnoses (eg, HbA1c, lipid profiles, eGFR) rather than relying on ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes alone. Fourth, we prioritized post-HT cardiometabolic burden and did not explore pre-HT risk factors for developing post-HT CKM dysfunction. Future studies investigating these variables may aid in early risk stratification of individuals at high risk for developing post-HT CKM dysfunction. Finally, this study was limited to a single-center experience, which may not be generalizable to all transplant centers.

CONCLUSIONS

In a large population of adult and pediatric heart transplant recipients at a high-volume center, we observed significant CKM disease that developed early post-HT. Novel drug therapies may substantially mitigate CKM burden and improve post-transplant outcomes. Further work is needed to guide timing of initiation of these therapies and to explore their influences on longer term outcomes, including graft vasculopathy and mortality.
