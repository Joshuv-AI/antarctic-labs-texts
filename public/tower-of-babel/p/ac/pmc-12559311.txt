Development and validation of a prognostic nomogram for overall survival in individuals with malignant meningiomas: a population-based study
Authors: Hongliang Ge, Yajun Jing, Jinye Su, Qiu He, Dengliang Wang, Jiawei Cai, Zanyi Wu, Dezhi Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant meningiomas are a rare type of central nervous system tumor. Therefore, little is known about the best methods for treating malignant meningiomas and their prognostic factors. The aim of this study was to identify risk factors and develop a prognostic model for patients with malignant meningiomas. First, 2360 cases of malignant meningiomas from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database were randomly divided into the primary and validation cohorts. Next, multivariate Cox regression identified several independent predictors of survival in malignant meningioma patients. These included patient demographics (age, gender, race), tumor features (laterality, size, stage), treatment timing (months from diagnosis to treatment), treatment modality, and tumor history (counts of both in situ/malignant and benign/borderline tumors), as well as year of diagnosis. Third, a nomogram was used to represent the prediction model, which was built based on independent predictors and optimized using the Akaike information criterion. Finally, we evaluated the predictive performance using the concordance index and receiver operating characteristic curve and clinical value using decision curve analysis. Notably, the strong discrimination ability of the model was demonstrated by the concordance index of the nomogram and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, both of which were between 0.7 and 0.8. The calibration plots in both cohorts showed high agreement between actual observation and nomogram prediction, and decision curve analysis demonstrated the significant clinical value of the nomogram. In conclusion, the nomogram is a practical and useful tool for assessing prognosis and determining effective treatment approaches.Keywords: Malignant meningioma, Cox regression analysis, Nomogram, Risk factor, PrognosisSubject terms: CNS cancer, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Meningiomas, a group of neurological tumors that arise from arachnoid epithelial cells, are the most common primary intracranial tumors in the United States, with an annual age-adjusted incidence rate of 8.58 per 100,000 persons1,2. The results from reporting of epidemiological studies indicated that meningiomas account for 36.6% of all primary CNS tumors and 53.2% of primary CNS tumors that are not malignant in the United States2. It is noteworthy that their annual percentage is rising. According to a recent study, nonmalignant meningioma has an incidence rate (IR) of 7.86 per 100,000 people; a rate that has significantly increased from 2004 to 2010, with an annual percentage change (APC) of 3%. In addition, a report of atypical, World Health Organization (WHO) grade II meningioma revealed increasing incidence in 2004–2010, with a 3.6% APC. In fact, an estimated 29,320 new meningioma diagnoses are projected in the United States for 2018 alone3. Interestingly, meningioma is much more common in adult women compared to men, and it arises in the cerebral meninges in the majority of cases, and less commonly in the spinal meninges.

Meningioma is primarily a sporadic, slowly growing, benign tumor. However, specific histological subtypes exhibit more aggressive biological behavior, correlating with an increased risk of recurrence and a less favorable prognosis. In light of this, meningiomas are categorized by the World Health Organization into three grades: Grade I (benign), Grade II (atypical), and Grade III (anaplastic). Moreover, higher grades are typically linked to more aggressive behavior. However, the recent identification of novel molecular markers—such as TERT promoter mutations and CDKN2A/B deletions—is reshaping the histological landscape. Notably, the vast majority of meningiomas (> 80%, and likely closer to 90%) remain classified as WHO Grade I under the 2021 CNS tumor classification4. Lesions in grade I often exhibit pleomorphic characteristics and sporadic mitotic figures. Despite the fact that this category is generally considered benign, there is a significant amount of intraclass variability, with recurrence rates ranging from 7% to 20% and variable rates of advancement to higher grades5,6. Atypical meningiomas (WHO Grade II) comprise 5–15% of cases and include chordoid and clear-cell variants, which demonstrate aggressive courses and up to ~ 50% five-year recurrence7,8. In contrast, anaplastic (WHO Grade III) or malignant meningiomas are rare (1–3%)9 yet display overt anaplasia or ≥ 20 mitoses per 10 high-power fields, often including rhabdoid and papillary subtypes10. Consequently, Grade III meningiomas feature frequent local invasion, recurrence, and metastasis, with five-year recurrence rates of 50–80% and median survival under two years11.

According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines for 2021, radiation therapy and/or surgery should be used as treatments for symptomatic meningiomas12. For higher-grade or recurrent lesions, the most common therapeutic approach is maximally safe gross total resection (GTR) or GTR followed by radiation therapy13. Notably, there has always been a variable risk of postoperative recurrence for higher-grade or recurrent lesions, despite extensive research on surgery and radiotherapy for the treatment and management of meningiomas14. Additionally, it is challenging to totally prevent the development of higher-grade or recurrent lesions with either surgery alone or surgery combined with radiation therapy. More importantly, it is currently impossible to produce guidelines or define standardized protocols for the treatment of malignant meningioma because there are no prospective studies or randomized controlled trials15. Moreover, because meningiomas tend commonly to be benign in nature, hardly much study has been done on them. As a result, thus far it is yet unknown what variables, particularly those related to malignant meningiomas, affect a patient’s prognosis. In the present study, we aim to identify prognostic factors and create a predictive model for malignant meningioma to improve clinical decision-making by combining conventional features, tumor characteristics, and data on surgery and radiation using the National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database16.

Methods and materials

Data sources

The study cohort comprised two datasets:

①SEER Cohort (Primary and Internal Validation). Data Retrieval: We accessed the SEER 18 Regs Custom Data (with additional treatment fields, November 2022 submission) via SEER*Stat software (version 8.3.9). We executed a custom case listing session, filtering on ICD-O-3 histology codes 9530/3,9531/3, 9532/3, 9534/3, 9535/3, 9537/3, 9538/3, and 9539/3, and primary site codes for intracranial meningiomas (C71.0–C71.9)17. Variable Selection: Demographic (age at diagnosis, sex, race/ethnicity), clinical (tumor size, laterality, stage), treatment (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy), and outcome (vital status, survival time) variables were specified in the SEER*Stat session. All variables and their SEER definitions are listed in SEER Program Coding and Staging Manual 2023 (https://seer.cancer.gov/archive/manuals/2023/SPCSM_2023_MainDoc.pdf).

Missing Data Handling: Continuous variables (e.g., tumor size): Records with missing or implausible values (> 99th percentile) were excluded (n = 42). Categorical variables: Cases with “unknown” or “unspecified” entries (e.g., laterality = “blank”) were also excluded in this study. Quality Control: Two authors independently validated the extraction queries and cross-checked the resulting datasets for consistency. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

②External Validation Cohort (Fujian Medical University Hospital). Case Identification: We reviewed the electronic medical records of patients treated between January 2010 and December 2021 for intracranial meningioma. Cases were identified by ICD-O-3 histology codes matching those in the SEER cohort and confirmed by surgical pathology. Variable Harmonization: Patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment modalities, and outcomes were extracted using a standardized case report form that mirrored SEER variable definitions. Two trained data managers performed manual chart review; ambiguous entries triggered source-document verification.

Missing Data Handling: Sporadic missing continuous data and categorical variables with missing rates < 10% were excluded from comparative analyses. Matching to SEER Variables: We mapped each hospital-derived variable to its SEER counterpart, ensuring consistent coding (e.g., tumor size in millimeters, surgery coded by extent). The final external dataset contained the same variables as the primary SEER cohort.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Figure 1 shows the screening flowchart for the study design. Since there is a lack of comprehensive research on prognosis, we focus our work primarily on the rare malignant meningiomas. As a result, we defined precise inclusion and exclusion standards for case selection. Inclusion criteria included malignant meningiomas of classes 9530/3, 9531/3, 9532/3, 9534/3, 9535/3, 9537/3, 9538/3, and 9539/3. Second, patients diagnosed between 1975 and 2021 were required. Finally, the diagnosis was confirmed by biopsy or surgical-pathological histology. One of the exclusion criteria was an unproven diagnostic technique. Meningiomas with ICD-O-3/1 and /3 without a positive histological diagnosis were excluded; (2) abnormal tumor characteristics such as size or quantity; (3) logical discrepancies, such as dates of surgery before admission; and (4) patients with metastases, concurrent cancers, or patients who were not receiving treatment at the reporting facility at the time of diagnosis. Ultimately, 2360 people suffering from malignant meningioma were selected for this study. All cases were then randomly divided into primary and internal validation cohorts in a 7:3 ratio18. Data from the primary cohort were used to develop the prediction model and nomogram, while the validation cohort was used for internal and external validation of the model.

The patient enrollment flow chart in this study.

Variable selection

The following factors were included in this study: age, gender, marital status, income, distribution of urban and rural areas, year of diagnosis, laterality (including paired site, right primary origin, left primary origin, and paired location)., but no information on laterality), stage (including localized, regional and distant), type of surgical resection (including autopsy only, tumor destruction, local excision, excisional biopsy, intermediate total resection, resection in the spinal cord or nerve, gross resection, partial resection of the cerebral lobe, Lobectomy and surgery, NOS), therapy (including autopsy only, radiotherapy [RT]; chemotherapy [CT]; surgical treatment; RT and CT, sequence unknown; RT after of surgery; RT after surgery and CT, sequence unknown; and RT after non-primary surgery and CT, sequence unknown), systemic therapy (whether systemic treatment should be given), tumor size, duration, total number of in situ/malignant tumors Patients (TNM), patient’s total number of benign/borderline tumors (TNB), and type of reporting source (TRS).

Construction of the predictive model

First, potential risk factors for malignant meningiomas were identified using univariate Cox regression analysis with a p value < 0.1. All potential risk factors were then included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis, with factors with p values < 0.05 considered as independent risk factors. Third, we built a prediction model based on all independent risk factors and gradually selected the optimal prediction model according to the results of the Akaike information criterion19. Finally, the model was visualized using the nomogram.

Evaluation of model performance and validation of model

The accuracy of the model was confirmed in the present study using internal and external validation cohorts. First, time-dependent ROC and C-index were used to evaluate the accuracy of the models20,21. A value of greater than 0.7 in the C-index and AUC values typically indicate a reliable prediction. In addition, we also used the calibration curves to evaluate the discrepancy between the actual measurements and the model predictions22. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the clinical utility of the model23.

Statistical analysis

In this study, all continuous variables (age, years of diagnosis, tumor size) were converted into categorical variables using the optimal cutoff values provided by X-Tile software (Yale University, New Haven, CT, USA; version 3.6.1)24. The optimal cut-off value was defined as the most statistically significant split of survival distributions across different categorical age groups and tumor sizes25. These new categorical variables were then used as predictors in the survival analysis. Statistical analyses in the present study were performed using R (http://www.r-project.org/; version 4.2.3). All factors were included in the univariate Cox regression analysis, and the factors whose p-values were less than 0.1 in the univariate Cox regression analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. A p-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance for all other tests.

To test the robustness of our model, we performed sensitivity analyses by varying the categorizations of continuous variables (age and tumor size) to assess the impact on model performance. For example, in the tumor size variable, we tested alternative cut-off points for categorization (e.g., using quartiles of the distribution rather than X-Tile–derived cut-offs) and examined whether the results remained consistent across these different categorizations. Similarly, we tested the impact of including or excluding borderline cases based on tumor grade and histology, observing that the overall model performance and the hazard ratios for key predictors remained stable.

Results

Baseline characteristics of meningioma patients

In the present study, a total of 2360 patients with malignant meningioma were analyzed. Among them, 1652 patients were included in the primary cohort, 708 in the internal validation cohort, and 60 in the external validation cohort. The baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Overall, the ratio of men to women was approximately 1:1, with a mean age of 60 years. The majority of patients were non-Hispanic (n = 2069, 87.67%), with most identifying as white. Regarding tumor laterality, a substantial proportion had paired location features (n = 1246, 52.79%), while 67.84% (n = 1601) of patients presented with localized tumor extension. Approximately 80% of patients (n = 169) were diagnosed between 2001 and 2016. Additionally, statistical comparisons were performed to examine the baseline differences between the primary, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. No significant differences in the demographic characteristics (gender, race, age) were observed between the primary cohort and the internal validation cohort (all p > 0.05). Univariate Cox regression analysis

We initially performed Univariate Cox regression analysis to assess the variables affecting overall survival in patients with malignant meningioma. The variables tested included age, gender, race, tumor stage (localized, regional, and distant), resection approaches (e.g., autopsy only, tumor destruction, local excision, excisional biopsy, subtotal resection, spinal cord or nerve resection, macroscopic resection, partial resection of the cerebral lobe, lobectomy, and NOS surgery), treatment approaches (e.g., autopsy only, surgery only, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, surgery combined with radiotherapy, radiotherapy combined with chemotherapy, and surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy), systemic treatment administration, time from diagnosis to treatment, tumor size, total number of in situ/malignant tumors (TNM), total number of benign/borderline tumors (TNB), type reporting source (TRS), and marital status.

The results of the univariate Cox regression analysis are summarized in Table 2. Among the variables tested, age (p < 0.001), sex (p < 0.001), race (p < 0.005), laterality (p < 0.05), tumor stage (p < 0.001), therapy (p < 0.005), whether receiving systemic treatment (p < 0.001), Months from diagnosis to treatment (p < 0.001), tumor size (p < 0.001), TNM (p < 0.005), TBN (p < 0.005), TRS (p < 0.005), marital status (p < 0.001), and surgical resection approach (p < 0.001) were found to be significantly associated with overall survival. Years of diagnosis, tumor site, average income, and other variables did not show statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). Notably, variables that did not show significance in the univariate analysis were partially included in the multivariate analysis, as they did not meet the statistical significance threshold of p < 0.05. These results highlight the importance of age, tumor stage, and resection approach in predicting survival outcomes for malignant meningioma patients.

Multivariate Cox regression analysis and survival analysis

Multivariate Cox regression (Fig. 2) identified significant predictors of malignant meningioma survival, with hazard ratios (HR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values reported for all significant effects. Key findings include race: white patients exhibited significantly lower mortality versus Black patients (HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.52–0.89, p = 0.0042; 32% reduction), while other races showed minimal reduction (HR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, p = 0.0003). Stage: localized tumors conferred 50% lower mortality than distant metastases (HR = 0.50,95%CI: 0.32–0.80, p = 0.0041), with regional tumors showing 26% lower mortality (HR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90, p = 0.002).

Forest plot for the results of the multivariate analysis of different factors (HR, 95% confidence interval). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Laterality: Unilateral lesions (Only one side: HR = 0.47,95%CI: 0.29–0.75, p = 0.0016; 53% reduction) and non-paired sites (HR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.31–0.78, p = 0.0027; 51% reduction) demonstrated significantly lower mortality versus bilateral lesions. Treatments: Radiotherapy (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.49–0.74, p < 0.0001;40% reduction) and chemotherapy (HR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.43–0.89, p = 0.0096; 38% reduction) reduced mortality, whereas Surgery + Radiotherapy paradoxically increased mortality by 324% (HR = 4.24, 95% CI: 1.71–10.51, p = 0.0018). Tumor Burden: Each unit increase in primary TNB increased mortality by 2% (HR = 1.02,95% CI: 1.01–1.03, p < 0.0001), with a secondary TNB measure showing 11% increased mortality per unit (HR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.01–1.23, p = 0.0388). Additional predictors included age (HR = 1.05/year, p < 0.0001), tumor size (HR = 0.84/mm, p < 0.0001), and care home treatment settings (HR = 3.45, p = 0.0001). Non-significant variables (e.g., income p = 0.0705) were excluded by statistical convention. Finally, using survival analysis as presented by Kaplan-Meier curves (Fig. 3), we evaluated the impact of each dependent prognostic variable on patient survival.

Establishment of prognostic nomograms

A prediction model was constructed based on the primary cohort and optimized by the Akaike Information Criterion. The model was finally visualized by a nomogram. As shown in Fig. 4, each level of each variable in the nomogram can be assigned a score on the point scale. Therefore, the ratings of different factors or their levels can be added together to get an overall score. Finally, based on the patient’s overall nomogram score, cancer-specific survival at 36, 60, and 120 months could be calculated and predicted for each individual patient.

Nomogram predicting cancer-specific survival for patients with malignant meningiomas at 36, 60, and 120 months. Prognostic variables may include tumor size, TNM, TNB, Type reporting source, income, age, gender, year of diagnosis, race, laterality, stage, therapy, months from diagnosis to treatment, and the scores provided on the points scale may match each level of every variable on the nomogram. As a result, the scores from the different factors or their levels were added to provide the final score. Ultimately, the overall score might be used to forecast the 36-, 60-, and 120-month cancer-specific survival for each individual patient. **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, *p < 0.05.

The scoring system quantifies predictor contributions as follows: age (10 points per decade), tumor size (40 points per 50 mm increment, plateauing at > 400 mm). Distant stage: 90 points vs. localized: 30 points. Radiotherapy: -30 points vs. combination therapy: +70 points. Care home setting: 60 points vs. hospital: 20 points. Total points are calculated by summing individual scores (range: 0-300 points), with thresholds defining three risk strata: low risk (0-100 points): 95% 3-year survival (95% CI: 92–97%). Intermediate risk (101–200 points): 70% 3-year survival (65–75%). High risk (201–300 points): 30% 3-year survival (25–35%). Notably, the proportions of sex and stage in the nomogram were lowest, whereas the proportions of tumor size and age at diagnosis were the highest. These results showed that the prognosis for individuals with malignant meningiomas continues to be influenced primarily by the size of the tumor and the age of the patient, with stage and gender having little influence on the prognosis. Finally, key nonlinear relationships require attention: Tumor size exhibits diminishing returns (> 400 mm contributes only 10 points per 50 mm). Combination therapies show non-additive effects (Surgery + Radiotherapy = 70 points vs. sum of monotherapies: 50). Recent diagnosis year (post-2020) confers 15 fewer points than pre-2000 diagnoses. The model demonstrates strong discrimination (c-index = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.78–0.84) with calibration slopes of 0.98–1.02 across strata. Bootstrap validation (1,000 samples) showed minimal optimism (Δc-index=-0.03).

Discrimination power of the nomogram model

Calibration curves are an important metric for validating the accuracy of predictive models. Calibration curves could measure how well, on average, a model’s predicted probabilities match the actual observed results. The calibration curve visualizes model performance across subsets, complementing overall accuracy. Therefore, well-predicted models exhibit minimal deviation from the 45° perfect fit line, whereas poorly predicted models manifest themselves in a growing divergence of expected from observed probabilities. In this study, calibration curves in Fig. 5 quantitatively assess the nomogram’s accuracy across predicted risk deciles using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and Brier score decomposition. The non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow χ²=4.6 (p = 0.80) confirms adequate fit. Internal validation (5B): Consistent calibration slope β = 0.97 (95% CI: 0.92–1.02) with moderate dispersion (Eₘₐₓ=0.08 in 70–90% risk range). Accuracy of the nomogram model

Accurate predictions effectively distinguish between people with and without the desired outcome. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve has emerged as the predominant metric for quantifying the discriminative ability of predictive models. Notably, several accuracy indices, including area under the curve (AUC), partial area, and TPF/FPF, have been proposed in the literature to precisely summarize salient features of ROC curves26–28, in which AUC is used as a one-dimensional summary metric, which indicates the overall position of the entire ROC curve. In the present study, Time-dependent ROC analysis (Fig. 6) demonstrated robust discrimination for the nomogram across all cohorts and timepoints, with AUC values consistently exceeding the 0.70 clinical utility threshold. In the primary cohort, 3-year AUC reached 0.82 (95% CI: 0.78–0.86), declining modestly to 0.77 (0.72–0.81) at 10 years. Internal validation showed comparable accuracy (3-year AUC: 0.80 [0.76–0.84]), while external validation maintained clinical relevance (3-year AUC: 0.78 [0.73–0.82]; 10-year: 0.70 [0.65–0.75]). Concordance indices further confirmed global discrimination: 0.81 (0.78–0.84) for the primary cohort, 0.78 (0.74–0.82) for internal validation, and 0.75 (0.71–0.79) for external validation. The narrow confidence intervals (< 5% width), minimal temporal decay (ΔAUC < 0.07 between 3 and 10 years), and inter-cohort consistency (ΔAUC < 0.04 at matched timepoints) collectively validate the model’s precision and generalizability.

Clinical usefulness of the nomogram model

Developed by Vickers and Elkin in a 2006 article in Medical Decision Making1, decision curve analysis is an approach to evaluating predictive models and diagnostic tests. The aim of the technique was to overcome the disadvantages of both comprehensive decision analysis techniques and traditional statistical measurements, such as calibration and discrimination, which do not directly indicate therapeutic benefit. Consequently, decision curve analysis was used to determine the practicality of this nomogram model. As shown in Fig. 8, the black dashed line represents the net benefit if all patients had died, and the pink dashed line represents the net benefit if no patient had died. If the red line representing the model lies between the black and pink lines, it is a sign of the clinical utility of the model. A higher net benefit or a greater distance of the model curve from the black and pink lines indicates better clinical utility of the nomogram. Notably, almost all threshold probabilities showed a significant net gain in the DCA curve analysis prediction model at 36, 60, and 120 months (Fig. 7 A for the primary cohort and Fig. 7B for the internal validation cohort), suggesting the potential clinical utility of the nomogram model. Importantly, the result of the DCA curve analysis for the external validation cohort showed a significant net improvement in the prediction model for almost all threshold probabilities at 36 and 60 months (Fig. 7C), indicating the potential of the model for clinical application.

Discussion

Meningiomas are tumors originating from arachnoidal cap cells. Of them, most types grow slowly, but some, particularly malignant meningiomas, behave more aggressively29. Notably, atypical meningiomas (WHO grade II) and malignant meningiomas (WHO grade III) generally showed more cellular abnormalities and grew more quickly than lesions in grade I. They also have an increased probability of infiltration into the brain and recurrence30. However, as most meningiomas are benign, malignant ones are less common. Large sample research on malignant meningiomas is therefore exceedingly uncommon. In addition, the lack of high-quality research on malignant meningiomas has resulted in a lack of knowledge regarding prognostic factors and the optimal treatment approach. For example, a retrospective cohort of 301 adult Grade III meningioma patients was reported by Jia et al.31, and Kshettry et al.32 described 552 anaplastic meningioma cases in a SEER-based epidemiological analysis. In contrast, our study leverages 2,360 adult malignant meningioma cases drawn from the SEER database (1975–2021), representing over a four‐fold increase in sample size compared to the next largest series. This large-scale cohort affords unprecedented statistical power to identify and validate prognostic factors, enabling the construction of the most comprehensive nomogram for malignant meningioma survival prediction reported to date.

It is well established that cancer incidence rises with age, reflecting its nature as an age-related disease33. Moreover, age stands out as one of the most extensively studied risk factors across cancer epidemiology34. Consistent with these observations, our analysis identified advanced age as a significant prognostic factor in malignant meningioma, with each decade of life increasing mortality risk (p < 0.05), in line with Prasad et al.35. Furthermore, gender is another significant factor influencing the prognosis of tumor patients. Gender orientation, for instance, has also been connected to the occurrence of gliomas and other central malignancies31,36. Interestingly, our findings indicated that female patients with WHO Grade III meningiomas exhibited substantially better survival than males. However, this female survival advantage is not universal; in contrast, an Asian cohort reported equivalent outcomes between sexes37. Therefore, understanding the hormonal and immunological underpinnings of these discrepancies is imperative38. Indeed, distinct hormone levels and immune profiles—such as androgen-driven CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion noted in bladder cancer and gliomas—may modulate meningioma behavior39–41. Consequently, future research should investigate the tumor immune microenvironment in malignant meningiomas to clarify these sex‐specific effects.

Of note, tumor size emerged as a significant prognostic factor in our cohort (p < 0.05). However, the effect of tumor size on survival remains controversial in the literature. For instance, several studies report that smaller tumors correlate with poorer outcomes42,43. In contrast, Dittmar et al.44 identified large tumor size as a high-risk indicator of mortality. Similarly, Tirada et al.45,46 demonstrated that increasing tumor diameter reliably predicts worse prognosis. Importantly, our findings align with the latter, showing that each centimeter increase in tumor size significantly raises the risk of death. Thus, tumor size should be considered a crucial component in prognostic modeling and treatment decision‐making for malignant meningioma patients.

Another emphasis of our investigation was the treatment regimens of meningiomas as well as how they affect survival. Of note, our results reinforce the principle that maximal safe resection remains the cornerstone of meningioma management16,37,47. Indeed, both multivariate and survival analyses demonstrated that gross total resection correlates with significantly improved overall survival, a finding echoed in National Cancer Database studies48,49. However, the impact of extensive resection on recurrence remains debated, as the potential neurological risks must be weighed against oncologic benefit50,51. Regarding adjuvant therapies, in contrast to chemotherapy, postoperative radiotherapy consistently shows a survival advantage in malignant meningiomas—a pattern also observed in medulloblastoma and extracranial chondrosarcoma cohorts52,53. Nevertheless, reports in atypical and malignant meningiomas are mixed: some series note minimal benefit from adjuvant radiotherapy54,55, whereas others report improved outcomes regardless of resection extent, especially in high-grade or mesenchymal subtypes56. Furthermore, evidence suggests that higher radiation does further enhance local control and survival, emphasizing the need for dose optimization57,58. Therefore, although our findings favor radiotherapy, more prospective studies are required before firm clinical recommendations can be made.

Moreover, racial disparities in meningioma outcomes have been well documented, with Black individuals consistently exhibiting poorer survival compared to other groups59,60. In keeping with these reports, our analysis demonstrated significantly worse outcomes for Black patients relative to White and ‘Other’ racial categories. However, we also observed that Hispanic patients had a lower mortality rate than non-Hispanic patients, mirroring findings from earlier studies61,62. Furthermore, our data indicated that cases reported through hospital registries had substantially better survival than those captured via other reporting sources. We speculate that this difference may reflect variability in treatment access and quality across reporting entities. Therefore, understanding and addressing these disparities—both racial and source-based—remains critical for improving malignant meningioma care.

According to guidelines published by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) in 2011, hydroxyurea, interferon-a [IFNa] 2B, and sandostatin long-acting release might be the only three drugs used to treat meningiomas medically63. However, these recommendations are derived from small, nonrandomized studies with significant limitations. Moreover, despite over three decades of research, no systemic agent has demonstrated robust efficacy in large clinical trials. In line with this, our data show that chemotherapy alone does not improve overall survival (p = 0.78), mirroring the results reported by Mazza et al.64. Furthermore, novel approaches such as ¹⁷⁷Lu-DOTATATE (LUTATHERA) are under investigation for somatostatin receptor–positive tumors, offering targeted radiation delivery65. In contrast, immunotherapy has garnered recent attention: higher PD-L1 expression correlates with worse prognosis in meningiomas, thereby justifying seven ongoing clinical trials of anti–PD-1/PD-L1 agents. Therefore, while conventional chemotherapy remains ineffective, these emerging targeted and immunotherapeutic strategies merit rigorous evaluation in future trials.

Limitations and advantages of the present study

Of note, compared to some previous case studies and small-sample analyses, our study provided more comprehensive details on prognostic factors. Additionally, a large reduction in intrinsic biases and heterogeneity was achieved along with a significant boost in statistical power. Most importantly, the risk factors included in the model are relatively easier to collect than risk variables found via costly and time-consuming processes. To sum up, our most recent population-based study provides important new information about the survival and epidemiology of individuals with meningiomas that are not benign. We created and tested prediction models for malignant meningioma overall survival, and these models have been validated multiple times. Despite its advantages, this analysis has many drawbacks. Initially, there were some crucial details on the patient’s prognosis that were unavailable, such as the radiation dosage, the chemotherapy regimen, and the resection method66. In addition, as molecular biology advances, the significance of molecular pathology is increasingly recognized, necessitating extensive data integration for the execution of trustworthy research projects47. Third, differences in reporting methods across research institutions, potential underreporting of asymptomatic cases in registry databases, and resulting selection bias are also challenging66. Lastly, the use of a single database also posed limitations to our investigation.

In conclusion, the present study identified a number of risk variables (such as age, gender, year of diagnosis, race, laterality, treatment, stage, and tumor size) that are associated with the prognosis of malignant meningiomas. Moreover, a nomogram that provided a precise and objective prediction of the prognosis for patients with malignant meningiomas was developed and validated using large-sample data from the SEER database. Importantly, our modeling aligns with previous research findings, supporting clinical decision-making by indicating treatments that confer greater patient benefits. It encourages clinicians to approach unfavorable risk factors with caution and identifies new research questions for further exploration.
