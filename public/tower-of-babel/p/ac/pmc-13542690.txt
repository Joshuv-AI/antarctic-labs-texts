Surgical Ablation of Atrial Fibrillation Using the Left Atrial Roof Approach
Authors: Joon Young Kim, Joon Bum Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMinimally invasive surgical ablation has demonstrated favorable outcomes when performed during mitral and tricuspid valve procedures; however, its application in proximal aortic and aortic valve procedures remains challenging. We present the left atrial (LA) roof approach via upper mini-sternotomy as an alternative technique.MethodsThe LA was exposed by transecting or laterally retracting the ascending aorta, followed by a 3–4 cm transverse incision near the ostia of the upper pulmonary veins (PVs). A left semicircular cryoablation lesion was created from the left margin of the LA roof incision and extended posterolaterally to encircle the left PVs. A right arc-shaped lesion was created from the right margin, extended posterolaterally to encircle the right PVs, and terminated at the posterior mitral annulus adjacent to the P3 scallop. Together, these lesions constituted a complete box lesion for PV isolation in conjunction with mitral isthmus cryoablation.ResultsFrom June 2018 to February 2023, 23 patients underwent surgical ablation using the LA roof approach. Aortic valve replacement, performed alone or combined with other procedures, was the most common procedure (60.9%), followed by arch repair (30.4%) and root replacement (26.1%). The median cross-clamp and bypass times were 92 and 117 minutes, respectively. No early deaths, cases requiring dialysis, or wound infections occurred. Three patients (13.0%) required permanent pacemaker implantation. Freedom from atrial fibrillation recurrence was 91.3% at 1 year and 82.6% at 2 years.ConclusionIn this preliminary experience, the LA roof approach via upper mini-sternotomy yielded favorable early postoperative and mid-term rhythm outcomes.

Introduction

Minimally invasive cardiac surgery has evolved substantially and is associated with reduced surgical trauma, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery [1,2]. Among these advances, minimally invasive surgical ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) has shown excellent outcomes, particularly when performed in conjunction with mitral or tricuspid valve procedures. However, its application in cases requiring concomitant proximal aortic or aortic valve procedures remains technically challenging [3].

In mitral and tricuspid valve surgery, right anterolateral mini-thoracotomy is commonly used for concomitant surgical ablation because it provides direct access to the left atrium through Waterston’s groove, the standard landmark for left atrial (LA) procedures [4]. However, this approach does not provide adequate exposure of the aortic root or arch, limiting its utility in procedures involving these structures. By contrast, right anterior mini-thoracotomy is effective for isolated aortic valve replacement (AVR) but restricts access to Waterston’s groove, making concomitant surgical ablation more difficult [5].

To address these challenges, we implemented the LA roof approach through an upper mini-sternotomy as an alternative technique to facilitate surgical ablation during concomitant proximal aortic and aortic valve surgery. Here, we present our initial clinical experience with this integrated strategy.

Methods

Operative procedures

For all patients, either an upper partial J-shaped or L- shaped mini-sternotomy was performed through a 5- to 8-cm skin incision extending to the third or fourth intercostal space, depending on the extent of the planned aortic procedure. An L-shaped upper mini-sternotomy was used for total arch replacement, whereas a J-shaped upper mini-sternotomy was used for all other procedures.

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was initiated through femoral venous and distal ascending aortic cannulation for isolated aortic root or valve procedures. In cases involving aortic arch repair, arterial cannulation was performed at 1 of 3 sites—the ascending aorta, innominate artery, or femoral artery—depending on the extent of surgery, the underlying pathology, and any concomitant procedures, as detailed in previous reports [6,7].

The LA was exposed by transecting the ascending aorta in cases requiring aortic repair (Fig. 1A) or by applying lateral traction when transection was unnecessary (e.g., isolated AVR). A transverse incision approximately 3 to 4 cm in length was made at the most posterior aspect of the LA roof, near the superior margin of the ostia of the right and left upper pulmonary veins (PVs). Silk sutures (1-0) were placed at the anterior, posterior, right, and left margins of the LA roof incision and retracted axially to optimize exposure of the LA.

From the left margin of the LA roof incision, a left semicircular ablation line was extended posterolaterally to encircle the left superior and inferior PVs. It then curved anterolaterally along the ostium of the left atrial appendage (LAA) and terminated medially between the right and left inferior PVs, forming the left component of the box lesion for pulmonary vein isolation (Fig. 1B, blue line). From the right margin of the incision, a second arc-shaped lesion was extended posterolaterally to encircle the right superior and inferior PVs, then carried anterolaterally across the endocardial mitral isthmus to the posterior mitral annulus near the P3 scallop (Fig. 1B, green line). Together, these lesions created a complete box lesion for pulmonary vein isolation with concomitant mitral isthmus cryoablation. When the cryoprobe length was insufficient, the lesions were completed in a staged manner. This surgical ablation technique is demonstrated in detail in Supplementary Video 1.

Cryoablation was performed using nitrous oxide–based (AtriCure) or argon-based (Medtronic ATS) probes. Each lesion was ablated for 120 seconds to ensure transmurality.

The decision to preserve or exclude the LAA was based on patient-specific and procedural considerations. Exclusion was preferred in patients with high-risk features for thromboembolism, such as an enlarged left atrium, fine AF, or advanced age [8-10]. When exclusion was performed, it was achieved either by internal obliteration (endocardial closure) or by external resection (epicardial detachment) using a linear stapler. After completion of the ablation, the LA roof incision was closed with a double-layer running suture.

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Asan Medical Center (IRB no., 2025-0090; approved January 16, 2025). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients and clinical outcomes

This retrospective review included consecutive patients who underwent AF ablation using the LA roof approach during concomitant aortic or aortic valve surgery via upper mini-sternotomy at Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Korea, between June 2018 and February 2023. Baseline demographics, clinical profiles, laboratory parameters, imaging findings (e.g., transthoracic echocardiography and brain magnetic resonance imaging), and 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) were obtained from the electronic medical records.

In patients with stable sinus rhythm, postoperative amiodarone and anticoagulation were not routinely used unless otherwise indicated. The decision regarding continued anticoagulation thereafter was individualized according to each patient’s estimated thromboembolic risk and the restoration of sinus rhythm with effective atrial contraction.

AF recurrence was defined as any documented atrial tachyarrhythmia (AF, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia) lasting longer than 30 seconds after a 3-month blanking period [11]. Postoperative monitoring was performed primarily during outpatient visits using snapshot ECGs. In patients without AF on snapshot ECGs, 24-hour Holter monitoring was performed to confirm the absence of arrhythmia.

Early outcomes of interest were defined as events occurring within 30 days after surgery or during the index hospitalization. These outcomes included early mortality, neurological events, bleeding requiring exploration, new-onset dialysis, sternal wound infection, and permanent pacemaker (PPM) implantation. Neurological events were defined as newly developed neurologic deficits confirmed by ischemic or hemorrhagic findings on neuroimaging. Disabling neurological events were defined as events that did not resolve during hospitalization and resulted in residual deficits; these events were confirmed by imaging and evaluated by neurology specialists in all suspected cases [12]. Late outcomes of interest included late mortality, thromboembolic events, AF recurrence, PPM implantation, and any valve- or aorta-related reoperations.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to estimate freedom from AF recurrence during follow-up. All statistical analyses were performed using R ver. 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 23 patients. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median EuroSCORE II was 3.1% (IQR, 1.8%–5.1%). Most patients (78.3%; n=18) had long-standing persistent AF, and 1 patient (4.3%) had previously undergone catheter ablation. Preoperative echocardiography showed mild LA enlargement in most patients, with a median LA diameter of 48.0 mm (IQR, 43.0–51.5 mm).

Operative characteristics and clinical outcomes

AVR was the most common operation, performed either alone or in combination with other procedures in 60.9% of cases (n=14) (Table 2). Aortic arch repair was performed in 30.4% of cases (n=7), and aortic root replacement in 26.1% (n=6). Isolated arch repair was performed in 2 patients (8.7%). Isolated MVR was performed in 1 patient with a history of tuberculosis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, in whom severe adhesions were anticipated. In this case, upper mini-sternotomy was considered the most appropriate approach to facilitate both minimally invasive surgical ablation and MVR.

The median aortic cross-clamp (ACC) and CPB times were 92 minutes (IQR, 75–120 minutes) and 117 minutes (IQR, 90–135 minutes), respectively. For isolated AVR, the median ACC and CPB times were 63 minutes (IQR, 57–72 minutes) and 99 minutes (IQR, 79–111 minutes), respectively. For isolated total arch replacement, the median ACC and CPB times were 80 and 121 minutes, respectively. In patients undergoing concomitant arch repair, the median total circulatory arrest time was 4.7 minutes (IQR, 4.4–6.5 minutes).

No cases of early mortality, new-onset dialysis, or sternal wound infection were observed. Ischemic stroke occurred in 1 patient (4.3%) who underwent combined ascending aortic and total arch replacement in the setting of severe aortic atherosclerosis. PPM implantation was required in 3 patients (13.0%): 2 with sick sinus syndrome and 1 with tachy-brady syndrome. In the 2 patients with sick sinus syndrome, preoperative ECG showed AF with a slow ventricular response, suggesting underlying intrinsic conduction system dysfunction.

Follow-up and late outcomes

All patients completed follow-up, with a median follow- up duration of 3.2 years (IQR, 2.6–4.8 years). During follow-up, there were no late deaths, thromboembolic events, newly implanted permanent pacemakers, or valve- or aorta-related reoperations.

After the 3-month blanking period, late AF recurrence was observed in 4 patients during outpatient follow-up. Of these, 3 developed atrial flutter and 1 developed AF. Freedom from AF recurrence was 91.3% at 1 year and 82.6% at 2 years (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this study, the LA roof approach via upper mini-sternotomy appeared feasible for surgical ablation of AF during concomitant proximal aortic and aortic valve procedures. ACC and CPB times remained relatively short at 92 and 117 minutes, respectively, values comparable to those reported for isolated aortic procedures [13,14]. Rhythm outcomes were also favorable, with freedom from AF recurrence of 91.3% at 1 year and 82.6% at 2 years. These findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of feasibility rather than definitive comparative evidence.

The LA roof lesion set was designed not only to achieve pulmonary vein isolation with concomitant mitral isthmus cryoablation, but also to address the LAA ostium while minimizing trauma to the atrial myocardium. To limit the inclusion of LA roof tissue within the box lesion, the transverse incision was placed at the most posterior aspect of the LA roof near the upper PV ostia. By preserving the LA roof myocardium, this approach may help maintain atrial booster function after restoration of sinus rhythm [10,15, 16]. These technical features were associated with acceptable ACC and CPB times even in complex combined procedures, such as total arch replacement.

Our cohort had no cases of early mortality, and postoperative stroke occurred in only 1 patient with significant aortic atherosclerosis. PPM implantation was required in 13.0% of patients, which may appear relatively high; however, this was primarily attributable to preexisting sinus node dysfunction identified before the procedure. Importantly, no PPMs were implanted because of atrioventricular block related to the ablation lesions, suggesting that the lesion set preserved conduction integrity.

Previous studies from our institution suggest that LA- only ablation can provide durable rhythm control in selected patients undergoing concomitant cardiac surgery, particularly when right atrial involvement is limited [17]. These studies also showed favorable long-term maintenance of sinus rhythm with low rates of PPM implantation [18,19]. Based on that experience, we adopted a simplified LA roof lesion set via upper mini-sternotomy for patients undergoing proximal aortic or aortic valve surgery, in whom ACC and CPB times are often prolonged. This streamlined approach avoids additional right atrial lesions and their associated conduction risks while still achieving rhythm outcomes comparable to those reported for more extensive biatrial lesion sets. However, unlike previously reported LA-only ablation strategies that included an additional epicardial coronary sinus lesion, the present LA roof approach was designed to create a complete box lesion for PV isolation together with endocardial mitral isthmus cryoablation through direct LA roof exposure. Therefore, the lesion set used in this study should not be considered identical to those used in prior reports.

LAA preservation was performed in 87.0% of patients undergoing surgical ablation for AF. This strategy is supported by previous studies from our institution showing that LAA preservation is associated with better maintenance of LA mechanical function than resection, without compromising rhythm control or clinical outcomes [10,15, 16]. These findings support our practice of preserving the LAA when clinically appropriate based on an individualized assessment of thromboembolic risk and hemodynamic considerations.

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective and conducted at a single center by a single surgeon in a small cohort; accordingly, the findings should be regarded as preliminary evidence of feasibility rather than definitive comparative evidence. In addition, the absence of a comparator group precluded direct comparison with conventional surgical strategies. Because this experience was derived from a high-volume tertiary referral center specializing in minimally invasive surgery for both valvular and aortic disease, the results may not be generalizable to other clinical settings.

In this preliminary single-center, single-surgeon experience, surgical ablation of AF using the LA roof approach via upper mini-sternotomy may represent a viable alternative for patients undergoing concomitant proximal aortic and aortic valve surgery. This technique provides direct access to the LA and facilitates creation of a durable lesion set while preserving the advantages of minimally invasive surgery. Given the favorable clinical and rhythm outcomes observed, this strategy may be a useful option in selected patients.
