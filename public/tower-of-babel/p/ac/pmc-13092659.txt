Laryngeal leishmaniasis presenting with hoarseness in a child from the highland of Nepal: A rare diagnostic challenge
Authors: Shankar Kafle, Bibek K.C., Fathimath Zeena Sobir, Arun K. Sharma, Narayan Raj Bhattarai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leishmaniasis, predominantly a disease endemic to rural lowlands in Nepal, is transmitted by sand flies and is extremely unusual at highland elevations. Respiratory mucosal involvement in leishmaniasis is rare, causing a diagnostic dilemma. We report a 6-year-old child from a hilly region of Nepal (2500 m above mean sea level), presenting with hoarseness of voice for a two-month duration, and ultimately diagnosed as isolated laryngeal leishmaniasis. This presentation highlights the diagnostic challenge in the context of evolving disease ecology, posed by the diverse clinical spectrum of leishmaniasis in the pediatric population and the importance of this differential in any unexplained granulomatous inflammation.

Background

Leishmaniasis is a vector-borne protozoan parasitic infection caused by Leishmania, transmitted by the bite of infected female phlebotomine sand flies. Since the first recorded case of leishmaniasis in Nepal in the 1960s, endemic rural disease has been prevalent in the lowland areas of Nepal [1]. However, recent studies have suggested that there is also local transmission of L. donovani in a few endemic doubtful districts in western Nepal at altitudes up to 1700 m above mean sea level (asl) [2]. Leishmaniasis seems to be a climate-sensitive disease, and rising temperatures can contribute to the transmission. Although the effects of climate change have been studied in our neighboring countries, such as China and Bangladesh, there is still a need for Nepal to investigate further the impact of climate change on Leishmaniasis distribution in Nepal [3]. We hereby present a child from the Kalikot district, a hilly region of Nepal, standing at an altitude of 2500 m. who presented with isolated hoarseness of voice and was diagnosed with isolated laryngeal leishmaniasis. We aim to highlight the unusually varied clinical spectrum of leishmaniasis and advocate for a higher index of suspicion of this treatable disease, even in non-endemic regions, if one or other potential risk factors are identified.

Case presentation

A 6-year-old boy from a farming family, residing in Kalikot district, Nepal, with an average elevation of 2500 m above sea level (asl), presented to our university hospital with a two-month history of noisy breathing. The patient had been previously healthy and was growing well, with no travel history before the disease presentation. There was no history to suggest any immunodeficiency, and he had received all his childhood vaccines as part of routine immunizations without complications. No other systemic symptoms were identified.

The hoarseness of voice was an insidious onset that worsened gradually. Subsequently, he also developed noisy breathing, which was more prominent during inspiration as well as during exertion and sleep in the supine position. He did not describe fever or other systemic symptoms and had not lost weight or developed other constitutional symptoms.

At the time of presentation to our institution, he weighed 26 Kg and did not appear to be in respiratory distress. Stridor was audible, but the rest of the clinical ENT examination of the head, neck, ear, nose, and throat was remarkable. Lymphadenopathy was not identified. Systemic examination was noncontributory. A hemogram reveals marked peripheral eosinophilia (absolute eosinophil count: 5500/ µL). Other routine laboratory parameters, including liver and kidney function tests, were within normal limits. For evaluation of airway anatomy to identify the cause of stridor, the patient underwent a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the neck, which showed an inflamed epiglottis with narrowing of the airways. He was further evaluated with endoscopy, which revealed an inflamed epiglottis, aryepiglottic fold, and arytenoids with roughening. A biopsy was also taken by endoscopy and sent for histopathological evaluation. Diffuse granulomatous inflammation was noticed. Along with granulomatous inflammation, there were round to oval organisms, both intracellular and extracellular, morphologically consistent with Leishmania species (Fig. 1), and also highlighted by Giemsa stain. Ziehl Neelsen stain was negative for acid-fast bacilli, and no fungal organisms were revealed in the special stain, Periodic Acid Schiff. Nasolacrimal aspirate was already sent for the GeneXpert test, which was negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Chest X-ray also revealed no abnormalities. This prompted a measurement of serum IgE, which was markedly elevated (IgE: 2914 IU/ml), and the rK39 strip test, which was positive for IgG to Leishmania antigen. Further confirmation was with a Leishmania donovani species-specific PCR (SSU-rDNA) following the method described elsewhere, and Leishmania was identified [4]. Ultrasound of abdomen and pelvis was reviewed; no gross abnormalities were noted. However, allergic-like rashes were present, including the face and trunk regions, and biopsies were also taken, which revealed that the patient had atopic eczema and were negative for cutaneous leishmaniasis. The allergic lesions might have explained the rise of eosinophils. He was treated with a regimen outlined in the national guideline on treatment of visceral leishmaniasis with a single dose of Liposomal Amphotericin B as a first-line regimen [5]. Additional, oral and topical antihistamine medicines were also given for skin lesions. The patient improved and was discharged after a week with improvement of respiratory symptoms. Discussion

Leishmaniasis is mainly caused by L. donovani and has been reported mostly from the Indian subcontinent, including Nepal, where visceral as well as mucocutaneous leishmaniasis are common [6]. A typical patient presents with multiple signs and symptoms, such as fever for 2 or more weeks, moderate to severe splenomegaly, for which the patient complains of abdominal distension, weight loss, loss of appetite, hepatomegaly, early satiety, anemia, and epistaxis [5].

This case highlights an unusual presentation of leishmaniasis involving the upper airways without systemic manifestations. A study by L.Thakur et al. stated that atypical visceral leishmaniasis cases were classically associated with cutaneous diseases and were increasingly causing the viscerotropic phenotype [6]. Our case had no cutaneous leishmaniasis, which was similar to the case reported by Dari et al [7]. He described an isolated mucosal leishmaniasis presented with hoarseness of voice, a similar presentation to our case, but it was an adult of 31 years old male.

The mucosal form often causes even higher potential for severe mutilations. Mucosal involvement in leishmaniasis is most often secondary to cutaneous disease. Studies have demonstrated a higher frequency of this progression in disseminated cutaneous leishmaniasis and in cases with lesions above the pelvic girdle [8]. Though there are a few reported cases of isolated mucosal leishmaniasis, it is exceedingly rare, especially in immunocompetent individuals [9], [10].

Nasal cavities are the most frequently affected by mucosal leishmaniasis, followed by the pharynx, oral cavity, and larynx. A study of 326 cases of mucosal leishmaniasis demonstrated 98% of these involved the nasal cavity, while laryngeal involvement was seen in 1.5% [11].

Both cutaneous and mucocutaneous forms of leishmaniasis were reported to be predominant in adult males [12]. As such, leishmaniasis might get overlooked as a differential diagnosis in the pediatric population. Moreover, a significant population of children was found to be affected with slight differences in clinical presentation. In a study by Suprien et al., among 571 cases of cutaneous leishmaniasis, 129 (22.6%) were children. They also noted that children had more lesions in the head and ulcers were smaller in size, as compared to adults [13]. These diversities further add to the diagnostic challenges, particularly in cases with unusual presentations, which can also delay diagnosis. Poloni et al. reported a median diagnostic delay of 189 days for cutaneous leishmaniasis and an even higher time for mucocutaneous leishmaniasis, varying from 151 to 648 days [14]. Complications of undetected and untreated leishmaniasis can be devastating, ranging from severe disfigurement, consequent stigmatization, and psychological impact to a more immediate threat to life, such as airway obstruction. Hence, heightened clinical suspicion is warranted even in pediatric populations.

The Nepal government had set a goal for elimination as a public health problem by 2026, achieving an annual incidence of < 1 case/10,000 population at the district level [15]. However, it seems to be difficult to achieve the goal as new case reports from previously non-endemic eastern and western hilly regions of Nepal are a rising concern [2]. Acharya B.K.et al. and González C et al. highlighted that such a situation might be due to global warming, causing climate change to support a suitable habitat for vector and reservoir species for leishmaniasis [3], [16]. Our case also raises the possibility of expanding transmission zones and warrants further epidemiologic investigation.

Conclusions

This case of laryngeal leishmaniasis in a child highlighted diagnostic challenges posed by the diverse clinical spectrum of leishmaniasis. Clinicians in Nepal and similar regions should consider leishmaniasis in the differential diagnosis of unexplained granulomatous laryngitis, even in pediatric patients without systemic symptoms and from highland areas previously considered non-endemic.
