Vasospastic Angina Masquerading as Plaque Erosion on Optical Coherence Tomography
Authors: Masaki Fujiwara, Kosuke Tanimura, Ryo Matsutera, Kenji Nakajima, Hideyuki Takaoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries has diverse mechanisms, and reliance on intracoronary imaging alone may lead to misdiagnosis.Case SummaryA 70-year-old man presented with non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Coronary angiography showed no significant stenosis, whereas optical coherence tomography (OCT) demonstrated an intraluminal thrombus over a lipid-rich plaque with an intact fibrous cap, suggesting plaque erosion. After treatment including a β-blocker, he developed recurrent chest pain 2 months later. Repeat angiography was unchanged, but acetylcholine provocation induced multivessel coronary spasm. Follow-up OCT showed thrombus resolution with persistent layered plaque and intimal irregularities, supporting vasospasm-induced thrombosis. Symptoms resolved after switching from β-blocker therapy to vasodilators.DiscussionThis case highlights that coronary vasospasm can mimic plaque erosion on OCT. Functional testing and assessment of pharmacologic response are essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management of myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries.Key words: acetylcholine provocation, case report, myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries, optical coherence tomography, vasospastic angina

History of Presentation

A 70-year-old man with a history of smoking was admitted to the emergency department due to persistent chest pain on awakening. On arrival, his blood pressure was 130/80 mm Hg, heart rate was 82 beats/min, body temperature was 36.5 °C, and oxygen saturation was 98% on room air. Physical examination revealed regular heart sounds without murmurs or gallops.

Take-Home Messages

•Optical coherence tomography findings suggestive of plaque erosion can represent secondary thrombus formation triggered by coronary vasospasm and should not be interpreted in isolation.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with active smoking, and postoperative lung cancer. There was no history of hypertension, diabetes, or prior coronary artery disease.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis included acute coronary syndrome (plaque disruption or erosion), coronary vasospasm, takotsubo cardiomyopathy, and acute myocarditis.

Investigations

Laboratory tests showed elevated troponin T (0.281 ng/mL; normal: <0.014 ng/mL) with normal serum creatine kinase (CK) and creatine kinase-MB levels (107 IU/L [normal range: 45-163 U/L] and 20 IU/L [normal range: 0-24 U/L], respectively). Electrocardiogram demonstrated first-degree atrioventricular block without St-T segment changes (Figure 1), and chest radiography revealed no cardiomegaly or pulmonary congestion. Echocardiography showed normal wall motion with a preserved ejection fraction of 60% and no valvular disease or pulmonary hypertension. Suspecting non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, emergency coronary angiography (CAG) showed no significant stenosis in the right coronary artery (RCA) but mild stenosis with ulceration in the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) (Figures 2A and 2B, Video 1). Because coronary blood flow was preserved and no culprit lesion was evident, optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Dragonfly opstar; Abbott Vascular) was performed. This revealed a lipid-rich plaque with an overlying thrombus at the LAD stenotic site (identified as ulceration on CAG) and a layered plaque on the contralateral wall at the bifurcation with the diagonal branch (Figure 2C), findings considered suggestive of plaque erosion (PE).

Management (Medical/Interventions)

Although thrombus aspiration was attempted using an aspiration catheter (RebirthⅢ; NIPRO), no thrombus was retrieved. Postaspiration OCT findings were unchanged, and coronary blood flow remained intact; therefore, additional interventions such as balloon angioplasty or stent implantation were not performed (Figure 2D). Conservative management was initiated with dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg), lipid-lowering therapy (atorvastatin 20 mg and ezetimibe 10 mg), and the β-blocker (bisoprolol 0.625 mg) for presumed PE; however, vasospastic angina could not be completely excluded at that time. Acetylcholine provocation testing was deferred during the acute phase because of concerns about procedure-related ischemic complications. The patient was discharged without complications on day 4.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Two months later, the patient was readmitted with early morning chest pain. Laboratory tests showed normal troponin T, CK and creatine kinase-MB levels. Electrocardiogram revealed slight T-wave inversions in leads V1 to V3 (Figure 3). Repeat CAG showed no new lesions. Given the recurrent symptoms in the absence of obstructive coronary disease, acetylcholine provocation testing was performed. This induced severe stenosis accompanied by chest pain and electrocardiographic changes in the RCA with a 20-μg acetylcholine infusion and in the LAD artery with a 50-μg infusion (Figures 4A, 4B, 4D, and 4E). Administration of nitroglycerine (1 mg) resulted in rapid resolution of the stenoses. Follow-up OCT of LAD demonstrated resolution of the previously observed thrombus, with persistence of layered plaque and vasa vasorum (Figure 4F). OCT of the RCA revealed a layered plaque at the proximal bifurcation of the posterior descending and posterolateral branches and an intimal bump at the bifurcation site (Figure 4C). Accordingly, a final diagnosis of myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) due to vasospastic angina–induced thrombosis was established. The patient experienced no recurrence of chest pain after discontinuation of the β-blocker, initiation of nicorandil (5 mg 3 times daily) and isosorbide dinitrate (20 mg twice daily), and reinforcement of smoking cessation.

Discussion

This case describes a patient initially diagnosed with MINOCA due to PE based on CAG and OCT findings, illustrating a key diagnostic pitfall in MINOCA, namely the potential misinterpretation of OCT findings when functional coronary abnormalities are not concurrently assessed.1, 2, 3

Although OCT has substantially advanced the identification of PE by enabling high-resolution visualization of thrombus over an intact fibrous cap, these morphologic features are not pathognomonic and may represent secondary phenomena rather than the primary etiology.4 Coronary vasospasm can induce intense, transient vasoconstriction, resulting in endothelial injury, platelet activation, and subsequent thrombus formation. Repeated cycles of vasoconstriction and relaxation may lead to layered plaque formation and intimal irregularities.5,6 Consequently, OCT findings such as thrombus overlying an intact fibrous cap, layered plaque, or intimal bumps may be indistinguishable from primary PE. In the present case, the repeat OCT after 2 months provided definitive diagnostic insight: the complete resolution of the thrombus, whereas the underlying layered plaque and vasa vasorum remained, strongly suggested that the initial thrombus was a secondary event triggered by vasospasm-induced endothelial injury rather than a primary plaque disruption. Therefore, initial OCT findings were more appropriately interpreted as intraluminal thrombus of indeterminate etiology.4,5

Functional testing with acetylcholine is critical in clarifying the underlying mechanism in patients with MINOCA and suspected vasospastic angina. When performed according to established protocols, acetylcholine provocation testing is generally safe, with a reported major complication rate of only 0.5%, and provides high diagnostic yield.7, 8, 9 Although some protocols advocate for immediate testing during the initial catheterization, the timing of acetylcholine testing should be individualized. In the acute phase of myocardial infarction, concerns regarding procedure-related ischemia or arrhythmia may justify deferral, particularly when coronary flow is preserved and the patient is hemodynamically stable.1,7,9 In this patient, acetylcholine provocation testing performed during a subsequent admission for recurrent symptoms unmasked multivessel coronary spasm, allowing a definitive diagnosis of vasospastic angina–induced MINOCA.7,8

Treatment strategies differ substantially depending on underlying mechanism of MINOCA. Initial management based on a presumed diagnosis of PE included dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and a purinergic receptor P2Y12 inhibitor), intensive lipid-lowering therapy, and β-blocker administration.1,4 However, β-blockers may exacerbate coronary vasospasm by leaving α-adrenergic vasoconstriction unopposed. In the present case, recurrence of chest pain during β-blocker therapy, followed by symptom resolution after its discontinuation and initiation of vasodilator therapy, strongly supports vasospasm as the dominant pathophysiological mechanism. The paradoxical worsening of symptoms under β-blocker therapy served as a crucial, albeit unintentional, pharmacologic response that pointed toward a vasospastic etiology. This clinical response represents a meaningful diagnostic clue that complements invasive functional testing, highlighting the importance of reassessing the diagnosis when standard postmyocardial infarction therapy yields unexpected results.10

Cardiac magnetic resonance is recommended in contemporary MINOCA guidelines to exclude alternative diagnoses such as myocarditis or takotsubo syndrome and to confirm myocardial injury. In this case, cardiac magnetic resonance was not performed, representing a limitation.1,2 Nevertheless, the presence of elevated cardiac biomarkers, the absence of nonischemic features on echocardiography, reproducible coronary spasm with acetylcholine, and complete symptom resolution after targeted vasodilator therapy collectively support an ischemic mechanism driven by vasospasm. Overall, this case underscores the limitations of relying solely on anatomic imaging in MINOCA and emphasizes the necessity of integrating functional assessment into the diagnostic pathway. When OCT findings suggest PE in the absence of obstructive coronary disease, clinicians should remain vigilant for vasospastic mechanisms, particularly in patients with recurrent symptoms or paradoxical responses to β-blocker therapy.

Conclusions

In this case of MINOCA, OCT findings mimicking PE were secondary to coronary vasospasm. Systematic functional assessment, careful evaluation of pharmacologic response, and awareness of β-blocker–induced symptom exacerbation are essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.
