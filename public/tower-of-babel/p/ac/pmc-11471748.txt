Immunotherapy and pan-apoptotic characterization of the tumor microenvironment in gastric cancer (STAD): a single-cell multidimensional analysis
Authors: Sheng Zhang, Jianhong Wang, Huan Zhang, Benhua Li, Shun Gao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to elucidate the critical role of autophagy-related gene aggregation in gastric cancer tumor microenvironment cells and to investigate their major roles in cellular functions. In particular, the expression of these genes in tumor-associated fibroblast subtypes was scrutinized in an attempt to explain their cell-subpopulation-specific roles in cell–cell communication and regulation of cellular functions.MethodsIn this study, single-cell RNA sequencing data were first analyzed in multiple steps, including data preprocessing, cell clustering, and cell classification. Cell subpopulations and gene expression patterns were identified and analyzed using unsupervised non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) techniques. The dynamic expression of autophagy-related gene aggregates in various cell types was deciphered by pseudotime trajectory analysis (PTA). Intercellular communication analysis was performed using the CellChat R software package, revealing the intricate communication patterns and exchange of key signaling molecules between cell subpopulations, and SCENIC analysis was used to identify gene regulatory networks and reveal the mechanisms behind cellular heterogeneity.ResultCell subpopulations associated with pan-apoptosis were identified by NMF decomposition and SCENIC analysis. Cell–cell communication analysis revealed intricate communication patterns and exchange of key signaling molecules between cell subpopulations. Dynamic expression of autophagy-related genes aggregated in the pseudotemporal trajectory of STAD was observed by PTA. In the fibroblast subtype, different ligand-receptor interactions and their key roles in immunomodulation were observed.ConclusionBy deeply analyzing and comparing gene expression patterns within cellular subpopulations and intercellular communication, this study provides new insights into the role of pan-apoptosis-related genes in regulating immune responses and cellular functions in gastric cancer. These findings pave the way for further exploration of the role of these genes in tumorigenesis and immune regulation, as well as laying the foundation for potential therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Stomach cancer (STAD) is the second most prevalent cancer worldwide and the third deadliest tumor [1]. Gastric cancer is primarily attributed to factors such as Helicobacter pylori infection, high salt intake, and exposure to N-nitroso compounds, which can be synthesized through dietary sources, tobacco use, and endogenous processes [2]. Clinical manifestations of gastric cancer often include symptoms like epigastric pain, lethargy, and anorexia [3]. When gastric cancer occurs, treatment options typically include surgical procedures, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and molecular targeted therapy, which are employed to control disease progression [4]. Regrettably, a significant proportion of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage with a poor prognosis due to the lack of clear clinical indications [5]. Relying solely on histological classification is insufficient for effective patient stratification and personalized treatment, ultimately hampering clinical outcomes [6]. Consequently, it is of utmost importance to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of STAD. Such exploration is vital to identify innovative and effective therapeutic approaches that aim to inhibit STAD progression and enhance the overall prognosis of STAD patients.

Immunotherapy has shown success in managing gastric cancer (STAD) [7]. However, only a minority of patients respond, partly due to the failure to induce inflammatory cell death [8]. Cell death can occur in an unregulated manner (necrosis) or be programmed (programmed cell death, PCD). While apoptosis is the most well-characterized form of PCD, several other non-apoptotic forms of PCD also manifest during cancer progression. Pyroptosis and necroptosis have emerged as two additional forms of PCD [9, 10]. Therefore, targeting pyroptosis and necroptosis to stimulate anticancer immunity in cases where apoptosis is blocked represents a promising strategy. A deeper understanding of the interplay between pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis has led to the integration of these three PCD pathways under a unified term: pANoptosis [11]. pANoptosis is an inflammatory form of PCD activated by the simultaneous engagement of components from pyroptosis, apoptosis, and/or necroptosis [12]. It exhibits key characteristics of each of the three PCD types but cannot be fully explained by any single pathway [13]. The precise relationship between pANoptosis and anticancer immunity remains unclear. In contrast to its typically negative role in inflammatory or infectious diseases, if one or more PCD pathways are obstructed in cancer cells, pANoptosis may enable the host to activate alternative cell death defense mechanisms, potentially contributing to cancer suppression [14]. Thus, gaining further insights into the mechanisms of pANoptosis could facilitate the development of novel therapies for STAD.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) has emerged as a crucial tool for investigating gene expression patterns at the individual cell level. It encompasses a wide range of data analysis techniques, including gene expression quantification, batch effect correction, normalization, dimensionality reduction, feature selection, cell clustering, trajectory inference, and related processes [15]. In cancer research, scRNA-Seq technology has gained significant traction and has been widely applied to various cancers, including cervical, bladder, pancreatic, and gastric cancers [16, 17]. These studies have explored the heterogeneity, cellular composition, and molecular characteristics of cancer cells in these malignancies through scRNA-Seq, providing valuable insights into tumor biology and potential therapeutic targets. Our study aims to integrate scRNA-Seq and bulk RNA-Seq analyses to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms driving STAD pathogenesis and progression, as well as to assess the immunotherapeutic responses in STAD patients. The ultimate goal is to provide novel insights that can contribute to advancing STAD treatment and improving patient outcomes.

Methods and materials

Acquisition and processing of research data

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from STAD patients were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, specifically GSM5101013, GSM5101014, and GSM5101015 (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). Batch RNA-seq data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and GSE66222 in the GEO database, with the raw datasets being annotated using the corresponding files. In the annotation process, when mapping to the same probes, the average value was calculated. Pan-apoptosis-related genes were selected based on a previously published study [18].

Clustering and annotation of single-cell RNA sequencing data

We employed the Read10X function to obtain single-cell datasets for STAD and utilized the “Seurat” package within the R environment to transform the acquired data into Seurat objects. The integration of four tumor samples was performed using the merge function. Subsequently, a quality control index was calculated by evaluating the proportion of mitochondrial ribosomal and erythrocyte genes in the cells. For cell inclusion, we considered cells with gene counts ranging from 4000 to 200. Cells with ribosomal proportions exceeding the predefined threshold of 15% (pctMT = 15) were omitted to exclude low-quality cells from the dataset. Finally, the merged dataset was normalized using the NormalizeData function.

In the analysis of single cells based on RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, principal component analysis (PCA) was utilized for dimensionality reduction and clustering. Initially, the FindVariableFeatures function was employed to identify the top 2000 genes exhibiting high variability. Subsequently, principal component scores were assigned within the range of 1–10 during the dimensionality reduction step. Finally, UMAP plots were generated to visualize the resulting unsupervised cell clusters.To label the cell types within each cluster, we relied on marker genes obtained from prior studies. Additionally, heatmaps were used to present and label differentially expressed genes specific to each cell cluster, highlighting characteristic genes.

Screening of differentially expressed genes

The TCGA dataset and a set of PANs were imported in R. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the STAD tumor group and the normal group were calculated and screened using the “limma” R package. DEGs were considered significant if the absolute log fold change (|log2 FC|) of the DEGs was greater than 2 and the p-value was less than 0.05. These screened DEGs were prioritized as key genes for subsequent analysis.

Non‐negative matrix factorization(NMF)

We conducted unsupervised NMF analysis using the NMF R software package. The analysis initially involved data extraction specific to each cell cluster. Subsequently, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) was employed for dimensionality reduction clustering, resulting in NMF cell clusters. These clusters were visualized using the UMAP method. The “FindAllMarkers” function was then utilized to identify the genetic features of each NMF cell cluster. Screening thresholds were set with parameters such as min.pct, logfc.threshold (set to 0.5), and adjusted p-value (<0.05). The highest-scoring gene in each NMF cell cluster was chosen as the metagenome for that cell cluster and designated as a metagenome-positive cell cluster. However, if the highest-scoring gene in an NMF cell cluster did not belong to the LRGs or had a logfc.threshold of less than 1, the cell cluster was classified as a non-aggregating (non-aggregating) cell cluster.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

To investigate the role of PANs in cellular pseudotemporal trajectories, we generated ‘mycds’ objects using the R software and the monocle package. To identify highly variable genes in the dataset, we applied thresholds of mean expression ≥ 0.1 and discrete experience ≥ 1*discrete fit. Subsequently, the dimension of the ‘mycds’ object was reduced using the ‘DDRTree’ method with ‘maxComponent = 2’. The ‘orderCells’ function was then employed to arrange the reduced results. Finally, heatmaps were created to visualize the dynamic expression of PANs in the pseudotemporal trajectories of different TME cell types in STAD.

Cell–cell communication analysis

To delve into intercellular communication and heterogeneity between individual cell clusters and NMF cell clusters, we conducted further analysis using the "CellChat" R software package. In this analysis, we employed the CellChatDB.human database to investigate intercellular communication patterns, which encompasses data derived from prior studies. This database enabled us to evaluate the input and output of critical signaling molecules between each cell cluster and the NMF cell cluster and to identify the ligand-receptor pairs participating in these interactions. Subsequently, we generated visual graphs illustrating the number of communication pathways, pathway weights, and noteworthy efferent and afferent pathways within the analyzed samples.

Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) analysis

SCENIC, a computational method used for inferring gene regulatory networks and identifying cellular states from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data, has evolved into a significant tool for gaining insights into the mechanisms underlying cellular heterogeneity (21). In this study, we employed the ‘SCENIC’ R software package for our analysis. To explore the gene regulatory networks associated with transcription start sites (TSSs) and transcription factors (TFs) in the scRNA-seq data, we focused on two specific gene sequence sets in the RcisTarget database: ‘hg19-500bp-upstream-7species.mc9nr’ and ‘hg19-tss-centered-10kb-7species.mc9nr’.

Functional enrichment analysis

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) is a comprehensive resource in the fields of genomics, bioinformatics, and systems biology, providing a wealth of genomic information. In this study, we employed two computational methods, Genomic Variation Analysis (GSVA) and Single-Sample Genomic Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA), to investigate the significance and quantification of LRGs in STAD. GSVA analyzes gene expression data at the pathway or genome level, while ssGSEA calculates the enrichment scores of individual samples in gene expression data. By utilizing these techniques, our goal is to unveil the functional roles and associated scores of LRGs in STAD.

Bulk RNA‐sequence sample score

To investigate the prognostic and immunotherapeutic roles of NMF cell clusters and PANs in STAD, we employed the "FindAllMarkers" function to identify marker genes for each NMF cell cluster. Subsequently, large volumes of RNA-seq data from TCGA and GEO were imported into the R environment for further analysis. We utilized the "gsva" function in R to compute enrichment scores for each NMF cell cluster in each sample using the "ssgsea" method. These scores were then aggregated and consolidated to create a new matrix for subsequent analysis.

Survival analysis of the STAD samples

We obtained survival data for a substantial number of RNA-seq single samples from the official TCGA and GEO websites. To evaluate the prognostic significance of marker genes for each NMF cell cluster, we conducted a Kaplan–Meier survival analysis using this survival data. We employed a significance threshold with a p-value less than 0.05, and accordingly computed scores for marker genes. For each NMF cell population, we categorized the corresponding marker genes as ‘high’ when the gene score exceeded the maximum cutoff value. The effects of high and low scores on the overall survival of STAD patients were subsequently compared, and the results were visualized through corresponding graphs. To ensure consistency, clusters of NMF cells with consistent results were identified by comparing data from both TCGA and GEO.

Univariate cox analysis of NMF cell clusters

Univariate Cox modeling is a widely employed statistical method in survival analysis, rooted in the Cox proportional hazards model. It facilitates the examination of the impact of a single factor on the risk of an event. In this study, we employed a univariate Cox model to evaluate the influence of each NMF cluster on the survival prognosis of STAD patients. By integrating survival time and survival status data for each sample, we conducted a comprehensive analysis using both TCGA and GEO datasets.

Immune checkpoint blockade immunotherapeutic(ICB)

To predict the response of STAD samples to immunotherapy, we employed the online tool ‘Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Rejection (TIDE)’ available at http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/. TIDE calculates TIDE scores as surrogate biomarkers to predict patient responses to immunotherapy. We input TCGA and GEO data into the TIDE website to predict the groups of STAD samples that respond and do not respond to immunotherapy. Subsequently, we integrated marker gene scores into the predictions and compared the marker gene scores between the responding and non-responding groups. This analysis was conducted separately for TCGA and GEO data. Finally, we compared the calculated results for TCGA and GEO data and visualized the combined results to provide a comprehensive insight into the immunotherapy prospects for STAD.

External immunotherapy cohort validation

To validate the substantial impact of pan-apoptosis-associated NMF cell cluster marker genes on cancer, we extended the study to include an external cohort of patients who underwent immunotherapy. The IMvigor210 dataset encompasses gene expression profiles, survival data, and outcomes following immunotherapy for a substantial number of bladder cancer samples, including complete response (CR), partial response (PR), disease stabilization (SD), and disease progression (PD). Employing the methods described earlier, we once again evaluated the scores of the lactation-associated NMF cell cluster marker genes in each immunotherapy group and conducted Kaplan–Meier survival analyses.

Somatic mutation analysis and single gene survival analysis

As mutations can also influence the prognosis of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, we conducted an additional analysis to investigate potential differences in the mutations of these marker genes. We utilized the ‘maftool’ package to create waterfall plots visualizing the mutation details of these genes. Additionally, we generated correlation heatmaps to assess the associations between mutations. Subsequently, we applied the ‘survival’ package to analyze the survival outcomes of STAD patients in the TCGA database based on different expressions of each marker gene, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of these genes.

Prognosis and immunoassay

We performed a prognostic analysis of all the marker genes on the website(https://www.xiantaozi.com) to look at the characteristics of this group of marker genes. Similarly, we performed immune and disease correlation analyses on the two specific marker genes screened to observe the function of these two genes in immunity and the differences in expression between them and the normal group on a variety of cancers. We looked for immunohistochemical sections of genes in STAD on The Human Protein Atlas (http://www.proteinatlas.org/) website to validate the expression of previously obtained genes.

Role of PANs-mediated fibroblasts in STAD

It is well known that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of various tumors, including STAD. In this study, we isolated fibroblasts from scRNA-seq data and investigated the effects of pan-apoptotic genes on the role of fibroblasts in STAD. Through the proposed time trajectory analysis, we observed that among multiple pan-apoptotic genes, genes such as BNIP3L, CHMP4B, CHMP2B, and IRF1 were gradually up-regulated during cell development. genes such as IRF2, CHMP2A, BAK1, and RIPK3 were gradually down-regulated during cell development (Fig. 2A). To delve into the potential relationship between pan-apoptotic genes and fibroblasts, we identified and characterized four different clusters of fibroblasts with positive expression of pan-apoptosis-related genes NMF and one cluster that was not identified as positive for the expression of pan-apoptosis-related genes:IRF1+CAF-C1, CHMP4B+CAF-C2, IGF1+CAF-C3, HMGB2+CAF-C4 and Non-Aggre+CAF-C5 (Fig. 2B). We analyzed the developmental trajectories of five different fibroblast NMF clusters and found that Non-Aggre fibroblast NMF clusters appeared predominantly in the pre-cellular developmental phase, and did not find significant differences in the developmental timing of the other four fibroblast NMF clusters (Fig. 2C). Next, analysis of intercellular communication between these clusters and epithelial cells showed that the fibroblast clusters mediated by IRF1 and CHMP4B had the highest intensity of the cellular communication pathway, HMGB2- and IGF1-mediated clusters of fibroblasts also showed significant cellular communication expression, and clusters of fibroblasts that were not identified as positive for pan-apoptotic genes had the lowest intensity of the cellular communication pathway (Fig. 2D). In addition, analysis of intercellular communication between these clusters showed that fibroblast clusters not identified as positive for pan-apoptotic genes had the lowest intensity of cellular communication pathways in fibroblast NMF clusters (Fig. 2E). And the CHMP4B cluster mainly functions to receive signals from the other four clusters. Expression and role of PANs in T cells

T cells are an integral part of cellular immunity and play a pivotal role in the development of STAD. We isolated and extracted T cells from scRNA-seq data for reclustering (Fig. 3A) and found a total of three different T cell types cytotoxic T cells (CD8-Tcells), helper T cells (CD4-Tcells), and natural killer cells (NKcells) (Fig. 3B), which showed that compared with the normal samples, the content of T cells was substantially increase in tumor samples compared with normal samples. We next extracted cytotoxic T cells (CD8-T cells) from among the T cells and analyzed them using NMF typing (Fig. 3C), which showed the presence of four distinct NMF cell clusters and one cluster not identified as positive for pan-apoptosis-related gene expression: YWHAE+CD8+T_cells-C1, GZMB+CD8+T_cells-C2, CHMP4A+CD8+TT_cells-C3, FASLG+CD8+T_cells-C4 and Non-Aggre+T_cells-C5. Cell communication analysis showed that in terms of afferent signaling patterns, NMF cell clusters communicated robustly with epithelial cells mainly through the MF, MK, GALGCTIN and TNF pathways. In terms of efferent signaling patterns, the per-T-cell NMF clusters communicated with epithelial cells mainly through involving PARS, IFN-II (Fig. 3D). There was strong signaling between epithelial cells and each T cell NMF cluster (Fig. 3E). Among the expression of key immune checkpoints, three subpopulations, YWHAE+CD8+T_cells-C1, CHMP4A+CD8+TT_cells-C3, and FASLG+CD8+T_cells-C4, had significant expression of genes related to immune checkpoints (Fig. 3F). In SCENIC analysis, individual NMF clusters were distinctly heterogeneous, whereas YWHAE-positive clusters had the most active TFs (Fig. 3G). In this study, we utilized marker genes associated with CD8+T-cell exhaustion and cytotoxic T-cells to assess T-cell function scores of different NMF clusters. Effects of macrophages regulated by PANs in STAD

We isolated and extracted myeloid cells from the scRNA-seq data for reclustering (Fig. 4A), dividing myeloid cells into 3 clusters (Fig. 4B). After annotating these 3 clusters, we found that there were two different cell types, monocytes and macrophages (Fig. 4C). Next, we extracted macrophages from among them for further analysis, and pseudo-temporal trajectory analysis showed that pan-apoptotic genes were significantly expressed in macrophages at all periods of time, and genes such as TNF, CASP1, and IL1A were progressively up-regulated during cellular development. genes such as CHMP2B, CHMP4A, CHMP4B, and LY96 were progressively down-regulated during cellular development, and genes such as GZMB, CASP7 and BOK were gradually down-regulated after being up-regulated during cell development (Fig. 4D).Two subgroups were obtained from the NMF dimensionality reduction clustering analysis, including the VCP-positive subgroup and the Non-Aggre subgroup (Fig. 4E). To deeply investigate the potential relationship between the two subclusters, we performed developmental trajectory analysis of the subclusters and found that Non-Aggre+Mac- C2 gradually developed to VCP+Mac- C1 (Fig. 4F). Cellular communication analysis showed that both clusters exhibited similar communication signals with epithelial cells (Fig. 4G). Notably, the communication pathway between the VCP+Mac- C1 cluster of macrophages and epithelial cells was significantly activated (Fig. 4H), suggesting a strong association between macrophages and epithelial cells mediated by pan-apoptotic genes. In SCENIC analysis, we observed significant heterogeneity in the expression activity of TFs in the two groups, with the VCP+Mac- C1 cluster and Non-Aggre+Mac- C2 regulating different parts of the transcription, respectively (Fig. 4I). Macrophages can be classified into two main subtypes based on their function and activation status: the M1 and M2. the primary functions of M1 macrophages are to kill and remove pathogens, modulate inflammatory responses, and promote immune responses, and they are usually activated in response to bacterial and viral infections, inflammation, and immune stimulation. the primary functions of M2 macrophages are to inhibit inflammation, promote tissue repair, modulate immune tolerance, and support tissue remodeling. When the M1 and M2 subtype scores of macrophages were examined, it was found that M1 macrophages (proinflammatory macrophages) in VCP-positive clusters had higher scores (Fig. 4J, K). Immune landscape of B cells in STAD and functional enrichment analysis

We isolated and extracted B cells from the scRNA-seq data for reclustering (Fig. 5A), dividing myeloid cells into five clusters (Fig. 5B). After annotating these 5 cell clusters, we found that there were two different cell types, namely B cells and plasma cells (Fig. 5C). We next extracted B cells from among them and analyzed them using NMF typing showing (Fig. 5D) that there were seven distinct NMF cell clusters and one cluster not identified as positive for pan-apoptosis-related gene expression: ly96+B_cells-C1, IRF1+B_cells-C2, BAX+B_cells-C3, HMGB2+B_cells-C4, YWHAE+B_cells-C5, CASP4+B_cells-C6, CHMP2A+B_cells-C7 and Non-Aggre+B_cells-C8. To investigate the distribution of each NMF cell cluster in plasma cells and B cells (Fig. 5E), we quantified the composition of each NMF cell cluster in both cell types. Discussion

Despite extensive discussion and research on its application in various cancers, few studies have specifically focused on the impact of pan-apoptotic effects on STA. The present study is a pioneering attempt to comprehensively investigate the potential tumorigenic role of pan-apoptosis-mediated TME patterns in STAD and their impact on tumor response to anti-immunotherapy. This experiment identified subpopulations of TME cells regulated by PANs using scRNA-seq, explored the interactions between these subpopulations as well as the interactions between TME cells and tumor cells, and subsequently confirmed these findings with extensive RNA-seq analyses and survival data, which provided us with new perspectives and insights.

TME has been recognized as an important player and potential therapeutic target in STAD. It affects the development and progression of gastric cancer from several aspects [19]. This study specifically focuses on four key TME components: cancer-associated fibroblasts, macrophages, T cells, and B cells. Epithelial cells play a crucial role in tumorigenesis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is closely related to tumorigenesis [20]. EMT can regulate and activate various complex signaling pathways in the mesenchyme of tumor tissues, thus promoting tumor invasion and metastasis. And the heterogeneity of epithelial cells will help accurate diagnosis and prognosis [21]. Therefore, studying the intricate interactions between epithelial cells and other TME cells is of utmost importance for the treatment and prevention of STAD.

Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) have a significant impact on STAD, and they play an important role in promoting tumorigenesis, angiogenesis, and cancer progression through paracrine signaling and regulation of the extracellular matrix [22]. In our current study, we observed that CAFs regulated by PANs exhibited closer intercellular communication with epithelial cells than CAFs not regulated by PANs. Previous studies have shown that IRF1 can affect the invasiveness of fibroblasts by regulating FSTL1 [23]. IRF1 also regulates cytokine production in tumor cells and CAFs. These cytokines can influence epithelial cell behavior, such as promoting epithelial cell proliferation, migration, or generating an inflammatory response, which can affect tumor growth and dissemination. The higher average regulatory activity of transcription factors (TFs) in IRF1-positive cell clusters in CAFs mediated by PANs suggests that IRF1-mediated CAFs have stronger cellular activity in STAD. Notably, IRF1-positive cell clusters were significantly expressed in a subpopulation of pro-inflammatory CAFs known as pan-iCAF-2. CHMP4B protein has important functions in cells and is associated with several cellular biological processes such as endosomal membrane fusion, biological processes of cytoplasmic negative vesicles (endosome), and cell division. During transcription, CHMP4B protein expresses higher regulatory activity as IRF1. During metabolic processes CHMP4B showed strong activity, which may influence CAFs to play an important role in tumor invasion and metastasis through metabolic reprogramming [24]. Therefore, we hypothesized that PANs-mediated CAFs may contribute to cell metabolism and proliferation, thereby promoting tumor cell invasion. The link between pan-apoptosis and immune cells has been extensively studied, revealing the important impact of pan-apoptotic factors as immunosuppressive factors in tumor immune evasion [25, 26]. Pathogen-induced autoimmune responses produce inflammatory cytokines that activate initiation proteins for focal, apoptotic, and necrotic apoptosis and assemble inflammatory vesicles targeting differentially programmed cell deaths [27], which, in turn, assemble into the PANoptosome, thereby driving both focal, apoptotic, and necrotic apoptosis. thereby exacerbating pathogen-induced cell death [28]. Notably, clusters of IL1B-positive macrophages appeared at the end of differentiation, while at the same time the MIF, GALECTIN pathway strongly mediated intercellular communication between macrophages and epithelial cells. This suggests that PANs may promote macrophage activity at the terminal stage.The IL1B gene encodes a protein, IL-1β, which plays an important role in the immune system and inflammatory response. It is a member of the family of immune cytokines with important implications for immune regulation, inflammatory responses, and the development of many diseases. More notably, among CD8+ T cells, FASLG-positive clusters play a major role in intercellular communication. Transcription factor analysis showed that YWHAE-positive clusters play a key role in the transcription process. While functional scoring of T cells showed a strong correlation between GZMB-positive CD8+ T cells in T cell exhaustion and cytotoxic T cells vs. This seems to indicate that each subpopulation delineated by PANs plays their respective roles in tumorigenesis with strong heterogeneity between functions.

Cancer epigenetics are not solely induced by individual molecules but are also influenced by systemic dysfunction and gene-coupled effects [29]. Therefore, studying the gene regulatory networks specific to tumor cells is crucial for understanding their underlying mechanisms. Our results demonstrate that NMF cell clusters regulated by PANs exhibit higher average TF expression activity compared to clusters not regulated by PANs. Additionally, distinct TF profiles were observed across all subtypes of TME cells. In CAFs, IRF-positive clusters showed elevated expression levels of TFs, including IRF8 and STAT1. Notably, STAT1 plays a critical role in the immune system, acting as part of the interferon signaling pathway, particularly for interferon-α and interferon-β. Upon exposure to viral infection or other immune stimuli, STAT1 becomes phosphorylated (activated), translocates into the nucleus, and induces the expression of a range of anti-pathogen genes to bolster the immune response. This includes upregulation of antiviral genes, immunomodulatory molecules, and anti-inflammatory factors [30]. Previous studies have shown that IRF8 induces senescence in cancer cells, thereby inhibiting cancer progression [31]. Furthermore, IRF8 is essential for the normal development and functional maintenance of multiple immune cell types, including macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer cells (NK cells), and certain lymphocyte subsets. It regulates the expression of various immune-related genes, enhancing the body’s resistance to pathogens. IRF8 deficiency may lead to immune system dysfunction and increased susceptibility to infections. In conclusion, cell clusters mediated by PANs play a pivotal role in the regulatory network modulating TFs, and their robust intercellular interactions contribute to the transformation and progression of the SYAD tumor microenvironment. Importantly, NMF cell clusters regulated by PANs exhibited higher levels of active TF expression and enhanced intercellular communication, suggesting that PANs may influence the regulation of certain TME components in STAD.

To further explore the findings of scRNA-seq, we performed additional analyses using bulk RNA-seq. Notably, differential analysis of STAD tumor samples and normal samples showed that the HMGB2+CAF-C4, YWHAE+CD8+T_cells-C1, CHMP4A+CD8+T_cells-C3, FASLG+CD8+T_cells-C4, YWHAE+B_cell-C5, and CHMP2A+B _cells-C7 clusters all scored statistically different from the normal group in the tumor group. This observation suggests that PANs may be involved in the pathogenesis of STAD. Further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. In addition, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis found that in the TCGA and GEO datasets, three subgroups, CHMP4B+CAF-C2, HMGB2+CAF-C4, and IGF1+CAF-C3, were important risk factors in STAD patients, and a subgroup, FASLG+CD8+T_cells-C4, was an important protective factor. These findings are consistent with scRNA-seq analyses as well as previous findings, emphasizing the important contribution of the pan-apoptotic pathway to STAD development. In conclusion, PANs-mediated TME cell clustering contributes profoundly to disease progression and tumor cell progression in STAD patients. Immune checkpoint blockade immunotherapy (ICB) is an important strategy for the treatment of tumors. Importantly, most clusters of NMF cells regulated by PANs exhibit the ability to enhance drug resistance in STAD tumor cells while suppressing anti-tumor immune responses within the TME. This effect was particularly notable when using the TCGA dataset to predict immunotherapy responses. Furthermore, it is worth highlighting that the CHMP4B+CAF-C2 and IGF1+CAF-C3 cell clusters exhibited significant pro-tumor anti-immune responses. This was evident in univariate Cox modeling analyses of STAD samples as well as in survival analyses of STAD patients in the TCGA and GEO datasets. In addition, consistent findings were observed in bladder cancer immunotherapy response cohorts and survival analyses. Together, these findings highlight the important role of CHMP4B+CAF-C2, IGF1+CAF-C3 cell clusters in promoting tumor progression and hindering anti-tumor immune responses in multiple cancer types. Combined with the previous sections, we can show that CHMP4B+CAF-C2 and IGF1+CAF-C3 cell clusters play important roles in tumor invasion and metastasis. Previous studies have shown that activation of IGF1 signaling promotes cancer progression, and dysregulation of IGF1-associated lncRNAs may also play an important role in the modulation of IGF1 resistance to chemotherapy [32]. Consistent with our study, our findings suggest that IGF1 has the potential to contribute to the poor prognosis of patients and to play a key role in suppressing anti-tumor immune responses against STAD. This finding suggests that IGF1 could be an important target for immunotherapeutic intervention. Meanwhile, NMF cell clusters regulated by PANs seem to hinder anti-tumor immune responses against STAD. Therefore, this mechanism may enhance drug resistance of STAD tumor cells by suppressing the antitumor immune response in STAD [33, 34].

Our study is the first to comprehensively explore the gastric cancer landscape within the framework of pan-apoptosis. We identified novel single-cell subpopulations of gastric cancer through non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) disaggregation, a method that decomposes complex cellular data into more interpretable subgroups. Moreover, we rigorously analyzed these cell subpopulations using advanced techniques, including cellular communication analysis, transcription factor profiling, and immunotherapeutic potential assessment. This multifaceted approach provides deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying gastric cancer and opens potential pathways for the development of more targeted and effective therapies.

However, as a preliminary investigation, our study is not without limitations. One of the primary constraints lies in the limited size of the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) samples used. Given the heterogeneity of gastric cancer, the small sample size may not fully capture the diverse cellular subpopulations present in a broader patient cohort. Consequently, further validation of our findings in a larger, more diverse patient population is essential to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the identified subpopulations [35, 36]. Additionally, while our study provides novel insights into the molecular landscape of gastric cancer, the use of only publicly available datasets underscores the need for further validation using actual tissue samples. By integrating data from patient-derived tissue samples, we could strengthen the reliability of our findings and better assess the clinical relevance of the identified subpopulations [37]. Such validation would also allow for a more accurate investigation of the therapeutic potential of targeting specific cell subpopulations in gastric cancer. In summary, although our study lays the groundwork for future research into the role of pan-apoptosis in gastric cancer, additional investigations involving larger sample sizes and patient-derived tissues are crucial for translating these findings into clinical applications [38].

Conclusion

In this study, we identified TME cell subpopulations mediated by PANs in STAD at the scRNA-seq level and explored the complex interactions between cell subpopulations as well as gene regulatory networks. We further validated the prognostic impact of each cell subpopulation on STAD patient prognosis and its role in immunotherapy at the bulk RNA-seq level and performed prognostic analysis of important marker genes with single gene analysis. In conclusion, we found that PANs can promote tumor cell development and inhibit anti-tumor immunogenesis by regulating or activating each TME cell component in STAD, thereby worsening the prognosis of STAD patients and increasing immunotherapy resistance of tumor cells.
