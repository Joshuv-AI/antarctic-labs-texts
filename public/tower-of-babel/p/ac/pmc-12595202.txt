Exploring the plausible genetic relationship of salivary and tongue microbiome with periodontitis: A mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jing Li, Sinuo Wang, Peng Luo, Zhengrui Li, Divya Gopinath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic periodontitis (CP) is associated with subgingival microbial dysbiosis and demonstrates specific microbial patterns, though definitive causal connections with microbiomes in distinct anatomical regions remain undetermined. Genome-wide association datasets for CP and oral microbial communities were sourced from a large European cohort and China National GeneBank DataBase (CNGBdb), respectively. Employing single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as genetic instruments, Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were conducted through the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) approach. Analysis methods were implemented through the ‘TwoSampleMR’ package (v0.6.4) in R software. Sensitivity analyses were performed to validate the robustness of the findings and mitigate the occurrence of horizontal pleiotropy. The MR analyses revealed three salivary bacterial taxa, Neisseria meningitidis (OR = 0.67, 95% CI, 0.49–0.98), Streptococcus vestibularis (OR = 0.74, 95% CI, 0.56–0.98), and Lancefieldella unclassified (OR = 0.68, 95% CI, 0.52–0.91) to be significantly associated with a reduced risk of CP (p < 0.05). In contrast, tongue microbial taxa Solobacterium unclassified (OR = 1.45, 95% CI, 1.04–2.04), Fusobacterium sp000235465 (OR = 1.40, 95% CI, 1.02–1.94), and Haemophilus parainfluenzae (OR = 1.56, 95% CI, 1.12–2.18) were associated with an increased CP risk (p < 0.05). No evidence of heterogeneity and directional pleiotropy was noted for these associations. This study highlights the association between specific salivary and tongue microbial taxa and CP, providing mechanistic linkages into the plausible relationship. It also suggests that some microbial taxa may be further explored as indicators for risk-stratified preventive measures and novel targets for precision prebiotics and therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44445-025-00087-y.

Introduction

Chronic periodontitis (CP) represents a globally prevalent chronic inflammatory disorder with non-communicable disease characteristics. In 2019, severe periodontitis affected 1.1 billion individuals globally, accounting for 10.6% of the population, with the age-standardized prevalence increasing by 8.4% from 1990 to 2019. (Wu et al. 2022) The main clinical manifestations of chronic periodontitis include significant dysbiosis of the periodontal microbiome. The pathophysiological progression manifests through periodontal pocket deepening, connective tissue detachment, and osteoclastic bone remodeling, which severely compromise the integrity of periodontal supporting tissues. (Kwon et al. 2021) In advanced cases, tooth loss may occur, significantly reducing the quality of life. (Agnese et al. 2024) Major risk factors for periodontitis include smoking, alcohol consumption, and poor oral hygiene. Despite the advancement in research, the etiology and mechanisms underlying periodontitis are not completely elucidated and require further investigation. (Łasica et al. 2024).

The oral cavity harbors a highly intricate microbial ecosystem that plays an indispensable role in human physiological homeostasis, second only to the gut microbiome. It has been closely linked to various oral diseases, including oral cancers, dental diseases, and periodontal diseases. (Gopinath et al. 2020) Emerging evidence substantiates that microbial community destabilization constitutes a pivotal pathogenic determinant in chronic periodontitis progression. (Deng et al. 2017) Sequencing profiling reveals characteristic taxonomic restructuring within the oral microbiota of chronic periodontitis patients, suggesting that certain bacteria may play important roles in disease progression. However, the microbiota composition varies significantly across different oral niches, such as supragingival, subgingival, and gingival sulcus regions, each with its distinct dominant bacterial communities. (Li et al. 2022) Additionally, factors such as lifestyle, systemic diseases, and oral hygiene practices can also lead to changes in the oral microbiome. (Gopinath et al. 2022; Guo et al. 2024) These complexities pose challenges for studying periodontitis due to confounding factors and reverse causation. Nevertheless, growing evidence suggests that investigating the relationship between the oral microbiome and chronic periodontitis can provide a mechanistic justification for disease heterogeneity, elucidating etiological pathways in risk-factor-naive populations through host-microbe crosstalk dynamics.

Mendelian randomization (MR), a robust epidemiological methodology, leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between modifiable exposures and disease outcomes through genotype–phenotype association analyses. (Davey Smith and Ebrahim 2003) Due to its benefits of temporal rationality and reduction of confounding variables, MR is regarded as a supplementary approach to randomized controlled trials.(Davey Smith and Ebrahim 2003; Liu et al. 2022) Previous studies have shown that the human genome significantly influences the composition of the oral microbiome. (Goodrich et al. 2017; Demmitt et al. 2017) Furthermore, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have delineated numerous host genetic determinants that modulate oral microbial community structure and ecological resilience, underscoring the genomic regulation of microbiome-host symbiosis. (Awany et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2024) However, the causal inference framework linking oral microbiota other than subgingival microbes to CP pathogenesis is still in its infancy, limiting our understanding of their interactions and hindering the formulation of precision intervention and targeted modulation.

This investigation implemented a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization framework, leveraging GWAS data from salivary and tongue dorsum microbiota biobanks, to delineate the causal relationship between the salivary and tongue microbiota and chronic periodontitis. We hypothesize that host genetics influence the abundance of specific, heritable taxa within the salivary and tongue dorsum microbiota, and that these genetically-influenced microbial communities, in turn, play a causal role in modulating an individual's susceptibility to chronic periodontitis. The findings are expected to establish a causal paradigm for oral microbiome-driven periodontitis etiopathogenesis through genetic instrumental variable analysis, while providing a mechanistic rationale for interventions that selectively recalibrate dysbiotic microbial networks without disrupting commensal homeostasis.

Methods

Study design and data sources

A two-sample MR analysis was conducted utilizing a previously published GWAS dataset on oral microbiota obtained from the CNGBdb database. (Liu et al. 2021) Summary statistics for chronic periodontitis (CP) were derived from the publicly available “finn-b-K11_PERIODON_CHRON” dataset, which contains genetic data from a large European cohort. The FinnGen consortium provided a European-ancestry case–control cohort comprising 3,046 clinically validated periodontitis cases and 195,395 population controls, with disease classification strictly adhering to ICD coding standards across versions: ICD-8 (5234), ICD-9 (5234), and ICD-10 (K05.30/K05.31) for chronic periodontitis subtyping. Variability in CP case definitions across studies, including the use of CDC and ICD criteria, may contribute to heterogeneity in the dataset. Detailed descriptions of the consortia and data sources are available in Table S1.

This investigation adhered to international ethical standards for secondary data analysis, utilizing exclusively identified, publicly available GWAS datasets. Reporting rigor was ensured through full compliance with STROBE-MR guidelines to ensure transparency and consistency.

Oral microbiome profiling and bioinformatic processing

The study incorporated dual-origin microbial genomic datasets comprising 2,017 tongue and 1,915 salivary metagenomes, acquired through standardized sequencing with paired shotgun metagenomic validation, resulting in a total of 3,984 participants (Table S1). Multidimensional quality assurance protocols were implemented across genomic datasets, mandating genotype missingness rate < 2% (≥ 98% completeness), average sequencing coverage exceeding 20 ×, and exclusion of population structure outliers identified through multidimensional scaling analysis (MDS) with Bonferroni-corrected thresholds (p < 1 × 10–6). Genetically related participants were removed based on pairwise identity-by-descent analysis. Further quality control measures included a minimum mean sequencing depth of 8 ×, HWE values > 10⁻5, and a genotype calling rate > 98% for analyzed variants.

Following these protocols, the dataset comprised approximately 10 million genetic variants, including frequency variants with a MAF of ≥ 0.5%. Taxonomic classification of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) was conducted using algorithms against curated reference databases, followed by cross-platform data harmonization employing variance stabilizing transformations and quantile normalization to enable multi-cohort analyses.

Instrumental variable selection

The Mendelian randomization framework utilized genome-wide significant associations (p < 5 × 10–8), implementing three-stage genetic variant selection: (1) microbial taxon-specific SNP identification, (2) cross-dataset harmonization through allele frequency matching, and (3) pleiotropy pruning via MR-PRESSO outlier detection (p < 0.05) to eliminate variants demonstrating horizontal pleiotropy.

The validity of Mendelian randomization analysis hinges on three core instrumental variable (IV) assumptions (Fig. 1): (1) Relevance Assumption: Genome-wide significant association (p < 5 × 10–8) between genetic instruments and target exposure (microbial taxa abundance). (2) Independence Assumption: Absence of instrument-confounder correlation, verified through multivariable MR adjustment and PhenoScanner v2.0 interrogation of pleiotropic pathways. Mendelian randomization analyses

The causal inference framework was anchored in inverse variance-weighted (IVW) meta-analysis, augmented with sensitivity triangulation through a four-approach robustness framework. (1) MR-Egger regression with intercept testing for directional pleiotropy, (2) weighted median estimator accommodating partial valid instruments, (3) contamination mixture methods addressing weak instrument bias, and (4) mode-based estimation prioritizing modal causal effects. The IVW method provides unbiased causal estimates under the assumption of no pleiotropy, while the additional methods account for potential pleiotropic effects, enhancing the robustness of the results.

Heterogeneity and pleiotropy assessments

The analytical workflow incorporated a four-dimensional validation framework. (1) Effect Heterogeneity Quantification: Cochran’s Q-statistic with permutation testing assessed instrument consistency, where Q_p < 0.01/SNP_count triggered random-effects IVW models, while significant heterogeneity indicating variability in SNP effects. (2) Directional Pleiotropy Interrogation: MR-Egger intercept permutation tests (α = 0.05) with SIMEX-corrected standard errors identified biased causal estimates. (Bowden et al. 2016; Verbanck et al. 2018) (3) The MR-PRESSO analysis also performed to detect and adjust for outliers. (4) Causal Directionality Verification: Steiger filtering with false discovery rate control (FDR < 5%) confirmed the causal direction between oral microbiota (exposure) and CP (outcome).

Statistical analyses

All causal inference analyses were executed in R v4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2023) utilizing the ‘TwoSampleMR’ v0.6.4 and ‘MRPRESSO’ v1.0 packages, with multiplicity correction via Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control (q < 0.05). Effect estimate distributions were visualized through three-dimensional forest plots (‘ggplot2’ v3.4.2) incorporating Bayesian credible intervals, complemented by microbiome-wide association bubble charts mapping − log10(p) values against phylogenetic taxonomy ranks.

Results

The Mendelian randomization investigation revealed taxon-specific causal associations across multiple phylogenetic levels, including 1phylum, 2 classes, 8 orders, 17 families, 15 genera, and 412 species. For saliva microbes specifically, associations were observed with two phyla, one class, four orders, 13 families, 96 genera, and 424 species. A total of 28,715 SNPs were retained, together with 29,500 additional SNPs showing genome-wide significance. These genetic variants surpassed the conventional threshold of 20, along with other filtering standards (Table S1), demonstrating the robustness of the instruments for representing saliva and tongue microbiota. The results of reverse MR analysis are not significant, and no SNPs can be found, let alone further analysis. Therefore, the causal relationship is almost certainly one-way, from the microbiome to the disease, and not the other way around. This makes the main findings of the study much more robust and reliable.

Associations between saliva microbiota and CP

The MR analyses identified notable correlations between the taxa of saliva microbiota and the likelihood of developing CP. Notably, Neisseria meningitidis (pheno.1182) (OR.ivw = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.49–0.98, P = 0.037), Streptococcus vestibularis (pheno.1304) (OR.ivw = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.56–0.98, P = 0.034), and Lancefieldella unclassified mgs2277 (pheno.3670) (OR.ivw = 0.68, 95% CI = 0.52–0.91, P = 0.008) were significantly associated with an reduced risk of CP (Fig. 2). Heterogeneity and pleiotropy analysis

To validate the reliability of the results, analyses of heterogeneity and pleiotropy were conducted as part of the MR framework. Cochran’s Q test was used to evaluate the heterogeneity among IVs. The findings, presented in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5, indicated no substantial heterogeneity in the majority of associations. For example, in the association between Neisseria meningitidis and chronic periodontitis, the Q values for the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method (Q = 0.244, p = 0.885) and MR-Egger method (Q = 0.201, p = 0.654) indicated no significant heterogeneity. Similarly, for Streptococcus vestibularis, no significant heterogeneity was observed (Q = 1.762, p = 0.623 for IVW; Q = 1.291, p = 0.524 for MR-Egger). This indicates consistency among the IVs used, supporting the reliability of the causal estimates derived from the analyses. The MR-Egger intercept test was applied to determine whether horizontal pleiotropy existed, with genetic variants potentially affecting outcomes through pathways independent of the primary exposure. The MR-Egger intercept values did not reach statistical significance, suggesting no indication of directional pleiotropy in the significant associations (Tables S6, S7). These strengthens confidence that the observed causal effects are not confounded by pleiotropic bias. These results collectively suggest that the causal estimates derived from the MR analyses are unlikely to be biased by either heterogeneity or pleiotropy, providing robust evidence for the observed associations.

Discussion

A hallmark of periodontitis is the presence of a dysbiotic subgingival microbiome. However, all these different oral niches are interconnected through saliva. Various periodontal pathobionts have been identified within the saliva of individuals with periodontitis. Originating primarily in the subgingival biofilm, these microorganisms can spread to additional oral sites, reshaping local microbial communities and contributing to dysbiosis. (Andjelkovic et al. 2017; Fernandes et al. 2010; Simas et al. 2021) However, the detailed links between periodontitis and microbial ecosystems across distinct oral habitats, as well as the advantageous biological functions within them, are still poorly understood. (Chew et al. 2024) We conducted an MR analysis to comprehensively investigate how salivary and tongue microbiota relate to chronic periodontitis. The results revealed that specific salivary microbes (Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus vestibularis, and Lancefieldella unclassified) were significantly associated with reduced CP risk, whereas tongue microbes (Solobacterium unclassified, Fusobacterium sp000235465, and Haemophilus parainfluenzae) were significantly associated with increased CP risk. Notably, salivary and tongue microbiota demonstrated distinct roles in CP risk; salivary microbes exhibited protective effects, while tongue microbes appeared to act as risk factors. This divergence may be related to the functional roles of saliva in chewing, digestion, and debris clearance, which help reduce the accumulation of pathogenic bacteria.(Belstrøm et al. 2021) We hypothesize that salivary microbes may exert protective effects by modulating inflammation through specific metabolites or signaling pathways. Salivary proteins may also synergistically contribute to these protective functions, positioning them as potential protective factors against periodontitis. (Marcotte and Lavoie 1998).

Conversely, dysbiotic tongue microbiota may interact with subgingival microbiota, promoting CP progression. With its papillae-rich structure, the tongue can harbor a complex microbiota protected by biofilms. This environment facilitates the growth of pathogenic microbes, potentially accelerating CP progression through interactions with subgingival plaque. Previous literature has reported that tongue surface can harbor periodontal pathogens in comparison to other mucous membranes. (Marcotte and Lavoie 1998; Velden et al. 1986).

Salivary Neisseria. meningitidis was identified as protective against CP in our study. As part of the normal oral microbiota, Neisseria. meningitidis is known to contribute to maintaining periodontal ecosystem stability. (Mager et al. 2003) However it has been shown to mimic other periodontal pathogens by influencing host iron metabolism, secreting immune-regulatory factors, or enhancing biofilm formation, potentially accelerating inflammation and tissue destruction. (Watle et al. 2023) It could regulate immune responses to mitigate inflammation, yet it might exacerbate tissue damage under certain pathological conditions. For example, studies have shown that Neisseria. meningitidis adhesion structures (PilE) bind host pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-8), potentially alleviating inflammation-related tissue damage to some extent. Furthermore, its lipooligosaccharides (LOS) may influence immune homeostasis by balancing pro-inflammatory (IL-1β) and anti-inflammatory (IL-10) cytokines. (Watle et al. 2023) These findings suggest that Neisseria meningitidis may initially confer protective effects but later shift to support pathogenic microbes during inflammation, accelerating disease progression. Further research is needed to clarify its specific mechanisms in CP. Similarly, Streptococcus vestibularis appears closely linked to periodontal health. It is enriched in healthy individuals and positively associated with beneficial metabolic pathways, such as carbohydrate metabolism, that support microbial homeostasis and inhibit pathogenic overgrowth. (Macpherson and Dawes 1991) Its metabolic activity, including urea metabolism, may increase local pH, inhibiting the formation of acidic environments that damage periodontal tissues. (Macpherson and Dawes 1991) Its enrichment in healthy gingival tissues further supports its protective role. (Bao et al. 2020) While limited research exists on Lancefieldella, it is believed to play dual roles in health and disease. In health, Lancefieldella may contribute to maintaining oral microbial balance, as part of the normal microbiota.(Veras et al. 2023) However, certain species, such as Lancefieldella rimae, have been associated with persistent or secondary root canal infections, suggesting a potential role in disease under pathological conditions. (Park et al. 2024) Therefore, although the role of Lancefieldella in maintaining a healthy microecology is worthy of recognition, the pathogenesis of Lancefieldella under pathological conditions still needs to be further studied.

In contrast, tongue-associated pathogens highlighted in our study are known to promote inflammatory tissue damage through toxin secretion and disruption of host barrier functions. For instance, Solobacterium species are strongly associated with oral diseases, particularly halitosis and periodontitis. They produce volatile sulfur compounds (VSCs) like hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and methyl mercaptan (CH₃SH), which not only cause halitosis but also exert toxic effects on oral tissues, contributing to CP progression. (Bachtiar et al. 2022; Vancauwenberghe et al. 2013) Solobacterium moorei abundance correlates with deep periodontal pockets and halitosis indices in CP patients, highlighting its potential pathogenic role.(Bachtiar et al. 2022) Similarly, Fusobacterium sp000235465, a recently identified strain, provides new insights into the well-established pathogenic role of Fusobacteria genera in CP. Haemophilus parainfluenzae has been linked to Crohn's disease-related gut inflammation and may exacerbate inflammation in CP by facilitating its colonization in the gut, intensifying systemic inflammatory responses. (Vancauwenberghe et al. 2013) It also plays a role in oral microbial dysbiosis and is associated with various oral diseases, including CP and microbiota-related gut alterations. (Sohn et al. 2023).

Our findings offer a novel perspective on the dual roles of oral microbiota in CP pathogenesis. By employing MR analysis, this study bridges the gaps in traditional correlation-based research, providing direct evidence for causal relationships between oral microbiota and CP. Unlike studies based on single data sources, this research integrated high-quality GWAS datasets (e.g., CNGBdb and FinnGen) and mitigated confounding factors and reverse causality, enhancing the generalizability and robustness of the conclusions. Sensitivity tests (e.g., MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO) further confirmed the reliability of the findings.

However, our study has certain limitations. Despite using high-quality GWAS data, heterogeneity in defining CP (e.g., ICD vs. CDC criteria) across datasets may introduce bias. Additionally, the periodontitis data were primarily derived from the Eurasian population and the oral microbiome from the Chinese, limiting generalizability to other ethnicities and regions. The SNPs that influence the composition of the oral microbiome may be entirely different, or may have different effect sizes, in Chinese populations compared to European populations. This is due to differences in linkage disequilibrium (how SNPs are correlated with each other) and allele frequencies across ancestries. Further MR analysis depends on the validity of instrumental variables, which may overlook key microbial genetic variations. Moreover, the limited number of SNPs for certain significant results could lead to reduced statistical power. Residual confounding from unmeasured environmental factors (e.g., medication use, oral hygiene behaviors, lifestyle, systemic diseases and diet) could indirectly influence both the genetic instruments and the outcome, potentially biasing the causal estimates.

While our MR analysis provides robust evidence for a causal relationship, it ultimately generates hypotheses that require validation through direct experimentation. By identifying specific causal microbes, it allows for the exploration and development of targeted diagnostic tests that stratify patient risk, facilitating early interventions like precision probiotics or antimicrobials long before clinical symptoms appear thereby a paradigm shift from reactive treatment to proactive, personalized prevention in oral healthcare. Furthermore, providing patients with tangible evidence of their high-risk microbial profile serves as a powerful motivator for behavioral change and adherence to personalized care plans. Ultimately, these findings pave the way for novel therapeutics and continuous microbiome monitoring to maintain oral health and preempt disease onset.

Conclusion

Our study provides the first MR-based evidence for causal relationships between salivary and tongue microbiome and CP, offering new insights into the etiology of periodontal disease. The results highlight the potential protective or pathogenic roles of specific salivary and tongue microbes in CP. These findings provide a theoretical basis for developing microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies, such as probiotics and microbiome replacement therapies, and pave the way for personalized prevention and treatment approaches for periodontal diseases. Future research should prioritize interventional studies in model systems to functionally characterize the mechanisms by which the identified microbial taxa influence periodontal health or disease.
