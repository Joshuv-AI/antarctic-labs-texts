Acute kidney disease in mice is associated with early cardiovascular dysfunction
Authors: Pauline Caillard, Youssef Bennis, Cédric Boudot, Denis Chatelain, Pierre Rybarczyk, Agnès Boullier, Sabrina Poirot, Dimitri Titeca-Beauport, Sandra Bodeau, Gabriel Choukroun, Saïd Kamel, Isabelle Six, Julien Maizel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are major health concerns due to their increasing incidence and high mortality. They are interconnected syndromes; AKI without recovery evolves into acute kidney disease (AKD), which can indicate an AKI-to-CKD transition. Both AKI and CKD are associated with a risk of long-term cardiovascular complications, but whether vascular and cardiac dysfunctions can occur as early as the AKD period has not been studied extensively. In a mouse model of kidney injury (KI) with non-recovery, we performed vasoreactivity and echocardiography analyses on days 15 (D15) and 45 (D45) after KI. We determined the concentrations of two major gut-derived protein-bound uremic toxins known to induce cardiovascular toxicity—indoxyl sulfate (IS) and para-cresyl sulfate (PCS)—and the levels of inflammation and contraction markers on D7, D15, and D45. Mice with KI showed acute tubular and interstitial kidney lesions on D7 and D15 and chronic glomerulosclerosis on D45. They showed significant impairment of aorta relaxation and systolic-diastolic heart function, both on D15 and D45. Such dysfunction was associated with downregulation of the expression of two contractile proteins, αSMA and SERCA2a, with a more pronounced effect on D15 than on D45. KI was also followed by a rapid increase in IS and PCS serum concentrations and the expression induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and endothelial adhesion molecules in serum and cardiovascular tissues. Therefore, these results highlight that AKD leads to early cardiac and vascular dysfunctions. How these dysfunctions could be managed to prevent cardiovascular events deserves further study.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) occurs frequently in conventional hospital departments and intensive care units (ICUs) [1]. Kidney function does not recover fully for 40 to 50% of the survivors of AKI that occurs in the ICU and progresses to acute kidney disease (AKD) and then chronic kidney disease (AKI-to-CKD transition) [2,3]. Several factors have been implicated in the progression to CKD after AKI, depending on the patient (cardiovascular risk factors, preexistent CKD) and AKI (etiology, severity, duration) characteristics [4,5]. AKI is also associated with long-term cardiovascular events and mortality, but few pathological mechanisms have yet been demonstrated [6,7]. As AKI, AKD is associated with early mortality and short and long-term kidney function decline, especially in the common context of AKI following cardiac surgery [8]. Moreover, it is still unclear whether the cardiovascular events that follow an AKI without recovery are due to early cardiovascular damage developed during the AKD period or whether they are complications of subsequent CKD [9].

The 5/6 nephrectomy model is an experimental model of definitive kidney injury (KI) initially used to explore the pathological effects of CKD, in particular, cardiovascular, six or more weeks after KI [10,11]. Uremia-associated vascular dysfunction has been shown to be characterized by both vasoreactivity and structural arterial alterations [12,13]. Uremic mice showed high pulse wave velocity (PWV), changes in aortic distensibility, and upregulation of the expression of vascular adhesion molecules (intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-1 and vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)-1) in the aorta [12–15]. A decrease in alpha-smooth muscle actin (αSMA) expression in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) has also been shown to correlate with an increase in PWV in CKD rats, underlying the role of this contractile protein in the dysfunction of vasorelaxation [16]. In addition, uremic mice show high blood pressure, diastolic cardiac dysfunction, and left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), associated with a decrease in the expression of sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase 2a (SERCA2a), a cardiomyocyte contractile protein [12,14,15,17–20]. However, in the 5/6 nephrectomy model, it is still unclear whether cardiovascular dysfunction appears before six weeks following KI and whether it is potentially linked to AKD or appears at a later time point due to the development of the CKD-related uremic state. The uremic state is characterized by the accumulation of gut-derived protein-bound uremic toxins (PBUTs), including indoxyl sulfate (IS) and para-cresyl sulfate (PCS), as well as pro-inflammatory cytokines, which are known to have deleterious cardiovascular consequences during CKD [21,22]. They are directly associated with endothelial dysfunction through the induction of oxidative stress and the upregulation of the expression of pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic molecules and are involved in cardiac diastolic dysfunction and LVH [23–26]. However, it is yet to be determined how their concentration evolve during AKD as compared to the cardiovascular dysfunction.

The aim of this study was to describe the early vascular and cardiac consequences of irreversible KI and to measure the accumulation kinetics for two major PBUTs, IS and PCS, and the early expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, endothelial adhesion molecules, and contractile markers in the aorta and heart.

Materials and methods

An expanded methods section is provided in the supplementary materials.

Experimental procedure for KI induction

Animal studies were performed on eight-week-old wildtype (WT) female C57BL/6J mice (Charles Rivers Laboratories, Lyon, France). The protocol design is shown in Figure 1. Mice were housed in polycarbonate cages in rooms with controlled temperature and humidity under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with free access to water and regular laboratory chow (Diet 2016, Harlan, Oxon, UK). KI was induced by cortical electrocauterization of the right kidney and left total nephrectomy two weeks later, as previously described [12]. They were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine and xylazine (80 and 8 mg/kg, respectively) before each operation and received a subcutaneous injection of analgesic (buprenorphine 0.15 mg/kg) after each operation. Mice were randomly assigned to Sham and uremic (KI) groups (n = 35 each). Twenty-eight mice (14 in each group) were used to analyze vascular dysfunction and the other 42 for the remaining evaluations. At the end of the experiments (day 7 (D7), day (D15), and day 45 (D45) after the second surgical intervention), mice (n = 14 for each time point) were euthanized with an overdose of ketamine and xylazine (100 and 20 mg/kg) and blood samples and tissues were collected. A portion of the heart, aorta, and kidney tissue was removed and stored at −80 °C and the rest fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (VWR France, 9701.5000) for histological analysis.

Protocol design detailing the functional explorations and the biological and tissue experiments conducted at D0, D7, D15 and D45.

Vascular reactivity and trans-thoracic echocardiography

Vascular reactivity was determined on D15 and D45. Each aorta was sectioned into 3.5-mm rings devoid of fat and connective tissue. The rings were placed in Kreb’s-Henseleit solution under an atmosphere consisting of 5% CO2 and 95% O2 at 37 °C. Aortic rings were maintained under a tension of 1.1–1.2 g (previously shown to be the optimal point in the length-tension relationship) and allowed to equilibrate for 60 min. They were then constricted by incubation with KCl (70 mM) to assess their vascular functionality and washed out to ensure a return to resting levels. The rings were then induced to contract in a phenylephrine solution (cumulative concentration of 10−9 to 3 × 10−5 M) and then relax by incubation with acetylcholine (ACh, from 10−9 to 3 × 10−5 M). The contractile response to phenylephrine is expressed as the percentage of contraction achieved in the KCl solution.

All mice underwent trans-thoracic echocardiography (TTE) on D15 and D45 with a commercially available echocardiograph (Philips Agilent 4500, Philips, Massy, France) and a 12-MHz transducer probe. TTE examinations were performed under isoflurane inhalation (vaporizer) general anesthesia at a heart rate of 400 to 450 bpm. The following parameters were measured in M-mode according to the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines [27]: left ventricular (LV) end-diastolic dimension, LV end-systolic dimension, diastolic posterior wall thickness, and diastolic septal wall thickness. Fractional shortening (FS), LV mass, systolic expansion rate of the aorta (ESAo), and isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT) formulas are presented in the supplementary materials.

Serum biochemistry, UTs, cytokines, and circulating adhesion molecules

Serum urea, creatinine, and calcium concentrations, the hematocrit, and hemoglobin and leukocyte levels were measured using an autoanalyzer. IS and PCS levels were determined using a validated high-performance liquid chromatography method [28]. Pro-inflammatory cytokine (interleukins (IL-1β, IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)α) concentrations were measured by Luminex immunoassay (Bio-Plex Pro Mouse Cytokine 6-plex, M6000007NY, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) using a magnetic bead system. Soluble ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 levels were determined by ELISA (Mouse ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 ELISA kits, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, # EMICAM1ALPHA and # EMVCAM1).

Reverse transcription and real-time quantitative PCR

We evaluated the influence of KI on the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and contractile markers (αSMA and SERCA2a) in thoracic aorta and heart tissues. RNA was extracted using an RNeasy® Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using a High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (4368813, Applied Biosystems™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed with Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (4367659, Applied Biosystems™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The primers are detailed in the supplementary materials.

Western-blot analysis

Heart and aorta tissue were ground in lysis RIPA buffer on ice and incubated for 4 h with continuous stirring. After centrifugation and quantification, 50 µg of the extracted protein (mixed with Laemmli buffer) was subjected to SDS-PAGE for 2 h at 90 V in running buffer. The proteins were then transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane in transfer buffer for 1 h at 100 V. After blocking the membranes, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies directed against IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, ICAM-1, VCAM-1, αSMA, SERCA2a, and GAPDH. After four washes, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the secondary antibodies and quantified. The antibodies and solution compositions are detailed in the supplementary materials. Uncropped, unedited blots are also included in the supplementary materials.

Histological analysis of the kidney, heart, and aorta after KI

Kidney and heart tissues were embedded in paraffin and cut into 3 µm-thick sections. The sections were stained using an automated staining system (Tissue TekPrisma®Plus, 61720117-0219, Sakura, Torrance, CA, USA). Periodic acid-Schiff and Masson trichrome staining were used for kidney analysis and hematoxylin-phloxin-safran staining for that of the heart. Histological quantification for the heart and each kidney compartment (glomerular, tubular, and interstitial) was carried out using a Leica DMR microscope (Wetzlar, Germany). Tubular and interstitial damage was quantified as the percentage of total cortical area of the biopsy according to the Banff Classification [29].

Aorta tissue was embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature compound (Tissue-Tek® O.C.T.™ Compound, #4583, Sakura, Torrance, CA, USA) and frozen. Aorta tissue was cut into 4 µm-thick sections and sections were incubated with peroxidase blocking reagent for 5 min, washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and then incubated for 60 min at room temperature with primary antibodies against ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and an endothelial cell marker (CD31). Sections were washed and incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 15 min. They were then rinsed in PBS and incubated with peroxidase-labeled streptavidin for 30 min, followed by incubation in 2% 3,3 -diaminobenzidine in substrate buffer for a few minutes. Hematoxylin was used as a counterstain. Sections were dehydrated by incubation in a series of alcohol dilutions, mounted in VectaMountTM mounting media (Vector® H-5000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and covered with a coverslip. Cross-sections were studied to quantify CD31, ICAM-1, and VCAM-1 immunostaining at the luminal surface. In each section, the percentage of the area covered by CD31, VCAM-1, and ICAM-1 was measured using Histolab software (Microvision Instruments, Evry, France). The antibodies are detailed in the supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

The results are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Relaxation responses are expressed as the percentage decrease in maximum vascular phenylephrine-induced tension. The significance of the differences of the mean values between the groups was assessed by two-way ANOVA with group and concentration as factors. We compared TTE parameters, UT, cytokine, and adhesion molecule levels in the serum and tissues, and the levels of contractile molecules in tissues between the groups using unpaired t tests or one-way ANOVA with a post-hoc Tukey test. All tests were two-tailed and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 6.01, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Early vascular dysfunction

We assessed early vascular dysfunction following KI by analyzing the vasoreactivity of aortas on D15, a time point by which the inflammatory reaction due to the surgical intervention should have weakened. Vasoconstriction induced by phenylephrine (expressed as the percentage of KCl contraction) did not differ between the Sham and KI groups (Figure 3A and C). On D15, acetylcholine-induced aorta relaxation was significantly weaker in the KI than Sham group (Figure 3B). This effect persisted until D45 (Figure 3D), but the difference in acetylcholine-induced relaxation between the KI and Sham groups was more pronounced on D15 than on D45 (Figure 3E). In addition, the ESAo, measured by TTE, was lower for the KI group on D15 (Figure 3F).

Early cardiac dysfunction

We assessed the KI-induced impairment of LV function by performing TTE on D15 and D45 on mice from the Sham and KI groups (Figure 4). FS was significantly lower on D15 and D45 in the KI mice compared to Sham mice (Figure 4A). The IVRT was significantly higher in the KI than Sham mice on D15 with the same trend on D45 (Figure 4B). Uremic toxins accumulate early in the serum

Serum IS and PCS concentrations on D7, D15, and D45 are presented in Table 1. The concentration of both PBUTs was significantly higher for the KI than Sham mice, but the kinetics of accumulation differed between these two molecules. IS was the first toxin to accumulate, on D7, with levels stabilizing between D15 and D45. The increase in PCS concentration occurred later, with significant accumulation observed on D15, and continued to increase until D45.

Early expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in tissues

We therefore assessed the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the serum and the aortas and hearts of mice from the Sham and KI groups. Serum concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα on D7, D15, and D45 were similar for the mice of the Sham and KI groups (Figs S1-S3A). Conversely, both transcript and protein levels for these pro-inflammatory cytokines were significantly higher in the aortas of KI mice on D15 than in those of Sham mice at the same time point, with a similar pattern in the left ventricles (Figure 5 and Figs S1-S3B-G). Transcript levels changed similarly for all three cytokines, with no significant effect of KI detected on D7 or D45 compared to Sham, even though transcript levels for aortic IL-6 (D45) and heart IL-1β (D7) and TNFα (D7 and D45) tended to be higher in KI mice. However, protein levels in the aorta on D7 were higher for the three cytokines in KI mice, with the same tendency in heart. On D45, protein levels in left ventricle were also more elevated in KI mice than those of Sham mice. Histological analysis of the myocardia showed no evidence of lesions affecting myocytes (changes in shape, cell detachment, or apoptosis) or artery cells and no infiltration of inflammatory cells or fibrosis in the KI group relative to the Sham group (Fig S4).

Early expression of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 in the serum and aorta

Endothelium activation is a critical pathogenic mechanism in AKI-associated organ damage. We therefore explored serum and aortic levels of two endothelial adhesion molecules, ICAM-1 and VCAM-1. Serum ICAM-1 concentration was not different in KI mice compared to Sham mice on D15 but was significantly higher on D45. Serum VCAM-1 concentrations were more elevated on D15 and D45 for the KI groups (Fig S5). Histological analysis of aortic tissue confirmed the integrity of the endothelium by CD31 immunostaining (data not shown) and showed more pronounced expression of ICAM-1, on D15 and D45 for KI mice than Sham mice, but no change in VCAM-1 expression (Figure 6). RT-PCR analysis showed also higher aortic ICAM-1 transcript levels in KI mice on D15 compared to Sham mice, but not on D7 and D45 (Figure 7). The expression pattern of VCAM-1 transcript levels was similar to ICAM-1 (Figure 7). ICAM-1 protein levels were also significantly higher in KI mice than in Sham mice on D7, D15 and D45, whereas VCAM-1 protein levels did not differ significantly between the two groups at the three time points (Fig S6).

In the aorta, αSMA transcript and protein levels on D7, D15, and D45 were lower for KI than Sham mice (Figures 8A, C, E, and G). Discussion

We describe the cardiovascular disorders that develop shortly after 5/6 nephrectomy. These disorders included altered vasorelaxation, with upregulation of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, altered LV diastolic and systolic functions, and a decrease in aortic αSMA and cardiac SERCA2a levels, with peak dysfunction on D15 post-KI. We also observed significant accumulation of IS and PCS in the serum and the overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in cardiovascular tissues.

As expected, our 5/6 nephrectomy mouse model showed a rapid and sustained increase in serum urea and creatinine concentrations. However, despite the definitive nature of the renal damage, we detected no histological lesions resembling those typically observed in CKD (glomerular sclerosis, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis) in the residual renal tissue of our mice before D45. We therefore considered D7 and D15 in this model to correspond to AKD. Early vascular outcomes were characterized by upregulation of the expression of the endothelial adhesion molecule ICAM-1 on D7, associated with strong impairment of endothelium-dependent relaxation of the aorta on D15, indicating significant endothelial activation from D7 post-KI. Such dysfunction has already been reported in experimental studies on AKI, particularly in the heart, but the mechanisms involved were not fully understood [30,31]. Our KI mice presented early alterations of both systolic and diastolic parameters, shown by TTE, with no evidence of structural cardiac lesions. However, early vascular and cardiac dysfunction was associated with changes in the levels of contractile proteins. The expression of SERCA2a, a calcium pump that plays a crucial role in cardiomyocyte contraction-relaxation [32], was downregulated in the heart on D15 and D45 after KI. This finding is consistent with those of several reports on CKD highlighting the role of SERCA2a in cardiac dysfunction [14,19,20,33]. Indeed, Kennedy et al. showed cardiac contractile dysfunction and changes in calcium cycling associated with a decrease in SERCA2a expression at four and six weeks in CKD rats [20]. Junho et al. also recently demonstrated a decrease in cardiac SERCA2a expression on D7 and D15 after kidney ischemia/reperfusion injury in mice, associated with contractile dysfunction in isolated cardiomyocyte and pro-arrhythmogenic events [34,35]. We also observed early downregulation of the expression of αSMA in our study, a key protein of VSMC contractility [36], in KI mice, as reported during CKD [16,37,38], associated with an increase in arterial stiffness [16]. Moreover, in a mouse 5/6 nephrectomy model, Madsen et al. found an association between decreased αSMA expression on D15 and subsequent aortic dysfunction at 12 weeks in uremic mice, but did not investigate early vascular dysfunction [37]

Here, we report, for the first time, the early accumulation of two key PBUTs, IS and PCS, in the serum, with a significant increase in IS levels observed from D7 post-KI. Two clinical studies have evaluated the kinetics of the accumulation of UTs. In the first study on patients developing AKI after cardiac surgery, IS levels increased rapidly during the first seven days of AKI and peaked earlier than PCS levels, as in our study [39]. Similarly, in the second study, IS and PCS levels increased significantly four days after the diagnosis of AKI in ICU patients with sepsis [40]. IS is one of the most important UTs involved in the deleterious effects on cardiovascular function during CKD [24,26,41,42]. Several experimental and human studies have also investigated the cardiovascular toxicity of IS during the first three days after AKI [31,43,44]. They demonstrated that IS induces oxidative stress, monocyte adhesion in the endothelial cell layer, and a reduced LV ejection fraction. Such a decrease in vasorelaxation was also observed ex vivo after acute IS exposure [45]. In addition, the use of AST-120, an oral carbon adsorbent known to decrease IS levels, improved vascular and cardiac function [31]. Increases in PCS levels have also been shown to be associated with cardiovascular dysfunction during CKD [25,46]. Moreover, acute vascular exposure to PCS was shown to provoke constriction of the thoracic aorta in mice through rho-kinase signaling associated with oxidative stress in endothelial cells and VSMCs [47]. Our results showed that the accumulation kinetics differed between IS and PCS. This could be explained by the fact that renal failure may differently affect the production of PBUTs via an alteration of the intestinal microbiota [48,49], and their elimination by modulating the expression of renal tubular transporters [50–52].

In addition to the accumulation of PBUTs, we observed an acute cardiovascular inflammatory state in our KI mice, characterized by the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, with a peak on D15. The cytokines produced are known to be overexpressed in the context of CKD and AKI and to be associated with acute cardiovascular effects in experimental AKI models [53]. TNFα and IL-1β have been shown to cause cardiomyocyte apoptosis and upregulation of E-selectin expression, respectively [43]. Moreover, the use of an IL-1 receptor antagonist decreased ventricular arrhythmias and increased SERCA2a levels in a model of myocardial infarction [54]. In contrast to other studies, we did not directly demonstrate an interaction between cytokines and PBUTs, but IS and IL-1β levels followed the same pattern of change in the serum and tissues in our KI mice, increasing from D7 and peaking on D15. ICAM-1 levels also increased early in the aorta, from D7, in our KI mice. Adhesion molecules are known to be associated with cardiovascular events and to accelerate atherosclerosis during CKD by the recruitment of leukocytes to the vascular wall [12,55,56]. These molecules may be induced by both pro-inflammatory cytokines and PBUTs [31,43]. In a study of Shen et al. patients with AKI showed high IS, IL-1β, and sICAM-1 concentrations associated with ICAM-1 overexpression in vitro in endothelial cells after exposure to IL-1β and IS [31]. Overall, these results suggest early endothelial dysfunction during AKI potentially promoted by the accumulation of PBUTs, particularly IS, in serum. Conversely, data showing a direct link between PBUTs and the downregulation of αSMA and SERCA2a are lacking. Only one study has explored the role of PBUTs in vascular contractile marker expression, showing a decrease in αSMA expression on D14 in aortic smooth muscle cells exposed to IS [38]. However, it is difficult to directly implicate PBUTs in cardiovascular dysfunction in AKI studies or to differentiate their pathological effects from those of other pathways, such as those induced by pro-inflammatory cytokines, because these pathways are activated at the same time and probably interact with each other [9]. Based on known interactions between these molecules, it is possible that PBUTs, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and endothelial adhesion molecules act together during AKD, leading to cardiovascular dysfunction.

Several studies have investigated the short- and long-term cardiovascular outcomes of an episode of AKI but little is known about the evolution of cardiovascular function during the AKD period [6,7,31,57]. The analyses performed on D7, D15, and D45 in our study made it possible to compare early and later time points in KI that are comparable to those for AKD and CKD. We observed significant cardiovascular dysfunction on D15 that persisted to D45, but we do not know whether such dysfunction would be likely to lead to late cardiovascular events. Nevertheless, our KI mice showed on average a 18% higher IVRT and a 24% lower FS than Sham mice on D15. The differences are clinically relevant given the rapidity of appearance of these LV functional abnormalities. Indeed, in the general population, a 10 ms rise of IVRT over a decade (i.e., a 12–16% increase from normal values in humans) would increase the risk of subsequent heart failure by 33% [58]. Moreover, IVRT has been reported 37.5% higher on average in patients with CKD stages 1, 2 or 3, as compared to healthy controls, and a significant 12.4% IVRT increase was shown between CKD stages 1, 2 or 3 and CKD stages 4 or 5 [59]. Furthermore, a 5% decrease in FS has been independently associated with the increased risk of cardiovascular events in peritoneal dialysis patients [60]. It will be important in future clinical studies to address the issue of cardiovascular dysfunction during AKD and evaluate its progression and the factors, such as PBUTs, that may be associated with it. Interestingly, it is notable that most of our results, such as relaxation failure, expression of IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, and endothelial adhesion molecule expression, showed the strongest phenotype on day 15 compared to acute (day 7) and chronic (day 45) time points. D15 could thus represent the peak of cardiovascular abnormalities during the early phase of the AKI-to-CKD transition. This observation suggests a potential for novel interventions or therapies during this subacute phase after AKI. Future experimental and/or clinical protocols could focus on the search for therapeutic targets to limit the accumulation of PBUTs. A first approach would be to further explore fecal microbiota transplantation to reduce the endogenous production of uremic toxins [61,62]. Chelating agents could also limit the passage of PBUTs precursors through the intestinal barrier. For example, AST-120 is able to reduce plasma concentration of IS in CKD patients, but needs to be further evaluated in terms of its impact on cardiovascular prognosis [63]. Today, hemodialysis is not effective to properly remove PBUTs, but new techniques are also in development and need to be further explored [64,65]. Finally, blocking the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), one of the targets of IS, could also be an interesting avenue to explore, in order to assess its effect on cardiovascular dysfunction during AKD [66,67].

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, we did not identify direct mechanistic links between the accumulation of uremic toxins and the observed cardiac and vascular dysfunctions. Additionally, while renal unilateral ischemia-reperfusion is a common AKI model, we used a 5/6 nephrectomy model, which is a model of massive and permanent nephron loss. However, this model is characterized by a progressive and reproducible loss of kidney function which mimics the human evolution of AKI to CKD in all mice of the KI group [3,68]. As in Dolkart’s study [69], we found signs of acute injury on D15 in the remnant kidney and signs of chronic injury afterward. Therefore, the D7, D15 and D45 time points allow us to compare early and later times of KI as AKD and CKD periods.

In conclusion, we demonstrated early vascular and cardiac dysfunctions in a mouse model of AKD. Such dysfunctions were associated with rapid accumulation of PBUTs, induction of expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and endothelial adhesion molecules from D7 and downregulation of contractile proteins αSMA and SERCA2a on D15 and D45.
