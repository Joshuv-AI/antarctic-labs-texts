Computational identification of cross-reactive TCR epitopes with ARDitox
Authors: Victor Murcia Pienkowski, Tamara Boschert, Piotr Skoczylas, Anna Sanecka-Duin, Maciej Jasiński, Bartłomiej Król-Józaga, Giovanni Mazzocco, Sławomir Stachura, Lukas Bunse, Jan Kaczmarczyk, Edward W. Green, Agnieszka Blum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCellular immunotherapies, such as those utilizing T lymphocytes expressing native or engineered T cell receptors (TCRs), have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy. However, some engineered high-affinity TCRs have caused fatal off-target immunotoxicity due to targeting epitopes later found to be expressed by both tumor cells and healthy tissues. Unfortunately, TCRs can be cross-reactive to epitopes with highly distinct sequences, making prediction difficult, and the exquisite sequence specificity of TCRs means that safety studies in mice miss human-specific epitopes.MethodsTo address this issue, we developed ARDitox, a novel in silico method based on computational immunology and artificial intelligence (AI) for predicting and analyzing potential TCR off-target toxicities. We tested the performance of ARDitox on four TCRs reported to target tumor-associated antigens, two of which are known to cause clinical immunotoxicity (MAGEA3112-120 and MAGEA3168-176 epitopes), one of which has experimentally identified off-target antigens (AFP158-166 epitope), and the last one for which no cross-reactive epitopes are known (NY-ESO-1157–165).ResultsARDitox confirmed the previously identified immunotoxic epitopes. We then expanded our analyses to a novel TCR targeting the tumor-associated antigen NLGN4X, frequently upregulated in gliomas. For this target, ARDitox identified a cross-reactive peptide that would not have been found using mouse models, highlighting the value of our computational approach.ConclusionsOur findings underscore the value of the ARDitox in silico method for the early and reliable identification of off-target epitopes for further preclinical evaluation. This platform strongly supports the development of safer TCR-mediated immunotherapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-025-06330-7.

Introduction

Immunotherapy is changing the landscape of available treatments for cancer, in particular for hematological malignancies, but increasingly also for solid tumors (Thomas et al. 2018; Tang et al. 2023; Desai et al. 2024). Among the most promising are adoptive cell therapies that use T lymphocytes engineered to express T-cell receptors (TCRs), alongside a range of antibody-based therapeutics, including TCR mimic (TCRm) antibodies and T-cell engagers like bispecific antibodies that are designed to mimic natural TCRs (Farkona et al. 2016; Duan and Ho 2021; Chervin et al. 2023; Klebanoff et al. 2023; Guidi et al. 2025). Unfortunately, like with any novel technology, several difficulties need to be addressed before such therapies become widely used. One of the main issues, which in some cases proved to be life-threatening to treated patients, is off-target immunotoxicity (Linette et al. 2013).

T-cell immunosurveillance consists of the TCR scanning of short peptides presented on the cell surface by receptors called human leukocyte antigens (HLA) (Coles et al. 2020). Importantly, only a small fraction of all peptides from the human proteome is presented by the HLA. In a multi-step process, a peptide is loaded onto the HLA and exported to the cell surface (Cruz-Tapias et al. 2013). For a given HLA of type I, it is estimated that the number of unique peptide sequences presented to T-cells is ∼1,000–25,000 (Newey et al. 2019). However, considering all theoretical peptides that could appear in the cell (e.g., derived from mutations or viral proteins), the number of putative epitopes increases to a set of > 209 (Newey et al. 2019). The human TCR repertoire consists of approximately 10⁸ unique TCR clonotypes, thus requiring each T-cell to recognize multiple epitopes to ensure a comprehensive epitope coverage (De Greef et al. 2020; Qi et al. 2014). This promiscuity in epitope recognition of a single TCR can cause lethal immunotoxicity targeted against an off-target epitope (OTE) in an autologous T cell transplantation. Moreover, the risk of cross-reactivity and potential immunotoxicity may be increased for TCRs that were engineered to stabilize the peptide HLA (pHLA)-TCR interaction and enhance their affinity (Linette et al. 2013).

Off-target toxicity has been described in two independent clinical trials in which four enrolled patients died due to cardio- and neurotoxicity (Linette et al. 2013; Morgan et al. 2013). Importantly, the sequences of the target epitope and the OTE do not necessarily have to be highly similar. The clinically relevant cases of cross-reactivity showed that a minimum of 5 identical amino acids is sufficient for off-target toxicity to occur (Dhanik et al. 2016). While testing all possible off-targets in the vast human proteome remains a formidable challenge for purely experimental approaches, innovative high-throughput methods are emerging, demonstrating growing possibilities in this field (Sharma, et al. 2025). However, it should be noted that even with these advances, the process of comprehensively testing the entire space of potential off-targets remains both labor-intensive and costly. Similarly, safety studies in mice miss off-target toxicities as only 85% of the protein-coding sequences are conserved in mice (Makałowski et al. 1996).

While significant progress has been made in the field of computational immunology, a key challenge remains in the ability to accurately and efficiently predict the safety profile of a TCR. Several landmark tools have been developed to address various aspects of TCR function, such as clustering TCRs with shared epitope specificity (e.g., GLIPH) or predicting peptide-MHC binding preferences based on TCR physicochemical properties (e.g., the method described by Dash et al.) (Glanville et al. 2017; Dash et al. 2017; Nielsen et al. 2024). These methods have proven invaluable for understanding the fundamental principles of TCR recognition. However, a critical gap exists in tools designed for comprehensive, large-scale off-target safety screening. To fill this gap, we developed ARDitox. The primary purpose of ARDitox is to predict all possible off-target peptides for a given epitope without requiring prior knowledge of a specific TCR's binding sequence, positioning it as a crucial upstream tool for in vitro safety assessment. By leveraging advances in computational immunology and AI, ARDitox analyzes a vast number of potential OTEs and prioritizes those with the highest probability of being dangerous, thereby narrowing down the search space for subsequent, resource-intensive in vitro validation. This approach aims to increase the number of safe and effective TCRs available for TCR-based therapies.

Furthermore, ARDitox is highly versatile; when empirical data on a given TCR's binding pattern (e.g., from X-scan or alanine scan) is available, it can leverage this information to make more refined, TCR-specific off-target predictions. This unique functionality allows ARDitox to serve as a complementary tool to existing methods, helping to bridge the gap between initial target selection and final therapeutic TCR validation.

Methods

Identification of all putative off-target sequences

Identification of all putative off-target sequences

Identification of all putative off-target sequences

ARDitox takes as input a target epitope from 8 to 11 amino acids long and its corresponding HLA type. The first step of the algorithm consists of the identification of all epitopes that have at least 5 amino acids shared with the target epitope. Importantly, only OTEs of the same length are taken into account, as it is considered rare for epitopes of different lengths to bind to the same T (Ekeruche-Makinde et al. 2013). This step generates a large number of putative OTEs, e.g., for an 8 amino acid target epitope, there are combinatorially 459,360 possible putative OTEs according to:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum\limits_{k = 0,1 \ldots n} \frac{N !}{(N - k)!k!}{20^{k} }$$\end{document}where k is the number of substituted amino acids, n is the maximum number of substituted amino acids, so 5 of them are shared with the target epitope, and N is the length of the epitope. Each of these putative OTEs is then checked for presence in the human reference proteome. The reference for the above-mentioned search can be found on the UniProt website (The UniProt Consortium et al. 2021) (https://www.uniprot.org/proteomes/UP000005640).

2.Addition of single-nucleotide variant epitopes

2.Addition of single-nucleotide variant epitopes

Addition of single-nucleotide variant epitopes

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are a major source of OTEs, as a given human genome contains ~ 7000 nonsynonymous germline SNPs (Kwak et al. 2017). As such, SNPs can be a major source of novel off-target sites in TCR-based therapies. Unfortunately, both rare and frequent SNPs are not included in the UniProt proteome reference sequences. ARDitox tackles this problem by accounting for frequent nonsynonymous mutations (occurring in more than 1% of at least one studied population) from the gnomAD database (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org (Chen, et al. 2022). gnomAD was selected as the sole database because it is the largest publicly available resource for human germline variant frequencies, aggregating high-quality genome and exome sequences from diverse populations. Its comprehensive coverage makes the inclusion of other datasets largely redundant for identifying frequent variants using the 1% threshold. An allele frequency of 1% is a widely accepted threshold in human genetics to distinguish between a common genetic polymorphism and a rare mutation (Saint Pierre and Genin 2014; Goswami et al. 2021). Variants with a frequency above this threshold are generally considered to be stable and prevalent within a population, making them relevant candidates for minor histocompatibility antigens that could provoke an immune response in a significant portion of a population. Therefore, we included only nonsynonymous mutations occurring in more than 1% of at least one studied population based on putative OTEs generated in the previous step. Each identified nonsynonymous frequent SNP occurring in the sequence corresponding to the OTEs is taken into account through an additional putative off-target epitope. For the OTEs derived from SNP, their frequency in the largest human subpopulation is provided in the final results. This step further increases the number of OTEs to be analyzed, making the algorithm more sensitive.

3.Selection of presented epitopes

3.Selection of presented epitopes

Selection of presented epitopes

The preceding steps yield a large pool of potential OTEs, the vast majority of which will not be bound or presented by class I HLA molecules. To focus on candidates with a higher likelihood of biological relevance, we filter for epitopes with predicted high or intermediate binding affinity of 2000 nM and predicted surface presentation. Binding affinity is evaluated using MHCflurry (O’Donnell et al. 2018), while surface presentation is assessed with an in-house–developed model (Mazzocco et al. 2021). The model is based on machine learning methods and trained on curated, publicly available datasets (Abelin et al. 2017; Di Marco et al. 2017; Sarkizova et al. 2020). The datasets consist of the results of mass-spectrometry experiments conducted on monoallelic human cell lines. The presentation model is based on artificial neural networks and uses both the peptide sequence and the HLA type as separate inputs. Overall, this model can be used to generate predictions for any canonical class I HLA (i.e., A, B, and C). The output consists of the pHLA probability of being presented at the cell surface. Only OTEs with a probability of being presented > 50% proceed to the next steps. This relatively relaxed threshold was chosen to ensure that all plausible off-target epitopes for subsequent analysis are captured. The primary goal of our pipeline is to identify and rank potential safety risks, not to definitively rule them out at this early stage. T-cell activation can occur at a wide range of binding affinities, and a more stringent cutoff might exclude a weak-binding but still immunogenic epitope, especially when its expression is high, as it has been shown that a tenfold increase in expression could approximately compensate for a 90% decrease in binding potential (Abelin et al. 2017).

4.Off-target epitopes ranking

4.Off-target epitopes ranking

Off-target epitopes ranking

In the target epitope, amino acids at different positions can interact with the HLA and with the TCR. For these interactions to occur, the physicochemical properties of the amino acids at certain positions must remain similar (Moise et al. 2013). In this step, we establish the positions of TCR-faced residues, depending on the HLA type the epitope binds to. The positions are based on the literature (Bijen et al. 2018; Border et al. 2019; Cameron, et al. 2013; Karapetyan et al. 2019) and database search (https://www.iedb.org/) that contains information on which residues are crucial for TCR binding. The comparison between the target epitope and the putative OTE is performed based on the differences in physicochemical properties of the TCR-facing residues. To this end, we consider physicochemical properties most relevant to the pHLA:TCR interaction that are acquired from https://www.genome.jp/. All the matrices containing these properties were linearized into vectors. The distance between each target epitope (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e)$$\end{document} and the putative OTE (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p)$$\end{document}, can be computed according to the following norm:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left| {e - p} \right| = \sqrt {\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{n} {\left( {e_{i} - p_{i} } \right)^{2} } } $$\end{document}where ei and pi represent the i-th physico-chemical property of the amino acids from peptides e and p, and n is equal to the target peptide length. The resulting score is the so-called Safety score, which is used for a final ranking of identified putative off-target epitopes. The lower the Safety score is, the more probable that this particular putative off-target epitope will be toxic. Analysis of cases like MAGEA3-TTN (Linette et al. 2013), MAGEA3-MAGEA12 (Morgan et al. 2013), AFP-RCL1 (Cai et al. 2020), and some additional unpublished data indicated that in vitro validation should focus on cases with a safety score < 5, with a high emphasis on cases with a safety score < 3.

mRNA and peptide tissue-specific expression

Finally, mRNA expression in TPMs (GTEx) (Lonsdale et al. 2013) and protein expression level (Human Protein Atlas (Uhlén et al. 2015) of the putative off-target epitopes are added in an attempt to identify tissues sensitive to off-target toxicity.

Implementation

The method was implemented using Python 3.7 (scikit-learn, pandas, and numpy) (Pedregosa et al. 2011; Reback et al. 2020; Harris et al. 2020) and R v4.0.2 (dplyr, ggplot2, and BSgenome) (Wickham et al. 2023; BSgenome (development version) 2025).

mRNA electroporation for TCR expression

TCRs were ordered from TWIST Biosciences in custom intermediate cloning vectors. In vitro transcribed RNA was generated using T7 Scribe Standard RNA IVT Kit (Biozym #150,404). RNA transfection was performed by electroporation using the 4D-Nucleofector electroporation system (Lonza).

Cell culture

Jurkat T cells (Leibnitz Institute DSMZ #ACC282) were cultured in RPMI 1640 Medium (Gibco, #61,870,143) + 10% heat-inactivated FBS (PAN-Biotech) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Capricorn Scientific #PS-B). The commercially available EBV-immortalized BOLETH cell line expressing HLA-A*02:01 was used as antigen-presenting cells. The BOLETH cells were cultured with 50 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific #11,360,039), and 1X MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco, #11,140,035).

In vitro off-target validation assay

BOLETH cells and NLGN4X-TCR expressing Jurkat cells were plated in a 1:3 ratio in a round-bottom 96-well plate, and the respective peptides were added at a final concentration of 10 µM. As a positive control, Jurkat T cells were plated in a well pre-coated with CD28 /CD3 monoclonal antibodies at a 1:400 dilution, with MOG peptide as a negative control. After 16 h of co-culture, T cell activation was assessed. Significance was calculated with one-way ANOVA in comparison to the Myelin Oligodendrocyte Glycoprotein (MOG) control peptide.

Dataset preparation

For the evaluation of our methodology, we considered three peptide groups restricted to either HLA-A02:01 or HLA-A01:01: (i) tumor-associated antigen (TAA) epitopes, (ii) known immunodominant viral epitopes, and (iii) epitopes arising from frameshift mutations (out-of-frame INDELs). TAA and viral epitopes were obtained from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) (Vita et al. 2019), while frameshift-derived epitopes were sourced from our Neo Open Reading Frame peptide library (Supplementary Table 1) (Koster and Plasterk 2019). Frameshift epitopes predicted by ARDisplay to be presented on the cell surface in the context of HLA-A02:01 were identified, and a subset of 16 was randomly selected. For an equal-sized comparator group, 16 TAAs presented by HLA-A02:01 were randomly selected from the IEDB dataset. All 16 frameshift-derived and 16 TAA epitopes were then analyzed using ARDitox to predict OTEs. The safety score distributions were compared between the OTEs derived from the two groups. A similar ARDitox-based safety score analysis was performed to compare all TAAs (148) with all viral epitopes (148) presented by the same HLA alleles, without subsampling.

Results

ARDitox estimates the safety of the putative OTEs using a Safety score that ranges from 0 to 14. A score close to 0 means that the putative OTE and the target epitope are predicted to have an almost identical pHLA:TCR interaction, and that cross-reactive binding is highly probable.

Case 1

Morgan et al. (Morgan et al. 2013) described a cell therapy using TCR-engineered T-cells against the MAGEA3112-120 epitope KVAELVHFL. The clinical study was performed in nine HLA-A*02:01-positive patients with metastatic cancer expressing both MAGEA3 and MAGEA12. Unfortunately, three patients developed severe side effects. Two patients went into a coma that ultimately resulted in their death. One patient developed Parkinson’s disease-like symptoms that lasted for four weeks after administration of the TCR-engineered T-cell (TCR-T cells). Based on molecular assays, the authors showed that genes from the same family as the target, specifically MAGEA12 and, to a lower degree, MAGEA1, MAGEA8, and MAGEA9, are expressed in the brain and most likely responsible for the off-target toxicity effects. A re-evaluation of potential OTEs by Martin et al. (Martin et al. 2021) suggested a further target: the epitope EPS8L2339-347 (SAAELVHFL) that is highly expressed in the brain.

The ARDitox pipeline identified 294 putative OTEs with a high probability of being presented at the cell surface for this case (Supplementary Table 2), with 24 OTEs being scored below 3.0 (Fig. 2A). ARDitox identified putative OTEs originating from MAGEA12, MAGEA8, MAGEA9, and EPS8L2 as the OTEs with the highest potential to generate off-target toxicities (having the lowest possible score of 0). As previously reported, MAGEA12, MAGEA8, and EPS8L2 are found to be expressed in different parts of the brain (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2).Fig. 2Distribution of ARDitox safety scores for putative off-target epitopes (A) case 1 – MAGEA3 (KVAELVHFL), (B) case 2 – MAGEA3 (EVDPIGHLY), (C) case 3 – NY-ESO-1 (SLLMWITQV), (D) case 4 – AFP (FMNKFIYEI), (E) case 5 – Epstein-Barr BZLF1 (SELEIKRY). Case 2

Linette et al. (Linette et al. 2013) described a case of off-target toxicity in a clinical study conducted on four patients diagnosed with myeloma and melanoma. The immunotherapy was directed against the MAGEA3168-176 epitope EVDPIGHLY presented by HLA-A*01:01. The first two patients who received the therapy went into cardiogenic shock and died within days. The causative off-target epitope had not been identified in preclinical studies; later work using cultured beating cardiomyocytes revealed a Titin (TTN) derived epitope ESDPIVAQY to be responsible for the toxicity effects.

In this case, ARDitox identified 84 potential OTEs, among which nine had a safety score below 3.0 and 24 below 5.0 (Fig. 2B, Table 2). Importantly, the epitope originating from TTN was a top hit with a safety score of 0 (Supplementary Table 2). The expression of TTN mRNA and the protein itself was found to be present in muscle and cardiac cells (Fig. 2B).

Case 3

Stadtmauer et al. (Stadtmauer et al. 2019) performed a clinical study in 25 high-risk multiple myeloma patients using TCR-T cells targeting the NY-ESO-1157–164 epitope SLLMWITQV, a cancer-testis antigen with elevated expression in multiple cancer entities. TCR-T cell therapy against NY-ESO-1 is considered one of the most promising approaches for cancer immunotherapy, demonstrating improved patient survival with no observed off-target toxicity, whilst the SLLMWITQV epitope is presented on the common HLA-A*02:01 allele. For that peptide-HLA combination, ARDitox has detected 203 putative OTEs, where only one LRBA657-665 epitope, FLLMFIKQL, had a safety score below 3.0 (safety score = 2.55, Fig. 2C, Supplementary Table 2).

Case 4

Cai et al. (Cai et al.2020) tested preclinically engineered T cells against the AFP158-166 epitope FMNKFIYEI. Based on an in vitro X-scan experiment, two off-target epitopes that could have activated the TCR had been identified: ENPP1436-444 (YLNKYLGDV) and RCL1215-223 (ILNKFIPDI).

Results provided by ARDitox showed 39 putative OTEs for this target, of which eight had a safety score below 3.0. These included a previously experimentally validated OTE: RCL1215-223, which ARDitox scored as having a high off-target toxicity potential (safety score ~ 2.5, Fig. 2D, Supplementary Table 2). The mRNA expression of RCL1 was found to be present across multiple tissues. The ENPP1 peptide was not identified as a potential off-target by ARDitox. However, according to the authors, ENPP1436-444 is neither processed nor presented on HLA-A02:01.

Case 5

Rist et al. (Rist et al.2015) showed that a high proportion of CD8 + T cells recognizing an EBV epitope derived from the BZLF1173-180 (SELEIKRY) protein presented on HLA-B*18:01 cross-reacted with an epitope originating from CPSF3L (DELEIKAY). Thus, the authors hypothesized that BZLF1 serves as an example of molecular mimicry.

ARDitox identified 661 putative OTEs for BZLF1173-180 SELEIKRY. Among them, CPSF3L DELEIKAY was found with a safety score of 3.94 and was defined as one of the top 15 cross-reactive peptides (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Table 2).

ARDitox identifies cross-reactive epitopes for novel targets

Case 6

We ran the ARDitox pipeline to identify OTEs that should be tested in vitro for the HLA-A*02:01 presented epitope NLGN4X131–139 (NLDTLMTYV), a recurrent TAA in glioblastoma (Moise et al. 2013) used in the IMA950 peptide vaccine trial (Hilf et al. 2019) and for which a TCR has recently been reported (Krämer et al. 2024). This vaccine-induced TCR demonstrated strong preclinical efficacy, showing 44.4% objective response and prolonged survival in glioblastoma models. ARDitox identifies variability in OTE burden among epitope classes

TAA vs. viral epitope OTEs

We hypothesised that TAA-derived epitopes would have more putative OTEs than viral epitopes based on the fundamental distinction between self- and non-self antigens. Viral proteins, having evolved separately from the human host, generally possess less homology to self-proteins. Consequently, T-cells recognizing viral epitopes are less likely to cross-react with the host's own proteome. This principle is a cornerstone of the immune system's ability to distinguish self from non-self, a process that is reinforced by thymic negative selection (Gautam et al. 1998; Koncz et al. 2021; Spencer et al. 2015). In contrast, TAAs are derived from self-proteins, increasing their inherent likelihood of having sequence homology with other human proteins. While self-reactive T-cells are largely eliminated during thymic selection, some may escape, or their activation may be triggered in a pro-inflammatory tumor environment, leading to a breakdown of tolerance. Therefore, it is a reasonable hypothesis that epitopes from TAAs would, on average, have a larger number of off-targets compared to viral epitopes.

To evaluate the performance of the proteome-wide search for OTEs and to assess the proposed scoring methodology, we compared TAAs with viral-derived epitopes. The dataset comprised 148 TAAs and 148 viral-derived epitopes, each presented by either HLA-A02:01 or HLA-A01:01 (Spaanderman et al. 2021).

Overall, TAA-derived epitopes yielded more putative OTEs (31,854) than viral-derived epitopes (22,279). A t-test on the resulting safety scores revealed a statistically significant difference between the two groups (mean TAA = 7.39, mean viral = 7.62, p < 2.2 × 10⁻1⁶). However, the effect size was small (Cohen’s d = –0.1514) (Sawilowsky 2009), suggesting that this difference, although statistically detectable due to the large sample size, has limited practical relevance. Importantly, when focusing on the subset of OTEs with safety scores below 3 (indicating the highest risk epitopes), the TAA group showed a significant enrichment compared to viral epitopes. A Fisher’s exact test confirmed this trend (odds ratio = 4.48, 95% CI [2.29–9.80], p = 3.37 × 10⁻⁷). Furthermore, we applied a binary logistic regression to test whether epitopes with safety scores < 3 were enriched in TAAs compared to viral epitopes. The analysis further supported the result, estimating that viral OTEs were ~ 78% less likely to have safety scores < 3 than TAA-derived OTEs (odds ratio = 0.22, p = 1.03 × 10⁻5). Together, these findings highlight a disproportionate representation of potentially unsafe OTEs within the TAA category.

TAA vs. frameshift epitope OTEs

We next sought to test the hypothesis that frameshift-derived neoepitopes, owing to their entirely non-self origin, exhibit reduced homology to the human proteome and are therefore predicted to generate fewer off-target epitopes (OTEs) compared with TAAs (Sæterdal et al. 2001; Schumacher and Schreiber 2015; Kina et al. 2025).

We compared 16 frameshift-derived epitopes with 16 randomly selected TAA-derived epitopes, all predicted to be presented by HLA-A*02:01. Frameshift-derived epitopes produced markedly fewer putative OTEs (336) than TAA-derived epitopes (3,911). A t-test on safety scores showed a statistically significant difference between groups (mean TAA = 7.21, mean frameshift = 7.67, p = 2.54 × 10⁻⁶). However, the effect size was small (Cohen’s d = 0.28), indicating that the statistical significance is more likely a consequence of the large number of OTEs than a substantial difference in average scores.

To further examine potential safety differences, we tested whether frameshift-derived epitopes were depleted in OTEs with safety scores below 3 (the threshold for high-risk predictions). Both Fisher’s exact test (p = 0.62, odds ratio = 0, 95% CI [0.00–3.51]) and binary logistic regression (odds ratio ≈ 1, p = 0.99) indicated no statistically significant difference. This result was driven by the fact that no frameshift-derived OTEs fell below the safety threshold, creating a “zero-event” scenario that limits statistical power.

Taken together, these results suggest a trend toward a safer off-target profile for frameshift-derived epitopes compared to TAAs. However, due to the absence of frameshift OTEs below the risk threshold, this observation cannot be formally confirmed (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

TCR-based cancer immunotherapy (e.g., TILs, engineered TCR-T cells, TCR mimic antibodies) is emerging as a promising alternative or a complementary approach to surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy (Hunter 2017). However, before it is fully embraced as a form of cancer treatment, several shortcomings need to be addressed, including the risks of adverse therapy effects caused by off-target toxicity (García-Fernández et al. 2021). To help mitigate this problem, we have introduced ARDitox—a novel computational method that can identify off-target epitopes even when they differ significantly from the targeted epitope presented on a given pMHC.

So far, very few computational algorithms for predicting off-target binding in TCR-based cellular cancer immunotherapies have been proposed. Similarly to ARDitox, Expitope (Jaravine et al. 2017) and iVax (Moise et al. 2013) approaches start with querying the human proteome for peptides homologous to the target with a predefined number of mismatches allowed. However, iVax does not consider the presentation of recognized putative OTEs in the process of ranking them, while Expitope estimates peptide presentation on HLA by proxy using a combination of the proteasomal cleavage probability and transport associated with antigen processing, as well as HLA binding. ARDitox is therefore the first method for off-target epitope detection that leverages HLA presentation probabilities and an understanding of epitope residue orientation. Overall, ARDitox is a novel approach to identify putative OTEs with a unique pipeline that includes an in-house AI-trained pHLA presentation model, a unique scoring function focused on physico-chemical properties of the TCR-facing residues, and an extended search of peptides derived from frequent mutations.

We tested ARDitox on four clinically validated cases of TCRs targeting TAA-derived epitopes, one TCR in a preclinical stage and one virus epitope, as well as on two datasets: i) TAA vs Virus epitopes and ii) TAA vs frameshift-derived epitopes. ARDitox correctly identified the OTEs that caused the immunotoxicity in the treated patients (Table 2). Furthermore, in Case 3, where no OTEs were experimentally observed, ARDitox did identify one putative cross-reactive epitope with a safety score < 3. While this finding is interesting from a methodological standpoint, given the lack of observed off-target cross-reactivity in patients, this result should be considered a potential false positive result of our in silico screen. However, it is also critical to consider the expression profile of the predicted off-target protein. If this protein is expressed at negligible levels or in a clinically inaccessible location, this could explain the lack of observed patient cross-reactivity, even if the epitope is predicted to bind. This highlights the need for further validation of our findings using wet-lab methods and also points to areas where our technique may require further optimization to reduce the number of false positive outcomes. In case 5, we have successfully identified an OTE that might lead to autoimmunity as a result of molecular mimicry after EBV infection, showing ARDitox’s potential usefulness in the development of vaccines, while taking into account molecular mimicry. The fact that the observed clinical cross-reactivity did not meet our 3.0 cutoff is a valuable finding. It likely highlights a scenario where individual clonotypes within the polyclonal mixture may have had weak off-target scores, but their combined, cumulative effect in vivo led to a clinical outcome. Lastly, in case 6, we experimentally identified the ADH1A epitope. This off-target would not be detected in mouse models due to the lack of presentation of the orthologous Adh1-derived epitope (PLDPLITHV) (Fig. 3E). Importantly, ADH1A had a safety score close to 5, which corresponded to a weak binding of the TCR. Early identification of this OTE is valuable as additional safety measures can be considered to ensure that activation of the TCR-T cell against ADH1A and ADH1B will not occur during clinical trials.

In assessing the risk of a given TCR therapy, ARDitox serves as a tool that provides qualitative guidelines aimed at giving a comprehensive view of potential threats, rather than predicting clinical outcomes. To this end, we recommend a detailed analysis of three key variables: i) The total number of potential off-target epitopes (OTEs), which provides a general idea of the scale of the potential issue, ii) The number of OTEs with a safety score < 3, which helps identify the most probable, high-risk cases, iii) The expression levels of OTEs with a safety score < 3, which is crucial for assessing potential clinical consequences.

Furthermore, to ensure maximum safety, we highly recommend that all putative OTEs with a score < 5 be subjected to in vitro verification, as some weak TCR off-target interactions (e.g., cases 5 and 6), especially if highly expressed in crucial organs, might have negative consequences for the patient's health. It should be stressed that the importance of the variables mentioned above should not be neglected, as exemplified by TTN’s OTE in use case 2. In this case, the number of all putative OTEs was considered moderate. Based on this variable, the TCR against this target epitope seemed to be very promising; however, if the cross-reactivity of each OTE with a safety score < 3 would be checked experimentally because low safety scores indicate that the TCR may bind to both the target and the OTE similarly. Lastly, checking the expression status of each putative OTE with a safety score < 5 should indicate which tissue types are of particular interest for the experimental verification. This would have been important in case 2, as during the preclinical in vitro studies, no toxicity towards the tested heart muscle cell line was detected, as the TTN protein is expressed only in contracting cardiac myocytes (Coles et al. 2020). Identifying TTN as OTE upfront could have enforced the addition of appropriate cell lines to the test panel. One potential shortcoming of our model is that, currently, for some less frequent HLA-types, incorrect amino acids may be scored as the ones facing the TCR. However, this problem is minor as it occurs only for HLA types that have a generally low frequency. Furthermore, it can be mitigated as more data regarding TCR-faced amino acid positions for rare HLAs becomes available through various experimental and computational methods. These include experimental techniques such as X-ray crystallography, X-scan, Cryo-EM, and peptide elution data, as well as AI tools like Boltz-2 for (Passaro, et al. 2025) generating structural data. Another limitation is that a fraction of the OTEs identified by ARDitox are likely false positives, including some with safety scores below 3. This is an inherent trade-off in a tool designed to prioritize potentially dangerous off-targets for subsequent in vitro validation, rather than to replace experimental testing. Importantly, our case studies demonstrate that ARDitox tends to assign high priority rankings to known, biologically relevant off-targets, supporting its utility as an effective pre-screening tool. Additionally, we acknowledge that a formal assessment of the false negative rate is essential for a safety-focused pipeline. This remains an important direction for future work to better define model sensitivity. A key consideration of our homology-based approach is its inability to capture off-targets arising from TCR promiscuity, where recognition occurs despite little or no sequence similarity. As the basic version of ARDitox relies on homology searches, such interactions may be missed. However, by incorporating empirical TCR binding motif data (e.g., from alanine- or X-scans) or structural modeling, the ARDitox pipeline can be enhanced to make TCR-specific off-target predictions. This functionality positions ARDitox as a unique tool that complements existing methods by bridging the gap between initial target selection and final therapeutic TCR validation.

In order to assess the effectiveness of the proteome search for OTEs and the proposed scoring methodology, we compared the analysis performed on TAAs and viral epitopes. The higher number of OTEs detected by ARDitox from TAA-derived peptides compared to viral epitopes and frameshift peptides is consistent with the hypothesis that, due to their self-origin, TAAs are more likely to share homology with the human proteome. These results support our recommendation for interpreting ARDitox output. When assessing the risk of a target causing off-target toxicity, the primary focus should be on verifying the number of putative OTEs with a safety score below 3.

Lastly, we wanted to check whether frameshift mutations are promising targets for immunotherapeutic strategies, since they give rise to multiple, out-of-frame, random protein products that should not map to the reference proteome (Spaanderman et al. 2021). Furthermore, frameshift-derived epitopes usually do not share functional domains with other genes found in the human genome, and, as such, the general number of putative OTEs should be both lower and with higher safety scores. To verify this, we used a database composed of 16 frameshift-derived neoepitopes that were predicted as presented by our presentation model (Chen, et al. 2022; Vita et al. 2019). When compared to 16 TAA epitopes, the number of OTEs from frameshift neoepitopes was 10 × lower (frameshift OTEs: 336 vs TAA OTEs: 3911). Furthermore, none of the putative frameshift OTEs had a safety score < 3 (Fig. 4B). As such, the results from ARDitox suggest that, provided nonsense-mediated decay does not occur, frameshift neoepitopes may be promising alternatives to TAA epitopes in TCR therapies.

In conclusion, we have developed a method for the identification of off-target toxicity that can be successfully applied in the development of cellular immunotherapies. Our tool, ARDitox, takes into account: (i) peptide processing, (ii) pHLA binding, (iii) pHLA presentation probability, (iv) determination and similarity of TCR-faced amino acids, (v) frequent variants as a source of off-target epitopes, and (vi) mRNA and protein expression levels. Most importantly, the application of our platform, ARDitox, to process data from several use case studies allowed efficient identification of OTEs, which proves its applicability in the development of TCR-based cancer immunotherapies.
