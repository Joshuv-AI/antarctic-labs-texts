Non-sputum-based samples and biomarkers for detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis: the hope to improve childhood and HIV-associated tuberculosis diagnosis
Authors: George W Kasule, Sabine Hermans, Derrick Semugenze, Enock Wekiya, Joachim Nsubuga, Patricia Mwachan, Joel Kabugo, Moses Joloba, Alberto L García-Basteiro, Willy Ssengooba, the Stool4TB Global Partnership
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 2014, the World Health Organisation (WHO) published target product profiles (TPP) for development of novel tuberculosis (TB) diagnostics. One of the key highlights is the need for point-of-care non-sputum-based tests capable of detecting all forms of TB through identification of characteristic biomarkers or biosignatures. Compared to the limitations associated with sputum-based TB tests, non-sputum samples are easy to collect, non-invasive, with potential to improve TB diagnosis among children and among people living with HIV/AIDS (PLHIV). This review gives an overview of the existing evidence on TB diagnostic studies of non-sputum-based samples collected non-invasively from or through the oral–gastrointestinal tract (GI) and nasal pharynx regions of humans and the biomarkers detected. We further summarized evidence of these biomarkers and sample types from research done in paediatric and PLHIV. The review identified; saliva, cough aerosols, oral swabs, oral wash, dental plaque, tongue swabs, face mask sampling, exhaled breath, and stool, as the non-sputum samples investigated. These biomarkers can be categorized into Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid (DNA), Ribonucleic Acid (RNA), inflammatory, antigen–antibody, volatile and non-volatile compounds, microbiome and microbiota. The biomarkers identified were derived both from the host and pathogen. Similar biomarkers were identified in the general population, children and among PLHIV. These biomarkers have been detected by either already approved simple point of care or sophisticated devices. Differences in methodology and sample types investigated, small sample size of children and PLHIV populations, bias due to confounding factors, were some of the identified challenges in these studies. There is need to conduct larger and standardized multi centre studies to evaluate non-sputum-based biomarker-based tests in children and PLHIV.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Biomarkers, And non-sputum

Background

In 2022, about 10.6 million people fell ill with TB globally. Children and people living with HIV/AIDS (PLHIV) accounted for around 12% and 6% of the estimated total, respectively. The rate of TB-HIV co-infection is highest in the African region and exceeding 50% in some South African countries [1]. There still exist gaps in diagnosis of TB in children and among PLHIV. It is estimated that only 56% of TB individuals among PLHIV and 35% among children were reported [1]. One of the main factors contributing to this TB detection gap is the use of and reliance on sputum-based specimens and diagnostic techniques [2–5]. One of the proposed mitigations is to use non-sputum-based samples [6]. Most non-sputum samples are easy to collect, readily available, and have less safety risks in vulnerable populations of children and PLHIV.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has highlighted the importance of non-sputum-based biomarker-based tests capable of detecting TB in urine, blood, stool, saliva, mucosal transudates, and breath condensate [6]. It highlights specific target product profiles (TPP) of performance characteristics in children and PLHIV [6] (Table 1).

A biomarker is defined as any characteristic that can be objectively measured as an indicator of health or disease process or as a response to a therapeutic intervention [7]. The WHO has in the past approved non-sputum-based biomarker-based TB tests; stool Xpert MTB/RIF (Xpert) or MTB/RIF Ultra (Ultra) in children and Urine Lipoarabinomannan (LAM) antigen kit among PLHIV [8]. Sputum Xpert has limitations of suboptimal sensitivity especially in smear-negative and TB-HIV coinfected patients, infrastructure requirements including electricity and air conditioners, difficulty to interpret trace result, relatively high cost of test [6, 8]. The urine LAM test is also faced with limitations of sensitivity but it increases with severity of HIV disease and lower cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) count, and has a low specificity in children [8, 9]. Both tests have a decreased sensitivity in smear-negative TB individuals, children, PLHIV, and extra pulmonary TB [8–10] Stool Xpert as a non-sputum-based test is currently only approved for use in children [8]. There is a need to evaluate the performance of alternative non-sputum specimens, including strategies of optimizing the use of existing platforms to test non-sputum samples to detect TB.

This review aimed at giving an overview of the existing evidence on TB diagnostic studies of non-sputum-based sample types collected non-invasively from or through the oral–GI tract and nasal pharynx regions of humans, by summarizing the characteristics of the biomarker tested (host/pathogen), their diagnostic performance, and their level of development/approval in the diagnostic pipeline. A secondary objective was to summarize evidence of these biomarkers and sample types from research done specifically in paediatric and PLHIV populations.

The nasal pharynx region includes the nose and the nasopharynx top part of the throat (pharynx). The oral–GI tract region is divided into the mouth and gastrointestinal system of pharynx (throat), esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, and anus. To identify relevant articles, we searched publications indexed in PubMed and Embase from 1980 to December 2023 using terms, such as non-sputum, tuberculosis and biomarkers. The findings were summarized into a Microsoft excel® standardized data capture form.

There was limited literature on biomarkers from nasal pharynx samples. TB in this upper respiratory tract region is also rare [11]. Common samples used for diagnosis include nasal swabs, aspirates, and invasive biopsies.

Nasal swabs/aspirates: M.tb IS6110/IS1081 biomarker: Xpert and Ultra target IS6110 and IS6110/IS1081 in nasopharyngeal aspirates (NPA) in adults and children irrespective of HIV status and has been approved by the WHO in children [8]. A polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based study detected M.tb DNA IS6110 in nasal swabs from TB patients and their contacts [12]. Microbiome and microbiota biomarkers: Microbiome are the collection of genetic makeup of all the microorganisms in an environment, such as stomach or the gut. Microbiota includes the variety of the microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi found within a specific environment. Using 16S rRNA sequencing, there were significant differences in the microbiota of TB from that of M.tb infection and healthy controls in NPA samples (HC) [13]. A few biomarkers and samples from this nasal–pharynx region have been investigated and according to our knowledge, none in children and PLHIV.

ii. Mouth

ii. Mouth

Saliva: Components in saliva have showed great potential for diagnosis of measles, mumps, rubella, congenital cytomegalovirus, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [14]. Differences in the levels of markers expressed in saliva in TB patients compared to non-TB patients have been used as biosignatures for TB detection [15, 16]. Cytokine biomarker: A mini-review of host-based biomarkers in saliva showed various cytokines and other proteins and/or in combination used to detect TB in adults [14]. Other studies have also detected cytokine biomarkers in TB adult s’ saliva [15–18]. TB detection using cytokines biomarkers improved when used as a combination signature [14–16] with an 8 marker biosignatures having sensitivity and specificity of 100% and specificity of 98% (after excluding HIV), respectively, against culture reaching the target TPP. M.tb IS6110/IS1081 biomarker: Saliva testing using Xpert and Ultra reported varying sensitivity and specificity [19–23] of up to 80% and 98% against culture, respectively. A commercial kit, FluoroType DNA assay (FT-MTB assay, Hain Lifescience), had a sensitivity 56.2% and specificity 90% in sputum culture confirmed case control study [21], respectively [23]. M.tb RNA biomarker: Biomarker 16S rRNA has been detected in saliva with a sensitivity of 71.8% in sputum culture confirmed individuals using an FDA approved commercial amplification Gen-Probe amplified MTD test [21]. Antigen: A protein; 38 kDa has been detected in all 5 pulmonary TB cases on a commercial Lateral Flow Immunochromato-graphic Assay Strip and is a potential biomarker for further studies even in children and PLHIV [24].

Dental plaque: Dental plaque is a diverse community of micro-organisms found on the tooth surface as a biofilm, usually yellowish in colour and enriched in several species including bacteria embedded in a matric of organic polymers of bacterial and salivary origin [25]. The accumulation of dental plaque is reduced by improved dental hygiene mainly through tooth brushing and hence control related diseases [26]. M.tb IS6110 biomarker: A PCR-based study detected IS6110 with 100% sensitivity in TB patients confirmed by culture and X-ray [20] and 68% in clinically diagnosed TB patients with periodontitis [27]. However, the presence of M.tb in plaque and saliva might pose a grave risk of transmission of the disease through aerosols during dental procedures and clinics [27, 28].

Oral swabs: Bacilli regularly pass from lungs and airways through the mouth and attach to the surfaces of oral epithelium lining production [29–31] during exhalation, coughing and sputum collection [32]. Oral swabs include tongue, cheek swab, gum, and buccal collected from different parts of the mouth using conventional or specialised collection swabs [32, 33], harvested, e.g., using lysis buffer [30, 32] and tested using routine TB tests such as nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) [32, 33]. M.tb IS6110/RNA: Different studies used PCR to detect M.tb IS6110 with variable diagnostic performance of up to 100% sensitivity and specificity [29, 30, 34–36] [30] in addition to being detected using existing and early stage platforms [37–40]. M.tb in oral swabs has been detected in paediatric population [33, 34, 41] and among PLHIV [29, 33, 40]. However, the sensitivity in children is lower compared to adults [33] and maybe affected by collection methods. Among PLHIV, oral buccal swabs sensitivity and specificity increased to 83% and 100%, respectively [29]. The site of oral swab collection and type of collection kit has been shown to affect the diagnostic performance with buccal and tongue swabs showing highest sensitivity [29, 30, 33–36].

Oral wash: Oral wash is collected by rinsing the mouth with normal saline or sterile water for a few seconds and using the wash sample for TB diagnosis [42]. Microbiome and microbiota: TB individuals and symptomatic controls had similar alpha and beta diversities in oral washes and sputum (Genera Paludibacter and Lautropia), however, beta-diversity differed in stool. In this study, HIV did not affect microbial diversity, composition, or relative abundances in oral wash and sputum other than in stool. [43]. SecA1 gene: SecA1 gene codes for the essential protein SecA1 which is a key component of the major pathway of protein secretion across the cytoplasmic membrane of the M.tb organism [44]. This was detected by a PCR-based technique in oral wash specimens of TB individuals with a sensitivity of 52% and 83% in HIV infected and HIV negative TB patients [42]. However, oral wash has not been analyzed on existing PoC like Ultra.

Face masks—M.tb aerosol sampling: Collection of aerosols can be through different aerosol sampling systems including face mask sampling (FMS) [45–48] and captured on gelatin filter, cellulose acetate membrane for processing. [45, 49–51]. Aerosol sampling using FMS offers a new approach to understanding and diagnosing tuberculosis [45, 49]. Aerosol studies have indicated to be more sensitive compared to sputum and able to detect early stage infection [45] and live bacilli [49]. M.tb DNA/RNA from face mask: Face mask bioaerosols have been used to detect and isolate viable M.tb [46, 48] and quantify RNA, respectively [50]. Face mask samples and cough aerosols have been found to have higher DNA/RNA and bacterial load compared to sputum [45, 49, 50, 52] and have been reported to detect more cases compared to sputum test in TB/HIV co-infected participants [45]. It is worth noting that only one study has been conducted in children using modified face masks and did not detect TB DNA [53].

Exhaled breath: The composition of exhaled breath reflects the chemical process in the lungs and in other organs that is used to differentiate TB and TB free individuals. The chemical composition of the exhaled breath condensate (EBC) includes potential markers from host and pathogen [54–61]. Exhaled breath composition has been used to study lung inflammation and redox markers [56, 62]. Most commercial devices use rapid cooling devices to get EBC [57]. The EBC can be analysed using several methods including simple electronic hand held Point of Care (PoC) electronic-nose to existing laboratory TB detection [59, 60, 63–65]. A systematic review (SR) by Saktiawati, et al. highlighted the diagnostic accuracy of electronic-nose and other devices in diagnosing TB [66]. The pooled sensitivity and specificity of electronic-nose in diagnosing TB were 92.0% (95% CI 82.0–97.0) and 93% (95% CI 88.0–96.0), respectively. And that for other devices other than electronic nose, ranged from 62 to 100% and 11% to 84%, respectively. M.tb IS6110 biomarker: the DNA IS6110 target TB biomarker target has not been detected in EBC using Xpert and other conventional PCR tests in in HIV negative TB patients [19, 67, 68] and the reasons have not been well established. M.tb urease biomarker: By-products from hydrolysis of urea into ammonia and carbondioxide by M.tb urease have been detected in breath with a sensitivity of 70% and specificity of 11% in sputum culture confirmed cases and healthy volunteers [69]. M.tb Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) biomarker: VOCs have high vapor pressure at ambient conditions [70]. The principle of detecting of VOCs is through; the ability of biomarker complexing with transition metals at particular valence state, direct oxidation [71], microarrays (e-nose), and oxidative stress. The quantifiable changes are made into a detectable unique breath signal (or “breathprint”) used by pattern recognition algorithms [72]. A number of biomarkers have been detected in breath of TB patients commonly using electronic noses and GC/MS [60, 73–78]. In children, a breath-print reached the WHO triage test TPP specification and identified more unconfirmed clinical TB [76]. In TB-HIV coinfected patients, a sensitivity and specificity of at least 90% was reached [63, 65]. However, these promising sensitivities are tested using sophisticated GC/MS techniques. Non-volatile organic M.tb biomarkers: Lipid-specific M.tb derived biomarkers of lipoarabinomannan, lipoglycan, and proteins have been detected in EBCs of confirmed TB individuals in adults (non-HIV) and children [79]. A total of 22 metabolites and lipid biomarkers were associated with Xpert positive TB individuals [61]. Considering LAM biomarker, the sensitivity and specificity was 93% and 98%, respectively, against sputum culture [79]. There was similarity in proportions of positive cases detected by EBC–LAM in HIV positive and HIV negative TB individuals using LAM Ag test kit (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA) [80]. More research is required for use of LAM biomarker in PLHIV and children and general population without algorithm restrictions. Exhaled nitric oxide (eNO): measurement of eNO had a sensitivity and specificity of 78% and 62%, respectively, in smear positive, culture-positive TB patients [81]. Lowered exhaled nitric oxide in exhaled air was an indicator of HIV and history of M.tb infections in children [82]. Antigens–Antibodies: A proof of concept study detected immunoglobulin A (IgA); total IgG and IgA, IgA and IgG specific to ESAT-6 and Psts1, in microdroplet of lung fluid collected from exhaled air using inhouse manufactured electrospun filters [83]. In the same study, neither M.tb cells nor M.tb DNA were detected in these samples.

iii. Gastrointestinal tract (GI tract) system

iii. Gastrointestinal tract (GI tract) system

Stool is one of the easiest samples to collect from vulnerable groups such as children and severely ill patients with HIV that cannot produce good quality respiratory sputum samples. Quantitative PCR studies have demonstrated that high bacillary load can be detected in stool of PLHIV, HIV negative and children [84, 85]. M.tb IS6110: This is the commonest DNA target for most PCR methods including the WHO’s endorsed Xpert and Ultra assays [8, 86].Various studies using quantitative PCR (qPCR) methods have showed better diagnostic performance compared to Xpert and Ultra with > 98% for both sensitivity and specificity in population of PLHIV and children [87]. M.tb 16S rRNA biomarker: This biomarker can detect and quantify viable M.tb [85, 88], and is the biomarker target of a novel Molecular Bacterial Load Assay (MBLA) that is in the late stages of development. The stool mean bacterial load was higher in HIV-positive compared to HIV negative TB patients. The stool PCR 16S rRNA marker has been used to differentiate between TB colitis and other conditions with similar clinical manifestation in comparison with colonoscopy, histopathology and clinical evaluation [89]. However, this study had low specificity. Microbiome and microbiota biomarker: The difference in the composition of microbiota in pulmonary and healthy individuals is largely detected using 16S rRNA sequencing [43, 90–93]. However, this is affected by differences, such as in population and geographical conditions [90]. Difference in the relative abundance of fecal microbiota between the TB and TB-free individuals is mostly observed at family, genus and species levels [92]. Other biomarkers have been detected in stool; calprotein [94].

This narrative review shows that the source of most of these non-invasive non-sputum samples used for M. tuberculosis detection are collected from or through the mouth compared to the nasal–pharynx and GI tract. This is similar to a systematic review on upper respiratory tract sample [103]. Most biomarkers are genotypic-based, i.e., DNA/RNA. The most frequent biomarkers were DNA IS6110 and RNA 16S rRNA. There are some studies that have been done in host and pathogen derived biomarkers from these non-invasively collected non-sputum samples from/in the mouth, nasal pharynx and oral–GI tract regions of humans (Table 3). Table 4 shows that some of the biomarkers can be detected on already existing point of care platforms like NAAT of GeneXpert, TB-LAMP and antigen lateral flow. Therefore, researchers need to investigate in evaluating new biomarkers on existing PoC devices.

Diagnostic performance of non-sputum-based biomarker-based tests of samples collected non-invasively from or through the oral–gastrointestinal tract and nasal pharynx regions

Studies in; cough aerosol [46, 95], face mask sampling [52], oral swabs [29, 31, 32, 36, 42], saliva [15, 23], exhaled breath [61, 76, 79] and stool [87, 88], were identified in this literature review to meet or are close to meet WHO’s TPP [23]. The commonest and promising biomarkers analysed include M.tb DNA (ISO IS6110 and IS1081), 16S rRNA, cytokines, Volatile organic compound, LAM. However, these are mainly tested in the general population, and few times, in a small population of children and PLHIV. In general, oral swabs have shown to contain more M.tb DNA than in plasma and urine samples [38] making them ideal alternate non-sputum samples for TB detection in children and PLHIV. Tongue swab [29, 31], face mask sampling [45] and saliva [23] tests targeting nucleic acids have also showed promising results as potential non-sputum-based sample. The amount of DNA/RNA and bacterial load is affected by the method of collection; type of mask (face mask versus membrane mask), duration of collection, storage, transport and preservation method [48, 50]. Tongue swabs have showed better diagnostic performance compared to other oral swabs of cheek and gum using Xpert Ultra and other in-house qPCRs [29, 31, 32, 34]. Morning and second consecutive swabs have showed to have higher bacterial load and more sensitive [29]. The type of swab used to collect the samples has been shown to increase the sensitivity of diagnostic method [32, 33, 97, 104]. Differences in study designs including collection and analytical procedures create, in addition, to limited research that has been conducted in these non-invasive non-sputum-based biomarkers in this review create data heterogeneity. A combination of either tests, markers or biosignature has shown to improve diagnostic performance of non-sputum-based tests [18, 76, 105–110] and have also shown promising results in reaching WHO’s TPP in children and PLHIV. There is need to conduct more translational research on these assays and their placement in the TB management cascade of screening, triage and diagnosis, for example, as complementary test when more than one test is used [40]. Face mask sampling has been used to detect TB in patients unable to produce sputum [49, 50] showing that non-coughing patients still expectorate M.tb bacilli from the respiratory tract and is detectable in non-sputum specimens. Therefore, non-sputum-based biomarkers and assays can be useful for incipient, sub-clinical and asymptomatic TB in the general population, children and PLHIV. However, M.tb. DNA was not detected in children’s FMS [53].

The summary of the diagnostic performance (sensitivity and specificity) of these non-sputum biomarker-based samples on either already approved or in advanced stages of technique shows varying sensitivities and specificities (Table 4). Most diagnosis has been done on GeneXpert targeting TB DNA and mainly in the general population without children and PLHIV subgroups. Most studies are focusing on determining the diagnostic accuracy of these biomarkers and samples on these tests and hence there are hardly any studies on cost implication, acceptability and other qualitative aspects that are required by TPP [6].

Non-invasive non-sputum biomarkers for M.tb detection in children

Table 3 summarise the biomarkers and tests of samples collected non-invasively from or through the oral–GI tract and nasal pharynx regions in children. It can be noted that fewer studies have been conducted in children compared to PLHIV and general population (Table 3). This may be attributed to the fact such samples are easier to collect in adults and adolescents compared to younger children. There is need to investigate the performance in children of other promising samples and biomarkers that have been already investigated in other populations. These include; face mask sampling (IS6110), EBC–LAM in children [79], oral wash and swabs (IS6110, 16S rRNA), tongue swab (IS6110), stool (16S rRNA), and other biomarkers in saliva [14, 15, 33, 53]. Systematic reviews [53, 104] highlighted the need for improved devices for collecting good quality aerosol related non sputum, samples collection especially in younger children will improve diagnostic performance of the tests [104]. Peaditric research of non-sputum biomarker-based are also affected by non-standardized methodology, sample collection challenges especially in younger children, and low or undetectable biomarkers yet they have been detected in adults.

Promising samples of stool, saliva, oral swabs and biomarkers of 16S rRNA, DNA have showed sensitivities of up to 71% and have been tested on existing PoC platforms [23] and those in advanced stages [88]. The correlation between HIV disease and difference the detectable biomarkers in PLHIV compared to HIV-negative patients is not fully understood. Studies have either reported increase, reduction or no correlation of the intended biomarker in TB patients with HIV compared to those without HIV [45, 63, 88, 98, 111]. Like pediatric research, majority of publication among PLHIV; are few, done from a small population, have differences in methodology creating heterogeneity and in early stages of development.

Challenges in research, implementation and development of non-sputum-based tests for M. tb detection: a focus for future research

The development of non-sputum-based biomarker-based for TB detection still face a number of challenges before they become a reality in routine TB diagnostic algorithms. There is need to identify which non-sputum specimens and testing technique should be targeted for future research. Another challenge is transforming the biomarker tests that have demonstrated good diagnostic performance in their sophisticated or complex format into a PoC without loss in performance [112] for example, 16s rRNA, VOC. In general, few research has been done especially in PLHIV and children to be considered for systematic review and meta-analysis.

The factors affecting the performance of these non-sputum-based assays have not been fully understood or investigated. Some which have been documented include; age, nutritional status especially in children, PLHIV [113, 114] environment [81], pre-analytical activities (location of sampling, collection method, differences in swab collection sites, time and frequency of sampling, sample processing for example oral swabs) [32, 33, 45, 53, 59, 88, 96, 104], population differences [115], effect on sensitivity of residual bacilli left in the mouth when samples are collected after coughing compared to those collected before or without coughing [23], concentration of markers [116], point of administration of the biomarker [69], and severity of the disease [117]. Device developers may consider research in improved sample collection devices especially for children mainly for aerosols and related samples to maximize the amount of bacilli detectable in these samples [53, 104]. Another review may consider looking at the advantages and disadvantage of host and pathogen markers in these specific samples.

Some biomarkers especially inflammatory are non-specific for a particular condition. Thus, this affects their specificity and makes such biomarkers more useful as screening tools rather than confirmatory tests for TB, hence the need to use more than one indicator including clinical picture [15]. In addition, these can be used as a combination of biomarkers (biosignatures), rather than individual biomarkers.

The positivity rate of some these assays is associated with older age, high smear/culture positivity, ability to produce sputum and cavitary disease [35, 96, 99]. The most common sputum-based NAAT platform, Xpert Ultra, has a “trace result” which is attributed to low bacilli in the specimen and can be interpreted as either true or false positive. The interpretation and association of low bacilli in non-sputum specimens with TB disease has not been fully studied. Therefore, studies can also target patients with low respiratory bacilli [53, 104]; smear negative culture positive and those with ‘Low’ to ‘Trace’ Ultra results in non-sputum samples. Non-sputum-based biomarkers and assays can be useful for sub-clinical and asymptomatic TB in the general population, children and PLHIV.

Most tests in these studies, though promising, are still in very early stages of development and have often used small sample sizes. This means that further validation studies are required to determine their diagnostic performance in a large sample sized study.

There is still a challenge in using a comparable reference method and/or population [118, 119] for non-sputum-based biomarker tests. For example, biomarker tests targeting non-coughing patients are usually done on a coughing population to produce sputum and non-sputum samples, such as stool. In addition, reference and index methods often target different biomarkers. For example, most of genotypic biomarkers are confirmed by reference of sputum phenotypic methods of TB culture which are prone to contamination and lower sensitivity.

In addition to the limited number of studies of non-sputum biomarkers targeting PLHIV or children, there is considerable heterogeneity in study methodology; population, age, enrolment criteria, sampling methods, sample collection methods, assays and reference methods, of research conducted which may be a challenge for future meta-analyses.

There is also limited translational research, for example, the use of these tests as add on or complementary tests in TB diagnostic cascade. These assays may be more beneficial to such scenarios instead of stand-alone tests.

Conclusion

This review has shown that several TB biomarkers detected in non-invasively collected non-sputum specimens collected from the mouth, nasal pharynx and GI tract are similar to those observed in respiratory specimens. Studies of specimens originating from or through the mouth; saliva, oral swabs, oral wash, tongue swabs, facemask sampling, exhaled breath, and dental plaque, seem to be conducted more compared to those from nasal–pharynx and GI tracts. Investigations in children and among PLHIV appear to be done mostly in breath, oral wash, stool, and additionally on cough aerosols, tongue swabs, saliva, and face mask sampling among PLHIV. In these samples, the commonest biomarkers targeted include DNA- and RNA-based IS6110 and 16S rRNA, respectively. Some of these samples and biomarkers have been used on already approved simple point of care and/or sophisticated assays and reached the WHO’s TPP. Similar biomarkers have also been identified in the general presumptive TB populations and in populations of children and PLHIV. Research with a big population of PLHIV and children with standardized research methodology in studies of non-sputum-based biomarkers is critical to minimize heterogeneity during meta-analysis and pooling of results. In children mainly, standardized and improved non-sputum sample collection and processing techniques may improve bacilli load and the diagnostic performance of tests. Promising biomarkers tested in adults should also be analyzed in children. There is need to transform assays detecting promising biomarkers like 16s rRNA, VOCs into PoC devices. Emphasis should be put on identifying biomarkers and testing their diagnostic performance on mainly existing PoC devices like NAAT where the target biomarker is the same for most of the recommended sputum-based methods, such as TrueNat and LAMP. This review has showed limited research in combination of biosignature markers compared to a single biomarker especially considering non-specific biomarkers like inflammatory ones from these non-invasive non sputum and other samples and biomarkers.
