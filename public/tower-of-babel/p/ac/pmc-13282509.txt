Investigating Physical Exercise and Cognitive Training Effects on Cognition and Brain Health in Men and Women with Heart Failure: The ReCARDIO Trial
Authors: Louis Bherer, Florent Besnier, Christine Gagnon, Tudor Vrinceanu, Thomas Vincent, Hânieh Mohammadi, Claudine Gauthier, Corentin Duquesne, Caroll-Ann Blanchette, Béatrice Bérubé, Stéphane-Nassif Said, Josep Iglesies-Grau, Philippe L'Allier, Jacinthe Boulet, Anil Nigam, Valérie Dionne, Hakima Benhalima, Marie-Pierre Dubé, Simon Duchesne, Sylvie Belleville, Patrice Brassard, Daniel Gagnon, Frédéric Lesage, Mathieu Gayda, Jean-Paul Soucy, Nadia Bouabdallaoui, Teresa Liu-Ambrose, Cindy Barha, Judes Poirier, Éric Thorin, Minh Vu, François Tournoux, Paolo Vitali, Eileen O’Meara, Normand Racine, Jean-Lucien Rouleau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive impairment can affect up to 50% of patients with chronic heart failure (CHF) and is associated with reduced treatment adherence, high mortality rates, and poor quality of life. Nonpharmacologic strategies, including cognitive intervention and physical exercise training, may help enhance cognition in patients with CHF. Recent studies in dementia prevention have shown that combining cognitive and exercise interventions could have synergistic effects on cognition, but scientific evidence for the benefits in CHF patients is lacking. Moreover, how men and women with heart failure may differ in their response to nonpharmacologic interventions is also unknown. This randomized controlled trial will investigate the effects of combining cognitive and exercise training, on cognition and cerebral blood flow regulation in men and women with CHF. To achieve this, 216 participants (50% female) with stable CHF regardless of etiology and left ventricular ejection fraction will be randomized to 1 of the 3 following arms: 1. combined cognitive and exercise training; 2. exercise training alone; 3. usual medical care with standard cardiovascular rehabilitation. The first 2 groups will engage in a 6-month intervention, whereas those in group 3 will take part in a standard 3-month cardiac rehabilitation program. The primary endpoint will be changes in cognitive performance from baseline to 6 months based on 4 cognitive composite scores (global cognitive functioning, memory, executive functions, processing speed). Secondary outcomes will include changes in cerebral blood flow regulation (neurovascular coupling, pulsatility, and autoregulation). Tertiary outcomes will include cardiorespiratory fitness, physical functioning, and quality of life.Clinical Trial RegistrationNCT04970888.Keywords: heart failure, cognitive training, brain function, exercise, physical activity, cardiac rehabilitation, combined training

Cognitive impairment (CI) can be present in up to 50% of elderly chronic heart failure (CHF) patients,1,2 specifically affecting episodic memory,3 executive functions, and processing speed.4 Cognitive deficits appear earlier in patients with CHF, compared to age and sex-matched controls, with CHF patients having a 2-fold increased risk of CI compared to controls. In CHF patients, CI is an independent predictor of mortality, rehospitalization, and rapid functional decline, and cognitive deficits have been shown to correlate with disease severity.1 The exact pathophysiological features linking heart failure (HF) to CI are still unclear and seem to be caused by multifactorial processes.5 These include reduced cerebral perfusion due to decreased acute or chronic cardiac output,6 structural deteriorations in brain areas,7,8 silent cerebral ischemia related to multiple cerebral microemboli,9 and abnormalities in the autonomic nervous system.10,11

Abnormal cerebral autoregulation and decreased cerebral vasoreactivity are frequent in CHF,12 potentiating the risk of impaired cerebral perfusion, and consequently leading to the development of premature CI.13 Cerebral hypoperfusion also has been strongly correlated to CI and identified as an independent predictor of mortality in CHF patients.14 Moreover, the impaired cerebral blood flow (CBF) observed in CHF patients particularly impacts specific areas of the brain, such as the frontal cortex and the parahippocampal gyrus, areas involved in episodic memory, learning, and executive functions.15 At the structural level, the same areas with low CBF have been associated with regional cortical atrophy. Indeed, atrophy in the medial temporal lobe regions in individuals with CHF is correlated with lower memory and executive function performance.16

Although recent advances have been made to understand the pathophysiology of HF-related CI,17 currently no specific pharmacologic treatment is available to alleviate these cognitive deficits. Only interventions that durably improve cardiac output, such as heart transplantation or mechanical circulatory support, have been shown to improve cognitive deficits in advanced HF patients.18 Comprehensive cardiac rehabilitation programs,19, 20, 21 physical exercise training,22, 23, 24, 25 and cognitive training26 are nonpharmacologic interventions that have been shown to independently improve cognitive functions such as attention and executive functions in individuals with CHF.27 Recent studies suggest that combined cognitive and physical exercise interventions could lead to larger cognitive improvements than exercise alone in healthy elders,28 individuals with cardiovascular risk factors,29 and older adults with CI.30 However, the effects of combined interventions on cognitive performance in patients with CHF are still understudied.

Recently, the Exercise and Cognitive Training Intervention in Heart Failure (EXCITE-HF) study, a pilot study with 69 CHF patients, suggested that a 6-month cognitive and exercise training program might have synergistic effects on verbal memory after 3 months, but this effect was not maintained at 6 months.31 In that study, the exercise training component consisted of self-initiated aerobic walking, and the cognitive training component is described vaguely as focusing on sensory integration and encoding. Although the training program matched the minimum length of 6 months that recent studies suggest is needed to observe cognitive benefits, it could have been lacking in terms of training dose or content.

Although recent studies have shown that longer training programs of at least 6 months that include a higher training dose are needed to observe cognitive benefits,32 the usual length of outpatient cardiac rehabilitation programs for CHF is only 12 weeks, with 2-3 sessions per week.33 In fact, the latest guidelines across the world suggest a minimum duration of 8 weeks, but studies have administered programs that last 12-24 and even 36 weeks, with 3-7 sessions per week.34,35 In those studies, longer programs appear to have increased benefits in decreasing the risk of major cardiac events,36 and even in studies in which a plateau in cardiac benefit is observed, longer cardiac rehabilitation programs can lead to additional benefits (eg, psychological health).37

Finally, a major recent development in the field of HF and cardiac rehabilitation has been the acknowledgment that men and women have different disease profiles, rehabilitation access and referral rates, and overall prognoses. This difference is highlighted in the 2025 scientific statement by the American Heart Association.38 From a medical point of view, men exhibit a greater prevalence of coronary heart disease earlier in life, whereas women show an accelerated cardiovascular deterioration that leads to HF after menopause.39 Additionally, sex hormones, such as estrogen and testosterone, have different impacts on cardiovascular health in aging men and women.40,41 From a social point of view, differences between men and women in cardiac rehabilitation referral, and completion rates, also have been highlighted.42

In addition, studies indicate that physical exercise training may also have different effects on the 2 sexes. Some studies suggest that women derive greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risk reduction than men from equivalent doses of physical activity.43 In the cerebral domain, exercise-induced neuroplasticity and preserved hippocampal and prefrontal cortex integrity appear more robust in female individuals, potentially reflecting sex hormones’ modulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factors and cerebral blood flow.44 Larger effects are also reported on cognitive performance, particularly executive functions, in studies including older adults with a higher proportion of female participants.45 However, research is limited on the direct comparison of sex-related differences in cognitive responses to exercise and cognitive training. One study that included individuals with mild CI found that female participants improved their performance on multiple cognitive tests after high-intensity aerobic training, whereas male participants’ performance improved on only one test.46 Similarly, cognitive training also could show different improvements between men and women,47 but very few studies address this topic. Nonetheless, how this difference may relate to cardiac rehabilitation in CHF remains unknown. Given the sex-related differences in HF pathophysiology, and in exercise and cognitive training efficiency, the impacts of an exercise and cognitive intervention might differ in female and male patients with CHF. This information could help develop targeted strategies to improve the management of cognitive impairment in HF, as part of a personalized management program (Central Illustration).

Objectives

The main objective of this study is to assess the effects of combined cognitive and exercise training interventions on cognitive performance in male and female patients with CHF. The second objective is to characterize baseline and intervention-related changes in cerebral blood flow regulation in male and female patients with CHF. Finally, we will compare the impacts of the different interventions on cardiorespiratory fitness, physical functioning, and quality of life.

Methods

Study design

This study is a multicentre, assessor-blind, parallel group trial of superiority in which patients with CHF (50% female participants) will be randomly assigned to one of the following 3 study arms (1:1:1): (i) combined cognitive and exercise training (individualized aerobic and resistance training combined with a cognitive training program); (ii) exercise training (individualized aerobic and resistance training program); and (iii) usual medical care, which includes a standard 3-month cardiac rehabilitation program. The 2 experimental interventions are 6-month long, and all participants will be assessed at baseline, and at 3 and 6 months.

Study setting

All assessments and interventions take place at the Preventive Medicine and Physical Activity Centre (ÉPIC Centre) of the Montréal Heart Institute (MHI). Participants will be referred by cardiologists from the 3 centres taking part in the study: the MHI, the Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal, and the Hôpital Maisonneuve-Rosemont, in Montreal, Canada. Usually, patients with CHF are referred from all those centres for an exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation program. Once referred for this program, participants are asked if they are willing to participant in the present randomized controlled trial.

Participant inclusion criteria

A total of 216 HF participants (50% women) aged ≥ 50 years will be recruited for this study. The diagnosis of HF required objective confirmation by at least one of the following documented in the medical record: (i) structural or functional cardiac abnormalities on echocardiography (including reduced or preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) with evidence of systolic or diastolic dysfunction); (ii) elevated natriuretic peptide levels (N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP]) consistent with HF; or (iii) a prior hospitalization for HF or documented episode of decompensated HF. Participants were required to be clinically stable, defined as having no hospitalization or emergency visit for acute HF or other acute cardiovascular event, nor outpatient diuretic treatment for hypervolemia, within one month prior to randomization. They also were required to be receiving guideline-directed medical therapy as determined by the treating cardiologist. Inclusion criteria are as follows: stable chronic HF regardless of etiology and across the full spectrum of LVEF; New York Heart Association (NYHA) class I to III; and ability to perform cognitive and physical training. Exclusion criteria are as follows: acute cardiovascular event within 1 month prior to randomization, including emergency visit or hospitalization for decompensated HF, acute myocardial infarction, stroke, or transient ischemic attack; planned cardiovascular intervention within 6 months (eg, implantation of any cardiac device, cardiac revascularization, heart transplantation); severe exercise intolerance; contraindications for exercise testing (eg, uncorrected severe aortic or carotid stenosis, severe pulmonary hypertension, severe non-revascularizable coronary disease including left main coronary stenosis, significant myocardial ischemia or arrhythmia during low-intensity exercise, acute HF); severe respiratory disease; non-cardiopulmonary limitation to exercise (eg, arthritis, claudication, or any peripheral neurologic disease including other neurodegenerative diseases such as disabling Parkinsons); uncontrolled diabetes or untreated thyroid dysfunction; current or recent malignancy with life expectancy < 1 year; chronic hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis; inability to read the informed consent form or inability to understand the oral explanations provided by the assessor.

Procedure and outcomes

After providing consent, participants will first meet with a cardiologist from the EPIC Centre of the MHI, who will confirm the eligibility criteria and then proceed to the cardiorespiratory exercise test. After completing baseline assessments, participants will be randomised into 1 of the 3 groups. Participants will complete 5 assessment visits prior to the intervention, as well as mid- (3 months) and post-intervention (6 months) assessment. At baseline, demographic and medical information, including chronic diseases, comorbidities, and medications will be collected by the medical staff. The primary outcome will be cognition assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)48 and 3 composite scores (executive function, episodic memory, and processing speed). Secondary outcomes will be changes in cerebral autoregulation and pulsatility assessed with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and transcranial Doppler (TCD), as well as neurovascular coupling during a Stroop task measured with NIRS. The tertiary outcomes will include changes in cardiorespiratory fitness (peak oxygen uptake [VO2peak]), physical function, and quality of life. All assessments are synthesized in Supplemental Table S1, and a detailed description of all administered assessments can be found in Supplemental Appendix S1.

Primary outcome

The trial includes 4 cognitive scores: MoCA, executive function composite score, processing speed composite score, and episodic memory composite score. The 3 composite Z-scores will be extracted from a comprehensive neuropsychological test battery, according to previously validated procedures.49 The neuropsychological assessment will include the following tests that will be administered in a fixed order: MoCA (global cognition); Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (episodic memory); Digit Symbol Substitution Test (processing speed); Stroop Color Word Interference Test (processing speed and executive functions); Trail Making Test (processing speed and executive functions); and Phonological and Semantic Fluency Test (executive functions and semantic memory). These tests are validated and normalized for an older adult population,50 and they are currently routinely used by our group in both research and the clinical setting. The assessments will be conducted in-person or by videoconference, with versions of traditional neuropsychological tests that have been validated for both administration settings, under the supervision of a clinical neuropsychologist. Computerized tasks targeting executive functions will also be administered: dual-task, N-back task, numerical Stroop task.

Secondary outcome

Cerebral autoregulation and pulsatility will be assessed at rest in passive mode at the level of the middle cerebral artery using transcranial Doppler (TCD; Multi-Dop T, DWL, Singen, Germany), and at the cortical level using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS; Brite 23, Artinis, Elst, Netherlands). Patients will be asked to stay in a supine position for 5 minutes, while undergoing recordings of TCD, fNIRS, continuous arterial blood pressure, and 5-lead electrocardiogram (Finapres NOVA, Finapres, Enschede, Netherlands). All physiological signals will be synchronized via a data acquisition platform (Powerlab 16/35, ADinstruments, Colorado Springs, CO). In all settings, end-tidal O2 and CO2 concentrations will be tracked by a gas analyzer (Gemini O2/CO2 monitor, CWE Inc, Ardmore, PA) as peak CO2 is a confounding factor of CBF variations. Cerebral autoregulation at the level of the middle cerebral artery will be quantified using the cerebral autoregulation index, which assesses the relationship between cerebral blood velocity obtained by TCD and the spontaneous fluctuations of the mean arterial pressure measured by pletysmography.51 Pulsatilty indices will be computed from beat-to-beat cardiac epochs as the normalized ratio between systolic and diastolic peaks from TCD and NIRS signals. Neurovascular coupling will also be investigated with fNIRS by measuring changes of oxy- and deoxy-hemoglobin concentrations evoked by a Stroop task. This task will comprise 75 inhibition trials (ink color naming with incongruent words), 25 switching trials (word reading with incongruent ink color), and 25 control trials (ink color naming of a series of X).

Tertiary outcomes

Cardiorespiratory fitness (peak oxygen uptake [VO2peak]) and physical function will be assessed with a maximal cardiopulmonary exercise test,52 a 6-minute walking test, a 10-meter walking test, the timed “up and go” test, and a handgrip test. The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire will be completed to assess quality of life.

Questionnaires

Several questionnaires routinely used in the context of CHF will be completed: the Physical Frailty Phenotype examination; the European Heart Failure Self-care Behaviour Scale; the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index; the Geriatric Depression Scale; the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory; and COVID-19-related questionnaires. See Supplemental Appendix S1 for details and additional exploratory outcomes.

Interventions

Participants will be assigned randomly to 1 of the 3 following study arms: Group 1, combined cognitive and exercise training; Group 2, exercise training intervention alone; and Group 3, usual medical care with standard cardiac rehabilitation. Participants from groups 1 and 2 will have the option to train either onsite at the EPIC Centre, at home, or at a combination of the 2 (hybrid mode: centre and home-based program) with a recommendation to train at the EPIC Centre for at least one session per week. All patients (from the 3 groups) will have access to the same medically relevant components of the cardiac rehabilitation program that include optimal medical therapy, physical activity counselling, and cardiovascular risk factor reduction (nutritional counselling, weight management, cessation of smoking, pharmacologic management) in line with the latest update of the Canadian Cardiovascular Society Guidelines for the Management of Heart Failure.53

Cognitive training component

Only the participants assigned to the combined training group will receive this intervention, on the same days, right before the exercise intervention. The cognitive training will involve attention-control training and memory training, for 30 minutes, 3 times weekly. Attention-control training has been developed previously by our group54 and includes 3 tablet- or computer-based tasks, as follows: (1) dual-task training that requires participants to maintain and prepare for many response alternatives (working memory) and to share attention between 2 concurrent tasks (divided attention); (2) inhibition training, which requires participants to refrain from giving automatic responses (ie, Stroop task); and (3) working memory training, in which participants must maintain and update information in working memory (ie, “n-back” task). Training difficulty will be tailored to participants’ performances and will include feedback, as well as a histogram of daily performances to encourage improvement. The memory training program will be an analog to the one used in another clinical study from our group.55 The memory program involves teaching participants to better encode information using a range of mnemotechnic strategies and was found to yield durable memory improvements in older adults with mild CI.56 Both types of cognitive training are administered by research assistants specifically trained to do this, supervised by a clinical neuropsychologist.

Exercise training component

This component will be administered to participants randomized in both of the active intervention groups (groups 1 and 2). Participants will follow a 20-week exercise training program, consisting of 60-minute sessions involving aerobic and resistance training 3 times per week, supervised by a certified kinesiologist. The aerobic training will be prescribed based on the baseline maximal aerobic power and the rate of perceived exertion (6 to 20) obtained from the cardiopulmonary exercise test, according to the F.I.T.T. principles (frequency, intensity, time, and type) of the latest recommendations.34,35 Before and after the aerobic exercise sessions, participants will do a 5-minute warmup and a 5-minute cool-down period at 30% of maximal aerobic power each. Every week, one session of moderate-intensity continuous exercise training and 2 sessions of high-intensity interval training (with short and long intervals) will be offered to the patients, according to their health and functional status, the feasibility of each session, and any limitations or precautions provided by the cardiologist, when needed. The resistance training duration is 30 minutes and is comprised of at least 3 of 5 exercises (leg press, seated row, lateral pull down, chest press, leg curl).

For home-training sessions, participants will have access to a complete exercise training program with aerobic and strength exercises with a structure similar to the program onsite. A kinesiologist will call participants weekly to ensure the smooth running of the training sessions and to individualize the exercise program according to the patient’s level of fitness. All the characteristics of these physical activities (including type of activity, intensity, heart rate, and duration) will be recorded using Polar Beat (Kempele, Polar, Finland) and heart rate monitors (H10 or verity sense, Polar, Finland). If participants do not have access to a smartphone and Polar Beat, they will be asked to list the duration, intensity, and nature of the activity via an agenda that will be provided to them. For more details about the composition of the exercise training program, including detailed durations and exercise intensities, see Supplemental Appendix S2.

Usual medical care with standard cardiac rehabilitation

Participants randomized to this group will benefit from a prescription to a standard exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation program35 at the EPIC Centre, but they will not receive any other additional form of intervention. The program lasts 3 months, with 2 supervised centre-based exercise training sessions per week, each lasting for 1 hour, including aerobic and resistance exercises. Patients are strongly encouraged to engage in a third home-based exercise training session. The exercise sessions include both aerobic (moderate-intensity continuous exercise training and high-intensity interval training alternatively) and resistance training. Participants in this group do not receive any type of training between the end of the 3-month usual care cardiac rehabilitation program and the 6-month evaluation. For more details about the exercise durations and intensities, see Supplemental Appendix S2.

Blinding and randomization

The randomization sequence was generated by a biostatistician from the Montreal Health Innovations Coordinating Centre (MHICC), where the randomization sequence was generated using a computer-generated random number sequence using permuted blocks of different undisclosed sizes. The randomization will be performed by a staff member who is not involved in any other aspect of the trial. Once a participant is ready to be randomized (after signing the consent form, fulfilling the inclusion criteria, and completing the baseline assessment), the randomization staff will open a sealed envelope and reveal the training group. The participant’s intervention group then will be shared with a different staff member in charge of the study training. Nobody else involved in the study has access to the randomization envelopes, or to any information related to the randomization procedure and sequence. Patients will be stratified according to sex and LVEF (< 40% and ≥ 40%) and will be equally randomized to 1 of the 3 groups (1:1:1). The assessors executing the measurements, and the investigators, will be blinded to group allocation. The statistician will be blinded until completion of the statistical analyses. Only the kinesiologists administering the training program will be aware of the assigned randomization group. Kinesiologists will not perform assessments at any time point. In the case of medical necessity, the participants could be unblinded, and the principal investigator as well as the cardiologist from the EPIC Centre will be contacted to allow the disclosure.

Sample size calculation

An a priori power and sample size analysis was performed by a biostatistician team independent of the study team. As no data were available with the exact sample profile and training programs, the analyses were based on data from a previous study from our group, administering a physical exercise training alone in frail older adults, with executive functions as the main outcome.57 In this publication, the standardized training group mean was μ = 0.357, and the common standard deviation was σ = 0.546, resulting in an effect size of δ = 0.653. Given that the combined training has been shown to result in a higher effect size than exercise training alone,30 an expected training mean of μ1 = 0.430 was selected, resulting in an expected effect size of δ = 0.788. Using an intent-to-treat principle with multiple imputations, a sample size of 26 subjects in each group will have 80% power to detect the expected effect size using a 2-group t-test with a 0.05 two-sided significance level. Considering a 25% attrition rate observed in our previous exercise studies, we will recruit 36 participants per sex group and intervention arm (36 x 2 sexes x 3 intervention arms), for a total of 216 participants (108 women and 108 men with CHF). Recruitment for a given sex will stop once its target is reached, and recruitment for the other sex will continue until its target sample is achieved.

Statistical analysis

The variables in the study will be presented using descriptive statistics. The mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, Q1, Q3, and maximum will be presented for continuous variables. The number and percentage will be presented for the nominal/ordinal variables. The assumptions of the statistical tests will be examined, and data transformation or nonparametric analyses may be used, as appropriate. All participants randomized will be included in the analyses using an intention-to-treat method. If missing data are shown to be missing at random (using Little’s MCAR [missing completely at random] test), the missing values will be imputed using 20 multiple imputations using the SPSS built-in Mersenne Twister Random Number Generator (SPSS, IBM, Armonk, NY), following the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method. Parameter estimates and standard errors across imputations will be pooled using Rubin’s rules to obtain final inferential statistics. The trial was prospectively powered on the executive functions composite score, which serves as the primary cognitive outcome for hypothesis testing. Although 4 cognitive measures were registered (MoCA, executive functions, processing speed, and episodic memory) due to their clinical relevance, the remaining 3 will be analyzed as key clinically relevant cognitive outcomes. No formal correction for multiple comparisons will be applied to the primary outcome.

Mean changes in cognitive performance from baseline will be analyzed using a repeated-measures analysis of covariance model including group effects (combined, exercise alone, and usual care), time of assessments (0, 3, and 6 months), and time x group interactions, with age, and education as covariates. Post hoc analyses will look at group differences at each time point, including an investigation of group effect at 3 months, after an equal length of the intervention has been administered to all participants. Primary analyses will be run separately for male and female participants. Secondary and tertiary outcomes will be analyzed using the same procedure as that for the primary outcome. To further characterize the impact of the training groups on the outcomes, supplementary analyses will be run in which we will test if the training dose mediates the effect of the training group on the main outcomes. SPSS version 29 or higher software or SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) or more recent version software will be used to conduct the analyses.

Data quality management

Data quality management (under the principal investigator’s supervision) includes conducting range checks to detect any errors in the data, coding data for consistency and standardization, anonymizing sensitive information to protect participant privacy, and implementing secure backup measures to prevent data loss. Access to the database is protected by a password and strictly controlled to maintain confidentiality and integrity. Following data collection, the database undergoes thorough verification by members of the Data Monitoring Committee, comprised of experts in neuroscience and physiology. Once verified, the database is frozen for statistical analysis. Additionally, data protection measures, such as assigning unique identifiers, subject codes, and password-protected data files, are implemented to safeguard against unauthorized access and maintain data confidentiality.

Availability of data and materials

Data and analyses will be made available by the principal investigator upon resonable request.

Adverse events (AEs)

An AE is described as any medical incident that is unexpected and may reasonably be suspected to be associated with participation in the research study. The documentation of AEs begins upon participants' enrollment in the study and concludes upon completion of their participation, unless a serious adverse event (SAE) occurs. AEs that precede any training session will be classified as unrelated to the intervention. The study coordinator maintains an ongoing paper log of all AEs, which is monitored by the physician until resolution. The physician assesses the severity of the event and its connection to study procedures. Any AE that meets the criteria for an SAE is reported to the local Research Ethics Board. For this study, an SAE encompasses significant medical issues causally linked to the study intervention, such as hospitalization, life-threatening conditions, and severe or permanent disability.

Discussion

This project will advance clinical care in individuals aged 50 years and older with CHF by documenting the impacts of nonpharmacologic interventions involving exercise training alone or combined with cognitive training on cognitive functioning. This trial was specifically structured to address the following clinically meaningful question: Does implementing a more intensive, evidence-informed intervention (characterized by greater frequency, longer duration, and the addition of cognitive training) yield superior patient outcomes relative to the current standard of care? This project will have important clinical repercussions, given the high prevalence of CI in this population. Moreover, the influence of exercise training on cerebral blood flow regulation, and its relationship with cognitive status also will be explored, which will allow better characterization of the mechanisms underlying CI in CHF. Finally, this project will characterize sex-related differences with regard to the response to nonpharmacologic interventions in CHF patients. This characterization will provide valuable information to advance personalized intervention prescription that will consider the specific needs and limitations of male and female individuals living with CHF. An advantage of this trial is that it offers the possibility for hybrid training modalities, thereby helping to include participants who might otherwise have barriers to cardiac rehabilitation participation. Should the present trial yield favourable outcomes, subsequent international replications will be essential to strengthen the evidence base and inform future revisions of cardiac rehabilitation guidelines for this population.

Trial Status

The ReCARDIO study was registered on August 2, 2021. Recruitment and the first inclusion for the study started in September 2021 and is planned to be finished in December 2026. The start of the randomized controlled trial was delayed by the COVID-19 health restrictions.

Ethics Statement

The study protocol was approved on July 2019 by the Montreal Heart Institute’s Research Ethics Board (ICM MP-33-2018-2406), and any modifications will first be submitted for approval. The study will also comply with International Conference on Harmonization for Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines, as well as with all regulatory requirements. No audit of the trial is pre-planned. All participants will provide informed consent prior to starting the study.
