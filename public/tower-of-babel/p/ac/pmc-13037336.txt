Background

Lingual thyroid is a rare developmental defect caused by failure of the thyroid gland to descend from the foramen cecum, which is usually located between the circumvallate papillae of the tongue and the epiglottis [1, 2]. This condition presents as a lobular mass at the base of the tongue, producing low levels of thyroid hormones leading to hypothyroidism, and may remain asymptomatic unless the gland enlarges [3]. Moreover, ectopic thyroid tissue has been found in different locations, such as the submandibular and lateral cervical regions, palatine tonsils, carotid bifurcation, pituitary gland, axilla, heart and ascending aorta, thymus, and different parts of the gastrointestinal and reproductive systems [4, 5]. Lingual thyroid is four to seven times more prevalent in females than males, with overall prevalence ranging from 1 in 100,000 to 1 in 300,000 individuals [2, 6].

Studies have depicted that 60–70% of the patients with thyroid ectopy suffer from hypothyroidism and may later develop clinical manifestations during the period of physiological stress, such as puberty, pregnancy, or menstruation. [1, 5–7] Dysphagia, dysphonia, cough, throat pain, foreign body sensation, bleeding, sleep apnoea, and dyspnoea are among the common symptoms. [2, 8] Apart from clinical symptoms, Technetium (Tc-99) scintigraphy is the most commonly used confirmatory diagnostic technique for lingual thyroid [7]. Diagnosis of lingual thyroid is often missed in asymptomatic patients, despite the biochemical evidence of hypothyroidism. [9]

Case presentation

This is a 5-year-old girl who presented with stunted growth and an unremarkable past medical history. She weighed 13.3 kg (kg), measured 96 centimeters in height (height-for-age: −2.7 SD), and had a body mass index (BMI) of 14.5 kg/m². Despite evidence of growth failure, she did not have any other complaints. A thorough physical examination revealed no abnormalities except a smooth, round, pink mass approximately 2 cm in size at the base of her tongue; she did not report any foreign body sensation (Fig.1A). Our differential diagnoses included lingual thyroid, lingual tonsil, and thyroglossal duct cyst. Her short stature and the presence of lingual mass guided us to perform thyroid function testing to evaluate hypothyroidism as primary aetiology.

A The clinical photograph showing the oral cavity and base of tongue (white dash circle) of the patient with a smooth, round and pink mass (white arrow), (B) remission of mass (diagnosed as lingual thyroid) following treatment with L-thyroxine

Therefore, we recommended thyroid function tests, neck ultrasonography, a thyroid scan, an X-ray of the left hand for bone age. Her serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level was 84 µIU/ml (reference range: 0.5–5.5 µIU/ml) and Free Thyroxine (FT4) was 18.13 pmol/L (reference range: 8.56–25.60 pmol/L), indicating early primary hypothyroidism. An ultrasound revealed an empty thyroid bed and a 17 × 17 mm oval-shaped soft tissue lesion at the base of the tongue. Technetium (Tc-99 m) thyroid scan revealed no radiotracer uptake in the thyroid bed, and intense radiotracer uptake at the nodular lesion in the base of the tongue (Fig. 2). Her bone age was appropriate for her chronological age. She was found to have borderline intellectual functioning through “Wechsler preschool and primary scale of intelligence” (WPPSI-IV) conducted by a trained psychologist. She was diagnosed as juvenile hypothyroidism with a lingual thyroid.

Technetium (Tc99m) thyroid scan revealing no radiotracer concentration in the thyroid base but intense concentration at the base of the tongue (white arrow) suggesting ectopic thyroid tissue

Treatment with L-Thyroxine was initiated at 4 µg/kg/day. At the first follow-up, TSH level decreased to 12.28 µIU/ml, and free thyroxine was 18.13 pmol/L. L-Thyroxine was then subsequently adjusted based on serial TSH levels. After three months, the size of the lingual thyroid had significantly decreased (Fig.1B). Clinically, she demonstrated better attention and engagement in daily activities. Growth measurements showed steady improvement: 96 cm at diagnosis, with a 6 cm increase in the next year and another 6 cm in the second year, indicating a positive response to thyroid hormone replacement. Her thyroid function has remained stable and she continues to receive regular follow-up care for hypothyroidism.

Discussion

Our index patient was prepubertal, and her lingual thyroid was diagnosed through careful physical examination before symptom onset, which were expected to occur during puberty [3]. Since there were no foreign body sensation, dysphagia, dysphonia or cough or respiratory symptoms, the diagnosis of lingual thyroid could easily be overlooked without direct examination of the oral cavity. This would further delay her crucial diagnosis and jeopardize clinical management. Meticulous clinical examination guided endocrine evaluation and contributed to the timely diagnosis of hypothyroidism.

Bone age was not delayed in proportion to the extent of short stature as is typically expected. This may be explained by the combined effects of malnutrition and hypothyroidism contributing to short stature, as her weight-for-age was below 5th percentile for age and sex. Her score was 75 on WPPSI-III assessment, which denotes borderline intellectual functioning. She was a slow learner and had challenges with daily life skills. Thyroid deficiency, either congenital or juvenile, may contribute intellectual disability [8, 9]. It is difficult to determine the contribution of factors other than hypothyroidism.

Neck ultrasound is commonly the first-line imaging modality due to its non-invasive nature, cost-effectiveness, and availability, especially in resource-limited settings. A Tc-99 scan can serve as a confirmatory tool to identify ectopic thyroid tissue deposits [7, 10]. However, fine needle aspiration cytology of the ectopic tissue can be considered in cases with suspicion of malignant transformation [11]. Although lingual thyroid is often the only functional thyroid tissue in most of the cases, the severity of symptoms, complications and thyroid function status provide guidance for management [5]. Asymptomatic euthyroid cases require follow-up, while hypothyroid cases can be managed with levothyroxine, which also reduces gland size. [1, 3, 12] Lingual thyroids causing airway obstruction may require radioactive iodine 131 (RAI) therapy or complete resection with subsequent levothyroxine supplementation [2, 10].

Given our patient’s age, lack of local symptoms, and hypothyroidism, we maintained thyroid hormone replacement therapy which led to the reduction of lingual thyroid size. Furthermore, based on available research, we recommended regular follow-up to detect any further progression or complication of the condition [10, 12].

Conclusion

In paediatric patients with growth failure, a detailed physical examination of the oral cavity is crucial to explore the possibilities of lingual thyroid. Moreover, long term follow-up is required in children who receive thyroid replacement therapy to monitor changes in size, complications, and their overall growth and development.
