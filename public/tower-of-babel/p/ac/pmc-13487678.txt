Changes in alcohol use following a diagnosis of alcohol-related liver disease: A clinical cohort study
Authors: Anna Emilie Kann, Lone Galmstrup Madsen, Jonas Hedelund Rønn, Debbie Lindsay Shawcross, Nicola Kalk, Marianne Lerbæk Bergmann, Peter Jepsen, Gro Askgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsAlcohol use is the primary driver of disease progression in alcohol-related liver disease (ALD). We aimed to describe changes in alcohol use following a diagnosis of ALD.MethodsBetween 2021 and 2025, we screened 299 consecutive patients newly diagnosed with ALD at Zealand University Hospital, Denmark. At baseline, patients were interviewed about their current and lifetime alcohol use and their motivation to reduce alcohol consumption, and the alcohol biomarker phosphatidylethanol (PEth) was measured. Patients were followed for 90 days to assess changes in alcohol use and predictors of abstinence at 90 days. One standard drink was defined as 12 g of alcohol.ResultsOf the 104 patients newly diagnosed with ALD, 30% had decompensated cirrhosis, the median age was 64 years, and 76% were men. Patients reported progressively increasing alcohol consumption during the 30 years preceding their ALD diagnosis, with 76% consuming ≥20 drinks/week during the 5 years before diagnosis. Shortly after diagnosis, only 24% reported consuming ≥20 drinks/week, and 56% reported abstinence, whereas PEth indicated abstinence in only 37% of patients. Approximately 60% of patients reported high motivation and high confidence in achieving abstinence at diagnosis. Among patients who were abstinent at baseline, the risk of relapse within 90 days was 37% (95% CI 26–52). Higher motivation to change, greater confidence in achieving abstinence, and lower baseline PEth concentrations predicted abstinence at 90 days, whereas liver disease severity and AUDIT score were not significantly associated with abstinence.ConclusionsA diagnosis of ALD leads to a profound change in alcohol use, with approximately half of patients reporting abstinence after years of escalating alcohol consumption. However, early relapse remains common.Impact and implicationsOur study demonstrated a window for behavioural changeshortly after a diagnosis of alcohol-related liver disease, during which many patients reduced alcohol use or achieved abstinence. These findings highlighted early behavioural change in alcohol consumption but also a considerable risk of relapse after diagnosis. The results support integrating structured treatment for alcohol use disorder into liver clinics, although the observational design, self-reported alcohol use, and small sample size should be considered when interpreting the findings.Keywords: Alcohol-related liver disease, alcohol use, motivation to change drinking, new health condition, drinking behaviour, behavioural change, clinical study, phosphatidylethanol

Introduction

Alcohol is among the leading causes of liver disease in Europe and the United States.1,2 Alcohol-related liver disease (ALD) is typically diagnosed after several years of hazardous alcohol use, and more than half of patients fulfil the criteria for alcohol dependence.3,4 Whereas knowledge is clear about alcohol being the key driver of progression in ALD, less is known about how alcohol drinking patterns may change in response to a diagnosis of ALD.5 According to a recent meta-analysis, half of patients report abstinence at different time points after a diagnosis of ALD.5 However, since no previous studies have investigated abstinence immediately after an ALD diagnosis, abstinence may be overestimated in earlier studies, as we could imagine the heaviest alcohol consumers dying shortly after ALD diagnosis.6 Furthermore, most previous studies did not include a biomarker to detect alcohol use, and therefore, alcohol abstinence may have been overestimated when based solely on self-report.7

Because of the seriousness of an ALD diagnosis and its close association with harmful alcohol use, receiving this diagnosis may motivate individuals to change their drinking behaviour, resulting in alcohol cessation or reduced consumption. For instance, among patients with ALD, a recent hospitalization was associated with greater motivation to reduce alcohol consumption and subsequent engagement in alcohol use disorder treatment.8 However, previous studies have not investigated motivation to change alcohol use or predictors of abstinence in patients shortly after an ALD diagnosis. These investigations are necessary to design targeted, specialized help to promote alcohol use cessation in patients newly diagnosed with ALD.

In this study, we aimed to describe alcohol use before and after a diagnosis of ALD and to assess changes following the diagnosis, supplemented by measurement of phosphatidylethanol (PEth), a biomarker of alcohol use covering the preceding 3-4 weeks. In addition, we investigated whether motivation to and confidence in change were predictors of 90-day abstinence. To do so, we conducted a prospective clinical cohort study of patients newly diagnosed with ALD from 2021 to 2025.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We used data from an ongoing single-centre clinical prospective cohort study following patients with newly diagnosed ALD with a comprehensive assessment of alcohol use since 1st July 2021, based at the Department of Medicine, Zealand University Hospital, Koege, Denmark. See study flowchart in Fig. S1. The Department of Medicine covers referrals to hepatology from seven municipalities with nearly 330,000 citizens and is part of the tax-supported healthcare system accessible to all citizens of Denmark.

Patients with newly diagnosed ALD

Patients newly diagnosed with ALD were recruited from 1st July 2021 to 1st March 2025 during hospital admission or an outpatient visit for ALD. The ALD diagnosis was established by a consultant in hepatology based on the history of alcohol use, clinical examination suggestive of liver disease, biochemical tests, and imaging (CT or ultrasound). Patients were deemed eligible for the study if they (1) had been diagnosed with ALD within 3 months before the baseline visit, (2) had a fasting fibroscan value of ≥8 kPa at the baseline visit, (3) were aged ≥18 years, and (4) reported alcohol use above the Danish sensible drinking limits (>7 drinks/week for women and >14 drinks/week for men [1 drink equals 12 g of pure alcohol]) at some point during the year before the baseline visit. The exclusion criteria were not speaking Danish, lacking or withdrawing informed written consent, or pregnancy.

Baseline assessments

Trained research nurses who had no involvement in the clinical management of the study participants performed all baseline assessments presented below. Patients had blood samples drawn to calculate the model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score. Severity of liver disease was classified based on data from the fibroscan, imaging and symptoms of hepatic decompensation (ascites, variceal bleed and hepatic encephalopathy) as follows: a) advanced liver fibrosis was defined by a liver stiffness value of 8-12 kPa and absence of imaging suggestive of cirrhosis and absence of symptoms or signs of decompensation, b) compensated cirrhosis was defined by a liver stiffness of kPa >12 kPa and absence of symptoms or signs of decompensation, and finally c) decompensated cirrhosis defined as a liver stiffness of kPa >12 kPa and a symptom of hepatic decompensation.9,10 The self-reported weekly use of alcohol or duration of abstinence was obtained. To supplement this information, the concentration of the alcohol biomarker PEth in blood was determined.11 PEth is a direct biomarker of alcohol use in the preceding 3-4 weeks and has been validated in patients with liver disease.12 The research nurses conducted the alcohol use disorders identification Test (AUDIT) to screen for alcohol use disorder (AUD).13,14 The research nurses conducted the lifetime drinking history interview with patients to assess lifetime alcohol use15,16 and provide information about average monthly drinking days and units per drinking day during periods of at least one year. Patients were screened for motivation (how motivated are you to change your drinking behaviour?) and confidence (how confident are you that you would succeed if you tried?) to change drinking behaviour with a visual analogue scale from 1-10, which has been used previously in motivation studies.[17], [18], [19] Higher values indicate stronger motivation to or confidence in obtaining or maintaining abstinence. Patients were asked about their use of any pharmacological or behavioural treatment for AUD, whether provided through private or public healthcare, during the year before the baseline visit.

Follow-up for alcohol use

The research nurses completed the timeline followback questionnaire20 interview on alcohol use with the patient at the follow-up telephone interviews 30 days (n = 100) and 90 days (n = 96) after the baseline assessment. The questionnaire covers recalled alcohol use for each day since the last assessment using a calendar approach.

Statistical analysis

For all alcohol measurements, we calculated the average alcohol use for each patient, with 1 drink of alcohol equalling 12 g of pure alcohol. We used median (IQR) when reporting alcohol use, as we could not count on normally distributed data.

In a prospective analysis of alcohol use after ALD diagnosis, patients were followed up for alcohol use until 90 days after baseline, death, or drop-out, whichever occurred first.

In self-reported abstainers at baseline, we used the cumulative incidence function to calculate the relapse risk after 90 days of follow-up, considering the competing risk of death.24 Any self-reported alcohol use of >0 drinks was considered a relapse for this analysis. Finally, we used unadjusted binary logistic regression to obtain odds ratios for abstinence 90 days after the baseline assessment according to sex, age, AUDIT, liver disease severity, PEth, motivation to chance and confidence in change.

Register-based comparison ALD cohort

We created a register-based cohort from the Danish National Patient Registry25 to investigate whether our cohort was comparable with all patients registered with a first-time ALD diagnosis in our department. We included all patients diagnosed with ALD (ICD-10-code: K70) at Zealand University Hospital during 2021-2023. From the registries, we obtained data on sex, age, 30-day and 90-day mortality, and the MELD score.

Ethics

All patients provided written informed consent before inclusion. The study was conducted according to the Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Danish National Committee on Health Research Ethics Region Zealand (study number SJ-895) approved the study. The study was registered by the Danish Data Protection Agency (registration number EG-149-2020).

Results

Of the 299 patients screened, 222 were eligible and 104 with ALD were included, corresponding to a participation rate of 47% (Fig. 1). The median time since ALD diagnosis at baseline was 1.4 months (IQR 0.9–2.3), and the median age was 64 years (IQR 54–68) (Table 1). Overall, 79 patients (76%) were men and 25 (24%) were women. Patients with decompensated cirrhosis were more likely to have been admitted to hospital during the preceding year than those with advanced fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis.

Self-reported alcohol use before ALD diagnosis

From the first-ever drink until the diagnosis of ALD, patients had been drinking for a median of 41 years with almost no abstaining years, irrespective of sex (Table S1). Patients reported escalating alcohol use in the years leading up to the ALD diagnosis: 76% (79/104) had consumed ≥20 drinks (240 g of alcohol) per week during the five years preceding diagnosis, compared with 39% (39/100) during the period 26–30 years before diagnosis (Table S2). Average weekly lifetime alcohol use differed between subgroups, with patients aged <60 years drinking more than patients aged ≥60 years (33 drinks [396 g] vs. 21 drinks [252 g] per week) and men drinking more than women (28 drinks [336 g] vs. 8 drinks [168 g] per week) (Table S1). Additionally, the pattern of escalating alcohol use in the years leading up to an ALD diagnosis was observed across all sex and age subgroups (Figs. 2 and S2; Table S2).

Self-reported alcohol use at baseline

At baseline, shortly after an ALD diagnosis, 63% of 104 patients expressed 10-out-of-10 motivation to change drinking behaviour, and 58% expressed 10-out-of-10 confidence in their ability to change drinking behaviour (Table 2). In line with this, we observed a substantial change in patients’ self-reported alcohol use after ALD diagnosis (Fig. 3). While 2.9% reported abstinence during the months before diagnosis, 56% reported being abstinent for a median of 2 months at the baseline visit conducted ≤3 months after diagnosis (Table 2, Fig. 3). Patients with decompensated cirrhosis were more likely to report abstinence and to have a PEth value of <0.05 μmol/L than patients with liver fibrosis or compensated cirrhosis. Furthermore, they were more likely to report high motivation and confidence to change (74% vs. 58% for a 10-out-of-10 score for decompensated cirrhosis vs. less severe liver disease).

Overall, weekly alcohol consumption among patients who continued drinking decreased from 35 drinks [420 g] in the year before diagnosis to 21 drinks [252 g] at baseline (Tables 2 and S3; Fig. 3). The number of patients consuming ≥20 drinks [240 g] per week declined to 24% (25/104) at baseline from 76% in the last 5 years before diagnosis (Figs 4 and S2). All subgroups of sex and age reported a decrease in alcohol use after ALD diagnosis. Still, the percentage decrease was higher for patients aged ≥60 years (59%, from 34 drinks [408 g] per week in the year before ALD diagnosis to 14 drinks [168 g] per week at baseline) than for patients aged <60 years (33%, from 42 drinks [504 g] to 28 drinks [336 g] per week) (Table S4).

Alcohol biomarker at baseline

The level of PEth in patients’ blood at baseline showed that at least 63% had consumed alcohol in the last 3-4 weeks up to baseline (PEth value ≥0.05) (Table 3). Thus, PEth indicated underreporting compared with self-reported alcohol use at baseline. In total, we observed underreporting of self-reported alcohol use in 42 of 104 patients (40%).

Alcohol use during 90 days of follow-up and predictors of abstinence

During 90 days of follow-up, 5 (4.8%) patients died, and 3 (2.9%) dropped out (Fig. 1). Of 58 self-reported abstaining patients at baseline, 36.5% (95% CI 25.9-51.5) relapsed to drinking after 90 days of follow-up. Patients with 10-out-of-10 motivation and confidence in achieving alcohol abstinence at baseline were more likely to be abstinent at 90-day follow-up, whereas the opposite was true for those with a higher PEth value at baseline (Table 4). Other baseline variables were not statistically significantly associated with abstinence at 90 days of follow-up. Weekly alcohol consumption declined from 21 drinks [252 g] at baseline to 14 drinks [168 g] per week for the 54 self-reported alcohol consumers in the third month of follow-up (Fig. 4; Table S4). Notably, a quarter of all patients reported drinking at least 20 drinks [240 g] per week at baseline, and this proportion was unchanged after 90 days of follow-up (Fig. 4).

Comparison with the register-based ALD population

We identified 215 patients recorded with a first-time ALD diagnosis at Zealand University Hospital during the inclusion period for the clinical cohort study (Table S5). The register-based cohort had a higher 90-day mortality of 11.9% compared to 4.8% in our cohort, but was otherwise similar with respect to sex, age, and liver disease severity.

Discussion

In this clinical cohort of 104 patients with newly diagnosed ALD, we observed escalating use of alcohol in the decades leading up to the diagnosis of ALD. Alcohol use was highest during the five years preceding diagnosis, with patients reporting a median consumption of 38 drinks (456 g of alcohol) per week. This decreased to 21 drinks (252 g of alcohol) per week at baseline (≤3 months after ALD diagnosis). At baseline, about 60% of patients reported being highly motivated to achieve abstinence and highly confident in their ability to do so. Although 56% reported being abstinent, only 37% were classified as abstinent based on PEth. Still, we observed a sizeable reduction in patients’ alcohol use after an ALD diagnosis. However, after 90 days of follow-up, 37% of the abstaining patients relapsed. High motivation and confidence in alcohol abstinence at baseline were predictors of abstinence at 90-day follow-up, whereas a PEth above 0.05 μmol/L at baseline was a predictor of non-abstinence at 90 days.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to follow alcohol use in an inception cohort enrolled shortly after an ALD diagnosis.26 Previous studies have investigated abstinence in patients with ALD,5 but none have described the trajectory of alcohol use up to an ALD diagnosis and how it changes afterward. Hofer et al.27 reported abstinence in 75% of 320 patients with alcohol-related cirrhosis, which is supported by Louvet et al.,28 who observed abstinence in 70% of 640 patients with alcohol-related cirrhosis compared to 56% in our study. Neither the study by Hofer et al. nor the one by Louvet et al. reported data on PEth to supplement self-reported abstinence. Patients in the study by Louvet et al. were diagnosed at a median of 24 months before enrolment, compared to a median of 1.5 months before enrolment in our cohort. The difference in abstinence rates may reflect survivor bias, whereby patients who continue to consume alcohol are more likely to die earlier, increasing the proportion of abstinent survivors over time. The differences in abstinence across studies may also reflect differences in study populations. The study by Hofer et al. included only patients undergoing hepatic venous pressure gradient measurements, while Louvet et al. restricted inclusion to patients with compensated cirrhosis confirmed by liver biopsy. In contrast, our study might have included a less selected ALD cohort that may be more representative of the general ALD population, as supported by our comparison with the registry-based cohort (Table S5).

Our findings add to the limited literature on lifetime drinking history in patients with ALD.4,29,30 In line with our results, Nielsen et al.29 and Stokkeland et al.30 reported a total number of drinking years of nearly 40 years in patients diagnosed with ALD and higher lifetime alcohol consumption in men than in women. The lifetime alcohol use for women was 14,000 drinks in the study by Stokkeland et al. (n = 13), about 33,000 in the study by Nielsen et al. (n = 13), and about 17,000 drinks in our study (n = 25). For men, lifetime alcohol use was 46,000 drinks in the study by Stokkeland (n = 37), 51,000 drinks in the study by Nielsen (n = 26), and 61,000 drinks in our study (n = 79). Our finding that patients aged <60 years had higher lifetime alcohol use than those aged ≥60 years is consistent with a study by Arico et al.31 This may reflect age-related vulnerability to liver injury, whereby lower levels of alcohol use can lead to chronic liver disease in older patients compared to younger patients.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate motivation to change alcohol use in a cohort of patients with newly diagnosed ALD and to show that high motivation predicted abstinence 90 days later. Of 46 patients with current use of alcohol at baseline, 35% had the highest motivation score of 10. This proportion was slightly higher than the 30% observed in our previous study of 436 patients with ALD who were still drinking and had been diagnosed up to 10 years before the motivation assessment (8). Chang et al. found the lowest motivation of the three studies, with less than 15% of patients with ALD being motivated to cut down.32 The differences in the fraction of motivated patients could be due to variability in measurement tools, but motivation most likely fluctuates according to time since diagnosis. Patients with newly diagnosed ALD may be more motivated to reduce drinking, as a new health condition is associated with a reduction in alcohol use.33 Lastly, uptake of AUD treatment was higher in our study than in the study by Rogal et al.34 (28% vs. 16%), although it remained low given the high prevalence of AUD.

The prospective data collection of alcohol use after an ALD diagnosis is a robust design,[35], [36], [37] allowing us to collect time-varying data on alcohol use. We do not believe attrition bias considerably affected our results, as only 2.9% dropped out. On the other hand, our study may be subject to selection bias, as included patients had to be fit for a face-to-face interview, and we, therefore, lack the most ill and likely the heaviest drinkers. This can affect results in different ways. First, our cohort may consume less alcohol during life than the background ALD population, and we may underestimate their true lifetime alcohol use. Second, the included patients were likely to be more motivated to reduce drinking and receive more AUD treatment than patients who refused to participate or did not show up. This could lead to an overestimation of the effect of ALD diagnosis on alcohol use. Lastly, we compared our clinical ALD cohort with a register-based cohort and found higher short-term mortality in the register-based cohort, which implies that our findings may not apply to patients with newly diagnosed end-stage liver disease who may have consumed more alcohol during life than our cohort.

Our study used retrospective lifetime alcohol histories, some extending over 20 years, and may be affected by recall bias. However, a validation study found high concordance between prospective assessment and the retrospective assessment of alcohol use with the lifetime drinking history interview, with correlation coefficients of 0.47-0.69 for alcohol quantity and frequency.16 Results based on the lifetime drinking history are more likely to be affected by recall bias than those obtained by PEth and timeline followback shortly after ALD diagnosis.

A final limitation was the lack of detailed self-reported data on alcohol use trajectories between the time of ALD diagnosis and the baseline visit, which may introduce some temporal misclassification. However, most patients were included 1–2 months after ALD diagnosis, and PEth provided information on alcohol use during the 3–4 weeks preceding the baseline visit. Approximately 90% of abstinent patients reported abstinence durations of less than 3 months from the diagnosis date, indicating that abstinence was largely initiated in close temporal proximity to diagnosis (Table S6).

A strength of our study was the use of one of the most sensitive direct markers for alcohol use, PEth,38 and validated alcohol questionnaires.15,20 When comparing self-reported alcohol use with PEth, we found a considerably lower underreporting of 40% than the 58% found by Scholten et al. in 279 patients with chronic liver disease.39 Finally, the magnitude of change before and after ALD diagnosis could not wholly be explained by underreporting at baseline, as PEth alone suggested that at least 37% (PEth <0.05 μmol/L) had reduced their alcohol use (Table 3).

In conclusion, our findings show a window of opportunity for behaviour change shortly after an ALD diagnosis, with more than one-third succeeding with alcohol cessation and many lowering their alcohol use. However, the remaining patients continued with harmful alcohol use or relapsed from abstinence shortly after diagnosis. We should pay particular attention to patients diagnosed with ALD who are younger than 60 years, as they appear to have greater alcohol dependence and higher alcohol consumption. We should acknowledge that patients with newly diagnosed ALD are motivated and capable of change, and at the same time, we need to address the frequently overlooked comorbidity of AUD in these patients, which may challenge the achievement of abstinence. The utilization of AUD treatment was low in our study, although it has been shown to decrease the risk of decompensation and improve long-term survival in patients with cirrhosis.34 Stigma, guilt, and lack of understanding of the importance of AUD treatment are barriers to initiating AUD treatment in patients with alcohol-related cirrhosis.40 This emphasizes the importance of investigating the effect of integrating AUD treatment into or in close collaboration with liver clinics, an approach that has shown promising results in a recent study by Mellinger et al.41 Further research is needed to evaluate alcohol use interventions integrated into liver clinics during the early period after an ALD diagnosis, when patients appear to be highly motivated to change.
