Pain and sleep disturbance were negatively associated with function and community mobility after hospitalization
Authors: Susan N Hastings, Karen M Stechuchak, Kasey Decosimo, Caitlin B Kappler, Daniel Guidot, Kelli D Allen, Amy Webster, Ashley Choate, Courtney H Van Houtven, Cassie Meyer, Jaime Hughes, Cynthia J Coffman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The inconsistent nature of functional recovery after hospitalization points to gaps in our understanding of its determinants. The goal of this study was to examine associations between post-discharge pain interference and sleep disturbance on function and community mobility following hospitalization for medical illness.Methods:We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of data collected as part of a larger study evaluating a hospital-based mobility program. Participants were aged ≥60 and within one month of discharge from one of eight Veterans Affairs (VA) hospitals between September 2017 and August 2019. Data were collected through telephone surveys. Primary predictors of pain interference and sleep disturbance were measured by PROMIS-4 short forms. Outcomes were function measured by Late-Life Function and Disability Instruments (LLFDI overall function and advanced lower extremity function scores) and community mobility measured by Life Space. For each outcome, separate multiple linear regression models were fit including the key explanatory variables of interest and selected covariates.Results:Participants (n = 618) were mean age 70.6 years, 95% male, 25% Black, and 34% lived alone; overall eight percent reported being discharged to a nursing home, and 15% being readmitted to the hospital. Overall, 39.9% of participants reported moderate/severe pain interference; of these, approximately half also reported at least some degree of sleep disturbance. Moderate/severe sleep disturbance was endorsed by 19%; of these, more than 75% also experienced some degree of pain interference. In adjusted models, pain interference and sleep disturbance were negatively associated with total function, lower extremity function, and community mobility (P-values <0.01).Conclusions:In this sample of recently hospitalized older adults, pain interference and sleep disturbance were independently associated with functional limitations and reduced community mobility. Primary care providers should consider assessing for pain and sleep problems during post-hospitalization visits to support optimal functional recovery.Keywords: Geriatrics, Pain, Sleep disturbance, Functional status, Community mobility

Introduction

Nearly one-third of older adult inpatients experience loss of independence after hospitalization [1,2]. Post-discharge functional status is often measured as the ability to perform activities of daily living; however, emerging evidence suggests that broader measures, such as community mobility, provide critical insight into not only physical capacity but also participation in society [3]. Lower community mobility has been associated with greater risk of subsequent hospitalization and skilled nursing facility stays, making it a potentially valuable and patient-centered measure of recovery [4]. Although the risk of functional decline following hospitalization has been widely reported [5], there is significant variation in how older adults respond to the acute stressor of hospitalization [6]. For example, one study demonstrated four trajectories of functional recovery after hospitalization for pneumonia ranging from very poor to excellent, with pneumonia severity measures only modestly correlated with functional status trajectory categories [7].

Persistent pain has been identified as one factor that can contribute to meaningful declines in physical function among older adults [8]. Pain, as well as chronic sleep disturbance, often characterized as non-restorative or poor-quality sleep [9], can lead to decreased mobility and increased risk of falls, further complicating recovery and potentially leading to unplanned readmissions [10]. Much of prior research on physical symptoms like pain or insomnia post-hospitalization has focused on patients experiencing critical illness and receiving care in the intensive care unit (ICU) [11,12]. The relationships between pain, sleep, and multiple domains of function following hospitalization for medical illness are not well-characterized.

The primary goal of this cross-sectional study was to examine associations of post-discharge pain and sleep disturbance with physical function and community mobility assessed in the month (30 days) following hospitalization. We hypothesized that post-discharge pain interference and sleep disturbances would be negatively associated with physical function after controlling for baseline frailty and other patient factors. Understanding whether pain and sleep disturbances factor in post-discharge function could suggest targets for future interventions for older adults during the high-risk hospital to home transition period.

Methods

Participants

We conducted a cross-sectional study of patients from an implementation trial of a hospital mobility program (Clinical Trials NCT03300336) [13]. Patients were eligible if they were ≥ 60 years of age, community-dwelling (not in a nursing home or institutional care) prior to admission, and able and willing to provide informed consent. Eligible patients were discharged between September 2017 and August 2019 from a participating general medical unit of a hospital in the parent study; patients treated in surgical or intensive care units were not included. Also excluded were patients with very short stays (≤ 2 days), high-risk for suicide (based on flag in the electronic health record), cognitive impairment, difficulty communicating, or no telephone access. Potentially eligible patients received a recruitment letter within 30 days of discharge, followed by a phone call to verify eligibility and obtain verbal informed consent for participation. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Durham VA Health Care System (IRB #2040).

Survey measures

Surveys were administered over the phone by trained research staff. Primary predictors of interest were pain interference, defined as the extent to which pain interferes with daily activities, and sleep disturbance, defined as decreased sleep duration, sleep quality, and/or restoration associated with sleep, measured by Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS®) 4-item short-forms [14]. Pain interference was measured with four questions asking about how much pain has gotten in the way of various aspects of respondent’s lives in the prior week (e.g., How much did pain interfere with your day-to-day activities? How much did pain interfere with your ability to participate in social activities?). Response options were on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). Sleep disturbance was measured with four questions. One sleep disturbance question inquired directly about sleep quality in the prior week (e.g., In the past 7 days…My sleep quality was…), with response options on a 5-point scale ranging from 5 (very poor) to 1 (very good). The other questions assessed sleep quality (e.g., My sleep was refreshing) as well as problems with sleep (e.g., In the past 7 days…I had a problem with my sleep…), with responses on a 5-point scale ranging from 5 (very much) to 1 (not at all). PROMIS scales produce standardized T-scores based on population reference values among the U.S. adult population (mean of 50 and standard deviation [SD] of 10), with higher scores indicating higher pain interference or sleep disturbance.

Outcomes

Outcomes were function and community mobility within one month (30 days) following discharge. Physical function was assessed with the overall function and advanced lower-extremity subscale of the Late-Life Function and Disability Instrument (LLFDI) [15]. For LLFDI Function Component, respondents report the current degree of difficulty they have in performing physical function tasks. Response levels include none, a little, some, quite a lot, or cannot do. Each LLFDI subscale is scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating high levels of physical function and lower scores indicating poor function. The Life Space Questionnaire (LSQ) assesses the extent of mobility in the person’s environment. Comprised of five items, each addressing a specific life-space zone ranging from the bedroom to areas outside of town and beyond, the LSQ is a reliable measure and has construct and criterion validity in samples of older adults [16,17]. Respondents are asked to reflect on their activities “within the past month since your discharge on xx/xx date”. Total LSQ score ranges from 0–120, where higher scores indicate a larger life space, combining the components of life-space level attained, degree of independence, and frequency of attainment.

Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize participant characteristics and scores for explanatory variables and outcomes. Histogram plots of PROMIS pain interference and sleep disturbance were generated and scores were also reported using established guidance for cut points as normal (0–55), mild (56–60), and moderate/severe (61 or greater) [18]. For each of the three outcomes (LLFDI total function, LLFDI advanced lower extremity, and LSQ), separate multiple linear regression models were fit including the key explanatory variables (pain interference and sleep disturbance) as continuous variables with selected covariates assessed from VA electronic health record data including VA hospital (8-level variable indicating which of the 8 VA hospitals the participant was admitted to), age, patient residence urban vs. rural, and Jen Frailty Index (JFI) [19] which is a score calculated from VA and Medicare diagnosis codes used to measure the risk of long-term care admissions. Survey-reported covariates were selected to represent factors that may be independently associated with function and included race, education, inadequate income, currently working for pay, lives alone, discharge location, and hospital readmission. Standard regression diagnostics were examined. To aid interpretation of multiple regression models, simple linear regression models for all three outcomes were fit with the key explanatory variables of interest as well as each of the selected covariates. Analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Survey respondents were recruited following discharge from eight VA hospitals in the Southern, Northeast, and Midwest regions of the United States with daily internal medicine census ranges from 32 to 120 patients [18]. Overall, 6580 hospitalizations were assessed for eligibility with chart reviews and phone-screens. A total of 644 respondents agreed to participate; of these, 618 had complete survey data and were included in analyses (Fig. 1). Patients’ mean age was 70.6 years (SD=7.4). Most were male (95%) and 25% were Black. Mean JFI score was 6.1 (SD=1.8), which is categorized as high frailty (Table 1) [19].

Discussion

In this sample of 618 older Veterans recently experiencing hospitalization, pain interference and sleep disturbance were common, often co-occurring, and associated with worse functional status. Both observed data and model estimates point to a relationship between higher pain interference and clinically important differences in physical function [20,21] as well as community mobility [22]. Effects were similar in direction but smaller in magnitude for sleep disturbance. Our findings extend previous work in three important ways. First, we demonstrate older adults’ experience of moderate/severe pain and sleep disturbance, both individually and together, in a broad population of older adults hospitalized for medical illness, adding to previous research limited to specific acute conditions [23,24] or settings [25]. Second, our 8-hospital study extends the generalizability of a recent single-site study that also demonstrated a negative relationship between poor sleep and post-discharge functional ability [26]. Third, we add to the literature on risk factors for reduced community mobility following hospital discharge [27], which had not previously examined pain or sleep as potentially contributing factors.

Our study assessed pain and sleep symptoms approximately one month following hospital discharge. We were unable to determine whether pain or sleep problems originated prior to or during hospitalization; however, it is noteworthy that the hospital environment itself has been identified as a potential contributor to worsening pain and sleep disturbances. Hospitalized patients spend most of their time being sedentary [28]. Inactivity is associated with chronic pain, and sedentary behavior is a robust predictor of insomnia symptoms [29]. Pain and sleep problems can also interact with one another. A recent systematic review concluded that sleep problems are associated with an increased risk of chronic musculoskeletal pain [30], and pain is often identified by patients as a leading cause of lack of sleep in the hospital [31]. Some evidence suggests that insomnia that begins in the hospital can be slow to resolve [11]. Taken together, this suggests that inpatient interventions to increase physical activity, reduce sedentary time, and promote healthy sleep during hospitalization could yield long-lasting benefits, reducing post-discharge symptom burden and improving long-term functional outcomes.

Our findings are highly relevant for primary care providers, who are often the first point of contact for patients transitioning from hospital to home. The prevalence of pain interference and sleep disturbance—both strongly associated with reduced physical function and community mobility—suggests that post-discharge assessments could yield clinically valuable information and should not be limited to those who were hospitalized with obviously painful conditions such as an injury or surgery. Recognizing pain and sleep disturbances as potentially modifiable contributors to poor post-discharge function can help primary care providers identify at-risk patients and support recovery through tailored symptom management and rehabilitation referrals during the high-risk transition period. Clinical innovations to support recovery of ICU survivors [32] such as post-discharge physical and occupational therapy interventions [33], and nurse-led post discharge recovery support programs [34] warrant further research as potentially valuable models of care for patients at high risk for functional limitations after hospitalization for medical illness.

Study findings should be interpreted in the context of an older, predominantly male sample, all of whom were community-dwelling prior to hospitalization. The sample was only Veteran participants who may have distinct physical or emotional exposures or injuries that could affect pain and sleep. Nonetheless, the inclusion of participants from eight hospitals across geographically diverse regions strengthens the generalizability of the results, and some VA populations may be reasonably compared to non-VA Medicare beneficiaries [35]. Older adults and those with poor health status such as mobility and function impairments are associated with lower survey response rates [36] and we noted that the mean age of survey respondents (70.6) was slightly lower than the age of patients in the parent trial (72.9) [13,37]. Lastly, a key limitation is the reliance on self-reported, cross-sectional data from a parent study focused on an inpatient mobility intervention, in which pain and sleep were not primary outcomes and clinical diagnostic assessments were not conducted. However, a notable strength is the use of validated patient-reported outcome measures, which provide valuable insight into the patient experience.

Conclusion

Our findings point to the need for further attention to pain interference and sleep disturbance in older adults prior to, during, and after hospitalization. Early identification and effective management of these symptoms may enhance recovery and improve post-discharge outcomes. Recommended strategies include targeting hospital-related contributors to sleep disruption [38], implementing routine screening for pain and sleep issues at discharge, and promoting behavioral interventions aimed at supporting activity levels and improving sleep quality in the post-hospital period [39].
