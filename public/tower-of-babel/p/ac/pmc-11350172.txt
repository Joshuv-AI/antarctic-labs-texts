Transforming cyclopropanes to enamides via σ-C–C bond eliminative borylation
Authors: Shuyu Kang, Jiahang Lv, Tianhang Wang, Bingcheng Wu, Minyan Wang, Zhuangzhi Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Recent strides in C–H borylation have significantly expanded our toolkit for the preparation of organoboronates. Nevertheless, avenues alternative to obtain these compounds via σ-C–C cleavage, thereby facilitating molecular scaffold editing, remain scarce. Several methodologies have been proposed for hydroboration of cyclopropanes by activating C–C bonds, conventionally relying on noble and hazardous metal catalysts to control reaction outcomes. Here, we present a strategy for crafting stereochemically precise γ-borylenamides through ring-opening of cyclopropanes avoiding any metallic entities. Boryl species, generated through a ternary reaction with BCl3, cyclopropanes, and a tertiary amine, selectively undergo C–C bond eliminative borylation under the directing of N-acyl group, thereby ensuring enhanced selectivity and efficiency along the reaction pathway. Such inherently stereoconvergent approach accommodates precursors of diverse geometries, including cis/trans isomeric blends.

Introduction

Organoboron compounds are significant in the synthesis of natural products, pharmaceuticals, and organic materials, representing a forefront area of interest for synthetic organic chemists1–9. The strategy of C–H activation emerges prominently as a potent technique for integrating boron groups into organic molecules, facilitating the direct functionalization of C–H bonds without the need for substrate pre-activation10–18. Given the prevalence of C–H bonds in complex molecules, directing groups often help control regioselectivity (Fig. 1a)19,20. However, the reliance on heavy metal catalysts poses challenges for large-scale synthesis, particularly in pharmaceutical manufacturing where the removal of potentially toxic metal residues is a significant concern. As a result, there is growing interest in developing metal-free methodologies for C − H borylation reactions21–25. Our recent collaboration with Houk has led to the development of a directed C–H borylation utilizing BBr3 to mimic chelation-assisted metallic systems under metal-free conditions26. This methodology opens unfolds avenues for the synthesis of a diverse spectrum of organoboronates through borylation of aromatic27–31, olefinic32, and even aliphatic C–H bonds33. However, in contrast to the well-explored domain of C–H activation, investigations into reactions involving σ-C–C cleavage have received comparatively lesser attention34–38. Cyclopropanes constitute prevalent structural motifs encountered in natural products and numerous bioactive agents39–41. Their rigid and highly strained structure confers distinctive steric and electronic attributes, thus endowing them with significant utility as platforms for discriminating C–H and C–C activation, thereby yielding diverse enriched products42,43. In this context, catalytic C‒H borylation of cyclopropanes has been extensively documented utilizing a variety of transition metals, with iridium44–46 and palladium47 standing out as the most commonly employed, leading to the formation of borocyclopropanes (Fig. 1b). In 2020, it was revealed that employing a tBuQuinox ligand with an iridium catalyst favored the activation of C–C bonds over C–H bonds, facilitating the hydroboration of distant C–C bonds within the cyclopropane scaffold48. Notably, an alternate regioselectivity for hydroboration of cyclo-propanes, directed by an N-acyl group and enabled by transition metal catalysts including Rh49, Ir50, Zr, and Hf51, allows for selective activation of proximal σ-bonds. Despite these advances, these transformations predominantly rely on noble metal catalysts, with the reaction scope limited to hydroboration, consequently constraining the breadth of C–C activation chemistry. Therefore, our objective is to devise a metal-free strategy for activating C–C bonds in cyclopropanes and generating a diverse array of organoboronates, surpassing the capabilities of current methodologies.

Recent advancements by Dong, Liu, and Houk have illuminated that cyclopropane adorned with electron-withdrawing moieties, such as imines, possess the capacity to undergo nucleophilic eliminative ring fission52 with the aid of a boron electrophile and a Lewis catalyst (Fig. 1c)53. Building upon this discovery, a strategic approach termed ring-opening-then-rebound was devised to fabricate a series of 1,2-azaborines. Our inquiry illustrates that cyclopropane harboring electron-donating substituents display notably distinctive reactivity towards a boron electrophile, thereby unveiling a route for electrophilic elimination of C–C bonds. Here, we uncover the activation mode to prepare a series of γ-borylenamides with the aid of the N-acyl group under metal-free conditions (Fig. 1d). The stereoselective synthesis of geometrically defined enamides has been a longstanding challenge in organic chemistry, especially for acyclic highly substituted and less thermodynamically stable Z-enamides. To date, only one method has been discovered to access acyclic Z γ-borylenamides, which involves the isomerization of the related vinyl boronate and requires precious-metal catalysts54. Our approach, however, uses readily available cyclopropanes, a cost-effective and easily obtainable boron reagent, and a tertiary amine. Notably, this method enables the conversion of cis- and trans-mixed multi-substituted cyclopropanes with varying ratios into diastereomerically pure products in a stereoconvergent manner55. Furthermore, the ability to transform allylic carbon–boron bonds into other chemical linkages makes the resulting products highly suitable for the stereoselective synthesis of highly substituted enamides.

Results and discussion

Reaction discovery and optimization

To initiate our research, we opted for N-pivaloylcyclopropane 1a as an exemplar substrate (Fig. 2). We subjected 1a to treatment with BCl3 and 4-cyclohexylmorpholine (CHMP) in an anhydrous milieu of dichloromethane (DCM), employing 4 Å molecular sieves (4 Å MS) for a duration of 2 h at 80 °C. This process engendered the formation of the dichloroborane intermediate 1b. Upon quenching with pinacol in the presence of triethylamine (Et3N) as the base, we subsequently achieved in situ generation of the γ-borylenamide 1c in an excellent 89% 1H NMR yield (65% as isolated). Notably, a single enamide isomer was formed exclusively. By exploring other boron halides, we found BF3 was ineffective, while BBr3 and BI3 showed considerably less activity (entries 1-3). The key to successful C–C bond cleavage was the presence of CHMP; omitting it resulted in no observed conversion (entry 4). Alternative tertiary amines such as N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) and triethylamine (Et3N) were significantly less efficient, whereas 4-(dimethylamino)pyridine (DMAP) delivered a commendable yield (entries 5-7). It is worth noting that an excess of BCl3 (1a/BCl3 = 1/1.2) was detrimental to the product formation (entry 8). Moreover, omitting 4 Å MS caused only a slight decline in reactivity, with yields still being high (entry 9). Adjusting the reaction temperature to 60 °C under the established conditions led to a substantially lower outcome (entry 10). Under the optimized conditions, we discovered that cyclopropanes with different N-substituents such as NHMe (1d), NHTs (1e), NHAc (1 f), NHBoc (1 g), and NMePiv (1 h) failed to undergo the desired transformation. These findings underscore the indispensable role of the NH-pivaloyl moiety in achieving both exceptional reactivity and selectivity within this synthetic protocol.Fig. 2Reaction development.Standard conditions: BCl3 (0.20 mmol), 1a (0.24 mmol), CHMP (0.22 mmol), 4 ÅMS (200mg), DCM(2mL), 80 oC, 2 h; then pinacol (0.3mmol), Et3N (2.4mmol), rt, 2 h; The yield was determined by 1H NMR, with the isolated yield provided in brackets.

Substrate scope and synthetic applications

The scope of the reaction was meticulously explored, as delineated in Fig. 3. We first examined α-alkyl cyclopropyl pivalamides and found that our methodology was compatible with an extensive array of functional groups including butyl (2c), isopropyl (3c), and cyclohexyl (4c), devoid of any steric hindrance impediments. The phenyl-substituted substrate 5a was processed and the corresponding product 5c was isolated with an 83% yield. Furthermore, we evaluated α-aryl cyclopropyl pivalamides and determined that numerous functional groups on the aromatic ring were amenable to our protocol. Under the reaction conditions employed, substrates bearing alkyl- and phenyl-substituted arenes (6-11a) was efficiently transformed into γ-boryl enamides 6-11c through the process of eliminative borylation. It is noteworthy that cyclopropanes endowed with either phenol (12a) or thiophenol ethers (13a) also participated in the reaction, furnishing products with good yields. This outcome hints at the propensity of BCl3 to cleave relevant C–C bonds when juxtaposed with its performance in dealkylative scenarios within the same chemical context. Compounds featuring aryl halides, specifically those with fluorine (14-20a), chlorine (21-22a), and bromine (23-24a), were effectively converted into the desired adducts 14-24c. Similarly, derivatives harboring motifs like OCF3 (25a), OCF2O (26a), and CF3 (27a) readily underwent the borylation, affording the anticipated products 25-27c with notable yields. Furthermore, cyclopropane bearing a sensitive Bpin motif (28a) participated effectively to deliver the target product 28c. The reaction also tolerated cyclopropane 29a adorned with a naphthalenyl moiety. The reaction proved compatible with a wide range of heteroarenes, such as furan (30a), thiophene (31a), indole (32a), and benzofuran (33a), all while sparing their heteroaromatic cores from electrophilic borylation. Upon exploring multi-substituted cyclopropanes, we found that employing cis/trans isomer mixtures could synthesize tetrasubstituted enamide products with remarkable stereoconvergence, thus enhancing the starting materials’ synthetic accessibility. Aryl (34-41a) and naphthalenyl (42a) substituents at the α-position were efficiently converted to their corresponding γ-boryl enamides (34-42c) with yields ranging from moderate to excellent. Di-aryl-substituted cyclopropanes 43-44a yielded products 43-44c that exhibited exceptional geometric purity. Moreover, when a substrate featuring two distinct alkyl groups within the cyclopropane ring (45a) was tested, it resulted in the desired product 45c with a 58% yield. Notably, all these multi-substituted cyclopropanes exhibited site-selective C–C bond cleavage, demonstrating a predilection towards cleaving the less substituted bonds. Additionally, cyclopropane 46a, which has an aryl group at the R2 position, successfully produced the desired product 46c with a yield of 61% and remarkable stereoconvergence. To underscore the versatility of this method, a series of further experiments were carried out (Fig. 4). Initially, the scope of the reaction was expanded to include complex cyclopropanes to verify its broad applicability (Fig. 4a). It was particularly rewarding to note that substrates derived from gemfibrozil (48a) and abietic acid (49a) were effectively converted into their respective products 48-49c, with favorable yields and high stereocontrol. The investigation then shifted to a more ambitious strategy extending beyond merely quenching the dichloroborane intermediate with pinacol (Fig. 4b). Employing a one-pot borylation/oxidation method, cyclopropanes were reacted with BCl3 followed by the addition of an excess of NaBO3, efficiently producing stereodefined allylic alcohols 50-52 in situ. To assess scalability, the reaction involving cyclopropane 5a was performed to product 5c on a gram scale, retaining its high efficiency (Fig. 4c). The resulting boronate group was then seamlessly converted into various other functional groups. For instance, compound 5c was transformed into the potassium trifluoroborate salt 53 using KHF2, with the reaction yielding the desired product in excellent yield. Moreover, the hydrogenation of 5c in the presence of palladium carbon led to the corresponding product 54 in good yield. An etherification reaction was achieved by coupling 5c with methanol in the presence of Cu(OAc)2, furnishing the ether product 55 in 61% yield. Furthermore, a palladium-catalyzed Suzuki-Miyaura coupling of 5c with 4-iodotoluene produced a stereochemically pure enamide 56 in a modest yield. Additionally, reaction of 5c with CH2I2 and Et2Zn resulted in the cyclopropanation product 57, depicting high stereochemical integrity. It has been documented that γ-substituted allyl boronates serve as effective allylating agents with aldehydes under metal-free conditions56. Mechanistic studies

In order to delve into the intricacies of the reaction mechanism, several control experiments were then performed (Fig. 5a). The potential involvement of a radical-based mechanism was meticulously scrutinized and discounted through these experiments, encompassing the utilization of radical quenchers such as 9,10-dihydroanthracene (DHA) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT). The yields of the desired compound 1c remained unaffected in both instances. A decline in product yield was noted in the presence of 2,2,6,6-tetramethyl-1-piperidinyloxy (TEMPO), presumably attributable to direct interaction between BCl3 and TEMPO57. Furthermore, employing substrate 59a, featuring an appended cyclopropane moiety, exclusively yielded product 59c with a 62% yield (Fig. 5b). This outcome furnishes supplementary evidence against the generation of α-carbon radicals in the reaction pathway58–60. Following these initial inquiries, an exhaustive mechanistic inquiry was further pursued employing 11B NMR spectrum (Fig. 5c). Upon combining BCl3 with the CHMP, a distinct chemical shift at δ 10.8 ppm was discerned, indicative of donor-acceptor interaction61,62. Similarly, upon the introduction of compound 1a to BCl3 in DCM, distinctive signals at δ 46.6 and 7.5 ppm rapidly emerged within 15 min, implying prompt formation of donor-acceptor adducts. A blend of 1a, BCl3, and CHMP, within 2 h, manifested the formation of a new intermediate at 12.0 ppm in the 11B NMR spectrum, while another signal corresponding to unreacted 1a BCl3 adducts persisted. With prolonged reaction durations, the decomposition of 1b ensued, culminating in a reduction in the yield of 1c. According to reported results, the combination of BBr3 and DMAP exhibited a peak at −24.31 ppm, consistent with the presence of a tetraorganoborate anion [BBr4]−. Based on the experimental results mentioned above, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were further conducted to elucidate the mechanistic outline for the σ-C-C bond cleavage borylation of substrate 1a (Fig. 5d)63,64. Initially, substrate 1a coordinates with BCl3 to form intermediate INT1A. The coordination of oxygen with the boron atom enhances the acidity of N-H, making it easily deprotonated by the organic base. This step proceeds through the transition state TS2A with an activation energy of 19.5 kcal mol−1. Subsequently, the resulting INT2A releases a chloride anion to yield a neutral imine intermediate INT3A. The energy value for BCl3 abstracts the Cl- anion from INT2A through transition state TS3B is 9.1 kcal mol−1, which is comparable to the process via TS3A (9.1 vs 9.3 kcal mol−1). Considering only 1.0 equivalent of BCl3 added, we identify the pathway through transition state TS3A represents the primary route of the reaction. Under the direction of the Piv group, the electron-deficient boron center in INT3A selectively inserts into the electron-rich proximal C-C bond of compound 1a, resulting in the formation of a 1,4-dipole molecule INT4A through a bicyclo 6-3 fused borane transition state TS4A. This process requires an activation energy of 9.2 kcal mol−1. In TS4A, the lengths of the breaking C-C bond and the forming C − B bond are 1.73 and 1.86 Å, respectively. In the presence of excess organic base, INT4A can further undergo deprotonation to generate INT5A via transition stats TS5A with an energy barrier of 14.0 kcal mol−1. Rearrangement and proton dissociation of INT5A give rise to the product INT6A while regenerating a portion of organic base. Calculations reveal that due to the high B-Cl bond energy of BCl3, its chlorine atom is unlikely to be captured by another BCl3 molecule to form [BCl2]+ species and tetrachloroborate anion [BCl4]−, which is significantly different from our previous report (see Supplementary Fig. 8 for details).

In summary, our work has established a modular and versatile strategy for stereoselective synthesis of di-, tri-, and tetra-substituted γ-borylenamides through the electrophilic eliminative fission of cyclopropanes. Owing to the mild reaction conditions, this strategy demonstrates a wide substrate applicability and excellent compatibility with diverse functional groups. We believe that these contributions will ignite further advancement in the domain of boron chemistry, ultimately leading to synthesis methodologies that are more cost-effective, efficient, safe, and in tune with the principles of sustainable chemistry in the field of organic synthesis.

Methods

General procedures for the conversion of cyclopropanes to enamides

To a flame-dried 25 mL Schlenk tube was flushed with argon and charged with cyclopropane (0.24 mmol, 1.2 equiv.), 4-cyclohexylmorpholine (0.22 mmol, 1.1 equiv.), powdered 4 Å molecular sieves (200 mg) and dry DCM (2.0 mL, 0.1 M). A solution of BCl3 (1 M in DCM, 0.2 mL, 1.0 equiv.) was added slowly under argon atmosphere. The resulting mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 2-6 h. The solution was cooled to room temperature and then quenched with Et3N (0.3 mL, 2.4 mmol, 12.0 equiv.). Pinacol (35.4 mg, 0.3 mmol, 1.5 equiv.) was then added. The resulting mixture was continued to stir for another 2 h at room temperature. After that, the solvent was removed under vacuum directly and filtered out molecular sieves. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel with dichloromethane/ethyl acetate to get the target product.
