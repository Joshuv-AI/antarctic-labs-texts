Macrolithotype-Controlled Permeability Variations in Anthracitic Coal under High-Pressure and High-Temperature Conditions
Authors: Xianyu Lu, Yong Li, Siying Hao, Yun Li, Lizhi Li, Yaxu Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The permeability of deep coal reservoirs is sensitive to high-temperature and high-pressure environments, while the responses of coals with different material compositions are not clear. Here, coals with four different macrolithotypes, i.e., bright, semibright, semi-dull, and dull, were measured under temperatures of 60, 70, 80, and 90 °C and confining pressures from 5 to 35 MPa. Then the sensitivity-influencing factors were discussed by Pearson’s correlation analysis. The results show that the permeability of all the four coals exhibits a negative power–law relationship with increasing effective stresses, with the bright and dull coals decreasing from 0.148 to 0.00005 mD and from 0.3472 to 0.0055 mD under 60 °C, separately. The bright and semibright coals are of high-permeability damage ratio and stress sensitivity compared to the semi-dull and dull coals. The permeability of bright and semibright coals gradually decreases with increasing temperature, while the semi-dull and dull coals exhibit no significant variation. Under high effective stresses, the temperature shows enhanced influences on permeability, e.g., the bright coal shows temperature-sensitivity coefficient decreases from 0.04 to 0.02 under 5 to 35 MPa under 60 °C. The Pearson correlation coefficients between the permeability and effective stress range from −0.631 to −0.856, while −0.021 to −0.221 between permeability and temperature, which also indicates that the effective stresses are of higher influences compared to temperature. The results provide theoretical support for the efficient exploration and development of deep coalbed methane with different macrolithotype compositions.

Introduction

Deep coalbed methane (CBM), an emerging important unconventional natural gas resources, is attracting increasing attention in China and worldwide. , Deep coals hold significant resource potential due to their deep burial and favorable preservation conditions. These factors combine to make deep coalbed methane a promising energy source for future development. However, deep CBM reservoirs face several challenges, including high geothermal gradients, increased reservoir pressures, and lower permeability. These factors complicate the extraction process and require careful management to optimize production. −

Coal permeability is a key factor in identifying sweet spots and optimizing production strategies, which are influenced by coal characteristics, saves conditions, and effective stress. Understanding how permeability varies is helpful in targeting productive areas and improves the overall extraction efficiency. − Consequently, understanding the factors and mechanisms that control coal matrix permeability is a key research priority for advancing the exploration and extraction of CBM. ,

Numerous studies have examined the factors influencing coal permeability, with findings consistently highlighting stress as a critical factor. − Deep coal reservoirs typically possess well-developed fracture networks and exhibit strong sensitivity to stress. − The permeability generally decreases exponentially as effective stress increases. − The gas desorption causes the coal matrix shrinkage, which in turn increases the effective stress. Elevated effective stresses reduce porosity, ultimately resulting in a decrease in permeability. , The anisotropic permeability mathematical model, incorporating directional stress-coupling mechanisms, , quantitatively characterized the evolutionary dynamics of directional permeability under varying effective stress conditions. Notably, thermal effects on seepage capacity became increasingly significant with the reservoir depth. Permeability experiments conducted under varying temperatures revealed the temperature-mediated modulation of effective stress effects on coal permeabilityelevated temperatures enhanced permeability under low effective stress conditions while suppressing it under high effective stress states.

Recent studies show that bright coal reservoirs offer key advantages for deep CBM development. − However, current research still lacks a systematic understanding of how dynamic permeability varies across different macrolithotypes. Therefore, this study investigates coal samples from different macrolithotypes in the northeastern Ordos Basin. By conducting permeability experiments under varying temperatures, we explore the temperature and effective stress influences on coal permeability between different macrolithotypes, providing insights to optimize the efficient production of deep CBM.

Methods

Samples

The coal samples were collected from the No. 8 coal seam of the Taiyuan Formation in the Ordos Basin, and the most representative samples of various macrolithotypes were selected for investigation to measure permeability under different temperature and pressure conditions (Figure ). The samples, measuring 5 cm in length and 2.5 cm in diameter, were labeled BC, SBC, SDC, and DC. They represent four macrolithotypes: bright coal, semibright coal, semi-dull coal, and dull coal. Additional fundamental parameters are listed in Table . The temperature gradient in research area is 2.7 °C/100 m, and the pressure coefficient ranges from 0.9 to 1.1 MPa/100 m. The burial depth of the No. 8 coal seam of the Taiyuan Formation ranges from 2450 to 2950 m. The reservoir pressure of the No. 5 coal seam ranges from 21.61 to 31.80 MPa, with the temperature between 73.15 and 86.65 °C. From bright coal to dull coal, the Aad (Ash yield) gradually increases while the FCad (Fixed carbon content) gradually decreases. Comprehensive data indicate that the A ad is significantly negatively correlated with vitrinite content, with bright coal being characterized by a high vitrinite content and low A ad. The results demonstrate a consistent variation pattern with other samples, − thus providing valuable reference for basins beyond the current study area.

Experiments

To simulate in situ geothermal conditions, the experimental temperatures were set at 60, 70, 80, and 90 °C. The effective stress settled from 5 to 35 MPa by 5 MPa increments. The permeability was measured using an HA150-II multiphase fluid seepage system (Figure ).

Schematic of HA150-II multiphase fluid seepage system

Experiment Procedure

The instrument uses the unsteady-state pressure pulse decay method to test permeability, providing clear advantages for low- and ultralow-permeability coal samples. The confining pressure is applied using a high-pressure gas, while the test gas utilizes 99.999% purity helium. The net confining pressure on coal samples is incrementally increased to simulate effective stress variations. According to the Chinese petroleum industry standard SY/T 6385-2016, the confining pressure was increased stepwise at a rate of 1 MPa every 30 min, starting from an initial effective stress of 5 MPa. Based on the initial pressurization data, the testing system indicated that equilibrium was reached within hours with stabilized permeability. Thus, to ensure sufficient fracture closure, the stabilization time was set at 12 h. Two cylindrical subsamples were extracted from each original sample and tested under identical conditions. The permeability results were compared, and data were adopted only when the differences were negligible to ensure the reliability of the permeability measurements; otherwise, the tests were repeated. The experimental procedure consists of the following steps:1.The airtightness of the instrumentation was checked, and vacuum degassing was performed for 12 h.2.The fluid temperature was maintained at 60 °C through the temperature control system. Helium was injected after the system temperature had stabilized for 1 h. The inlet and outlet pressures were regulated using high-precision pressure-reducing valves and back-pressure valves.3.The confining pressure was increased stepwise at a rate of 1 MPa every 30 min, starting from an initial effective stress of 5 MPa. Constant temperature and effective stress conditions were maintained for 12 h after stabilization to ensure full fracture closure.4.Effective stress was increased in 5 MPa increments from 5 to 35 MPa, with a loading rate of 1 MPa every 30 min to reach the next stress level. At each stress level, confining pressure was applied and maintained for 12 h to ensure seepage equilibrium in the coal sample.5.The above steps were repeated at 70, 80, and 90 °C, respectively.

Throughout the process of effective stress increase, both PDR and α P exhibited a progressive decline from bright coal to dull coal. When the PDR exceeded 99% and the α P approached approximately 0.033, the four coal samples demonstrated nearly indistinguishable values for both parameters. When the effective stress is lower than 10 MPa, α P decreased rapidly, while PDR increased sharply. At 10 MPa, the α P of bright coal to dull coal are 0.159, 0.149, 0.131, and 0.110 MPa–1, respectively, and their corresponding PDRs were 79.73, 74.30, 65.31, and 54.93%, with an average value of 68.57%. The PDR of bright coal is about 31% higher than that of dull coal. When the effective stress ranges from 10 to 20 MPa, the α P of the four coal samples declined gradually. At 15 MPa, the α P of four coal samples range from 0.078 to 0.095 MPa–1, and their PDRs are 94.86, 91.53, 87.48, and 77.76%, respectively, with an average of 87.91%. The PDR of bright coal is about 18% higher than that of dull coal. At 20 MPa, α P of all samples ranged from 0.059 to 0.066 MPa–1, exhibiting essentially consistent values across samples, except for dull coal, which is slightly lower than the others. Semibright coal achieved a PDR of 99%, while dull coal showed the lowest value at 94%. When the effective stress exceeds 20 MPa, the α P and the PDR curves of the coal samples with different macrolithotypes exhibit a gentle trend. At 30 MPa, the PDR of semi-dull coal reaches 99%. At 35 MPa, the α P values of all four coal samples reach their lowest value and the PDR exceeding 99%.

Temperature Sensitivity

The temperature sensitivity of the permeability varies slightly across different macrolithotypes. Bright coal ranges from 0.022 to 0.067 MPa–1, semibright coal from 0.009 to 0.052 MPa–1, and semi-dull coal from 0.002 to 0.027 MPa–1, and dull coal from 0.002 to 0.012 MPa–1. The temperature-sensitivity coefficients decrease sequentially across the macrolithotypes from bright coal to dull coal. In both bright and semibright anthracitic coals, the permeability becomes less sensitive as temperature increases. Bright coal shows a 42% decrease in its temperature-sensitivity coefficient, while semibright coal drops by 41%. In semi-dull and dull anthracitic coals, the temperature sensitivity of permeability remains largely unchanged with increasing temperature. Even under effective stresses above 20 MPa, only minor fluctuations are observed.

Discussion

Coupling Effect of Stresses and Temperatures

The coupling effects of the temperature and effective stress lead to a complex nonlinear reduction in coal permeability. To verify the degree of influence of temperature and effective stress on permeability, Pearson's correlation analysis is employed to investigate the dependence of coal permeability on effective stress and temperature, with results presented in Figure . The correlation coefficients between permeability and temperature/effective stress are as follows: −0.631 and −0.221 for bright coal, −0.749 and −0.070 for semibright coal, −0.749 and −0.025 for semi-dull coal, and −0.856 and −0.021 for dull coal (Negative values denote inverse correlations between the respective parameters). The overall results demonstrate that the temperature sensitivity weakens sequentially from bright coal to dull coal across different macrolithotypes. The impact of temperature on the permeability of bright and semibright coal is relatively significant, whereas its effect on semi-dull and dull coal is negligible. In contrast, effective stress significantly affects coal permeability, with correlation coefficients increasing progressively from bright coal to dull coal. This indicates that effective stress is a dominant factor influencing permeability, whereas temperature plays a nonsignificant role.

Macrolithotype Controls on Permeability Variations

Analyze the data in Table and the permeability variation curves in Figure . The permeability curves of semi-dull and dull coal nearly coincide across different temperatures, indicating that their permeability shows minimal temperature dependence. In contrast, the influence of temperature on bright coal and semibright coal is primarily observed in the effective stress range less than 20 MPa. As temperature increases, the permeability of these coals decreases slightly, with bright coal exhibiting a more pronounced reduction compared to semibright coal. When the effective stress exceeds 20 MPa, the influence of the temperature on the coal permeability becomes negligible.

The stress sensitivity regression coefficient α reflects the degree to which effective stress influences coal permeability. Combining the data curves of PDR and α P in Figure allows analysis of the stress sensitivity of coal permeability. At effective stress levels below 10 MPa, coal samples of different macrolithotypes exhibit strong stress sensitivity. Severe stress sensitivity effects lead to a rapid permeability decline and accelerated PDR, reflecting the rapid closure of pores and fractures as well as a significant reduction in flow capacity. Bright coal and semibright coal, characterized by superior coal quality and better-developed fractures compared to semi-dull coal and dull coal, shows sequentially decreasing PDR and α P from bright coal to semibright coal, semi-dull coal, and dull coal. During the stage where the effective stress is between 10 and 20 MPa, the gaps in PDR and α P between different macrolithotypes gradually narrows. When effective stress exceeds 20 MPa, bright coal and semibright coal reach a state where PDR exceeds 99% earlier than semi-dull coal and dull coal, indicating that the flow channels are essentially completely closed. Ultimately, PDR and α P converge across different macrolithotypes. According to eqs and , a higher effective stress corresponds to lower permeability, approaching near-zero values. At this stage, PDR and α P depend solely on effective stress, resulting in consistent values across different macrolithotypes.

The temperature-sensitivity coefficient curve in Figure clearly demonstrates the differences in the temperature sensitivity of permeability among different macrolithotypes. The temperature sensitivity of bright coal and semibright anthracite decreases with rising temperature, while that of semi-dull coal and dull anthracite exhibits only minor fluctuations. This is directly reflected in the permeability curves (Figure ), with the temperature-sensitivity coefficient showing an increasing trend as the effective stress rises.
