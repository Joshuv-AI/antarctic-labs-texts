Equity gaps in neglected tropical disease research in Malaysia: a systematic review, 2014–2024
Authors: Mohd Amiru Hariz Aminuddin, Faizul Akmal Abdul Rahim, Agus Naba, Ahmad Mohiddin Mohd Ngesom, Nazri Che Dom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeglected tropical diseases (NTDs) disproportionately affect marginalised populations, yet the distribution of research attention across pathogens, populations, and settings remains uneven. Malaysia, a middle-income country with strong research capacity and a diverse NTD burden, provides an important case for examining research equity. This review aimed to systematically map NTD research in Malaysia and identify equity gaps across pathogens, population groups, study designs, and geographic settings.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. The Web of Science Core Collection was searched for peer-reviewed studies published between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2024. Eligible studies reported human NTD data from Malaysia and addressed infectious NTDs of viral, bacterial, helminthic, or protozoal aetiology. Data were extracted on publication characteristics, study design, population age groups, settings, geographic location, and pathogen category. Descriptive analyses, visualisation techniques, and chi-square tests were used to assess equity patterns.ResultsA total of 336 studies were included. Viral and bacterial infections accounted for more than two-thirds of publications, while helminthic and protozoal infections were consistently underrepresented. Among studies reporting age information, adults and adolescents were most frequently studied, whereas children were comparatively underrepresented; however, age was not specified in 35% of studies. Most research employed observational or cross-sectional designs, with few interventional or outbreak-focused studies. Research output was geographically concentrated in urban and high-density states, with limited coverage of rural and indigenous settings. Population–pathogen inequities were statistically significant (χ² = 51.22, df = 9, p < 0.0001).ConclusionsNTD research in Malaysia is characterised by substantial research equity gaps, including pathogen bias towards outbreak-driven diseases, inadequate demographic reporting, limited interventional evidence, and urban-centred research visibility. These inequities in knowledge production may reinforce downstream health inequities by constraining evidence-informed and equity-sensitive policy responses. Addressing these gaps will require rebalancing research priorities towards neglected pathogens and populations, strengthening reporting standards, and aligning national research agendas with the equity principles of the WHO NTD Roadmap 2021–2030.

Introduction

Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) remain a group of communicable diseases that affect more than one billion people worldwide, disproportionately burdening poor, marginalised, and underserved populations [1–3]. Despite their combined impact on disability, mortality, and economic productivity, NTDs have historically received less scientific, political, and financial attention than other infectious diseases [4, 5]. To address this imbalance, global strategies such as the WHO NTD Roadmap 2021–2030 and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 3.3) have prioritised integrated approaches, health equity, and universal access to prevention and treatment [6]. Progress has been documented in some areas, including mass drug administration programmes for helminth control and expanded dengue surveillance systems in Asia [7–9]. However, the overall distribution of research and policy attention across pathogens, population groups, and geographic settings remains uneven, reflecting longstanding inequities in global health priorities [10–12].

Malaysia provides an important lens through which to examine these disparities [13]. As a middle-income country with well-established academic and health infrastructure, it is hyperendemic for dengue and reports a rising burden of other NTDs such as leptospirosis, melioidosis, soil-transmitted helminths, and sporadic protozoal infections [14, 15]. National control strategies have been largely disease-specific and reactive, often shaped by outbreak-driven agendas that prioritise dengue while underinvesting in other equally important pathogens [16]. This imbalance has direct implications for health equity, particularly for rural, indigenous, and low-income communities where helminths, rickettsial diseases, and bacterial infections are more prevalent. Existing studies also point to persistent challenges in demographic reporting, with vulnerable groups such as children and adolescents frequently neglected in surveillance and research outputs [17, 18]. These gaps are particularly concerning in Malaysia, given the country’s otherwise strong research capacity, and they raise questions about whether national knowledge production is aligned with equity-driven health priorities.

In this review, the term “equity gaps” is used to denote systematic imbalances in the production, distribution, and reporting of scientific evidence, rather than differences in disease burden or health outcomes alone [19]. While health inequities typically refer to unfair and avoidable differences in morbidity, mortality, or access to care across population groups, and disease inequities describe unequal exposure or vulnerability to specific conditions, research equity gaps arise upstream at the level of knowledge generation itself [20]. They reflect disparities in which pathogens are prioritised, which populations are studied or omitted, which geographic settings are represented, and which study designs dominate the evidence base. Conceptually, research equity gaps can be understood through a three-stage framework: (1) research inputs, including funding priorities, surveillance capacity, and academic attention; (2) research outputs, reflected in the volume, design, population focus, and reporting quality of published studies; and (3) downstream policy relevance, whereby uneven evidence visibility constrains equitable decision-making and intervention design. Within this framework, underrepresentation of certain pathogens (e.g., helminths and protozoa), populations (e.g., children or rural communities), or study types (e.g., interventional research) constitutes a form of structural inequity that may persist even in settings with strong health systems and research infrastructure. Framing inequities in this way aligns with the equity-centred principles of the WHO NTD Roadmap 2021–2030 and the Sustainable Development Goals, which emphasise not only reducing disease burden but also ensuring that vulnerable populations are adequately represented in the evidence used to guide policy and practice. By focusing on equity gaps in research production, this review seeks to identify structural blind spots that shape national and global NTD agendas, and to highlight opportunities for more inclusive, policy-relevant, and equity-sensitive research.

The concept of equity in this review is grounded in Paula Braveman’s health equity framework, which defines health equity as the absence of systematic, avoidable, and unjust differences in health or in the social determinants of health across population groups [21]. Within this framework, inequities are understood as socially produced disparities that disproportionately affect disadvantaged populations and are therefore amenable to policy and structural intervention. Building on Braveman’s framework, this review conceptualises research inequity as an upstream analogue of health inequity. Rather than reflecting unequal disease burden or health outcomes, research inequity arises from systematic imbalances in how knowledge is generated, reported, and prioritised. These imbalances shape which diseases, populations, settings, and intervention types become visible within the scientific literature and, consequently, which health problems are most likely to inform policy and resource allocation. Applying this lens to NTD research, inequities in research attention such as the underrepresentation of helminthic and protozoal infections, limited inclusion of children and rural populations, and the dominance of descriptive over interventional studies represent structural gaps in knowledge production. Consistent with Braveman’s framework, such gaps are not random but reflect broader social, institutional, and policy drivers, and may ultimately reinforce downstream health inequities by constraining evidence-informed and equity-sensitive decision-making.

Although global and regional reviews of NTDs have been conducted, no systematic synthesis has yet quantified the distribution, inequities, and methodological gaps in NTD research in Malaysia. A critical evaluation of this evidence base is needed to assess whether research activities reflect both the national disease burden and the principles of equity and universality emphasised in the WHO NTD Roadmap. The objective of this review was to systematically map NTD research in Malaysia between 2014 and 2024, quantify inequities across pathogens, study designs, population groups, and geographic settings, and identify structural gaps in reporting and research focus. By highlighting these disparities, the review aims to inform future research priorities, strengthen policy relevance, and contribute lessons of global significance for advancing equity in NTD research.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a systematic review of neglected tropical disease (NTD) research in Malaysia in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19]. The review protocol was designed to ensure transparency and reproducibility, and each stage was documented to facilitate replication. The process included pathogen selection, a structured literature search, predefined eligibility screening, standardised data extraction, and quantitative synthesis. By adhering to these methodological standards, the review sought to minimise bias, enhance comparability, and provide a comprehensive assessment of research output across pathogens, populations, study designs, and geographic settings.

Disease (pathogen) selection

The scope of this review was restricted to infectious neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) relevant to Malaysia. Diseases were identified using classifications and guidance from the World Health Organization (WHO), the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and PLoS Neglected Tropical Diseases [20]. Although the WHO recognises toxin-mediated conditions, including snakebite envenoming, as a distinct category of NTDs, snakebite envenoming was excluded from the present analysis for analytical rather than conceptual reasons. Its toxin-mediated aetiology involves exposure pathways, outcome measures, and research typologies that differ substantially from those used in infectious disease research, which would have limited comparability within a unified, equity-focused analytical framework [21]. This exclusion does not diminish the public health importance of snakebite envenoming or its status as a profoundly neglected condition driven by underdiagnosis and weak surveillance; rather, it reflects a deliberate scoping decision to maintain methodological coherence, while acknowledging the need for dedicated equity-focused reviews of toxin-mediated NTDs, particularly in Southeast Asia. In contrast, rickettsial diseases and melioidosis, although not formally listed as NTDs by WHO, were included due to their infectious nature, documented endemicity, substantial disease burden, and public health relevance in Malaysia and the wider Southeast Asian region [22]. Both conditions disproportionately affect rural and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, including agricultural workers and indigenous communities, and are characterised by under-recognition, limited surveillance, and low research visibility despite high case fatality rates. From an equity perspective, excluding these infections would overlook locally important diseases that remain neglected within national research agendas; their inclusion therefore represents a pragmatic, context-specific application of the NTD framework consistent with previous regional reviews that extend beyond formal WHO classifications to capture under-recognised but high-impact infectious diseases.

Literature search strategy

We systematically searched the Web of Science Core Collection for studies on neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) in Malaysia published between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2024, using the keywords “neglected tropical diseases”, “Malaysia”, and pathogen-specific terms (e.g., dengue, leptospirosis, helminths, protozoa). The search was restricted to studies conducted in human populations to ensure public health relevance. No language restrictions were applied at the initial search stage, although only English-language full texts were included in the final review. Reference lists of included studies were also screened to identify additional eligible publications. The Web of Science Core Collection was selected as the primary database due to its broad coverage of high-quality, peer-reviewed biomedical and public health journals, standardised indexing, and compatibility with reproducible systematic review workflows. Although additional databases such as PubMed or Scopus may capture supplementary records, prior methodological studies have shown substantial overlap in indexed public health literature, and the use of a single, well-curated database is considered acceptable for focused, descriptive equity analyses.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

All records identified through the search were screened in two stages according to predefined eligibility criteria, with titles and abstracts reviewed for relevance followed by full-text assessment to confirm inclusion. Eligible studies were peer-reviewed articles published between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2024 that reported data from Malaysia, involved human populations, and addressed infectious neglected tropical diseases of viral, bacterial, helminthic, or protozoal aetiology. Included study designs comprised observational, interventional, cross-sectional, surveillance, outbreak investigations, and case reports or case series. Excluded were editorials, letters, commentaries, book chapters, non-peer-reviewed conference proceedings, qualitative studies, environmental or laboratory-only studies without human data, non-human studies, and research focusing on non-infectious neglected conditions, including snakebite envenoming. Qualitative studies were excluded because the objective of this review was to quantitatively map research equity patterns across pathogens, populations, study designs, and geographic settings using standardised and comparable indicators; although qualitative research is essential for understanding lived experiences and social drivers of inequity, its inclusion would have required a fundamentally different analytical framework and synthesis approach, limiting comparability across studies. All excluded articles were documented with explicit reasons for exclusion, and the study selection process was summarised using a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened independently by at least two reviewers. Any disagreements regarding study eligibility were resolved through discussion and consensus. Where consensus could not be reached, a third senior reviewer was consulted to make the final decision.

Data extraction and management

Data from eligible studies were systematically extracted into a standardised Microsoft Excel form to ensure accuracy and comparability. Extracted variables included bibliographic details (publication year and first author), study period, study design, study setting, study population, geographic location, and pathogen classification. Study populations were categorised as children (≤ 17 years), adults and adolescents (18–65 years), mixed groups including both children and adults, or not specified. These age categories were selected based on the level of age reporting consistency across the included studies, as most publications reported broad age groupings rather than finer stratifications. Although disaggregation for children under five years and older adults (> 65 years) would be highly valuable for assessing disease burden, such detail was infrequently reported and could not be applied systematically across the evidence base. The limited representation and inconsistent reporting of these age groups therefore reflect a structural gap in the existing literature and are highlighted in this review as an important research equity concern. Study settings were classified as community, hospital, school, workplace, laboratory, clinic, or not specified. Geographical categorisation covered all 13 Malaysian states and 3 federal territories. Pathogens were classified into four major categories: viral, bacterial, helminthic, and protozoal. Missing demographic information was handled using a transparent, non-imputational approach. Studies that did not report age or population characteristics were retained in the analysis and classified as “not specified.” No assumptions or imputations were made for missing demographic variables, as this could introduce misclassification bias. Instead, analyses comparing population representation across pathogens were conducted using reported categories, while the proportion of studies with missing demographic data was quantified and examined as an indicator of reporting quality and research equity.

Data synthesis and analysis

Data synthesis combined descriptive statistics and visualisation to identify research trends and equity gaps. Waffle plots were used to describe the distribution of study populations, study designs, and research settings; circular bar plots illustrated the relative distribution of pathogens; heatmaps with cross-tabulation highlighted inequities across population–pathogen categories; lollipop plots compared studies with age-specified and non-specified populations; and geospatial maps visualised the distribution of research output by pathogen category across Malaysia. To formally test for inequities, we performed chi-square analyses to assess differences in population representation across pathogens, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. All analyses and visualisations were performed using R (version 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [23].

Role of the funding source

This study received no external funding. The authors had full access to all data and were solely responsible for study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, and the decision to publish.

Results

Between 2014 and 2024, a total of 336 studies on neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) were identified in Malaysia. Among studies that reported age information, adults and adolescents were the most frequently studied groups, while children were comparatively underrepresented; however, age was not specified in 35% of studies (n = 119). Most studies were conducted in community and hospital settings, with school and workplace environments rarely examined. Observational and cross-sectional designs dominated, whereas intervention and outbreak studies were scarce. As illustrated in Fig. 2, this reliance on descriptive research may reflect an evidence base that generates limited intervention-focused knowledge, which may constrain translation into policy and prevention. Equity analysis revealed marked disparities across age groups. Helminth studies focused disproportionately on children (33.3%), whereas viral studies were concentrated among adults (37.9%), while bacterial and protozoal research showed no clear age focus. Notably, a substantial proportion of studies did not specify population age, ranging from 36.2% of viral to 50.0% of bacterial research. As illustrated in Fig. 4, this high level of age non-reporting limits the precision of age-specific interpretations and indicates that observed disparities reflect patterns among age-specified studies rather than the full evidence base.

Nearly half of all NTD studies in Malaysia therefore lack the demographic detail necessary for age-targeted planning, severely limiting their policy relevance for child- and adolescent-focused interventions such as school-based deworming or dengue awareness campaigns.

Finally, the spatial distribution of research was uneven. As shown in Fig. 6, viral NTD research was heavily clustered in urban and high-density states, reflecting strong surveillance and outbreak-driven priorities. Helminth studies were concentrated in rural and indigenous communities, consistent with known endemicity, while bacterial studies were geographically fragmented. Protozoal infections remained almost invisible across the national research landscape despite documented sporadic outbreaks. This geographic skew highlights how the location of populations dictates research visibility, with urban dengue receiving extensive attention while rural protozoal diseases remain absent from the scientific agenda.

Discussion

This systematic review interprets patterns of research attention rather than restating descriptive results, and highlights how the current NTD research landscape in Malaysia aligns or fails to align with equity-oriented public health priorities. Overall, the findings suggest a persistent imbalance in knowledge production, characterised by pathogen bias, demographic blind spots, methodological concentration, and geographic skewness. At the pathogen level, the dominance of viral and bacterial research, particularly dengue and leptospirosis, is consistent with outbreak-driven surveillance priorities and funding agendas, rather than a comprehensive reflection of endemic disease burden. In contrast, helminthic and protozoal infections, which are closely associated with poverty, chronic morbidity, and rural exposure, remain comparatively underrepresented in the literature [24, 25]. This pattern mirrors global critiques of NTD research, where high-visibility pathogens receive disproportionate attention while long-standing endemic infections remain neglected. Such imbalances may reflect structural drivers of research prioritisation rather than epidemiological need alone.

Demographic equity represents another important dimension of imbalance. Although children and adolescents bear a substantial burden of several NTDs, their limited visibility in the published evidence base, combined with the high proportion of studies lacking age specification, constrains age-sensitive interpretation and policy translation. Rather than indicating absence of disease, incomplete demographic reporting may obscure vulnerability among key population groups, including young children and older adults. From an equity perspective, this reporting gap constitutes a structural weakness in research practices, as populations that are not explicitly documented are less likely to be prioritised in intervention design and resource allocation [26–28].

Methodologically, the predominance of observational and cross-sectional designs appears to reflect an evidence base oriented towards burden description rather than solution generation. While descriptive studies are essential for surveillance and situational awareness, the relative scarcity of interventional and implementation-focused research may limit the capacity of evidence to inform actionable policy responses [29]. This pattern is not unique to Malaysia, but its persistence in a country with strong academic and health infrastructure suggests missed opportunities for translational and equity-driven research. Geographically, the concentration of research in urban and high-density states is consistent with areas of strong surveillance capacity and outbreak activity, particularly for dengue. Conversely, the limited visibility of protozoal and helminthic infections in rural and indigenous settings may reflect systematic under-investigation rather than low public health importance. Similar urban–rural research gradients have been reported internationally, reinforcing concerns that marginalised populations remain underrepresented in the scientific literature [30–32].

Taken together, these findings have several implications. At the national level, Malaysia’s NTD research agenda remains too narrowly focused on high-visibility pathogens and too reliant on descriptive designs to guide equitable health policy. At the global level, Malaysia’s experience illustrates how inequities in NTD research persist even in middle-income settings with established infrastructure, and are likely to be magnified in lower-income countries. These results are broadly consistent with previous critiques of the global NTD agenda, although Malaysia differs in its unusually strong emphasis on dengue, reflecting both its hyperendemic status and well-developed surveillance systems [33]. In contrast, neighbouring countries such as the Philippines and Laos continue to report higher research visibility for helminths, highlighting the ways in which national priorities, not only disease burden, shape the production of scientific evidence [34].

In this review, it is important to distinguish research inequity from health inequity. Health inequities refer to unfair and avoidable differences in disease burden, exposure, or access to care across populations, whereas research inequities arise upstream, reflecting systematic imbalances in what diseases, populations, settings, and study designs are prioritised in the scientific literature [35]. Research inequity does not necessarily mirror disease burden; rather, it shapes which health problems become visible, measurable, and actionable in policy discourse. The findings of this review demonstrate that, in Malaysia, inequities in knowledge production particularly the underrepresentation of certain pathogens, children, and rural settings may reinforce downstream health inequities by limiting evidence-informed and equity-sensitive interventions. Malaysia’s research profile also differs from that of several neighbouring Southeast Asian countries. For example, studies from the Philippines and parts of Indonesia report greater research attention to helminthic infections, reflecting long-standing mass drug administration programmes and school-based surveillance, whereas Malaysia’s literature remains heavily dominated by dengue-focused research [36]. In contrast, countries such as Laos and Cambodia, despite more limited research infrastructure, report proportionally higher visibility of parasitic and rural-associated NTDs. These contrasts suggest that national research agendas are shaped not only by disease burden, but also by surveillance systems, funding priorities, and outbreak-driven policy attention [37].

This study is not without limitations. The reliance on a single database (Web of Science) may have excluded grey literature and non-indexed local reports, though reference list screening mitigated this to some extent. The analysis was descriptive and did not assess study quality or risk of bias within individual publications. Restricting inclusion to English-language full texts may have excluded some locally published evidence. Future reviews should therefore incorporate multiple databases, include non-English literature, and apply formal quality appraisal tools. Triangulation of published research with national surveillance data would also strengthen the alignment between research production and actual disease burden. In conclusion, this review demonstrates that NTD research in Malaysia remains unevenly distributed across pathogens, populations, and settings, limiting its ability to inform equitable and evidence-based health policy. By systematically documenting these inequities, the study provides evidence to support a reorientation of the research agenda. Greater investment in neglected pathogens, systematic inclusion and reporting of vulnerable groups such as children, and a shift toward interventional and translational studies will be essential if Malaysia is to align its outputs with both national needs and global priorities. Without such changes, NTD research risks perpetuating inequities rather than advancing the ambitions of the WHO NTD Roadmap and the Sustainable Development Goals.
