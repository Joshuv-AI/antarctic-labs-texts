Detection of respiratory pathogens in infants using targeted next-generation sequencing versus conventional methods in Qingdao

Background

Respiratory infections in infants encompass a spectrum of diseases, including upper respiratory tract infections (URIs), bronchitis, and pneumonia. Acute URIs typically manifest as nasal congestion, rhinorrhea, cough, sneezing, and sore throat, and, while often self-limiting, can rapidly progress to severe illness in infants [1, 2]. Globally, respiratory infections account for a substantial proportion of pediatric healthcare utilization, with infants representing the most frequently hospitalized group for these conditions. Major risk factors in this population include premature birth, environmental tobacco smoke exposure, lack of breastfeeding, and daycare attendance [2–4].

In China, pneumonia remains one of the leading causes of death in children under five years, with infants facing the highest risk. It is estimated that approximately 350,000 children die from pneumonia each year in China, representing about 10% of global childhood pneumonia deaths [5]. A wide array of pathogens—including viruses, bacteria, atypical bacteria (such as Mycoplasma pneumoniae [M. pneumoniae] and Chlamydia pneumoniae [C. pneumoniae]), and fungi—can cause respiratory tract infections in infants [6, 7]. Among these, viruses are generally the predominant cause of acute upper respiratory tract infections in infants and young children [7]. The most frequently detected viral pathogens include respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza viruses, adenovirus (AdV), and human rhinovirus (HRV), while common bacterial pathogens include Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae), and M. pneumoniae [6]. The prevalence of specific pathogens varies according to age, season, immunization status, environmental exposures, and geographic location [8].

However, the clinical manifestations of different pathogens often overlap in infants, making etiological diagnosis challenging in routine practice [9, 10]. Although earlier studies suggested that the causative pathogen could be identified in 65%–86% of pediatric pneumonia cases—including 30%–67% viral and 23%–33% mixed viral-bacterial infections—these studies predated the widespread use of multiplex molecular diagnostics and likely underestimated the true burden of co-infection and pathogen diversity [11]. Recent studies using advanced multiplex molecular diagnostics have significantly expanded our understanding of the etiological spectrum of pediatric pneumonia. With these technologies, the identification rate of causative pathogens in pediatric community-acquired pneumonia has increased to approximately 80%–95% in recent reports, with viral infections accounting for 50%–80% of cases and mixed viral-bacterial infections found in 20%–40% of cases [12]. Isothermal amplification is a molecular technique for nucleic acid detection that allows rapid amplification of target DNA or RNA sequences at a constant temperature, eliminating the need for thermal cycling as in conventional PCR. This approach, often combined with microfluidic technology, enables high-throughput, sensitive, and specific detection of infectious agents, and has been widely applied in respiratory pathogen diagnostics [14].

Early and accurate identification of causative agents is critical for guiding appropriate treatment, minimizing unnecessary antibiotic use, and reducing the risk of adverse events and antibiotic resistance. However, conventional single-pathogen detection technologies have limited sensitivity, specificity, and detection range, which is insufficient for the complex reality of infant respiratory tract infections, where mixed infections are frequent [13]. Therefore, understanding the current epidemiology of respiratory pathogens in infants within specific regions, and evaluating the performance of advanced diagnostic methods, is essential for improving clinical management.

Therefore, the present study aimed to characterize the respiratory pathogens in infants aged 0–1 year with respiratory tract infections in Qingdao, China, and to compare the diagnostic performance of targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) with conventional detection methods.

Methods

Study design and patients

This study included infants aged 0–1 year with respiratory infection (presenting with at least two of the following: fever, cough, nasal congestion, sputum, sneezing, or dyspnea), admitted to Qingdao Women and Children’s Hospital from March to May 2023. Exclusion criteria were: (1) receipt of glucocorticoids outside the hospital; (2) congenital or acquired immunodeficiency, chronic underlying diseases, or long-term use of hormones or immunosuppressants; (3) pneumonia caused by inhalation of foreign objects, interstitial lung disease, lung tumors, hospital-acquired pneumonia, or persistent or chronic pneumonia.

The study was approved by the clinical ethics committee of the Qingdao Women and Children’s Hospital (QFELL-YJ-2024-70). Written informed consent was obtained from caregivers prior to enrollment.

Data collection and samples

Patient demographic and clinical data (sex, age, diagnosis, and laboratory results) were collected by laboratory staff. Prior to initiation of antibiotic therapy on the day of admission, respiratory secretions were collected via cough induction using pharyngeal stimulation followed by negative pressure aspiration. Additionally, nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal samples were obtained by swabbing both palatal arches and tonsils with a flocked swab. Sputum samples were sent for culture if sufficient material was obtained. Only specimens meeting quality criteria (white blood cells ≥ 10–25 and squamous epithelial cells < 10 per low-power field) were processed; samples not meeting these criteria were recollected. Routine sputum cultures were initiated within 30 min of receipt, and bacterial growth was identified using an automated microbial analyzer (VITEK® 2 Compact, bioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France).

Nucleic acid isothermal amplification analyzer based on microfluidic chips

In this study, nucleic acids were extracted from respiratory samples using the magnetic bead method. Isothermal amplification was performed on microfluidic chips (CapitalBio Corp., Hong Kong, China) at a constant temperature (65 °C) with real-time fluorescence monitoring. The microfluidic isothermal amplification panel used here is a research-use-validated method [14], capable of simultaneously detecting 13 common respiratory pathogens: S. pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), Escherichia coli (E. coli), Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (S. maltophilia), H. influenzae, Legionella pneumophila (L. pneumophila), Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), M. pneumoniae, and C. pneumoniae.

For viral detection, a multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay was used. Multiplex PCR is a well-established molecular technique that enables the simultaneous amplification and detection of multiple target sequences in a single reaction, thereby increasing diagnostic efficiency and throughput for pathogens with overlapping clinical presentations [15]. In this study, multiplex PCR (Sansure Biotech Inc., Changsha, China) was performed with fluorescent probes, targeting six respiratory pathogens: RSV, AdV, IAV, influenza B virus (IBV), M. pneumoniae, and HRV.

Targeted next generation sequencing (tNGS)

Total nucleic acids were extracted automatically using the MagNA Pure 96 DNA and Viral NA Small Volume Kit (CN6543588001, Roche, Germany) on the MagNA Pure 96 system (Roche, Germany). Reverse transcription, multiplex PCR preamplification, and library preparation for tNGS were performed using the 100TM Plus Respiratory Pathogen Microorganism Multiplex Testing Kit (KingCreate Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China), which detects 199 species, including 80 bacteria, 79 viruses, mycoplasma, seven types of Chlamydia, 32 fungi, and drug-resistance genes [carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), MRSA, and M. pneumoniae macrolide resistance]. The tNGS protocol followed the manufacturer’s recommendations and has been validated for research use in previous studies [16–18].

Nucleic acid concentration was measured in all samples using a 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA) and a Qubit™ 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to ensure a library concentration of ≥ 0.5 ng/µL; if this was not achieved, nucleic acids were re-extracted or libraries were reconstructed. Qualified libraries were sequenced using the KM MiniSeq Dx-CN platform (KingCreate Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 19.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Age was presented as median (range). Categorical data were presented as n (%) and analyzed by chi-squared test. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 95 infants aged 1–12 months (median age: 2 months) were enrolled in the study, including 59 males (62.1%) and 36 females (37.9%). The most common clinical symptoms were cough (96.8%), throat phlegm (78.0%), fever (41.0%), reflux (29.5%), choking on milk (34.7%), wheezing (21.1%), and shortness of breath (12.6%). Additionally, 55.8% of infants had a contact history (Table 1).

Pathogens detection and infection patterns

Detection methods

Respiratory pathogens were identified based on an integrated assessment of clinical manifestation and laboratory findings from both conventional methods (bacterial culture, isothermal amplification, multiplex PCR) and tNGS. In cases of discordant results, the tNGS findings were prioritized due to its higher sensitivity and broader detection range. For the bacterial culture, 15 patients did not undergo the examination, and 8 of the remaining 80 patients were positive. For Pathogen 13, 2 of 14 patients were positive; for Pathogen 6 or 9, 20 of 53 patients were positive.

Pathogen prevalence

The most frequently identified pathogens were RSV (34/95, 35.8%), IAV (17/95, 17.9%), A. baumannii (17/95, 17.9%), HRV (15/95, 15.8%), and S. pneumoniae (12/95, 12.6%). Table 2 shows the full list.

Comparison of detection methods: conventional vs. tNGS

The overall pathogen detection rate was significantly higher for tNGS (91.6%, 87/95) than for conventional methods (27.4%, 26/95). When conventional and tNGS results were compared for the 26 conventionally positive cases, the results were concordant in 20 samples (76.9%). Table 5 provides a side-by-side comparison; additional data including clinical diagnosis and individual test results are provided in the Supplementary Table 2. Among RSV-positive infants, those with additional bacterial pathogens detected (such as A. baumannii, S. aureus, E. coli, or K. pneumoniae) were more likely to be diagnosed with severe pneumonia or bronchopneumonia. Specifically, of the 13 RSV-positive cases, 9 infants had co-detection of at least one bacterial pathogen, and 7 of these were diagnosed clinically with severe pneumonia or bronchopneumonia. In contrast, RSV-positive infants without additional bacterial detection more commonly had milder diagnoses. These findings support that RSV-bacterial co-infection is associated with greater clinical severity in this cohort.

Discussion

This study provides an updated overview of the epidemiological characteristics and pathogen spectrum of respiratory tract infections in infants under one year of age in Qingdao, China, during the spring months of 2023. Our results demonstrate that RSV, IAV, A. baumannii, HRV, and S. pneumoniae were the most frequently detected pathogens in this population. Notably, we observed a high rate of mixed infections, with nearly half of the cases involving more than one pathogen, most commonly a combination of viruses and bacteria.

Our findings are largely consistent with previous studies in China, where RSV, HRV, and IAV have been reported as leading viral pathogens in pediatric respiratory infections [19]. The predominance of RSV in infants is well established, as this virus is a major cause of severe lower respiratory tract disease and hospitalization in young children [20, 21]. Infants under six months are particularly vulnerable to severe outcomes, including wheezing, hypoxemia, and even respiratory failure [22]. While RSV activity in China is generally reported to peak in winter and early spring [23], our data showed a high incidence of RSV infection from March to May. This supports the observation that RSV activity can remain elevated into early spring in this region. However, as our data do not include the winter months (December–February), we may not have captured the entire seasonal peak. Future surveillance covering a full calendar year is needed to more precisely characterize the seasonal distribution of RSV in this area.

The detection of A. baumannii as a leading bacterial pathogen is noteworthy. While pre-pandemic studies identified S. pneumoniae, M. pneumoniae, and H. influenzae as the most common bacteria [19], recent evidence suggests shifts in pathogen prevalence following the COVID-19 pandemic, likely due to changes in public health measures, antibiotic use, and hospital ecology [24]. A. baumannii is a well-recognized nosocomial pathogen with extensive drug resistance, and its presence in community-onset pediatric respiratory infections is concerning. However, it is possible that changes in healthcare-seeking behavior or transmission patterns in the post-pandemic period contributed to this finding. Similar shifts or off-season outbreaks for other respiratory pathogens may also reflect the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on viral and bacterial circulation, as reported in other regions [24]. These epidemiological changes highlight the need for ongoing surveillance and further studies to track trends in the post-pandemic era.

A significant proportion of infants in our study had mixed infections, with the most frequent combination being virus–bacteria co-detection, followed by bacteria–bacteria and virus–virus. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that viral infection can disrupt the epithelial barrier and immune responses, facilitating secondary bacterial colonization and infection [25–27]. The rate of viral co-infection was lower, which may be partly explained by viral interference and resource competition [28, 29]. Nevertheless, co-infections—particularly with multiple viruses—have been associated with increased morbidity in children [30], underlining the importance of comprehensive pathogen detection.

By integrating clinical diagnoses with laboratory findings, our study provides insight into the clinical relevance of co-infections. For example, among RSV-positive infants, those with concurrent bacterial pathogens (as detected by tNGS) were more likely to be diagnosed with severe pneumonia or bronchopneumonia compared to those with RSV mono-infection [31, 32]. This suggests that co-infection may contribute to disease severity and highlights the importance of comprehensive microbiological assessment for optimal patient management.

Our comparison of tNGS with conventional methods (bacterial culture, isothermal amplification, and multiplex PCR) showed that tNGS had a significantly higher overall detection rate for both viruses and bacteria, with a positive rate of 91.6% versus 27.4%. The concordance between methods was 76.9% in conventionally positive samples. tNGS offers several advantages: it enables simultaneous broad-range detection of viruses, bacteria, atypical pathogens, and resistance genes in a single assay, and is especially useful for cases with complex or mixed infections. This capability is particularly valuable in infants, where clinical features are non-specific and co-infections are common. Moreover, tNGS detected additional co-infecting pathogens not identified by conventional methods, providing important clinical information that may influence treatment decisions.

However, tNGS also has drawbacks. Its high sensitivity may result in the detection of colonizing or non-pathogenic organisms, which complicates interpretation. The technology is costly, requires specialized equipment and expertise, and may have longer turnaround times than some rapid molecular assays. tNGS results can be affected by sample quality, potential contamination, and do not indicate organism viability [15, 33–36]. Additionally, interpretation of tNGS reports may be complex, and clinical correlation is always essential [35, 36]. Despite these limitations, tNGS is a promising tool for comprehensive pathogen detection, especially in cases of severe or atypical infection or when initial diagnostics are inconclusive.

There are also limitations to our study. First, it was conducted at a single center in Qingdao over a three-month period, which may not fully reflect the seasonality or geographic variability of respiratory pathogens in China. We did not have patient data for the winter months (December–February), and thus could not assess the full seasonal dynamics, particularly for RSV and influenza. The relatively small sample size also limits the generalizability of our findings. In addition, while we collected data on contact history and inhalation history, these were not included as clinical symptoms and may have influenced pathogen acquisition or detection rates. Finally, no follow-up data were available to evaluate clinical outcomes or responses to therapy.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that RSV, IAV, A. baumannii, HRV, and S. pneumoniae are important pathogens in respiratory infections among infants in Qingdao, with a high prevalence of mixed infections. tNGS offers significant advantages over conventional methods for pathogen detection, particularly in complex cases, but also presents new interpretive and operational challenges. Ongoing epidemiological surveillance and larger, multicenter studies are needed to further clarify pathogen dynamics and optimize diagnostic strategies in the post-pandemic era.
