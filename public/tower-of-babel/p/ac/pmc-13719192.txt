Immune-mediated Myocarditis after Durvalumab/tremelimumab Treatment for Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Shogo Mawatari, Ryu Sasaki, Yasuhiko Nakao, Masanori Fukushima, Masafumi Haraguchi, Satoshi Miuma, Hisamitsu Miyaaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are important for the treatment of unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Furthermore, severe immune-mediated adverse events (imAEs) can be life threatening. We herein report a case of myocarditis and myositis after ICI treatment. A male patient with unresectable HCC was treated with durvalumab and tremelimumab. Three weeks later, the patient presented to the emergency department with syncope. Electrocardiography revealed a complete atrioventricular block. Therefore, immune-mediated myocarditis was suspected. High-dose corticosteroid therapy was initiated immediately and temporary pacing was required. The patient's cardiac conduction gradually improved and his spontaneous rhythm was restored. This case highlights the importance of the early recognition and prompt management of imAEs, particularly myocarditis. Clinicians should be vigilant as delayed intervention may be fatal. Keywords: immune-mediated adverse events, myocarditis, hepatocellular carcinoma, durvalumab, tremelimumab

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are now approved for an expanding range of malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (1). Anti-programmed cell death protein 1(PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1(PD-L1) and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen 4(CTLA-4) antibodies exert their effects at different stages of the immune response and their combination is thought to produce synergistic antitumor activity (2). The HIMALAYA trial demonstrated that a combination of durvalumab (anti-PD-L1) and a single priming dose of tremelimumab (anti-CTLA-4) significantly improved overall survival of patients with unresectable HCC, leading to its approval as a first-line treatment (3). However, ICI treatment increases the risk of immune-mediated adverse events (imAEs). Common imAEs, including diarrhea and dermatitis, are usually manageable and rare; however, potentially life-threatening events, including myocarditis and myositis, require prompt recognition and treatment (4,5). We herein report a rare case of myocarditis and myositis following combination therapy with durvalumab and tremelimumab for unresectable HCC.

Case Report

An 80-year-old man with a medical history of atherothrombotic stroke, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and IgA vasculitis was diagnosed in 2021 with intermediate-stage multifocal HCC, classified according to the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer staging system. The patient underwent multiple sessions of transarterial chemoembolization as a locoregional therapy. Systemic therapy was initially discontinued because of an elevated thrombotic risk.

Following the approval of a vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor-free regimen in Japan, combination immunotherapy with durvalumab (anti-PD-L1 antibody) and tremelimumab (anti-CTLA-4 antibody) was initiated at our institution. The patient was discharged in stable condition on day 7 after treatment initiation. Outpatient follow-up visits on days 12 and 19 revealed no clinical symptoms or any abnormal laboratory results.

On day 24, the patient experienced a sudden syncopal episode at home and was transported to the emergency department. On arrival, the patient was hemodynamically unstable. Electrocardiography revealed a complete atrioventricular block (Fig. 1). Laboratory data revealed marked elevations in cardiac biomarkers including troponin T, creatine kinase (CK), and CK-MB (Table 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction without regional wall motion abnormalities.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit where temporary transvenous pacing was promptly initiated. Coronary angiography revealed no evidence of significant stenosis. The endomyocardial biopsy specimen revealed mild lymphocytic infiltration within the myocardial tissue, mild interstitial edema, and focal myocardial fiber desquamation, without myocyte enlargement or nuclear atypia. There was no evidence of iron or mucin accumulation, amyloid deposition, granulomatous inflammation, or ischemic changes. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated that the inflammatory infiltrate was predominantly composed of CD3-positive T lymphocytes, with CD8-positive cytotoxic T cells outnumbering the CD4-positive helper T cells. Only a few CD20-positive B have been identified. These findings were consistent with those of immune-mediated myocarditis (Fig. 2). Nonetheless, considering the clinical course and temporal association with immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, immune-mediated myocarditis was strongly suspected.

High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1,000 mg/day) was administered for three days, followed by a tapering regimen starting at 80 mg/day, which was decreased by 10 mg every five days. Serum cardiac biomarkers gradually decreased following the therapy (Table 2, Fig. 3). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed focal thinning and hypokinesis of the left ventricular apex, consistent with an apical aneurysm (Fig. 4A). In addition, T1 mapping demonstrated diffusely elevated values extending from the interventricular septum to the inferior and lateral walls of the left ventricle. Late gadolinium enhancement was predominantly observed on the endocardial surface, although the distribution pattern was atypical for classical myocarditis (Fig. 4B).

By day 33, the patient had recovered intrinsic cardiac conduction, and temporary pacing was discontinued on day 36.

On day 43, the patient developed bilateral lower-limb weakness. An MRI of the thighs demonstrated high signal intensity on short tau inversion recovery sequences in the tensor fascia latae, gastrocnemius, and tibialis anterior muscles, suggesting possible immune-mediated myositis (Fig. 5). As the patient was already receiving high-dose corticosteroids, no additional immunosuppressive therapy was initiated.

Follow-up contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) performed 10 weeks after the initiation of durvalumab and tremelimumab revealed a progressive enlargement of all hepatic tumors, and the patient was diagnosed with progressive disease (Fig. 6). By day 84, the prednisolone dose was tapered to 15 mg/day and the patient was transferred to a rehabilitation facility for continued physical therapy and supportive care.

Discussion

Immune-mediated myocarditis is rare, with an estimated incidence of 0.06-1.14%, but the risk appears to be elevated in patients receiving combination immunotherapy involving anti-CTLA-4 antibodies compared to monotherapy with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agents. This observation suggests the synergistic potentiation of autoimmune toxicity in dual-agent regimens (6).

Myocarditis is characterized by myocardial inflammation accompanied by myocyte necrosis, which may present as a diffuse or focal pattern. Diffuse involvement can result in fulminant heart failure, arrhythmia, conduction disturbances, or sudden cardiac death. The initial symptoms are often nonspecific and may include fatigue, chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, or syncope.

Laboratory and imaging findings that support the diagnosis include elevated cardiac biomarkers (troponin T/I, and CK-MB), abnormal electrocardiographic findings, elevated natriuretic peptides (e.g., N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide), left ventricular dysfunction on echocardiography, regional wall motion abnormalities, pericardial effusion, and increased myocardial wall thickness. However, it is important to note that up to 10% of patients with immune-mediated myocarditis may have normal electrocardiograms, thus potentially delaying the diagnosis (7).

Given that the clinical presentation may mimic acute coronary syndrome, coronary angiography is often warranted to exclude ischemic heart disease. A myocardial biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis because it can reveal inflammatory cell infiltration and myocyte damage. In cases where a biopsy is not feasible, the diagnosis may be made clinically based on the presence of elevated troponin levels or cardiac MRI findings consistent with myocardial edema, in conjunction with appropriate symptoms and the clinical context (8).

The onset of immune-mediated myocarditis typically occurs within the first three months after treatment initiation and it appears to be independent of the cumulative dose (9). Although the precise pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear, histopathological features often include the infiltration of CD8-positive T lymphocytes and macrophages within the myocardial tissue. Inflammatory changes frequently extend to the cardiac conduction system, and contribute to atrioventricular blocks and potentially fatal arrhythmias. Although an endomyocardial biopsy revealed only mild lymphocytic infiltration and interstitial edema without myocyte necrosis, ICI-related myocarditis exhibits a wide pathological spectrum. Some patients present with fulminant myocarditis accompanied by extensive necrosis, as described by Yamaguchi et al. (10), while others develop severe conduction disturbances with only minimal inflammatory changes on biopsy. In our case, the left ventricular ejection fraction was preserved, and the main clinical issue was complete atrioventricular block. These findings suggest that the immune-mediated injury is predominantly localized to the cardiac conduction system rather than to the working myocardium. Because conduction tissue occupies a small anatomic area, sampling errors during biopsy may have contributed to the apparent discrepancy between the clinical severity and histopathological findings.

If immune-mediated myocarditis is clinically suspected, then the immediate discontinuation of ICI therapy is recommended, and high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1,000 mg/day) should be initiated without delay, ideally under continuous electrocardiographic monitoring. In steroid-refractory cases, other immunosuppressive therapies, including intravenous immunoglobulin, mycophenolate mofetil, infliximab, rabbit anti-thymocyte globulin, plasma exchange, alemtuzumab, and abatacept, have been explored with variable success (11). Early steroid initiation should not be withheld while awaiting a confirmatory diagnosis when the clinical suspicion is high.

In the present case, myositis was observed during the patient's clinical course. ImAEs may occur simultaneously in different organs, and careful attention should be paid to the “3M triad” of myocarditis, myositis, and myasthenia gravis. These events often overlap, thus leading to a rapid clinical deterioration. Once severe imAEs such as myocarditis and myositis develop, they are associated with poor outcomes despite the prompt initiation of high-dose corticosteroids and supportive therapy. Recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that ICI-related myocarditis accompanied by myositis and/or myasthenia gravis has a markedly worse prognosis. The “3M triad” syndrome has been reported to have an in-hospital mortality of approximately 38%, reflecting profound systemic immune activation and involvement of the respiratory and bulbar musculature (12). Our patient presented a “2M” phenotype (myocarditis with myositis but without myasthenia gravis). Although the absence of overt myasthenic symptoms may have contributed to the favorable clinical course, the coexistence of myositis itself has been reported to be a predictor of poorer outcomes in ICI-associated myocarditis (13).

Therefore, careful monitoring and early recognition of these complications are essential, particularly when combination immunotherapy is used. The identified risk factors for immune-mediated myocarditis include combination ICI therapy (particularly anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4), a history of imAEs in other organs, underlying cardiovascular disease, and previous cancer treatment-related cardiac dysfunction.

Conclusion

We encountered a case of immune-mediated myocarditis following combination immunotherapy with anti-PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies in a patient with an unresectable HCC. This regimen is currently recommended as a first-line treatment. However, as this case highlights, imAEs, such as myocarditis, although rare, may be fatal. Therefore, it is essential that institutions administer ICIs to establish and implement systematic monitoring protocols that enable early detection and timely intervention for serious imAEs.
