GZR18, a GLP-1 analog with once-weekly or bi-weekly dosing for body weight management: A randomized, placebo-controlled, phase 1b/2a trial
Authors: Linong Ji, Leili Gao, Ruihua Dong, Mingxia Yuan, Dong Zhao, Shuguang Pang, Jing Zhao, Liyuan Zhao, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGZR18 is a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist under development for overweight/obesity. In this randomized, placebo-controlled phase 1b/2a trial in Chinese adults with overweight or obesity, different dose titration regimens and dosing frequencies (once-weekly [QW] vs. bi-weekly [Q2W]) are evaluated to assess safety and weight-loss efficacy over 26 weeks (Part A) or 35 weeks (Part B). Sixty participants are enrolled, of whom 46 complete the trial. The least-squares mean change in body weight is −9.36% for GZR18 vs. 6.68% for placebo in Part A and −17.8% (QW) and −12.8% (Q2W) for GZR18 vs. 0.7% for placebo in Part B; GZR18 also improves other weight-related and metabolic parameters. GZR18 is generally safe and well tolerated, with mostly mild-to-moderate gastrointestinal adverse events and no investigational-product-related serious adverse events reported. These findings warrant larger and longer trials of GZR18 for body weight management. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06256536).Keywords: overweight, obesity, ultra-long-acting, GLP-1, weight reduction, bofanglutide, GZR18, randomized controlled trial, Chinese population

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a global epidemic and a significant public health challenge.1 World Health Organization (WHO) reported that over 2.5 billion adults aged 18 years and older were overweight, and more than 890 million of them were obese. The increasing prevalence of obesity is closely associated with heightened morbidity and mortality, primarily due to comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and certain cancers.2 These comorbidities significantly increase healthcare costs, underscoring the urgent need for effective and sustainable treatment strategies.3

Lifestyle interventions have been identified as the fundamental approach to long-term weight management.4,5 However, individuals with obesity or with overweight and associated comorbidities, particularly those who have failed to lose weight or are unable to maintain weight loss with lifestyle interventions, are recommended to initiate anti-obesity medications immediately according to current clinical guidelines.6,7 Nevertheless, traditional anti-obesity medications are difficult to use to achieve or maintain a weight reduction goal of 5%–10%, which has been proven to confer multiple cardiovascular and metabolic benefits.8,9,10,11 Despite that bariatric surgery offers an effective alternative for some severely obese patients,12 it is not a viable option for most patients due to the high cost and the associated risks and adverse effects of the invasive procedure,13 highlighting the demand for better anti-obesity medications with enhanced efficacy and good safety.

Glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (RAs) have emerged as effective and safe treatment options for overweight or obesity. Several GLP-1-based therapies including single-target agonists and multi-target analogs (e.g., liraglutide [3.0 mg], semaglutide [2.4 mg], and tirzepatide [15 mg]), have demonstrated robust efficacy in clinical trials, with mean body weight reductions of up to 5.4%–20.2% compared with placebo.14,15 Despite the considerable therapeutic potential of daily or weekly GLP-1 RAs for body weight management, long-term adherence and persistence in real-world settings are generally poor,16,17 resulting in less favorable outcomes than those reported in prospective controlled studies.18,19,20 One contributing factor may be the frequent injection requirements with current GLP-1 RAs,21,22,23 underscoring the unmet need for effective GLP-1 therapies with less frequent dosing intervals, such as bi-weekly or monthly formulations.

GZR18 (generic name: bofanglutide) is a subcutaneously administered GLP-1 RA currently under development for the treatment of T2D and overweight or obesity. It was modified from native GLP-1 by incorporating a 22C fatty diacid side chain to extend its half-life. This modification confers high albumin binding and low receptor affinity, leading to reduced free drug concentration and prolonged release from the subcutaneous depot, thereby resulting in a moderate and gentle bioactivity.24 Since this bioactivity correlates with GLP-1-mediated adverse reactions,25 its modulation attenuates the dose-related gastrointestinal adverse events that are common to potent, short-acting GLP-1 RAs.24 Preclinical studies in Sprague Dawley (SD) rat and db/db mouse models confirm its safety and anti-obesity effects.24 In the phase 1a trial, GZR18 was safe and well tolerated in healthy participants, achieving weight reduction of up to −2.63% after single or double administration.26 Together, the observed tolerability supports exploring higher doses of GZR18 to maximize therapeutic efficacy. This trial was designed to evaluate the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), and efficacy of GZR18 in Chinese adults with overweight or obesity. Specifically, the trial investigated the potential of GZR18 to reduce body weight through different dose-escalation strategies and target doses and evaluated the feasibility of administering GZR18 at different frequencies (once-weekly [QW] and bi-weekly [Q2W]).

Results

Participant disposition, demographics, and baseline characteristics

Participant disposition for the trial is shown in Figure 1. Between July 14, 2022 and October 22, 2023, a total of 24 participants were randomized and received treatment in Part A, while 36 participants were randomized and 35 of them received treatment in Part B (one participant withdrew informed consent form before treatment administration). After dose escalation to 9 mg/week in Part B, 26 participants treated with GZR18 were divided into dosing subcohorts of QW (N = 16) or Q2W (N = 10). Overall, 19 (79.2%) and 27 (75%) participants completed the trial in Part A and Part B, respectively. The primary reason for trial discontinuation was withdrawal by participants’ decision. Regarding GZR18 exposure, among the 18 participants enrolled in the Part A, 17 participants (94.4%) reached the maximum dose of 4.8 mg and 1 participant (5.6%) reached 3.6 mg. Among the 26 participants enrolled in the Part B, 17 participants (65.4%) reached the maximum dose of 30 mg, 3 participants (11.5%) reached 24 mg, and 6 participants (23.1%) received 18 mg or lower. Demographics and baseline characteristics were well balanced across treatment groups within each part (Table 1).

Safety profile

In Part A, 95.8% (23/24) of participants reported at least one treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE), with 100% (18/18) in the GZR18 group and 83.3% (5/6) in the placebo group. All TEAEs were classified as grade 1 or 2 in severity, and no serious adverse events (SAEs) or discontinuations due to TEAEs were reported. The most frequently reported TEAEs were gastrointestinal adverse events (GIAEs), including decreased appetite, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, which occurred in 55.6% (10/18) of participants in the GZR18 group and 50.0% (3/6) in the placebo group. Two hypoglycemic events occurred in two participants (11.1%) in the GZR18 group, one grade 1 event (probably related; occurred at midnight 3 days after the first 0.6 mg dose and resolved spontaneously without intervention) and one grade 2 event (unlikely related; occurred during a fasting visit prior to the 2.4 mg dose after nearly 17 h of fasting and resolved following intake of sugar lumps). Both were classified as probable symptomatic hypoglycemia (non-blood glucose-confirmed). Injection site reaction was reported in two participants, one in each of the GZR18 and placebo groups (Table 2).

In Part B, a total of 88.6% (31/35) of participants reported TEAEs, with 87.5% (14/16) in the GZR18 QW group, 100% (10/10) in the GZR18 Q2W group, and 77.8% (7/9) in the placebo group. Most TEAEs were classified as grade 1 or 2 in severity. Only one participant reported an SAE (appendicitis) in the GZR18 QW group, which was considered to be unlikely relatced to investigational product (IP) by the investigator. The TEAEs leading to trial discontinuation occurred in three participants in the GZR18 QW group; all of them were GIAEs. GIAEs were the most common TEAEs, occurring in 56.3% (9/16) of participants in the GZR18 QW group, 40% (4/10) in the GZR18 Q2W group, and 33.3% (3/9) in the placebo group. The incidence of IP-related GIAEs was observed to be highest at the 5 mg dose and decreased gradually over time (Figure S2). Two participants (7.7%) in the GZR18 group experienced six hypoglycemic events. One event was reported in one participant (6.3%) in the QW group (probably related; occurred 1 h after the 24-mg dose and resolved after food intake). Five events were reported in one participant (10.0%) in the Q2W group (all possibly related; occurred mainly before breakfast, 2–4 days after administration of 18 mg or 24 mg doses, and resolved after food intake). All events were grade 1 in severity and classified as probable symptomatic hypoglycemia (non-blood glucose-confirmed) (Table 2).

There were no deaths reported in the trial, and all TEAEs resolved during the trial. A total of three (16.7%) and two (7.7%) participants treated with GZR18 developed anti-GZR18 antibodies in Part A and Part B, respectively. Nevertheless, no participants had neutralizing antibodies throughout the trial. Furthermore, no thyroid events or pancreatic events were reported among GZR18-treated participants.

Weight-related outcomes

In part A, a decrease in body weight was observed in GZR18-treated participants over the treatment period, including 18-week dose escalation and 8-week maintenance (Figure 2A). At week 26, the least-squares mean (LSM) percent change in body weight was −9.36%, compared to 6.68% with placebo, with the estimated treatment difference (ETD) of −16.1% (95% confidence interval [CI]: −26.4, −5.7) (Figure 2B). After 26 weeks of treatment, 66.7% of the participants treated with GZR18 had a weight reduction of at least 5%, while none of those with placebo demonstrated such a reduction (Figure 2C). Consistent with the outcomes of body weight, significant reductions in body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference were observed at week 26 for GZR18 versus placebo, with a BMI of −3.0 and 1.9 kg/m2 and waist circumference of −8.0 and 0.2 cm in the GZR18 and placebo groups, respectively (Figures 2D and 2E). Notably, during the treatment period at the highest dose of 4.8 mg from week 14 to week 26, body weight continued to decrease in participants who received GZR18, with an LSM percent change in body weight of −4.24% compared to 1.22% for placebo (ETD of −5.5% [95% confidence interval (CI): −10.5, −0.4]) (Figure 2B). Significant improvements in BMI and waist circumference were also observed (Table S1).

In part B, GZR18 achieved enhanced body weight reduction in both the QW and Q2W regimens at a high target dose (30 mg) (Figure 3A). At week 35, the LSM percent change in body weight was −17.8% in the QW group and −12.8% in the Q2W group, compared to 0.7% in the placebo group. Both GZR18 dosing frequencies were superior to placebo, with ETD relative to placebo of −18.6% (95% CI: −25.5, −11.6) for the GZR18 QW group and −13.5% (95% CI: −21.0, −6.0) for the GZR18 Q2W group (Figure 3B). After 35 weeks of treatment, 100% of participants in the GZR18 QW group and 71.4% in the Q2W group achieved at least a 5% reduction in body weight, compared with none in the placebo group. Furthermore, 90%, 80%, and 40% of participants in the GZR18 QW group achieved a weight reduction of ≥10%, 15%, and 20%, respectively, and proportion was 71.4%, 42.9%, and 14.3%, respectively, for the GZR18 Q2W group (Figure 3C). Additionally, significant reductions in BMI and waist circumference were observed in the GZR18 QW and Q2W groups relative to the placebo group, with BMI of −5.7, −4.4, and 0.4 kg/m2 and waist circumference of −12.6, −10.7, and 2.0 cm in the GZR18 QW, GZR18 Q2W, and placebo groups, respectively (Figures 3D and 3E).

Metabolic profiles

Participants who received GZR18 demonstrated significant improvements in several metabolic parameters, including lipid profile, fasting blood glucose, fasting insulin, and blood pressure. These improvements were observed in both Part A (Table S2) and Part B (Figure 4), though they were more pronounced in Part B. In Part B, significant reductions in metabolic parameters were evident in both the GZR18 QW and GZR18 Q2W groups, with slightly more pronounced benefits in the GZR18 QW group. Specifically, significant reductions in lipid profile were observed in the GZR18 groups in comparison to the placebo group, with changes of −1.2, −0.9, and 0.1 mmol/L in triglycerides; −0.7, −1.1 and −0.2 mmol/L in total cholesterol; and −0.4, −0.7, and −0.1 mmol/L in low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol in the GZR18 QW, GZR18 Q2W, and placebo groups, respectively (Figure 4A). Fasting blood glucose levels decreased by 1.0 mmol/L in the GZR18 QW group and 0.7 mmol/L in the GZR18 Q2W group, compared to a modest reduction of 0.2 mmol/L in the placebo group (Figure 4B). A similar trend was observed in the fasting insulin levels, which exhibited a significant reduction in GZR18 groups compared to placebo (Figure 4C). Additionally, a notable reduction in blood pressure was observed in the GZR18 groups in comparison to the placebo group, with changes of −13.0, −11.4, and −2.8 mmHg in systolic blood pressure (SBP) and −6.6, −9.6, and 0.6 mmHg in diastolic blood pressure (DBP) in the GZR18 QW, GZR18 Q2W, and placebo groups, respectively (Figures 4D and 4E).

Pharmacokinetics

The pharmacokinetics of GZR18 were characterized through the measurement of plasma concentrations at various doses, ranging from 0.6 mg to 4.8 mg in Part A and from 1.5 mg to 30 mg in Part B. The PK profile demonstrated a consistent increase in plasma trough concentrations with escalating doses in QW frequency, in either Part A or Part B (Figure 5). In the Q2W sub-cohort of Part B, a similar trend was observed at doses ranging from 1.5 to 7 mg (all participants experienced once-weekly dosing) and from 18 to 30 mg (all participants experienced bi-weekly dosing). Furthermore, the trough concentration stabilized in the dose-maintenance period of GZR18 at 4.8 mg, indicating that GZR18 reached steady-state after four consecutive administrations.

Discussion

This multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 1b/2a trial investigated the efficacy and safety of GZR18 for weight reduction in Chinese adults with overweight or obesity, particularly exploring a bi-weekly dosing regimen. GZR18 at doses up to 30 mg demonstrated robust placebo-adjusted body weight reductions of 18.6% (QW) and 13.5% (Q2W), accompanied by improvements in several metabolic parameters and a favorable safety profile.

Previous in vitro pharmacological studies suggested that GZR18 exhibits comparable or moderately lower bioactivity compared to semaglutide.24 Therefore, we investigated a low-dose-escalation regimen from 0.6 mg to 4.8 mg in Part A, which was slightly wider than the dose-escalation range of semaglutide (0.25 mg–2.4 mg).27 As a result, GZR18 demonstrated favorable safety and tolerability at doses up to 4.8 mg, with no IP-related SAEs, grade 3 or higher TEAEs, and GIAE rates comparable to placebo.

Consistent with recent trends, one direction for novel GLP-1 RAs development is to explore whether higher doses can enhance therapeutic efficacy. Like semaglutide, a 7.2 mg dose for weight management (beyond the approved 2.4 mg dose) was under review for marketing.28 As Part A indicated that GZR18 was well tolerated up to 4.8 mg, Part B was conducted to explore its therapeutic potential at higher doses (1.5 mg–30 mg). Throughout Part B, GZR18 exhibited an acceptable safety profile without IP-related SAEs or unexpected safety concerns. Nausea, diarrhea, and vomiting were the most common IP-related TEAEs. These GIAEs were all mild to moderate, with incidence similar to that reported for incretin-based therapies.27,29,30,31,32,33,34 Notably, GIAEs occurred primarily at the beginning of dose escalation, with incidence peaking at 5 mg and then declining over the treatment, suggesting that participants may develop tolerance with continued treatment. Further studies are needed to confirm this and evaluate the long-term safety with a large sample size.

The incidence of hypoglycemia observed with GZR18 was numerically higher than the <1% reported for semaglutide 2.4 mg in the STEP 1 trial.15 Several factors may contribute to this difference. First, this trial enrolled an exclusively Asian (Chinese) population, whereas STEP 1 was conducted in a predominantly White cohort; notably, the incidence was 3% in the Asian-predominant STEP 7 trial.27 Second, as an early-phase trial, we employed proactive surveillance using a separate hypoglycemia form, which likely increased the detection and reporting of events. This is supported by a phase 2 trial of semaglutide that used a similar form and reported an incidence of 1%–9.7%.35 Furthermore, several hypoglycemic events in this trial occurred in the context of prolonged fasting required for pre-specified clinic visits. Third, the small sample size of this trial can amplify the apparent incidence rate when a small number of participants experience hypoglycemia. Importantly, all hypoglycemic events in this trial were mild (grade 1) and rapidly reversible, with no episodes of severe hypoglycemia. These findings nevertheless underscore the need for ongoing vigilance regarding hypoglycemia risk in the future clinical development and potential application of GZR18, particularly with dose escalation or expansion to broader populations.

Once-weekly GZR18 demonstrated potent weight-loss efficacy, with mean placebo-adjusted reductions of 16.1% (Part A) and 18.6% (Part B). The observed weight increase in the placebo group (6.68%) contributed substantially to the treatment difference in Part A. This increase may be partly related to lifestyle changes resulting from widespread COVID-19 infections and associated room confinement during the latter part of the treatment period, which affected a majority of participants in both the GZR18 and placebo groups. Additionally, the small sample size of the placebo group may have amplified the observed mean change, as limited numbers are more susceptible to individual variability and random fluctuation. In this context, the absolute weight reduction of 9.36% achieved with GZR18 remains clinically meaningful. Moreover, higher doses of GZR18 in Part B produced even greater absolute reductions (up to −17.8% in the QW group). Together, these results suggest promising weight-loss efficacy for GZR18. While the observed reduction appears numerically comparable to or greater than that reported for established incretin-based therapies,27,36 such indirect comparisons are constrained by differences in trial designs and populations. Therefore, a direct head-to-head comparison of GZR18 versus 15 mg tirzepatide and 2.4 mg semaglutide in participants with overweight or obesity is currently being conducted in the United States (NCT06737042) and in China (NCT07150975), respectively. The results from these studies will be critical to robustly establish the relative efficacy and safety of GZR18 compared with other incretin-based therapies. Despite no dose-maintenance period was included in Part B, approximately 40% of participants receiving once-weekly GZR18 achieved ≥20% weight reduction. This strong efficacy likely results from the high therapeutic doses achieved in Part B, which may be attributed to the optimized molecular design and the associated moderate bioactivity of GZR18.24,26 Based on the unplateaued weight-loss trajectory observed, extending treatment duration may yield even greater weight reductions. This has been tested in phase 2b and phase 3 clinical trials.

Longer dosing intervals for GLP-1 RAs may potentially improve medication adherence and treatment persistence.37 Accordingly, a bi-weekly GZR18 regimen starting at 9 mg was evaluated based on preclinical and clinical data: (1) in healthy participants, doubling the GZR18 dose achieved comparable plasma concentrations at day 14 to standard dose at day 726; (2) a single administration of 9-mg dose maintained plasma GZR18 levels above the effective exposure threshold for 2 weeks, as extrapolated from minimal effective dose studies in obese mice and human pharmacokinetic data.24,26 In the present trial, bi-weekly GZR18 demonstrated a placebo-adjusted weight reduction of 13.5% after 35 weeks, along with favorable tolerability. The potential of bi-weekly administration was corroborated in a subsequent phase 2b trial, in which 48 mg Q2W GZR18 achieved a 15.0% placebo-adjusted weight reduction at week 30, alongside an acceptable safety profile.38 Moreover, bi-weekly GLP-1 RAs may also bring an advantage in the perceived duration of GIAEs: median durations of nausea/vomiting/diarrhea of bi-weekly GZR18 was similar to that reported for once-weekly semaglutide,39 but symptoms occurred during approximately 25% of the dosing interval (3/14 days) for GZR18 versus approximately 40% (3/7 days) for semaglutide.

Recent research has highlighted the comprehensive therapeutic potential of GLP-1 RAs beyond weight loss and glycemic control, such as on cardiovascular and other metabolic benefits.40 Consistent with these observations, this trial (particularly Part B) demonstrated that GZR18 improved multiple metabolic parameters, including waist circumference (−12.6 cm), lipid profile (triglycerides: −1.2 mmol/L; total cholesterol: −1.1 mmol/L; LDL cholesterol: −0.7 mmol/L), fasting blood glucose (−1.0 mmol/L), fasting insulin (−9.6 μIU/mL), blood pressure (SBP: −13.0 mmHg; DBP: −9.6 mmHg), aminotransferase (−27.2 U/L), and aspartate aminotransferase (−12.5 U/L). These improvements may translate into a reduced risk of cardiovascular disease and metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis,41,42 thus supporting the further investigation and development of GZR18 in treating these weight-related comorbidities.

In conclusion, GZR18 demonstrated favorable tolerability and safety profiles and exhibited robust efficacy in reducing body weight and improving metabolic profiles in Chinese adults with overweight or obesity at doses up to 30 mg. These findings provide preliminary evidence of GZR18 as a promising agent for the treatment of overweight or obesity. Future larger and longer trials are warranted to fully assess its efficacy at higher doses, with extended dosing intervals (e.g., bi-weekly administration), and to assess long-term benefits.

Limitations of the study

The present trial has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and short duration of the current trial restrict conclusions on long-term safety and efficacy. Specifically, participants in Part B received the final target dose for only 4 weeks, limiting thorough evaluation of sustained efficacy and adverse events of GZR18. The efficacy and complete safety profile of GZR18, particularly the incidence of rare adverse events, will be further evaluated in large-scale phase 3 confirmatory trials (NCT06728124; NCT07150975). Second, the present trial consisted exclusively of Chinese participants, limiting generalizability of these findings to other racial and ethnic groups. A phase 2 clinical trial is currently ongoing in the United States to evaluate GZR18 in a more diverse population (NCT06737042). Third, dosing frequency in Part B was assigned based on individual preference. Nevertheless, the double-blind treatment assignment (GZR18/placebo) was strictly maintained throughout the trial, and all analyses were conducted between GZR18 and placebo groups.

Experimental models and study participant details

This multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 1b/2a trial was conducted at four clinical centers across China to evaluate the safety and efficacy of GZR18 in adults with overweight or obesity. This trial was prospectively registered on Chinadrugtrials.org.cn (CTR20221495; June 15, 2022) before the first participant enrollment (July 14, 2022) and later on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06256536; February 5, 2024) to enhance international visibility. And the trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards of all participating centers, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. As part of the trial design, we first conducted a low-dose escalation trial of GZR18 (0.6 mg–4.8 mg, QW) in participants with overweight or obesity (Part A). Based on the preliminary data indicating the favorable safety and tolerability results from Part A, a high-dose escalation ranging from 1.5 mg to 30 mg with a larger dose increment was conducted in obese participants (Part B).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1. Male or female aged 18–65 years (inclusive). 2. Able to understand the trial procedures; willing to strictly adherence to the protocol, and having voluntarily provided signed informed consent. 3. Part A: Obese participants (BMI ≥28.0 kg/m2) or overweight participants (24.0 ≤ BMI <28.0 kg/m2) who met at least one of the following: 1) a ≥50% increase in food intake in the 6 months prior to screening; 2) concomitant diagnosis of pre-diabetes (defined per American Diabetes Association criteria as fasting plasma glucose 5.6–6.9 mmol/L or 2-h post-oral glucose tolerance test [OGTT] 7.8–11.1 mmol/L), hypertension, dyslipidemia, or fatty liver; 3) concomitant weight-bearing joint pain; 4) obesity-induced dyspnea or obstructive sleep apnea syndrome; or Part B: Obese participants (BMI ≥28.0 kg/m2).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1. Suspicion of allergy to any investigational product (IP), its ingredients, or related congeners, as assessed by the investigator. 2. Body weight change >5.0% for any reason (e.g., diet, exercise) within 12 weeks prior to screening. 3. Use of the following drugs or treatments within 3 months prior to screening: 1) glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonists, GLP-1R/glucagon receptor (GCGR) dual agonists, glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor (GIPR)/GLP-1R dual agonists, or GIPR/GLP-1R/GCGR triple agonists; 2) drugs affecting body weight, including systemic corticosteroids, antidepressants (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs], serotonin noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors [SNRIs], tricyclics, tetracyclics), antipsychotics, or sedatives (e.g., imipramine, amitriptyline, mirtazapine, paroxetine, phenelzine, chlorpromazine, thioridazine, clozapine, olanzapine, valproic acid/derivatives, lithium salts); 3) Chinese herbal medicines, health-care products, or meal replacements affecting body weight; 4) weight-loss medications (e.g., sibutramine, orlistat, phentermine, phenylpropanolamine, mazindol, diethylpropion, lorcaserin, phentermine/topiramate, naltrexone/bupropion); 5) hypoglycemic agents (e.g., metformin, α-glucosidase inhibitors, sulfonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 [DPP-4] inhibitors, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 [SGLT-2] inhibitors, thiazolidinediones); 6) participation in other interventional clinical trials. 4. Any laboratory parameter meeting the following criteria at screening (one retest allowed during screening if clinically justified, with reason documented): 1) serum calcitonin ≥50 ng/L (pg/mL); 2) ALT ≥3.0 × upper limit of normal (ULN) and/or AST ≥3.0 × ULN and/or total bilirubin ≥2.0 × ULN; 3) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (CKD-EPI equation); 4) thyroid dysfunction (thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH] >6 mIU/L or <0.4 mIU/L); 5) fasting triglycerides ≥5.64 mmol/L (500 mg/dL); 6) blood amylase or lipase >1.5 × ULN; 7) international normalized ratio (INR) of prothrombin time > ULN; 8) hemoglobin <110 g/L (men) or <100 g/L (women). 5. Resting heart rate <50 or >100 beats/min on 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) at screening. 6. Clinically significant ECG abnormalities at screening, including second- or third-degree atrioventricular (AV) block, long QT syndrome or QTc >500 ms, left or right bundle branch block, pre-excitation syndrome, or other significant arrhythmia (excluding sinus arrhythmia). 7. Pregnancy, lactation, or unwillingness to use effective contraception throughout the trial and for 6 months after the last IP dose. 8. Blood donation/loss ≥400 mL or bone marrow donation within 3 months prior to screening, or history of hematological disorders (e.g., hemoglobinopathy, hemolytic anemia, thalassemia, sickle cell anemia). 9. A total of 59 participants were included in the full analysis set (FAS): 24 in Part A and 35 in Part B. In Part A, the mean (SD) age was 34.2 (9.7) years in the GZR18 group and 31.5 (6.4) years in the placebo group; the proportion of males was 44.4% and 66.7%, respectively. Method details

Randomization and masking

Participants were randomized in a 3:1 ratio, with 18 participants assigned to receive GZR18 and 6 assigned to receive placebo in Part A, and 27 participants assigned to receive GZR18 and 9 assigned to receive a placebo in Part B. Randomization was managed centrally via an interactive web response system (IWRS) to ensure allocation concealment and minimize selection bias. Both participants and investigators were blinded to treatment allocation throughout the trial, with GZR18 and placebo identical in appearance and administration to maintain blinding. Unblinding was permitted only in the case of medical emergencies or after the database was locked at the end of the trial to ensure data integrity.

Intervention

To evaluate the safety, tolerability, PK, and efficacy of GZR18, we first conducted a 26-week, dose-escalation trial with a low target dose in 24 participants with overweight or obesity (Part A) (Figure S1; 07/14/2022–03/30/2023). Specifically, 24 participants were randomized in a 3:1 ratio to receive subcutaneous administration of GZR18 or placebo (0.6 mg–4.8 mg). The dose-escalation schedule was performed with a small dose increment: 0.6 mg for one week, followed by 1.2 mg for one week, 2.4 mg for two weeks, 3.0 mg for two weeks, 3.6 mg for four weeks, 4.2 mg for four weeks, and 4.8 mg for four weeks. During the trial conduct, protocol amendments were implemented to introduce an efficacy exploration phase (Amendment V2.0, September 16, 2022). Those who tolerated the highest dose of 4.8 mg continued on this dose for an additional eight weeks in an efficacy exploration phase, resulting in 12 weeks of treatment at the dose of 4.8 mg. Participants received a total of 26 weeks of treatment, followed by a three-week safety follow-up period.

Part B was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of GZR18 in treating obesity at higher doses (1.5 mg–30 mg) (Figure S1; 12/9/2022–10/22/2023). Its initiation was contingent upon meeting all of the following criteria in Part A: at least 15 participants had cumulatively reached the 3.6 mg dose level; all these participants had completed a minimum of two weeks of dosing at that level; and no safety signals meeting the pre-defined termination criteria had emerged. Participants in this part were also randomized in a 3:1 ratio to receive GZR18 or placebo beginning at 1.5 mg for one week, followed by 3 mg for two weeks, 5 mg for four weeks, 7 mg for four weeks, 9 mg for four weeks, 12 mg for four weeks, 15 mg for four weeks, 18 mg for four weeks, 24 mg for four weeks, and 30 mg for four weeks. After dose escalation to 9 mg/week, participants were divided into dosing sub-cohorts of once-weekly (QW) or bi-weekly (Q2W) based on individual preference. Nevertheless, the double-blind assignment to GZR18 or placebo was strictly maintained throughout the trial (i.e., both QW and Q2W cohorts included GZR18 and placebo groups, and participants remained blinded to their specific treatment assignment). To ensure the safety during the dose escalation, additional safety assessments were conducted prior to each dose escalation beginning with the dose of 5 mg, and once during treatment at the 30 mg dose level. Participants in Part B received a total of 35 weeks of treatment, followed by a three-week safety follow-up period to comprehensively monitor adverse events. During the trial conduct, protocol amendments were implemented to introduce a bi-weekly administration regimen (Amendment V3.0, January 16, 2023) and to explore higher dose levels of 24 mg (Amendment V4.0, April 3, 2023) and 30 mg (Amendment V5.0, July 4, 2023).

Outcomes and assessments

The primary outcome of this trial was to evaluate the safety of GZR18. This was assessed by monitoring the incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs), including gastrointestinal adverse events (GIAEs; e.g., nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea), hypoglycemic episodes, and injection site reactions. Safety evaluations also included vital signs (e.g., blood pressure and heart rate), results of physical examinations, 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs), and clinical laboratory parameters, including hematological assessments, liver and kidney function tests, and lipid profiles. Immunogenicity was assessed by testing for anti-GZR18 and neutralizing antibodies. All adverse events were classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0, and the association between an AE and the investigational product (IP) was assessed by the investigator based on pre-specified criteria (definitely related, probably related, possibly related, unlikely related, definitely not related). In Part A, the primary endpoint also included the change from baseline in body weight at the end of treatment.

Secondary outcomes included the efficacy and PK characteristics of GZR18. Efficacy endpoints included changes from baseline in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and blood pressure (systolic and diastolic). Additionally, metabolic outcomes including fasting blood glucose, fasting insulin, and lipid profiles (total cholesterol, triglycerides) were assessed. An additional efficacy measure was the proportion of participants achieving a ≥5% reduction in body weight from baseline. PK endpoints included measurements of trough concentrations of GZR18 following repeated doses, which assessed drug exposure over time. In Part A, exploratory endpoints included changes in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, fasting blood glucose and fasting insulin at a dose-maintenance of 4.8 mg from week 14 to week 26.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The target sample size was initially set at 114 participants (Part A: 24; Part B: 90). Following favorable safety and tolerability data from Part A and previous studies of GZR18, the Part B protocol v2.0 was amended to explore higher doses, and its target sample size was revised to 36. The final enrolled sample size was 60 participants (24 in Part A and 36 in Part B). This adjustment was based on the assessment of data from preclinical studies and phase 1 clinical trial of GZR18,24,26 as well as information on GLP-1 RAs with similar molecular structure that are under development or already marketed. This information was considered sufficient by the investigator and sponsor to meet the trial objectives, and it aligns with common practice and relevant regulatory guidelines.43,44

Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the safety and efficacy outcomes of the trial. The safety analysis set (SS), which included all randomized participants who received at least one dose of IP, was used for safety outcomes. The FAS, which included all randomized participants who received at least one dose of IP and had at least one post-baseline efficacy variable data, was used for the analysis of efficacy outcomes. Outliers were prospectively defined in the trial protocol as clinically implausible values and were subject to review by a blinded Data Review Committee. Upon review, no values met this criterion; consequently, all analyses were performed using the full, unmodified dataset. Efficacy outcomes, measured as changes from baseline to the planned endpoint, were analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model on the FAS. For participants who discontinued early, their last available post-baseline body weight measurement was carried forward to the planned trial endpoint. The ANCOVA model included baseline value as the covariate, group as the fixed effect, and the change from baseline as the dependent variable, which was chosen as it increases precision by adjusting for the baseline value of the outcome, providing a valid test of the treatment effect in randomized studies. The model’s key assumptions were verified: linearity and homoscedasticity were assessed visually via residual plots and supported by Levene’s test (p > 0.05); residual normality was assessed via Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test (p > 0.05). Results were presented as the least-squares mean (LSM) and its two-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) of the change from baseline in both groups, and the LSM difference between the treatment and control groups and its two-sided 95% CI. Statistical significance for comparisons versus placebo was assessed using a Z-test based on these model estimates. All efficacy analyses were conducted for comparison between the GZR18 groups and the placebo group, not for comparison between the QW and Q2W cohorts. Due to the small sample size in the placebo group, participants receiving placebo, whether in the QW or Q2W cohort, were pooled into placebo control group for all analyses. Summaries for other efficacy and safety variables included descriptive statistics for continuous measures (mean and standard deviation or standard error) and for categorical measures (sample size, frequency and percentages). All analyses were performed with SAS software (version 9.4).
