Expression profile of thyroid hormone deiodinases in the adult laying hen (Gallus gallus domesticus)
Authors: Dorota Katarzyńska-Banasik, Kinga Kowalik-Chanek, Anna Kozubek, Andrzej Sechman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the mRNA expression of three deiodinases (DIO1, DIO2, and DIO3) was investigated in adult (96-week-old) female chicken tissues by using real-time quantitative PCR and specific TaqMan probes. The analysis included the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, thyroid gland, lungs, heart, stomach, small intestine, spleen, pancreas, skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, skin, and reproductive system (RS). Within the RS, deiodinase expression was measured in all oviductal segments (infundibulum, magnum, isthmus, shell gland, and vagina) and the prehierarchical (small [SWF] and large white follicles [LWF]) and preovulatory (F3, F2, and F1) ovarian follicles, with separate analyses for the theca and granulosa layers. The three deiodinases were present in all tissues. Compared to control thyroid tissue, the highest expression of DIO1 was in the liver, followed by the kidney and small intestine. DIO2 exhibited the most significant expression levels in the liver and pituitary gland. The most outstanding result was the extremely high expression of DIO3 in the ovary, with the maximal level in the granulosa layer of preovulatory follicles, followed by the theca layers and the prehierarchical follicles. The expression profiles of deiodinases in adult chicken hens are highly tissue-specific, reflecting the diverse physiological roles of thyroid hormones in these tissues. The exceptionally high expression of DIO3 in the ovary, particularly in the granulosa layer, indicates an essential role of this enzyme in maintaining an appropriate 3,3′,5-triiodothyronine (T3) concentration for follicular development and its participation in protection against excessive transfer of maternal T3 to the oocyte yolk.Keywords: Deiodinases, Expression, RT-qPCR, Chicken

Introduction

In birds, the thyroid gland primarily secretes thyroxine (T4), which is converted to 3,3′,5-triiodothyronine (T3) in peripheral tissues through deiodinases. Three types of deiodinases (DIO1, DIO2, and DIO3) have been identified, with their cDNAs cloned and characterised as selenoproteins (Gereben et al., 1999; Darras et al., 2000). The tissue-specific and developmentally regulated expression of deiodinases reflects their role in maintaining local and systemic thyroid hormone (TH) homeostasis in chickens. DIO1 converts T4 to T3 (the active form of TH) and 3,3′,5′-triiodothyronine (reverse T3; rT3) to 3,3′-diiodothyronine (T2). DIO1 is the primary deiodinase in the liver and kidney, where it plays a role in converting T4 to T3 for systemic circulation. Immunocytochemical studies have shown that DIO1 is localised in hepatocytes, particularly in a thin layer of cells bordering the blood vessels (Verhoelst et al., 2004). DIO2 also converts T4 to T3 to maintain adequate TH signalling within cells; however, it is expressed mainly in the brain, pituitary gland, and skeletal muscles. DIO2 expression is crucial in the brain, especially in the choroid plexus, which forms part of the blood-brain barrier. DIO2 in the choroid plexus converts T4 to T3, which is then transported into the cerebrospinal fluid and distributed to neurons. This mechanism maintains the brain’s T3 homeostasis, which is crucial for neuronal function and development (Verhoelst et al., 2005). Skeletal muscles are another crucial site for DIO2 expression in chickens. This enzyme exerts the highest expression of all deiodinases in chicken muscles during the last week of embryonic life (Van der Geyten et al., 2002). DIO2 activity has been found in slow-twitch and fast-twitch muscles in mice, with slow-twitch muscles having a higher level of activity. It has been suggested that DIO2 is responsible for regulating T3 levels in muscle tissue, which is vital for muscle function and metabolism (Marsili et al., 2010). DIO2 is also expressed in chicken growth plates, where it functions as a regulator of chondrocyte differentiation. According to Shen et al. (2004), tibial dyschondroplasia, a genetic leg defect in broilers, is linked to reduced expression of DIO2 in the growth plates. DIO3, by converting T4 to rT3 and T3 to T2, protects tissues from an excess of TH, especially during development. In chickens, DIO3 has been detected in almost all tissues, with the highest activity observed in the liver and kidneys (Darras et al., 2000; Van der Geyten et al., 2002). In the kidneys, DIO3 protein has been localised in renal tubular epithelial cells, where it inactivates T4 by converting it to rT3 (Verhoelst et al., 2004). This enzyme has also been localised in Purkinje cells of the cerebellum, where it regulates local T3 levels by inactivating T4 (Verhoelst et al., 2002).

The expression and activity of deiodinases are regulated by various factors, including hormones, environmental conditions, and developmental stages. THs regulate the expression of deiodinases through feedback mechanisms, e.g., T3 upregulates the expression of DIO3 in the cerebellum, while T4 is responsible for the upregulation of DIO2 in the choroid plexus (Verhoelst et al., 2005). Glucocorticoids have been shown to increase DIO2 expression in the brain, whereas growth hormone has a stimulating effect on DIO1 expression in the liver. Temperature and nutrition are environmental factors that impact deiodinase expression and activity. Cold exposure enhances DIO2 activity in skeletal muscle, while high ambient temperatures promote the production of rT3 by DIO3 in the liver and kidneys. Fasting, on the other hand, results in reduced DIO1 activity in the liver and decreased T3 levels (Kühn et al., 1984). The expression of deiodinases is also developmentally regulated. DIO2 expression in the brain increases during embryonic development, particularly in the choroid plexus (Gereben et al., 2004). On the other hand, expression of DIO3 in the liver and kidney declines during the final stage of embryogenesis, reflecting the increased demand for T3 in the last days before hatching (Darras et al., 2006).

Despite many studies showing deiodinase mRNA abundance in individual tissues during embryogenesis and just after hatching, there is no comprehensive analysis of the expression level of deiodinases in the tissues of mature laying hens. Therefore, the goal of the present study was to analyse the expression patterns of three deiodinases, DIO1, DIO2, and DIO3, in adult hens across a variety of tissues, with a special focus on individual compartments of the reproductive tract.

Material and methods

Animals

The experiment was conducted using eight 96-week-old ‘Bovans’ commercial laying hens that were kept in individual cages with continuous access to water and feed. The hens were maintained under standard poultry management practices with a light schedule of 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness. Egg production was recorded daily for three weeks prior to the experiment to predict the time of ovulation and confirm stable and consistent reproductive output in all birds. The hens were decapitated 22 hours before ovulation. Tissues were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at −80°C until isolation. The experimental procedures were approved by the First Local Animal Ethics Committee in Krakow, Poland (234/2022).

Gene expression analysis

RNA was isolated from the following tissues: hypothalamus, pituitary gland, thyroid gland, lungs, heart, stomach, small intestine, spleen, pancreas, skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, skin, ovary: prehierarchical (small [SWF] and large white follicles [LWF]), theca and the granulosa layer from the preovulatory (F3, F2, and F1) follicles, and oviductal segments (infundibulum, magnum, isthmus, shell gland, and vagina). To isolate the RNA, we followed the manufacturer's directions using the ReliaPrep RNA Tissue Miniprep System (Promega, USA). The isolated RNA’s concentration and purity were determined using a Thermo Scientific NanoDrop Lite spectrophotometer (Madison, WI, USA). The wavelength ratio between OD260 and OD280 for each sample ranged from 1.9 to 2.0. Furthermore, the quality of isolated RNA was determined through electrophoretic separation.

Under the manufacturer’s recommendations, 1 µg of total RNA with no signs of degradation was transcribed using a RevertAid RT Transcription Kit (ThermoScientific, USA) in a Mastercycler personal thermocycler (Eppendorf, Germany). First-strand cDNA was synthesised for 5 min at 25°C, followed by 60 min at 42°C. The reaction was terminated by heating at 70°C for 5 min. The reverse transcription samples were diluted 10 times with RNase-free water for real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR).

The RT-qPCR was performed in 96-well plates in a StepOne Plus thermocycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Each reaction was conducted in duplicate with a total volume of 10 μl. One μl of 10-fold diluted cDNA was added to 9 μl of the reaction mix, which consisted of 5 μl of SensiFAST Hi-ROX Kit (Meridian Biosciences Inc., Cincinnati, Ohio, USA), 3.5 μl of nuclease-free water, and 0.5 μl of TaqMan Gene Expression Assay. The thermal cycling conditions were set as follows: 2 min at 50°C, 10 min at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles consisting of 15 s of denaturation at 95°C and 1 min of annealing at 60°C. No template controls, consisting of nuclease-free water, were included on each plate to check for reagent contamination. The TaqMan Gene Expression Assays were made and provided by Applied Biosystems (Table 1). Each gene’s amplification efficiency was determined by creating a standard curve with six 5-fold dilutions of template cDNA (pool of all samples) and reaching 90–105 %. The reference genes were chosen using NormFinder and GeNorm software. Both programs identified the hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT) gene as the most stable among 60S ribosomal protein L13, succinate dehydrogenase complex, subunit A, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, and vimentin. To calculate the normalised relative quantity (NFQ) of the tested genes, the Pfaffl method (Pfaffl, 2001) modified by Hellemans et al. (2008) was employed. Expressions were counted in relation to the thyroid gland as the major organ involved in TH synthesis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using the Sigma Plot version 12.3. A univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Data that did not meet the assumptions of normal distribution (Shapiro-Wilk test) were transformed in a suitable manner to ensure accurate analysis. Statistical differences were considered significant if P < 0.05. The values are presented as means ± SEM. The figures were generated using Grapher 19, developed by Golden Software Inc.

Deiodinase type 1 (DIO1) mRNA expression

The levels of DIO1 expression were 173-fold, 12-fold, and 8-fold higher in the liver, small intestine, and kidney, respectively, in comparison with the level in the thyroid gland (P < 0.05, Fig. 1A, B). On the other hand, DIO1 mRNA levels were 6-fold lower in the heart, spleen, and pancreas (P < 0.05, Fig. 1A). The lowest DIO1 expression was in the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and skeletal muscles, which were 76-fold, 39-fold, and 12-fold lower, respectively, compared to the thyroid gland (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D). Similar levels of DIO1 expression were found in the shell gland (Fig. 1D), lungs (Fig. 1B), and adipose tissue and skin (Fig. 1B). In all ovarian follicles (Fig. 1C) and oviductal segments (Fig. 1D), the levels of DIO1 transcripts were lower in comparison to the thyroid gland (Fig. 1). The DIO1 mRNA levels in SWF and LWF follicles were on average 42-fold lower, and in the granulosa layer and theca layer of F3-F1 follicles, on average 108-fold lower (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C). In the infundibulum, magnum, isthmus, and vagina, except for the shell gland, the levels of DIO1 transcripts were 22-fold, 48-fold, 10-fold, and 3-fold lower, respectively (P < 0.05, Fig. 1D).

Deiodinase type 2 (DIO2) mRNA expression

Compared to the DIO2 expression level in the thyroid gland, the highest expression levels of DIO2 were in the liver (66-fold, P < 0.05, Fig. 2A) and pituitary gland (7.6-fold, P < 0.05, Fig. 2B). DIO2 expression was lower in all ovarian compartments. The lowest expression level was in the F1 granulosa layer (15-fold, P < 0.05, Fig. 2C), while in the other ovarian compartments the expression levels were 3–6-fold lower (P < 0.05, Fig. 2C). Lower levels of DIO2 transcripts were in the magnum, isthmus, and vagina (averaging 4-fold, P < 0.05, Fig. 2D). The DIO2 expression in the shell gland (Fig. 2D) was comparable to that in the thyroid gland, while in the infundibulum it was 2.8-fold higher (P < 0.05, Fig. 2D).

Deiodinase type 3 (DIO3) mRNA expression

DIO3 expression in the liver was 3-fold higher than in the thyroid gland. In contrast, levels of DIO3 in the heart (Fig. 3A), hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and stomach (Fig. 3B) were 9-fold, 13-fold, 10-fold, and 12-fold lower (P < 0.05), respectively. In the ovary, the expression levels of DIO3 were significantly higher in all examined follicles (P < 0.05). In SWF and LWF prehierarchical follicles, the DIO3 expression levels were 6-fold and 4.5-fold higher, respectively, in comparison to the thyroid gland (P < 0.05, Fig. 3C). In the theca layer of the preovulatory follicles, the expression of DIO3 was on average 6-fold higher (P < 0.05, Fig. 3C). The granulosa layers of these follicles exhibited remarkably high DIO3 expression with 99-fold in F3, 230-fold in F2, and 165-fold in F1 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3C). DIO3 expression in the oviduct was significantly lower (P < 0.05) in the magnum (3.4-fold), isthmus (5.4-fold), and shell gland (3.4-fold) (Fig. 3D).

Discussion

Deiodinases, including DIO1, DIO2, and DIO3, play a crucial role in TH metabolism in chickens. Most studies to date have focused on the expression of deiodinases during the embryonic stage or their expression in specific tissues (Van der Geyten et al., 2002; Verhoelst et al., 2004, 2005). Our research describes the comprehensive expression of deiodinases in adult chicken tissues and, for the first time, identifies the presence of deiodinases in different parts of the ovary and oviduct.

DIO1 is expressed in the liver and kidney, but no expression has been found in the brain, thyroid, muscle, or skin in embryonic tissues (Van der Geyten et al., 2002). Our study confirmed the presence of DIO1 in all tissues of adult chickens, including those in which it was absent during embryonic development. This inconsistency may arise from the use of a high-precision RT-qPCR method, whereas Van der Geyten et al. (2002) employed a relatively low-sensitivity Northern blot technique. The highest expression of DIO1 was observed in the liver, kidney, and small intestine of adult birds, which aligns with the studies of chicken embryos. The liver significantly contributes to the circulating pool of T3 through DIO1-mediated conversion of T4, thereby ensuring the availability of T3 for peripheral tissues. THs also play an essential role in the kidneys in terms of sodium reabsorption, glomerular filtration rate, regulation of the renin-angiotensin system, tubular function, and electrolyte balance (Dousdampanis et al., 2014). High expression of DIO1 indirectly modulates these processes by adjusting the availability of active T3 in renal tissue.

Given its extensive surface area and metabolic activity, the intestine may represent a significant source of THs comparable to the liver. In chickens, DIO1 has been shown to exhibit high activity in the intestine during the perinatal period. This suggests that locally generated T3 plays a critical role in the differentiation and maturation of intestinal epithelial cells at this stage of development (Suvarna et al., 1993). Moreover, it cannot be ruled out that high DIO1 activity in the small intestine is related to the processes of absorption and metabolism of iodine necessary for the synthesis of THs (Maia et al., 2011; Bruinstroop et al., 2023). Furthermore, our observation of sustained high DIO1 expression in the adult intestine implies that intestinal T3 production remains functionally relevant beyond early life, potentially supporting tissue maintenance and homeostasis. The intestinal epithelium is one of the most rapidly renewing tissues in both humans and animals. TH is known to regulate multiple aspects of intestinal physiology, including epithelial cell proliferation, differentiation, and barrier integrity (Giolito and Plateroti, 2022). These processes are crucial for normal digestive function and for the capacity of the gut to recover from injury. While the role of TH in intestinal regeneration is well documented in mammals, data on their function in avian species remain limited, underscoring the need for further investigation in this area.

The brain is the only organ that expresses DIO2 in the developing chicken embryo (Van der Geyten et al., 2002). However, in adult chickens, we demonstrated the presence of this deiodinase mRNA in all examined tissues. The highest expression of DIO2 was detected in the liver, aligning with previous studies (Kowalik et al., 2023; Katarzyńska-Banasik et al., 2024) but differing from that seen in mammals (Bruinstroop et al., 2023), where its expression has not been demonstrated. The presence of DIO2 in the chicken liver facilitates local production of T3, which is crucial for regulating specific metabolic processes in this organ, particularly lipid metabolism that is essential for egg yolk formation (Ren et al., 2017; Emami et al., 2021). DIO2 in the hypothalamus and pituitary gland plays a role in maintaining homeostasis by regulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis. The relatively high level of DIO2 expression in the pituitary gland may represent one such mechanism, as local T3 production by DIO2 inhibits the secretion of thyroid-stimulating hormone (Mohácsik et al., 2016). On the other hand, in the hypothalamus, DIO2 expression, along with the presence of TH transport proteins (organic anion transporting polypeptide 1C1 for T4, monocarboxylate transporter 8 for T3), enables DIO2 to convert the uptake of T4 to T3, which then diffuses to thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) neurons in the paraventricular nucleus, inhibiting TRH gene transcription (Nakao et al., 2008; Williams and Bassett, 2011).

Relatively high expression of DIO2 was also observed in oviductal segments, including the infundibulum and the shell gland, with expression levels in the shell gland comparable to those observed in the thyroid gland. Since T3 regulates smooth muscle contraction, it is crucial for the proper capture of the ovulatory oocyte by the infundibulum (Apperson et al., 2017). Moreover, the relatively high expression of DIO2 in the shell gland results from the demand for T3 availability in the eggshell production process. This process utilises energy from ATP for active calcium transport from the blood. T3, as the main regulator of cellular metabolism, is responsible for increasing mitochondrial activity and stimulating ATP production. Consequently, the epithelial cells of the shell gland can effectively transport Ca2+ ions against their concentration gradient (Kowalik and Sechman, 2020; Muhammad et al., 2021). T3 also contributes to the regulation of the expression of calcium transport proteins (plasmalemmal Ca²⁺-ATPase and sodium-calcium exchanger), which are located in the cell membranes of the shell gland cells (Pizzolante et al., 2009; Kowalik and Sechman, 2020; Muhammad et al., 2021). In other sections of the oviduct, lower DIO2 expression suggests a minor demand for T3 in the processes occurring in these parts.

DIO3 is considered ubiquitous, with its expression demonstrated in all chicken embryonic tissues. Our study also confirmed its widespread expression in numerous adult organs and tissues. In the pancreas and small intestine, expression levels were comparable to those in the thyroid gland. In the liver, DIO3 expression was 3-fold higher compared to the thyroid gland. During the final weeks of embryonic development in chickens, a significant decline in hepatic DIO3 expression was observed (Van der Geyten et al., 2002), which is consistent with our findings. The most outstanding result of our investigation is that a high DIO3 expression was also found in the ovary of the adult laying chicken, aligning with the results Van der Geyten et al. (2002) obtained from embryos. However, our analysis did not focus on the entire ovary, but rather on the specific ovarian compartments. The structure exhibiting extremely high DIO3 expression was the granulosa layer of preovulatory follicles. As the follicle matured, DIO3 expression increased, reaching its peak in the F1 follicle. The markedly elevated expression of DIO3 in the granulosa layer of preovulatory follicles may suggest a regulatory role in controlling local TH availability during follicular maturation. Sechman et al. (2013) demonstrated that induction of a hyperthyroid state through the administration of T3 over a short duration leads to a decrease in levels of luteinizing hormone, estradiol, and progesterone, a reduction in ovarian weight, the induction of atresia in preovulatory follicles, and ultimately the cessation of egg laying. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that T3 and 3,5-T2 influence cAMP synthesis and the expression of key steroidogenic genes, including steroidogenic acute regulatory protein, cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1, and 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase/Δ5-4 isomerase, in the granulosa cells (GC) of chicken follicles. This suggests that the ovarian follicles of the laying hen are an essential target tissue for the action of the TH (Sechman et al., 2011). Since DIO3 inactivates T3 and T4, its elevated expression may serve to precisely regulate local THs synthesis and activity, preventing premature GC differentiation or excessive metabolic stimulation by restricting THs signalling. This tightly controlled local environment may be essential for proper follicle development, ovulation timing, and oocyte quality. It cannot be excluded that the high expression of DIO3 in the wall of ovarian follicles may protect the follicles against the overaccumulation of THs, thereby preventing follicular atresia and maintaining reproductive performance.

In summary, deiodinase expression in adult chickens is highly tissue-specific, reflecting the diverse and critical roles of THs in metabolic regulation, reproduction, growth, and homeostasis across multiple organ systems. The liver exhibits high levels of DIO1 and DIO2, along with moderate DIO3 expression, indicating its central role in converting T4 to the active form of T3 and regulating systemic THs availability. The high abundance of deiodinases in the liver is also related to the demand for iodothyronine in this organ, indicating its key role in lipid metabolism and, consequently, in the processes related to vitellogenesis and egg production. The kidneys also express high DIO1, supporting electrolyte balance and renal function. The intestine exhibits strong DIO1 and DIO3 expression, indicating ongoing T3 production for epithelial renewal and digestive health. In the brain, DIO2 displays high expression in the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, regulating the thyroid axis via local T3 feedback. The oviduct, particularly the infundibulum and shell gland, shows high DIO2 expression, linking T3 to smooth muscle contraction and eggshell formation. The ovary, specifically the granulosa layer of preovulatory follicles, exhibits extremely high DIO3 expression, supporting the hypothesis that it plays an essential role in THs availability for developing ovarian follicles and protecting oocytes against an excess of metabolically active T3. The presence of all deiodinases in the ovarian tissues suggests that the appropriate concentrations of THs are pivotal for the regulation of follicular development and their endocrine function.
