Associations of the Charlson comorbidity index with asthma and mortality (NHANES 2007–2018): a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jia Zhang, Huan Chen, Jin Liu, Yuqian Chen, Yuanjie Qiu, Huizhong Hu, Yihan Meng, Qianqian Zhang, Manxiang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with asthma often suffer from multiple comorbid conditions, which can exacerbate disease severity and elevate the risk of mortality. This study aims to investigate the relationship between the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and asthma, as well as their combined impact on mortality.MethodsThis is an observational cross-sectional study based on the NHANES database conducted from 2007 to 2018. The study included a total of 26,002 adult participants. Logistic regression was performed to assess the relationship between the CCI and asthma. Survival analysis, including Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox regression, was conducted to investigate the combined effect of the CCI and asthma on all-cause mortality. Subgroup analyses were performed to confirm the reliability of the outcomes.ResultsAfter adjusting for sex, age, race, body mass index, and other relevant covariates, our analysis showed an association between CCI and asthma (OR, 1.120; 95% CI, 1.059–1.186). Moreover, a higher CCI score was closely associated with increased mortality risk (HR, 1.143; 95% CI, 1.109–1.179) after adjustment of confounding factors. The risk of mortality was substantially higher for individuals with both asthma and a CCI score of ≥ 2 compared to those with no asthma and a CCI score of 0 (HR, 1.853; 95% CI, 1.440–2.383). Subgroup analyses further confirmed the trend that higher CCI levels are associated with poorer survival outcomes across multiple subgroups.ConclusionThe study indicates an association between the CCI and the risk of asthma, as well as their combined impact on elevated all-cause mortality. Since a high CCI score is associated with an increased risk of asthma and mortality, further studies are warranted to explore the causal relationship between these factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01129-1.Keywords: CCI, Comorbidity, Asthma, Mortality, NHANES

Introduction

Asthma is a prevalent chronic respiratory disease characterized by airway remodeling and cellular inflammation, especially type 2 inflammation [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of asthma involves the activation of the innate and adaptive immune systems, such as the activation of neutrophils, macrophages, and eosinophils, and is manifested by symptoms of bronchial obstruction, bronchial hyperreactivity, mucus overproduction, and airway remodeling [3–5]. It impacts 300 million patients worldwide and contributed to 21.6 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) globally in 2019 [6, 7]. Cases of asthma in adults often exhibit higher rates of morbidity and mortality, especially in the aging population [8]. The presence of comorbidities in patients with asthma exacerbates disease severity and raises healthcare costs [9, 10].

Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) is a widely acknowledged assessment instrument to systematically evaluate the burden of comorbid conditions in patients [11]. It was originally developed by Charlson to predict 1-year mortality in 1984 [12]. The CCI score is derived from the quantity and severity of various chronic conditions, such as chronic pulmonary disease, cancer, congestive heart failure, diabetes with chronic complications, and renal or liver disorders, and can be used to evaluate the health burden of individuals [13, 14].

Previous studies have shown that the onset and progression of asthma are associated with several chronic diseases encapsulated within the CCI score [15–17].

Asthma is associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [15, 18]. Specifically, patients with asthma, whether early- or late-onset, have an increased risk of heart failure, with HRs of 2.2 and 1.5, respectively [18, 19]. Moreover, individuals with asthma face an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality compared to those without asthma [20].

Several studies have found significant correlations between asthma and diabetes, with insulin resistance being confirmed as a contributing element to the onset of asthma [16, 21]. Research indicates that patients with asthma and elevated body mass index (BMI) or obesity require more medication and healthcare use and experience poorer symptom control, indicating that metabolic dysfunction is a key factor exacerbating the severity of asthma [21]. Additionally, studies confirm the concept that the lungs are susceptible to diabetic damage, with individuals with diabetes exhibiting notably diminished lung function [22]. Furthermore, chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection also accelerates lung function decline and independently increases asthma severity in patients [23, 24].

The pathogenesis of asthma involves immune system imbalance, which potentially increases the risk of developing cancer later on [17, 25]. A study suggests that the hospitalized asthma-related cancer standardized incidence ratio is 1.36 [17]. Asthma is associated with an increased risk of lung cancer and can trigger pulmonary metastases in breast cancer patients [26, 27]. Studies have also confirmed that asthma increases the risk of esophageal cancer, colon cancer, rectal cancer, liver cancer, pancreatic cancer, and prostatic cancer [17, 28, 29].

Since numerous chronic diseases encapsulated within the CCI score are associated with elevated risk of asthma and can exacerbate the prognosis for patients with asthma, it is important to examine the relationship between the CCI score and asthma, as well as the combined effect of the CCI score and asthma on all-cause mortality. We hypothesized that a higher CCI score is associated with an increased asthma prevalence and mortality risk.

Materials and methods

Data source

We utilized data collected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which was conducted between 2007 and 2018. The protocols for NHANES received approval from the Institutional Review Boards of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Center for Health Statistics. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Survival analyses were carried out utilizing data from the US National Death Index Archive updated to 31 December 2019 to record the survival status and follow-up time of participants.

Participants

A total of 59,842 subjects were enrolled in 6 consecutive NHANES survey cycles from 2007 to 2018. After excluding 25,072 participants for being aged < 20 years, 31 participants for missing asthma data, 116 participants for missing survival data, 3,775 participants for missing poverty income ratio (PIR) data, 1,441 participants for missing BMI data, 16 participants for missing cigarette smoking status data, 1,623 participants for missing total cholesterol (TC) data, and 1,766 participants for missing hypertension data, the final sample comprised 26,002 participants. Figure S1 depicts the detailed participant screening procedure. The NHANES dataset is matched with the US National Death Index Archive through the code “Respondent sequence number”.

Asthma definition

No asthma was defined as answering “No” to the NHANES question MCQ010, “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have asthma?”. Those who replied “Yes” were categorized as having asthma.

Assessment of CCI

As shown in Table S1, the CCI score encompasses several components, such as chronic pulmonary disease (MCQ160K) for 1 score, heart failure (MCQ160B) for 1 score, diabetes (DIQ010) for 1 score, diabetic retinopathy (DIQ080) for 2 scores, and cancer (MCQ230A-D) for 2 scores. No disease was defined by answering “No” to questions such as “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have this illness?” or having a negative laboratory test result. Those who answered “Yes” or had a positive laboratory test result were classified as having the disease. Individuals who did not provide information about a specific disease were classified as healthy.

Covariates

The covariates of the study included age, sex, race and ethnicity, PIR, BMI, cigarette smoking status, hypertension, and TC. Race/ethnicity was divided into five categories, including Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black and other Race. Smoking status was categorized into two groups: never smoking or ever smoking, based on whether the participant had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. The ever-smoking group was then subdivided into current or former smoking groups depending on their smoking status at the time of the interview. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. The NHANES question codes used to define the covariates are as follows: age: RIDAGEYR; sex: RIAGENDR; race and ethnicity: RIDRETH1; PIR: INDFMPIR; BMI: BMXBMI; cigarette smoking status: SMQ020 and SMQ040; hypertension: BPXSY and BPXDI; TC: LBXTC.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (version 25.0) and R (version 4.2.1). Sampling weights were utilized in all analyses (chi-square tests, independent t-tests, analyses of variance, logistic regression and Cox regression). Continuous variables were reported as the mean (± SE), and categorical variables were displayed as frequencies with weighted percentages. We used chi-square tests, independent t-tests, and analyses of variance to compare the characteristics of participants with or without asthma. Logistic regression was used to determine the association between CCI and asthma. Collinearity analysis was performed to ensure that the variance inflation factor was less than 5.00. After adjusting for covariates, the odds ratio (OR) and the 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. A restricted cubic spline (RCS) was applied to identify potential non-linear relationships with 4 knots (20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th).

The survival status of participants was determined by using data from the US National Death Index Archive, updated to December 31, 2019. The median follow-up duration for the overall participants is 80.00 (interquartile range 46.00–118.00) months. We estimated the survival curves of the participants using a Kaplan–Meier analysis. For the adjusted survival analysis of the CCI score, the Cox proportional hazard model was applied after its proportional hazard assumption was checked. We constructed 3 models using Cox regression (model 1 was not adjusted for covariates; model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, and BMI; model 3 was adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, PIR, cigarette smoking status, hypertension, and TC) and calculated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. Subgroup analyses were performed to determine model stability. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance for all tests conducted.

Descriptive results

Our sample consisted of 26,002 participants, the characteristics of whom are shown in Table 1. Among them, 3,784 were diagnosed with asthma, with females accounting for 59.19% of the asthma group (n = 2,208). Compared to participants without asthma, those with asthma tended to have a higher BMI and a lower PIR index. Self-reported smoking status revealed that people with asthma were more likely to be current smokers. There were 18.73% current smokers among the participants without asthma, whereas 22.98% of the participants with asthma were current smokers. Individuals with asthma also exhibited a higher CCI score of 0.90, compared to 0.60 for those without asthma. However, the rates of all-cause mortality and CVD mortality showed no significant difference between the two groups.

The association of CCI with asthma

To examine the association between the CCI score and asthma, we employed logistic regression analysis. The association between covariates and asthma was assessed using logistic regression, as detailed in Table S2. When considering the CCI score as a continuous variable and adjusting for all covariates, the adjusted OR for asthma was calculated at 1.120 (95% CI, 1.059–1.186). The individuals were then divided into three groups according to the CCI definition and tertile of CCI score: T1 (score = 0), T2 (score = 1), and T3 (score ≥ 2). Initial analysis without adjustments showed that individuals in the T2 and T3 categories had significantly greater odds of asthma compared to the T1 category. This trend remained consistent even after adjusting for sex, age, race and ethnicity and BMI in Model 1. In Model 2, after accounting for all covariates, the results revealed that the odds of asthma still increased substantially at T2 and T3 when compared with T1, with ORs of 1.873 (95% CI, 1.638–2.142) and 2.140 (95% CI, 1.861–2.462), respectively (Table 2).

Survival analysis

A total of 1,987 (6.42%) participants without asthma and 314 (5.84%) participants with asthma had died by the end of the follow-up period. The median follow-up duration was 77.00 (interquartile range 45.00–115.00) months for participants with asthma and 80.00 (interquartile range 47.00–118.00) months for those without.

After adjusting for all covariates, the RCS analysis showed a non-linear, positive association between CCI and mortality, with a positive association existing between the CCI score and mortality when the CCI score exceeded 1.00 (P for overall < 0.001, P for non-linear < 0.001) (Fig. 1B).

Kaplan–Meier analysis found a significant correlation between higher CCI score and increased all-cause mortality (P < 0.001 [log-rank]) (Fig. 2 A). The participants were then further divided into four groups: no asthma with CCI < 2, asthma with CCI < 2, no asthma with CCI ≥ 2, and asthma with CCI ≥ 2. Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis revealed a striking difference: individuals without asthma and CCI < 2, as well as those with asthma and CCI < 2, exhibited significantly improved survival rates compared to those without asthma and CCI ≥ 2 and those with asthma and CCI ≥ 2 (Fig. 2B). This suggests that the higher CCI score is associated with increased all-cause mortality rates, irrespective of whether the individuals had asthma.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves for all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. Kaplan–Meier estimates for all-cause mortality across CCI tertiles (A) and CCI/asthma group (B), and CVD mortality stratified by CCI tertiles (C) and CCI/asthma group (D)

As for the association between CCI score and CVD mortality, Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed a significant correlation between higher CCI score and increased CVD mortality (P < 0.001 [log-rank]) (Fig. 2 C). Survival curve analysis also showed that individuals without asthma and with CCI ≥ 2, as well as those with asthma and CCI ≥ 2, had increased cardiovascular mortality rates when compared with individuals without asthma and with CCI < 2 and those with asthma and CCI < 2 (Fig. 2D).

For the adjusted survival analysis (Cox’s proportional hazards model), participants in the T2 and T3 tertiles showed a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to those in the T1 tertile. After taking sex, age, race/ethnicity, and BMI into account in Model 1 and adjusting for all covariates in Model 2, the survival analysis showed consistent results with the crude model, with HRs of 1.395 (95% CI, 1.199–1.624) and 1.809 (95% CI, 1.586–2.062), respectively (Table 3).

The research participants were subsequently divided into six categories based on whether they had asthma and their CCI score. The categories included no asthma with T1 tertile (CCI score = 0), no asthma with T2 tertile (CCI score = 1), no asthma with T3 tertile (CCI score ≥ 2), asthma individuals with T1 tertile (CCI score = 0), asthma individuals with T2 tertile (CCI score = 1), and asthma individuals with T3 tertile (CCI score ≥ 2). As shown in Table 3, the analysis revealed a notable increase in all-cause mortality across the higher CCI tertiles for both individuals with and without asthma. Compared to those without asthma and in the T1 tertile, the HRs for the other 5 groups were 1.391 (95% CI, 1.175–1.646), 1.808 (95% CI, 1.580–2.070), 1.049 (95% CI, 0.794–1.385), 1.459 (95% CI, 1.134–1.877), and 1.853 (95% CI, 1.440–2.383).

Subgroup analysis

We further examined the association between CCI score and mortality in different subgroups. The trend of the higher CCI score associated with poorer survival outcomes remained consistent when the data were stratified by sex, age (≥ 40), race (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black and Other Races), BMI, PIR (< 3.50), smoking status, and history of hypertension. Discussion

This research investigates the relationship between CCI score and asthma risk. It also examines the association of the CCI score with all-cause and CVD mortality in individuals with and without asthma. The results indicate a positive association between CCI score and the prevalence of asthma. Additionally, individuals with a higher CCI score are associated with an increased risk of all-cause and CVD mortality, and the combined impact of asthma and a higher CCI score increases the risk of mortality. These findings highlight the need to consider the cumulative burden of chronic diseases when evaluating the risks and potential outcomes of the asthma.

CCI is a comorbidity assessment tool proven to predict long-term mortality with high reliability, sensitivity, and predictive validity [30]. The CCI score incorporates many of the chronic conditions that have been shown to influence asthma risk, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer [17, 31–33]. These chronic diseases are not only associated with asthma but can also contribute to elevated mortality rates among affected individuals [32, 34].

Our study showed that individuals with a higher CCI score are at a higher risk of asthma, and this relationship persists after adjusting for multiple factors. This is consistent with previous studies of asthma-related complications in other populations [35–37]. This possibly relates to the similar pathogenesis of these chronic diseases with asthma and their systemic impact on multiple bodily functions. Asthma is characterized by elevated levels of serum immunoglobulin E, eosinophils, neutrophils, and cytokines [38–41]. Studies have shown that cancers with high mutational burdens share similar inflammatory networks, as tumor cells produce various cytokines and chemokines that attract leukocytes [42, 43]. Evidence also suggests a potential relationship between asthma and CVD, pointing to overlapping pathogenic mechanisms. For instance, the lungs of patients with asthma, as well as the injured hearts and vessels of CVD patients, are associated with elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and immunoglobulin E [44]. Furthermore, both asthma and atherosclerosis involve the accumulation of inflammatory cells, such as macrophages, eosinophils, and neutrophils, in the airways and the arterial walls, respectively [45, 46]. Research has also shown that chronic airway inflammation in asthma could trigger systemic vascular exposure and elevate the risk of developing CVD [47].

Our study further indicated that both asthma and no-asthma participants with a higher CCI score had a higher risk of all-cause mortality. This is consistent with an Italian population-based study [48]. Another previous study has also shown that the mortality of asthma is affected by the presence of comorbid conditions, including malignant neoplasms of the respiratory organs and cardiovascular diseases [49]. Asthma and its associated comorbidities can affect each other, making their treatment more challenging and increasing the risk of other diseases developing [50]. For example, β-blockers, which are used to treat cardiovascular disease, are avoided in cases of asthma, as they can trigger acute bronchoconstriction [51]. Furthermore, high doses of inhaled corticosteroids can increase the risk of developing diabetes [52].

These remarkable results suggest that healthcare providers should focus on the simultaneous management of asthma and accompanying chronic diseases when treating patients with asthma and a high CCI score.

Our study has several limitations that need to be taken into account. Notably, causal inferences cannot be established, as the NHANES database employs an observational cross-sectional approach rather than a prospective study design. We used data from the 2007–2018 NHANES, and the lack of other cycles may affect generalizability. Moreover, the case definitions of asthma and comorbidities relied on self-reported physician diagnoses in questionnaires rather than clinical confirmation, which potentially impacted the precision of case diagnoses. The lack of adjustment for medication use may lead to certain biases in the results. Furthermore, these questionnaire results are affected by recall bias of the participants, which potentially leads to information bias. Additionally, the absence of data for several diseases with a CCI score in NHANES means that these comorbidities could not be verified properly, potentially affecting the accuracy of the results. We didn’t analyze the association between the chronic diseases covered by the CCI score separately due to the missing data of these chronic diseases in the NHANES database, this may affect the completeness of the study. Given the limitations of our study, further research across varied demographics is needed to ensure validation, and a prospective study is warranted to confirm causality. The intervention trials targeting comorbidities and the studies exploring the mechanisms linking CCI and asthma are also vital in the future research.

Nonetheless, this research has several notable advantages. The results are representative and reliable since they are derived from the NHANES database—a comprehensive, demographic survey of the U.S. population. To account for potential confounding variables, regression models were carefully adjusted, and subgroup analyses were conducted to verify the stability of the results. This research addresses a gap in the existing study by examining the relationship between CCI, asthma, and all-cause mortality. Previous studies primarily focused on the isolated associations between individual comorbidities and asthma outcomes, lacking an understanding of how the integrated score of various chronic diseases interacts with asthma to influence patient prognosis. Our work bridges this knowledge gap by using CCI as a composite measure for asthma outcomes. It helps us to understand the disease burden of various chronic diseases in patients with asthma and highlights the clinical implications of developing better management strategies for patients with asthma and a higher CCI score.

Conclusions

Our study found that there’s a positive association between CCI score and the prevalence of asthma. Furthermore, individuals with asthma and a higher CCI score face an increased risk of all-cause and CVD mortality. It highlights the necessity of the early diagnosis and treatment of comorbidities in patients with a high CCI score in order to minimize the risk of mortality. Longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm causality, as the NHANES database is an observational approach, and further research across varied demographics is needed to ensure validation.
