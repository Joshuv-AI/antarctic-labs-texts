Weak enforcement of Brazil's E-cigarette ban and the expansion of a dangerous illicit market
Authors: Vitoria Borges Spinola, Lucas Porto Santos, Hamilton Roschel, Bruno Gualano, Megan E Roberts
Published: 2025

Since 2009, the Brazilian Health Surveillance Agency has banned e-cigarette sales in the country. However, despite this ongoing prohibition, a recent nationally representative household survey1 of 51,575 adults aged 18–64 found a 600% relative increase in past-30-day e-cigarette use between 2018 and 2023, illustrating the inefficacy of the existing policy. Additional data found nearly one-quarter of Brazilian adolescents have experimented with e-cigarettes.2 Among adults, around 3.1 million exclusively use e-cigarettes, while approximately 8 million use both e-cigarettes and another tobacco product.3

This widespread use and availability of e-cigarettes is the result of a thriving illicit market that circumvents regulatory controls, flooding the market with unauthorized—and potentially even more hazardous– e-cigarettes than those found in regulated environments.4 Many are “knock-off” devices that imitate well-known brands but lack basic safety standards, traceable origins, or health warnings. A government-backed investigation uncovered 1822 illicit social media advertisements or accounts related to e-cigarettes, underscoring the easy online access to banned products.5 Strikingly, e-cigarettes are also frequently sold at retail points of sale, often quite openly (Fig. 1).

Concerns about vaping-related health risks in Brazil are mounting. A recent study found disposable vapes in Brazil contained metal concentrations far above acceptable levels for inhaled materials.6 Currently, Brazil has no system in place to monitor the incidence of health problems related to e-cigarette use. However, reports of severe respiratory symptoms, hospitalizations, and deaths linked to vaping have been widely covered in the media. In some aspects, descriptions of severe respiratory impairment echo the 2019 EVALI outbreak in the U.S. Additionally, and consistent with the literature on conventional cigarettes, exposure time plays a central role in the development of health-related outcomes.7

It is crucial that Brazil adopts measures to mitigate the risks associated with e-cigarettes. This includes thoroughly documenting prevalence of use, explicitly distinguishing lifetime use, past-30-day use, and co-use with combustible tobacco products, as well as the nature and number of vaping-related health cases and the extent of illicit trade. Precise and unbiased data from representative surveys among both adults and adolescents, ideally conducted by independent institutions, will allow regulatory decisions based on solid evidence.

Apart from increased monitoring, more effective public policies and procedures should be considered, including greater sales restrictions through online and social media platforms, as well as stronger enforcement at brick-and-mortar stores (i.e., regular checks by an enforcement agency with fines and penalties for violations). Brazil has made progress in confiscating illegal e-cigarettes; for example, in 2024, the Federal Revenue Service confiscated nearly 2 million units valued at approximately R$ 1.1 billion, a 57% increase compared to 2023. However, continued efforts are needed to restrict the supply chain flow of illegal products across its borders.

Another potential step is to revisit the debate between regulation and total prohibition, with consideration for nuanced and alternative approaches. Some countries have opted for regulation as part of a harm-reduction strategy, with the idea that a legal market will provide some level of product supervision and consumer protection. However, evidence suggests that challenges with illicit supply remain even in regulated markets such as seen in the USA.8

This discussion must be based on scientific evidence and experience of other nations that have adopted different regulatory approaches. In Europe, restrictions on advertising and the implementation of health warnings and child-resistant packaging have proven somewhat effective in reducing youth e-cigarette initiation.9 In Canada and the US, increasing prices and restricting use in public spaces have been associated with lower prevalence and reduced dual use.10 Smoke- and vape-free policies have also played a role in shifting public perceptions and limiting secondhand exposure.9 These experiences indicate that well-designed regulatory frameworks can yield measurable public health gains, particularly when focused on youth protection and public education.

Brazil's globally recognized success in tobacco control was achieved by a comprehensive regulatory approach. Key elements included taxation, strict enforcement of smoke-free laws, and robust public education. Currently, Brazil seems to weigh total prohibition—marked by weak enforcement—against an undefined alternative. Importantly, a public health-centered, evidence-based approach aimed at a robust regulation must not be conflated with the tobacco industry's agenda, which often promotes denialist narratives in favor of looser regulations to protect its commercial interests.

E-cigarette use in Brazil is widespread, occurring within an illicit, unregulated, and rapidly expanding market. This reality underscores that prohibition alone has failed to contain use or illicit trade. More effective strategies must include tighter enforcement, supply chain oversight, evidence-based education initiatives, and an open, informed debate on regulatory options. This is not merely a choice between prohibition and regulation, but a reckoning with the risks of doing neither while knowingly allowing harmful products to proliferate.
