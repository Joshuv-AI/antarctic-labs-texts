Quantitative evaluation of muscle quality and quantity using an ultrasound radio frequency signal
Authors: Lan Zeng, Li Zhang, Jinhua Shao, Jianwen Luo, Qiong He, Yang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to investigate the utility of quantitative ultrasonographic (QUS) parameters derived from radio frequency (RF) signals for the assessment of muscle quality and quantity.MethodsA total of 200 hospitalized patients (102 men, 98 women; mean age: 59.11 ± 27.58 years) were prospectively enrolled in the study. Their skeletal muscle density (SMD) and skeletal muscle index (SMI) were calculated from computed tomography images, as the gold-standard methods of evaluating muscle quality and quantity, respectively. At the L3 vertebral level, the paraspinal muscle (PSM) thickness was measured by ultrasound, and the RF signals of the PSM were recorded, from which QUS parameters were obtained. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of SMD and SMI. Receiver operating characteristic curves were used to evaluate the predictive performance of combination of parameters for low SMD and low SMI.ResultsThe RF signals-based QUS parameters MusQBOX.NNM(C), MusQBOX.NNM(L), MusQBOX.Nα(C), and MusQBOX.NK(L) were identified as independent predictors of low SMD (all P < 0.05). The PSM thickness and the parameter MUSQBOX.NMI(L) were independent predictors of low SMI (both P < 0.05). The combination of MusQBOX.NNM(C), MusQBOX.NNM(L), MusQBOX.Nα(C), and MusQBOX.NK(L) had high diagnostic efficiency in the prediction of low SMD [area under the curve (AUC): 0.899 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.852–0.945), P < 0.001]. In addition, the combination of PSM thickness and MUSQBOX.NMI(L) showed moderate efficacy for the prediction of low SMI [AUC: 0.779 (95% CI 0.714–0.836), P < 0.001].ConclusionsThe RF signals-based QUS parameters derived from PSM are effective means of assessing both low SMD and low SMI and provide an alternative noninvasive method of evaluating muscle quality and quantity.

Background

Sarcopenia is a progressive systemic skeletal muscle disorder that is characterized by rapid reductions in muscle quantity and function [1]. It is associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including a higher risk of falls, functional decline, physical frailty, and mortality, according to the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) [2]. Moreover, sarcopenia is associated with a higher risk of hospitalization and substantial healthcare costs [3]. Indeed, patients who have sarcopenia when they are admitted to hospital incur more than five times the cost of hospitalization than those without [4]. Therefore, the early identification and assessment of sarcopenia can aid the determination of prognosis and facilitate timely interventions aimed at preventing negative outcomes and reducing the risk of hospitalization and the cost of healthcare.

The EWGSOP has identified low muscle quality and quantity to be key diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia [2]. Muscle quality is a relatively new concept that encompasses both microscopic and macroscopic aspects of muscle structure and composition, such as the level of intramuscular or intermuscular fat infiltration [5]. Indeed, the fat content of skeletal muscle has become important for the assessment of muscle quality in patients with sarcopenia.

Currently, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are considered to be the gold-standard methods for the evaluation of muscle quality and quantity [6]. Two key parameters can be derived from CT images: the skeletal muscle density (SMD), which reflects the fat content of the muscle (i.e., quality) and the skeletal muscle index (SMI), which is used to quantify muscle mass (i.e., quantity). Both parameters are significantly associated with the prognosis of various diseases [7–9]. However, the use of CT is limited by the associated exposure to ionizing radiation, which makes it unsuitable for repeated examinations and long-term monitoring. Conversely, although MRI does not involve ionizing radiation, it is expensive, lacks portability, and requires highly skilled operators, which limits its accessibility and use in primary care settings [6].

Ultrasonography has emerged as a promising alternative to CT and MRI. It is a portable and widely available imaging modality that does not involve ionizing radiation can be used for the dynamic assessment of soft tissues [10], making it particularly suitable for use in community and primary care environments. Parameters such as muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, fascicle length, pennation angle, and echo intensity (EI) can be measured using grayscale ultrasonography and used to assess both muscle quality and quantity [11]. However, EI, a commonly used indicator of muscle quality, is highly variable, owing to differences in system settings, such as the gain, focal depth, and vendor-specific post-processing algorithms [12, 13], which often lead to conclusions being drawn that seem to be inconsistent to the expected physiologic effects of exercise [14].

Radio frequency (RF) signals are raw echo signals that are captured by ultrasonographic transducers. These signals retain all the information regarding the wave’s propagation and its interaction with tissues [15, 16]. Quantitative ultrasonographic (QUS) parameters that are derived from RF signals are less affected by system settings and post-processing algorithms, and therefore are less variable [17]. They have potential for use in the assessment of both muscle quantity and quality. Previous studies have shown that RF signal-based QUS parameters exhibit high diagnostic performance for the evaluation of hepatic steatosis [area under the curve (AUC): 0.73–1.0] [18, 19].

A few previous studies have used RF signals to analyze skeletal muscle. For example, Lucas et al. demonstrated that a machine learning model based on ultrasound RF signals could be used to distinguish between the muscle contraction and relaxation states of healthy individuals. In our previous study, we found that QUS parameters for the forearm muscles correlated with muscle strength and quantity [20]. However, the paraspinal muscle (PSM) differs from the forearm muscles with respect to both pathophysiology and treatment strategies [21], and therefore it remains unclear whether RF signal-based QUS parameters could be used to evaluate the quality and quantity of the PSM. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to investigate the utility of RF signal-based QUS parameters for the evaluation of PSM quality and quantity.

Methods

Participants

A total of 200 patients at our hospital were prospectively enrolled between June 2023 and June 2024. The inclusion criteria were (1) age > 18 years, (2) previous or scheduled abdominal CT within 1 month at our hospital, (3) stable vital signs and intact consciousness, (4) an absence of spinal deformities, (5) an absence of neurological disorders affecting the paraspinal muscles, (6) an absence of lumbar skin defects, (7) an absence of allergies to ultrasonographic couplants, and (8) the provision of written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were (1) no abdominal CT examination performed within 1 month before or after ultrasonographic examination, (2) inability to perform ultrasonographic muscle imaging because of severe acoustic attenuation, (3) withdrawal from the study, and (4) incomplete clinical data. Ultrasonographic assessment

Ultrasonographic assessments of the PSM were performed using a 7.5-MHz linear-array transducer (EQTouch, Hisky, Wuxi, China). The reliability of this system for the measurement of muscle RF signal-based QUS parameters in healthy volunteers had been validated in our previous study [20]. The participants were instructed to lie prone on an examination bed in a fully relaxed state, to minimize voluntary muscle contraction. The L5 spinous process was located by palpation, and the probe was placed longitudinally to identify the L3 spinous process (Fig. 2a, c). RF data from the right PSM at the L3 level were collected in this sagittal plane. The probe was then rotated 90° and placed transversely at the L3 level to capture cross-sectional images of the right PSM (Fig. 2b, d). The PSM thickness was measured from the superficial border of the muscle to the L3 transverse process, and the RF data were collected. The raw channel RF data were processed through beamforming, signal analysis, and parameter extraction to acquire QUS parameters.

Using the MusQBOX software toolkit (Hisky), the multifidus muscle was manually segmented as the region of interest (ROI). Thereafter, from the transverse section, the QUS parameters MusQBOX.NNW(C), MusQBOX.NNM(C), MusQBOX.NLP(C), MusQBOX.Nα(C), MusQBOX.NK(C), and MusQBOX.NMI(C) ; and from the longitudinal section, the MusQBOX.NNW(L), MusQBOX.NNM(L), MusQBOX.NLP(L), MusQBOX.Nα(L), MusQBOX.NK(L), and MusQBOX.NMI(L) were calculated by automatic signal processing of RF data with MusQBOX software toolkit, respectively. These parameters respectively characterize the scattering and nonlinear features of the ultrasound echo signals. Specifically, MusQBOX.NNW is related to the strength of the echo envelope (scattering intensity), MusQBOX.NNM, Nα, NK reflects features of the scattering distribution, MusQBOX.NLP represents the nonlinear characteristics, and MusQBOX.NMI represents the overall ultrasonic signal intensity.

The ultrasonography and ROI segmentation were performed by a radiologist who had 10 years of experience of musculoskeletal ultrasonography and was blinded to the CT and clinical data.

CT-based identification of low SMD and SMI

A radiologist who had 5 years of experience of abdominal CT and was blinded to the ultrasonographic and clinical findings annotated the CT images using MATLAB software (v. 4.10.2, MathWorks, American).

The SMD was defined as the mean attenuation [in Hounsfield units (HU)] of the skeletal muscles at lumbar L3 level. A low SMD was defined as < 41 HU for patients with body mass index (BMI) < 25 kg/m2 and < 33 HU for those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 [22]. The skeletal muscles analyzed at the level of lumbar L3 were the psoas, erector spinae, quadratus lumborum, transversus abdominis, external and internal obliques, and rectus abdominis. The skeletal muscles at lumbar L3 level were identified on axial CT images using a threshold range of − 29 to + 150 HU. The mean attenuation of the skeletal muscles was automatically calculated by the software (Fig. 3a, b).

The SMI was defined as the cross-sectional area (cm2) of the skeletal muscles at lumbar L3 level divided by the participants height squared (m2). A low SMI was defined as < 47.1 cm2/m2 for men and < 34.4 cm2/m2 for women [23]. The cross-sectional area was calculated by counting the pixels within the range and multiplying by the pixel surface area (Fig. 3a, b).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc version 20.0.4 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). Continuous, normally distributed datasets are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical datasets are presented as frequency and percentage.

Binary logistic regression was used for univariate analysis to assess the relationships between QUS parameters and SMD/SMI. Parameters that were significant on univariate analysis were then included in multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors of low SMD and SMI. Predictive probabilities obtained from the regression models were used to construct receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the diagnostic performance of combinations of parameters. The optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index, and the corresponding sensitivities and specificities were calculated.

Discussion

We extracted scattering and attenuation-related parameters from RF signals and evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of combining these QUS parameters for the identification of SMD and SMI, as a potential novel non-invasive means of assessing muscle quantity and quality.

One of the most commonly used QUS parameters for the evaluation of intramuscular fat and muscle quality is EI [24]. However, EI is influenced by multiple factors, including the equipment settings, operator skills, and subcutaneous fat thickness [25], and currently lacks standardized measurement protocols [26]. There was a poor correlation between raw EI and intramyocellular lipid content measured using MRI (r = 0.17–0.32, P > 0.05) [27]. The novel method employed in the present study, using parameters derived from raw echo signals, is less susceptible to differences in equipment settings than EI. Our results indicated that multiple RF signal-based QUS parameters that reflect echo signal scattering and the nonlinear characteristics of the waves were significantly associated with low SMD (P < 0.05). The use of a combination of these parameters yielded a high AUC [0.899 (95% CI 0.852–0.945), P < 0.001] for the prediction of low SMD, with a sensitivity and specificity of 88.64% and 75.97%, respectively. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies of the use of the attenuation coefficient (AC), a parameter similar to RF signal-based QUS, for the quantification of hepatic steatosis. A meta-analysis generated a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 76% and 84% for the use of AC for the diagnosis of hepatic steatosis [19]. In a previous study, we evaluated the efficacy of RF signal-based QUS parameters for the assessment of forearm muscle SMD [20]. However, the PSMs are deeper and more susceptible to fatty infiltration than the forearm muscles, and in the present study, we found that PSM RF signal-based QUS parameters could also be an effective tool for the assessment of SMD.

Ultrasonography is recognized to be a reliable means of assessing muscle mass [2]. Indeed, ultrasonographically measured muscle thickness and lean body mass evaluated using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) closely correlate [28]. Furthermore, the ultrasonographically measured thicknesses of the gastrocnemius, rectus femoris, tibialis anterior, soleus, rectus abdominis, and geniohyoid muscles have AUC values ranging from 0.76 to 0.83 for the diagnosis sarcopenia [29]. Consistent with previous findings, we have shown that PSM thickness significantly correlates with SMI (P < 0.05). However, the relationship between EI and muscle quantity remains controversial. Whereas Fu et al. demonstrated that combining muscle cross-sectional area with EI improved its diagnostic accuracy for sarcopenia [29], a study by Hayden et al. showed no significant correlation between DEXA-measured lean mass and EI [30]. We hypothesize that these discrepancies may result from the highly variable nature of EI. In the present study, MUSQBOX.NMI(L) significantly correlated with SMI (P < 0.05). Although conceptually similar to EI, MUSQBOX.NMI(L) is less dependent on equipment settings and may provide more accurate muscle characterization. In the present study, the combination of PSM thickness and PSM MUSQBOX.NMI(L) yielded a sensitivity and specificity of 78.79% and 72.33%, respectively, for the prediction of low SMI.

In the present study, MUSQBOX.NMI(L) significantly correlated with SMI (P < 0.05), but there was no correlation between MUSQBOX.NMI(L) and SMD (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that MUSQBOX.NMI(L) predicts a reduction in muscle quantity primarily by reflecting microstructural cellular alterations, such as changes in fibrous tissue, water distribution, and extracellular matrix composition [12], rather than by fatty infiltration.

In this study, PSM thickness did not correlate with SMD (P > 0.05), possibly because muscle atrophy does not always accompany fatty infiltration. Muscle fat infiltration is now considered to be a distinct condition to sarcopenia; fatty infiltration can exist independently of muscle loss and may be influenced by various clinical factors [31]. For instance, a study of 123 patients who were undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy showed that 40.7% had sarcopenia, 25.2% had fatty infiltration, and only 11.4% had both [32]. Furthermore, Hsieh et al. reported no difference in the SMIs of patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver or nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, but those with severe muscle fat infiltration were more likely to have early-stage nonalcoholic steatohepatitis [33]. These findings are consistent with the notion that muscle fat infiltration and sarcopenia can exist independently, and this may explain why PSM thickness was not significantly associated with SMD in the present study.

Limitations

The present study had several limitations. First, although the sample size was relatively large, it was a single-center study, and therefore there may have been selection bias. Second, the intra- and inter-observer agreements of this method for true positive findings were not evaluated. Third, patients with various underlying diseases were included, and therefore the utility of the method for the assessment of therapy and the prognosis of specific diseases was not assessed.

Conclusions

For the assessment of both low SMD and low SMI, RF signal-based QUS parameters of PSM are useful tools. They provide a noninvasive, accurate, and clinically feasible method for the assessment of muscle quality and quantity that is particularly suitable for use in primary healthcare settings and for long-term muscle monitoring. Future studies should investigate the intra- and inter-observer reliability of their use in true positive findings. Larger multicenter trials are also warranted to explore the value of PSM RF signal-based QUS parameters for the prediction of treatment response in, and the prognosis of, patients with specific diseases.
