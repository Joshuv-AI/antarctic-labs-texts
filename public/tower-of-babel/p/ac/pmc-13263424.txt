Diagnostic value of serum inhibin B, anti-Müllerian hormone, and estradiol for central precocious puberty and early-fast puberty in girls: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lei Xu, Haimeng Zhang, Hui Huang, Li Yang, Liling Xie, Dongguang Zhang, Yu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCentral precocious puberty (CPP) and early and fast puberty (EFP) in girls pose diagnostic challenges due to reliance on invasive gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) stimulation tests. Identifying non-invasive, cost-effective biomarkers is imperative for early detection and risk stratification. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic value of serum inhibin B (INHB), anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), and estradiol (E2)—independent of uterine volume—for distinguishing CPP and EFP from premature thelarche and healthy controls.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed 423 girls [CPP: n=141; EFP: n=97; premature thelarche (PT): n=116; healthy controls: n=69] from Jiangxi Provincial Children’s Hospital [2020–2022]. Clinical parameters, hormone levels (AMH, INHB, gonadotropins), uterine volume (UV), and ovarian volume (OV) were assessed. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves; biomarker relationships were examined using Spearman correlation.ResultsSignificant differences emerged across groups: CPP patients demonstrated lower AMH vs. PT (1.79 vs. 4.97 ng/mL, P<0.001) but higher INHB (32.20 vs. 21.75 pg/mL, P<0.001), while EFP showed elevated INHB relative to PT. INHB exhibited very high diagnostic accuracy for CPP [area under the curve (AUC) =0.82; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.76–0.87; sensitivity 83.0%, specificity 62.7% at 21.95 pg/mL], surpassing AMH (AUC =0.58). Notably, AMH correlated inversely with gonadotropins (P<0.001). For distinguishing EFP from PT, INHB alone demonstrated high accuracy (AUC =0.894; 95% CI: 0.857–0.928; sensitivity 90.7%, specificity 76.8% at an optimal cutoff of 25.80 pg/mL). To avoid incorporation bias, UV was excluded from the primary predictive model; instead, the combination of INHB, AMH, and E2 achieved AUC =0.905 (95% CI: 0.867–0.938; sensitivity 80.4%, specificity 86.5%). INHB positively correlated with UV in EFP (ρ=0.26, P=0.009).ConclusionsINHB is a useful diagnostic biomarker for CPP, while AMH helps differentiate CPP from PT. The INHB + AMH + E2 panel, which avoids UV to eliminate incorporation bias, shows high accuracy (AUC =0.905) for EFP detection and may assist clinical risk stratification.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Inhibin B (INHB) demonstrates strong diagnostic accuracy [area under the curve (AUC) =0.82] for central precocious puberty (CPP) with 83.0% sensitivity and 62.7% specificity at 21.95 pg/mL.

• Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels are significantly lower in CPP vs. premature thelarche (PT) patients, supporting its utility in differentiating these conditions.

• INHB alone achieves high diagnostic accuracy (AUC =0.894) for distinguishing early and fast puberty (EFP) from PT, and the INHB + AMH + estradiol (E2) combination (AUC =0.905) further improves performance without reliance on uterine volume (UV), thereby avoiding incorporation bias.

What is known and what is new?

• AMH and INHB have been individually investigated as potential biomarkers for CPP, but their combined use with pelvic ultrasound parameters has not been systematically evaluated.

• This study demonstrates that the INHB + AMH + E2 combination panel, which avoids UV to eliminate incorporation bias, provides high cross-sectional discrimination between EFP and PT (AUC =0.905), offering a non-invasive diagnostic approach.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The INHB + AMH + E2 panel may be useful for diagnostic assessment and clinical risk stratification in girls presenting with early pubertal signs. Multi-center validation studies with larger sample sizes are needed before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Background

Precocious puberty, defined by the onset of secondary sexual characteristics before age 8 years in girls (or 7.5 years in some Asian populations, including Chinese national guidelines) or 9 years in boys, is classified into central precocious puberty (CPP) and peripheral forms based on hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis activation. CPP refers to gonadotropin-dependent early puberty driven by premature activation of the HPG axis. Early and fast puberty (EFP) is characterized by HPG axis activation occurring after the precocious threshold but before the typical age of puberty onset, resulting in accelerated sexual maturation and skeletal advancement. Premature thelarche (PT) refers to isolated breast development without other signs of puberty or HPG axis activation. The prevalence of CPP among girls worldwide is generally low, with data from some countries showing an annual incidence of about 0.09% (e.g., Denmark) (1). In studies from China and other regions, the prevalence of generalized precocious puberty ranges from 0.43% to 0.5% (2). Regarding EFP, the prevalence in girls aged 8 to 10 years can reach around 10% in certain areas (3). Delayed diagnosis can result in compromised final adult height (mean loss 10–20 cm) and psychological distress in affected children (4).

Rationale and knowledge gap

The gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) stimulation test remains the diagnostic gold standard for CPP (5). However, this method is invasive, costly, and logistically demanding due to serial blood sampling. Consequently, identifying simpler, cost-effective diagnostic alternatives is imperative.

Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), a glycoprotein secreted directly by ovarian granulosa cells, has emerged as a candidate biomarker. Evidence indicates significantly lower AMH levels in CPP vs. PT cohorts (6), supporting its utility in differentiating these conditions. Further, Hu et al. (7) propose AMH as a potential diagnostic biomarker for CPP (8). Similarly, inhibin B (INHB)—produced by antral follicle granulosa cells—demonstrated elevated levels in CPP compared to non-CPP groups (4), with Jeong et al. validating its diagnostic capacity (9). It is worth noting that Jeong et al. (9) reported a contrasting perspective, suggesting that AMH may have limited clinical utility as a standalone biomarker for CPP diagnosis. Our receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis [area under the curve (AUC) =0.58 for AMH in CPP diagnosis] is consistent with this caution, and any statement regarding the clinical usefulness of AMH should be tempered accordingly. AMH appears most informative as a complementary marker in the CPP-PT differential rather than as a primary diagnostic tool. While both AMH and INHB show promise as CPP biomarkers, neither is integrated into current diagnostic criteria.

EFP, HPG axis activation occurring after the precocious threshold but before typical pubertal age, manifests as accelerated sexual maturation and skeletal advancement (5). The term “fast” refers to the tempo of pubertal progression, conventionally defined as advancement by at least one Tanner stage within 6 months. In this retrospective study, pubertal tempo was assessed based on documented Tanner stage records at the initial visit and at a prior clinic visit within 6 months, supplemented by bone age advancement and growth velocity data. We acknowledge that this retrospective approach may not capture the full longitudinal trajectory for all patients, and this is addressed as a study limitation. Critically, predictive biomarkers remain underexplored. Although AMH and INHB have been investigated separately as potential biomarkers for CPP, several gaps remain: prior studies have largely focused on binary CPP vs. non-CPP comparisons, have not systematically evaluated the clinically challenging distinction between EFP and PT, and have not assessed the incremental diagnostic value of combining hormonal markers with pelvic ultrasound parameters. Furthermore, most existing studies have not adjusted for the confounding effects of age and Tanner stage. The present study was therefore designed to address these gaps.

Objective

Therefore, our study aimed to assess the diagnostic value of serum AMH, INHB, and estradiol (E2)—independent of uterine volume (UV)—in the evaluation of CPP and EFP in girls. We investigated whether AMH and INHB—quantified across CPP, EFP, PT, and healthy cohorts—serve as diagnostic indicators for distinguishing EFP from PT, enabling timely intervention to optimize outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-1-0037/rc).

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cross-sectional cohort study analyzed 423 girls evaluated at the Department of Endocrinology, Genetics, and Metabolism of Jiangxi Provincial Children’s Hospital, China, between October 2020 and December 2022. Diagnostic classification (CPP, EFP, and PT) was based on predefined clinical, hormonal, and ultrasound criteria according to national consensus guidelines, determined prior to and independent of the retrospective biomarker analyses. We acknowledge that the use of UV in both the diagnostic reference standard and prediction models may introduce incorporation bias, which is further addressed in the Discussion. Participants were classified into four diagnostic groups: CPP (n=141), EFP (n=97), PT (n=116), and age-matched healthy controls (n=69). Diagnoses adhered to the 2015 Consensus on Diagnosis and Treatment of Central Precocious Puberty.

Inclusion criteria were group-specific: CPP required (5) updated by subsequent guidelines (10): (I) onset of secondary sexual characteristics before age 8 years with progressive development; and (II) GnRH-stimulated peak luteinizing hormone (LH) >5.0 IU/L and LH/follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) peak ratio >0.6 (consistent with the definition of a “positive” GnRH stimulation test used for diagnostic classification in this study). The GnRH test data in Table 1 reflect only repeat or follow-up GnRH tests performed after initial diagnosis; all CPP patients had met criterion (I) at the time of diagnostic evaluation and (II) pelvic ultrasound showing uterine length ≥3.4 cm, unilateral ovarian volume (OV) ≥1 mL, and ≥3 follicles >4 mm. EFP required: (I) HPG axis activation between ages 8–10 years, as evidenced by basal LH ≥0.1 IU/L and/or a GnRH-stimulated peak LH ≥5.0 IU/L with a peak LH/FSH ratio ≥0.6. (II) Accelerated pubertal progression (Tanner stage advancement within <6 months). Of the 97 EFP patients, 73 (75.3%) had documented Tanner stage assessments at two clinic visits within 6 months confirming direct stage advancement; the remaining 24 (24.7%) were classified based on bone age advancement (>1 year) and accelerated growth velocity (>2 cm above age-expected rates), consistent with established clinical surrogates. This limitation is further discussed in the “Strengths and limitations” section. (III) Bone age advancement >1 year beyond chronological age. PT required: (I) isolated breast development (Tanner II), in the absence of other signs of puberty, including no pubic or axillary hair development, no accelerated linear growth velocity, no significant bone age advancement, and no pelvic ultrasound evidence of uterine or ovarian enlargement consistent with HPG axis activation; (II) basal LH <0.1 IU/L; and (III) age-matching (±12 months) with healthy controls. Healthy controls were recruited from the general pediatric outpatient clinic and the routine health check-up service. All controls underwent clinical evaluation to exclude endocrine, chronic systemic, or genetic disorders, and none were receiving medications known to affect growth or pubertal development. Excluded criteria applied to all groups included: secondary CPP (e.g., adrenal/cranial pathology), structural reproductive tract anomalies, genetic/metabolic disorders (e.g., Turner syndrome), and incomplete clinical data.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Provincial Children’s Hospital (ethics approval No. JXSETYY-YXKY-20240002). Clinical and laboratory procedures

The age, height, weight, and sexual development of the included children were collected. Detailed laboratory and imaging protocols are described below.

Anthropometric measurements [height, weight, body mass index (BMI) and Tanner staging] were performed by trained pediatric endocrinologists. Tanner staging was assessed based on breast development (B1–B5), pubic hair (P1–P5), and axillary hair development, according to the criteria of Marshall and Tanner. Bone age was assessed from left-hand/wrist radiographs using the Tanner-Whitehouse 3 (TW3) method. All images were read by two experienced pediatric radiologists, with repeated calibration sessions; discrepant readings (>0.5 years) were resolved by consensus. Fasting morning blood samples (2 mL) were collected via venipuncture. For LH, FSH, and E2, serum concentrations were measured using chemiluminescence immunoassays on the ADIVA Centaur XP platform (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). The functional sensitivities were 0.1 IU/L for LH, 0.1 IU/L for FSH, and 10 pg/mL for E2, with intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) <5% and inter-assay CVs <7%.

AMH and INHB levels were measured via ELISA [Ansh Labs, Webster, TX, USA; Cat. No. (AMH Cat. No. AL-105) and (INHB Cat. No. AL-107)]. The functional sensitivities were 0.01 ng/mL for AMH and 1.0 pg/mL for INHB, with intra-assay CVs <8% and inter-assay CVs <10%. All blood samples were processed within 2 hours of collection, centrifuged, and stored at −80 ℃ until batch analysis to minimize preanalytical variability.

For CPP and EFP participants, a standard GnRH stimulation test was conducted: 2.5 µg/kg (max 100 µg) synthetic GnRH (Ferring Pharmaceuticals) was administered intravenously, with serial LH/FSH measurements at 0, 30, 60, and 90 minutes post-injection, Gonadotropin responses were measured on the ADVIA Centaur XP platform, and HPGA activation was defined as a peak LH ≥5.0 IU/L and a peak LH/FSH ratio ≥0.6, consistent with current consensus recommendations. Transabdominal pelvic ultrasound (GE Voluson E10, Chicago, IL, USA; 4–8 MHz transducer) assessed UV and OV, calculated as: volume (mL) = 0.523 × longitudinal × anteroposterior × transverse diameters.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis utilized SPSS 26.0 (IBM) and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Continuous variable normality was evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk tests. Group comparisons employed ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests (normally distributed data) or Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn-Bonferroni correction (non-normal data), presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median interquartile range (IQR), respectively. Spearman’s rank correlation (ρ) analyzed associations between hormonal/structural markers, with correlation strength categorized as weak (|ρ|<0.3), moderate (0.3≤|ρ|<0.7), or strong (|ρ|≥0.7). Bonferroni correction addressed multiple comparisons (adjusted α=0.01 for 5 tests/group).

Multivariable logistic regression (backward stepwise selection; entry P<0.10, retention P<0.05) identified independent predictors for CPP diagnosis and EFP risk, with Firth’s penalized likelihood resolving complete separation. ROC curves evaluated biomarker performance, with optimal cutoffs maximizing Youden’s index. ROC curves for individual biomarkers were based on univariable models, whereas combination panels (e.g., INHB + UV) were derived from multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, BMI, and Tanner stage. Combination panels derived from logistic regression probability scores were compared using DeLong’s method. The primary endpoints were the diagnostic accuracy (AUC) of INHB for differentiating CPP from non-CPP and of the INHB + AMH + E2 combination for distinguishing EFP from PT. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed P<0.05.

Given the retrospective, cross-sectional design and the absence of longitudinal follow-up, analyses involving EFP reflect cross-sectional discrimination between EFP and PT/CPP at the time of evaluation rather than prospective prediction of future pubertal tempo.

Results

Comparison of clinical indicators between groups

The study cohort comprised four distinct groups: EFP (n=97), CPP (n=141), PT (n=116), and healthy controls (n=69) (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics revealed significant intergroup heterogeneity (P<0.001 by Kruskal-Wallis test) across anthropometric, hormonal, and gonadal parameters (Table 1). Healthy controls demonstrated expected prepubertal profiles with exclusive Tanner I staging. The PT cohort showed anthropometric similarity to healthy controls but distinctively elevated INHB and larger UV/OV. Critical hormonal and structural divergences were visualized through boxplot analyses (Figure 2A-2G). AMH exhibited marked suppression in CPP [1.79 (IQR, 0.09–3.32) ng/mL] compared to both EFP [4.21 (IQR, 3.10–5.45) ng/mL; P=0.001] and PT [4.97 (IQR, 3.43–6.73) ng/mL; P<0.001], with non-overlapping IQRs confirming group separation (Figure 2A). INHB progressively increased from healthy controls [16.60 (IQR, 13.80–20.40) pg/mL] to PT [21.75 (IQR, 17.28–27.75) pg/mL], CPP [32.20 (IQR, 23.60–42.50) pg/mL], and EFP [36.00 (IQR, 29.10–44.70) pg/mL], demonstrating a clear hierarchy across pathological states (Figure 2B). Basal gonadotropins and E2 revealed similar stratification: LH was 10-fold higher in EFP [0.71 (IQR, 0.23–1.84) IU/L] vs. PT [0.06 (IQR, 0.06–0.09) IU/L], while FSH and E2 showed stepwise elevations from healthy/PT groups to CPP/EFP cohorts (Figure 2C-2E). UV and OV were markedly elevated in the EFP group compared to PT [UV: 4.16 (IQR, 3.00–5.68) vs. 1.67 (IQR, 1.29–2.13) mL; OV: 2.32 (IQR, 1.80–3.18) vs. 1.14 (IQR, 0.86–1.52) mL; both P<0.001; Figure 2F,2G]. It should be noted that uterine length ≥3.4 cm and unilateral OV ≥1 mL were inclusion criteria for the CPP group; therefore, the observed UV/OV differences between CPP and other groups partly reflect the study design rather than independent discriminatory findings, and conclusions regarding the CPP-PT comparison on these parameters should be interpreted with caution. Several outlier values (OV >10 mL) were observed among healthy controls in Figure 2G. Medical records for these subjects were re-examined; all met the inclusion criteria for healthy controls, with no endocrine or structural pathology identified. These values likely represent the upper physiological range of prepubertal OV in this age cohort. These cases were retained in the analysis and their presence does not alter the overall group comparisons. Pairwise comparisons with covariate adjustment further quantified these relationships (Table 2). The AMH suppression in CPP vs. PT persisted after Tanner-stage and age adjustment (Tanner stage-age adjusted P<0.001), whereas EFP vs. PT differences were attenuated post-adjustment (Tanner stage-age adjusted P=0.18), corroborating the boxplot-distinct trajectories. Structural markers (UV/OV) remained robustly elevated in both CPP and EFP vs. PT after full adjustment (all Tanner stage-age adjusted P≤0.003).

Correlation analyses of key biomarkers

Building upon the established group differences, we further examined within-group correlations between critical hormonal and structural markers. In the EFP cohort, INHB showed a weak but statistically significant positive correlation with UV (ρ=0.26, P=0.009; Figure 3A). Basal LH showed a moderate association with UV (ρ=0.44, P<0.001; Figure 3B), suggesting some degree of coordinated hypothalamic-pituitary-uterine activation. INHB also showed a weak positive correlation with OV (ρ=0.20, P=0.045; Figure 3C), though the effect size was modest and should be interpreted with caution. In the CPP group, AMH showed a weak positive correlation with UV (ρ=0.20, P=0.02; Figure 3D), and INHB showed a similarly weak association with OV (ρ=0.21, P=0.01; Figure 3E). Given that all ρ values below 0.3 fall within the “weak” category, conclusions regarding clinical significance of these correlations should be appropriately moderated. Notably, a weak positive correlation between AMH and UV was observed exclusively in the CPP group (ρ=0.20, P=0.02). Since AMH is suppressed and UV is enlarged in CPP relative to PT, a positive within-group correlation may reflect a shared dependence on follicular pool size: girls with a larger remaining preantral follicular reserve (higher relative AMH) may also exhibit greater uterine stimulation. Alternatively, this association could reflect residual confounding by body size or Tanner stage within the CPP group. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify this relationship.

Diagnostic modeling

To identify independent factors distinguishing pathological puberty from healthy states, we performed logistic regression analyses (Table 3 and Table S1). For CPP diagnosis, multivariate modeling revealed AMH [odds ratio (OR) =0.73; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.66–0.80; P<0.001] and INHB (OR =1.036; 95% CI: 1.01–1.04; P<0.001) as robust independent predictors. Each 1 ng/mL decrease in AMH increased CPP odds by 37.3% (1/0.73), while every 10 pg/mL INHB elevation raised odds by 29.6%. Tanner stage was non-estimable due to complete separation from healthy controls (all CPP ≥ Tanner II vs. healthy Tanner I). For distinguishing EFP from PT at the time of evaluation, basal LH (OR =1.56; 95% CI: 1.15–2.11; P=0.005) and UV (OR =1.30; 95% CI: 1.13–1.49; P<0.001) emerged as key multivariate predictors. A 1 IU/L LH increase conferred 55.6% higher EFP risk, and each 1 mL UV enlargement increased risk by 29.5%. INHB and E2 lost significance after adjustment (P>0.24), indicating their association is mediated through other variables.

Diagnostic accuracy of biomarkers

ROC curve analyses quantified the discriminatory power of key biomarkers. For CPP diagnosis, INHB demonstrated strong performance (AUC =0.82; 95% CI: 0.762–0.868; P<0.001), with sensitivity 83.0% and specificity 62.7% at the optimal cutoff (21.95 pg/mL). AMH showed marginal diagnostic utility (AUC =0.58; 95% CI: 0.512–0.63; P=0.02), though its low sensitivity (23.4%) limits clinical application despite high specificity (91.9%) at 8.35 ng/mL (Figure 4).For distinguishing EFP from PT, INHB (AUC =0.90; 95% CI: 0.86–0.94; P<0.001) emerged as an outstanding individual predictor, achieving 90.7% sensitivity and76.8% specificity at an optimal cutoff of 25.80 pg/mL (Figure 5A). Of note, basal LH was not included as a diagnostic discriminator in this comparison, as low basal LH (<0.1 IU/L) was an explicit inclusion criterion for the PT group, which would artifactually inflate its apparent discriminatory performance. AMH lacked independent discriminative value for EFP vs. PT (AUC =0.44, P=0.12). To avoid incorporation bias—UV was used in the diagnostic reference standard for CPP and should therefore not serve as an independent predictor in this comparison—the primary combination model was constructed using biomarkers only (Figure 5). The INHB + AMH + E2 panel achieved AUC =0.905 (95% CI: 0.867–0.938; P<0.001) with 80.4% sensitivity and 86.5% specificity (Figure 5B), representing meaningful improvement over INHB alone while remaining free of incorporation bias. For exploratory reference, UV alone (AUC =0.944; 95% CI: 0.92–0.97) and the INHB + UV panel (AUC =0.963; 95% CI: 0.94–0.98; sensitivity 93.8%, specificity 87.4%) are reported in Figure S1; however, these estimates are subject to incorporation bias and should not be considered primary conclusions. The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, which, combined with the high AUC and specificity, indicates a robust model for identifying negative cases (Figure S2).

Discussion

Key findings

This study demonstrates that serum INHB has significant diagnostic value for CPP (AUC =0.82) and that an unbiased biomarker combination of INHB, AMH, and E2 (without UV) achieves high accuracy (AUC =0.905) for distinguishing EFP from PT, with INHB alone yielding AUC =0.894. However, it should be noted that INHB levels rise physiologically with advancing age. Given the significant age differences across groups (EFP > CPP > PT > healthy controls), chronological age may constitute a source of confounding bias. Future studies should include age-stratified or age-adjusted analyses. Of note, the INHB ROC analysis for CPP (AUC =0.82) was based on a CPP-vs.-all-non-CPP comparison. A dedicated CPP-vs.-PT ROC was not separately performed; however, the significant INHB difference between these two groups (P<0.001; Table 2) and the complementary value of AMH suggest that INHB retains discriminatory capacity in this comparison. Future studies should quantify CPP-vs.-PT diagnostic performance specifically. From a clinical standpoint, the most challenging differential diagnosis is not between CPP/EFP and healthy prepubertal girls—which is typically apparent clinically—but rather between EFP/CPP and PT, where the clinical presentation overlaps considerably. The present analyses therefore prioritize this clinically relevant comparison. Comparisons with healthy controls are retained for descriptive and reference purposes but should not be the primary focus of clinical interpretation. AMH levels were significantly lower in CPP vs. PT, supporting its potential utility in differentiating these conditions.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the comprehensive assessment of multiple biomarkers across well-defined diagnostic groups, standardized laboratory and ultrasound protocols, and rigorous statistical analysis, including covariate adjustment and ROC curve evaluation. Limitations include the single-center retrospective design, which may limit generalizability. An additional important limitation is the significant age difference between the EFP group (mean, 109 months) and the PT group (mean, 90 months), a gap of approximately 1.5 years. Since hormone levels—including INHB, LH, and FSH—change substantially with chronological age, this age discrepancy may confound between-group comparisons and inflate the apparent discriminatory performance of hormonal biomarkers. Although covariate-adjusted analyses were performed, these adjustments may not fully eliminate age-related bias given the cross-sectional study design. A critical limitation of this study is the incorporation bias introduced by using UV as both a diagnostic inclusion criterion for CPP and as a predictor variable. This is not merely “partial” bias—UV will inevitably appear to discriminate between groups selected partly based on UV. Consequently, the previously reported AUC =0.96 for the INHB + UV panel should not be interpreted as a primary finding. We have reconstructed Figure 5 to present the unbiased INHB + AMH + E2 panel (AUC =0.905; 95% CI: 0.867–0.938) as the primary model. UV-containing models are provided in Figure S1 for reference only. Regarding GnRH testing: while these biomarkers may assist in clinical risk stratification, the methodological limitations—particularly incorporation bias and the single-center retrospective design—preclude a recommendation to reduce reliance on GnRH stimulation testing. Additionally, while 73 of 97 EFP patients (75.3%) had two documented Tanner assessments within 6 months confirming rapid progression, the remaining 24 (24.7%) relied on surrogate indicators (bone age and growth velocity), introducing some diagnostic heterogeneity within the EFP group. Prospective studies with standardized interval assessments are needed to address this. Multi-center prospective validation is required before any such clinical application can be recommended. The cross-sectional nature precludes assessment of predictive validity for future pubertal progression. Additionally, the relatively small sample size warrants validation in larger, multi-center cohorts.

Comparison with similar research

The initiation of puberty primarily stems from the activation of the HPG axis, and its initiation time is associated with factors such as genetics, environment, dietary nutrition, obesity, and lifestyle (11-16). Generally, puberty is initiated at 10–12 in girls and 12–14 in boys. However, with the rapid development of society and the economy, there is a trend of early initiation of puberty around the world. A systematic review suggests that the age of initiation of breast development in girls worldwide has been 0.24 years earlier per decade over the 36 years since 1977 (17). AMH is a glycoprotein whose gene is located on the short arm of chromosome 19 (18). AMH is secreted in both the ovaries of women and the testes of men. For women, it is secreted by granulosa cells of the primary follicle at 36 weeks of embryonic age, remains at a low level after a transient peak in the neonatal period, gradually increases with age, peaks around the age of 16 years, gradually declines around the age of 20–25 years, and is almost undetectable in the blood after menopause (19-21). Our results regarding INHB are consistent with previous studies. Jeong et al. (9) found that the cut-off value of INHB level in diagnosing CPP patients was 19.59 pg/mL, with a sensitivity of 83.3% and a specificity of 82.9%. Wu et al. (22) found that serum INHB had a sensitivity of 80.13%, specificity of 77.62%, and a cut-off value of 28.16 pg/mL in predicting CPP. The differences in specificity and cut-off values between studies may be attributable to differences in study populations; some studies included only CPP and healthy girls without PT cases (23).

Explanations of findings

This study found that the AMH level in the CPP group was lower than that in the PT group, consistent with previous reports. Sahin et al. (24) found that AMH levels decreased during HPGA activation. Lashen et al. (25) found that AMH levels in adolescent girls were 25% lower than in prepubertal girls. The decrease in AMH levels may be due to follicle recruitment during activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis. Preantral and early antral follicles produce AMH but not in the later stages of follicle formation (26-28). AMH levels may decrease due to the relative reduction of the preantral and early antral follicular pools during axis activation. A negative correlation between serum AMH and FSH levels has been found in previous studies (29-31). This study suggests a negative correlation between AMH levels and basal values of FSH and LH, as well as peak levels. This result supports the conclusion that AMH levels decrease after activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis (32). INHB is synthesized and secreted by granulosa cells of the ovary and grows with follicle diameter (33). The INHB level started to increase with follicular development during prepuberty and peaked at Tanner IV after entering puberty. The conclusions of this study coincide with the physiological variation pattern of INHB levels (34,35).

Implications and actions needed

From a clinical perspective, the INHB + AMH + E2 panel may be useful for risk stratification and for identifying girls who merit closer follow-up and consideration of treatment. While pelvic ultrasound parameters showed statistically significant group differences, their interpretation requires consideration of the substantial age difference between EFP and PT groups, and ultrasound findings should be integrated with the overall clinical context rather than used as standalone discriminators (36). While pelvic ultrasound parameters, particularly UV (37-40), were integral to diagnostic classification, their inclusion in both reference standards and predictive models may have introduced some potential bias, highlighting the need for future studies to consider alternative reference standards or perform sensitivity analyses to address this.

Future directions should include multi-center validation studies with larger sample sizes, longitudinal assessment of biomarker trajectories, and evaluation of cost-effectiveness compared to standard GnRH stimulation testing.

Conclusions

In summary, by investigating the diagnostic value of AMH and INHB in girls with CPP and EFP, this study confirms that serum INHB has diagnostic value for CPP and that serum AMH levels are significantly lower in CPP compared to PT, a finding that is unlikely to be affected by incorporation bias (as AMH was not used in the diagnostic classification criteria) and therefore represents one of the more methodologically robust conclusions of this study. AMH may serve as a useful complementary biomarker for distinguishing CPP from PT.

Our results also suggest that higher INHB levels are strongly associated with the EFP phenotype, and that combining INHB with AMH and E2—without incorporating UV to avoid incorporation bias—provides high cross-sectional discrimination between EFP and PT (AUC =0.905) at the time of evaluation. These biomarkers may therefore be useful for diagnostic assessment and clinical risk stratification in girls presenting with early pubertal signs. However, as the number of subjects included in this study is small and the study is single-center, further data are required to support the conclusions. In the later stage, it is necessary to add more centers and increase the sample size for further research.
