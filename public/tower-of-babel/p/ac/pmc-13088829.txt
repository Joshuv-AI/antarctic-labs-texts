Augmenting the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II for intensive care unit mortality prediction and benchmarking: a French nationwide study
Authors: Toufik Kamel, Thierry Boulain
Published: 2026

Background

Severity scoring systems for intensive care unit (ICU) patients are widely used worldwide to construct hospital mortality prediction models [1–10]. In practice, these models are applied chiefly to support benchmarking and performance comparisons among ICUs [11] and for research purpose [11–13]. Although they demonstrated good discrimination and acceptable calibration at the time of their development [9], their accuracy is insufficient for precise individual prognostication in routine clinical decision-making [14].

The Simplified Acute Physiology Score, version 2 (SAPS II), developed in the early 1990s through a European-North American collaboration [6], is widely used [15, 16]. It incorporates a set of physiologic and laboratory variables collected during the first 24 h of ICU stay but includes very few variables describing the patient’s chronic health status or circumstances of admission (only AIDS, urgent surgery, hematologic malignancy, and metastatic cancer are considered). Subsequent studies have consistently confirmed its discriminatory performance (how well the model separates patients who die from those who survive) but revealed poor calibration (how closely its predicted mortality matches actual outcomes in the population) in contemporary populations. These findings mirror those observed with other severity scores and are largely driven by evolving case-mix and therapeutic advances [15, 17–26]. These limitations challenge the reliable comparison of performance across ICUs and over time.

In France, the calculation and reporting of SAPS II score for each ICU stay have been mandated by law since 1997 [27]. Authorized stakeholders have access to a comprehensive national government database containing standardized hospital discharge summaries, the Programme de médicalisation des systèmes d’information (PMSI). It includes detailed ICU stay information, including SAPS II and all diagnoses coded with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) along with other information (detailed below).

Building on consistent evidence that SAPS II is poorly calibrated in contemporary practice [26], we sought to address this limitation using routinely collected case-mix information. We hypothesized that this would materially improve calibration and benchmarking performance compared with both the original and a recalibrated SAPS II model.

We therefore used the nationwide PMSI database to recalibrate SAPS II and develop an augmented mortality prediction model that enhances SAPS II with ICD-10–derived case-mix indicators from all ICU stays in France between 2015 and 2024. The primary objective of this work was to improve ICU benchmarking through more accurate estimation of expected in-hospital mortality at the institutional level, which underpins standardized mortality ratios (SMRs). The augmented model was developed to optimize aggregate calibration and hospital-level benchmarking performance, rather than to support bedside decision-making. Model development was performed using ICU stays from 2015 to 2023, with validation on 2024 data. A sensitivity analysis will be conducted using early 2025 data to assess potential calibration drift. In addition, we describe national trends in crude ICU mortality and SAPS II-based standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) over the study period.

Given that SAPS II is the only severity score mandatorily and consistently available for all ICU stays in the French national database, this study focuses on improving SAPS II–based benchmarking using routinely collected data, rather than adopting an alternative score that would require additional data collection. Beyond calibration improvements, a key goal of this work was to reduce unexplained between-hospital variability in outcomes after case-mix adjustment, thereby strengthening the equity and plausibility of institutional comparisons.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a nationwide retrospective cohort study using de-identified data from the PMSI database, which contains a mandatory, standardized discharge summary for every public and private acute-care hospital stay in France. These summaries include patient demographics, length of stay, vital status at discharge, primary ICU admission diagnosis if any, SAPS II for ICU stay, diagnosis-related group (DRG) and all acute and comorbid diagnoses coded with ICD-10.

The study complied with French regulations for retrospective observational research using de-identified data. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University Hospital of Orléans, France (#CERO 2505–04). Individual patient notification, which was impracticable given de-identification, was not requested. Reporting follows the STROBE statement and its RECORD extension, as well as the TRIPOD statement for the development and validation of prediction models for individual prognosis [28–30].

Study objective and estimand

The estimand of interest is expected in-hospital mortality at the ICU level for use in institutional benchmarking based on SMRs.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included all adult (≥ 18 years) ICU stays between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2024. Standardized discharge summaries that were available for 2025 from January to May at the extraction date (July 26, 2025) were also extracted but treated separately due to incomplete reporting. Records with missing SAPS II or implausible SAPS II values (> 163, above the theoretical maximum) were excluded. Although a SAPS II of 0 is theoretically possible, we observed that many hospitals appeared to enter “0” when the score was unavailable: indeed, one-third of such records were for patients ≥ 40 years old, who could not plausibly score below 7. Therefore, records with SAPS II = 0 were also excluded. When multiple ICU stays occurred during the same hospitalization, only the last ICU stay was retained to align the analysis with hospital-level benchmarking, ensuring a single outcome per hospitalization and avoiding disproportionate weighting of complex care trajectories.

Hospital identification

Our full cohort included 402 distinct hospitals, some of which were regrouped for reasons detailed in the Expanded Methods (see Additional File 1). After regrouping, the dataset comprised 293 unique hospital identifiers.

Comorbidity coding

To characterize case-mix, we applied the Elixhauser classification (eTable 1 in Additional File 1) [31–33]. Comorbidity codes were identified across all ICD-10 diagnosis fields for each hospital stay, excluding the principal ICU admission diagnosis.

Clinical subgroups

Twelve diagnostic categories were defined a priori (some overlapping) using ICD-10 codes and, when relevant, DRGs: sepsis, septic shock, bacterial pneumonia without SARS-CoV-2 co-infection, bacterial pneumonia (all), viral pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), stroke (ischemic stroke or non-traumatic intracerebral haemorrhage), non-operative severe traumatic brain injury, cardiac arrest (all), out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, surgical stay with organ failure or use of organ support during ICU stay, and surgical stay without organ failure/support during ICU stay (coding details provided in eTable 2).

Data analysis

Outcomes

We examined two main outcomes: 1) the SMR, defined as observed (O) in-hospital deaths divided by expected (E) deaths (SMR > 1 indicates higher-than-expected mortality; SMR < 1 lower-than-expected). Expected deaths were computed either from (i) the original SAPS II equation [6], (ii) a recalibrated SAPS II model (r-SAPS II), or (iii) from the case-mix augmented model (Aug-SAPS II) (see below). SMRs were reported overall and stratified by calendar year, hospital, and predefined diagnostic subgroups; 2) the individual in-hospital mortality, used as the dependent variable to develop and evaluate r-SAPS II and aug-SAPS II.

SMRs and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained using the exact Poisson (Garwood) method [34]. For simultaneous estimation across many strata, we fitted Poisson generalized linear models with a log link and log(E) as an offset [35].

Descriptive analyses (2015–2024)

We summarized annual ICU admissions, observed deaths, SAPS II–based SMRs (with 95% CIs) for the full 2015–2024 cohort and provided stratified results by year, hospital, and prespecified diagnostic subgroups.

Model development (Derivation cohort 2015–2023)

First, we fitted a logistic recalibration model (r-SAPS II) with in-hospital mortality as the outcome and the logit of the original SAPS II–predicted probability (computed from the published SAPS II equation [6]) as the sole predictor. Then, we fitted aug-SAPS II, a logistic model that included the same SAPS II logit term and all Elixhauser comorbidity indicators, with pre-specified interactions between SAPS II and each comorbidity, and between age and the same factors (eTable 3). We also added the month of discharge to the list of covariables in aug-SAPS II, to account for seasonal variation in case-mix, whereas calendar year was deliberately not adjusted for in order to preserve sensitivity to secular changes in outcomes and calibration drift over time. We performed variable selection and shrinkage using elastic-net regularization [36, 37]. The mixing parameter (α) and penalty parameter (λ) were optimized by tenfold cross-validation, selecting the combination that minimized prediction error (deviance). Predictors with non-zero coefficients were retained in the final logistic model.

Discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Potential overfitting was evaluated with bootstrap bias-corrected calibration plots (200 resamples) on the continuous log-odds scale.

We assessed calibration using Cox’s calibration regression [38, 39]. For each model (SAPS II, r-SAPS II, and aug-SAPS II), we regressed observed in-hospital death on the logit of predicted probability. We reported the intercept (calibration-in-the-large, α; ideal = 0) and the calibration slope (β; ideal = 1) with 95% confidence intervals. To account for clustering within hospitals, standard errors were estimated using a cluster-robust sandwich estimator. Calibration across prespecified diagnostic subgroups was evaluated by adding subgroup indicators and their interactions with the linear predictor. As a complementary descriptive analysis, we computed SMRs within 2% quantiles of predicted risk and compared them to the ideal value of 1.

To quantify the shift of SMRs from the SAPS II-based to r-SAPS II and to aug-SAPS II, SMRs were compared using a Poisson GLM with a model indicator. Hospital-level variability in SMR was estimated with mixed effects Poisson regression including a random intercept for hospital; we summarized heterogeneity via the random-effects variance.

Temporal external validation (2024 cohort)

The coefficients estimated in the derivation set were applied to the independent validation set to generate predicted probabilities of death. Discrimination, calibration, shift in SMRs from the SAPS II-model, and hospital-level variability were estimated using the approaches used for the derivation cohort.

Sensitivity analysis

For the partial 2025 data, hospital-level SMRs were estimated using Poisson log-linear model with cluster-robust standard errors (hospital as the clustering unit). Year-to-year changes (2025–2024) were summarized as absolute differences. Wald 95% CIs were calculated using the delta method, combining variances from 2024 and 2025 while conservatively ignoring cross-year covariance. Overall drift was estimated using a single Poisson model including a year indicator with hospital-level clustering.

Because the dataset represents the complete national adult ICU population, we did not conduct null-hypothesis significance testing and therefore do not provide p-values. Instead, we report point estimates with 95% confidence (compatibility) intervals to indicate the range of values most consistent with the data [40]. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3) [41].

Results

From the PMSI database, we extracted 2,531,019 standardized hospital discharge summaries for patients aged > 18 years who had an ICU stay between January 1, 2015, and May 31, 2025. We excluded 6,087 records (0.24%) with missing SAPS II scores, 140 (0.006%) with SAPS II values above 163, and 28,058 (1.1%) with a SAPS II value of zero.

After these exclusions, the dataset comprised 2,496,734 ICU stays: 2,398,797 covering the complete 2015–2024 period and 97,937 from 2025, which was only partially reported at the time of extraction (July 26, 2025).

Description of the whole cohort

The characteristics of the 2015–2024 cohort are presented in Table 1 and eTable 4. During this period, 36.6% of patients were female, the mean age was 62.5 years (SD 16.4), ICU mortality was 17.1%, and in-hospital mortality was 19.8%. ICU admissions increased steadily from 228,842 in 2015 to 243,193 in 2019 (a > 1.5% rise per year), followed by a marked rise during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021), a temporary decline in 2022, and a renewed increase thereafter. Crude in-hospital mortality declined from 20.9% in 2015 to 18.5% in 2024, with a transient plateau or increase during the pandemic years (eFigure 1). These patterns were observed in both female and male patients (eFigure 2).

Performance of the original SAPS II over the whole cohort

The discriminative ability of the SAPS II–based predictive model for in-hospital death, as assessed by the AUC, was 0.840 (95% CI, 0.839–0.841). Global SMR decreased from 0.643 (95% CI, 0.637–0.648) in 2015 to 0.560 (95% CI, 0.555–0.565) in 2024, again showing a transient increase during the pandemic (eFigure 1). Overall, SMRs derived from the SAPS II model remained well below 1 throughout the study period, indicating systematic overprediction of mortality and progressive miscalibration over time, across groups of varying probability of in-hospital death (eFigure 3), and across most prespecified diagnostic subgroups (eFigure 4, eFigure 5).

Except during the COVID-19 pandemic years, the temporal evolution of SMRs closely mirrored that of crude mortality rates. A similar pattern was observed when SMRs were calculated at the hospital level (Fig. 1), highlighting the limited ability of SAPS II alone to account for changes in case-mix and outcomes over time.

Derivation cohort

The derivation cohort included ICU admissions from 2015 to 2023, encompassing 2,156,332 stays across 293 hospitals. The final augmented model (aug-SAPS II) retained 109 of the 111 candidate predictors (eTable 3).

Discrimination and overfitting

In the derivation cohort, aug-SAPS II showed higher discrimination than the original SAPS II–based model (AUC 0.866 [95% CI, 0.865–0.866] vs. 0.841 [95% CI, 0.840–0.841]). An AUC was not calculated for r-SAPS II because first-level recalibration adjusts only calibration parameters (intercept and slope) without altering discrimination. Overfitting was negligible, as shown in eFigure 6.

Calibration

Cox’s calibration regression showed marked improvement with both r-SAPS II and aug-SAPS II compared with SAPS II. Calibration-in-the-large (α) improved from –1.10 (95% CI, –1.14 to –1.06) under SAPS II to 0.00 (95% CI, –0.04 to 0.04) after recalibration. Similarly, the calibration slope (β) increased from 0.72 (95% CI, 0.71–0.74) to 1.00 (95% CI, 0.98–1.02). Subgroup analyses confirmed better calibration across diagnostic categories (eTable 5; eFigures 7–8), although residual miscalibration persisted in a few groups and across 2% risk-quantile strata (eFigure 9).

Temporal and hospital-level performance

Both r-SAPS II and aug-SAPS II shifted SMRs markedly toward 1 over time (eFigure 10) and across hospitals (eFigure 11). Importantly for benchmarking fairness, aug-SAPS II also reduced unexplained between-hospital heterogeneity, as reflected by a substantial decrease in the hospital-level random-effects variance. The between-hospital random-effect variance decreased by 35.3% with aug-SAPS II but remained unchanged with r-SAPS II.

Validation cohort

The validation cohort comprised ICU admissions in 2024, including 242,465 stays across 274 hospitals.

Discrimination

The AUC for aug-SAPS II was 0.862 (95% CI, 0.860–0.864), compared with 0.835 (95% CI, 0.833–0.837) for SAPS II.

Calibration

Monthly SMRs derived from r-SAPS II and aug-SAPS II remained close to 1, contrasting with the systematic overprediction by the original SAPS II (Fig. 2). Across 2% risk-quantile strata, observed and predicted mortality were closely aligned, although small residual deviations persisted (Fig. 3). At the individual level, Cox’s calibration regression confirmed excellent calibration with minimal overfitting for both r-SAPS II and aug-SAPS II. Under SAPS II, calibration-in-the-large (α) was –1.26 (95% CI, –1.31 to –1.21) and the calibration slope (β) was 0.72 (95% CI, 0.71–0.74). After recalibration, αmproved to 0.00 (95% CI, –0.05 to 0.05) for r-SAPS II and –0.09 (95% CI, –0.14 to –0.03) for aug-SAPS II, while β increased to 1.00 (95% CI, 0.97–1.03) and 0.98 (95% CI, 0.96–1.01), respectively (eFigure 12). Residual miscalibration persisted only in a few diagnostic subgroups (eTable 6; eFigures 13–14).

Hospital-level performance

At the hospital level, SMRs were substantially shifted toward 1 (Fig. 4). With SAPS II, only 3 of 274 hospitals (1.1%) had SMR > 1, compared with 120 (43.8%) under r-SAPS II and 155 (56.6%) under aug-SAPS II. The proportion of hospitals with 95% CIs including 1 increased from 21 (7.7%) to 160 (58.4%) and 170 (62.0%), respectively. SMRs also clustered near 1 across most of the diagnostic subgroups considered (eFigures 15–16). However, this was not the case for the low-risk surgical stays representing 10.8% of the validation cohort, for which aug-SAPS II did not completely eliminate overestimation of mortality risk, with an SMR averaged across hospitals of 0.49 compared with 0.41 under the r-SAPS II and 0.22 under the original SAPS II model.

Between-hospital heterogeneity

The overall between-hospital random-effects variance was unchanged with r-SAPS II compared with SAPS II, but decreased by 28.7% with aug-SAPS II. This reduction indicates a narrowing of residual between-hospital variability not attributable to measured case-mix, thereby strengthening the plausibility of institutional comparisons. When assessed by diagnostic subgroup, the reduction in between-hospital variance from r-SAPS II to aug-SAPS II was consistent across clinical categories (eTable 7; eFigures 17–18).

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis included ICU admissions from the partial 2025 cohort, comprising 97,937 stays across 272 hospitals.

Discrimination

The AUC for in-hospital mortality was 0.826 (95% CI, 0.823–0.830) with the SAPS II–based model and 0.855 (95% CI, 0.852–0.858) with aug-SAPS II.

Calibration and hospital-level performance

At the hospital level, aug-SAPS II again shifted SMRs toward 1 (eFigure 19). Only 8 of 272 hospitals (2.9%) had an SMR point estimate > 1 with SAPS II, compared with 147 (54.0%) with r-SAPS II and 121 (44.5%) with aug-SAPS II. The proportion of hospitals with a 95% SMR confidence interval including 1 increased from 49 (18.0%) to 198 (72.8%) and 204 (75.0%), respectively. These findings mirrored those observed in the 2024 validation cohort, with consistent temporal patterns when examined by month (eFigure 20).

Between-hospital heterogeneity

The overall random-effects variance decreased by 11.5% with r-SAPS II and by 22.1% with aug-SAPS II.

Temporal and subgroup stability

The global change in SMR derived from aug-SAPS II between 2024 and 2025 was minimal (–0.020 [95% CI, –0.038 to –0.002]). At the diagnostic subgroup level, SMR differences (2025–2024) were predominantly negative—10 of 12 subgroups showed slightly lower SMRs in 2025—but magnitudes were close to the overall drift. None of the subgroup-specific differences had 95% confidence intervals excluding 0 after clustering by hospital, indicating no meaningful subgroup-specific drift beyond the modest global decrease (eFigure 21).

Discussion

In this nationwide French study including more than two million ICU stays, augmenting the SAPS II model with comorbidity information derived from the Elixhauser classification not only improved discrimination but also markedly enhanced calibration overall and at the diagnostic subgroup and hospital levels. Whereas the original SAPS II equation systematically overestimated mortality risk—yielding SMRs well below 1 for nearly all hospitals—the augmented model shifted SMRs toward unity. Conceptually, SAPS II primarily captures acute physiological derangement at ICU admission, whereas chronic comorbidities reflect baseline vulnerability and long-term health status. Incorporating Elixhauser comorbidities therefore complements physiological severity by accounting for underlying disease burden that may not be fully represented in acute variables alone.

From a benchmarking perspective, the shift observed in SMR is critical, as it was accompanied by a clustering of SMRs around 1, with only a minority of hospitals showing excess mortality—an outcome that is both statistically plausible and clinically credible.

The observed improvement in AUC may appear numerically modest. However, even incremental gains in discrimination can meaningfully improve the allocation of expected mortality across risk strata in large populations. This refinement contributes to greater stability of hospital-level SMRs and enhances the interpretability of benchmarking comparisons.

A central finding of this study is the substantial reduction in between-hospital random-effects variance observed with the augmented model. Because hospital-level random effects capture residual variability after case-mix adjustment, their reduction supports more plausible institutional comparisons by limiting heterogeneity driven by incomplete risk adjustment rather than true differences in performance. Nevertheless, reduced variance should be interpreted as indicating less unexplained heterogeneity after adjustment, rather than as proof that all remaining differences are unrelated to institutional performance.

Although improved case-mix adjustment likely mitigates spurious heterogeneity, reduced variance may also reflect partial absorption of true differences in quality of care or systematic differences in documentation practices correlated with comorbidities. For example, hospitals with more complete documentation of chronic comorbidities or greater coding intensity, potentially influenced by local administrative practices or incentives, may appear to treat higher-risk patients, leading to upward adjustment of expected mortality and lower SMRs. Conversely, hospitals with less complete coding may appear to treat lower-risk patients. In addition, improved case-mix adjustment may partially absorb true performance differences if certain hospitals which systematically achieve better outcomes within specific comorbidity subgroups. Such differences in coding depth could influence both calibration and the observed reduction in between-hospital heterogeneity.

Because multilevel modelling cannot disentangle these mechanisms using administrative data alone, reduced random-effects variance should be interpreted as increased robustness of benchmarking comparisons rather than definitive evidence of diminished true heterogeneity in quality of care.

Because calibration of severity scores typically deteriorates over time—owing to therapeutic advances and organizational improvements—numerous studies have emphasized the need for periodic first-level recalibration of existing models [42, 43]. Our simply recalibrated version of SAPS II (r-SAPS II) achieved such a global realignment of SMRs. The augmented model (Aug-SAPS II), which also included the SAPS II logit as a covariate, provided similar recalibration while adding a meaningful gain in discrimination and a substantial reduction in hospital-level heterogeneity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to perform such an approach using a complete national ICU database covering an entire decade.

Despite the known temporal drift of severity scores, our results suggest that calibration of the augmented model remained stable between 2024 and 2025. Nevertheless, continued monitoring will be necessary, and annual recalibration would be straightforward, since all required variables are routinely available in national administrative datasets. Of note, our augmented model deliberately excluded acute diagnostic codes defining the immediate reason for ICU admission. While inclusion of such information could improve discrimination, even the most recent scores (e.g., APACHE IV, SAPS III) still require local customization when applied to new populations [19, 22, 24, 44]. Across most of the 12 diagnostic subgroups we examined, the augmented model demonstrated acceptable calibration, although systematic overestimation of mortality persisted among the least severe surgical patients, suggesting that SAPS II may be ill-suited for this subgroup. In this context, caution is warranted when interpreting SMRs in centers with a predominantly elective surgical profile.

Seasonal variation in ICU mortality was evident, with higher crude mortality observed during specific periods of the year. Including month of discharge in the augmented model was intended to account for system-level and case-mix–related fluctuations, including seasonal respiratory epidemics that may precipitate or exacerbate underlying chronic conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or immunosuppression. This adjustment contributed to improved temporal calibration and more stable SMRs across months, which is particularly relevant for benchmarking purposes, even though expected mortality partly reflects calendar timing; SMRs should therefore be interpreted within a shared seasonal framework rather than as entirely season-neutral measures of performance.

This study has several major strengths. It relies on a nationwide cohort encompassing more than two million ICU stays, ensuring high statistical power and generalizability. Model performance was evaluated not only globally but also at the hospital and diagnostic-subgroup levels, providing a comprehensive appraisal of calibration and between-hospital variability. The augmented model builds upon SAPS II by incorporating its logit (thus ensuring formal recalibration) alongside comorbidities and admission characteristics available in administrative data. Several studies have suggested that the incorporation of comorbidity markers may enhance the performance of mortality prediction scores in the ICU [45–47]. We used the validated Elixhauser classification [31, 48] to describe case-mix, and the model’s incremental benefit over both the original and r-SAPS II models was clearly demonstrated. Importantly, the model excluded variables prone to endogeneity [49], and did not adjust for calendar year, thereby preserving the ability to detect true temporal drifts in performance. Because it relies solely on routinely collected data (e.g., ICD-10 comorbidity codes), it can be readily implemented for benchmarking and longitudinal monitoring without additional data collection (All regression coefficients are provided in eTable 3, enabling local researchers to directly apply the augmented model to their own unit for benchmarking). Although developed using French nationwide data, the augmented model is based on internationally standardized ICD-10 coding and the validated Elixhauser framework, supporting potential applicability in other healthcare systems using comparable administrative data. Nevertheless, external validation and, if necessary, local recalibration remain essential. Although bootstrap analyses suggested minimal statistical overfitting, the structural complexity of the augmented model, including numerous predictors and interactions, may still limit transportability or long-term temporal stability, warranting continued re-validation over time and in other healthcare settings. Given this interaction-rich structure, individual coefficients should not be interpreted in isolation; rather, model adequacy for benchmarking should be judged primarily by calibration performance and reduction in unexplained between-hospital heterogeneity.

Limitations

Several limitations should also be noted. First, calibration within specific clinical subgroups was imperfect, although perfect individual prediction is unrealistic [50]. We consider the observed reduction in between-hospital heterogeneity to be a more relevant indicator of model adequacy for benchmarking. Second, the inclusion of the month of admission to capture seasonality may limit generalizability to Southern Hemisphere settings, where coefficients would need adaptation. Third, as the French PMSI database does not record patient ethnicity, we could not assess its potential contribution as a socioeconomic or physiological modifier [51]—an issue warranting investigation in other health systems. Fourth, some clinically distinct populations, including post–cardiac surgery patients, extreme age strata, planned versus unplanned admissions, or patients admitted during the COVID-19 pandemic, may require tailored prognostic approaches. However, excessive subgroup fragmentation may compromise the stability and interpretability of hospital-level benchmarking. Dedicated studies focusing on these populations are therefore an important direction for future research. Fifth, frailty and functional status at admission, which are important determinants of outcome in critically ill patients, could not be incorporated into the model because such information is not routinely or reliably captured in the PMSI database; inclusion of these factors might further improve model performance but raises additional interpretative challenges. Sixth, our study relies on administrative and coding data, the accuracy and completeness of which cannot be fully ascertained. However, missed diagnoses and comorbidities have not been reported to be frequent in previous evaluations of similar databases [52]. Seventh, although the impact of ICU admissions solely related to organ donation on SMRs has been discussed in the literature [53], this could not be specifically evaluated in our study, as our database does not allow differentiation between these patients and those who develop brain death during their ICU stay. Finally, benchmarking was conducted at the hospital level rather than at the individual ICU level. In institutions hosting multiple ICUs with heterogeneous case-mix or organizational structures, hospital-level aggregation may mask internal variation. However, because all model coefficients are provided, performance metrics such as SMRs can be calculated at the individual ICU level where local data permit more granular assessment.

Conclusions

This large-scale national study showed that augmenting SAPS II with routinely available comorbidity information meaningfully improved both discrimination and calibration of hospital mortality prediction among ICU patients. The augmented model reduced unexplained hospital-level heterogeneity, and provides a practical, easily deployable tool for more robust and calibrated benchmarking of ICU outcomes across hospitals and over time.
