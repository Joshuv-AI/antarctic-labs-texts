Effect of a 45S5 bioactive glass–containing gel on the remineralization of artificial caries-like lesions in primary teeth: an in vitro study
Authors: Bilge Akarca Ulgen, Ali Can Özarslan, Ceren Emir, Sevil Yücel, Pınar Kınay Taran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis in vitro study investigated the remineralization of artificial caries-like lesions in primary teeth using a multimodal microscopy approach. Micro- and nano-sized 45S5 bioactive glass (BAG) gels were compared with fluoride varnish in terms of surface morphology and volumetric mineral density changes.MethodsEnamel–dentin blocks prepared from extracted human primary molars and incisors were demineralized and randomly assigned to three treatment groups (n = 10): fluoride varnish, micro-sized BAG gel, and nano-sized BAG gel. Following treatment, all specimens were subjected to a 6-day pH-cycling protocol. Surface characteristics and elemental composition were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS), while three-dimensional mineral density (MD) distributions were quantified using micro-computed tomography (µCT). MD differences within and between groups were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsSEM/EDS analysis demonstrated that both BAG gels produced relatively uniform superficial reaction products compared to fluoride varnish. However, µCT measurements revealed significantly higher mineral density in the fluoride-treated specimens (1.39 ± 0.10 g/cm3) than in the MicroBAG (1.09 ± 0.21 g/cm3) and NanoBAG groups (0.98 ± 0.16 g/cm3) (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe combined findings highlight a discrepancy between surface-level observations and subsurface mineral recovery, emphasizing the importance of volumetric µCT analysis when assessing remineralization efficacy. Fluoride achieved higher volumetric mineral density than BAG gels. This study demonstrates the value of integrating surface and volumetric microscopy techniques for the accurate evaluation of mineralized dental tissues.Keywords: Bioactive glass, Initial enamel caries, Primary tooth, Remineralization, Fluoride

Background

Dental caries occurs when the balance of demineralization and remineralization in the oral environment is disturbed, resulting in mineral loss from the tooth surface. This imbalance causes changes in the morphology and chemical structure of enamel [1]. The traditional restoration method to treat caries in primary teeth can be difficult, especially in patients who are not compatible with treatment. In the early stages of caries, non-invasive treatment may be a better choice to reverse the demineralization process. Many mineralization agents have emerged in recent years to preserve enamel integrity and prevent the formation of cavitations [2].

Fluoride is a well-established and widely used remineralizing agent in caries management due to its ability to enhance enamel resistance to acid dissolution and promote mineral deposition [3]. During the remineralization process, fluoride ions facilitate the precipitation of calcium and phosphate ions from saliva into demineralized enamel, leading to the formation of fluorapatite or fluoridated hydroxyapatite, which is less soluble in acidic environments. However, this fluoride-induced remineralization predominantly occurs at the superficial layer of the caries lesion, potentially limiting mineral diffusion into deeper lesion areas [4].

Although fluoride is highly effective at low concentrations, the use of high-fluoride products has been associated with potential adverse effects, including dental fluorosis, enamel mottling, and concerns regarding systemic fluoride intake, particularly in young children [3, 4]. Moreover, excessive surface remineralization may result in the formation of a hypermineralized surface layer that restricts further ion penetration, thereby preventing complete subsurface lesion remineralization [5]. Consequently, while fluoride-mediated remineralization remains the cornerstone of contemporary caries management, the need for deeper and more homogeneous remineralization, together with the potential risks associated with high-fluoride exposure, has driven the development of alternative remineralization strategies [2, 5].

45S5 bioactive glass (BAG), first introduced by Larry Hench, is a biocompatible material capable of interacting with body fluids and bonding to both hard and soft tissues through a well-established surface reaction mechanism [6–8]. Upon exposure to physiological fluids, network-modifying cations such as sodium and calcium are leached from the glass surface, leading to the formation of a hydrated silica-rich gel layer. This silica-rich layer provides favorable nucleation sites for the subsequent precipitation of a hydroxyapatite-like layer from calcium and phosphate ions present in the surrounding biological fluids, enabling chemical bonding to mineralized tissues [7, 8]. BAG is a multicomponent inorganic material composed primarily of sodium, calcium, phosphorus, and silica, commonly referred to as sodium–calcium phosphosilicate [9]. Through these surface reactions, BAG has demonstrated the ability to neutralize acidic environments and promote the localized accumulation of calcium and phosphate ions, facilitating the formation of hydroxyapatite under physiological conditions. Owing to this biomimetic mineralization process, BAG is considered a promising agent for enamel remineralization [10].

BAG has been used in many areas of dentistry in recent years, thanks to its biocompatibility with body tissues [10, 11]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of BAG in promoting remineralization of enamel and dentin [12, 13]. It has been reported to exhibit low cytotoxicity toward dental pulp cells, and its intraoral antimicrobial activity has also been confirmed [13, 14].

Studies on the remineralization effect of BAG in dental tissues have mainly focused on dentin and permanent tooth enamel. Wu et al. stated that BAG-containing paste can improve microhardness in dentin and promote mineral deposition in the superficial layer of demineralized dentin [9]. Similarly, Abbassy et al. reported that BAG-containing paste successfully reduced the lesion depth and mineral loss in the permanent enamel [13]. However, there are still evidence-based deficiencies regarding the efficacy of BAG on primary teeth. In addition, commercially available BAG-containing toothpastes used in previous studies typically contain additional active ingredients, such as fluoride and other remineralizing agents, whose independent contributions cannot be easily distinguished from the effects of BAG itself, thereby limiting the interpretability of findings from such formulations [14–16].

It has been reported that as a result of the interaction of 45S5 BAG glass with phosphoric acid, a layer rich in calcium and phosphate named the ‘interaction layer’ is formed on the dentin surfaces [17]. The penetration of high calcium and phosphate ions released from BAG paste after contact with dentin may suggest that these calcium and phosphate ions may have the ability to penetrate and remineralize sub-surface demineralized enamel.

In vitro models for creating artificial caries-like lesions are widely used in cariology research and have been validated as reliable tools for investigating demineralization and remineralization processes under controlled conditions [18]. Chemical demineralization models, which use acid buffers to simulate the drop in pH below the critical threshold, mimic the caries process and are employed in the vast majority of in vitro remineralization studies [18, 19]. Despite differences in study protocols, micro-computed tomography (µCT)-based mineral density measurements obtained from in vitro pH-cycling models have been shown to produce results comparable to those of in situ studies, supporting the validity of in vitro approaches for evaluating remineralization efficacy [20, 21]. However, it should be acknowledged that in vitro models cannot fully replicate the complexity of the intraoral environment, and calcium, phosphate concentrations and pH values used in vitro are often adjusted to accelerate demineralization compared to the in vivo process [18, 22]. Nevertheless, these models offer the advantage of standardized, reproducible conditions that allow the controlled evaluation of remineralizing agents, free from the confounding variables inherent to clinical or in situ studies.

Particle size is an important determinant of BAG reactivity, as it directly influences the surface area available for ion exchange and hydroxyapatite precipitation. Nano-sized BAG particles offer a substantially greater surface-area-to-volume ratio compared to micro-sized counterparts, which may facilitate more rapid ion release and potentially enhance remineralization kinetics [23].

The aim of the present study was to evaluate the remineralization effect in caries-like lesions artificially created on primary tooth enamel by BAG-containing gel composed of BAG and phosphoric acid or by fluoride varnish. The null hypotheses were that no significant difference would be found in the remineralization effect among the fluoride, micro-sized BAG gel, and nano-sized BAG gel groups.

Methods

Preparation of tooth specimens

The sample size calculation was performed using GPower software, with a significance level of 5% and a test power of 80%. The minimum sample size required was determined to be 10 samples per group. The teeth were collected under the approval of the Institutional Review Board of … (protocol no. 20/373), …. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all pediatric patients whose extracted primary teeth were used in the study.

Thirty extracted human primary molars and incisors that were without caries or restorations, developmental defects, and erosions were used. Roots were separated at the cementoenamel junction using a high-speed diamond disk under water cooling. Then, the crowns were divided into two in the mesiodistal direction with a long-cycle diamond saw under water cooling, and 30 enamel/dentin blocks shaped in a rectangular form (approximately 5 × 5 × 2 mm) were obtained from the buccal/lingual surfaces of each crown.

The samples were embedded in epoxy resin. To expose the enamel surface to be treated and to obtain a flat enamel surface, 500, 1200, and 5000 grit aluminum oxide (Al2O3) abrasive paper discs (DEMPAX, abrasive paper discs, Metkon Instruments, Bursa, Turkey) were used under water cooling. Half of the flat enamel surfaces were covered with two layers of protective nail varnish and served as the intact (sound) enamel reference for subsequent mineral density comparisons. The remaining exposed enamel surfaces were subjected to the demineralization protocol described below, and these surfaces are referred to as ‘demineralized’ throughout the manuscript. Following treatment application and pH cycling, the same surfaces are referred to as ‘remineralized’. The nail varnish was removed prior to µCT scanning to allow simultaneous imaging of all three surface conditions within the same specimen.

All samples were kept in buffered demineralization solution composed of (CaCl2 2.2 mM/L, NaH2PO4 2.2 mM/L, and acetic acid 0.05 M/L, pH 4.4 with KOH 1 M/L) for 72 h in room temperature to form caries lesion [24]. The solution was refreshed at 24-hour intervals for each sample. All the demineralized enamel specimens were randomly assigned into 3 groups having 10 specimens in each group. Group 1 (Fluoride Group), 5% NaF fluoride varnish (ProShield Varnish, PD Dental, Hamburg, Germany) application; Group 2 (MicroBAG Group), micro-sized BAG gel (consisting of a mixture of 0.15 gr 45S5 BAG powder and 0.5 ml 50% phosphoric acid) application; Group 3 (NanoBAG Group), nano-sized BAG gel (consisting of a mixture of 0.15 gr 45S5 BAG powder and 0.5 ml 50% phosphoric acid) application.

Application of fluoride varnish

Fluoride varnish (ProShield Varnish, PD Dental, Hamburg, Germany) containing 5% NaF was applied to the enamel surface of the primary tooth with artificial caries for 5 min with its applicator. After fluoride application, the enamel surfaces were washed off with deionized water.

Preparation and application of micro-sized BAG gel

Micro-sized 45S5 BAG (BONE-G Active Ref: Bga025.05/2, Meta Biomühendislik ve Arge Hizmetleri A.Ş., Istanbul, Turkey) composed of 24.5 wt% Na2O, 24.5wt% CaO, 6wt% P2O5, and 45wt% SiO2 was obtained by the melt-derived method. Production of melt-derived 45S5 BAG was detailed in previous studies [25]. 0.15-gram micro-sized BAG powder (average particle size < 100 μm) was mixed on a glass slab by a spatula with 0.5 mL of 50wt% phosphoric acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to form into a gel, as previously described by Bakry et al. [26]. lt was applied to the demineralized enamel surfaces of the samples by a micro brush. A layer of bonding agent (Clearfil SE Bond, Kuraray Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was applied over the BAG-containing acidic gel and then light-cured.

Preparation and application of nano-sized BAG gel

Nano-sized 45S5 BAG (Yıldız Technical University, Faculty of Chemistry, Metallurgy, Department of Bioengineering) composed of 24.4 wt% Na2O, 26.9wt% CaO, 2.6wt% P2O5, and 46.1wt% SiO2 was obtained by the sol-gel method. The nano-sized BAG particles (90–110 nm) and the micro-sized BAG particles described above (< 100 μm) differ substantially in scale; accordingly, units appropriate to each size range have been used throughout. Production of sol-gel 45S5 BAG was detailed in previous studies [27]. 0.15-gram nano-sized BAG powder was mixed on a glass slab by a spatula with 0.5 mL of 50wt% phosphoric acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to form into a gel. lt was applied to the demineralized enamel surfaces of the samples by a micro brush. A layer of bonding agent (Clearfil SE Bond, Kuraray Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was applied over the BAG-containing acidic gel and then light-cured.

pH cycling

A 6-day pH cycle procedure was applied to all the samples. Each cycle included 6 h of demineralization and 17 h of remineralization per day. The content of the demineralizing solution was 1.5 mM CaCl2, 0.9 mM KH2PO4, and 50 mM acetic acid, pH 5.0 whereas the remineralizing solution consisted of 1.5 mM CaCl2, 0.9 mM KH2PO4, 130 mM KCl, and 100 mM Tris, pH 7.0. At the beginning of each daily cycle, specimens were rinsed with distilled water, after which the remineralizing agents were freshly prepared and applied. Fluoride varnish was applied to the enamel surface for one minute, after which excess varnish was removed. For the MicroBAG and NanoBAG groups, freshly prepared BAG gel was applied to the enamel surface, followed immediately by application and light-curing of the bonding agent (Clearfil SE Bond, Kuraray Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), which served to retain the gel on the enamel surface throughout the entire cycle. Specimens were then transferred to the demineralization solution for 6 h, followed by 17 h in the remineralization solution, providing approximately 23 h of sustained BAG-enamel contact per cycle.

Micro-computed tomography (µCT) evaluation

Each specimen was scanned using a high-resolution µCT device (SkyScan 1174v2, Kontich, Antwerp, Belgium) at 89 kV and 112 µA, with a rotation step of 0.7°, random movement set to 20, and a frame average of 5, using an Al + Cu filter at 1 K. The scanning was performed with a voxel size of 12.5 μm and an exposure time of 2600 ms. The acquired projection images were reconstructed into three-dimensional datasets using NRecon software (Ver. 1.6.10.2, Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium), and horizontal cross-sections were saved in BMP format. During reconstruction, calibration drift correction was automatically applied to ensure the stability and consistency of the grayscale data. Mineral density (MD) calibration was performed using hydroxyapatite phantom rods of known density (0.25 and 0.75 g/cm³), and grayscale values were converted to MD values (g/cm³) accordingly. Quantitative MD analysis was performed using CTAn software (Ver. 1.16.4.1+, Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium). For image segmentation and MD analysis, an adaptive thresholding range of 0.86 to 3.10 g/cm³ was consistently applied to isolate the regions of interest. For each specimen, a circular region of interest (ROI) was delineated semi-automatically on the horizontal plane of the cross-sections. The ROI was determined once following demineralization and the same ROI was applied consistently for post-treatment scans of the same specimen to ensure spatial comparability across intact, demineralized, and remineralized conditions. ROI selection and all µCT measurements were performed by an examiner blinded to treatment group allocation. Three-dimensional modeling images were obtained using CTVol software (Ver. 2.3.2.0, Bruker microCT, Kontich, Belgium). MD values were expressed as relative mineral density measurements to allow comparison between experimental conditions.

Scanning electron microscopy/ energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (SEM/EDS) analysis

For SEM/EDS analysis, separate specimens representing intact, demineralized, and remineralized surface conditions were selected from each group. Before analysis, all samples were dehydrated and gold-coated in an airless environment. The elemental composition of intact, demineralized, and remineralized enamel surfaces was analyzed using SEM/EDS (Cressington Sputter Coater 108 auto, Cressington Scientific Instruments, Watford, UK). In addition, SEM (EVO LS10, Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany) images were obtained with 12 kV at x1000 and x2500 magnifications. EDS analysis was carried out with 12 kV at x1000 magnification, by measuring from 3 points determined from the sample surface. All SEM/EDS analyses were performed by an examiner blinded to treatment group allocation.

Statistical analysis

Means and standard deviations for the MD scores of enamel surfaces were calculated using SPSS 23.0 program (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-way analysis of variance was used to compare MD values within and between groups. Tukey test was used in multiple comparisons. Evaluation of the results was set at p < 0.05 significance level.

Discussion

The null hypothesis of the present study was rejected. No significant difference was found between the MicroBAG and NanoBAG groups (p = 0.871); however, fluoride varnish demonstrated significantly higher volumetric mineral density than both micro- and nano-sized BAG gels following the pH-cycling protocol (p < 0.001). This study demonstrates that surface-based microscopy alone may overestimate enamel remineralization. Although SEM/EDS revealed superficial reaction products following BAG application, µCT showed that these changes did not correspond to effective subsurface mineral recovery. The discrepancy between surface morphology and volumetric mineral density highlights the limitations of relying solely on surface-sensitive techniques. Therefore, integrating volumetric imaging methods such as µCT with conventional microscopy is essential for accurately assessing remineralization, particularly in primary enamel.

Although BAG has been reported to promote enamel remineralization under various experimental conditions, the contrasting volumetric deficit observed in our study may be attributed to several factors. First, the acidic nature of the BAG–phosphoric acid gel, while facilitating ion release and surface reactions, may have contributed to additional enamel dissolution, particularly in the relatively thin and less mineralized enamel of primary teeth [26]. The role of the acidic vehicle in the observed mineral loss warrants further elaboration. Upon application, the gel may have caused immediate acid etching of the enamel surface before the BAG-mediated ion-release and self-neutralization mechanism could take effect. Furthermore, although Bakry et al. [26] reported that the pH of the gel rises to approximately 4 within ten minutes of application, a pH of 4 remains below the critical pH for enamel dissolution (approximately 5.5), suggesting that prolonged exposure to the gel — even after partial neutralization — may have continued to drive mineral loss rather than facilitate remineralization. This effect may have been compounded by the repeated daily reapplication of freshly prepared gel throughout the six-day pH-cycling protocol, subjecting the enamel to recurrent acid challenges in addition to those imposed by the demineralization solution. Second, under the standardized pH-cycling conditions employed in this study, which are widely used to simulate the dynamic demineralization and remineralization cycles of the oral environment [19], the ion-releasing capacity of BAG may have been insufficient to counteract the repeated acid challenges, particularly given the additional dissolution burden imposed by the acidic gel vehicle itself. Notably, the same pH-cycling model produced effective remineralization in the fluoride group, suggesting that the model itself was not the limiting factor, but rather the remineralization potential of the BAG formulation under these conditions. In contrast to fluoride, which rapidly promotes the formation of acid-resistant mineral phases at the enamel surface [3, 5], BAG-mediated mineralization is time-dependent and relies on the sustained formation and maturation of a calcium phosphate layer, requiring hours to initiate stable bioactivity and crystal formation [10, 17, 26, 28, 29]. Collectively, the superficial reaction products formed on the surface may have remained insufficiently integrated into the enamel subsurface to produce a measurable increase in mineral density by µCT, which may explain why the BAG-containing gels did not exhibit the expected remineralization efficacy under the experimental conditions of the present study. It should also be noted that the acidic gel formulation may have inherently disadvantaged BAG relative to the neutral fluoride varnish, as the low-pH vehicle introduced an additional demineralization burden not present in the comparator group. Future studies employing neutral BAG delivery systems would allow a more unbiased assessment of BAG’s intrinsic remineralization potential.

The structural and compositional differences between primary and permanent enamel may further explain the observed outcomes. Primary enamel is significantly thinner than permanent enamel (approximately 1.14 mm vs. 2.58 mm) and exhibits lower calcium and phosphorus content, reflecting a reduced overall degree of mineralization [30]. The mineral content of primary enamel has been reported to range between 81.3 and 94.2 wt%, whereas permanent enamel approaches approximately 97% mineral content [31]. Furthermore, primary enamel contains a higher numerical density of enamel rods and a greater organic matrix fraction, which may influence its susceptibility to acid dissolution and its capacity for ion uptake during remineralization [30, 31]. These characteristics render primary enamel inherently more vulnerable to the acidic BAG–phosphoric acid gel formulation compared to permanent enamel, on which the protocol was originally developed and validated. Since the majority of existing BAG remineralization studies have been conducted on permanent tooth enamel or dentin [8, 17, 26, 32], evidence regarding its efficacy on primary teeth remains limited, and the interpretability of studies using commercial BAG formulations is further confounded by the presence of additional active ingredients such as fluoride and other remineralizing agents, whose independent contributions cannot be easily distinguished from those of BAG itself [8, 13–16]. There are only a few studies specifically addressing primary teeth: Zhang et al. reported a promising remineralization effect of BAG on artificial lesions in deciduous enamel [33], while Benjasuwantep et al. demonstrated remineralization potential of BAG-containing toothpaste on primary teeth, albeit with lower efficacy than 1000 ppm fluoride-containing toothpaste [34].

In the present study, a pH cycle was applied to mimic the daily pH change of the oral environment in accordance with Buzalaf et al.’s study [19]. The samples were subjected to a series of demineralization and remineralization to simulate the dynamics of mineral changes in caries formation in the pH cycle protocol. Calcium and phosphate ions released from the BAG-containing paste can penetrate below the surface of the initial enamel carious lesions [17, 26]. To ensure sufficient BAG-enamel contact time consistent with these requirements, remineralization agents were freshly prepared and applied once daily at the beginning of each pH cycle. It was shown that the MD values were lower than the MD values of the intact enamel after the application of BAG-containing gels in MicroBAG and NanoBAG groups. Notably, the µCT data revealed that in both MicroBAG and NanoBAG groups, the MD values of remineralized surfaces were not only lower than intact enamel, but also significantly lower than demineralized surfaces prior to treatment (MicroBAG: 1.09 ± 0.21 vs. 1.33 ± 0.12 g/cm³; NanoBAG: 0.98 ± 0.16 vs. 1.23 ± 0.12 g/cm³), which is consistent with the µCT images of both groups. These findings should not be interpreted as a general property of BAG as a material, but rather as an outcome specific to this formulation and experimental design.

The remineralizing efficacy of fluoride and BAG-containing gels was evaluated with µCT on demineralized primary tooth enamel in the present study. This method has been used to measure the mineral density changes in many previous in vitro studies [20, 21]. µCT has the advantage of being non-destructive and enabling high-resolution three-dimensional (3D) analysis, visualizing the bulk internal structure of the sample. In addition, the elemental composition and surface morphology of enamel surfaces were evaluated with SEM/EDS analysis. Although SEM images revealed a relatively homogeneous layer on the BAG-treated enamel surfaces, µCT images clearly exhibited eroded surfaces consistent with the dissolution effects of the low-pH gel on the structurally vulnerable primary enamel. Therefore, the SEM/EDS findings were used to support surface-related observations rather than to draw quantitative conclusions regarding bulk enamel remineralization, and these findings further underscore the need for future studies specifically designed to evaluate BAG-based formulations on primary tooth enamel.

The findings of the present study demonstrate that the BAG–phosphoric acid gel formulation, as investigated under the current experimental conditions, is not suitable for the remineralization of primary tooth enamel. The acidic nature of the formulation, combined with the structural vulnerability of primary enamel, resulted in further mineral loss rather than mineral recovery. Substantial reformulation, including reduction of acid concentration, elimination of the bonding agent requirement, and evaluation in neutral or buffered delivery systems, would be necessary before any clinical application could be considered.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. SEM/EDS analysis provided mainly qualitative and surface-specific information; moreover, separate analyses of untreated enamel and region-specific EDS measurements could not be performed. Methodologically, the absence of both a demineralized untreated control group and a phosphoric acid-only control limits the ability to clearly distinguish between active demineralization caused by the gel, inadequate remineralization, and the overall effects of the pH-cycling model. In addition, the inclusion of a bonding agent in the BAG groups, whereas no bonding agent was used in the fluoride group, introduced an additional source of asymmetry, as the polymerized resin layer may have partially restricted ion diffusion into the enamel subsurface. The acidic nature of the BAG–phosphoric acid gel may have also placed the BAG groups at an inherent disadvantage compared with the neutral fluoride varnish. Furthermore, because the formulation investigated in this study was experimental, direct comparison with commercially available remineralization products should be made cautiously. Finally, the inclusion of both primary molars and incisors without separate subgroup analyses may have contributed to variability in mineral density measurements. Future studies should include a demineralized untreated control, a phosphoric acid-only control, and a BAG formulation without a bonding agent. Evaluating BAG in neutral or near-neutral delivery systems would also help provide a more rigorous assessment of its intrinsic remineralization potential.

Conclusions

Fluoride varnish showed superior remineralization of demineralized primary enamel compared to both micro- and nano-sized BAG gels. Although BAG gels formed surface deposits, they did not effectively increase mineral density. Nano-sized BAG produced a more homogeneous layer than micro-sized BAG, but this did not improve remineralization. These findings suggest that, under the conditions tested, fluoride remains the most effective non-invasive option for treating early enamel lesions in primary teeth. The results apply specifically to the experimental acidic BAG–phosphoric acid gel formulation and should not be extrapolated to commercially available BAG-containing products or neutral BAG systems. Future studies should evaluate neutral or buffered BAG formulations, clinically applicable delivery systems, and long-term ion release behavior.
