Small extracellular vesicle-integrated by herbal hydrogels for spatiotemporal immunomodulation and neurovascular repair following traumatic brain injury
Authors: Yao Wu, Yuanyuan Sun, Jingjing Chen, Mingrui Hu, Xindi Zhang, Xinyu Xiong, Zhe Yu, Xiya Yang, Hui Li, Yang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstrctTraumatic brain injury (TBI) causes acute neuronal and vascular damage accompanied by intense neuroinflammation, yet current surgical and pharmacological interventions yield limited long-term benefits. Embryonic stem cell-derived small extracellular vesicles (ESC-sEV) carry potent pro-repair signals but suffer from poor brain targeting and rapid clearance in the acute inflammatory window. To address these critical limitations, we engineered an injectable ESC-sEV–glycyrrhizic acid (GA) co-assembled hydrogel (EG-gel) in which sEVs act as a functional gel factor interpenetrating GA nanoscaffolds. GA molecules were self-assembled into nanoscaffolds via hydrogen bonding, with polar head groups coordinating to sEV membranes while hydrophobic cores insert into lipid bilayers, yielding a robust, hierarchical matrix. EG-gel exhibited brain-compatible mechanical properties, rapid self-healing, shear-thinning injectability, and strong tissue adhesion, which collectively enhance local sEV accumulation at the lesion site. In a mouse TBI model, the EG-gel showed superior neuroprotective effects and functional recovery outcomes compared with the GA-gel. Transcriptomics combined with experimental validation confirmed a spatiotemporal synergistic mechanism: GA mediated early inflammatory suppression and immune microenvironment stabilization, while co-assembled sEVs drove angiogenesis and neuronal repair. Therefore, the EG-gel played a synergistic role in establishing a sequential “first anti-inflammatory, then vaso-neural regeneration” microenvironment, thereby promoting neuroprotection after TBI. This work highlights the EG-gel as an up-and-coming candidate for translational therapy in TBI.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major global public-health and clinical challenge [1,2]. In the acute window, primary mechanical insult produces widespread neuronal loss and axonal disruption, together with vascular injury, blood-brain barrier breakdown, and focal hemorrhage. This rapidly provokes intense neuroinflammation that amplifies secondary damage and accelerates functional decline [3,4]. Because the central nervous system has limited regenerative capacity and the pathology is multi-targeted and time-dependent, current surgical and pharmacological interventions remain insufficient to restore long-term neurological function [3,5]. There is therefore an urgent need for integrated strategies that combine inflammation control, neuroprotection, and structural repair. Stem-cell therapy has attracted considerable attention in neural repair due to its regenerative potential and multimodal mechanisms. And several cell therapies have advanced into late-stage clinical trials, fuelling substantial industrial investment and market expectations [[6], [7], [8]]. Media-level “celebrity effects” have further amplified public and capital interest (for example, press reports concerning Rafael Nadal and Michael J. Fox), accelerating clinical and commercial momentum. Stem cell types are diverse and include broadly applied mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) as well as pluripotent embryonic stem cells (ESCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), and neural stem cells (NSCs). Compared to MSCs, ESCs have significant advantages in terms of in vitro proliferation rate, yield, and abundance of secreted factor profiles. Therefore, they show higher potential for use in indications that require large-scale tissue regeneration and vascular repair, such as TBI. However, direct clinical application of ESCs is constrained by immune rejection, poor lesion retention, uncontrolled differentiation, tumorigenic risk, and ethical concerns [9].

To address safety and controllability, ESCs-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEV) have emerged as an acellular alternative that enables functional translation of stem-cell biology [10]. The sEVs carry miRNAs, mRNAs, and proteins, exhibit low immunogenicity and nanoscale tissue penetration, and are amenable to scalable production and quality control [11,12]. They have demonstrated anti-inflammatory and reparative potential in multiple in vivo models. Nevertheless, in the acute phase of TBI, the high concentration of inflammatory mediators and activated phagocytes significantly accelerates the clearance of sEVs, thereby weakening their immunomodulatory and reparative effects. At the same time, it is difficult to maintain sufficient enrichment of sEVs in the lesion by conventional routes of administration, resulting in a significant decrease in the local duration of action and the therapeutic efficacy of sEVs [13,14]. Consequently, single-agent sEV delivery strategies are severely limited during the acute inflammatory window.

Materials engineering offers a feasible route to enhance sEV bioavailability in the lesion [[15], [16], [17]]. Although conventional polymeric hydrogels can provide local loading and sustained release, some synthetic systems exhibit slow degradation, poor biocompatibility, and potential brain toxicity, which limit their translational potential [18,19]. Critically, without concurrent modulation of local inflammation, sEV cannot exert maximal therapeutic effects. Therefore, an optimal local delivery system must be able to suppress focal inflammation early to improve the tissue microenvironment. It should sustain the release of functional sEV under conditions of relatively diminished inflammation to promote neurorepair and angiogenesis.

On this rationale, we developed an sEV-glycyrrhizic acid (GA) co-assembled hydrogel, in which sEVs act as functional gel components and co-assemble with GA to form a cooperative delivery matrix. In this system, GA provided early anti-inflammatory action to rapidly suppress focal inflammation and improve the immune microenvironment [20], thereby creating a favorable biological window. Thereafter, encapsulated sEVs were released in a sustained manner to drive angiogenesis, support neuronal survival, and promote synaptic/axonal reconstruction. Thus, the EG-gel implemented a time-sequenced “first anti-inflammatory, then vaso-neural regeneration” strategy (Fig. 1). First, we reported the formulation and structural-functional properties. Subsequently, on this basis, we evaluated the efficacy of the EG-gel in promoting inflammatory regression, prolonging sEV retention in the brain, and improving tissue structural and functional recovery in a TBI mouse model. Finally, this study aimed to propose a material-biology synergistic strategy, thus providing new therapeutic ideas for the clinical translation of TBI.Fig. 1Diagrammatic workflow for the synthesis and application of EG-gel. Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of sEV and EG-gel

We collected embryonic stem cell conditioned medium after 48–72 h, cleared cells and large debris by centrifugation at 500×g for 5 min and 2500×g for 20 min, and then concentrated the supernatant using a 100 kDa ultrafiltration device. We filtered it through a 0.22 μm filter. We pelleted embryonic stem cell sEV (ESC-sEV) by ultracentrifugation at 120,000×g for 120 min at 4 °C, washed the crude pellet in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and re-pelleted at 120,000×g for 90 min at 4 °C (Fig. 2A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed uniform, cup‐shaped vesicles of 40–150 nm (Fig. 2B), and dynamic light scattering measured an average hydrodynamic diameter of 152.1 ± 20.06 nm (Fig. 2C). Combining the excellent bioactivities of ESC-small extracellular vesicles (sEV) and glycyrrhizic acid (GA), we created a multifunctional EG-gel by co-assembly between sEV and GA. We first prepared EG-gel by dissolving GA in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.34) at 80 °C, cooling the solution to 37 °C, and then adding sEV. Upon returning to room temperature, the mixture was homogenized to form a transparent EG-gel (Fig. 2E and F). The sEV accounted for 1/4 of the total volume of the EG-gel. As shown in Fig. S1A, the EG-gel maintained a homogeneous and transparent appearance over 7 days of storage at 4 °C. Consistently, the Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectra showed that the characteristic absorption profiles of EG-gel remained essentially unchanged after storage at 4 °C for 7 days (Fig. S1B), with no apparent peak shift or intensity variation. This indicated that the EG-gel was optically stable and showed no obvious aggregation or precipitation during short-term storage. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of EG-gel revealed a densely interconnected nanofibrillar 3D network (Fig. 2G), whereas GA-gel exhibited a comparatively sparse framework (Fig. S1C), indicating that sEV incorporation markedly promoted GA supramolecular assembly and improved network density and structural stability.

To validate the self-assembly mechanism of EG-gel, we performed a systematic analysis using multiple spectroscopic and structural characterization methods. Firstly, the Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy revealed that the characteristic vibration of GA at 548.17 cm−1 was shifted to 525.51 cm−1 in the EG-gel (Fig. 2H). This indicated that the backbone vibrations in the supramolecular network were restricted [21]. Crucially, the emergence of a peak at 1383.68 cm−1 corresponds to the symmetric stretching of carboxylate groups (Fig. 2H). It suggested that the carboxylic acid moieties of GA participate in hydrogen bonding and potential electrostatic interactions with the amino acid residues on the sEV surface [22]. Next, in the Raman spectrum, GA exhibited a pronounced C–H stretching vibration at 2907.23 cm−1. Upon the formation of the EG-gel, this peak underwent a significant shift to 2929.54 cm−1 (Fig. 2I). Such a substantial shift typically indicated a transition of the GA triterpenoid skeleton from a relatively disordered state to a more highly ordered hydrophobic environment [23]. This may be due to the intercalation of GA molecules into the sEV lipid bilayers. In addition, a new resonance at 1659.51 cm−1 in the EG-gel showed vibrational coupling between the GA carbonyl groups and the amide I regions of sEV membrane proteins [24]. We further analyzed the UV-Vis spectroscopy (Fig. 2J). The π-π absorption peak of GA was shifted from 253 nm to 252 nm with a twofold increase in absorbance, suggesting tighter aggregation of GA in EG-gel [25]. As shown in Fig. S1D, the 1H NMR spectrum revealed that the hydrophobic triterpene nucleus of GA was shielded in the EG-gel at δ ≈ 0–1.3 ppm and overlapped with the exosomal lipid signals, indicating hydrophobic stacking of triterpene nucleus. Meanwhile, the sugar/carboxyl terminus exhibited shift and broadening in the δ ≈ 3.2–5.0 ppm region, demonstrating that these hydrophilic groups coordinate with the sEV membrane headgroup/proteins via hydrogen bonding and proton exchange [26]. Finally, X-ray Powder Diffraction (XRD) patterns showed that all GA crystalline peaks (12.2°, 14.6°, 19.7°, 23.4°) vanish in EG-gel, while sEV lamellar (27.4°) and wide-angle (31.7°, 45.4°) reflections persist with only modest intensity loss (Fig. 2K). This confirmed the transformation of GA molecules into amorphous nanoscaffolds [27] that encapsulates and maintains the ordered structure of the sEV membrane [28].

We proposed that during the cooling of the EG-gel, GA molecules may co-assemble into nanoscaffolds via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Subsequently, GA may embed into sEV through interactions between polar head groups and the insertion of its hydrophobic cores into the lipid bilayer. This process drove the formation of the 3D network of the EG-gel (Fig. S1E).

Gelation properties of EG-gel

We prepared three EG-gels with different ratios, with sEV constituting 1/2, 1/4, and 1/8 of the total EG-gel volume, respectively. We evaluated their viscoelastic properties by strain‐sweep rheology. The 1/8 EG-gel exhibited a high storage modulus (G′ ≈ 1700 Pa) but failed at a critical strain (γ_c) of only 6.3% (Fig. S2E), indicating brittle fracture under minimal deformation. As shown in Fig. S2F, the 1/2 EG-gel showed moderate stiffness (G′ ≈ 380 Pa) yet similarly low strain tolerance (γ_c = 5.9%), rendering it unable to accommodate physiological movements. In contrast, the G′ value of 1/4 EG-gel was approximately 350 Pa (Fig. 3A), closely matching the 0.1–1 kPa stiffness range of native brain tissue [29]. Furthermore, this hydrogel exhibited an outstanding γ_c value of 53.3%, well above cardiac-induced brain pulsations (typically sub-percent to a few percent) and exceeding reported axonal-injury thresholds (7–15%), indicating ample mechanical margin for physiological and surgical deformations [[30], [31], [32]]. We further tested the self-healing capability of EG-gel through the step-strain experiments. As illustrated in Fig. 3B and Fig. S2G and S2H, after four consecutive cycles of low strain (0.5%) and high strain (50%), the G′ values of all three EG-gel ratios fully recovered within seconds, demonstrating the rapid self-healing ability of EG-gel. Time‐sweep and frequency‐sweep tests confirmed that both G′ and G″ of the EG-gel remained essentially constant under physiological dynamic conditions (Fig. 3D and E). Next, we measured the flow curve of the EG-gel. The results showed that the viscosity decreased by over an order of magnitude as the shear rate increased (Fig. 3C). This indicated that the EG-gel had significant shear‐thinning behavior, further ensuring smooth injection through fine‐gauge needles without clogging or damaging surrounding tissue. Taken together, 1/4 EG-gel can withstand the large deformations encountered during syringe delivery, self‐recover to maintain local mechanical support, and match the compliance of neural tissue to minimize secondary injury. We then evaluated the injectability, adhesion, hemostatic properties, and in vitro release and degradation profiles of the EG-gel to assess its suitability for repairing TBI. In shear-thinning assays, we loaded EG-gel into a standard syringe and observed that simple manual extrusion could repeatedly print a variety of complex three-dimensional (3D) architectures. Shown in Fig. 3F and Fig. S2A, we successfully fabricated numerals (“123”), geometric shapes (square, heart, circle), and letters, all maintaining their structural integrity immediately after injection. The formation of these injectable patterns was attributed to the shear-thinning properties of EG-gel. EG-gel demonstrated its ability to fill irregular cavities conformally, a crucial property for treating the uneven surfaces characteristic of TBI. Next, we assessed the adhesion of the gel by first preparing the EG-gel on a transparent culture dish and then gently inverting the substrate. The EG-gel remained firmly attached without slippage or detachment (Fig. S2B). Similarly, when applied to freshly excised mouse organs (brain, heart, lung, liver, kidney, spleen), EG-gel also adhered to tissue surfaces and bridged adjacent organs together (Fig. 3G). The adhesive performance of EG-gel likely arise from a combination of multivalent hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions between polar headgroups of the GA and tissue proteins [33], and the increased contact area provided by the self-assembled nanoscaffold [34]. Together, these results validated the excellent injectability of EG-gel, enabling precise, low-force delivery via syringe, and demonstrated good adhesion, ensuring stable retention at the injury site. In addition, in the in vitro hemostasis test, the blood in the blank control remained uncoagulated and free-flowing. However, after 5 min of contact with the EG-gel, the blood coagulated and retained its shape even in the inverted state (Fig. S2C). Concurrently, EG-gel markedly reduced the blood clotting index compared with the blank control (Fig. S2D). These data indicated that EG-gel had a hemostatic effect.

Furthermore, we investigated the in vitro release and degradation behavior of the EG-gel. The slow-release experiments showed that GA was released rapidly within 3 days, followed by a slow-release phase, and the cumulative release was nearly complete by day 7. While sEV was released at a much slower and sustained rate with a steady increase in cumulative release over 7 days (Fig. 3H). Also, the EG-gel was largely degraded within a week (Fig. 3I), reducing the risk of long-term retention with chronic foreign body reactions. In conclusion, the EG-gel combined rheological, adhesive, and hemostatic properties, as well as predictable in vitro release and timely degradation, making it an ideal candidate material for scaffold-based compliant therapies in TBI repair.

To further test the suitability of the EG-gel for TBI, we introduced an additional in vivo experiment to evaluate its biodistribution and retention within the brain. We established a controlled cortical impact (CCI) model in C57BL/6 mice to simulate TBI. Using stereotactic techniques, we directly injected DiR-labeled free sEV or DiR-labeled EG-gel into the injured brain regions of the mice (Fig. 3J). In Fig. 3K, mice were imaged by a small-animal in vivo imaging system at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 7 days following injection. Quantitative ROI analysis of cranial images showed that the EG-gel produced a markedly higher and more sustained brain signal than free sEV at every measured time point (Fig. 3L). Overall, these data indicated that the 3D network of EG-gel effectively immobilized and concentrated sEV within the TBI lesion, markedly prolonging local retention and increasing the local payload available for tissue repair.

Overall Efficacy of EG-gel in the TBI mouse model

TBI immediately produces typically irreversible structural damage, such as contusion, hematoma, and diffuse axonal injury. Subsequently, multiple secondary pathologies develop over hours to weeks, including impaired perfusion and disrupted energy metabolism, ion imbalances and excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and a significant inflammatory response [3]. These cascade reactions lead to disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB), cerebral edema, and raised intracranial pressure, ultimately triggering neuronal apoptosis and axonal degeneration [4]. Because an ideal therapeutic hydrogel must do more than provide favorable physicochemical properties, we evaluated whether EG-gel exhibited neuroprotection in a CCI mouse model of TBI via local injection (Fig. 4A). We randomly divided the mice into five groups: Sham, TBI (model), GA-gel, Free-sEV, and EG-gel. All treatments were administered immediately after surgery via local injection into the injury site. At first, we quantified neural dysfunction in mice using a battery of neurobehavioral tests, including the Neurological Severity Score (NSS), the string suspension grip scores, and the foot-fault rate (Fig. 4B–4D). Results suggested that both EG-gel and GA-gel treatments significantly reduced foot-fault rate and NSS on day 3 compared to the TBI group, indicating partial recovery of overall neurological function. However, only the EG-gel group showed improvement in string suspension grip scores and a reduced foot-fault rate on days 3 and 7. In contrast, the Free-sEV group showed no significant improvement on any behavioral measure compared to the TBI group. These behavioral data revealed that the GA-gel had some short-term efficacy. For further exploration of the microscopic effects of EG gel on mouse brain tissue (Fig. S3A), we performed histopathological examinations of the cortical tissue surrounding the TBI lesion. As depicted in Fig. 4E, Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining exhibited disordered neuronal arrangement, nuclear dissolution, and hyperchromasia in the TBI group, whereas only the EG-gel group ameliorated these histological changes. Nissl staining uses basic dyes to stain the rough endoplasmic reticulum in neuronal cell bodies, clearly showing the distribution and morphology of neurons. Fig. 4F and G illustrated that neuronal cell bodies shrank and darkened after TBI, with massive neuronal death. However, following EG-gel treatment, some neurons partially restored normal neuronal morphology, and the number of surviving neurons increased. Thereafter, we assessed apoptosis via TUNEL assays. TBI induced widespread TUNEL+ cells at the lesion border. Free sEV did not reduce the number of apoptotic cells. GA-gel produced a modest, non-significant decrease, but EG-gel significantly and markedly reduced the TUNEL+ cells (Fig. 4H and I, and Figure S3B). This demonstrated that EG-gel possessed a significant anti-apoptotic efficacy. Given the critical role of water homeostasis in post-traumatic swelling, we probed aquaporin-4 (AQP4) expression by immunofluorescence [35]. As shown in Fig. 4H and I and Figure S3B, AQP4 was upregulated in the acute phase after TBI, the EG-gel group reversed this upregulation. It suggested that EG-gel alleviated cytotoxic and vasogenic edema through regulating AQP4 expression and water flux across the BBB. Finally, we assessed BBB integrity in each group by detecting endogenous IgG in the brain parenchyma. Compared with the Sham group, the TBI group produced extensive IgG leakage. But both GA-gel and EG-gel reduced IgG extravasation (Fig. 4J). The finding indicated that gel better preserved the integrity of BBB after TBI, thereby limiting plasma protein infiltration and the secondary inflammatory cascades. In summary, the behavioral, histological, and molecular biological outcomes suggested that EG-gel exerted more neuroprotective effects than GA-gel and FreesEV in TBI mice.

Mechanism-specific prediction of TBI treatment by EG-gel

Glycyrrhizic acid (GA), a naturally occurring anti-inflammatory compound derived from licorice, can ameliorate the inflammatory microenvironment at injury sites [36]. Embryonic stem cell-derived sEV can stimulate endothelial angiogenesis and aid neural repair by modulating neuronal survival, axon growth, and synaptic plasticity pathways [11,37]. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective effects of the EG-gel on TBI, we performed mRNA sequencing of peri-lesional cortex from Sham, TBI, and EG-gel-treated mice on day 3 post-injury. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation among the three groups, indicating distinct transcriptional programs (Fig. 5A). Firstly, we screened differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on the following criteria: fold change ≥1.5, p-value <0.05, and q-value <0.05. The volcano plot (Fig. 5B) showed that 588 DEGs (440 upregulated, 148 downregulated) were identified between the TBI and the Sham, and 815 DEGs (405 upregulated, 310 downregulated) were found between the EG-gel and the TBI. Venn diagrams and heatmap analysis revealed 15 genes that were markedly downregulated following TBI treatment but significantly upregulated after EG-gel treatment. Additionally, 43 genes were upregulated by TBI treatment but markedly downregulated by EG-gel treatment (Fig. 5C and D). Thereafter, we conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Gene Ontology (KEGG) and Genes and Genomes (GO) enrichment analyses of these DEGs to investigate the mechanisms underlying gel therapy for TBI. The evidence demonstrated that pathway and ontology enrichment analyses of these DEGs set revealed three complementary biological axes that collectively explained the therapeutic effect of EG-gel (Fig. 5E–5G, Fig. S4A). First, an anti-inflammatory axis: The EG-gel suppressed TBI-upregulated genes, including Aif1, Trem2, Gpsm3, Hspb1, and Prdx6, which were closely associated with microglial activation, cellular stress, and pro-inflammatory signaling [[38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. KEGG and GO enrichment analyses mapped these changes to the immune system and MAPK signaling pathways, as well as to terms such as regulation of the actin cytoskeleton and dendritic cell differentiation, consistent with the rapid suppression of acute pro-inflammatory cascades. Second, a vascular repair and angiogenesis axis was emerged from the upregulation of genes (such as Mas1, Gucy1a3, Mapk4, and Prkca) on the EG-gel and enrichment in pathways of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling, actin cytoskeleton regulation, platelet activation, and cell migration [[43], [44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]]. These transcriptional signatures implicated that sEV promoted vascular repair and angiogenesis at TBI injury sites by driving endothelial cytoskeletal remodeling, proliferation signaling, and angiogenesis programs. Third, the neural remodeling axis: EG-gel enhanced the expression of genes including Syngap1, Pcdhb17, Syne1, Slc12a5, Stim2, Prkca, and Mapk4, which co-regulate synaptic plasticity, axon-cytoskeletal organization, ion homeostasis, and calcium signaling [46,[51], [52], [53], [54], [55], [56]]. Furthermore, the calcium signaling, gap junctions, GABAergic synapses, and long-term potentiation pathways enriched by the analysis were closely associated with promoting axonal regeneration, synaptic remodeling, and functional network recovery [54,57,58]. Then, we validated the mRNA expression levels of several DEGs identified by transcriptomic screening using qRT-PCR, and the observed expression trends were consistent with the transcriptomic predictions (Fig. S4B). These validation results further support the reliability of the RNA-seq data, indicating that the up-/down-regulation trends of the selected genes were reproducible.

Hence, based on the neuroprotective effects of EG-gel in TBI mice described above and the mRNA transcriptomic data, we hypothesized that EG-gel may exert its effects through multiple complementary pathways: Physically, the gel provided 3D mechanical support at the injury site. Biologically, GA primarily mediated early anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating pro-inflammatory/stress genes such as Aif1, Trem2, Gpsm3, and Hspb1, while sEV primarily promoted vascular repair and neural regeneration by upregulating genes including Mas1, Gucy1a3, Mapk4, Prkca, Syngap1, and Pcdhb17by activating VEGF, Rap1/Ras-MAPK, calcium signaling and cytoskeletal remodeling pathways. This multimodal mechanism significantly enhanced neuronal survival and functional recovery compared with GA-gel alone. In conclusion, co-delivery of sEV and GA within the EG-gel could establish the synergistic effect of “GA suppressing inflammation and sEV promoting repair” in the damaged area of TBI at the same time. This synergistic effect constructed a “low-inflammatory, high-repair” microenvironment that was favorable for vascular regeneration and nerve recovery, and thus more effectively promoted the structural and functional recovery of TBI.

In vitro and in vivo validation of transcriptome-predicted mechanisms

To validate the transcriptomic predictions that EG-gel had synergistic anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and neuroregenerative effects, we conducted in vitro and in vivo validations using cell cultures and a TBI mouse model. At the cellular level, we initially evaluated the angiogenic activity of the EG-gel by employing human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). In tube formation assays, HUVECs treated with EG-gel formed significantly denser vascular networks than either the GA-gel or control groups (Fig. 6A). Quantitative analysis revealed that the EG-gel exhibited substantially higher vessel percentage area, total number of junctions, and average vessel length at 2 and 6 h (Fig. 6B). We next assessed the effect of EG-gel on endothelial migration via a scratch assay with mouse brain endothelial cells (Bend.3). As presented in Fig. 6C and D, the EG-gel notably raised endothelial cell migration rate compared with the control and GA-gel. The above data demonstrated that sEV in the EG-gel could boost transcriptomics-predicted vascular cell migration and tubule formation. Afterward, to probe the anti-inflammatory activity of the EG-gel in vitro, we established a lipopolysaccharide (LPS) -induced neuroinflammation model in BV2 [59]. Fig. 6F illustrated that unstimulated BV2 cells exhibited small, round morphology characteristic of the resting state, while LPS induced classic activation of BV2 cells, leading to enlarged cell bodies and an amoeboid morphology. However, treatment with GA-gel or EG-gel reversed this activated morphology toward the resting phenotype. Accordingly, LPS successfully increased the mRNA levels of proinflammatory cytokines (interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)), whereas both GA-gel and EG-gel significantly reduced the expression of proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 6E). We further confirmed the impact of the gel on inflammatory phenotypes by detecting pro-inflammatory M1 (iNOS) and anti-inflammatory M2 (CD206) markers via immunofluorescence staining. Fig. 6G and I displayed that, compared with the model, both GA-gel and EG-gel lowered the number of iNOS-positive cells and increased CD206 expression. This implied that both gels could convert microglia from an pro-inflammatory to a anti-inflammatory phenotype, thereby reducing the occurrence of neuroinflammation. At the animal level, to further evaluate the effect of the hydrogel on post-TBI neuroinflammation, we measured markers associated with the neuroinflammatory response. First, we examined mRNA expression of proinflammatory factors on day 1 after TBI. We found that both GA-gel and EG-gel significantly reduced the mRNA expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α compared with the TBI group (Fig. S5A), suggesting an early suppression of the inflammatory response. Secondly, we tested serum inflammatory markers via ELISA on day 3 post-TBI. Results revealed that pro-inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-α significantly increased, and anti-inflammatory factors interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) decreased after TBI. Following GA-gel treatment, TNF-α expression declined and IL-10 expression enhanced, whereas EG-gel treatment lowered both IL-6 and TNF-α levels while simultaneously boosting IL-10 and TGF-β expression (Fig. 6H). Third, the immunohistochemical analysis of TBI brain tissue further corroborated that the EG-gel reduced expression levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β in the cortical tissue surrounding the lesion (Fig. 6J). Fourth, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is an injury-associated inflammatory mediator [60], and GA is its natural inhibitor [61]. Using immunofluorescence to detect HMGB1 expression, we found that both GA-gel and EG-gel suppressed HMGB1 upregulation post-TBI (Fig. 7A and B). Finally, since microglia and astrocytes jointly regulate the inflammatory response during the acute phase of TBI [62], we separately detected their markers ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). Results showed that compared with the TBI group, both GA-gel and EG-gel groups reduced Iba-1 fluorescence intensity, indicating that both gels attenuated microglial activation. However, only EG-gel significantly degraded GFAP expression (Fig. 7C–7F), suggesting superior regulatory effects on reactive astrocytic proliferation. What's more, early neurovascular repair appeared one week after TBI [63,64]. Thus, we elucidated the efficacy of EG-gel in promoting neurovascular repair following TBI by assessing neurovascular markers on day 7. To begin with, NeuN is a protein localized to the nuclei of mature neurons [65], while β3-tubulin is a neuron-specific tubulin [66]. Both are commonly used as markers for neurons and their differentiation. NeuN and β3-tubulin immunostaining revealed significant loss of mature neurons after TBI, whereas EG-gel treatment reversed this damage (Fig. 7G and H). Next, GAP43 is a membrane-associated protein involved in axonal growth and synaptic plasticity, serving as a marker for axonal regeneration and neural plasticity [67]. The level of GAP43 decreased after TBI but recovered to baseline levels after EG-gel administration, implying that EG-gel facilitated axonal remodeling (Fig. 7I and J). Summing up, the aforementioned experiments employed immunofluorescence to detect neuronal and axonal markers. The results demonstrated that only EG-gel fostered neural plasticity and axonal regeneration, suggesting a neuroprotective role for sEV within EG-gel. Last but not least, CD31 serves as a marker for angiogenesis [68]. We proceeded with its immunostaining. The images and quantitative analysis displayed enhanced vascularization activity post-TBI, which was further amplified by EG-gel (Fig. 7K and L). VEGF is a central regulator of angiogenesis and vascular repair and is widely used as a key biomarker of tissue vascular regeneration and repair capacity [69]. We measured serum VEGF levels by ELISA on day 7 post-injury in each group of mice (Fig. S5E). The EG-gel group showed the highest VEGF concentration, indicating that EG-gel treatment markedly promoted VEGF expression, which was associated with angiogenesis and vascular repair. These proved that the EG-gel facilitated angiogenesis within the lesion microenvironment.

Collectively, the in vitro and in vivo data validated the two complementary mechanisms predicted by our transcriptomics analysis: GA primarily attenuated the acute inflammatory response, while embryonic stem cell-derived sEV promoted endothelial remodeling, neuronal repair, and angiogenesis. Therefore, the EG-gel played a synergistic role in establishing a “low-inflammation, high-repair” microenvironment, further promoting neuroprotection and structural regeneration after TBI.

Biocompatibility of EG-gel

Excellent biocompatibility is fundamental to the translational potential of any hydrogel for TBI repair [70]. Glycyrrhizic acid (GA), as a small-molecule drug without artificial modifications, inherently exhibits excellent biocompatibility [71]. The sEV can avoid immunogenicity and oncogenic risks, unlike pluripotent stem cells, further enhancing safety. We first performed an in vitro gel swelling assay to assess whether the EG-gel would expand after implantation. As presented in Fig. 8A, the EG-gel did not exhibit positive swelling at any time point, showing progressive mass reduction and complete loss of measurable wet mass by day 7. These data indicated that EG-gel does not undergo expansion that could compress adjacent brain tissue. We next assessed blood compatibility by incubating EG-gel with fresh mouse erythrocytes, using Triton X-100 and PBS as positive and negative controls, respectively. EG-gel induced a hemolysis rate below 1% (Fig. 8B and S6A), representing negligible red blood cell damage. Then, we estimated in vivo safety by monitoring body weight and clinical behavior in TBI mice treated with EG-gel. Throughout the study, the body weight of animals receiving EG-gel treatment remained stable, with no mortality or abnormal behaviors (Fig. S6B). Afterward, we collected serum from TBI mice to measure liver and kidney function indicators, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (UREA), and creatinine (CREA). Fig. 8C and S7C exhibited no significant alterations in hepatic or renal markers compared with the Sham group on days 3 and 7. We harvested the heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney for histopathological analysis via HE staining. The tissue architecture without inflammation, necrosis, or fibrosis in all examined organs on days 3 and 7 (Fig. 8D and S6D). Finally, in order to track EG-gel metabolism in vivo, we performed ex vivo fluorescence imaging in the brains of TBI mice after intracerebral injection of DiR-labeled sEV or EG-gel. As shown in Fig. 8E and F, the fluorescent signals predominantly accumulated in the liver, confirming hepatic clearance as the primary elimination route for EG-gel. Conclusions

We developed and validated a composite EG-gel co-assembled from embryonic stem cell-derived sEV as a gel factor with GA, demonstrating its transformative potential from material design to biological activity. The co-assembly mechanism of the EG-gel likely involved GA forming nanofibers through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic stacking. These nanofibers coupled with the sEV membrane via polar head group coordination and hydrophobic core insertion, thereby driving the formation of an interpenetrating 3D gel network. Rheological screening determined the 1/4 EG-gel ratio, which achieved an optimal balance between mechanical compatibility and injectability. This gel exhibited excellent self-healing, shear-thinning injectability, hemostatic, and adhesion properties. The EG-gel enabled long-term retention of sEV at TBI lesion sites, thereby improving the efficacy and prolonging the therapeutic window. The mechanism of EG-gel treatment for TBI may be that GA first inhibited the acute pro-inflammatory response and provided a non-inflammatory microenvironment for sEV, which in turn promoted endothelial remodeling, angiogenesis and neuronal repair. Additionally, the EG-gel appeared biocompatible at effective doses. In summary, EG-gel provided a “first anti-inflammatory, then vaso-neural regeneration” strategy for localized drug delivery. This approach addressed the problem of rapid clearance and poor targeting of free sEV in injured brain tissue, demonstrating potential for clinical translation. We will focus subsequent studies on the long-term functional efficacy of EG-geland identification of key exosomal effector molecules.

Isolation and purification of sEV by differential ultracentrifugation. The sEV were isolated from embryonic stem cell conditioned medium (CM) using an ultracentrifugation protocol. Briefly, conditioned medium (collected after 48–72 h culture) was first centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min and 2500×g for 20 min at room temperature to remove detached cells and large debris. The supernatant was optionally subjected to sequential low-speed spins to remove apoptotic bodies and large microvesicles when required (2500×g for 10 min to pellet apoptotic bodies, 12,000×g for 30 min to pellet large microvesicles). The clarified supernatant was concentrated using a 100 kDa molecular-weight-cutoff ultrafiltration device (to reduce volume, typical concentration factor 5 × , final volume 20–60 mL depending on starting volume) and then filtered through a sterile 0.22 μm filter to remove remaining large particles. Filtered, concentrated CM was transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes balanced by mass and centrifuged at 120,000×g for 120 min at 4 °C to pellet sEV. After carefully removing the supernatant, the crude pellet was resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and washed by a second ultracentrifugation step at 120,000×g for 90 min at 4 °C. The final pellet was gently resuspended in 50–100 μL sterile PBS (volume adjusted to pellet size) and aliquoted for downstream analysis. The sEV were stored at −80 °C. All manipulations were performed aseptically in a biological safety cabinet when possible, and centrifugation/filtration steps were conducted at low temperature (4–8 °C) to preserve sEV integrity. To minimize loss and membrane fouling during ultrafiltration, single 100 kDa units were not overloaded, membranes were kept wet, and devices were rinsed/handled according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Identification of sEV. Morphological characteristics were confirmed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), particle size distribution was determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS), and the presence of classical exosomal markers (Alix, TSG101, CD81) was validated by Western blotting.

Synthesis of the hydrogel. Glycyrrhizic acid (GA) was weighed to give a final concentration of 14 wt.% in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.34), and the mixture was heated with stirring in a metal water bath to 80 °C until GA fully dissolved, yielding a clear solution. The GA solution was cooled to 37 °C, then a defined volume of sEV suspension (1 mg/mL) was slowly added so that the sEV volume comprised one-quarter of the final hydrogel volume. The mixture was stirred for 30 s, then returned to room temperature and left undisturbed for several minutes to allow gelation. The GA-only hydrogel was designated GA-gel, and the composite hydrogel containing sEV is named EG-gel. Formed gels were stored at 4 °C for up to one week and photographed on days 0, 3, and 7.

Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) Images. The hydrogel was diluted 1:1 with 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.34), and an aliquot was deposited onto clean silicon wafers, frozen at −20 °C overnight, then dried under vacuum for 24 h to obtain lyophilized hydrogel samples. Dried specimens were mounted on SEM stubs with conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with gold (Mini Coater, SuPro Instruments, China) at 10 mA for 45 s to form a conductive layer. Samples were imaged on a MIRA LMS scanning electron microscope (TESCAN, Czech Republic) at 3 kV to record morphological features.

Ultraviolet-Visible (UV-Vis) Spectra Analysis. Lyophilized gel powder was dissolved in distilled water. Samples were placed in a 1 cm pathlength quartz cuvette and blanked against the same buffer. UV-Vis absorption spectra were recorded at room temperature on a GENESYS 180 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Scans were acquired from 200 to 450 nm with a 1 nm bandwidth and a medium scanning speed.

X-ray Powder Diffraction (XRD) Test. Approximately 50 mg of the lyophilized gel powder was gently spread onto the sample holder and flattened with a glass slide to form a smooth thin layer. The sample holder was mounted on an X-ray powder diffractometer (SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan). Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) was used as the excitation source. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 5°–90° with a step size of 0.02°–0.05° and a scan speed of 2° min−1. Diffraction peaks were indexed against standard databases and the interplanar spacings (d-values) were calculated accordingly.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) Analysis. Approximately 10 mg of lyophilized gel powder was mixed with 200 mg of dry anhydrous potassium bromide (KBr) to prepare FT-IR samples. The powder mixture was thoroughly ground in an agate mortar for 3 min, then dried in an oven at 100 °C for 10 min to remove adsorbed moisture. While still warm, an appropriate amount of the mixture was transferred into a pellet die and pressed with a hydraulic press at 10–15 MPa for 30 s to produce a transparent, uniform KBr pellet. The pellet was mounted on the sample holder and scanned in transmission mode on a Nicolet iS20 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) over the spectral range 4000–400 cm−1.

1H Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (1H NMR). Approximately 15 mg of lyophilized gel powder was dissolved in deuterium oxide (D2O) and vortexed until fully dissolved. A 0.5–0.7 mL aliquot of the solution was transferred into a 5 mm NMR tube, which was capped to prevent solvent evaporation or sample oxidation. The sample was placed in an Ascend TM 600 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). The deuterated solvent signal was used to lock, and shimming was performed to optimize field homogeneity and improve resolution. Chemical shifts were referenced to the residual solvent peak (HOD in D2O at about 4.7–4.8 ppm). 1H NMR acquisition parameters were set as follows: spectral window 0–12 ppm, acquisition time 5 s, and a 90° pulse angle.

Raman spectroscopy. Lyophilized gel powder was first dried in an oven at 100 °C for 2 h, then cooled to room temperature to remove residual moisture. Liquid samples were loaded into a sealed quartz cuvette. Spectra were acquired in single-point scanning mode on a LabRAM HR Evolution Raman spectrometer (Horiba, Japan) using a 532 nm excitation laser at 2 mW power. The grating was set to 2400 lines/mm, the integration time was 55 s, and spectra were collected over the range 50–4000 cm−1.

Rheology tests. Hydrogel rheology was measured on a rotational rheometer (TA Instruments, USA). Samples were loaded and the following tests were performed to record storage modulus (G′), loss modulus (G″) and viscosity: (1) Strain sweep: at a constant frequency of 1 Hz, strain amplitude was linearly swept from 0.01% to 100% to determine the linear viscoelastic (LVE) region and the crossover point (G′ = G″, flow point); (2) Step-strain recovery: starting at 0.5% strain held for 2 min, the strain was jumped to 50% for 2 min and then returned to 0.5% for 2 min; the 0.5%↔50% cycle was repeated 5 times at 1 Hz to assess shear-reversibility and self-healing; (3) Frequency sweep: at a fixed strain of 0.1%, frequency was scanned linearly from 0.1 to 10 Hz to characterize frequency-dependent viscoelasticity; (4) Time sweep: at constant strain 0.1% and frequency 1 Hz, dynamic monitoring was performed from 0 to 600 s to evaluate short-term stability. (5) Flow curve: the shear rate was swept from 0.01 s−1 to 60 s−1, and the viscosity was recorded. All measurements were carried out at 25 °C.

Injectability and adhesion of EG-gel. (1) Injectability: We briefly preheated the prepared EG-gel at 37 °C to obtain a flowable, injectable consistency. We then filled a sterile syringe with the gel and extruded it onto a culture dish in predefined patterns (e.g., numerals, letters, square, circle, heart). After printing, the deposited structures were allowed to rest at room temperature for several minutes to complete gelation and stabilization. Photographs of the structure are then taken for documentation. (2) Adhesion: A quantitative EG-gel piece was placed at the center of a sterile culture dish, which was then inverted 180° and held upside-down for 60 s while detachment or sliding was observed and recorded. Freshly excised mouse organs (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were rinsed in PBS, blotted dry, the EG-gel was placed on each surface and lightly pressed, and photographs were taken immediately to document adhesion.

In vitro hemostasis assay. A blood mixture (anticoagulated whole blood mixed with 0.2 M CaCl2 at a 9:1 ratio; total volume 200 μL) was transferred into tubes containing EG-gel or into empty tubes (control). At 1 min and 5 min after transfer, the tubes were inverted to assess clot formation. After 5 min, 200 μL of distilled water was added to lyse any non-coagulated erythrocytes. Samples were centrifuged at 2000×g for 5 min, and 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate for absorbance measurement at 540 nm. The blood clotting index (BCI)% = [ODsample/ODcontrol]×100%.

In vitro release study. To evaluate the release behavior of EG-gel, luciferase-labeled sEVs were prepared using a membrane-targeted Nano luciferase construct. E14 cells were transduced with second-generation lentivirus carrying the construct, and a stable cell line was obtained by puromycin selection. After expansion, sEVs were isolated from the conditioned medium by differential centrifugation, followed by ultrafiltration and ultracentrifugation to obtain luciferase-labeled sEVs, which were then incorporated into the EG-gel. Briefly, 200 μL of EG-gel was placed into the upper chamber of a transwell and incubated with 1 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) in the chamber at 37 °C. At predetermined time points (2, 4, and 6 h, and 1, 3, 5, 6, and 7 days), the entire release medium (1 mL) was collected and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS. The collected samples were analyzed to quantify the released glycyrrhizic acid (GA) and luciferase-labeled sEVs. GA content was determined using UV-Vis spectroscopy, while sEV release was quantified based on luciferase activity. The cumulative release percentages of GA and sEVs were calculated relative to the initial loaded amounts. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Degradation and swelling tests. The initial wet weight (W0) of the EG-gel was recorded, and then the gels were placed in 1.0 mL of prewarmed PBS separately (3 per group). The gels were returned to the 37 °C incubator after each measurement and weighed at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 days to record the wet mass at time t (Wt). The swelling and degradation rates were calculated: swelling ratio% = [Wt−W0]/W0 × 100%; Degradation ratio% = [1−Wt/W0] × 100%.

Animal Experiments. All animal procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (approval no. 20232146) and were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines to minimize animal suffering. Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old, 20–25 g, Vital River) were housed under controlled conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 50–60% relative humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) and acclimatized for at least 7 days before surgery. Mice were anesthetized with 0.3% sodium pentobarbital solution (ip, 0.22 ml/g body weight), secured in a stereotaxic frame, and maintained on a heating pad to keep body temperature at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C with continuous monitoring of vital signs.

TBI model construction. A controlled cortical impact (CCI) injury was produced in the right parietal cortex. Following a midline scalp incision and exposure of the skull with bregma identified as the reference point, a 4 mm diameter craniotomy was made centered at AP = −0.5 mm, ML = +0.5 mm relative to bregma. The dura was left intact and the cortical surface was kept moist with sterile saline. CCI was delivered using a TBI-0310 impactor (Precision Systems and Instrumentation, USA) with a 3.0 mm diameter impactor tip, impact velocity 5.0 m/s, impact depth 1.5 mm (relative to the cortical surface), and a dwell time of 80 ms, with the impactor oriented perpendicular to the brain surface to generate a moderate-to-severe injury. After impact, the cortical surface was gently rinsed with sterile saline and excess fluid removed. The musculature and skin were closed with absorbable sutures, animals were placed in a warmed recovery chamber until fully ambulatory, and postoperative care was provided per institutional protocol.

Drug administration and treatment. Mice were randomized into five groups: Sham, TBI (model), GA-gel, Free sEV, and EG-gel. Treatments were administered locally into the lesion site immediately after CCI. The Sham group underwent craniotomy without impact and received an equal volume of sterile PBS at the same stereotaxic coordinates. The TBI group received PBS after impact. The GA-gel group received an equal volume of GA-gel, and the EG-gel group received an equal volume of EG-gel containing 10 μg sEV. EG-gel was prepared with sEV at a stock concentration of 2 mg/mL and a volumetric fraction of 1/4 (sEV:total gel volume). The free sEV group received a liquid injection containing 10 μg sEV, the same amount as the EG gel group. The injection volume for each animal was 20 μL. All injections were performed under sterile conditions with the injected material maintained at 37 °C. After completing the injection, the needle was left in place for 60 s to allow the gel to stabilize, then slowly withdrawn and the scalp sutured.

In vivo and in vitro fluorescence imaging. The sEVs were labeled with the near-infrared dye DiR (1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide). Briefly, purified sEV were incubated with DiR at 37 °C for 20 min, then at room temperature for an additional 15 min. Free dye was removed by ultracentrifugation, and the labeled sEVs were resuspended in PBS. The protein concentration was measured, and dosages were normalized accordingly. DiR-labeled sEV were then incorporated into the GA formulation to prepare DiR-EG-gel. Mice were assigned to two groups (DiR-labeled sEV and DiR-EG-gel) and imaged using an IVIS Spectrum system (Bio-Tek, USA). Longitudinal whole-head imaging was performed at 0, 1, 2, 3, and 7 days post-injection. Prior to imaging, Mice were anesthetized with 0.3% pentobarbital sodium (0.022 mL/g, i.p.). Whole-head fluorescence images were acquired with identical exposure and gain settings across all groups and time points. For each animal and time point, an identical-sized region of interest (ROI) was drawn over the lesion site, and total radiant efficiency was quantified using the Living Image software. At the terminal time point (7 days), a subset of animals was sacrificed, and major organs (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested for ex vivo fluorescence imaging and quantification to assess systemic biodistribution.

Behavioral Tests. The neuro severity score (NSS) was obtained according to the methodology described by Flierl et al. [72]. The score was calculated for each mouse at 1, 3, and 7 days post-injury. The string suspension grip was assessed according to the published scoring criteria. Briefly, we suspended a thin wire (2 mm diameter) across a horizontal frame at a height of 50 cm above a padded surface. Each mouse was allowed to grasp the center of the wire with both forepaws and was then released. We recorded the latency to fall (seconds). Performance was scored on an ordinal scale, with normal function = 0 and scores incrementing with poorer performance, up to a maximum of 6. For the foot-fault test, mice were placed on a 2.5 × 2.5 cm wire-grid platform and allowed to walk freely for 1 min while we recorded the number of forelimb foot-faults (a fault = paw slips through a grid aperture). We tabulated left- and right-side faults and calculated the left-side fault rate as left faults/total steps × 100%. All behavioral tests were repeated three times per mouse and conducted by a single blinded operator.

HE staining. Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 3 μm. Paraffin sections were dried in a 60 °C oven for 60 min, deparaffinized twice in xylene, and rehydrated by a graded ethanol series. Sections were rinsed in running tap water to remove residual solvents, stained with hematoxylin for 6 min, rinsed in distilled water, and rapidly differentiated in 0.3% acid alcohol for 3–10 s. After differentiation, sections were immediately blued under running water for 5 min. Following a brief transition in 70% ethanol, sections were counterstained with eosin for 1 min, cleared twice in xylene, and finally mounted with neutral resin.

Nissl staining. Dewaxed and rehydrated brain sections were immersed in Nissl staining solution preheated to 60 °C and stained for 15 min. After staining, sections were rinsed under running water to remove excess dye, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped with neutral mounting medium.

TUNEL staining. The TUNEL staining was done according to the One Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit. After completing the labeling reaction, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Imaging was performed on a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, AXI0 IMAGER M2, Germany). The number of tunnel-positive cells was counted by ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence staining. Sections were heated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave for antigen extraction. Sections were blocked with blocking solution for 60 min at room temperature to prevent non-specific binding. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. Then, fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 60 min. Sections were washed again with PBS, counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium to label nuclei, coverslipped, and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. The mean intensity was visualized using ImageJ.

IgG staining. The paraffin sections were heated in a microwave in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating sections in 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 20 min. Sections were then blocked with blocking solution at room temperature for 1 h. HRP-conjugated IgG antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Chromogenic development was performed using DAB, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, sections were dehydrated, mounted, and observed under a light microscope.

mRNA sequencing. On day 3 post-injury, peri-lesional cortical tissues were collected and stored at −80 °C. The Sham, TBI, and EG-gel groups each had 6 cerebral cortical tissues. Sequencing and primary data processing were performed by Shu Pu (Shanghai) Biotechnologies LLC (Aksomics, Shanghai, China). Briefly, total RNA concentration was measured by a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, USA). For library construction, 1–2 μg of total RNA per sample was used. mRNA was enriched using oligo (dT) selection, and libraries were prepared using the KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Roche). Double-stranded cDNA synthesis used the dUTP method, followed by high-fidelity PCR, to generate strand-specific RNA-seq libraries. Library quality was assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA), and libraries were quantified by qRT-PCR.

Equimolar pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, USA). Sequencing workflow included NaOH denaturation to generate single strands, on-flow-cell cluster amplification, and 150 bp paired-end (PE150) cyclic sequencing. Image processing and base calling were performed using the Solexa pipeline version 1.8 (Off-Line Base Caller v1.8). Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC, and adapters were trimmed with Cutadapt. Cleaned reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2. Transcript abundance was estimated with StringTie using official annotation files. Gene-level expression (FPKM) was calculated using the Ballgown package in R, and differential expression was assessed at the gene level. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected using the following criteria: fold change ≥1.5, p-value <0.05, q-value (FDR) <0.05, and group mean FPKM ≥0.5. Downstream analyses included principal component analysis (PCA), correlation analysis, clustering of DEGs, and functional enrichment (GO and KEGG pathway) analysis.

Cell culture. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and the mouse brain endothelial cells (Bend.3) were cultured in endothelial cell medium. BV2 microglial cells (BV2) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium. All cells were kept at 37 °C in a cell culture chamber with 5% CO2.

Tube-formation assay. 96-well plates, pipettes, and Matrigel were pre-chilled on ice (4 °C). 50 μL of Matrigel was added to each well and solidified at 37 °C for 60 min. Meanwhile, HUVECs in logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized, counted, and resuspended at the appropriate concentration. 1.5 × 104 cells were seeded into each well, and treatment solutions were added according to group (Blank control, GA-gel, and EG-gel). After seeding, plates were gently rocked to distribute cells evenly and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Images were acquired at 2 h and 6 h using an inverted microscope (DMi8, Leica, Germany). Images were analyzed with AngioTool to quantify vessel percentage area, total number of junctions, average vessel length, and total covered area.

Scratch assay. Bend.3 were grown to 90% confluence and serum-starved in 1% FBS medium for 12 h before wounding to minimize proliferation. Uniform linear scratches were made in each well of a 24-well plate using a sterile 10 μL pipette tip. Prewarmed treatment media were added, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Images of the same fields were captured immediately after scratching (0 h) and at 6 h using an inverted microscope. Wound closure was quantified in ImageJ by measuring the wound area at 6 h and calculating percentage closure as ((Area_0 h−Area_6 h)/Area_0 h) × 100%.

Cellular inflammation model. BV2 microglia were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells/well and divided into four groups: Control, Model, GA-gel, and EG-gel. The Model group was stimulated with 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or 20 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4) to induce an inflammatory response. GA-gel and EG-gel groups received the corresponding treatments concurrently with LPS or IL-4 and were incubated for 24 h. Then, cell morphology in each group was documented using an inverted microscope.

Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis. Following the 24 h treatment, total RNA was extracted from each group using the RaPure Total RNA Kit. Genomic DNA was removed, and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. Gene-specific primers for interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) allograft inflammatory factor 1 (Aif1), G-protein signalling modulator 3 (Gpsm3), guanylate cyclase 1, soluble, alpha 3 (Gucy1a3), heat shock protein 1 (Hspb1), mitogen-activated protein kinase 4 (Mapk4), Harvey rat sarcoma oncogene, subgroup R (Rras), Synaptic Ras GTPase-activating Protein 1 (Syngap1) and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (Trem2) were designed.

mRNA levels of these genes were quantified by qRT-PCR using GAPDH as the internal reference. Each sample was run in triplicate technical wells.

Immunofluorescence for M1/M2 phenotype. After 24 h treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10min, and blocked with blocking solution at room temperature for 60 min. LPS-treated cells were incubated with an anti-iNOS antibody (M1 marker), and IL-4-treated cells were stained with an anti-CD206 antibody (M2 marker). All remaining steps were performed as previously described.

ELISA and MPCLIA assay. The mouse cortical tissues and serum were harvested for quantification of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10, transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), and VEGF. ELISA and MPCLIA kits were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC). This process was similar to the aforementioned IgG staining method.

Hemolytic activity test. Fresh blood was collected from C57 mice into sodium citrate anticoagulant tubes and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min to remove plasma. The erythrocyte pellet was washed three times with physiological saline until the supernatant was clear, then resuspended to make a 2% (v/v) red blood cell suspension. Equal volumes of the RBC suspension were mixed with GA-gel, EG-gel, negative control (PBS), and positive control (1% Triton X-100), and the mixtures were incubated at 37 °C in an oven for 60 min. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm. Hemolysis ratio (%) was calculated as: (OD_sample−OD_negative)/(OD_positive−OD_negative) × 100%. Finally, photographs of the RBC suspensions from each group were taken for visual documentation.

Hepatic and Renal Function Analysis. On days 3 and 7, under deep anesthesia, blood was collected from mice via the orbital venous plexus. The whole blood was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the serum. The levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine (CREA), and blood urea nitrogen (UREA) were measured by an automated biochemical analyzer (Chemray 800, China).

Statistical Analysis. Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, we used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple-comparisons test for pairwise comparisons. All tests were two-tailed, and p-values below 0.05 were considered significant.
