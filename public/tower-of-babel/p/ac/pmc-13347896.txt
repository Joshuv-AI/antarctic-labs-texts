Explainable machine learning revealing the impact of mental and physical health on arthritis
Authors: Md Atik Shams, Sumaiya Fatema, D M Hasibul Islam, Anindita Datta, David Eisenberg, Danastan Tasaouf Mridula, Junnatul Mawa, Asma Sultana, Nafiya Ahmed, Monowarul Islam, Tanmoy Sarkar Pias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop a robust and interpretable machine learning framework for arthritis risk prediction and to identify important risk factors associated with the disease.MethodsThis study used four datasets, which are Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) 2019, BRFSS 2021, National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) 2020 and NHIS 2022. We evaluated 11 machine learning and deep learning architectures including a custom stacked ensemble combined with five resampling techniques to address class imbalance and 9 imputation methods to handle missing data. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity and balanced accuracy are used to evaluate model performance, with fivefold cross-validation to ensure robustness. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) provided feature interpretability in prediction.ResultsThe combination of the stacked ensemble, Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) imputation and random undersampling (RUS) achieved the best performance with an AUROC of 0.8007 and sensitivity of 0.8060 in the test set of BRFSS 2019. This combination also showed a balanced performance across both subgroups of male and female by maintaining high performance with AUROC of 0.808 and 0.800, respectively, in the test set of BRFSS 2021. The stacked ensemble model with GAN imputation and either Random Undersampling (RUS) or Random Oversampling (ROS) achieved the best performance across all four datasets. SHAP analysis identified higher age, walking difficulty and high body mass index (BMI) as the top physical factors. Among the psychosocial factors, parental separation showed a high impact on predicting arthritis.DiscussionThe risk of arthritis is influenced by health, lifestyle and childhood experiences. SHAP shows that difficulty walking, age, BMI and childhood stress play important roles in predicting arthritis risk.ConclusionThese results confirm important predictors of arthritis risk and provide a clear, interpretable framework.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, BMJ Health Informatics, Public health informatics

Introduction

Arthritis affects approximately one in five adults in the USA,1 with a staggering 54 million adults living with the condition.2 As the population continues to grow and age, the number of people with arthritis is expected to rise significantly. This condition causes damage to the joints, the tissues surrounding the joints and other connective tissues, leading to pain, stiffness and a reduced range of motion.3 Some forms of arthritis develop as a result of normal, lifelong wear and tear on the joints, while others occur after injuries or as a consequence of other chronic conditions, such as obesity, diabetes and heart disease, which are frequently associated with individuals who have arthritis.2 While there are over 100 types of this disease, the most prevalent include osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis and gout.4 In some cases, surgical interventions, such as joint replacement, may become necessary to improve mobility and alleviate pain.5

Artificial intelligence (AI) means developing computer systems that can perform tasks that usually require human intelligence, such as learning complex patterns, learning from large amounts of information and making choices on their own. Recently, AI has revolutionised the healthcare sector by providing powerful, data-driven tools. It helps to detect diseases early, achieve accurate diagnosis and personalise treatment planning. Many recent studies have used explainable AI with machine learning (ML) and deep learning in various medical domains. Significant applications used models like Extra Trees Classifier6 and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)7 for early and accurate prediction of type 2 diabetes. Systematic applications are also used with these models to diagnose and predict polycystic ovary syndrome.8

Despite extensive prior research, limitations such as class imbalance, missing values9 and weak feature selection remain. Handling missing data is important, as it directly affects the reliability of real-world clinical datasets. Earlier studies applied Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) based imputation,10 as well as K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations with Predictive Mean Matching (MICE-PMM).11 Another study of seven methods (mean, mode, median, random variables (RV), MICE, random forest (RF)) identified random value imputation as the most effective. However, most approaches still rely on traditional imputation and basic feature reduction, often ignoring advanced techniques such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs).12 One study reported its best performance using an Extra Trees Classifier on Principal Component Analysis (PCA) reduced features, followed by comparisons across KNN, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Logistic Regression (LR), Naive Bayes (NB), RF and Support Vector Classifier (SVC). Previous models mainly focused on physical biomarkers and demographic factors,13 ignoring psychosocial influences. Additionally, many deep learning models operate as ‘black boxes’,14 limiting interpretability. Previous studies are further constrained by limited explainable ML use and reliance on single datasets. While Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) has been widely used,15 and National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) has been applied for statistical analysis,16 no predictive or explainable arthritis prediction study exists to the best of our knowledge. To generalise our proposed approach, we worked on both large-scale health surveys, BRFSS and NHIS, by exploring a significant amount of existing research with several advanced explainable ML techniques, along with resampling strategies to address the class imbalance issue. Methods

Dataset description

This study used the BRFSS 2019,17 BRFSS 2021,18 NHIS 202019 and NHIS 202219 datasets. These datasets were obtained through telephone surveys and include information on the health behaviours, conditions and socioeconomic factors of the US population.

The BRFSS 2019 and 2021 datasets include 418 268 participants with 342 features and 438 693 participants with 304 features, respectively. The NHIS 2020 and 2022 datasets include 31 568 participants with 617 features and 27 651 participants with 637 features, respectively. Feature selection

Based on an extensive review of the literature on arthritis and chronic health risk factors, input features from distinct categories based on health status, adverse childhood experiences, demographic characteristics and disability information were selected, which are listed in online supplemental tables 1–4 and online supplemental tables 5–7. In the BRFSS dataset, 27 features are categorical and four are numeric, while in the NHIS dataset, 19 features are categorical and two are numeric.

Data splitting

To prevent any kind of data leakage, this study split the dataset into a train and test set before data imputation, data balancing and data scaling. The dataset was split into training and test sets using a stratified splitting approach to preserve the class distribution of the target feature. Approximately 80% of the data were used for training, while the remaining 20% were used for testing. The training set was later used to fit models after data processing, imputation, balancing and scaling. On the other hand, the test set was separately processed and imputed using the parameters learnt from the training set without any data balancing to evaluate the final performance.

Data preprocessing

We renamed the original columns to make the datasets easier to understand and analyse. Columns containing dots were modified by replacing the dot with an underscore and the rest of the name was capitalised, for example, X.state became _STATE. Then, specific features relevant for analysis were selected. Each original feature (eg, ACEDIVRC, _AGEG5YR, SMOKE100, DEAF) was assigned a descriptive new name (eg, parental separation, age, smoking status, hearing difficulty). Most selected features in the BRFSS and NHIS datasets contained missing values coded as ‘7’ or ‘9’, representing responses such as ‘don’t know/unsure’ or ‘refused’, were converted into null values. Binary variables such as ‘no’ and ‘yes’ were recorded as 0 and 1, respectively. This preprocessing step ensured uniform representation and comparability of all features. For the NHIS dataset, disability variables such as ‘difficulty walking’ were kept in their real categories (ranging from 0 for ‘no difficulty’ to 3 for ‘cannot do at all’) to capture detailed clinical insights.

The BRFSS and NHIS datasets contain a significant amount of missing values. To address this, this study used 10 imputation methods to fill in missing values, such as forward-backward, KNN, autoencoder, XGBoost imputation, diffusion, GAN, matrix factorisation, mean and drop method. We evaluated the data properties after imputation by using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) statistics.

Resampling methods

As the dataset is highly imbalanced, this study addressed this issue by implementing four resampling algorithms: random oversampling (ROS), random undersampling (RUS), synthetic minority oversampling technique, adaptive synthetic sampling builds. Moreover, class balance improved significantly after applying these resampling techniques over different filling methods (online supplemental tables 11 and 12).

Machine learning models

We implemented and analysed 11 ML techniques, including LR, decision tree, XGBoost, AdaBoost, RF, GB, and LightGBM. We designed three artificial neural network (ANN) models with different layers with specified numbers of neurons, which are ANN (one layer), ANN (two layers) and ANN with attention. Online supplemental table 14 shows the exact hyperparameter configurations, neural network architectures and cross-validation strategies for all 11 evaluated models. These models are first trained on the training set of BRFSS 2021 and evaluated on the test set of BRFSS 2021 and the test set of BRFSS 2019. Then these models are trained on the training set of NHIS 2022 and evaluated on the test set of NHIS 2022 and the test set of NHIS 2020. For model evaluation, predictions were calculated as class probabilities using the respective model-specific prediction functions (eg, predict_proba for ML models). For the stacked ensemble, the outputs of base learners were combined using a meta-learner (LR) to generate final predictions. The study reporting follows the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) AI guidelines, and the completed checklist is provided in online supplemental table 15.

The best-performing models across different sampling and filling methods are presented in table 2. The performance is ranked based on balanced accuracy and AUROC. For the BRFSS 2019 dataset, the stacked ensemble model with GAN-based filling and RUS achieved the highest sensitivity of 0.8060 and the highest AUROC of 0.8007, while the stacked ensemble model with GAN-based filling and ROS achieved the highest specificity of 0.6772. Additionally, the LR model using GAN-based filling combined with RUS achieved the highest balanced accuracy of 0.7222. In the BRFSS 2021 dataset, the highest AUROC of 0.8098 (cross-validation AUROC 0.8090±0.001), the highest balanced accuracy of 0.7318 and the highest sensitivity of 0.7700 were observed in the stacked ensemble model with GAN-based filling and RUS. The LR model achieved the highest specificity of 0.7082 using ROS and GAN. Confusion matrix analysis of the top five models of BRFSS 2021 is shown in online supplemental figure 1. The results show that our customised stacked ensemble model consistently performed well with the combination of RUS and GAN. Overall, GAN leads models towards the best performance among the 10 imputation techniques and ROS and RUS are the dominators among the five sampling techniques.

The stacked ensemble model with GAN-based filling consistently outperformed other methods, achieving the highest AUROC values of 80.07% for BRFSS 2019 and 80.98% for BRFSS 2021. The stacked ensemble also performed well with AE, achieving an AUROC of 83.37% for NHIS 2020 and 84.50% for NHIS 2022. These results show that the stacked ensemble model with appropriate sampling techniques such as RUS and ROS and GAN-based imputation in the BRFSS dataset and AE in the NHIS dataset ensures balanced model performance. SHAP analysis

In both datasets (as shown in figure 1 and figure 2), age showed the highest associative magnitude with arthritis prediction (maximum SHAP: +2.94 in BRFSS and +2.36 in NHIS). The beeswarm plots confirmed these findings, showing that older age pushed the model towards a positive arthritis prediction. Physical stress-related features, such as difficulty walking, also showed the strongest impacts (+1.38 in BRFSS and +1.60 in NHIS). While biological and mechanical factors ranked highest among the predictive list, socioeconomic and psychosocial features also contributed significantly to the models’ prediction. In the BRFSS dataset, physical health (+1.24) and employment status (+0.95) showed significant predictive impact. In the NHIS dataset, cognitive and access-related factors, such as difficulty concentrating (+0.65) and health insurance (+0.62), also made notable contributions. Discussion

This study predicts arthritis using the four versions of the BRFSS and NHIS datasets. To enable low-cost, large-scale early detection, it addresses major data challenges. Imputation methods are evaluated using K-S statistics, which showed that all imputation methods kept the original data properties very well. Among them, GAN leads to better arthritis detection performance. It outperformed other baseline imputation methods due to its ability to capture the complex, non-linear multivariate joint distributions inherent in clinical survey data through adversarial architecture.20 Although the forward-backward imputation method showed the lowest value in the K-S statistics, it duplicates real data and fills missing data that does not replicate the real-world scenario. That is why the forward-backward imputation method did not lead to good performance on detection. On the other hand, GAN generated new data that learns from the original data and fills missing data with newly generated data that replicates the real-world scenario.20,22

SHAP analysis demonstrated the impact of each feature on the prediction of arthritis risk. Consistently, SHAP analysis identifies age as a top predictive feature. Difficulty walking is another significant predictor, as impaired mobility and slower walking speed are linked to a higher risk.23 24 Physical and mental health are also important predictive factors. SHAP analysis indicates that regular checkups have mixed effects. A high body mass index (BMI) increases joint stress, especially in the knee and hip joints, significantly increasing the risk of arthritis. The SHAP analysis also shows that parents’ separation is impacting the prediction. Although these are not directly related to arthritis, they have an indirect long-term impact on a person’s mental and physical health. Traumatic experiences that occur at a young age can cause chronic stress, depression and unhealthy lifestyle behaviours such as poor diet and irregular health checkups. Over time, these factors can contribute to a higher BMI and increased blood pressure, which increases the risk of arthritis.25 26

There are several limitations to this study. First, it relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to response bias. However, these datasets remain valuable because they include a wide range of people who may be unaware of their disease status. Conclusion

This study demonstrates a strong ML framework for arthritis prediction by using two self-reported national health datasets. Systematically, this study integrated 10 advanced imputation methods and five resampling techniques and successfully addressed the problem of data imbalances. This ensures high model stability and reliability across distinct populations. The primary clinical contribution of this study was the SHAP analysis, which addressed the black-box nature of the models. This interpretability showed that age, difficulty walking, childhood psychosocial stressors and mental health were the primary associative factors impacting arthritis.

These findings support medical information that joints naturally deteriorate in elderly individuals, while also prioritising the importance of the relationship between mental and physical health. From a public health perspective, these results show the need for prevention initiatives. Recognising that psychological stress is frequently responsible for deteriorating physical strain allows public health campaigns to be more focused on overall patient well-being. As a result, these insights provide useful information for healthcare practitioners, strongly supporting the incorporation of mental health concerns and socioeconomic examinations into regular arthritis prevention programmes.

Operationally, this predictive framework is designed to function as a Clinical Decision Support tool integrated into electronic medical record (EMR) systems. Patient data are regularly updated during their visits to hospitals. From these data, the model can run in the background to predict the chance of arthritis and detect the vulnerable or high-risk patients. It will help medical experts to start treatment early by conducting targeted joint examinations and initiating early lifestyle interventions. Finally, this tool is developed to serve as an automated triage mechanism to transition arthritis care from reactive treatment to proactive prevention. Future research should focus on longitudinal analyses that will examine the change of these predictive risk factors throughout a patient’s lifetime. Finally, clinical trials focusing on the real-time integration of these models within real-time EMR decision-support systems will be important to determine the impact of this framework on clinical workflows.
