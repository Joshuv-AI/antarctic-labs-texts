Turkish Adaptation of the School Participation Questionnaire (T‐SPQ) and Investigation of Validity, Reliability and Clinical Compatibility in Children With Attention‐Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder
Authors: Barkin Kose, Cansu Emine Güler, Güleser Güney Yılmaz, Müberra Tanrıverdi, Süleyman Yıldızdal, Donald Maciver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionChildren with attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) often experience difficulties in academic performance, completing classroom tasks, engaging in social and emotional interactions, which can lead to limited participation. These challenges are most evident in everyday environments such as home and school, where children spend much of their time. Given the critical role of the school environment, evaluating participation in this context is essential. The School Participation Questionnaire (SPQ) assesses school participation, and this study aims to adapt the SPQ for Turkish children and investigate its validity and reliability in children with ADHD.Participants and MethodsThe study included 250 children diagnosed with ADHD (study group) receiving treatment in the occupational therapy department and 100 undiagnosed children (control group). Participants were aged 7–12 years. The SPQ was administered to both groups to assess school participation. Assessments were completed by 12 classroom teachers, each with a minimum of 5 years of professional experience. To evaluate test–retest reliability, 100 children from the ADHD group completed the SPQ again 2 weeks after the first assessment.ResultsThe control group demonstrated significantly higher school participation scores across all SPQ subscales compared with the ADHD group (p < 0.001). Internal consistency of the Turkish SPQ was high, with Cronbach's α values ranging from 0.82 to 0.91 and McDonald's ω values ranging from 0.83 to 0.92 across subscales. Test–retest reliability was strong, with ICC ranging from 0.84 to 0.92. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis indicated acceptable to good discriminative ability across subscales (AUC = 0.705–0.808).ConclusionThe Turkish version of the School Participation Questionnaire is a valid and reliable tool for assessing school participation in children with ADHD, offering valuable insights for interventions aimed at improving their school engagement.

Introduction

Attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental difference associated with variations in attention regulation, activity levels and impulse control. These differences interact with environments and expectations, meaning that children may experience barriers to participation in certain environments and situations (Barry et al. 2002). DHD typically becomes evident in childhood and frequently continues into adulthood, influencing how individuals experience, engage with and are perceived in everyday settings such as home, school, work and social contexts (Barkley et al. 2007; Jitendra et al. 2008). The cognitive and behavioural characteristics associated with ADHD can affect how individuals navigate educational, social and recreational environments, particularly where flexibility and individualized support are limited (Imms et al. 2017; Kooij et al. 2010; Sharma and Khan 2021). Without appropriate understanding and inclusive practices, these differences may contribute to experiences of exclusion, unmet support needs or difficulties in accessing opportunities on an equal basis. In unsupportive environments, this may lead to outcomes such as disengagement from education and difficulties in peer relationships (Anaby et al. 2019; Polderman et al. 2010).

Participation in the school environment is critical for the overall development of children, particularly those with ADHD. Schools are key settings in which children acquire essential academic, social and emotional skills; therefore, their capacity for meaningful engagement has a significant impact on personal and academic growth (Vaz et al. 2015). ADHD often leads to participation restrictions within school environments, affecting not only academic achievement but also peer relationships and emotional well‐being. Therefore, assessing school participation in children with ADHD is essential for identifying specific areas of difficulty and developing targeted interventions (Simon‐Dack et al. 2016).

Several widely recognized instruments are used to assess participation among children with ADHD. The Child and Adolescent Scale of Participation (CASP), for instance, evaluates participation across domains such as home, school and community. Although it provides a general overview of daily activities through parent report, it lacks specificity regarding school‐based participation challenges relevant to ADHD (Bedell 2009). The Children Participation Questionnaire (CPQ) also assesses participation across various domains, including self‐care, home activities, play, leisure, social participation and school‐based activities. However, its primary focus remains on broader daily living activities rather than the academic context (Rosenberg et al. 2010). The Children's Assessment of Participation and Enjoyment (CAPE) and the Participation and Environment Measure for Children and Youth (PEM‐CY) assess participation across home, school and community settings, with an emphasis on environmental factors influencing participation. Nevertheless, neither tool provides targeted evaluations specific to the school‐based needs experienced by children (Coster et al. 2011; Imms 2008). Conversely, the Social Participation Inventory and the Sensory Processing Measure evaluate sensory processing and social participation within school settings but do not adequately address the broader dimensions required for comprehensive assessment of school participation (Bernard et al. 2024; Miller‐Kuhaneck et al. 2007).

The School Participation Questionnaire (SPQ) addresses these gaps, specifically developed to assess school participation by identifying reasons for reduced participation (difficulties with attention, concentration, mood disturbances, fatigue, child's self‐perception and role performance, and both physical and social aspects of the school environment such as objects, places, peer interactions outside of class, teachers, etc.) rather than merely quantifying participation extent (Kara et al. 2025; Maciver et al. 2019; Miller et al. 2012). This characteristic makes it particularly relevant for children with ADHD, who encounter distinctive school‐related challenges (school routines and rules, sensory load, overall accessibility, teacher support, positive teacher–student relationships, peer acceptance and friendship support) (Kara et al. 2025; Saracostti et al. 2024; Saracostti et al. 2025). Moreover, the SPQ integrates individual, developmental and environmental factors, thereby providing a holistic approach to identifying participation barriers and informing supports (Maciver et al. 2022).

Examining the clinical applicability of the adapted SPQ is crucial for practitioners and educators working with children diagnosed with ADHD, who commonly face significant barriers to school participation that may exacerbate academic and social challenges (Shabat et al. 2021). Existing research highlights increased school absences and difficulties in social engagement among children with ADHD, underscoring the importance of effective assessment tools to accurately identify and address these issues (Birchwood and Daley 2012; Evans et al. 2016). Validation of the SPQ will enable clinicians to better understand participation levels among Turkish children with ADHD and inform supports aimed at enhancing their school experiences.

Given the specific challenges faced by children with ADHD in school environments, the SPQ's emphasis on identifying underlying causes of participation restrictions presents a distinct advantage over existing assessment instruments (Maciver et al. 2019). Considering the limited number of Turkish studies that objectively assess school participation, this study aims to adapt the teacher‐rated SPQ into Turkish and evaluate its validity, reliability and clinical applicability in children diagnosed with ADHD.

Methods

Participants

The study was conducted at the paediatric clinic of the department of Occupational Therapy at the University. Inclusion criteria for the study group were the following: (1) diagnosed with ADHD according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders V criteria by Child and Adolescents Psychiatric, (2) between 7 and 12 years of age, (3) volunteered to participate in the study, (4) not being diagnosed with any other neurodevelopmental disorder other than ADHD (e.g., specific learning disabilities, autism, etc.) and (5) continuing primary school education. Inclusion criteria for the control group were as follows: (1) between 7 and 12 years of age, (2) voluntary participation to the study, (3) having no reported diagnosis (physical, neurological, cognitive and psychological disease) or documented history in their medical records and (4) continuing primary school education.

The minimum sample size was determined based on the recommendation by Tabachnick and Fidell (Bayaga 2010; Tabachnick 2007; Tabachnick and Fidell 2001), which suggests that a sample should be 5–10 times the number of items to ensure the validity and reliability of multivariate analyses. Accordingly, the initial target sample size was set at 220 participants. Accounting for a potential 10% nonresponse or attrition rate, the final target sample was adjusted to 242. For the known‐groups validity analysis, a control group size was calculated as 25% of the clinical sample, resulting in a target of 60 participants (Allen 2011; Tabachnick 2007).

Participants for the clinical group were identified by screening the archives of the Occupational Therapy Department for current and former admissions. Eligible children diagnosed with ADHD who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were informed about the study. A total of 250 children with ADHD who volunteered to participate were enrolled as the study group. For the known‐groups validity, a control group of 100 age‐matched, typically developing peers was recruited. These control participants were required to have no reported or documented medical diagnosis of any neurodevelopmental or clinical condition, ensuring an appropriate comparison for the study's validity.

Procedures

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to the commencement of the study, ethical approval was obtained from the University of Non‐Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision Date and Number: 2024/09). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all participating children. This consent explicitly covered the completion of the demographic information form, the administration of the SPQ and the authorization to verify the children's medical records to ensure compliance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Furthermore, informed consent was obtained from the children's classroom teachers, who voluntarily agreed to complete the SPQ assessments based on their professional observations of the students within the school environment. All participants and their families were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without any legal or academic penalty.

Demographic data were obtained through parental reports. The SPQ was administered to both the study and control groups to identify potential barriers to school participation. To establish test–retest reliability, the SPQ was readministered 2 weeks after the initial assessment to 100 participants from the study group. All SPQ assessments were completed by the children's classroom teachers, who provided individual evaluations based on their professional observations of each student. The SPQ assessments were completed by 12 classroom teachers, each with a minimum of 5 years of professional teaching experience.

Measures

Demographic Information Form

A demographic information form was completed by the participants' parents. This form gathered data regarding the participants' age, gender, school grade, number of siblings, mode of transportation to school and medication use. Additionally, it assessed the parents' income level, educational background and marital status.

School Participation Questionnaire

The SPQ is designed according to a novel conceptual framework that identifies factors influencing school participation (Maciver et al. 2019; Maciver et al. 2020). It aims to evaluate key factors related to participation for children aged 5–12 years. The SPQ is comprised of four subscales with a total of 44 items, each rated on a 4‐point Likert scale (1 = ‘disagree’, 4 = ‘agree’). The ‘competence’ or ‘doing’ subscale, consisting of 11 items, evaluates aspects such as choices, persistence, expectation fulfilment, role performance and skills. Higher scores on this subscale reflect positive characteristics, such as the statement, ‘The child does what is expected of him/her.’ The ‘Mind and Body Experience’ or ‘Symptoms’ subscale includes five items that gauge symptoms of low energy, fatigue, insomnia, pain, mood disturbances and anxiety. Positive wording of items implies that higher scores indicate a better symptom status (e.g., ‘The child seems to sleep well when he/she comes to school’). The ‘identity’ or ‘being’ subscale, which comprises nine items, assesses the child's thoughts, feelings, knowledge, preferences, self‐perception and role perception. Higher scores denote positive attributes, such as ‘The child understands school routines.’ The ‘Environment’ subscale contains 19 items that evaluate both the physical (e.g., places and objects) and social (e.g., peers, teachers and routines) aspects of the school environment. Higher scores on this subscale indicate a more supportive environment, as illustrated by the item, ‘The school building is fully accessible for the child.’ The reference period for the questionnaire is the past 2 weeks, and observations are based on this timeframe. The SPQ's psychometric properties, including Cronbach's alpha, test–retest reliability, interrater reliability and single dimensionality, have been previously validated (Maciver et al. 2022; Maciver et al. 2020).

Translation and Cross‐Cultural Adaptation

The SPQ was translated and culturally adapted into Turkish following the standardized guidelines proposed by Beaton et al. (Beaton et al. 2000). Two independent forward translations from English into Turkish were performed by native Turkish speakers fluent in English. The translated versions were synthesized into a single Turkish version through discussion among the translators and the research team.

Subsequently, a back‐translation into English was conducted by a bilingual translator who was blinded to the original questionnaire. An expert committee (comprising the two forward translators, the back‐translator, two occupational therapists and two researchers) consisting of the translators and researchers reviewed all versions to ensure semantic, idiomatic and conceptual equivalence with the original instrument.

The prefinal Turkish version was pilot tested with teachers of children with ADHD to evaluate clarity, comprehensibility and cultural relevance. Based on their feedback, minor wording adjustments were made, and the final Turkish version was established for the psychometric evaluation (Beaton et al. 2000).

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software (Version 24.0, SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and Jamovi software, which leverages the R programming environment, with p values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics such as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (Alpar 2016; Alpar 2001; Lilliefors 1967).

The SPQ was developed as a clinimetric instrument rather than a traditional psychometric scale. Unlike reflective models where items are expected to be manifestations of a single latent trait, SPQ is based on a formative‐like conceptualization of school participation, where different barriers and facilitators contribute to the overall participation profile. This clinimetric nature, as emphasized by the original developers (Maciver et al. 2022), suggests that items may demonstrate local dependency and varying clinical relevance, which prioritizes clinical utility and known‐groups validity over a factor‐analytic structure. We conducted known‐groups validity by comparing children diagnosed with ADHD to typically developing children. Due to the nonnormal distribution of data, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for group comparisons. In addition to Cronbach's alpha, McDonald's omega (ω) was calculated to assess internal consistency, as it provides a more accurate reliability estimate when items have unequal factor loadings, addressing potential violations of the tau‐equivalence assumption that underpins Cronbach's alpha (Alpar 2016; Dunn et al. 2014). Test–retest reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to evaluate the stability of the SPQ over a 2‐week period. Test–retest agreement between the two measurement occasions was additionally evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered for all analyses (Portney and Watkins 2009). To assess the sensitivity, specificity and predictive values of the Turkish version of the School Participation Questionnaire (T‐SPQ), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for each subscale (being, doing, symptoms and environment). Based on these analyses, optimal cutoff points were identified to distinguish children diagnosed with ADHD from typically developing peers. For each subscale, the most appropriate threshold was selected, and diagnostic accuracy metrics including sensitivity, specificity and Youden's Index were calculated to determine the overall discriminatory performance. The Youden Index, defined as (sensitivity + specificity − 1), was used to identify the cutoff that maximizes the balance between sensitivity and specificity. Additionally, the DeLong test was employed to compute the standard error of the AUC values and to determine whether the differences in AUCs among subscales were statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

The average age of the children in the study was as follows: for the study group (n = 250), the mean age was 9.78 ± 1.34 years (range: 7–12 years), and for the control group (n = 100), the mean age was 9.93 ± 1.42 years (range: 8 to 12 years). Statistical comparison revealed no significant difference between the two groups, indicating that age was not a confounding factor in the analysis of school participation outcomes. Within the study group (n = 250), 235 children were receiving pharmacological treatment, and 15 were not. None of the children in the control group (n = 100) were using medication.

Validity

Construct Validity

The construct validity of the T‐SPQ was assessed through known‐group validity, which involves comparing the study group (children with ADHD) and the control group (typically developing children) across all subtests. The results revealed that the control group demonstrated significantly higher levels of school participation than the study group in all subtests, indicating that the T‐SPQ is effective in differentiating between these two populations. The differences were statistically significant in all subscales (p < 0.001), suggesting construct validity of the T‐SPQ. Effect size estimates based on rank‐biserial correlation ranged from 0.41 to 0.62 in magnitude, indicating moderate to large group differences. Clinical Compatibility

The area under the curve (AUC) values ranged from 0.705 to 0.808, indicating acceptable to good discriminative ability (Table 4). As illustrated in Figure 3, the ROC curves demonstrated that the Doing subscale had the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.808, 95% CI: 0.755–0.862) with an optimal cutoff score of 38.5, yielding a sensitivity of 88.8% and specificity of 63% (Youden's Index = 0.518). The Environment subscale also demonstrated acceptable performance (AUC = 0.750, 95% CI: 0.693–0.807) with an optimal cutoff value of 63.5, corresponding to 73.6% sensitivity and 74% specificity (Youden's Index = 0.476). The Symptoms subscale yielded an AUC of 0.745 (95% CI: 0.688–0.803), with an optimal cutoff score of 19.5, producing 88.8% sensitivity and 53% specificity (Youden's Index = 0.418). The Being subscale showed the lowest AUC value (0.705, 95% CI: 0.638–0.772). At a cutoff score of 32.5, sensitivity and specificity were 79.2% and 62%, respectively (Youden's Index = 0.412). DeLong's test (Table 5) revealed that the Doing subscale had a significantly higher discriminative capacity compared with the other dimensions. Specifically, the AUC for the Doing subscale was significantly higher than that of the Being subscale (ΔAUC = −0.104, z = −6.076, p < 0.001), the Symptoms subscale (ΔAUC = 0.063, z = 2.444, p = 0.015) and the Environment subscale (ΔAUC = 0.059, z = 2.115, p = 0.034). In contrast, no statistically significant differences in discriminative performance were observed among the Being, Symptoms and Environment subscales (p > 0.05), indicating comparable diagnostic accuracy across these three dimensions.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the T‐SPQ and to assess its clinical utility among children with ADHD. Our findings indicate that the teacher‐rated T‐SPQ demonstrates adequate reliability and validity for measuring school participation in this population.

A significant implication of this study is the cross‐cultural applicability of the SPQ. The scale has demonstrated effectiveness in evaluating participation issues specific to children with ADHD and other neurodevelopmental conditions across Turkish, Persian and Italian adaptations. Although minor cultural variations in educational expectations may exist, the core constructs measured by the SPQ—competence, identity and environmental support—remain relevant across diverse educational systems (Fekar Gharamaleki et al. 2026). In the Italian adaptation, Pietragalla et al. (2024) emphasized the critical role of the school environment in facilitating participation, particularly for children with neurodevelopmental challenges (Pietragalla et al. 2024). This finding aligns with the current Turkish study, in which significant differences were identified in the ‘Environment’ subscale, further reinforcing the importance of supportive educational settings. The consistency of these findings across cultures underscores the SPQ's adaptability to various languages without compromising its primary focus on identifying participation barriers.

Fekar Gharamaleki et al. (2026) highlighted the significance of integrating qualitative responses with quantitative measures in the Persian version (P‐SPQ), reporting a positive correlation (r = 0.64) between overall participation scores and teacher assessments (Fekar Gharamaleki et al. 2026). This correlation indicates moderate‐to‐strong validity, suggesting that higher P‐SPQ scores align with teachers' observations of student participation. Consequently, these findings support the construct validity of the P‐SPQ and provide valuable insights for educators concerning student engagement. In the present study, known‐group validity was examined by comparing school participation between children with ADHD and their typically developing peers. The results demonstrated that typically developing children consistently achieved higher scores across all subscales, thereby establishing the T‐SPQ's capacity to effectively distinguish between these two groups. These findings further reinforce the construct validity of the T‐SPQ and confirm its utility in differentiating populations based on psychosocial characteristics, particularly regarding attention‐related challenges.

Maciver et al. (2022) reported Cronbach's α values of 0.79 or higher, indicating good internal consistency across domains including identity, competence, symptoms and environment. However, reported scalability coefficients (Hi values ranging from 0.46 to 0.67 for identity and 0.37 to 0.73 for competence) suggest variability in item cohesion within these domains. Mokken analysis, used to evaluate unidimensionality through item (Hi) and overall (Hs) scalability coefficients, indicated that while certain items did not perfectly align with the latent trait, overall homogeneity remained acceptable. The exclusion of an item failing scalability criteria further reflects a rigorous approach to construct reliability (Maciver et al. 2022). In contrast, Fekar Gharamaleki et al. (2026) reported substantially higher internal consistency, with α values between 0.98 and 0.99 across most domains and an overall reliability of 0.99 (Fekar Gharamaleki et al. 2026). These coefficients indicate adequate internal consistency, demonstrating the reliability of the scale's items. Similarly, the Italian version of the SPQ demonstrated strong internal consistency, with α values ranging from 0.80 to 0.93 (Pietragalla et al. 2024). The present study utilized both Cronbach's α and McDonald's ω to assess the internal consistency of the T‐SPQ, yielding coefficients between 0.82 and 0.92 for the subscales. Employing both metrics strengthens the reliability assessment and confirms the structural integrity of the T‐SPQ. Comparative analyses suggest that McDonald's ω is particularly advantageous when factor loadings are unequal, underscoring the importance of multimethod reliability evaluations. These high coefficients indicate that T‐SPQ items consistently measure school participation, providing valuable insights into these constructs across diverse populations.

The T‐SPQ demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability, with ICC values ranging from 0.84 to 0.92. These findings are consistent with results from both the original English and Italian adaptations, where test–retest reliability was similarly high (Maciver et al. 2022; Pietragalla et al. 2024). Specifically, the original SPQ study reported ICC values exceeding 0.80, confirming the temporal stability of the scale's measurements. The consistency of ICC values across these adaptations affirms the SPQ's reliability for longitudinal research and clinical practice, enabling the accurate monitoring of longitudinal changes in school participation.

ROC analysis supports the discriminative validity and clinical utility of the T‐SPQ among children with ADHD. The AUC values observed across the subscales indicated acceptable‐to‐good classification performance. Among the dimensions, the ‘Doing’ subscale demonstrated the strongest discriminative capacity, while the ‘Being,’ ‘Symptoms,’ and ‘Environment’ subscales yielded comparable levels of diagnostic accuracy. The identified cutoff values and the corresponding balance between sensitivity and specificity further suggest that the T‐SPQ subscales provide clinically meaningful thresholds for identifying participation‐related difficulties in this population. Pairwise comparisons using DeLong's test indicated that the discriminative performance of the Doing subscale was significantly higher than that of the other dimensions, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed among the Being, Symptoms and Environment subscales. These findings suggest that participation behaviours captured within the Doing dimension may be particularly sensitive indicators of functional differences among children with ADHD (Demler et al. 2012). These results should also be interpreted in relation to the Persian adaptation of the SPQ reported by Fekar Gharamaleki et al. (2026). Their study reported a substantially lower AUC value, indicating limited discriminative capacity despite ostensibly high sensitivity and specificity estimates. Such discrepancies may reflect variations in sampling procedures, score distributions or methodological frameworks. In contrast, the present findings demonstrate a more balanced and stable pattern of classification performance, further validating the clinical utility of the T‐SPQ (Fekar Gharamaleki et al. 2026).

The primary limitation of this study concerns the selective nature of the ADHD sample, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to the broader clinical population. To ensure a diagnostically homogeneous group and to minimize the potential confounding effects of co‐occurring conditions on school participation, children with ADHD were included only if they did not have additional neurodevelopmental or psychiatric diagnoses. While this approach, conducted within the framework of a broader research protocol designed for diagnostic clarity allowed for a more precise evaluation of the T‐SPQ's psychometric properties specifically in relation to ADHD‐related characteristics, it is important to acknowledge that ADHD frequently co‐occurs with other conditions (e.g., learning disabilities, anxiety or developmental coordination disorder) in real‐world clinical practice. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution regarding their external validity. Future studies are needed to evaluate the clinical utility of the instrument in more heterogeneous ADHD populations that include common comorbidities to better reflect the complexity of typical clinical scenarios. Another limitation is the absence of factor‐analytic validation may limit the comparability of our findings with traditional psychometric validation frameworks. Although our approach aligns with the clinimetric nature of the SPQ, establishing empirical evidence regarding the internal factor structure of the Turkish version remains an objective for future studies with larger cohorts.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the T‐SPQ has demonstrated acceptable validity, reliability and internal consistency, with clinical utility comparable to findings from other international adaptations. The inclusion of McDonald's omega as a reliability measure adds a rigorous dimension to the analysis, further reinforcing the structural integrity of the scale. The SPQ's capacity to identify participation barriers across diverse cultural contexts establishes it as a valuable instrument for both clinicians and researchers. Future research should further investigate the applicability of the scale in different cultural and linguistic contexts and extend its use to other neurodevelopmental conditions beyond ADHD, while also examining the potential relationships between sociodemographic variables and SPQ scores.
