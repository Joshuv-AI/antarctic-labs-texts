SARS-CoV‑2 Antivirals Identified from Small Molecule Modulators of Programmed −1 Ribosomal Frameshifting
Authors: Emily G. Swanson Hay, Madison M. Maille, Martina Zafferani, Maritza Puray-Chavez, Ming Xia, TinTin B. Luu, Dhanasheel Muralidharan, Mei-Ling Li, Gary Brewer, Sebla B. Kutluay, Kathryn D. Mouzakis, Amanda E. Hargrove
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Remaining SARS-CoV-2 vulnerable populations and the threat of viral resistance to existing immunity and COVID-19 vaccines highlight the need to expand treatment options. While the number of SARS-CoV-2 viral proteins is limited for therapeutic targeting, the RNA genome offers a wide range of conserved functional structures. Previous studies suggest that modulating the RNA-regulated programmed −1 ribosomal frameshift (−1 PRF) step in the coronavirus life cycle can inhibit viral replication. In this work, we aimed to identify small molecules that target the highly conserved SARS-CoV-2 frameshift site pseudoknot. An RNA-biased synthetic small molecule library screen against the RNA pseudoknot structure identified several candidate molecules. Of these, seven showed evidence of dose-dependent activity in in vitro and in cellulo frameshift assays and inhibited SARS-CoV-2 replication in cell culture. While binding was required for activity, affinity did not correlate with frameshift inhibition. Similarly, small molecule impacts to pseudoknot stability, as measured with an RT-qPCR assay, did not predict impacts on the frameshift efficiency. Comparison of the hits, as well as previously published inhibitors, across several assays supports the need for multiple characterization methods in small molecule–RNA targeting and the possibility of multiple modes of action for −1 PRF modulation. This work underscores the complexity of targeting dynamic RNA structures and the benefits of screening early for function and later optimizing for mode of action. The use of orthogonal techniques led to the identification of several promising hits and a lead antiviral compound.

Nearly six years since its initial identification and over four years since the development of COVID-19 vaccines, SARS-CoV-2 is an endemic virus, yet vulnerable populations remain, including the elderly and immunocompromised individuals. Despite the high efficacy of the vaccines, there is a great need for complementary antiviral therapeutics. An unprecedented global effort to identify SARS-CoV-2 antivirals resulted in a handful of US FDA-approved treatments. These include host immune modulators, monoclonal antibodies that target viral envelope proteins, and small molecule inhibitors of viral enzymes, such as the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) and the MPro protease. Unfortunately, early monoclonal therapies were outpaced by the emergence of resistant SARS-CoV-2 variants. While the small molecule viral enzyme inhibitor nirmatrelvir (a component of PAXLOVID) has shown efficacy in reducing COVID-19 symptoms for those at high risk of severe disease, it has shown limited efficacy in mild-to-moderate cases, and drug-resistant viral isolates have been reported. Thus, there is still a need for antivirals that target distinct SARS-CoV-2 mechanisms.

Highly conserved regulatory viral RNA structures represent an underexplored class of antiviral targets. Recent studies identified essential RNA regulatory elements that showed significant sequence conservation among SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 variants. These included the SARS-CoV-2 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) and the frameshift site located at the 1a and 1b open reading frame (ORF) junction. − Early structural characterization of the UTRs resulted in the identification of several RNA stem-loop structures. , Our previous small molecule targeting campaign against these stem-loop structures resulted in the discovery of the first 5′ UTR RNA-targeted SARS-CoV-2 antivirals.

RNA tertiary structures, such as pseudoknots, are often considered to be ideal small molecule targets due to the higher likelihood of forming appropriate binding pockets and of being more unique among structures in the transcriptome. , During SARS-CoV-2 translation, the RNA elements in the viral frameshift site, including a highly conserved RNA pseudoknot structure, work together to promote a change to the reading frame of the ribosome that moves it one nucleotide in the 5′ direction (Figure ). This programmed −1 ribosomal frameshift (−1 PRF) occurs a specific fraction of the time the frameshift site is encountered. The frequency of slippage, known as the frameshift efficiency (FSE), dictates the molar ratio of proteins produced from the original (0 frame) and an alternate (−1) reading frame. Frameshift sites are thought to have evolved to produce optimal ratios of protein expression required for each virus’ unique reproductive needs. Consequently, increasing or decreasing the frameshift efficiency can severely impact virus infectivity. , For example, alterations to the SARS-CoV frameshift efficiency drastically reduced viral genome replication. Similarly, decreases in SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency correlated with decreases in viral titer. Therefore, −1 PRF may be a rate-limiting step in the virus life cycle suitable for antiviral targeting. ,,

While a three-stemmed pseudoknot structure predominates in the crystallographic and cryo-EM studies published, − the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift site structure is dynamic and readily samples alternate conformations. ,− Probing studies of the viral RNA within the context of an infected cell revealed a variety of structures that the frameshift site can adopt while being translated, with the pseudoknot determined in crystal structures being just one of the many conformations adopted within the ensemble. − The structural ensemble plays a critical role in viral protein synthesis. Indeed, mutations that selectively stabilize individual conformers, targeted disruption of the predominant pseudoknot structure with antisense oligonucleotides, , and small molecule driven changes to the conformational landscape all inhibit SARS-CoV-2 frameshift site function.

Several studies aimed at targeting the −1 PRF event have confirmed its potential as an antiviral target. Reported SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF targeting small molecules with antiviral activity published in the course of this study include antibacterial compounds (merafloxacin, Geneticin, and carrimycin), a protease inhibitor (nafamostat), and a SARS-CoV pseudoknot-targeting ligand (MTDB , ) with varying binding, inhibitory, and antiviral efficacy. While their antiviral activity remains untested, the aminoquinazolines and ribonuclease targeting small-molecule chimeras also represent promising classes of −1 PRF inhibitors. Many of these small molecules were identified through high-throughput screens that evaluated large commercial compound libraries in phenotypic assays. ,− More recently, structure-based design screening approaches and RNA-biased libraries were used against the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot. ,, The use of smaller RNA-focused libraries often leads to increased hit rates when compared to large commercial libraries or screens of FDA-approved drugs. By enriching libraries with unique features of RNA binding ligands, lead molecules are more likely to be identified, and additional insights into structure–activity landscapes can be revealed. ,,

In this work, novel SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot targeting small molecules were discovered using an RNA-biased synthetic library. The library was first screened for binding to the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot using an indicator displacement assay (IDA). Candidate compounds identified in the primary screen were tested for their ability to alter the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency using in vitro and in cellulo dual-luciferase frameshift assays, which were validated with published SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF inhibitors. Both assays consistently revealed seven synthetic inhibitors with dose-dependent activity, and each inhibitor reduced viral titers for SARS-CoV-2 in lung cell lines at nontoxic concentrations. The antiviral activity of one inhibitor greatly surpassed that of merafloxacin, the leading industry standard for SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF inhibitors. Preliminary mechanistic studies suggested that at a minimum, weak pseudoknot binding was required for activity and that multiple binding conformations and/or mechanisms of action might be at play. Overall, this work used a unique experimental pipeline for the discovery of synthetic small molecules that target the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift site pseudoknot structure and decrease its −1 PRF efficiency in in vitro and in cellulo reporter assays. The substantial antiviral activities of these inhibitors in lung cell lines highlight that they can be further derivatized to increase potency.

Results and Discussion

Hit Compound Identification

To identify hit compounds that target the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift site pseudoknot, an in-house synthetic library of amiloride (DMA) and diminazene (DMZ) compounds (Figure A) was screened against the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot using a fluorescent IDA. − DMAs have been explored for targeting a variety of RNA secondary structures and through diversification of various positions (Figure A) have proven to be tunable for different RNA structures. − DMZs have been shown to bind and modulate the stability of a triple helix structure in vitro. These two scaffolds represent distinct and tunable chemical space shown to target structures similar to those found in the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot. The high affinity binding of the RiboGreen fluorescent indicator to the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot facilitated the development of an IDA for library screening. As previously described, this assay leverages the fluorescent properties of RiboGreen to measure ligand binding, where small molecule displacement of the dye is observed as a decrease in fluorescence. The screening was performed at 25 μM small molecule concentrations and hit compounds were defined as small molecules that displaced RiboGreen by more than 25% (Figure B). This cutoff was more stringent than a previously used 20% displacement cutoff, due to the high (39%) hit rate observed. Thirteen diverse hits (Figure S1) and five nonhits (Figure S2) were selected for further screening in functional assays.

Establishing the Use of a SARS-CoV-2 Dual-Luciferase Frameshift Assay to Identify Novel −1 PRF Inhibitors

While binding is a prerequisite for directly modifying the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency, it does not guarantee it. Historically, dual-luciferase, and more recently dual-fluorescent, , reporter assays were implemented to quantify frameshift efficiencies using two RNAs. The “experimental” RNA has the viral frameshift site (the slippery sequence, spacer, and pseudoknot) located between two ORFs that encode reporter proteins, such that the downstream/upstream reporter protein molar ratio is dictated by the −1 frameshift efficiency. The “in-frame control” RNA has the same components, but the frameshift site slippery sequence is mutated to eliminate frameshifting and a nucleotide is added after the slippery sequence to place the downstream ORF in-frame. When this RNA is translated, the upstream and downstream proteins are produced in equimolar amounts. Thus, the frameshift efficiency is calculated by dividing the downstream/upstream protein ratios produced from the experimental and in-frame control RNAs, respectively.

To test small molecule hits for their activity against the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift site, we adapted and optimized a dual-luciferase frameshift assay for in vitro and in cellulo screening (Materials and Methods). In this assay, the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift site, along with three (5′ end) and six (3′ end) nucleotides (nts) of flanking viral RNA (nts 13459–13548 from NC_045512.2), was placed between the Renilla and firefly luciferase ORFs. The SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiencies measured in vitro and in cellulo were 26.0% ± 0.4% and 51.9% ± 0.9%, respectively (Table , untreated). These values are in line with published SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiencies that range from ∼15% to ∼60% depending on the experimental context. ,,,,,,−

To further demonstrate the use of these assays to identify SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency inhibitors, the impacts of three previously published SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF inhibitors (merafloxacin, MTDB, and nafamostat) were evaluated (Table ). In vitro, the reporter RNAs were incubated with a small molecule and buffer prior to their translation in rabbit reticulocyte lysate. At 50 μM in vitro, merafloxacin was the best inhibitor, nafamostat had a relatively modest impact, and MTDB had no effect. To evaluate the effects of small molecules in cellulo, Vero E6 cells were pretreated with the small molecule prior to the transfection of capped and polyadenylated reporter RNAs. At 50 μM in cellulo, MTDB was a better inhibitor than merafloxacin, and both were more potent than nafamostat. MTDB’s ability to modulate the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency varies in published literature, ,,,, so its inconsistent behavior across these assays is not unprecedented. Nafamostat’s relatively modest impact on the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency reported here is corroborated by work from Jäger and colleagues but contrasts with the inhibition observed in its original identification. Given the consistency of merafloxacin across assays, additional data at different concentrations (Table S2) were collected to verify that its SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF inhibition was dose-dependent (Figure S3), as previously reported. Collectively, there is good agreement between the merafloxacin results reported here and those published using similar experimental systems, ,,,, supporting its use as a reliable positive control in the experiments that follow.

Evaluating Small Molecules Identified from the IDA Screen for Their Ability to Modify the SARS-CoV-2 Frameshift Efficiency

With the validity of the in vitro and in cellulo assay established, the experimental system was next used to determine if any of the IDA hits were novel SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF modulators. The impact of 18 small molecules (13 IDA hits and 5 IDA nonhits) on the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency were evaluated in vitro. Data collected following a 50 μM small molecule treatment were initially assessed to determine if protein translation was generally impacted at that concentration. Conditions that changed the Renilla luminescence levels by less than 50% were considered to have minimal impacts on general translation (Table S3); a benchmark previously established in the field. Additional data at more dilute concentrations were collected for the subset of small molecules that inhibited Renilla luminescence at 50 μM. Further, a control where small molecules were added after translation was performed to ensure changes in firefly and Renilla luminescence were not due to altered enzyme activity (Figure S4).

In vitro hits were identified by comparing data at the highest small molecule concentration tested where translation was minimally impacted relative to the SARS-CoV-2 (1% DMSO control) frameshift efficiency (Figure and Table S4). High-throughput screening hits are typically identified using the mean ± three standard deviations (3SD) cutoffs. However, when the SARS-CoV-2 (1% DMSO) mean ± 3SD (23.5% ± 5.7%) was used as a cutoff, only DMZ-P8 (50 μM) and DMZ-P1 (10 μM) qualified as hits (both had an FSE ≤17.8%). To avoid overoptimization of the in vitro hits at the expense of potential in vivo activity, the hit threshold was relaxed to the mean ± 2SD (23.5% ± 3.8%). When this cutoff was applied (FSE ≤19.7% or FSE ≥27.3%), and the small molecule treatment frameshift efficiencies with SEMs that overlap the thresholds were included, seven small molecules qualified as hits (Figure ). Of these seven hits, only one (DMZ-P0) was not a hit in the primary indicator displacement assay. This low rate of false negative hits from the primary screen is within previously reported ranges. , At 50 μM (Figure A), four small molecules (DMA-210, DMA-212, DMA-213 and DMZ-P8) met the hit criteria without impacting general translation. At this concentration, the hits were better inhibitors than nafamostat and MTDB, but none were as potent as merafloxacin (Table and Table S4). At 25 μM (Figure B), one small molecule (DMZ-P0) produced a FSE below the hit threshold and at 10 μM (Figure C), two small molecules (DMZ-M15 and DMZ-P1) met the hit criteria. At both concentrations, merafloxacin was a more effective inhibitor than the three hits identified at those concentrations (Table S4).

Next, in vitro hits were evaluated using the in cellulo dual-luciferase frameshift assay (Table S5). The 50 μM data set was used to determine if these small molecules also qualified as hits when evaluated in cells. When the SARS-CoV-2 (1% DMSO control) frameshift efficiency mean ± 3SD was used as a cutoff, all seven small molecules qualified as hits in cells (Figure S5). The seven tested hits minimally impacted general in cellulo translation at all concentrations tested (10 μM, 25 μM, 50 μM, and 100 μM), as determined by changes in Renilla luminescence levels (data not shown). In the in cellulo assays, at 50 μM all hits were better inhibitors than nafamostat, but none were as potent as merafloxacin or MTDB (Table vs Figure S5). Overall, seven novel frameshift inhibitors were identified in the in vitro and in cellulo assays.

Several SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF Inhibitors Have Evidence of Dose-Dependent Activity

Following the identification of hits from the in vitro and in cellulo frameshift assays, the dose-dependent profile of each hit was evaluated (Figures and S6), though a limited number of concentrations could be considered in vitro given impacts on general translation. The inhibition profiles of DMA-210 (Figure A) and DMZ-M15 (Figure B) demonstrated a clear dose-dependency, and DMA-212, DMA-213, DMZ-P0, DMZ-P1, and DMZ-P8 followed a similar trend (Figure S6). Overall, these results suggest that the seven inhibitors directly modulate the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency.

Binding and RT-qPCR Assays Reveal Complex Modes of Small Molecule −1 PRF Inhibition

With dose-dependence demonstrated for seven synthetic small molecules, we looked toward additional assays that may provide insight into the mechanism of action (Figure ). To gain insight into relative affinities, titrations were performed using the aforementioned IDA with RiboGreen and the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot (Figure S7). Comparing the calculated CD50, or concentration where the dye is 50% displaced, to in vitro and in cellulo activity does not show strong trends (Figure C). Furthermore, the positive control merafloxacin, which consistently showed the strongest activity in frameshift assays in this work as well as another recently published study, had one of the weaker relative affinities. Similarly, the strongest synthetic inhibitor in cellulo was DMZ-M15, which appeared to have the weakest affinity by IDA titration. Conversely, synthetics that showed strong affinity, such as DMZ-P8, had modest effect on the frameshift efficiency. This disconnect between affinity and activity has been observed in other studies of RNA-targeting with small molecules ,, and may point to specific shifts in conformational landscape that are more impactful than overall affinity.

Members of these scaffolds have been reported to bind to RNA structures in other systems, which may also impact cellular activity. As mentioned above, the DMZ scaffold was previously used to target the MALAT-1 triple helix and the CD50 values of the four DMZ hits herein were reported (Table S6). Similarly, the DMA scaffold was previously used to target the HIV-1 TAR stem-loop, stem-loops in the SARS-CoV-2 5′ UTR, and the EV71 IRES. While the DMA hits herein were not utilized in those studies, a subset of their CD50 values for stem-loops in the EV71 IRES and SARS-CoV-2 5′ UTR were measured to better understand their selectivity (Table S6). The DMA and DMZ hit CD50 values for other RNA structures were generally comparable in magnitude to their CD50 for the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot (Figure C), suggesting they may have limited RNA binding selectivity, though differences in assay set up (indicator, buffer, RNA concentration) impact these comparisons.

We next used an in vitro assay that might give insight into conformational changes via impacts in read-through stability, specifically an in vitro reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) method for measuring small molecule stabilization or destabilization of an RNA structure. The assay reports on the relative efficiency of reverse transcriptase to produce cDNA in the absence and presence of small molecules, with which the RNA was pretreated, by comparing the PCR quantification cycle (Cq). If the RNA structure is stabilized by a small molecule, the reverse transcriptase will less efficiently synthesize cDNA. As was previously shown, both merafloxacin and nafamostat impacted the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot stability as seen by statistically significant shifts in cDNA amplification when present (Figure A). Three of the seven hits (DMZ-M15, DMZ-P1, DMZ-P8), as well as nafamostat (Figure A,B), were classified as pseudoknot destabilizers. These molecules produced a statistically significant and positive change in Cq (ΔCq, Figure B). The relative shift compared to DMSO in this assay (Figures B and S8) indicates that the small molecules alter the conformation to a structure that is easier for the RT to readthrough, such as a destabilized pseudoknot. This destabilized structure might be expected given the decreased frameshifting observed for these molecules (Figure C). In contrast, merafloxacin was a pseudoknot stabilizer and decreased readthrough in this assay. This result is consistent with a previous report using RT-qPCR and with what one would expect given the recent evidence for its thermal stabilization of SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot. While the RT-qPCR results provide insights into potential modes of action, this method is also not sufficient for explaining activity in the reporter assays. This result is consistent with recent work by Rahaman et al., who did not observe a correlation between the thermodynamic stability of PNA-RNA complexes and SARS-CoV-2 frameshift inhibition.

Evaluation of SARS-CoV-2 Antiviral Activity

To gain insight into the relevant biological properties of the hit compounds, we evaluated their toxicity profiles and antiviral activities in two human lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, Calu-3 and HCC827, that are naturally permissive for SARS-CoV-2 replication. Cells were incubated with 1, 2, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μM of each compound as well as merafloxacin as a control and viability was assessed at various time points postincubation. While merafloxacin, DMZ-M15, DMZ-P0, DMZ-P1 and DMZ-P8 displayed no toxicity, DMA-210, DMA-212 and DMA-213 substantially decreased cell viability at 50 and 100 μM concentrations for both Calu-3 (Figure S9A) and HCC827 (Figure S9B) cells. The CC50 values for the DMAs on the Calu-3 cells ranged from 43–47 μM (46.98 μM (DMA-210), 46.24 μM (DMA-212), and 43.76 μM (DMA-213)) at 20–24 h post-treatment, the time point when antiviral activity was tested in these cells. The CC50 values on HCC827 cells were lower and ranged from 21 to 29 μM (27.85 μM (DMA-210), 21.27 μM (DMA-212), and 28.52 μM (DMA-213)) at 48 h post-treatment.

We next determined the compound antiviral properties at concentrations that did not affect cell viability. Calu-3 and HCC827 cells were preincubated with merafloxacin (20 μM), DMA-210 (10 μM), DMA-212 (10 μM), DMA-213 (10 μM), DMZ-M15 (50 μM), DMZ-P0 (50 μM), DMZ-P1 (50 μM), and DMZ-P8 (50 μM) or 0.2% DMSO as a control for 2 h before inoculation with SARS-CoV-2 (2019-nCoV/USA-WA1/2020) and during the course of infection. Cell-associated viral RNA (vRNA) and infectious virus release in cell culture supernatants was analyzed at 24 h postinfection (hpi) for the more permissive Calu-3 cells and 48 hpi for the less permissive HCC827 cells. Reference compound merafloxacin led to a 5–10-fold decrease in cell-associated vRNA and infectious virion release in both cell models (Figure A,B). DMZ-based compounds generally impacted virus replication more modestly in both cell lines, though in some cases the differences were statistically significant (Figure A,B). DMA-based compounds more potently inhibited SARS-CoV-2 replication, with DMA-210 leading to a 50 and a 1000-fold reduction in cell-associated vRNA levels in Calu-3 and HCC827 cells, respectively, which translated to over a 3-log decrease in infectious virion release (Figure A,B). The higher antiviral potency of the DMA-based compounds compared to DMZ-based compounds may in part be ascribed to their cytotoxicity. However, it is noteworthy that DMA-210 displayed significant antiviral activity at 10 μM without any apparent compound-induced toxicity, which was much greater than DMA-212 and DMA-213 despite similar toxicity profiles among the three DMAs (Figures A,B and S9, S10). DMA-210 lost its antiviral activity at 1 μM (Figure S10), suggesting a EC50 value between 1 and 10 μM.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate the antiviral properties of the hit compounds in relevant infection settings. While the SARS-CoV-2 has diversified considerably since the beginning of the pandemic, in particular due to mutations within the S gene that drives immune escape, the frameshifting sequence remained well conserved. Thus, although we used the parental 2019-nCoV/USA-WA1/2020 strain in the above-mentioned experiments, we anticipate that the compounds will be equally effective against more recent SARS-CoV-2 strains. Ultimately, the results highlight that the seemingly small changes in SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency induced by DMA- and DMZ-based compounds in in vitro and in cellulo reporter assays may translate into a significant impact on virus replication, though additional modes of action must be considered. For example, antiviral activity could also be derived from small molecule binding to other regulatory structures in the SARS-CoV-2 genome, such as the 5′ UTR.

Conclusion

This work identified and characterized novel RNA-targeted SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF modulators through a variety of assays. A library of in-house synthesized amilorides (DMA) and diminazene (DMZ) compounds was screened via IDA to identify SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot binding molecules, and 18 compounds were tested for their ability to alter the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency using in vitro and in cellulo dual-luciferase frameshift assays. Seven inhibitors showed evidence of dose-dependent activity in both assays and were further evaluated for their relative affinities to the pseudoknot as well as impacts on the stability of the pseudoknot structure, as inferred via RT readthrough. Finally, the inhibitors were evaluated for anti-SARS-CoV-2 activity and found to inhibit replication at concentrations with no observed toxicity to human cells. In these assays, the DMAs and DMZs were generally more and less potent than merafloxacin, respectively, and DMA-210 demonstrated markedly increased antiviral activity, highlighting it as a lead compound for further development.

While the IDA titrations confirmed that some binding is required for activity, little correlation was noted between the biochemical (relative affinity, pseudoknot stabilization), dual-luciferase (in vitro and in cellulo FSE), and antiviral assays. The apparent discrepancies are not entirely unexpected given past precedent for differences in affinity and functional trends, ,, likely due to the specific shift in RNA conformational landscapes needed to modulate function and the well-established dynamic landscape of the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot. ,− ,,− Differences in impacts between the biochemical and biological assays further support that −1 PRF inhibitors may achieve functional modulation through a variety of binding modes and that RNA binding proteins may play an important role. , In addition, it is now known that construct length and long–range interactions outside of the frameshift site influence the pseudoknot conformational landscape and should be considered. ,− , Finally, the limited correlation between −1 PRF modulation and antiviral activity likely reflects the contextual importance of the actively replicating viral genome and/or the need to investigate potential off-target effects, including off-target RNA binding. This work underscores the complexity of targeting dynamic RNA structures and the benefits of screening for function early and later optimizing for mode of action, as the use of orthogonal techniques ultimately led to the identification of DMA-210 as a lead antiviral candidate for future development.

In sum, the combination of an IDA RNA-biased synthetic library screen with dual-luciferase frameshift assays, antiviral assays, and mechanistic biochemical studies represents a unique approach to the discovery and characterization of small molecules targeting the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift site pseudoknot. It led to the identification of seven synthetic SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF inhibitors that were generally comparable to the positive controls in binding, inhibitor activity, impacts to pseudoknot stability, and antiviral activity, but likely exhibit distinct modes of action. As a class, the DMAs were more potent antivirals than the leading −1 PRF inhibitor, merafloxacin, with DMA-210 showing promising potential as a highly potent antiviral scaffold for future development. Future directions will include the derivatization and expansion of the RNA-biased small molecule library, optimization of DMA-210 for activity and selectivity, and in-depth work to examine the differences in mechanism of action, including changes to the conformational landscape inferred by NMR, chemical reactivity profiling, and strategic mutations to the pseudoknot structure. Modifications to pseudoknot construct design that incorporate more recent knowledge about pseudoknot structural dynamics, ,− ,,,− which were published after this study was initiated, will further enhance these studies. Overall, this work facilitated the discovery of synthetic small molecules that target the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift pseudoknot structure, decrease its −1 PRF efficiency, and significantly reduce viral titers. Given the constant threat of antiviral resistance and future coronavirus pandemics, as well as the millions of people impacted by postviral sequelae (i.e., long COVID), the importance of identifying new SARS-CoV-2 antivirals cannot be overstated. Ultimately, this work demonstrates the need for multiple modes of characterization in small molecule-RNA targeting programs and the possibility of multiple, distinct modes of action to achieve small molecule modulation of SARS-CoV-2 −1 PRF and antiviral activity.

Materials and Methods

Indicator Displacement Assay

The protocol was adapted from a previously reported procedure by the Hargrove laboratory. A serial dilution of the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot RNA (nucleotides 13,472–13,551 from NCBI Reference Sequence NC_045512.2, Table S7) was performed in HEPES buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH, 52.6 mM KCl, 0.1 mM MgCl2, pH = 7.4) in a 96 well plate in triplicate. Eight μL of each dilution were transferred to a 384 well plate followed by 8 μL of a 500 nM solution of RiboGreen dye in the same buffer (Invitrogen). The plates were excited at 487 nm (8 nm slit) and emission was recorded at 530 nm (8 nm slit, focal height 11.3 mm) using a CLARIOstar plate reader (BMG labtech). The affinity of the dye for the RNA construct was determined by fitting the raw fluorescence in GraphPad Prism version 8.3.1 for Macintosh (GraphPad Software, La Jolla California USA, (www.graphpad.com)) by fitting to the Log­[Agonist] vs response–variable slope model using eq . 1 Y=Bottom+(XHillSlope)×(Top−BottomXHillSlope+log⁡CD50HillSlope) where Y is normalized % change in fluorescence intensity, X is RNA concentration, Bottom is lowest fluorescence % change, and Top is highest fluorescence % change. Affinity of the dye for the RNA construct was used as the ideal RNA concentration (102 nM) for high-throughput screening and small molecule titrations.

For the high-throughput screening, small molecules were tested at 25 μM (stocks in DMSO), for dose dependent-response a serial dilution of small molecule (0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150 μM) was performed by robotically transferring small molecules using an Echo 550 Acoustic liquid handler (Labcyte) in a 384 well plate. For dose dependent-response, small molecule stocks were prepared in DMSO, with the exception of DMZ-P0 which was prepared in water due to low solubility in DMSO after centrifuging the plate prior to transferring using the Echo 550 Acoustic liquid hander. Then, 10 μL of the solution with 102 nM CoV-2 PK and 0.5 μM of RiboGreen (Invitrogen) was dispensed in the 384 well plates using a ThermoFisher liquid dispenser. The RNA was previously annealed by heating to 95 °C for 5 min and cooling to 0 °C on ice for 30 min. The 384 well plates were shaken for 5 min, centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 1 min, and incubated in the dark for 20 min. The plates were excited at 487 nm (8 nm slit) and emission was read at 525 nm (8 nm slit, focal height 11.3 nm) using a CLARIOstar plate reader (BMG Labtech). Percent fluorescence indicator displacement (% FID) was calculated by subtracting and, subsequently, dividing by the blank wells with RNA-dye complex and no small molecule as shown in eq . 2 %FID=(F0−FF0)×100 where F 0 is the fluorescence of the blank well with RNA + dye and no small molecule and F is the fluorescence of the well with all three components (RNA + dye + small molecule).

Each technical triplicate was averaged, and the resulting FID values were averaged between three independent experiments. The binding curve and (CD50) value was obtained by using a nonlinear fit curve ([Inhibitor] vs response) with variable slope at four parameters (GraphPad Prism Software version for Macintosh 9.4.1, La Jolla, California, USA (www.graphpadprism.com) as shown in eq . Reported values are averages of three independent experiments ± standard error of the mean.

p2luc Plasmid Construction

Experimental and in-frame control plasmids were cloned to produce the SARS-CoV-2 (nucleotides 13,459–13,548 from NCBI Reference Sequence NC_045512.2, Table S7) reporters. In each p2luc plasmid, the Renilla (rluc) and firefly (fluc) luciferase genes were separated by DNA coding for a frameshift site that is also flanked by three and six nucleotides of SARS-CoV-2 sequence on its 5′ and 3′ ends, respectively. In the experimental plasmid, fluc was positioned downstream from the frameshift site and was in the −1 reading frame relative to rluc. This positioning is analogous to the positioning of the coronavirus ORF1a and ORF1b genes. In the in-frame control plasmids, the frameshift site slippery sequence was modified (UUUAAAC was changed to CUUCAAC) to create a nonfunctional frameshift site. Additionally, a C nucleotide was added on the 3′ end of the modified slippery sequence to place fluc in-frame with rluc. In all plasmids, a T7 RNA polymerase promoter was included upstream of the rluc gene and a Pmll restriction site was included downstream of the fluc gene. Cloning was accomplished for the SARS-CoV-2 plasmids similarly to work previously described. Briefly, DNA inserts were prepared by the restriction digestion of 30 ng of gBlock Gene Fragments DNA with BamH1 and SacI (NEB). The digested inserts were subsequently purified with a QIAquick PCR cleanup kit (Qiagen). The p2luc vector was prepared by digestion with the same restriction enzymes, dephosphorylated with recombinant Shrimp Alkaline Phosphatase (NEB), and gel purified (Qiagen). The p2luc plasmids were ligated using a 1:6 vector to insert molar ratio. Recombinant plasmids were transformed into 5-alpha Competent E. coli (NEB), purified (Qiagen), and their sequences were verified with Sanger Sequencing (Laragen). Plasmids DNA was linearized using the Pmll restriction enzyme (NEB) and purified using QIAquick PCR cleanup kits (Qiagen).

RNA Synthesis and Purification for the In Vitro Frameshift Assay

Microgram quantities of RNA for the frameshift assay were in vitro transcribed and purified with minor differences from a previously described protocol. The 510 μL transcription reactions included: NTPs (11.03 mM), linearized p2luc plasmid DNA (0.05 μg/μL), transcription buffer (30 mM Tris pH 8.0, 10 mM DTT, 2 mM spermidine trihydrochloride, 0.01% Triton X-100), MgCl2 (38 mM), His6-tagged T7 RNA Polymerase (7.84 X), RNasin Plus RNase A inhibitor (0.78 U/μL), and DEPC-treated (0.002%) milli-Q water. The reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. Inorganic pyrophosphate was removed by centrifugation (5 min, 13,200 rpm, 4 °C). The template DNA was then digested by adding 25.5 μL of RQ1 RNase-free DNase (1 U/μL, Promega), 60 μL of RQ1 buffer (10X), and 4.5 μL of DEPC-treated (0.002%) milli-Q water to the 510 μL transcription reaction. The 600 μL mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Once again, the inorganic pyrophosphate was removed by centrifugation. The nucleic acids were separated from proteins using a phenol/chloroform extraction. Amicon Ultra 15 mL 100 kDa MWCO centrifugal filters were used to further separate the full-length RNA from remaining reaction components. After an initial dilution to 12 mL with DEPC-treated (0.002%) milli-Q water, the samples were centrifuged according to manufacture instructions (Amicon). The samples were desalted with four additional 12 mL washes of DEPC-treated (0.002%) milli-Q water. RNA integrity and purity were assessed using 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and the A260/A280 purity ratios, respectively. Single use aliquots containing 32.8 μg of RNA (∼1 μg/μL) were stored at −80 °C.

In Vitro Frameshift Assay

In a total volume of 8.76 μL, RNA (0.25 μg/μL, 0.28 μM) was mixed with buffer (50 mM Tris, 50 mM KCl, pH 7.4), DMSO (5%), the small molecule (5X), and DEPC-treated (0.002%) milli-Q water. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Amino acids (0.02 mM, Promega), RNasin Plus RNase A inhibitor (0.8 U/μL, Promega), nuclease-treated Rabbit Reticulocyte Lysate (70% of the total volume, Promega), and additional DEPC-treated (0.002%) milli-Q water were added to reach a total volume of 43.75 μL. In this volume, the initial component final concentrations were RNA (0.05 μg/μL, 0.056 μM), buffer (10 mM Tris, 10 mM KCl, pH 7.4), DMSO (1%), and small molecule (1X; equivalent to 10, 25, 50, or 100 μM depending on the experiment). Reactions were incubated at 30 °C (Hybex heat-block) for 90 min and then on ice for 20 min. Three 12.5 μL aliquots were transferred to nonadjacent wells (technical replicates) in a 96-Well Half-Area Polystyrene plate (Corning). Luminescence was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay (Promega) and a GloMax Navigator dual-injector Microplate Luminometer (Promega), as previously described. , Following the addition of 25 μL of the Luciferase Assay Reagent II (Promega) and a 2 s delay, firefly luminescence was measured for 10 s. Renilla luminescence was measured after an equivalent addition of the Stop & Glo (Promega) reagent and time delay.

Each independent replicate FSE was calculated by dividing the average firefly/Renilla luminescence ratio produced from the experimental RNA translation reaction by the average firefly/Renilla luminescence ratio from the corresponding control RNA translation reaction. Where each average firefly/Renilla luminescence ratio was determined from the three in-plate technical replicates. Data are reported as the mean FSE ± SEM. While the addition of buffer is not typical of dual-luciferase frameshift assays, it was included to facilitate small molecule solubility and promote RNA folding at a physiologically relevant pH (pH 7.4). The buffer had only a minor impact on the SARS-CoV-2 frameshift efficiency (data not shown). Control experiments used to evaluate the impact of the small molecules on luciferase enzyme activity were completed in triplicate in a manner identical to the above with one exception. In these experiments the small molecules, which were used at their highest concentration tested where translation was minimally impacted, were added after the 90 min incubation at 30 °C. Meaning that the 2.19 μL of small molecule (5×) was added to a mixture of 41.56 μL following the incubation step.

All small molecules were initially screened at 50 μM. Additional data were collected at 10 μM, 25 μM, and 100 μM to evaluate their dose-dependence inhibition profiles. These data were also used to empirically determine the concentration at which each small molecule had no to minimal (less than 2-fold) impact on protein translation, as determined from changes in the Renilla luminescence levels. Changes in experimental RNA Renilla levels that were less than 50% when comparing the small molecule treatment and the 1% DMSO control were considered minimal. This benchmark was previously established. Hits were selected using data from 50 μM experiments or the highest small molecule concentration tested where translation was minimally impacted, whichever was lower. Hits were defined as small molecules that caused a change in SARS-CoV-2 (1% DMSO) frameshift efficiency by greater than the mean ± 2 standard deviations.

RNA Synthesis and Purification for the Cell-Based Frameshift Assay

RNA synthesis and purification were performed as described. Briefly, plasmids p2luc experimental and p2luc in-frame control were linearized using restriction enzyme PmlI and purified using the QIAQuick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). Template DNAs were used for in vitro transcription using the MEGAscript T7 Transcription Kit (Invitrogen) and the cap analog m7G­(5′)­ppp­(5′)­G (Ambion) to produce 5′-capped RNAs. They were subsequently 3′-end polyadenylated using the RNA Poly­(A) Tailing Kit (Invitrogen). Purified RNAs were quantified by spectrophotometry using the Nanodrop 2000 and assessed for integrity by agarose gel electrophoresis. Capped and polyadenylated RNAs were stored at −80 °C.

Cell-Based Frameshift Assay

Cell culture and dual luciferase assays were performed as described. Briefly, Vero E6 cells (African green monkey kidney; ATCC) were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (ThermoFisher). Cells were seeded in 24-well plates 1 day before transfection and grown to ∼90% confluency for transfection. Cells were cultured with 1% DMSO (vehicle control) or small molecules at 50 μM and 100 μM final concentrations for 30 min prior to transfection with 200 ng of control or FS-luciferase reporter RNAs. Cells were lysed after 24 h for dual luciferase assay-based frameshift assays as described for the in vitro-based translation assays above. Data were obtained from three transfections and three dual luciferase assays for each transfection. Frameshift efficiencies were calculated as described for the in vitro frameshift efficiency measurements.

RNA Synthesis for the In Vitro RT-qPCR Assay (SARS-CoV-2 Pseudoknot with 3′-SHAPE Cassette)

To facilitate these experiments, the SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot used in the IDA had an additional sequence (5′ UCGAUCCGGUUCGCCGGAUCCAAAUCGGGCUUCGGUCCGGUU 3′), a structure SHAPE cassette, appended to its 3′-end, which permits primer binding in a location outside of the RNA structure of interest (Table S7). DNA template sequence, forward and reverse primers were purchased from Integrated DNA technologies (IDT). For PCR amplification reagents were added to reach a 50 μL final reaction volume as detailed in Table S8. First, the entire working space was treated with RNase Zap to prevent contamination. The DNA template was then amplified for 30 cycles in an Eppendorf Echo thermocycler. A Zymo DNA-cleanup kit was then utilized to clean up the desired DNA sequence. A solution of amplified DNA in water was made to reach 50 ng/μL. The sequence was then in vitro transcribed (IVT) using the protocol detailed in Table S8 for each 50 μL reaction. The reaction was then incubated at 37 °C for 17 h. The reaction was then treated with 3 μL of DNase I (1000 units, NEB) and 6 μL DNase I buffer (NEB) twice for each 50 μL reaction in intervals of 30 min at 37 °C, followed by addition of 10% of the reaction volume of EDTA. The desired RNA was then extracted using phenol chloroform extraction and the aqueous layer was then subjected to ethanol precipitation. The concentrated product was then reconstituted in water and vortexed to obtain a fully dissolved heterogeneous solution. Purity and size of the RNA construct was confirmed by 10% TBE-Urea polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (ThermoFisher, Figure S11).

Two-Step RT-qPCR Assay

For a given reverse transcription reaction, RNA (SARS-CoV-2 pseudoknot with SHAPE cassette) that was slow-cool annealed with primer (5 min at 95 °C, 45 min at benchtop, and 15 min on ice) was incubated in buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH, 52 mM KCl, 0.1 mM MgCl2 at pH 7.4) with DMSO or small molecules at room temperature for 20 min. After incubation the PCR strip was placed on an ice block. A Mastermix of reverse transcription reaction reagents was prepared and aliquoted in each reaction to reach a total of 20 μL. Components were added to the reverse transcription reaction to reach the final concentration listed in Table S9. The PCR strip was then incubated at 37 °C in a thermocycler (Eppendorf, Nexus Gradient) for 15 min before inactivating the RT enzyme for 3 min at 98 °C. The PCR strip was then placed on an ice block and diluted with water to a total volume of 50 μL. In the meantime, a qPCR mix was prepared according to the concentrations listed in Table S9. The qPCR Mastermix was aliquoted in each well of a 96-well lightcycler plate (Roche 96 or Roche 480) and 2 μL of diluted RT reaction was added to each. The amplification protocol was run by incubating at 95 °C for 3 min followed by a 3-step amplification for 30 cycles and a final cooling to 40 °C over the course of 5 min. Results were analyzed using the Roche 96 light cycler software v1.1 or Roche 480 software. Due to differences in programs Cq values were calculated using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1) by setting the quantification limit to 10% of the max fluorescence value for a given experiment.

Cells, Toxicity Assessment and SARS-CoV-2 Infections

Calu-3 (kind gift of Sean Whelan lab) and HCC827 (ATCC CRL-2868) cells were maintained respectively in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) and Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. For toxicity assessment, cells were incubated with compounds at various concentrations and evaluated by RealTime-Glo MT cell viability assay kit (Promega) at various time points postinoculation following manufacturer’s instructions. CC50 values were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism software. For assessing the impact of compounds on SARS-CoV-2 replication, cells were pretreated with Merafloxacin (20 μM), DMA-210 (10 μM), DMA-212 (10 μM), DMA-213 (10 μM), DMZ-M15 (50 μM), DMZ-P0 (50 μM), DMZ-P1 (50 μM), and DMZ-P8 (50 μM) and 0.2% DMSO as control for 2 h before inoculation with SARS-CoV-2 strain 2019-nCoV/USA-WA1/2020 at MOIs displayed in Figure legends. Virus inoculum was diluted in DMEM cell culture media supplemented with 2% FBS and incubated with cells for 1 h. Following this incubation, cells were washed with 1x phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and replaced with cell culture media supplemented with the same concentrations of the compounds. Infections were monitored by focus-forming assay, and RT-qPCR of cell-associated viral RNA (N subgenomic RNA). Briefly, for focus-forming assay, Vero E6-TMPRSS2 (a gift of Sean Whelan lab) cells were inoculated with 10-fold serial dilutions of virus containing supernatants from Calu-3, and HCC827 cell lines, incubated for 1 h at 37 °C followed by addition of 2% methylcellulose and 2X MEM containing 4% FBS. Thirty hours postinfection cells were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained for SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (Sino Biological Inc.) followed by Goat antirabbit secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated (ThermoFisher) and manually counted under the microscope. For RT-qPCR, cell-associated RNA was extracted by Trizol following manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo-Fisher Scientific), SARS-CoV-2 viral RNA was detected by reverse transcription and amplification using the TaqMan RNA-to-CT 1-Step Kit (ThermoFisher), utilizing the primers (Forward 5′ ATGCTGCAATCGTGCTACAA 3′; Reverse 5′ GACTGCCGCCTCTGCTC 3′) and probe (5′ TCAAGGAACAACATTGCCAA 3′) sequences as described before (PMID: 34214467).
