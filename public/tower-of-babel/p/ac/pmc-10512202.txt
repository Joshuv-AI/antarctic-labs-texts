Cold stimulation test in patients with plaque psoriasis
Authors: Teodor Aleksiev, Zlatina Ivanova, Hristo Dobrev
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

The cold stimulation test (CST) is a noninvasive method to evaluate impaired skin microcirculation, mainly in subjects with primary and secondary Raynaud's phenomenon. In this test, fingers are exposed to cold water, and a skin thermometer is used to determine how long it takes to return to baseline temperature. The skin temperature measured immediately after cold stimulation shows the rate of vasoconstriction, while changes in temperature during the so‐called recovery period indicate the rate of vasodilation. The time of baseline temperature recovery of all fingers less than 15 min is considered normal, that is, the test is negative. A longer recovery time is considered abnormal, that is, the test is positive and indicates vascular pathology.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the applicability of a novel finger skin temperature measurement device for performing a cold stimulation test in patients with psoriasis vulgaris compared to healthy control subjects.

A total of 25 patients with histologically verified plaque psoriasis (11 men, 14 women, average age 45.9 ± 18.1 years) and 35 healthy control subjects (5 men, 30 women, average age 44.4 ± 14.9 years) were studied after giving informed consent. Patients with psoriasis did not have hand involvement, and the measurements were made before any treatment was applied. All participants were assessed according to the “PAL” criteria and had a total score <4 points, excluding the diagnosis of Raynaud's phenomenon. 1 Standardized CST consisting of exposure of both hands to water at a temperature of 10°C for 5 min was performed. Changes in skin temperature of both wrists and 2–5 fingers were measured using a novel infrared, noncontact skin finger temperature device (Courage + Khazaka, Cologne, Germany). Measurements were made before and 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min after cold stimulation. All measurements were taken after the subjects had acclimatized for at least 15 min at room temperature (22–24°C).

In terms of the percentage of cooling of the skin of the fingers after stimulation with cold water, there was no statistically significant difference between the studied groups of patients and controls.

CST was normal in 22 (86.0%) of the patients with psoriasis and in 29 (82.8%) of the healthy control subjects.

The average time for complete recovery of the initial temperature of the first finger and all fingers II‐V of both hands was as follows: 10.5 min and 14.1 min (psoriasis), and 8.7 min and 12.9 min (healthy control subjects).

There were no statistically significant differences between patients and controls (Table 1).

Additionally, we analyzed the CST results obtained according to the scoring scale introduced by Kadan et al. (2015). 2 Depending on the time for temperature recovery (min), the CST is evaluated as grade 0, 0–10 min (normal, i.e., healthy person); I, 11–15 min (slightly impaired, i.e., almost healthy person); II, 16–20 min (moderately impaired, i.e., moderately expressed vasospastic condition); III, 21–30 min (seriously impaired, i.e., moderately expressed vasospastic condition), and IV, > 31 min (seriously impaired, i.e., serious vasospastic condition).

Serious impairment of microcirculation (temperature recovery time for the first finger of both hands > 31 min) was found neither in the healthy control subjects, nor in the patients with plaque psoriasis.

Serious impairment of microcirculation (temperature recovery time for all fingers of both hands > 31 min) was found in none of the healthy control subjects and in one (4%) of the patients with plaque psoriasis (Table 2).

Our results showed that approximately the same percentage of psoriasis patients and healthy controls have a normal CST (86 vs. 83%) (p > 0.05; Student's t‐test). In psoriasis, the average time for full recovery of all fingers is longer than in the healthy controls (14.1 min vs. 12.9 min), but the values were in the normal range of less than 15 min, and the difference is not statistically significant (p > 0.05; Student's t‐test). Only one patient with psoriasis (4%) showed serious impairment of microcirculation (temperature recovery time of all fingers > 31 min) compared to none of the healthy control subjects.

These data suggest thе absence of significant skin peripheral microcirculation impairment in patients with plaque psoriasis without hand involvement.

In the present study, we applied CST for the first time to study the peripheral microcirculation in patients with psoriasis.

We found in the literature only one study measuring the skin temperature of the hands in patients with psoriasis. 3 However, it is distinguished by two features: the investigated patients have psoriasis on the hands, and two thermal stresses were performed: first, by immersion in an ice‐water at 0°C (cold challenge), and later, in a hot‐water bath at 45°C (heat challenge), each for a period of 60 s. The authors observed that in psoriasis patients, the fingertip temperature decreased on a cold challenge significantly less than in healthy controls. There was no significant difference in the response to the heat challenge between psoriatic and normal subjects.

There is a study that made an evaluation of thermal recovery times after thermostimulation at 5°C for 20 s. of the psoriatic skin before and after a course of cyclosporine therapy. 4 The results showed initial hyperthermia with precocious thermal recovery times, in comparison with the healthy perilesional skin. After therapy, a prolongation of the thermal recovery time was observed in the patients, associated with a significant improvement in psoriasis.

A significantly increased skin temperature among psoriasis patients compared to the control healthy group is reported by Sundareswaran et al., 2023. 5

In psoriasis, the increased skin temperature is related to the increased skin blood in the lesions. Interestingly, using a noninvasive handheld perfusion imager, Schaap et al. (2021) demonstrated increased blood perfusion not only in the psoriatic plaques but also in the perilesional skin. Moreover, this feature may have a predictive value on future psoriatic lesion expansion. 6

Further investigations using CST should be made in psoriatic patients with hand psoriatic involvement in order to explore the role of increased perilesional blood perfusion for temperature recovery from cold in these patients.

In conclusion, the novel skin finger temperature device proved to be useful for evaluating skin temperature changes after CST in patients with psoriasis vulgaris and healthy subjects. Using CST, we did not find a significant presence of pathological skin microcirculation in patients with psoriasis without hand involvement.
