Effects of enhanced recovery after surgery nursing on patients undergoing laparoscopic colectomy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Dan Zhang, Miaoxiu Zhong, Liujia Jin, Lingling Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) nursing protocols on patients undergoing laparoscopic colectomy.MethodsRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies investigating the impact of ERAS nursing on laparoscopic colectomy patients were retrieved from databases including CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, CBM, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science, with a search period spanning from inception to April 1, 2025. The quality of the included studies was assessed using appropriate tools; while the overall quality was acceptable, some studies had a higher risk of bias. Data were statistically analyzed using RevMan 5.3 software.ResultsA total of 7 RCTs and 7 quasi-experimental studies involving 1991 patients were included. The meta-analysis demonstrated that the ERAS nursing group exhibited significantly shorter hospital stays (SMD = -0.95, 95% CI: -1.64 to -0.25, P = 0.007), lower complication rates (OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.40–0.64, P < 0.00001), and earlier first flatus time (SMD = -1.05, 95% CI: -1.56 to -0.54, P < 0.0001) compared to the control group. High heterogeneity (I² >75%) was observed for both hospital stay and time to first flatus. However, no statistically significant difference was observed in readmission rates between the two groups (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.56–1.81, P = 0.97).ConclusionERAS nursing significantly shortens hospital stay, reduces the incidence of postoperative complications, and promotes the recovery of gastrointestinal function (earlier time to first flatus). However, due to the high heterogeneity and varying quality of the included studies and the relatively low quality of some studies, its effect on reducing readmission rates has not been definitively established. Therefore, further research is needed to minimize bias and enhance the reliability of the data.Trial registrationThe study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251165792).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04396-8.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common malignant tumor globally, with persistently high incidence and mortality rates [1]. Laparoscopic colectomy (LC) has become a primary surgical approach for colon cancer due to its minimally invasive advantages, with benefits including reduced postoperative pain and faster recovery [2]. However, issues such as delayed recovery of gastrointestinal function, postoperative complications, and prolonged hospital stay continue to impact the overall recovery process. To improve perioperative management, the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocol employs a multidisciplinary approach that integrates preoperative optimization, precise anesthesia, and early postoperative mobilization, aiming to reduce surgical stress response and facilitate recovery [3]. Although ERAS has been widely adopted in laparoscopic colectomy in recent years, its efficacy remains controversial. Multiple studies indicate that ERAS can significantly shorten hospital stays, reduce the incidence of postoperative complications, and promote the recovery of gastrointestinal function [4]– [5]. However, some studies suggest that certain interventions within the ERAS protocol may increase the risk of postoperative adverse reactions (such as postoperative nausea and vomiting) and readmission rates [6].

The current body of research varies in quality, and compliance with the ERAS protocol differs significantly across medical centers, leading to considerable heterogeneity in outcome evaluation. Therefore, this review aims to synthesize the latest clinical evidence through a systematic review and meta-analysis to objectively evaluate the impact of ERAS on key outcome measures in patients undergoing laparoscopic colectomy and to explore its applicability across different healthcare settings. By addressing existing knowledge gaps in current research, our study will provide a robust foundation for optimizing ERAS care pathways and updating clinical practice guidelines.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Studies with insufficient data, duplicate publications, or unavailable full texts. (2) Reviews, conference abstracts, case reports, or animal studies. For multiple studies derived from the same cohort, only the highest-quality study was retained, based on factors such as timing of investigation and completeness of outcome measures.

Rationale for study design inclusion

This analysis included both RCTs and quasi-RCTs. Although RCTs represent the gold standard, certain studies adopted quasi-RCT designs due to ethical concerns or difficulties in achieving full randomization. Despite differences in randomization rigor, both types of studies demonstrate considerable comparability in evaluating clinical outcomes. Therefore, we included both in the analysis and implemented quality assessment to mitigate the risk of bias.

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was performed across the following electronic databases from their inception to April 1, 2025: China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, VIP, China Biology Medicine (CBM), PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science. The complete and executable search strategies for each database, including all MeSH terms and free-text terms, are provided in the Supplementary Material (Additional File 1). Briefly, search terms encompassed concepts related to “Enhanced Recovery After Surgery” and “Colectomy” or “Laparoscopic Surgery”. No language restrictions were applied. This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251165792).

Literature screening and data extraction

The literature screening process initially involved automatic deduplication using the NoteExpress reference management system. After deduplication, preliminary screening was conducted by reviewing titles and abstracts. Articles that passed the initial screening were further evaluated by reading the full text, and those not meeting the inclusion criteria were excluded. Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies between the two researchers were resolved through consultation with a third researcher. The extracted data included the title, authors, publication date, study design, disease type, age, sample size, intervention methods, and outcome measures (mean ± standard deviation).

Quality assessment of literature

Two researchers independently evaluated the quality of the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (Handbook 5.1.0) [7] for RCTs and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for quasi-RCTs (2016) [8] from the JBI Centre for Evidence-Based Healthcare in Australia. For RCTs, the evaluation criteria included the following: Specific methods and procedures for random sequence generation; Allocation concealment methods; Blinding of participants and personnel; Blinding of outcome assessors; Completeness of outcome data and reporting of missing data; Selective reporting of outcomes (positive or negative); and other potential sources of bias. For quasi-RCTs, the evaluation criteria consisted of nine items: Demonstration of causality in the study; Baseline comparability between groups; Consistency of additional measures across groups; Presence of a control group; Multiple measurements of outcome indicators before and after intervention; Completeness of follow-up; Consistency in outcome measurement; Reliability of measurement methods; and appropriateness of data analysis methods. Studies were rated as follows: Grade A: low risk of bias (fully met al.l criteria); Grade B: moderate risk of bias (partially met the criteria); Grade C: high risk of bias (did not meet the criteria). In cases of disagreement between the two researchers, a third researcher was consulted to reach a consensus. If there is a discrepancy in the quality assessment of a study between the two reviewers, a third researcher will conduct a re-evaluation. The final decision was adopted as the consensus opinion. All assessment processes are thoroughly discussed by the research team to ensure consistency and impartiality in the quality evaluation.

Statistical methods

The statistical analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3 software (Review Manager Version 5.3; The Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK), with clinical heterogeneity assessed using the I² test. When P ≥ 0.05 and I² < 50%, it indicated no significant statistical heterogeneity among the studies, and a fixed-effects model was applied for the meta-analysis. Conversely, if statistical heterogeneity was present, the sources of heterogeneity were first analyzed. If no significant clinical differences were identified and no definitive statistical source of heterogeneity could be determined, a random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The included outcome measures in this study—hospital stay duration and time to first flatus—were continuous variables; thus, the standardized mean difference (SMD) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were used as the effect size. For categorical parameters, such as complication rates and readmission rates, the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% CI were adopted as the effect measure. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted based on research design (RCT vs. quasi-RCT), sample size, geographical region, and differences in interventions (such as specific components of the care protocol). In sensitivity analyses, the impact and stability of each study on the overall results were assessed by sequentially removing each study from the analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup analysis results

The subgroup analysis results regarding length of hospital stay showed that, based on country and region subgroups, ERAS demonstrated a greater effect in Asian countries (SMD = − 1.56, 95% CI: − 2.46 to − 0.66) compared to European countries (SMD = − 0.60, 95% CI: − 1.12 to − 0.08). However, the between-group difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.294). Subgroup analysis by sample size revealed that small-sample studies (n < 100) showed a larger effect size (SMD = − 1.42 vs. − 0.10), with a significant between-group difference (P < 0.0001), suggesting the possible presence of a small-sample effect. There was no significant difference in effect sizes across different study designs (P < 0.0001) (Table 3).

Results of publication bias analysis

To quantitatively assess publication bias, Egger’s linear regression test was performed (Fig. 7). The results showed no statistical significance (P = 0.08). Integrating both qualitative and quantitative analysis outcomes, this study found no significant evidence of publication bias, indicating that the existing meta-analysis results are relatively stable and reliable.

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of ERAS nursing on hospital stay duration through a meta-analysis. The results demonstrated that the ERAS group had a significantly shorter hospital stay compared to the control group. This finding aligns with conclusions from existing ERAS-related studies, further supporting the clinical advantage of ERAS nursing in reducing hospitalization time [23]. ERAS in nursing accelerates patient recovery by reducing surgical stress responses through multimodal interventions. Key measures include preoperative education, psychological support, early mobilization, early nutritional support, and multimodal analgesia management. These approaches aim to optimize postoperative recovery processes, reduce complications, shorten hospital stays, and improve overall patient outcomes. It is important to distinguish this from surgeon-led ERAS elements, such as preoperative carbohydrate loading, minimally invasive techniques, and intraoperative fluid management, which focus on minimizing surgical trauma. The differing intervention emphases between the two approaches may lead to divergent research outcomes.

By optimizing pain management (e.g., epidural analgesia) and promoting early mobilization, ERAS nursing reduces the incidence of complications such as pulmonary infections and ileus [24]. Furthermore, ERAS nursing advocates for early enteral nutrition, avoiding prolonged fasting, thereby hastening the recovery of intestinal motility. This effect is particularly pronounced in laparoscopic surgeries, which inherently reduce surgical trauma. By restricting intraoperative fluid overload and the application of glucocorticoids, ERAS effectively reduces inflammatory responses, thereby ameliorating postoperative fatigue syndrome and facilitating early patient discharge [25].

However, this meta-analysis also revealed that while ERAS significantly shortens hospital stay, its effects exhibit considerable clinical heterogeneity, which limits the generalizability of the conclusions. This heterogeneity may stem from variations in the implementation of interventions across studies, as well as differences in patient baseline characteristics (e.g., age, sex, and disease severity). Although sensitivity analysis and the use of a random-effects model partially mitigated this issue, heterogeneity persists and may affect the universality and precision of the results.

Therefore, future research should focus on high-quality randomized controlled trials to further clarify the optimal implementation protocol and target populations for ERAS, with the aim of refining perioperative management strategies.

Regarding complications and readmission rates, the meta-analysis in this study reveals the complex effects of ERAS in perioperative management. Although the intervention group showed a significantly lower incidence of complications compared to the control group, there was no statistically significant difference in readmission rates between the two groups. This phenomenon can be explored in depth from multiple dimensions, including intervention mechanisms, study design, and clinical practice. ERAS significantly reduces surgical stress responses and organ dysfunction by optimizing preoperative preparation (e.g., shortened fasting periods, psychological interventions), intraoperative management (e.g., temperature control, fluid balance), and postoperative recovery (e.g., early mobilization, multimodal analgesia). However, readmission rates are influenced by multiple factors, such as healthcare resource accessibility, socioeconomic factors, patient compliance, and long-term rehabilitation support. It should be noted that the interventions included in this study primarily focused on short-term optimization during the perioperative period, potentially failing to adequately address patients’ rehabilitation needs after discharge. For example, one study on colorectal surgery found that while the ERAS nursing group did not show a significant difference in overall readmission rates, they had a higher proportion of readmissions due to bowel obstruction. This suggests a potential contradiction between early postoperative recovery speed and long-term bowel function restoration [13]. This finding suggests that improving readmission rates may not solely rely on perioperative optimization measures, but also requires more comprehensive interventions focused on long-term recovery. Therefore, we hypothesize that while ERAS is effective in reducing perioperative complications, its impact on readmission rates may necessitate more holistic interventional strategies for support. The meta-analysis results of this study on the first exhaust time demonstrated that ERAS significantly shortened the first exhaust time in the intervention group, which is consistent with previous research findings [21]. A possible explanation is that ERAS nursing achieves more precise pain management by combining non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, opioids, and regional block techniques, thereby reducing opioid-related side effects (e.g., intestinal paralysis). Secondly, ERAS nursing advocates early postoperative enteral nutrition (typically within 6–12 h after surgery), which promotes the recovery of intestinal motility through mechanical stimulation and nutrient substrate supply. Relevant studies indicate that early enteral nutrition advances the first exhaust time by an average of 18 h, and the underlying mechanism may involve increased secretion of intestinal hormones (such as motilin) and optimization of gut microbiota structure [26]. Early postoperative ambulation plays a crucial role in shortening the first exhaust time by stimulating vagal nerve excitability and leveraging gravitational effects to enhance intestinal peristalsis. Future research should further clarify the best practice model of ERAS nursing through stratified analysis, mechanistic exploration, and intervention optimization to provide patients with more efficient perioperative management solutions.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the number of included studies is limited, and significant heterogeneity was observed in the comprehensive evaluation of these studies. Potential sources of heterogeneity may be attributed to the following aspects: Type of study design: for instance, differences between RCTs and quasi-RCTs; Patient characteristics: such as age, gender, and disease severity; Interventions: whether the specific components of ERAS interventions are consistent; Clinical settings: variations in practices across different hospitals and regions; Outcome measurement methods: whether different evaluation criteria and methodologies exist. Although sensitivity analysis and the use of a random-effects model partially mitigated this issue, it may still affect the generalizability and precision of the results. Second, the included literature comprised both RCTs and quasi-RCTs, and the higher methodological risk reduced the certainty of the findings. Additionally, as most of the included studies were short-term trials, the long-term intervention effects and potential adverse reactions remain unclear. Future high-quality studies with long-term follow-up are needed to validate the sustained benefits of ERAS nursing. This study assessed publication bias using funnel plots and Egger’s test. For the outcome of first flatus time, although the number of included studies was limited, no significant evidence of publication bias was detected. This suggests that our findings may be relatively stable and not substantially influenced by unpublished small-scale studies. However, given the potential for “positive results bias” in surgical research—where results with statistical significance are more likely to be published—caution should still be exercised when interpreting the results. Future large-scale, high-quality studies are needed to further validate the long-term benefits of ERAS protocols on gastrointestinal function recovery.

Conclusions

In conclusion, compared with the control group, ERAS nursing significantly reduced hospital stays, decreased complication rates, and promoted postoperative gastrointestinal function recovery (earlier time to first flatus). However, no significant advantage was observed in reducing readmission rates. Nevertheless, due to the high heterogeneity in some outcome measures, the limited number of included studies, and potential biases, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution. As more high-quality studies are published in the future, a comprehensive combined effect analysis and stratified multi-level subgroup analyses are planned. Additionally, future large-sample, high-quality, and standardized studies are still needed to further validate the long-term efficacy and clinical benefits of the intervention to optimize patient management strategies.
