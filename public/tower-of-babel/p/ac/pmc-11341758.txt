Tests of abnormal gaze behavior increase the accuracy of mild cognitive impairment assessments
Authors: Yoshiki Tamaru, Futoshi Matsushita, Akiyoshi Matsugi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTests to detect mild cognitive impairment (MCI) should have high sensitivity and accuracy. Previously, we reported on a cognitive composition test (CCT) that we developed to detect MCI. In this study, we compared gaze behavior parameters, namely, gaze fixation duration and gaze-shift frequency, during the CCT in participants with MCI and healthy controls (HC) to determine whether these parameters would accurately detect MCI in older adults. Participants performed CCT-A, -B, and -C tasks with varying difficulty levels while wearing eye-tracking devices. Performance time, gaze fixation duration, and gaze-shift frequency were analyzed. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to assess the identification accuracy. The MCI group was significantly slower in completing the CCT-C task and had a higher gaze-shift frequency into both the sample object space and workspace than the HC group. Gaze fixation duration in the sample object space increased in the MCI group as the CCT became difficult. Our findings indicated that combining the CCT with performance time and gaze pattern improved the accuracy of distinguishing between individuals with and without MCI and that patients with MCI have abnormal gaze behavior during cognitive tasks. Therefore, evaluation of gaze parameters may improve the accuracy of identifying patients with MCI.Subject terms: Health care, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

The rapidly increasing number of older adults with dementia is a serious social problem worldwide1. Non-pharmacological interventions, such as lifestyle habits modification, aerobic exercise, and cognitive training, at the prodromal stage of dementia or mild cognitive impairment (MCI), are possible ways to treat dementia2–4. In addition, pharmacological interventions such as LEQEMBI5 and ADUHELM6,7 have demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of older adults with MCI in clinical trials. Both interventions potentially slow the progression of dementia and/or aid improve cognitive impairment. Regardless of the intervention, detecting and beginning the intervention at the MCI stage is of vital importance.

Compared with healthy older adults, those with MCI exhibit early symptoms, including a decline in working memory8,9, attention8,10,11, visuospatial cognition, executive functions12–19, and upper limb dexterity20. We developed the cognitive and composition test (CCT), which combined a cognitive task and a composition task, as a screening test for MCI21. The CCT is a simple short-duration test that can be conducted anywhere. This test, which involves rearranging sticks in the same order as in a sample figure, assesses attention, working memory, and perception of object composition because it requires recognition of the arrangement, direction, and order of overlap of the sticks, and reproduction of the original composition. The CCT has three levels of difficulty, depending on the number of sticks, including the number of overlapping sticks. The performance time in this test was significantly longer as the difficulty level increased in individuals with MCI than in healthy individuals21.

Recently, behavioral methods for the early detection of MCI have attracted increasing attention, particularly the evaluation of eye movement using an eye-tracking device. Eye tracking is generally used to evaluate gaze movements noninvasively, quantitatively, and objectively22. Tales et al. reported that older adults with MCI made more errors in visual search tasks than healthy older adults23. Mclaughlin et al. and Dragan et al. reported delays in target search24,25, while Kapoula et al. reported that the gaze of older adults with MCI shifts to a non-target area13. Other studies reported that older adults with MCI have increased gaze fixation to target signals in gaze search tasks26–28, and that gaze fixation increases when recognizing complex figures29. Gaze behavior is associated with higher cognitive processes such as visuospatial perception and executive function12–19. Different thought processes are required during CCT, such as inputting figures in the space on the left and outputting figures in the space on the right. Therefore, eye movement is required. We hypothesized that incorporating gaze movement patterns as parameters in CCT would improve its effectiveness as a screening tool.

Notably, many early symptoms of MCI have been identified; however, only a few studies have developed screening evaluations, such as the Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA), which has become the global gold standard and was developed by combining the early symptoms of MCI (memory, attention, and visuospatial cognition). MoCA has high identification accuracy; however, it is a paper test, which requires much time and burdens the participant. We have previously developed a CCT that combines early symptoms of MCI using the MoCA development method as a model, and the identification system has been confirmed to be effective. Therefore, in the present study, we hypothesized that employing the task environment of CCT, a component task, combined with the gaze behavior of patients with MCI during task processing, would improve the identification accuracy better than using CCT alone.

We hypothesized that the gaze fixation duration and gaze-shift frequency may increase in older adults with MCI, and that adding these parameters to the model of MCI detection reported in our previous study may improve the accuracy of the CCT.

Methods

Study setting and participants

In this cross-sectional study, older adult participants were divided into two groups: those with MCI (MCI group) and healthy controls (HC group). The criteria for the classification of healthy older adults and those with MCI were as follows: for the HC group, a MoCA-Japanese version (MoCA-J) score ≥ 26 and a mini-mental state examination-Japanese version (MMSE-J) score of 28–30, and for the MCI group, an MoCA-J score ≤ 25 and an MMSE-J score of24–2721. Older adults who did not meet these criteria and those with visual impairments, cerebrovascular disorders, or other diseases that would affect the study were excluded. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2, Dusseldorf, Germany)30 before the study commencement. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2, Dusseldorf, Germany). The calculation parameters were as follows: test family = t test; statistical test = means: difference between two independent means (two groups); Type of power analysis = A priori: compute required sample size—given α, power, and effect size. With an effect size d = 0.8, α err prob = 0.05, power (1-β err prob) = 0.8, and allocation ratio N2/N1 = 0.84, the total sample size was calculated to be 52.

Therefore, 25 healthy older adults (HC group, age: [average.] 72.1 ± [standard deviation] 5.6; males, 7 and females, 18; MoCA-J: 27.1 ± 1.0; and MMSE-J: 28.8 ± 0.8) and 21 older adults with MCI (MCI group, age: 74.4 ± 5.7; males, 8 and females, 13; MoCA-J: 23.1 ± 1.9; and MMSE-J: 25.4 ± 1.2) were included in this study (Table 1). This study was conducted at two medical and nursing care facilities. The study period was between 2021 and 2023. Recruitment targeted older adults living in medical and nursing care facilities. The age interval for this study was 65 years or older, with no upper limit. There were no incentives for participants. In the present study, we defined abnormal gaze behavior as a case in which the gaze fixation duration and shift frequency evaluated using eye tracking differed from those of healthy older adults.

Outcome measures

The participants performed the CCT-A, -B, and C while seated on a chair. They were instructed to recreate a sample figure in the workspace, i.e., arrange the sticks placed in front of them in numerical order and orient the numbers on the sticks to match that in the sample figure. Gaze movements were measured using an eye tracking system (View Tracker III; DETECT Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). A head mount (TKD-UK1, NAMOTO Co., Ltd., Chiba, Japan) was used to minimize the effect of the head and neck movements during the gaze behavior analysis experiment. The chins and heads of the participants were in contact with the head mount.

The CCT evaluation commenced when the examiner placed the sample figure in the sample object space, and the task was assessed until its completion. First, we examined whether the CCT performance time between the MCI and HC groups differed. Next, we compared the differences in gaze patterns between the groups by area analysis using a common region of interest (ROI)31–33. For gaze analysis, the gaze fixation duration within specific areas (sample object space and workspace) relative to performance time (gaze fixation duration [%total of fixation time]) and the number of times the gaze entered the sample object space/workspace (number of gaze shifts) were calculated.

Cognitive composition test (CCT)

The CCT is a task-oriented test developed for early detection of MCI. The experimental environment for CCT comprised numbered sticks ([length] 11.4 cm, [width] 1.0 cm, [height] 0.2 cm, and [weight] 1.3 g) placed in front of the participant, a sample figure placed on the participant’s left side (sample object space), and a workspace on the participant’s right side. The numbered sticks are used to recreate a sample figure, and the performance time until completion is measured. The CCT involves moving the gaze to the right and left, so the subject’s gaze movements can be clearly evaluated. The CCT has three types with varying degrees of difficulty. The CCT-A is the easiest task, with four sticks and zero overlapping points; the CCT-B is a moderately difficult task, with six sticks and two overlapping points; and the CCT-C is the most difficult task, with eight sticks and ten overlapping points21 (Fig. 1).

Cognitive composition test. (a) Three sample figures used in CCT. CCT-A is a sample figure consisting of four sticks, and the participant is required to draw the same figure as the sample figure. CCT-A is the simplest task with no overlap. CCT-B is a sample figure consisting of six sticks, with two places where the sticks overlap. CCT-C is a sample figure consisting of eight sticks, with eight places where the sticks overlap. (b) After viewing the sample figures in the sample object space, the participant recreates the figure in the workspace using the numbered sticks placed in front of them. The task involves correctly configuring the direction and overlapping of the sticks when recreating the figure. The measurement is the performance time to complete the drawing of each figure in the CCT.

The CCT-C has a high discrimination accuracy with a sensitivity and specificity of 84% and 78%, respectively, when the cutoff value for MCI and HC is 65.75 s21. The following instances were excluded from the analysis: (1) participants mis ordered the sticks, (2) incorrect placement and direction of sticks, and (3) incorrect order of overlapping sticks.

Eye tracking system

The eye tracking device weighed approximately 40 g. In addition, an infrared camera (sampling 200 Hz) captured the movement of both eyes, and a scenery camera (sampling 30 Hz) projected the participant’s gaze target. Data were recorded by the eye-tracking device that was connected to a computer via a single cable, and the captured video was analyzed using the gaze analysis software ViewTracker3 (version 1.0518). In the gaze-analysis experiment during the CCT, calibration was performed for each participant to exclude any individual differences. The gaze analysis method used in this study was an area analysis of the ROI. The ROI was the participant’s gaze target area. In CCT, there are two areas: the sample object space (left side of the participant) and the workspace (right side of the participant), which are both 17.0 cm long and 22.0 cm wide (Fig. 2). The eye-tracking device can objectively capture whether a participant is looking at the sample object space or the workspace while performing CCT. In the present study, we measured gaze fixation duration and the frequency of gaze entry into each region (gazing point) within each specific area (sample object space and workspace) while participants performed the CCT task.

Example of gaze analysis during the CCT-C. The left side of the participant exhibits the sample object space in which a sample figure is presented, while the right side is the workspace. The difference between sample space and workspace is that the sample space requires encoding (input) the shape of a figure, whereas the workspace requires recall (output). The gaze point is measured using an eye-tracking system to calibrate the position of both eyes and the participant’s gaze target. The red circle and line indicate the gaze position and target, respectively. Gaze fixation within specific areas (sample object space and workspace) relative to performance time is defined as gaze fixation duration (% total of fixation time). Gaze-shift frequency (number of gaze-shifts) is the number of times the gaze enters each space; in this example, the gaze-shift frequency is three times.

Statistical analysis

JASP (ver.0.17.1 University of Amsterdam, Netherlands)34 was used for statistical analysis in this study. The Shapiro–Wilk test revealed that the percentage of total fixation time in the workspace in CCT-C and the gaze shift frequency (number of gaze shifts) into the sample object space and workspace in CCT-A were non-normally distributed data. Therefore, the Mann–Whitney test was performed. All other data were normally distributed; therefore, an independent t-test was performed (Table 1).

We performed logistic regression analysis to examine the discriminative ability of CCT, and then CCT combined with gaze analysis, to discriminate between healthy subjects and MCI. Three models were examined: (1) a model using only CCT as the explanatory variable, (2) a model using CCT and gaze fixation duration, and (3) a model using CCT and gaze-shift frequency. By comparing these area under the curve (AUC) obtain from receiver operating characteristic (ROC), we can examine whether the addition of gaze analysis results to CCT improves the ability to discriminate between healthy and MCI, because AUC indicate discriminatory power. Further, the Wald test was used to test the null hypothesis that a particular coefficient is equal to zero, in other word to test whether the factor has a significant effect on the accuracy of the predictive model. The statistical significance level was set at 5%.

Ethical considerations

This study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines and was approved by the Shijonawate Gakuen University, Faculty of Rehabilitation Research Ethics Review Committee (Approval No. 21-5). All participants provided written consent after they were informed about the aims of the study.

Results

Performance time, gaze fixation time, and gaze shift frequency according to the difficulty of each CCT were compared between the two groups. In addition, the results of the logistic regression analysis of CCT-C were presented.

Gaze fixation duration in the sample object space

In the CCT-A, the gaze fixation durations (mean ± SD) in the HC and MCI groups were 12.204 ± 4.773% and 14.110 ± 5.655%, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.222, effect size = − 0.367). In the CCT-B, the gaze fixation durations in the HC and MCI groups were 14.948 ± 6.145% and 16.219 ± 3.418%, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.403, effect size = − 0.250). However, in the CCT-C, the gaze fixation duration was significantly longer in the MCI group than in the HC group (15.232 ± 4.954% vs. 20.319 ± 2.965%, respectively; P < 0.001, effect size = − 1.220) (Table 2) (Fig. 3).

Gaze fixation duration in the workspace

In the CCT-A, the gaze fixation durations (mean ± SD) in the HC and MCI groups were 60.792 ± 3.794% and 59.460 ± 5.536%, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.146, effect size = 0.438). In the CCT-B, the gaze fixation durations in HC and MCI groups were 59.460 ± 5.498% and 58.676 ± 4.288%, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.598, effect size = 0.157). In the CCT-C, the gaze fixation durations in the HC and MCI groups were 61.984 ± 5.534% and 61.071 ± 3.886%, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.529, effect size = 1.88) (Table 2) (Fig. 3).

Gaze-shift frequency into the sample object space

In the CCT-A, the gaze-shifts (mean ± SD) in the HC and MCI groups were 5.840 ± 1.281and 6.095 ± 1.338 times, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.513 effect size = − 0.195). In the CCT-B, the gaze-shifts in the HC and MCI groups were 12.440 ± 2.434 and 13.571 ± 2.461 times, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.125 effect size = − 0.463). However, in the CCT-C, the gaze-shift was significantly higher in the MCI group than in the HC group (27.120 ± 4.438 vs. 34.286 ± 4.507 times, respectively; P < 0.001, effect size = − 1.603) (Table 2).

Gaze-shift frequency into the work space

In the CCT-A, the gaze-shifts (mean ± SD) in the HC and MCI groups were 6.240 ± 0.779 and 5.905 ± 1.261 times, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.276 effect size = 0.327). In the CCT-B, the gaze-shifts in the HC and MCI groups were 11.760 ± 2.385 and 12.095 ± 1.375 times, respectively, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.572 effect size = − 0.168). However, in the CCT-C, the gaze-shift was significantly higher in the MCI group than in the HC group (26.520 ± 3.454 vs. 30.667 ± 3.337 times, respectively; P < 0.001, effect size = − 1.219) (Table 2).

Results of logistic regression analysis

A logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the discriminative ability of only CCT, CCT with gaze fixation duration or gaze-shift frequency on the identification of MCI. The findings from each model are as follows:

Model 1 demonstrated that PT had a significant effect on the identification of MCI. Specifically, the estimated coefficient for PT was 0.11 (SE = 0.04, P = 0.004), with an odds ratio of 1.12 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.04, 0.19). This indicates that for each unit increase in PT, the risk of having MCI increases by approximately 12%. The AUC for this model was 0.78, indicating a moderate level of discrimination.

Model 2 showed that, in addition to PT, gaze fixation duration in the sample object space significantly contributed to the identification of MCI (estimated coefficient = 0.63, SE = 0.20, P = 0.002, odds ratio = 1.65, 95% CI 0.11, 0.89) and gaze fixation duration in the workspace significantly contributed to the identification of MCI (estimated coefficient = 0.35, SE = 0.17, P = 0.032, odds ratio = 1.74, 95% CI 0.11, 1.00). The AUC for this model was 0.92, reflecting a higher discriminatory power compared to model 1.

Model 3 further incorporated gaze-shift frequency in the workspace, yielding the highest discriminatory power. The estimated coefficient for gaze-shift frequency in the sample object space was 0.50 (SE = 0.20, P = 0.011, odds ratio = 1.87, 95% CI 0.11, 0.89), and the estimated coefficient for gaze-shift frequency in the workspace was 0.55 (SE = 0.23, P = 0.017, odds ratio = 1.42, 95% CI 0.10, 1.00). The AUC for this model was 0.95, indicating that the combination of PT and gaze behavior metrics was highly effective in identifying MCI.

These results suggest that the combination of gaze fixation duration and gaze-shift frequency is a more effective predictor for the early detection of MCI than CCT alone. (Table 3).

Discussion

The CCT used in this study was a screening test developed for the purpose of early detection of MCI. The CCT is a task in which participants must construct a figure identical to a sample figure using several sticks. The time it takes to complete the figure is assessed. There are three types of CCT with different levels of difficulty: CCT-A is the easiest task, with four sticks and no overlapping points, CCT-B is of medium difficulty, with six sticks and two overlapping points, and CCT-C is the most difficult task, with eight sticks and ten overlapping points. We found that the MCI group performed significantly slower than the HC group on the CCT-C (the most complex task), and the gaze fixation duration in the sample object space significantly increased in the MCI group. Furthermore, the gaze-shift frequency was significantly higher in the MCI group than in the HC group. Additionally, adding the results of gaze fixation duration and gaze-shift frequency to CCT was shown to improve discriminative ability between healthy and MCI. These findings suggest that abnormal gaze behavior becomes apparent during complex cognitive tasks in older adults with MCI and that the inclusion of gaze pattern parameters can improve the accuracy of discrimination in MCI detection models.

The finding of a decreased performance time in the MCI group in the CCT-C alone and no significant between-group differences in the CCT-A and -B was consistent with that reported in our previous study21 on the efficacy of the CCT. If the difference in the performance time for each CCT type was merely due to the difference in task difficulty, it would be similarly decreased in both groups. We hypothesized that the MCI group was significantly slower than the HC group in the CCT-C because the components of the CCT tested for a combination of executive function, memory, and attention deficits, which are early symptoms of MCI9,10,35,36. Therefore, in addition to components of the CCT, task difficulty is an important factor in distinguishing healthy older adults and those with MCI.

We observed abnormal gaze behavior during the CCT in the MCI group. Although the gaze fixation duration in the sample object space was significantly longer in the MCI group than in the HC group in the CCT-C, no significant between-group differences were observed in the CCT-A and -B. Gaze fixation duration in the workspace was not significantly different between the groups for all CCT tasks. Regarding the characteristics of cognitive processing of the sample object space and workspace, first, the sample object space required attention to the positions and order of overlap of the sticks to be placed, while the workspace required recall. Treleman et al. and Contestabile et al. reported that considerable attention is required to accurately recognize complex shapes29,37. Moreover, a higher number of visual distractors required a longer time for target fixation29,38. In other words, the MCI group may have spent a considerable amount of time on gaze fixation recognizing the sample object in the CCT-C, owing to a diminished attention function. The results of the present study were similar to those of previous studies26–29 that showed an increase in gaze fixation, suggesting that this gaze abnormality may reflect MCI.

Second, the gaze-shift frequency during the CCT was significantly different in the MCI and HC groups. As the difficulty level of the CCT increased, both the MCI and HC groups exhibited an increased gaze-shift frequency into both the sample object space and workspace. Notably, in the CCT-C, the MCI group exhibited a significantly higher gaze-shift frequency into both spaces than the HC group. Gaze-shift frequency was a distinctive characteristic observed in the MCI group. The increased gaze-shift frequency may be attributed to the early symptoms of diminished working memory8,9 and attention function8–11 in the MCI group because the CCT-C task requires attention to multiple objects in the sample object space, such as the placement of sticks, numbers, directions, and overlap points and temporary memory as well.

The results of the logistic regression analysis in this study showed that the combination of gaze fixation and gaze frequency was more effective in detecting MCI than CCT alone. In our previous study, we used only the performance time for the CCT, which included all the time spent on cognition, planning for execution, and actual execution, to predict MCI21. However, the abnormality in visual exploratory behavior for object recognition was not included in this prediction model, despite its importance in MCI39. This abnormality could not be estimated by task performance time and had to be measured independently. Our results suggest to the possibility of constructing a predictive model of MCI that combines temporal parameters in the execution of cognitive and compositional tasks with features that indicate abnormalities in visual exploratory behavior. CCT is a difficult task for patients with MCI, and eye movement is required during CCT. Therefore, using an eye-tracking device, it is possible to objectively evaluate the gaze movement of patients with MCI during the CCT task. In the present study, we showed that the gaze movements of patients with MCI differed from those of healthy older adults. Factors that characterize the disease or dysfunction should be incorporated into the prediction model to improve the accuracy of their screening tests40. The most important finding of the present study is that the analysis of gaze behavior during the CCT can improve the accuracy of MCI testing.

This study had some limitations. First, older adults with MCI without a definitive medical diagnosis were enrolled. However, in this study, older adults with MCI were defined on the basis of the MoCA-J and MMSE-J, similar to previous studies21. Second, although this study revealed that the MCI and HC groups had varying gaze behaviors, the reason for these variations could not be clarified. Therefore, further studies are necessary. Third, the sample size of this study was insufficient, as it was lower than the required sample size calculated using G*power by six participants. However, when recalculating using the post-hoc test [Effect size d = 1.026847, α err prob = 0.05, power (1-β err prob) = 0.8, and N2/N1 = 0.84], the total sample size was calculated to be 34, and the Actual Power was 0.8258347. Fourth, although the CCT revealed differences in gaze patterns between HC and MCI, because the CCT is a mixed evaluation of cognition, attention, dexterity, etc., it is not clear how this affected gaze abnormalities. Therefore, the sample size of this study was found to be valid.

In conclusion, the performance time during the CCT in older adults with MCI was significantly slower than that in the HC. The gaze fixation duration in the sample object space and gaze-shift frequency into both the sample object space and workspace were significantly increased in older adults with MCI. Adding gaze behavioral parameters to performance time during the CCT can improve the accuracy of MCI assessments. A combination of cognitive, compositional, and gaze behavioral tasks may be optimal for the detection of early stage dementia.
