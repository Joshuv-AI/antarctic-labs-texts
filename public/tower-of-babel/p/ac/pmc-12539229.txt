Deep learning for Three‐Class Classification of ground-glass nodules on non-enhanced chest CT: A multicenter comparative study of CNN architectures
Authors: Meihua Shao, Jian Wang, Lin Zhu, Jianfei Tu, Guohua Cheng, Linyang He, Hengfeng Shi, Cui Zhang, Hong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop, validate, and compare four three-dimensional (3D) convolutional neural network (CNN) models for differentiating ground-glass nodules (GGNs) on non-contrast chest computed tomography (CT) scans, specifically classifying them as adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH)/adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), or invasive adenocarcinoma (IA).Materials and methodsThis multi-center study retrospectively enrolled 4284 consecutive patients with surgically resected and pathologically confirmed AAH/AIS, MIA, or IA from four hospitals between January 2015 and December 2023. GGNs were randomly partitioned into a training set (n = 3083, 72 %) and a validation set (n = 1277, 28 %). Four 3D deep learning models (Res2Net 3D, DenseNet3D, ResNet50 3D, Vision Transformer 3D) were implemented for GGN segmentation and three-class classification. Additionally, variants of the Res2Net 3D model were developed by incorporating clinical and CT features: Res2Net 3D_w2 (sex, age), Res2Net 3D_w6 (adding lesion size, location, and smoking history), and Res2Net 3D_w10 (sex, age, location, the mean, maximum, and standard deviation of CT attenuation, nodule volume, volume ratio, volume ratio within the left/right lung, and the maximum CT value of the entire lung). Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, recall, precision, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsRes2Net 3D outperformed others, achieving AUCs of 0.91 (AAH/AIS), 0.88 (MIA), and 0.92 (IA). Its F1-scores were 0.416, 0.500, and 0.929, respectively. All Res2Net variants achieved accuracies between 0.83–0.84.ConclusionThe Res2Net 3D model accurately differentiates GGN subtypes using non-contrast CT, showing high performance, especially for invasive adenocarcinoma.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a prevalent cancer diagnosis and is the top reason of deaths linked to cancer on a global scale [1]. More ground glass nodules (GGNs) are being detected due to the implementation of chest CT scans for lung cancer screening. GGNs can indicate a range of pathological conditions, including atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (AAH), adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), and invasive adenocarcinoma (IA). The WHO's 2021 classification considers AAH and AIS to be adenomatous precursor lesions, with MIA and IA being categorized as adenocarcinomas [2]. Pure GGNs (pGGNs) and part-solid nodules are the two types of GGNs, with most pGGNs classified as AAH, AIS, or MIA. AIS and MIA both achieved 100 % recurrence-free survival over 10 years, with a low chance of spreading to lymph nodes or distant metastasis [3], [4]. In comparison, the 5-year disease-free survival rate for individuals with IA stands at around 79 % [5], [6], [7]. In such instances, lobectomy with lymph node dissection is the approved surgical treatment, whereas segmentectomy and wedge resection are not regarded as suitable [8]. Thus, assessing the invasiveness of lung nodules is crucial for choosing the right clinical decision-making approach.

Recently, deep learning, which is a form of machine learning, has shown potential as a decision-support method for the automated examination of medical images [9], [10], [11], [12]. Notably, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been effectively used for classifying and detecting lung nodules, as well as predicting their tumor invasiveness [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]. Prior research has highlighted the potential of three-dimensional (3D) CNN in evaluating the invasiveness of GGNs. The research conducted by Zhao et al. demonstrated that the 3D DenseSharp Network and 3D DenseNet were more effective than the 2D DenseNet, with accuracies of 64.1 %, 59.4 %, and 43.0 %, respectively [14]. Ni et al. applied a 3D CNN named Attention-v1 to assess GGNs' invasiveness, achieving an accuracy of 85.2 %, which exceeded the 80 % accuracy of seasoned radiologists [18]. Yu et al. employed 3D multi-task learning to evaluate the invasiveness of GGNs, achieving an accuracy of 87.42 % for the binary classification task (AAH/AIS/MIA vs. IA) and 64.9 % for the three-category classification task [19]. There is considerable variation in the results of the 3D depth learning models mentioned above, with limited studies examining the differences between different 3D CNN models. This study aimed to create, validate, and evaluate various deep learning models to distinguish GGNs on chest CT scans that indicate IA from those indicating AAH/AIS or MIA. In addition, we picked the best 3D model based on accuracy to merge multiple clinical details to assess whether the diagnostic performance could be boosted and to verify the model's consistency.

Materials and methods

Patients

The local hospital approved this research, and written informed consent was not required because the data analysis was done retrospectively and anonymously. Patient electronic medical records were reviewed for those who had surgery for suspected primary lung cancer between February 2015 and December 2023 at four hospitals. The criteria for inclusion were: GGNs lacking complete solid parts on CT scans using lung window settings; subjects who received chest CT scans with a slice thickness under 1.5 mm in the month prior to the resection; and lesions diagnosed pathologically as AAH/AIS, MIA, or IA. Exclusion criteria included patients lacking preoperative thin-section chest CTs (slice thickness <1.5 mm), with solid nodules, patients with poor CT image quality, or incomplete clinical information. Radiologists J.W. and Q.G.M., each with 14 and 20 years of experience, independently analyzed the CT scans to verify the presence of a GGN nodule. A consensus was used to establish the presence of GGNs if the two radiologists had different interpretations. Fig. 1 illustrates the summary of how patients were selected. CT examinations

CT scans were conducted with one of the five available CT devices from four different hospitals (SOMATOM Emotion16, SIEMENS, Germany; Deﬁnition AS, SIEMENS, Germany; Optima CT680, GE USA; LightSpeed VCT, GE USA; Revolution, Optima 680, LightSpeed VCT XT GE USA). The tests were conducted with tube voltages between 100 and 120 kVp, a matrix size of 512 × 512, a pitch from 0.875 to 1, and collimation between 1 and1.25 mm. Patients were examined while lying on their backs and taking a deep breath. The scan covered the area from the bottom of the lungs to the top of the chest.

Image preprocessing and CNN model

To illustrate, the 3D Res2Net model is a CNN architecture created to process medical images by extending the Res2Net framework into a three-dimensional form. In this study, we standardized the data preprocessing by unifying the window width and level (-[1200–600]) and spacing (0.8 mm in the x, y, and z directions) to minimize imaging differences as much as possible. Prior comparative experiments on different dicing methods showed that the optimal cutting size is 32x32x32, ensuring the lesion area does not exceed 24. The proposed 3D Res2Net employs a hierarchical multi-scale architecture to process volumetric input data with a shape of [1, 32] (channels, depth, height, width). The difference in the xyz direction of the image is uniformly reduced to 0.8 mm. Experiments comparing various data augmentation methods are performed, and the final methods include random offset block at the center point (offset pixel points <=4), random flip, random scaling (0.8–1.25), and gaussian noise. Four stages of 3D Res2Net bottleneck blocks progressively extract features, doubling channel depth at each stage (32 → 256) while reducing spatial dimensions via 3D max pooling (32 ³ → 4 ³). After global pooling, the 256-dimensional volumetric features are merged with clinical traits and passed through two fully connected layers to yield the final 3-dimensional output. This design leverages Res2Net’s grouped convolution strategy for efficient multi-scale feature learning in 3D space while integrating heterogeneous data modalities. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 80 and an initial learning rate of 0.001. The learning rate was reduced by a factor of 0.1 every 20 epochs, for a total of 60 training epochs. Cross-entropy loss was employed as the loss function for this multi-class classification task. Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics and CT were summarized separately in training and validation set. Mean ± SD was used to express continuous variables that adhered to a normal distribution. Frequencies were used to express categorical variables. Appropriate statistical tests, including the chi-square test, Fisher exact test, and Mann-Whitney U test, and Kruskal-Wallis test, were used to compare characteristics between groups. The effectiveness of the classification models was confirmed by determining their accuracy, macro-average, weighted-average, precision, recall, and F1 scores. Accuracy is determined by dividing the number of correctly classified samples by the total number of samples. The ratio of true positive outcomes to all positive outcomes is used to calculate precision. The macro-average involves computing precision, recall, and F1 for each class individually, and then taking the average of these metrics across all classes. The weighted average is determined by taking into account the ratio of samples in each category to the overall number of samples in all categories. The recall metric calculates the ratio of true positives to the sum of true positives and false negatives, while the F1 score represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

Disscussion

The widespread use of high-resolution CT has made it easier to detect GGNs. Patients with preinvasive lesions (AAH/AIS and MIA) had a better prognosis than those with invasive adenocarcinoma (IA) [7], [20], [21]. For AAH and AIS, it is often suggested to have continuous follow-up or observe for 4–6 months to rule out temporary inflammatory nodules. However, if MIA is suspected, long-term monitoring is not recommended, and elective surgery should be planned [6]. Additionally, it is important to carry out more accurate presurgical planning, such as deciding on a lobectomy rather than an atypical resection when invasiveness is obviously evaluated, or reviewing the CT follow-up schedules for patients who are not having operation [22]. CT scans delay or eliminate the need for surgeries with poor cost-effectiveness because nodules are not very biologically aggressive, particularly in older patients. Radiologists primarily rely on common CT characteristics like size, CT values, lesion edges, solid parts, and bubble lucency for diagnosing lesions, although there is some overlap between preinvasive and invasive lesions [23], [24]. From what we are aware, few studies have utilized 3D CNN models to forecast the invasiveness of GGNs that had chest CT scans prior to surgery, using data from multiple centers and large samples. Our research demonstrated that the Res2Net 3D model, using preoperative plain-phase chest CT scans, performed well in identifying IA from AAH/AIS or MIA compared to other 3D deep learning models. Furthermore, we integrated 2, 6, and 10 clinical features into the Res2Net 3D model to develop the Res2Net 3D_w2, Res2Net 3D_w6, and Res2Net 3D_w10 models, respectively. Our findings indicated that the models maintained a stable diagnostic performance, achieving AUCs between 0.86 and 0.93 for the three-class task.

GGNs are mainly located in the upper lobe and are more common in women [19]. Furthermore, the likelihood of GGN becoming invasive rises with its size. Our analysis of baseline and CT features revealed significant differences in gender and location among the AAH/AIS, MIA, and IA groups, aligning with previous research [19], [24]. Additionally, differences in smoking status, nodule type, tumor markers, shape, lesion size (LD, SD), CT values, and the presence of ILD and emphysema, along with lobulation and spiculation signs, were significant among these groups, some of which match earlier research results [23], [25]. Despite earlier efforts, it remains challenging to differentiate between invasive and pre-invasive lesions due to significant overlap. Although solid pulmonary nodules have clearly defined radiological characteristics, researchers have pointed out that variability in interpretations is common both between different observers and within the same observer [26]. Therefore, a more unbiased practical definition is needed to clearly distinguish IA from MIA, and AAH/AIS.

Radiomics and deep learning models are applied to determine how invasive GGNs are at present. Radiomics often has low reproducibility and is challenging to incorporate into clinical workflows when using different CT devices [27]. A 3D CNN-based model is a widely used approach for pulmonary nodules field, as it effectively captures the 3D spatial details of nodules and performs well in identifying nodules of a particular size [28], [29]. Yu and colleagues assess the invasiveness of GGNs with a 3D deep learning model; however, this model only achieved an AUC of 64.9 % for this three-category classification task [19]. Wang et al. classified invasive adenocarcinomas from non-invasive adenocarcinomas with 3D Deep-RadNet model; nonetheless, these researchers focused solely on a binary classification [17]. Zhao and colleagues employed a 3D CNN model to forecast the invasiveness of subcentimeter pulmonary adenocarcinomas, but the sample size for each category was quite limited [14]. The suggested multi-task deep learning framework makes full use of 3D CT scans, enhancing the representational data and boosting the reliability and precision of classification outcomes, achieving an accuracy more than 0.8, which was slightly higher than previous studies [14], [19]. The accuracy of the Res2Net 3D model was 0.847 in this study, outperforming the DenseSharp 3D model at 0.843, the Resnet50 3D model at 0.839, and the ViT 3D model at 0.818. While the overall accuracy differences between these models are small, the clinical value of a model is determined by its performance on critical endpoints. The primary imperative is to reliably identify IA, as these nodules require prompt intervention. Here, Res2Net-3D demonstrated the best balance, achieving the highest F1-score (0.929) and high Precision (0.933) for IA. This indicates a superior ability to minimize both missed cancers and unnecessary surgeries compared to other models, making its modest accuracy advantage clinically meaningful. Moreover, to develop the Res2Net 3D_w2, Res2Net 3D_w6, and Res2Net 3D_w10 models, we incorporated two (sex, age), six (adding lesion size, location, and smoking history), and ten clinical and CT features—the latter including the mean, maximum, and standard deviation of CT attenuation, nodule volume, volume ratio, and the maximum CT value of the entire lung. These models achieved comparable performance, with accuracies ranging between 0.83 and 0.84. The Res2Net residual framework maximizes the use of multi-scale data from pulmonary nodules by extracting features at a fine scale and diminishes overfitting through the use of pre-activation. Zhang et al. found that the Res2Net 3D model improved upon the ResNet model, with a 1.2 % increase in nodule detection sensitivity and a 0.5 % increase in the competition performance metric score, while the model size was reduced by approximately 50 % [29]. The diagnostic performance of the ViT 3D model in this study was inferior to other 3D models, with an accuracy level of 0.818. The potential reason was that ViT networks lack the layer-by-layer feature modeling structure of CNN networks that captures local features of lung nodules, so their classification ability does not greatly surpass that of CNNs [30], [31]. These variables were systematically integrated through weighted fusion modules, generating three hybrid variants: Res2Net 3D_w2 (low clinical feature weighting), Res2Net 3D_w6 (moderate weighting), and Res2Net 3D_w10 (high weighting). Contrary to expectations, none of these augmented models yielded improved diagnostic performance over the pure imaging-based Res2Net 3D model. All hybrid variants achieved accuracies within a narrow range (0.83–0.84) for the three-class classification task. These results suggest that imaging-derived features extracted by 3D CNNs may inherently encapsulate the biological determinants of invasiveness, rendering supplementary clinical variables redundant for this specific diagnostic context. The deep learning model’s ability to autonomously characterize microarchitectural heterogeneity—such as subtle solid component distribution, interface irregularity, and vascular convergence patterns—likely supersedes the discriminatory power of conventional clinical parameters in stratifying GGN subtypes.

The study encountered several constraints. Initially, as a retrospective study, the research is inherently susceptible to selection bias due to its reliance on pre-existing clinical data. Patient inclusion criteria, variations in diagnostic practices across institutions, and missing or incomplete records may have influenced the composition of the study cohort. Such biases could affect the generalizability of the findings, particularly if the sample does not fully represent the broader population. Secondly, the chest CT images used in this study were acquired using different scanning devices and imaging parameters across multiple institutions. Variations in slice thickness, reconstruction algorithms, tube voltage, and other technical factors may have introduced inconsistencies in CT attenuation values. These discrepancies could potentially affect the extraction and interpretation of deep learning features, leading to biased model performance. To mitigate this issue, future work should incorporate harmonization techniques to reduce inter-scanner variability. Additionally, establishing standardized imaging protocols in prospective studies would enhance the robustness of the findings. Thirdly, the study cohort exhibited an underrepresentation of AAH/AIS and MIA cases compared to IA, resulting in a class imbalance in the three-category classification task. To mitigate this issue, class resampling was applied—specifically, 5 × resampling for AAH/AIS and 4 × resampling for MIA—to reduce inter-class disparity. Furthermore, expanding the dataset to include a more balanced distribution of pathological subtypes would improve the model’s reliability in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Our study involved comparing several 3D deep learning models and merging subjective clinical information to build a new model, offering a non-invasive, quantitative, and reproducible technique for the three classifications of GGNs sensitively and specifically. Our 3D CNN model may improve the diagnostic accuracy of GGNs and facilitate the planning of the most appropriate treatment strategy.
