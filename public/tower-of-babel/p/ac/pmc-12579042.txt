Pharmacokinetic Characteristics of a Once-Weekly Combination Therapy of Insulin Icodec and Semaglutide Versus Its Separate Components in Chinese Individuals with Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Fangfang Wang, Zijian Luan, Raluca Maltesen, Asbjørn T Reenberg, Lisbet Westergaard, Dongyang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIcoSema is under development as a once-weekly injectable combination therapy of icodec (basal insulin) and semaglutide (glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist). This study assessed the pharmacokinetic characteristics of icodec and semaglutide following IcoSema administration vs. administration of icodec and semaglutide alone in Chinese individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D).MethodsIn a randomized, double-blind, three-period crossover study, 20 Chinese individuals with T2D (18–64 years, body mass index 18.5–34.9 kg/m2, glycated hemoglobin ≤ 9.0%) were given single subcutaneous administrations of IcoSema, icodec, or semaglutide separated by 6–9 weeks. Blood was drawn for pharmacokinetic measurement until 840 h post dose.ResultsCombining icodec with semaglutide had no impact on icodec pharmacokinetics. The ratio and 90% confidence interval of IcoSema/icodec was 1.04 [0.99;1.08] for area under the curve from zero to last quantifiable observation (AUC0–t) and 1.02 [0.96;1.09] for maximum concentration (Cmax), i.e., within the bioequivalence acceptance interval of 0.80–1.25. Likewise, combining semaglutide with icodec had no impact on semaglutide AUC0–t (IcoSema/semaglutide 0.99 [0.94;1.05]). However, semaglutide Cmax was higher for IcoSema vs. semaglutide alone (1.42 [1.31;1.53]) and occurred earlier for IcoSema (12 vs. 66 h). All three treatments were safe with no differences in frequency, severity or outcome of adverse events, or relationship to study product.ConclusionIn Chinese individuals with T2D, icodec pharmacokinetics and semaglutide total exposure are unaffected when combining icodec and semaglutide in IcoSema. However, maximum semaglutide concentration is higher and occurs earlier with IcoSema. This information may help to ensure suitable dose recommendations for IcoSema.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT05435677.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-025-01803-x.Keywords: Pharmacokinetics, Insulin icodec, Semaglutide, China, Type 2 diabetes

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?IcoSema is a once-weekly injectable combination therapy of basal insulin icodec and the glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist semaglutide and is currently in phase 3 clinical development for the treatment of type 2 diabetes (T2D).To help establish suitable dose recommendations for IcoSema across different racial backgrounds, this study investigated whether there is a difference in the pharmacokinetics of icodec and/or semaglutide between IcoSema administration and administration of icodec and semaglutide alone in Chinese individuals with T2D.What was learned from the study?While the combination of icodec and semaglutide in IcoSema did not influence icodec pharmacokinetics and semaglutide total exposure, a higher and earlier maximum semaglutide concentration was observed following IcoSema administration as compared to semaglutide administration alone.The current results are in accordance with previous findings in white individuals with T2D.

Introduction

The currently recommended pharmacological treatment approach for individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) includes oral non-insulin glucose-lowering medications, injectable glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), and insulin as the disease progresses [1–3]. An alternative approach for treatment intensification is the combination of basal insulin and GLP-1RA. This option addresses several aspects of the underlying pathophysiology of T2D, and has the potential to offer effective glycemic control with less hypoglycemia and no weight gain compared to basal insulin therapy [4, 5]. In China, individuals with T2D with inadequate glycemic control after treatment with oral non-insulin glucose-lowering medication should initiate insulin therapy, which may be in the form of basal insulin and GLP-1RA combination or as basal insulin, co-formulation insulin, or pre-mixed insulin [3].

Two combination therapies of basal insulin and GLP-1RA are currently available: insulin degludec/liraglutide (IDegLira) and insulin glargine/lixisenatide (IGlarLixi), both for once-daily administration [6]. A combination product of basal insulin and GLP-1RA suitable for once-weekly administration could reduce the burden associated with daily dosing and may also be able to address insulin treatment inertia in individuals with T2D [7–9]. Furthermore, evidence has shown that once-weekly versus once-daily administration of subcutaneous GLP-1RAs led to improvements in treatment persistence and adherence [10].

IcoSema is a once-weekly injectable combination therapy of basal insulin icodec and the GLP-1RA semaglutide, and is currently in phase 3 clinical development for the treatment of T2D [11, 12]. Currently, results from three phase 3a trials have been published. In the COMBINE 1 trial in individuals with T2D inadequately controlled on daily basal insulin, with or without oral glucose-lowering medications, IcoSema as compared to once-weekly icodec provided superiority in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) reduction and in body weight change from baseline to week 52 and in rate of combined clinically significant or severe hypoglycemia [13]. In the COMBINE 2 trial in individuals with T2D inadequately controlled with GLP-1RA, with or without additional oral glucose-lowering medications, IcoSema as compared to semaglutide 1.0 mg provided superiority in HbA1c reduction from baseline to week 52 and greater improvement in fasting plasma glucose with comparable rate of combined clinically significant or severe hypoglycemia, while the weight change was in favor of semaglutide 1.0 mg [14]. In the COMBINE 3 trial in individuals with T2D inadequately controlled on daily basal insulin, IcoSema provided non-inferior reduction in HbA1c from baseline to week 52 and superior body weight change and rate of combined clinically significant or severe hypoglycemia compared with basal-bolus insulin treatment that included once-daily insulin glargine U100 and insulin aspart given two to four times per day with main meals [15].

In a clinical pharmacology study in white individuals with T2D, the combination of icodec and semaglutide in IcoSema did not influence icodec pharmacokinetics or semaglutide total exposure but caused the maximum semaglutide concentration to be higher and to occur earlier [16]. These results gave rise to an initial dose of IcoSema in the phase 3a trials corresponding to 40 units of icodec and 0.114 mg of semaglutide [13–15]. As race may influence drug pharmacokinetics [17, 18], it remained to be confirmed whether the conclusions from the clinical pharmacology study in white individuals also pertained to individuals of other racial background. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to assess the pharmacokinetic characteristics of icodec and semaglutide following IcoSema administration versus administration of icodec and semaglutide alone in Chinese individuals with T2D.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This completed single-center (Drug Clinical Trial Center, Peking University Third Hospital, Beijing, China), randomized, double-blind, three-period crossover study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05435677; registration date 23 June 2022) was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [19] and ICH Good Clinical Practice [20]. The protocol for this study was approved by a suitably constituted independent ethics committee (Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital, Beijing, China). All participants provided informed consent.

Participants

Eligibility for participation in this study required individuals to be Chinese men or women between the ages of 18 and 64 years (both inclusive) and having been diagnosed with T2D ≥ 180 days prior to screening. Individuals should be insulin naïve and should receive stable dose(s) of any of the following oral non-insulin glucose-lowering medications: metformin, dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) inhibitors, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, and/or alpha-glucosidase inhibitors. Short-term insulin treatment for a maximum of 14 days prior to screening and previous insulin treatment for gestational diabetes were, however, allowed. Individuals should also have a body weight of ≥ 50 kg, HbA1c ≤ 9.0% (75 mmol/mol), and a body mass index between 18.5 and 34.9 kg/m2 (both inclusive). Screened individuals were excluded from participation if they had any disorder that could jeopardize their safety or compliance with the study protocol. Conditions associated with T2D were exempted if they were under stable treatment. Individuals were also excluded if they had supine blood pressure outside of 90–159 mmHg (systolic) or 50–99 mmHg (diastolic), calcitonin ≥ 50 ng/L, presence or history of acute or chronic pancreatitis, renal impairment defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, hepatic impairment defined as an alanine aminotransferase level ≥ 2.5 times the upper normal limit, a bilirubin level > 1.5 times the upper normal limit, uncontrolled and potentially unstable diabetic retinopathy or maculopathy, recurrent severe hypoglycemia (> 1 severe hypoglycemic episode within the last 180 days), hypoglycemia unawareness, or hospitalization for diabetic ketoacidosis within the last 180 days. Women that were pregnant, breast-feeding, or had the intention to become pregnant were also excluded from participation.

Procedures and Assessments

The overall study design, including procedures and assessments, was largely comparable with a previous study conducted in white individuals with T2D [16].

The current study comprised a screening visit (up to 3 weeks before randomization), a DPP4 inhibitor washout period, three treatment periods, and a follow-up visit (Fig. S1). The DPP4 inhibitor washout period of 5–6 days prior to randomization was included for participants treated with any DPP4 inhibitor at entry into the study in order to discontinue the DPP4 inhibitor treatment. Participants were allowed to continue treatment with other oral glucose-lowering medications throughout the study at a stable dose. Each treatment period consisted of a single dose of study product followed by 5 weeks of pharmacokinetic blood sampling. The treatment periods were separated by 1–4 weeks from the end of pharmacokinetic blood sampling until the next single dose.

The single dose of study product received at the beginning of each treatment period consisted of IcoSema, icodec alone, or semaglutide alone in randomized sequence. The IcoSema dose contained 40 U icodec (equivalent to 240 nmol) and 0.114 mg semaglutide (equivalent to 27.7 nmol). The icodec dose administered alone contained 40 U, while the semaglutide dose administered alone contained 0.1072 mg (equivalent to 26.1 nmol). IcoSema and semaglutide were provided in 1.5-mL pre-filled PDS290 pen injectors, while icodec was provided in a 3-mL pre-filled PDS290 pen injector (Novo Nordisk, Bagsværd, Denmark). The IcoSema study product strength was 700 U/mL icodec and 2 mg/mL semaglutide (Novo Nordisk). The icodec study product strength was 700 U/mL (Novo Nordisk), and the semaglutide study product strength was 1.34 mg/mL (Novo Nordisk). The slight difference in semaglutide dose between administration in IcoSema (0.114 mg) and administration alone (0.1072 mg) was due to the difference in semaglutide concentration between IcoSema (2 mg/mL) and semaglutide alone (1.34 mg/mL) combined with the injection volume equivalent to each increment on the PDS290 pen injector. Semaglutide 1.34 mg/mL provided in a 1.5-mL pre-filled PDS290 pen injector was the only semaglutide product available in China at the time of conduct of the present study. All three study products were scheduled to be injected subcutaneously in the left thigh by trained site staff at approximately 08:00 hours. Participants remained in-house at the clinical study site until 84–96 h after each administration of study product.

Pharmacokinetic assessment was carried out as previously described [16]. In brief, blood samples for analysis of icodec and semaglutide concentrations were drawn as shown in Table S1. Serum icodec concentrations were measured following IcoSema and icodec administration using a validated luminescence oxygen channelling immunoassay (LOCI). Plasma semaglutide concentrations were measured following IcoSema and semaglutide administration using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry assay.

Safety assessments included adverse events (AEs), hypoglycemic episodes, clinical laboratory assessments, vital signs, physical examination, and electrocardiogram.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

The primary endpoints in the current study were the area under the serum icodec concentration–time curve and the dose-normalized area under the plasma semaglutide concentration–time curve from zero to last quantifiable observation after a single dose (AUC0–t,SD,Icodec and dose-normalized AUC0–t,SD,Semaglutide). In the previous study with similar overall design in white participants with T2D, the standard deviation of the within-participant differences was 0.162 for log(AUC0–t,SD,Icodec) and 0.164 for log(dose-normalized AUC0–t,SD,Semaglutide) [16]. Assuming a standard deviation for both endpoints of 0.18 and that 16 participants completed the study, then the 90% confidence interval (CI) for the estimated geometric mean ratio between IcoSema and one of the two components administered alone would range from 0.91 to 1.10 times the geometric mean ratio with 90% probability. This was considered sufficient for comparison of relative bioavailability between the study products. To ensure 16 completers, 20 individuals were randomized.

Calculation and statistical analysis of pharmacokinetic endpoints were conducted essentially as described previously [16]. AUC0–t,SD,Semaglutide and the maximum concentration after a single dose for semaglutide (Cmax,SD,Semaglutide) were, however, dose-normalized in the current study as a result of the slightly different dose of semaglutide administered in IcoSema versus semaglutide alone. Evaluation of AUC0–t,SD and Cmax,SD for both icodec and semaglutide was carried out on the basis of guidelines for bioavailability and bioequivalence studies [21, 22].

Safety parameters were presented by descriptive statistics. AEs and hypoglycemic episodes were defined as treatment emergent if occurring from each single dose administration until 35 days later. Hypoglycemic episodes were classified as level 1 (hypoglycemia alert value; plasma glucose < 3.9 mmol/L and ≥ 3.0 mmol/L), level 2 (clinically significant hypoglycemia; plasma glucose < 3.0 mmol/L), and level 3 (severe hypoglycemia; associated with severe cognitive impairment requiring external assistance for recovery) [23].

Pharmacokinetic Modelling

Pharmacokinetic modelling was used to simulate the pharmacokinetic profiles at steady state based on the observed pharmacokinetic results of the present single-dose study. The steady-state pharmacokinetic profile of icodec was simulated after IcoSema and icodec dosing, while the steady-state pharmacokinetic profile of semaglutide was simulated after IcoSema and semaglutide dosing. The pharmacokinetic models for icodec and semaglutide as well as the modelling approach have been described previously [16]. In brief, 10 weeks of once-weekly dosing of IcoSema (40 U icodec/0.114 mg semaglutide), icodec (40 U), or semaglutide (0.1072 mg) was simulated for each participant. Maximum icodec concentration at steady state (Cmax,SS,Icodec) was compared between IcoSema and icodec alone, while the dose-normalized Cmax,SS,Semaglutide was compared between IcoSema and semaglutide alone, using the same statistical analysis as described above for AUC0–t,SD and Cmax,SD.

Results

Participants Disposition and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 66 individuals went through screening; 20 of these were randomized and exposed to at least one of the three study products and 19 completed the study (Fig. S2). One participant was withdrawn after two treatment periods upon decision by the principal investigator and therefore did not receive IcoSema. Baseline characteristics for the 20 randomized and exposed participants are presented in Table S2.

Icodec Pharmacokinetics

Serum icodec concentration–time profiles after IcoSema dosing and after dosing of icodec alone are shown in Fig. 1 for the 1-week dosing interval after a single dose and simulated at steady state. Results of the statistical analysis of icodec pharmacokinetic endpoints are presented in Table 1. Similar relative bioavailability of icodec was shown for IcoSema dosing versus icodec dosing alone, as the 90% CI for the ratio of total icodec exposure after single dose (AUC0–t,SD,Icodec) between IcoSema and icodec alone was fully contained within the bioequivalence acceptance interval of [0.80;1.25]. The same was also seen for maximum icodec concentration after single dose (Cmax,SD,Icodec) and simulated at steady state (Cmax,SS,Icodec). The median time to maximum icodec concentration after single dose (tmax,SD,Icodec) was 24 h both after IcoSema dosing and after icodec dosing alone. Taken together, the combination of icodec and semaglutide in IcoSema has no impact on the pharmacokinetic properties of icodec.

Semaglutide Pharmacokinetics

Dose-normalized plasma semaglutide concentration–time profiles after IcoSema dosing and after dosing of semaglutide alone are shown in Fig. 2 for the 1-week dosing interval after a single dose and simulated at steady state. Results of the statistical analysis of semaglutide pharmacokinetic endpoints are presented in Table 1. Similar relative bioavailability of semaglutide was shown for IcoSema dosing versus semaglutide dosing alone, as the 90% CI for the ratio of total semaglutide exposure after single dose (AUC0–t,SD,Semaglutide) between IcoSema and semaglutide alone was fully contained within the bioequivalence acceptance interval of [0.80;1.25]. However, the 90% CIs for the ratios of maximum semaglutide concentration after single dose (Cmax,SD,Semaglutide) and simulated at steady state (Cmax,SS,Semaglutide) between IcoSema and semaglutide alone extended above the interval of [0.80;1.25]. Furthermore, the median time to maximum semaglutide concentration after single dose (tmax,SD,Semaglutide) was lower for IcoSema (12 h) versus semaglutide alone (66 h). The results therefore show that total exposure of semaglutide is similar for IcoSema and semaglutide alone, while the shape of the semaglutide pharmacokinetic profile is different for IcoSema than for semaglutide alone. Thus, when semaglutide is combined with icodec in IcoSema, the maximum concentration of semaglutide is increased and occurs earlier.

Safety

A summary of treatment-emergent AEs is presented in Table S3. In total, 56 AEs were reported in 17 participants. No differences were observed between treatments with respect to frequency, severity or outcome of AEs, or relationship to study product. Most AEs were mild and all AEs were recovered/resolved before the end of the study. There were no severe or serious AEs or AEs leading to withdrawal from the study. The pattern of AEs reported within different system organ classes and preferred terms was also comparable between treatments. The most frequently reported AEs were diarrhea, COVID-19, nausea, and decreased appetite (12, 10, 7, and 6 events, respectively). Specifically, all COVID-19 events were assessed by the investigator to be unlikely related to study product. One AE of hypersensitivity was reported in a participant 2 days after icodec administration in the second treatment period after receiving semaglutide in the first treatment period. The event was reported as allergic rash/dermatitis, moderate in severity, assessed by the investigator to be probably related to study product and had a duration of 2 days. The investigator decided to withdraw the participant from the study prior to planned IcoSema dosing in the third treatment period. One level 1 hypoglycemic episode was reported in the study, while no level 2 and no severe hypoglycemic episodes were reported. There were no clinically significant observations in clinical laboratory assessments, vital signs, physical examination, or electrocardiogram.

Discussion

The key findings of the current study in Chinese individuals with T2D were that (a) icodec pharmacokinetic characteristics were unaffected by the combination of icodec with semaglutide in IcoSema; (b) semaglutide total exposure was not influenced, but semaglutide maximum concentration was higher and occurred earlier, when combining semaglutide with icodec in IcoSema relative to semaglutide administration alone; and (c) IcoSema, icodec, and semaglutide were all safe with no differences in AE frequency, severity, causality, or outcome.

A higher and earlier maximum concentration of semaglutide following IcoSema administration relative to administration of semaglutide alone was also observed in a previous study in white individuals with T2D [16]. In vitro and animal data suggested that the change in semaglutide absorption pharmacokinetics in IcoSema is due to competition for albumin binding locally at the injection site, where icodec outcompetes semaglutide [16]. Following semaglutide administration alone, the major fraction of semaglutide binds to albumin at the injection site, which results in protracted absorption into the circulation occurring slowly via the lymph. Following IcoSema administration, the competition for albumin binding between icodec and semaglutide results in a greater fraction of free, unbound semaglutide at the injection site. The free fraction of semaglutide is absorbed faster into the circulation, which in turn leads to the observed higher and earlier maximum semaglutide concentration.

It might be speculated whether the higher and earlier maximum semaglutide concentration following IcoSema administration will be further exacerbated in individuals with hypoalbuminemia or in those taking other albumin-binding drugs. First, it should be emphasized that the limited pool of albumin and the competition between icodec and semaglutide are restricted to the local injection area. In the circulation, there is a great surplus of albumin as compared to the concentrations of icodec and semaglutide. Consequently, any impact from co-administration of other albumin-binding drugs would require that these are injected at the same area as IcoSema, which is highly unlikely. Second, when it comes to the effect of hypoalbuminemia on the absorption of semaglutide from the subcutis, a mechanistic pharmacokinetic model for absorption of subcutaneously administered acylated peptides in humans developed on the basis of semaglutide data has been applied. From this model, it is estimated that as a result of the competition with icodec for albumin binding locally at the injection site, less than 1% of semaglutide is bound to albumin in the subcutis following IcoSema administration (Alifrangis L., 2025, unpublished data). Thus, it is expected that hypoalbuminemia will not, to any relevant extent, reduce the albumin-bound fraction of semaglutide further and will therefore also have negligible influence on the semaglutide pharmacokinetic profile after IcoSema administration.

The impact of combining semaglutide with icodec in IcoSema on semaglutide maximum concentration and time to maximum concentration was slightly smaller in the current study in Chinese individuals than in the previous study in white individuals [16]. Notably, semaglutide pharmacokinetic results after IcoSema administration were essentially similar between the two studies. Rather, the differences in semaglutide maximum concentration and time to maximum concentration between the two studies were mainly observed following semaglutide administration alone. This could be due to one or more parameters that differed between the current and the previous study: semaglutide dose (0.1072 vs. 0.5 mg), semaglutide formulation strength (1.34 vs. 1 mg/mL), injection device (PDS290 vs. NovoPen 4), and/or body mass index of the participants (26.2 vs. 31.1 kg/m2).

IcoSema, icodec, and semaglutide were all safe in the current study. In particular, the frequency of gastrointestinal related AEs was similar for IcoSema and semaglutide alone. This differs from the previous study in white individuals, where the frequency of gastrointestinal related AEs was higher for IcoSema than for semaglutide [16], and at an overall higher level than in the current study. The dose levels of semaglutide both in IcoSema (0.114 mg) and when dosed alone (0.1072 mg) were lower in the current study compared with the previous study (0.5 mg) [16] and most likely explains the overall lower frequency of gastrointestinal related AEs in the current study. Thus, the findings indicate that a higher frequency of gastrointestinal related AEs for IcoSema versus semaglutide at a 0.5 mg semaglutide dose level can be mitigated by reducing the dose to approximately one fifth. Accordingly, the initial dose of IcoSema in the phase 3a clinical trials corresponded to a semaglutide dose of 0.114 mg [13–15], which is lower than the 0.25 mg starting dose used for semaglutide alone. Furthermore, the dose titration of IcoSema included weekly adjustments corresponding to ± 0.029 mg semaglutide in contrast to the increases in dose to 0.5 mg and 1.0 mg every 4 weeks for semaglutide alone. The currently available results from the IcoSema phase 3a trial program suggest a benign safety profile during 52 weeks of treatment in individuals with T2D [13–15]. The results also support the therapeutic potential of IcoSema in terms of glycemic control, weight management, and low risk of hypoglycemia [13–15]. Thus, it appears that any potential adverse influence of the higher and earlier maximum semaglutide concentration with IcoSema versus semaglutide alone on the efficacy and safety profile of IcoSema can be minimized through careful adaptations to the dose titration schedule of IcoSema as tested in the phase 3a clinical trials. It is expected that the pharmacokinetic results of the current study in Chinese individuals combined with the dose titration and efficacy and safety outcomes of the IcoSema phase 3a clinical trials will be able to inform dose recommendations for IcoSema in China.

The current study had a few limitations that must be considered when interpreting the results. First, the study was a single-dose study, and the observed results therefore only reflect the first dose of IcoSema after initiation of treatment. A multiple-dose study design with achievement of steady state for each of the three treatments would have required at least 5 weeks per treatment period because of the long half-life of approximately 1 week for both icodec and semaglutide. This was considered too burdensome for the participants. Instead, pharmacokinetic modelling was used to simulate the pharmacokinetics at steady state based on the observed single-dose pharmacokinetic results. Still, the lack of observed steady-state assessments may reduce the applicability of the conclusions to chronic treatment. Second, there was a minor difference in the semaglutide dose between administration of IcoSema (0.114 mg) and semaglutide alone (0.1072 mg). This difference was unavoidable given the injection volume per increment for the 1.5-mL PDS290 pen injector, as the semaglutide concentration in IcoSema was 2 mg/mL, while the only semaglutide product available in China at the time of study conduct had a semaglutide concentration of 1.34 mg/mL. To address the difference in semaglutide dose between IcoSema and semaglutide alone, semaglutide exposure was dose-normalized. It has previously been shown in healthy Chinese individuals that semaglutide total exposure and maximum concentration increase in a dose-proportional manner [24]. Thus, dose-normalization was assumed to correct for the semaglutide dose difference, although potential minor influence on the pharmacokinetic conclusions cannot be excluded. Third, the relatively small sample size of 20 participants should be acknowledged and may put certain limitations on the generalizability of the conclusions of the study. The study included close to equal numbers of men and women. Comparison of the results between men and women was, however, not conducted as this was not considered meaningful because of the limited sample size.

Conclusion

In Chinese individuals with T2D the combination of icodec and semaglutide in IcoSema did not influence icodec pharmacokinetics and semaglutide total exposure. However, a higher and earlier maximum semaglutide concentration was observed, which is in accordance with previous findings in white individuals with T2D. Knowledge of the difference in semaglutide pharmacokinetics between administration of IcoSema and semaglutide alone across different racial backgrounds is highly relevant when establishing suitable dose recommendations for IcoSema.
