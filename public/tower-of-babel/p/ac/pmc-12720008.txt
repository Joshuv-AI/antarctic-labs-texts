Feasibility and Usability of an Omnidirectional Treadmill–Based Virtual Reality Rehabilitation Game: A Mixed–Methods Feasibility Study
Authors: Adria Quigley, Dru Pierson, Juhui Jeong, Youngbin Choi, Mayra Barrera Machuca, Courtney Pollock, Anouk Lamontagne, Heather F Neyedli, Alison McDonald, Melanie Dunlop, Tsz Hei Yip, Gregory Jones, Janice J Eng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImportanceVirtual reality (VR) has been introduced to stroke rehabilitation along with omnidirectional (360°) treadmills to promote improvements in walking and balance.ObjectiveThe primary objective was to develop a rehabilitation omni-VR game and estimate the feasibility and usability of the game according to individuals with chronic stroke and physical therapists. The secondary objective was to generate preliminary walking characteristics and postural sway data among survivors of stroke.DesignThis was a mixed-methods feasibility study.SettingThis study took place at a stroke research lab in Halifax, Canada.ParticipantsThe participants were individuals with chronic stroke and stroke rehabilitation physical therapists.ExposureThe research team developed a VR game involving 8 walking and balance tasks on an omnidirectional treadmill. Physical therapists and survivors of stroke tested the game in a single session and participated in a semi structured interview.Main Outcomes and MeasuresMain outcomes and measures were recruitment rate, user experiences, participant burden, technical issues, safety, and exercise dose. Descriptive statistics were calculated for feasibility outcomes and interview data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsEight survivors of stroke (4 female, 4 male) and 4 female physical therapists participated in the study. Feasibility and usability targets were met, except for user experience scores and technical issues. For survivors of stroke, the mean steps taken during the VR game was 693.0 (standard deviation, SD = 223.6), they reached 66.4% SD = 10.7 of predicted maximum heart rate, and the mean game testing time was 25.5 minutes SD = 13.5. Five qualitative themes emerged: benefits of the omni-VR system, difficulties & challenges with omni-VR, suggestions for improvements, application to rehabilitation, and safety of the system.ConclusionsThere is preliminary evidence for the feasibility of omni-VR for stroke rehabilitation.RelevanceThe VR game appears to be of adequate intensity, provides a larger number of repetitions, and is of comparable duration to traditional rehabilitation sessions.

INTRODUCTION

Stroke is the second–leading cause of death and the third–leading cause of death and disability combined globally, with balance and walking deficits affecting 48% to 83%1,2 of survivors of stroke. Patients with stroke have identified that the highest research priority is related to walking and balance.3 However, to walk independently, an individual needs to perform dual tasks, navigate irregular terrain, inclines, and obstacles, and manage distractions.4 Rehabilitation is effective for survivors of stroke, regardless of the stage of recovery. Optimizing the potential for neuroplasticity that underpins stroke recovery requires interventions to encompass high doses of task repetition.5 Task salience as well as participant motivation and attention are essential for neuroplasticity to occur.6 Therefore, survivors of stroke must be meaningfully engaged and motivated in therapy.7

Virtual reality (VR) technology is one way to engage survivors of stroke and has been introduced to the field of rehabilitation to promote improvements in walking and balance. A new type of low-cost (less than $15,000 USD) treadmill technology, an omnidirectional treadmill, which allows for locomotor movement in all directions and is compatible with VR has emerged. Virtual reality allows users to interact with multiple realistic sensory environments and obtain real-time feedback on performance. High-quality evidence is indicating that VR can improve balance and walking characteristics among survivors of stroke.8 More recently, it has been used in conjunction with omnidirectional treadmill technology (omni-VR) to fully immerse the user in the virtual environment while safely challenging task-oriented exercises. This novel technology can offer a large therapy dose, real-time feedback, and active patient participation, all of which are essential neuroplasticity principles.6 The gamification of rehabilitation using omni-VR could address many of the problems with conventional therapy by allowing for therapeutic walking and balance activities that are intense, motivating, and engaging.9 The largest advantage of omni-VR is to fully immerse the participant in the virtual environment by allowing them to move 360 degrees while safely challenging cognitive-physical task-oriented exercises with small space requirements. Multidirectional balance skills are foundational to functional movements during activities of daily living and there is evidence of balance impairments, particularly in the medial-lateral direction among survivors of stroke.10

The success of omni-VR is based on user experiences and similarity to real-world situations.11 If omni-VR is to be implemented widely as part of rehabilitation, its feasibility and usability from the perspective of survivors of stroke and physical therapists must be investigated. However, user experiences and preferences of survivors of stroke and physical therapists are missing from the literature.12

PURPOSE AND OBJECTIVES

Our primary aim was to develop a rehabilitation omni-VR game and estimate the feasibility and usability of the omni-VR system according to individuals with chronic stroke and physical therapists in terms of recruitment rate, user experiences, participant burden, technical issues, safety, and exercise dose (number of steps, intensity [maximum heart rate, % of maximum predicted heart rate, average heart rate], duration). Both quantitative and qualitative data are needed to inform the use of the omni-VR rehabilitation game in a future randomized controlled trial (RCT), providing a holistic understanding of the feasibility and usability of this system. A quantitative approach was needed to measure recruitment rate, user experiences, participant burden, technical issues, safety and exercise dose, while a qualitative approach was needed to understand the in-depth user experiences and feasibility challenges with the omni-VR game and make changes based on feedback from the participants. Our secondary objective was to compare preliminary walking characteristics (step width, step length, and cadence) data among survivors of stroke during omnidirectional treadmill and overground walking, as well as standing postural sway. Walking characteristics data on the Cyberith Virtualizer ELITE 2 omnidirectional treadmill13 have not been previously collected among survivors of stroke.14

METHODS

Study Design

This is a convergent parallel cross-sectional mixed–methods feasibility study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Dalhousie University Research Ethics Board (REB File Number: 2024-7017) and this study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained for all participants. We used the Mixed Methods Reporting in Rehabilitation & Health Sciences checklist; please see the checklist in Supplementary Material 1.15

Inclusion Criteria

Survivors of stroke were eligible if they were/had: adults aged ≥18 with self-reported stroke at least 6 months prior, living within 50 kilometers of Halifax, Nova Scotia, a functional Ambulation Category ≥3,16 able to exercise for ≥15 minutes, no visual deficits or spatial neglect, no other cardiovascular, orthopedic, or neurological diseases impacting walking or balance, no severe cognitive impairment (Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA] scores >1817), and a score of 27 or less on the adapted Motion Sickness Susceptibility Scale-Short Questionnaire (MSSQ-short).18 Physical therapists were eligible if they had: at least 50% of their caseload consisting of survivors of stroke, at least 2 years of clinical practice in stroke rehabilitation, and a score of 27 or less on the adapted MSSQ-short.18 Since VR can to induce motion sickness in some individuals, this questionnaire was used to identify the susceptibility of the participants to motion sickness, with higher scores reflecting higher susceptibility.19

Recruitment

We recruited survivors of stroke and physical therapists from rehabilitation centers, clinics, and stroke recovery clubs in Halifax, Nova Scotia. We used purposive sampling to have equal numbers of male and female survivors of stroke.

Equipment

Our omnidirectional treadmill, the Cyberith Virtualizer ELITE 2, is self-paced and electrically-powered that allows for real-world movements such as walking backwards, turning, and dodging obstacles20 and provides haptic feedback from the baseplate. The user’s movements are detected by 6 optical sensors located in the baseplate, which measure the velocity and direction of the user’s movements at a frame rate of 1.000 hertz.21 This system has a safety harness and adjustable ring to prevent falls. The user wears overshoes to allow them to slide on the slippery surface of the treadmill. The treadmill adjusts the elevation and position of the baseplate as the user moves so that the incline is in front of them.21 We also utilized fully-immersive VR technology, including a HTC Vive Pro 2 head-mounted display22 and trackers for full-body tracking for measuring step cadence, width, and length, and sway.20,21,23 We chose Cyberith Virtualizer and HTC Vive technology as they are relatively low-cost and commercially available, with the aim for them to be purchased for use in clinical settings.24

VR Game Development

The VR game was developed by our research team consisting of computer scientists, computer science students, patient partners, rehabilitation clinicians, and physical therapist researchers. Game development was led by a computer science student using Unity Technologies software.25 The game was developed based on best practices for stroke neurorehabilitation (including task and goal-oriented training, treadmill-based gait training, and voluntary and reactive balance training)26,27 and includes a beginner, moderate, and difficult version of each task. In the game, participants collect gems for successfully completing 8 walking, balance, and dual tasks in a forest scenario, including dodging bananas thrown by monkeys, counting fruits in the trees while walking, and walking on slippery rocks. The tasks were developed to promote fast walking, movements in the medial-lateral direction, dual tasking, and walking with a narrow base of support. The omnidirectional treadmill was carefully matched to the movements of the user so that their movements were accurately reflected in the VR game. We tested the game with people who are unimpaired (n = 11) and patient partners (n = 3) prior to the present study. See Supplementary Material 2 for pictures of the tasks and instructions.

Screening

Screening for inclusion of survivors of stroke took place via Zoom (Version 6.0.2; Zoom Communications; San Jose, California, USA) and included the MoCA-BLIND,28 the functional ambulation category,16 MSSQ-short,19 and eligibility questions. Screening for physical therapists also took place on Zoom and involved the MSSQ-short19 and eligibility questions.

Assessments

Assessments took place at the ReBEL (Rehabilitation, Balance, and Exercise Lab) research group at Dalhousie University. Participants visited the study laboratory for a single session, where they played the VR game under the supervision of a physical therapist or physical therapist student. During the session, participants completed a tutorial where they learned to walk in the game before completing the beginner version of the 8 tasks. The participants tested the game for as long as it took for them to finish the 8 tasks once or if they decided to terminate the session earlier.

Quantitative Feasibility Data

Guided by a feasibility framework,29 we measured aspects of feasibility and usability including process, resources, management, and scientific outcomes. All participants were asked to complete the System Usability Scale (SUS)30 and modified Virtual Reality Neuroscience Questionnaire (VRNQ).31 The SUS is a 10-item widely–used usability questionnaire scored out of 100, with higher scores reflecting better usability.30 A score of 50.9 is considered to be fair and a score of 71.4 is considered to be good.30 The VRNQ is a 20-item questionnaire with 4 subscales (user experience, game mechanics, in-game assistance, and VR-induced symptoms and effects [VRISE]).31 Responses are scored on a 7-point scale, with higher scores reflecting a better outcome.31 We used the user experience and VR-induced symptoms and effects subscales as the other subscales were not relevant to the VR game. Exercise intensity (maximum heart rate, mean heart rate) was measured using a Polar Electro H10 heart rate monitor32 (sampling rate of 1000 hertz), and duration (total minutes spent testing the game) was recorded using REDCap (Vanderbilt) (Research Electronic Data Capture).33 Number of steps was measured using the sensors on the omnidirectional treadmill.

Quantitative Walking and Balance Data

Following the VR game testing, participants who are survivors of stroke performed walking and postural sway assessments. The 4-Meter Walk Test (4MWT) took place at self-selected and fast gait speeds in 2 different conditions (first using the omnidirectional treadmill followed by walking overground). The VR headset was not worn for either testing condition. For the overground walking test, participants walked along a walkway of 4 m with an additional 1 m marked with tape at either end for acceleration or deceleration.36 On the treadmill, the walking parameters were collected after the participants had begun walking for a few steps to allow them to accelerate similar to the overground walking condition. For each condition, participants were asked to complete 2 trials at each walking pace. All survivors of stroke completed the 4MWT on the treadmill first, followed by the overground walking. Walking characteristics (step cadence, step width, step length) were measured using HTC Vive trackers secured around the ankles of the participants.

The postural sway assessment included a 30-second static sway test, where the participant was asked to stand with their feet together and their arms crossed across their chest. Static sway was measured using an HTC Vive tracker positioned in the lumbar area at the level of the posterior superior iliac spine over 2 trials. A previous study found similar methods of measuring sway to have acceptable validity and reliability.37 A customized C# script and the SteamVR plugin for Unity were used to provide integration with the HTC Vive trackers to record the position and orientation of the trackers during the walking and balance tests with a sampling rate of 90 samples per second.

Qualitative Data

Using community–engaged research approaches, 3 patient partners, 2 rehabilitation clinicians, and 2 physical therapist researchers developed 2 separate semi structured interview guides for survivors of stroke and physical therapists based on those developed by Moan et al.14 The questions included participants’ overall impressions of the game, experiences using omni-VR for improving walking and balance, the rehabilitation potential of the game, barriers and facilitators to using the omni-VR system, safety considerations, and recommendations to enhance the protocol for a future RCT. For the physical therapist interviews, the focus was on the suitability of the VR game for stroke rehabilitation. See Supplementary Material 3 for the interview guides. The interviews were audio-recorded, then transcribed verbatim using Otter.ai (Otter.ai Incorporated),38 and checked and de-identified by a research team member.

Quantitative Data Analysis

We calculated a sample size of 12 participants (power = 0.8, α = .05, constant mean = 68, anticipated mean = 80 on the SUS). We used descriptive statistics such as frequencies, means, and standard deviations (SD) to present the demographic information of the sample. To explore potential sex differences in our outcomes, we followed the Sex and Gender Equity in Research guidelines and present outcomes disaggregated by sex.39 Descriptive statistics were calculated for feasibility and usability data and were compared against our a priori criteria for success. System Usability Scale total scores were calculated using the formula: SUS = 2.5 (20 + SUM [SUS01, SUS03, SUS05, SUS07, SUS09] – SUM [SUS02, SUS04, SUS06, SUS08, SUS10]).30 Virtual Reality Neuroscience Questionnaire scores were calculated by adding the scores of each item for the user experiences and VRISE subscales.31 Participant characteristics and feasibility results are presented separately for clinicians and survivors of stroke, and females and males.

Using the equation from Gellish et al (HRmax = 207− [0.7 * age]),40 we calculated maximum predicted heart rates and the percentage of predicted heart rate achieved during the testing session. We also collected maximum heart rate achieved, average heart rate, the amount of time spent testing the game, and number of steps taken while playing the game and present those results using descriptive statistics.

Descriptive statistics were also calculated for postural sway and walking characteristics. This study was not adequately powered for hypothesis testing with our secondary outcomes, therefore, we calculated pairwise mean differences, SDs of the difference, 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the difference, and Hedges g for step width, step length, and cadence while walking on the omnidirectional treadmill and overground.41 We used a threshold of 1 SD of the difference between the 2 conditions to determine if there were clinically-relevant differences between walking overground and walking on the omnidirectional treadmill.42,43 Exercise dose, postural sway, and waling characteristics are presented separately for female and male survivors of stroke. SPSS software (Version 29.0.2.0; IBM; Armonk, New York, United States)44 and Excel (Version 16.97.2; Microsoft; Redmond, Washington, United States)45 were used for all quantitative analyses.

Qualitative Data Analysis

Interview data were analyzed deductively and inductively using the 6-steps of reflexive thematic analysis by Braun and Clark.46 Each transcript was coded by 2 independent team members (D.P., Y.C., R.J., T.H.Y., A.Q.) in Excel. Resulting codes were grouped into themes and sub-themes and were integrated into the codebook. Regular analytical sessions were held among the research team with the principal investigator (A.Q.) to compare interpretations and resolve any differences. Differences were resolved using discussion among the independent coders and the use of a third researcher to resolve conflicts. We did not quantify the number of conflicts as they were resolved during research team discussions. The qualitative analysis team reviewed and provided input on the final themes and sub-themes. Qualitative analysis was complete when data saturation was obtained, meaning that no new themes or sub-themes emerged from the interviews.

Trustworthiness of the qualitative data was built around 4 pillars: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability.47 To maintain credibility and dependability, detailed records of data collection and analysis procedures were maintained using excel software. Investigator triangulation was used by having 2 independent researchers analyze the data, with a third researcher helping to resolve any disagreements, as needed. Transferability was emphasized by having discussions among the data analysis team about how applicable our findings are to similar contexts and by providing a description of the participants’ demographics and clinical characteristics. We also focused on confirmability using peer debriefing to discuss our biases and improve objectivity of our findings.47

Mixed-Method Analysis and Integration

Following the independent qualitative and quantitative analyses, the findings were integrated for comparison to identify instances of confirmation (quantitative and qualitative findings supported each other), expansion (divergent findings provided a broader understanding), or discordance (findings were in disagreement).48 Results are reported narratively, integrating both data types thematically.48 See the Figure 1 for a visual depiction of the mixed-methods analysis and integration.

Exercise Intensity and Postural Sway and Walking Characteristics for Survivors of Stroke

Supplementary Material 4 shows the comparison between walking characteristics on the omnidirectional treadmill and walking overground during the 4MWT. Step width while walking on the treadmill and overground was similar at a self-selected pace (mean diff = −0.02 m, SD = 0.04, 95% CI = −0.07 to 0.02, Hedges g = 0.5) and fast walking pace (mean diff = −0.01 m, SD = 0.03, 95% CI = −0.04 to 0.03, Hedges g = 0.2). However, step length while walking on the treadmill was clinically shorter than overground walking (as the mean difference > 1 SD of the difference) at both a self-selected pace (mean diff = −0.30 m, SD = 0.22, 95% CI = −0.53 to −0.07, Hedges g = 1.1) and a fast walking pace (mean diff = −0.35 m, SD = 0.31, 95% CI = −0.67 to −0.03, Hedges g = 1.0). Cadence while walking on the treadmill was clinically slower than overground walking at a fast pace (mean diff = −33.6 steps/min, SD = 22.8, 95% CI = −54.7 to −12.5, Hedges g = 1.3) but not at a self-selected walking pace (mean diff = −5.1 steps/min, SD = 29.4, 95% CI = −32.3 to 22.0, Hedges g = 0.2).

Theme 1—Benefits of the Omni-VR System

The participants identified several benefits of the omni-VR system, which were grouped into 4 subthemes (helping recovery, altruism, interest in technology, and enjoyment). One survivor of stroke found the omni-VR system to be particularly helpful for strengthening the connection with their affected leg for stroke recovery. According to 2 participants, they did not have strong interest in gaming, but they were still able to recognize the potential value for their stroke recovery. Five participants believed that the system could appeal to a wide range of survivors of stroke. Three of the participants identified that the omni-VR system could be a valuable clinical rehabilitation tool. Overall, 11 of 12 participants felt that the VR game was engaging and entertaining.

Theme 2—Difficulties & Challenges With Omni-VR

Participants identified some difficulties and challenges while using the omni-VR system. These challenges spanned across physical, technological, and accessibility barriers. One physical challenge reported by participants was adapting to the slippery surface of the omni-directional treadmill. As users wear overshoes to facilitate walking on the treadmill, their feet must slide with each step. One survivor of stroke found it challenging to engage their affected side, making it harder to synchronize their movements and stay focused on the task. Three survivors of stroke noted difficulties with maintaining a symmetrical gait pattern. Two participants noted that their affected side, due to stroke-related weakness, made turning especially challenging. One survivor of stroke with upper limb hemiparesis found it difficult to turn, as their arm and hand would make contact with the ring harness system. Finally, 1 participant noted that the water sounds were too loud during the slippery rocks task. One survivor of stroke reported that the omni-directional treadmill was not consistently registering their steps if their lower limb was not moving properly over the sensors in the treadmill. As a result, the participants had to take multiple steps to have a single step properly registered. Some participants also identified that the incline of the treadmill resulted in fatigue.

In general, the physical therapists identified similar issues to the survivors of stroke, but the physical therapists also identified some potential barriers to the integration of the omni-VR system into clinical practice. Some highlighted that access to the omni-VR system presents challenges such as the need to purchase the treadmill for clinical use, adequate space to accommodate it, and the presence of a trained individual to assist users with getting on and off the system, as well as operating the equipment. They also identified system–wide access barriers such as rehabilitation waitlists and narrow criteria for rehabilitation eligibility.

Theme 3—Suggestions for Improvements

Participants made several suggestions for system, protocol, and patient selection improvements. In terms of changes to the system, a survivor of stroke suggested making the tutorial shorter to reduce fatigue and ensure better engagement with the rehabilitation tasks. In terms of changes to the protocol, 4 physical therapists suggested to provide more frequent breaks and comfortable seating during the breaks. One participant emphasized the need to select patients who are not prone to motion sickness.

Theme 4—Application to Rehabilitation

This theme involves the application of the omni-VR system to clinical practice. The subthemes that emerged included rehabilitation goals, practice/learning, setting or environment, and appropriate difficulty level/progression. Three physical therapists identified that the VR game involved dual tasking activities and other tasks that offered unique challenges that are not easily replicated in therapy. Two survivors of stroke and 1 physical therapist felt that there is a learning curve with the VR game, but that they would improve with practice. Two survivors of stroke and 2 physical therapists said the system had an appropriate difficulty level and the progression through the tasks was reasonable. One survivor of stroke and 1 physical therapist identified that the system also has promise for home-based rehabilitation, allowing participants to practice in a familiar setting.

Theme 5—Safety of the Omni-VR System

The participants considered the omni-VR system to be safe. However, some survivors of stroke reported that while they felt safe in the system, their mental perception of safety needed adjustment as they had been conditioned to prioritize safety after their stroke. Only 1 participant (a physical therapist) reported during the interview that they felt some mild motion sickness during the game testing.

Findings from 12 of 21 items from the SUS and VRNQ were confirmed by the semi-structured interviews, 5 of the items were expanded upon by the interviews, and only 4 were discordant. The interviews confirmed that participants appreciated the immersion of the game, found it enjoyable, noted the graphics were of high quality, reported only mild nausea, disorientation, and dizziness, and found the overall quality of the VR technology was very high. While the participants reported in the VRNQ that it was difficult to move in the virtual environment, the interviews provided more information about the difficulties with hemiparetic limbs and the slippery surface of the treadmill. Participants also expanded on their experiences with fatigue and instability (VRNQ items) and learning how to use the system and the awkwardness of the system (SUS items).

DISCUSSION

This mixed–methods feasibility study provides promising preliminary evidence for the feasibility of omni-VR as a stroke rehabilitation strategy, with 5 of the 7 feasibility outcomes exceeding the a priori criteria for success. For the SUS scores, we set an ambitious target of 80 for all participants, which was not achieved. However, the mean score among physical therapists (73.6, SD = 14.9) and male survivors of stroke (71.9, SD = 6.6) is considered to be good and the mean score among female survivors of stroke (56.9, SD = 14.9) is considered to be fair.50 Furthermore, the scores on the VRNQ user experiences and VRISE subscales for all participants were above our a priori criteria for success (25.8, SD = 4.3 and 27.8, SD = 6.2, respectively). The other area where the feasibility outcome fell short was the proportion of participants who experienced a technical issue. In future studies, we will provide careful instructions and cueing to participants to ensure their lower limb moves over the sensors of the treadmill so that their virtual movements align better with their real-life movements.

We determined the participant self-selected exercise dose (number of steps, intensity, duration) of the rehabilitation VR game. The mean number of steps taken while playing the VR game was 693.0 (SD = 223.6), which far exceeded the mean number of steps taken during a typical lower limb stroke rehabilitation session (357 [95% CI = 296–418]).34 The maximal intensity achieved during the game was 66.4% (SD = 10.7) of predicted heart rate values, which corresponds to a moderate intensity according to the American College of Sports Medicine.51 This value also aligns with the 2019 Aerobic Exercise Recommendations to Optimize Best Practices in Care After Stroke (AEROBICS).35 Furthermore, the mean duration (25.5 minutes, SD = 13.5) of our participant self-selected session aligns with the AEROBICS recommendations to have exercise sessions last at least 20 minutes.35 Taken together, our VR game appears to be of adequate intensity, provides a larger number of repetitions than traditional therapy, and is of comparable duration to traditional rehabilitation sessions.

The findings of the qualitative thematic analysis were positive and largely agreed with the quantitative findings, with 17 of 21 items (81%) from the usability questionnaires confirmed or expanded upon by the interviews and only 4 discordant items. The interviews were particularly useful for expanding on the findings of the quantitative SUS and VRNQ questionnaires, as well as identifying tangible areas for improvement to the system, protocol, and patient selection processes. Our findings expand upon those of a recent study that involved focus groups with clinicians and survivor of stroke-clinician dyads that tested their VR scenario, which revealed similar challenges with walking on the treadmill and suggestions for improvement.24

We also reported on walking characteristics including step width, step length, and cadence on both the omnidirectional treadmill and while walking overground. We found that there were clinically-relevant reductions in step length during treadmill walking at self-selected and fast paces as well as fast pace walking cadence. Our findings are similar to those of Smit & Lamontagne, who found that their omnidirectional treadmill (the previous version of the Virtualizer Elite), leads to a different walking pattern than overground walking among young individuals who are healthy.52 In that study, they suggested that the reduction in step length could be due to the low-friction surface of the treadmill, which can induce the perception of imbalance.52 This is was noted in our study with our participants reporting a mild feeling of instability on the VRNQ. As Smit & Lamontagne point out, it will be important to investigate the longer-term impact of walking on the omnidirectional treadmill on these gait parameters,52 as it is possible that the participants may adopt a walking pattern more similar to overground walking with practice.

Limitations

Many of our participants were younger than the average age of stroke and were highly functional, which reduces the generalizability of our findings. The age of the participants could reflect a worldwide trend of increased incidence of stroke among young adults.53 It could be that younger and more functional survivors of stroke were interested in this study as it involves emerging technology. Further work is needed to extend these results to older individuals and individuals with lower function. We did not collect data on the time post stroke and lesion location, so we are unable to comment on how these characteristics may have affected our results. We are also unable to draw conclusions on user experiences separately for clinicians and survivors of stroke, as the power analysis was based on all 12 participants. We did not quantify the number of conflicts during the qualitative analysis.

We were able to obtain some preliminary estimates of walking characteristics. However, we could not compare walking speed among survivors of stroke on the treadmill to overground walking, as estimates on the omnidirectional treadmill only reflect walking speed in the virtual environment. Other studies have used motion capture to overcome this limitation52; future studies could use this technology to compare walking speed on the omnidirectional treadmill to overground walking among survivors of stroke. A final limitation was the amount of missing data related to the use of the Vive trackers, which can be mitigated in future studies by carefully reviewing the data following each walking and balance trial to ensure accurate measurements are being made.

Conclusion

Based on our results, the feasibility of a pilot RCT is supported, with 5 of 7 feasibility outcomes exceeding the a priori criteria for success. Using the feedback from participant interviews, further changes to the VR game and protocol will enhance the usability of the omni-VR system. The rehabilitation dose of the VR game is appropriate, with a moderate intensity of walking and a greater total number of steps than traditional rehabilitation. The next steps will be to implement a pilot RCT to compare 12 weeks of omni-VR training to traditional physical therapy on measures of cognition, brain activation, walking ability, and dynamic balance. We also plan to make the VR game available open access on GitHub.
