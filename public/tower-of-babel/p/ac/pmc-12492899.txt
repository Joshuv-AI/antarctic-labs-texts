FOXK1 regulates apoptosis, migration and angiogenesis in high glucose-induced vascular endothelial cells through p-AKT/AKT signaling pathway
Authors: He Long, Qinghua Hu, Lan Yang, Xin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundForkhead box K1 (FOXK1) is a newly discovered gene encoding a transcription factor in the FOX family. It plays important roles in the cell cycle, cell growth, proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, metabolism, DNA damage, drug resistance, angiogenesis, and carcinogenesis by binding to DNA to function as a transcription factor. Many studies have confirmed that FOXK1 plays a role in promoting cancer in a variety of tumors. However, the pathogenic role of FOXK1 in diabetic retinopathy (DR) is still unclear. The present study was conducted to investigate the mechanism of FOXK1 in the vascular endothelial dysfunction of DR.MethodsHuman umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were exposed to high glucose (HG) concentrations (25 mmol/L) to establish a vascular endothelial dysfunction model of DR. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was used to detect the differential expression of FOXK1 in HUVECs treated with HG and low glucose (LG). CCK-8, Transwell, flow cytometry and tube formation assays were used to investigate the effect of FOXK1 on HUVECs function. To explore the downstream signaling pathway of FOXK1, Western blotting was subsequently used to detect the protein expression of p-AKT and AKT after FOXK1 was knocked down in HUVECs. The AKT inhibitor MK-2206 was used to treat HUVECs, and cell migration, apoptosis and angiogenesis were observed.ResultsThe results showed that FOXK1 expression was significantly increased in HUVECs cultured with high glucose. In this endothelial dysfunction model, knockdown of FOXK1 increased HUVECs activity and migration, inhibited HUVECs apoptosis, angiogenesis and VEGF expression, and activated the p-AKT/AKT signaling pathway. All of these were reversed by the AKT inhibitor MK-2206.ConclusionsFOXK1 may mediate vascular endothelial dysfunction in DR by inhibiting p-AKT/AKT pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-025-01048-3.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a predominant ocular complication of diabetes mellitus [1], presents substantial global health challenges. As reported by the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 537 million working-age individuals worldwide have diabetes. Among them, the prevalence of DR stands at 22.27%, severely compromising patients’ quality of life [2]. However, while vascular endothelial dysfunction is recognized as the central pathological mechanism in DR, its precise molecular etiology remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include protein kinase-C activation, polyol pathway induction, oxidative stress amplification, inflammatory cascade activation, and advanced glycation end-product accumulation [3–5]. The emergence of anti-VEGF drugs represents a major breakthrough in the molecular therapy of DR, which can effectively control the progression of intraocular neovascularization in DR patients. Nevertheless, their clinical utility is severely constrained by the demanding requirements for repetitive intravitreal administrations, imposing considerable economic burdens. Furthermore, therapeutic resistance persists in 15–25% of patients, manifesting as either non-response or suboptimal clinical outcomes [6]. These limitations highlight the pressing need for elucidating novel molecular pathways and developing alternative therapeutic strategies.

The structural alterations and dysfunction of endothelial cells, which result from a series of molecular abnormalities, are crucial in the pathogenesis of DR. The fork head box (FOX) protein family, comprising 43 evolutionarily conserved transcription factors with endothelial expression profiles, has emerged as pivotal regulators of vascular homeostasis. FOX protein dysregulation can induce pathological angiogenesis, thereby contributing to multifactorial disorders including neoplastic diseases and diabetic complications [7]. FOXK1 represents an emerging member of this family and functions as a transcriptional regulator orchestrating diverse biological processes through DNA-binding activity. Its pleiotropic roles encompass cell cycle modulation, proliferation/differentiation control, metabolic regulation, apoptosis mediation, angiogenesis coordination, and carcinogenesis facilitation [8]. Substantial evidence from oncological research demonstrates FOXK1’s oncogenic potential in multiple malignancies, particularly colorectal carcinoma, gastric adenocarcinoma, and hepatocellular carcinoma. These effects are primarily exerted through mechanisms involving proliferative enhancement, apoptotic resistance, metastatic promotion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) modulation, and tumor-associated [9–12]. Notably, despite these established roles in carcinogenesis, FOXK1’s pathophysiological involvement in DR remains poorly characterized.

The GSE221521 dataset was downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/?acc=GSE221521). Bioinformatic analysis of GEO datasets revealed significant FOXK1 overexpression in the peripheral blood of DR patients. Building upon this clinical observation, an in vitro experimental system was hereby designed utilizing high-glucose-induced HUVECs to systematically investigate the functional consequences of FOXK1 modulation. Through targeted genetic manipulation, FOXK1-associated signaling pathways were mechanistically delineated for the establishment of a molecular foundation for targeted therapeutic development in DR management.

Methods and materials

Cell culture and cell transfection

HUVECs (purchased from Xinyu Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Shanghai) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37℃ with 5% CO2. Passages 3–5 were used for all assays. The following six experimental groups were established and maintained for 48 h: (1) LG (low-glucose control): 5.5 mmol/L glucose. (2) HG (high-glucose model): 25 mmol/L glucose. (3) HM (osmotic control): 5.5 mmol/L glucose + 19.5 mmol/L D-mannitol (total osmolarity matched to HG). (4) HG + si-NC: 25 mmol/L glucose + non-targeting siRNA (100 nmol/L). (5) HG + si-FOXK1: 25 mmol/L glucose + FOXK1-specific siRNA (100 nmol/L). (6) HG + FOXK1-OE: 25 mmol/L glucose + pcDNA3.1-FOXK1 plasmid (2 µg/mL). Unless otherwise stated, all groups were seeded at 2 × 10⁵ cells/well in 6-well plates (or equivalent density for other formats) 24 h before treatment initiation. Media were replaced every 24 h with fresh media containing the same additives. All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates (n = 3), each with three technical replicates.

RT-qPCR assay

Total RNA from HUVECs was extracted using Trizol Reagent (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA utilizing a reverse transcription kit (Servicebio, Wuhan). RT-qPCR was performed with reference to the fluorescence quantitative kit (Servicebio, Wuhan), and the data were processed using the 2−ΔΔCq method [13]. The primers utilized for PCR were as follows (Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd):

FOXK1 forward, 5’-ACCCACGAATAGCTTGACTGG-3’ and reverse, 5’-GCATTA GCGGCTACTGAGACG-3’; VEGF forward, 5’-GGGCAGAATCATCACG AAGTG-3’ and reverse, 5’-CACCAGGGTCTCGATTGGAT-3’; GAPDH forward, 5’-GCACC GTCAAGGCTGAGAAC-3’ and reverse, 5’-ATGGTGGTGA AGACGCCAGT-3’. Each RT-qPCR reaction was repeated with three independent cell batches (biological replicates, n = 3).

CCK-8 assay

The CCK-8 high Sensitivity Rapid test kit (Servicebio, Wuhan) was hereby employed. HUVECs were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Following that, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The optical density (OD) at a wavelength of 450 nm was measured utilizing an enzyme labeling apparatus. Each experiment was repeated in triplicate. The activity of the HUVECs in each group was calculated and analyzed according to the following formula: (experimental group OD-blank group OD)/(control group OD-blank group OD)×100%. Three independent HUVEC batches were analyzed (biological replicates, n = 3).

Flow cytometry

HUVECs were analyzed for apoptosis using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (Servicebio, Wuhan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells from each group were harvested and centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min to pellet the cells without causing damage. The cells were mixed with 500 µL of binding buffer and 5 µL of FITC-annexin V and incubated in the dark for 15 min. Subsequently, the samples were supplemented with 5 µL of PI and immediately analyzed by flow cytometry. Three independent experiments were carried out using separate HUVEC isolations (biological replicates, n = 3). Flow cytometry acquisition was performed on a BD FACSCanto II cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA).

Transwell assay

Transwell migration assays were performed using an 8 μm Transwell system (Corning, USA). The culture medium was placed in the lower well of the Transwell system, and 100 µL of cell suspensions from each experimental group were seeded into the upper chamber. Upon a 24-hour incubation period, cells remaining in the upper chamber were gently removed using a cotton swab. The cells were subsequently fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained using crystal violet solution. The entire chamber was imaged using a microscope to capture the overall cell distribution, given the potential heterogeneity of cell density within the chamber. Subsequently, the migrating cells were counted under the microscope in multiple randomly selected fields of view to ensure accuracy. Three independent experiments were carried out using separate HUVEC isolations (biological replicates, n = 3). To ensure that migration differences did not stem from variations in initial cell counts or survival rate bias, live cells were counted immediately after 48-hour high-glucose treatment using the trypan blue exclusion assay. An equal number of viable cells (5 × 10^4 cells/100 µL, viability > 90%) were then seeded into the upper chamber, thus eliminating interference from survival rate differences on migration outcomes.

Tube formation assay

The 96-well plates with Matrigel (BD, USA) were incubated in a 37 °C chamber for 30 min to allow the Matrigel to solidify. Different groups of HUVECs were counted and seeded into 96-well plates at 20,000 cells per well and cultured for 8 h. An inverted microscope was used for tube formation evaluation. AngioTool software was utilized to calculate the angiogenic capacity in terms of “tube length.” Three independent experiments were carried out using separate HUVEC isolations (biological replicates, n = 3).

Western blot analysis

Treated HUVECs were collected and lysed in RIPA buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan) to extract total protein. Following detection and quantification, the protein mixture was denatured by boiling at high temperature for 5 min, 30 µg of sample protein was subjected to gel electrophoresis, the membrane was transferred using the semidry method, and the culture was closed for 60 min. The membranes were incubated with the specified primary antibodies (Abcam, England) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, England) at room temperature for 60 min. A chemiluminescence reagent was introduced for the development process, and images were generated. Finally, the gray values of the protein bands were quantitatively analyzed using Image J software (Supplementary material 1). Three independent experiments were carried out using separate HUVEC isolations (biological replicates, n = 3).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 software, with the results expressed as the means ± standard deviations. Two treatment groups were compared via the independent t test, and multiple group comparisons were tested by one-way ANOVA. Each experiment was repeated in triplicate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of a statistically significant difference.

Results

FOXK1 expression was upregulated in both the blood of DR patients and high glucose-induced HUVECs

The GSE221521 dataset was downloaded from the GEO database, and the analysis revealed that FOXK1 expression was significantly increased in the blood of DR patients (Supplementary material 2).

HUVECs were cultured under LG (5.5 mmol/L) and HG (25 mmol/L) conditions for 48 h. Quantitative assessment using RT-qPCR demonstrated an increase in FOXK1 mRNA expression in HG-treated cells compared to LG controls (Fig. 1A). WB detection revealed that the expression of FOXK1 protein in the HG group was significantly greater compared to that in the LG group and the HM group (Fig. 1B, C).

To exclude hyperosmolarity as a confounder, we included a high-osmolality control (HM, 5.5 mmol/L glucose + 20 mmol/L mannitol). FOXK1 mRNA and protein levels in the HM group were indistinguishable from those in the LG group (Fig. 1A and C), indicating that the up-regulation of FOXK1 observed under HG is attributable to glucose itself rather than osmotic stress. Consistently, functional assays described below revealed that HM treatment did not alter cell viability, apoptosis, migration or tube formation compared with LG (data not shown), further validating HG-specific effects.

FOXK1 knockdown significantly increased the activity of HUVECs and inhibited apoptosis of HUVECs induced by high glucose

The expression of FOXK1 was verified by RT-qPCR after plasmid transfection and siRNA knockdown. Figure 2A presents the knockdown and overexpression effects. FOXK1 knockdown simultaneously enhanced cell viability (Fig. 2B) and reduced apoptosis (Fig. 2C, D). Conversely, FOXK1 overexpression decreased viability while increasing the apoptotic index, demonstrating that FOXK1 negatively regulates endothelial cell survival under HG conditions. To quantify apoptosis independent of overall cell death, we gated on Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ cells (early apoptosis). Early apoptotic cells (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻) were significantly decreased in FOXK1-knockdown HUVECs (Fig. 2D, middle bar) and increased upon FOXK1 overexpression (Fig. 2D, right bar).

Knocking down FOXK1 promoted the migration of HUVECs under high glucose conditions

The results of Transwell assay clearly showed that the migration ability of FOXK1 knockdown cells was significantly higher than that of the control group, and the overexpression of FOXK1 weakened the migration ability of cells (Fig. 3A, B).

Knocking down FOXK1 inhibited the angiogenesis ability of HUVECs induced by high glucose, meanwhile, the expression of VEGF was inhibited

In the tube formation assay, the angiogenesis ability of HUVECs with knockdown of FOXK1 expression was notably inferior to that of control cells. In contrast, the overexpression of FOXK1 facilitated the formation of cell tubes by the HUVECs (Fig. 4A, B). Concurrently, RT-qPCR was utilized to assess VEGF expression. The results uncovered that VEGF expression in HUVECs was significantly decreased upon FOXK1 knockdown, whereas VEGF expression was significantly increased after FOXK1 overexpression (Fig. 4C).

Knocking down FOXK1 activated the p-AKT/AKT signaling pathway to increase HUVECs migration and inhibit apoptosis, angiogenesis and VEGF expression

WB showed that upon FOXK1 knockout, the expression level of p-AKT was elevated, and the p-AKT/AKT (p-AKT/AKT is a ratio and meaning that higher ratio means higher pathway activity) signaling pathway was activated (Fig. 5A, B). In addition, it also revealed that the expression of p-AKT/AKT protein in the HG group was significantly lower than that in the LG group and the HM group (Fig. 5C, D). Treatment of cells with the AKT inhibitor MK-2206 for 24 h reversed the ability of FOXK1 knockdown to promote HUVECs migration (Fig. 6A, C). In addition, AKT inhibitor MK02206 reversed the ability of FOXK1 knockdown to inhibit apoptosis, angiogenesis, and VEGF expression in HUVECs (Figs. 6B and D and 7A, B, C, D and E).

Discussion

Contrary to the scenario where a high-sugar culture environment can promote the activity or migration of certain fibroblast cells or tumor cells, a high glucose environment leads to vascular endothelial dysfunction. This dysfunction is characterized by reduced cellular activity and heightened apoptosis within the vascular endothelial cells. Ultimately, these changes culminate in vascular necrosis and occlusion. In addition, vascular atresia induces increased VEGF expression due to hypoxia, thereby leading to the appearance of retinal neovascularization in DR. High glucose-induced vascular endothelial dysfunction is caused by multiple factors, such as oxidative stress, retinal mitochondrial dysfunction, advanced glycosylation end products and receptors, proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, growth factors, and microRNAs [14]. Specifically, Wang Shaohua’s team showed that the downregulation of lncRNA MIR181A2HG by HG impaired HUVECs proliferation and migration by dysregulating the miRNA/AKT2 axis [15]. Nie Jieming’s research suggested that UNC0321 inhibited the apoptosis and promoted migration by inhibiting Rab4/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in high glucose-induced HUVECs [16]. These are consistent with the present experimental observations that cell activity was decreased under high glucose environment, and that the migration ability was also affected. When FOXK1 gene was knocked down, both the cell viability and migration ability showed an increase, and the damage to endothelial cells was alleviated. Although the Transwell assay required an extended duration, we standardized the seeding number by counting viable cells and seeding equal quantities immediately after the 48-hour high-glucose treatment. This approach ensured consistent initial cell counts and viability across all groups, thereby minimizing interference from survival rate variations on migration readouts. Consequently, the observed changes in migratory capacity can be primarily attributed to FOXK1’s direct regulation of cellular motility, rather than differences in cell survival.

FOXK1, also known as myo-nuclear factor, represents a DNA-binding protein composed of 733 amino acid sequences that can specifically recognize the DNA sequence “WRTAAAYA”. Bioinformatics expression analysis [17] revealed that FOXK1 was expressed in poorly differentiated tissues of the brain, eye, heart, lung, and thymus. Studies [18] also reported FOXK1/K2 regulating sets of genes involved in mitochondrial metabolism, cell apoptosis, and cell survival in mouse liver cells. Knocking down FOXK1 and FOXK2 in a mouse hepatocyte cell line markedly altered the transcription of genes associated with lipid metabolism and mitochondrial functions. In addition, FOXK1 regulated oxidative stress damage in Parkinson’s disease patients through the ceRNA-FOXK1-Akt1 axis [19]. FOXK1 could play a role in the development of endothelial dysfunction in diabetic patients through the induction of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. However, additional research is warranted for validation.

In this study, it was found that in high glucose-induced HUVECs, silencing FOXK1 activated the p-AKT/AKT signaling pathway. Consistent with previous reports, AKT activation promotes the survival of endothelial cells, which holds considerable significance in the process of cell death induced by a variety of death signals [20, 21]. The PI3K/AKT pathway may act as a positive regulator of endothelial NO synthase in endothelial cells and play a key role in the processes of endothelial cell survival, mobilization, migration, and homing. AKT can induce the expression of the antiapoptotic gene Bcl-2 while activating the cell proliferation gene p70, thereby promoting cell survival [16]. An increasing number of studies have indicated the essential role of the PI3K/Akt/eNOS signaling pathway in the repair of endothelial damage under HG conditions [22, 23]. Once activated, phosphorylated AKT can directly phosphorylate eNOS and then produce NO, which is an endothelial cell survival factor. Animal models of diabetic ischemic injury reveal that the impaired angiogenesis may be partly attributed to the deficiency of NO. The PI3K/Akt/eNOS agonist 740 Y-P could significantly alleviate the high glucose-induced injury of HUVECs, including cytotoxicity, apoptosis, endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, and inflammatory response. Undoubtedly, the pathogenesis and progression of DR constitute a complex pathology involving multiple factors and multiple levels, dimensions, and interrelated processes. As a chronic condition, however, high glucose-induced cell models are unable to fully recapitulate the in vivo retinal microcirculation of diabetic patients. The possibility that FOXK1 acts in concert with other signaling pathways cannot be ruled out. Nevertheless, as a transcription factor, FOXK1 may regulate vascular endothelial cells injury in DR through multiple pathways and thus holds much potential as a therapeutic target.

Despite achievements, possible limitations of this study should still be acknowledged. Firstly, HUVECs are derived from the umbilical vein and cannot completely represent retinal microvasculature. Further experiments on retinal vascular endothelial cells and animal studies are required in the future to verify whether these effects are present in the development of retinal vascular endothelial damage in DR. Secondly, this study has demonstrated the role of FOXK1 in high glucose-induced endothelial damage through alterations in total FOXK1 expression and functional evidence. Given the current absence of rigorously validated phospho-FOXK1 antibodies for HUVECs, we have not yet assessed its activation status. Future work will employ mass spectrometry or phosphoproteomic approaches to systematically characterize FOXK1 phosphorylation sites under high-glucose conditions and elucidate their relationship with transcriptional activity, thereby further clarifying the underlying molecular mechanisms. Thirdly, recognizing that CCK-8 primarily reflects metabolic activity rather than direct proliferation rates, subsequent investigations will employ complementary Ki67 immunofluorescence staining and EdU incorporation assays to rigorously delineate FOXK1’s distinct effects on HUVEC proliferation. Fourthly, given this study’s exclusive focus on high-glucose-induced injury models, all functional assays were conducted under 25 mmol/L HG conditions; no si-FOXK1 groups were established in 5.5 mmol/L LG settings. Consequently, our experimental design does not permit direct assessment of whether FOXK1 knockdown fully restores HUVEC behavior to normal LG baseline levels. Future investigations should validate FOXK1 functionality in both LG and normoglycemic environments to definitively establish its high-glucose-specific regulatory nature. Finally, notably, although this study confirms that FOXK1 modulates HUVEC function by suppressing p-AKT/AKT signaling, we have not directly measured phosphorylation levels of its critical downstream effector eNOS. Published studies report that AKT activation promotes NO production via p-eNOS to maintain endothelial survival; consequently, future work will necessitate validation of whether FOXK1 mediates high-glucose injury through regulating the p-eNOS/NO axis.

Conclusion

To summarize, the current in vitro study demonstrated that FOXK1 attenuated the cell activity and migration of HUVECs induced by high glucose. Meanwhile, it promoted their apoptosis, angiogenesis, and VEGF production via a mechanism involving the inhibition of the p-AKT/AKT pathway. This study makes the first endeavor to elucidate the mechanism of FOXK1/p-AKT/AKT pathway in high glucose-induced endothelial injury. The findings indicate that the targeted silencing of FOXK1 may be a potential target for preventing high glucose-induced endothelial dysfunction in DR.
