Taurine Intake Alleviates Oxidative Damage During Transportation in Culter alburnus
Authors: Shuxuan Chen, Long Ren, Junjun Wei, Xue Xue, Yuan Wang, Yiping Han, Shang Wang, Dongpo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simple SummaryIn summary, dietary taurine regulated the antioxidant capacity and immune function of Culter alburnus through multiple mechanisms: optimizing nutritional metabolism to promote the growth of juveniles, alleviating gill tissue damage induced by transport stress, activating antioxidant enzyme systems to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), and regulating immune gene networks to balance immune responses. And, the effect was the best when the taurine supplementation was 2.0 g/kg. This study also systematically revealed the transcriptome response characteristics of the gill tissue of C. alburnus under transport stress and clarified the molecular pathway of taurine regulating its anti-stress, which provided basic data and mechanism reference for the transcriptome study of C. alburnus stress physiology.

1. Introduction

Fish transportation is a critical component of aquaculture operations but frequently induces acute stress responses that compromise fish health and survival. During transport, fish are subjected to multiple stressors, including temperature fluctuations, crowding, handling, elevated ammonia and nitrite levels, and hypoxia, which can disrupt physiological homeostasis and lead to immune suppression, oxidative damage, and even mortality [1]. One practical approach to mitigating transport stress involves preconditioning fish through dietary supplementation. Functional feed additives such as polysaccharides (e.g., β-1,3-glucan), herbal extracts (e.g., aloe), vitamin C, and probiotics have been used as immunostimulants to enhance physiological resilience [2,3]. Recent studies further demonstrate that specific amino acids can confer protective effects. For example, glycine supplementation at 2.5–10 g/kg has been shown to alleviate hyperammonemia, oxidative stress, and mortality during transport [4], while dietary arginine regulates inflammatory cytokines and enhances stress resistance [5]. However, the potential of taurine, a sulfur-containing β-amino acid derivative with known antioxidant and immunomodulatory properties, remains underexplored in this context. Taurine (β-aminoethanesulfonic acid), first isolated from bovine bile, is a chemically stable compound widely distributed in animal tissues, primarily in the form of small peptides or free amino acids [6]. In aquatic animals, taurine is involved in a variety of key physiological processes and has a wide range of physiological functions [7]. As an antioxidant, taurine can alleviate the oxidative damage caused by free radicals to the cell membrane in vivo and prevent apoptosis, autophagy and necrosis caused by abnormal cell function by capturing too many free radicals so as to maintain the homeostasis of cells and play an antioxidant role [8,9,10]. In addition, studies have found that taurine has immunomodulatory functions, which can regulate the activity of immune cells and promote the proliferation and activation of immune cells such as macrophages and lymphocytes [11,12], thereby enhancing the body’s ability to remove pathogenic microorganisms. Previous research has shown that waterborne taurine supplementation can alleviate acute ammonia-induced stress in yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco) [13]. However, most applications of taurine in aquaculture have focused on its use as a water additive, with relatively few studies examining its efficacy as a feed additive during transport-related stress. Given taurine’s capacity [7] to promote growth and enhance antioxidant defenses, we hypothesize that dietary taurine supplementation may improve stress tolerance and survival in fish during transportation.

Culter alburnus, a widely distributed freshwater species in eastern China, is an ecologically and economically important fish inhabiting the Yangtze River and Huaihe River and Yellow River basins [14]. It plays a key ecological role by suppressing small-bodied fish populations, thereby reducing predation pressure on zooplankton and promoting ecosystem stability [15]. Despite ongoing stock enhancement programs, restocking effectiveness remains limited, primarily due to the species’ high sensitivity to handling and transport stress, which reduces post-release survival.

In this study, we hypothesized that dietary taurine supplementation can mitigate transport-induced stress in C. alburnus. To test this, juvenile fish were fed diets containing graded concentrations of taurine, followed by 12 h of simulated transport. We assessed growth performance, survival rate, and key antioxidant and immune indices to identify an optimal taurine dosage that enhances physiological resilience and post-release viability. In addition, transcriptomics studies were conducted to further explore the molecular mechanism of stress response and oxidative loss caused by transport stress for juvenile C. alburnus and to contribute to the development of stress physiology and antioxidant defense theory in aquatic animals.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Experimental Diets

The experimental diets were supplemented with different concentrations of taurine on the basic diet (control: 0 g/kg; TAU 0.5: 0.5 g/kg; TAU 1.0: 1 g/kg; TAU 1.5: 1.5 g/kg; TAU 2.0: 2 g/kg). From Table 1, it can be seen that the main protein types contained in the feed are fish meal, soybean meal, and soybean protein concentrate, etc., and the main fat sources are fish oil and soybean oil, etc. All the ingredients were crushed and ground into powder using a 60-mesh sieve. The dough was fully mixed with fish oil and water and passed through a granulator (F-26 (II), South China University of Technology, Guangzhou, Guangdong Province, China) and dried in a ventilated oven at 45 °C for 24 h [16]. The dried feed was stored at −20 °C.

2.2. Experimental Procedure

In this study, healthy C. alburnus fingerlings were provided by the Suzhou Fishery Aquaculture Farm, located in Huzhou City, Zhejiang Province, China. The rearing trial was carried out in floating cages at the Jingjiang Experimental Aquaculture Base, which belongs to the Freshwater Fisheries Research Center, Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences. Before the start of the experiment, healthy juvenile C. alburnus [average initial body weight (IW) 0.4 ± 0.01 g] were acclimated to the cage (1 m × 1 m × 1 m) conditions for 2 weeks. Water quality indicators (dissolved oxygen concentration, ammonia nitrogen content and pH value) were measured once a week. A total of 4800 juvenile C. alburnus were randomly divided into 5 groups and reared separately in 15 cages (320 fish per cage). In the feeding trial, the fish were fed regularly three times (8: 00, 12: 00 and 16: 00) every day for 8 weeks (56 d) according to the standard of fish satiation and no residual bait. During the culture period, the water temperature was maintained at (27.0 ± 0.5 °C), the pH value ranged from 6.0 to 7.5, and the dissolved oxygen levels were maintained above 6 mg/L. The continuous aeration system was used to ensure the amount of dissolved oxygen in the water.

Simulated transport test: 1/3 volume of water was added to the double-layer plastic fish transport bag (30 × 52 cm), 50 fish of the same size were randomly placed and filled with 2/3 volume of pure oxygen. The transport bag was knotted with a rubber band and placed on a simulated automobile transporter (Changzhou, China) to simulate the transportation process of the fish for 12 h (the frequency of the transporter was set to 100 rpm during the simulated transportation).

The detailed operational procedures of the two experiments are illustrated in Figure 1.

2.3. Sampling

After the 56-day feeding trial, all fish were fasted for 24 h. The number of fish in each cage was counted, and 10 fish were randomly selected from each replicate cage for measurement, after which growth parameters were calculated. The simulated transportation test was carried out after fasting for 48 h; the control group and taurine groups (TAU 0.5, TAU 1.0, TAU 1.5, TAU 2.0) were set up. There were three replicates for each treatment. A total of 100 mL of water in the transport bag of three replicate groups was collected for subsequent determination of ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) content in the water. After anesthesia with MS-222 (60 mg/L), gill tissue samples were collected: one was frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent enzyme activity analysis and transcriptome sequencing, and the other was fixed in Bouin’s fixative for histological analysis.

2.4. Growth Index Analysis

The final body weight (FW) of the experimental fish in each cage was measured, and the weight gain rate (WGR), specific growth rate (SGR) and feed conversion rate (FCR) of each group were calculated. The specific calculation formulas are shown in Formulas (1)–(3).(1)Weight gain rateWGR,%=FWg−IWg÷IW(g)×100%(2)Specific growth rateSGR,%day−1=lnFWg−lnIWg÷d×100(3)Feed conversion rateFCR=Dry feed fedg÷Wet weight gaing

2.5. Analysis of Water Quality Parameters

Dissolved oxygen, pH and temperature in transport water were measured using a portable water quality analyzer (HACH Series, Loveland, CO, USA). The concentration of ammonium nitrogen was measured with a microcomputer ammonia nitrogen (MR) colorimeter (HANNA HI97715, Hanna, Villafranca Padovana, Italy).

2.6. Histopathological Analysis

Gill samples were fixed in Bouin’s fixative for 24 h and then stored in 70% ethanol. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to washing, gradient ethanol dehydration, clearing, paraffin infiltration, embedding, trimming, and sectioning. After dewaxing, the sections were stained with H.E. staining. Following staining, mounting, observation under a light microscope, and image acquisition were performed sequentially. Each experimental group included three biological replicates, with samples independently analyzed from each replicate.

2.7. Biochemical Assays

The gill samples of fish larvae in each group were homogenized with sterile cold normal saline at a mass (g)/volume (mL) ratio of 1:9. After being fully mixed, they were put into a refrigerated centrifuge and centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 10 min. The supernatant of the centrifuged sample was collected to obtain gill tissue homogenate, which was used to determine the antioxidant and immune enzyme activities. Each experimental group included three biological replicates, with samples independently analyzed from each replicate. Catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GPH-Px), active malondialdehyde (MDA), acid phosphatase (ACP), alkaline phosphatase (AKP) and total nitric oxide synthase (T-NOS) were determined by commercial detection kits. The concentration of total protein (TP) in the supernatant of gill tissue homogenate was determined by the Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) method. [The specific kit models are as follows: CAT: A007-1-1, GSH-Px: A005-1-2, MDA: A003-1-2, ACP: A060-2-2, AKP: A059-2-2, T-NOS: A014-2-2, and TP: A045-2-2. (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China)]

2.8. Transcriptome Sequencing

The TAU 2.0 group exhibited optimal growth, enzyme activity, and gill health post-transport, so we selected it for transcriptomic analysis to elucidate the mechanisms of the most effective dosage. The TAU 2.0 non-transport group was set as the control group (T-0h), and the TAU 2.0 group was the experimental group after 12 h of transport (T-12h). Three replicates were collected in each group, with a total of 6 gill tissue samples. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA was extracted from 6 samples (T-0h-1, T-0h-2, T-0h-3, T-12h-1, T-12h-2 and T-12h-3) in T-0h and T-12h, and the A260/280 ≥ 2.0 and A260/230 ≥ 2.0 of these samples were measured. The integrity and purity of RNA can be used for sequencing analysis. RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The transcriptome cDNA library was constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). The transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. All sequence data were deposited in the NCBI SRA database under accession number PRJNA1303038. Fastp (v0.20.0) [17] software was used to process raw reads in fastq format, and clean reads were obtained after removing low-quality reads for subsequent data analysis. The clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.4) [18]. FPKM [19] of each gene was calculated and the read counts of each gene were obtained by HTSeq-count [20]. PCA analysis were performed using R (v 3.2.0) to evaluate the biological duplication of samples. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened from the T-0h group and the T-12h group using DESeq2 software (v1.10.0) [21]. The DEG screening criteria were |log2FC| ≥ 1, q < 0.05 (where FC is the fold change). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using R software (v 3.2.0). R software was used to draw a histogram or enrichment analysis circle diagram for the significant enrichment function items.

After analyzing the significantly enriched signaling pathways, six immune-related DEGs were randomly selected as the validation objects of qRT-PCR to confirm the reliability of RNA-Seq results. qRT-PCR was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq TM II (Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) reagent, reaction system (Table S1), and primer sequence (Table S2), and gene primers were designed using NCBI. The relative expression level of DEGs was calculated by 2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the internal reference gene.

2.9. Data Analysis

Data were organized in Excel 2019, and one-way analysis of variance of the data was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 before statistical analysis. p < 0.05 indicated that the numerical difference was statistically significant. All data results were presented as mean ± standard error. The significant differences between the treatment groups were determined by Tukey test. GraphPad Prism 8 software was used to draw the graphics. The slices were observed using CaseViewer (https://www.sysmex-europe.com/products/products-detail/caseviewer/ accessed on 1 January 2026).

3. Results

3.1. Growth Parameters

The effects of dietary taurine on growth performance of juvenile C. alburnus are shown in Table 2. Compared with the control group, WGR and SGR of the TAU 1.5 and TAU 2.0 groups were significantly increased (p < 0.05), while FCR was significantly decreased (p < 0.05). These results indicate that dietary taurine supplementation at high concentrations can effectively promote the growth of Culter alburnus and improve its growth performance.

3.2. Water Quality Indexes

The specific data on the effect of taurine addition in feed on the quality of transport water are shown in Table 3. There was no significant difference in water temperature, pH value and NH3-N between the TAU 0.5, TAU 1.0, TAU 1.5, and TAU 2.0 groups and the control group after transportation (p > 0.05). In TAU 2.0 group, the NH3-N content was 14.24% lower than in the control group. The survival rate (SR) of the control group after transportation was 98.67%, and the other groups were 100%.

3.3. Histological Influence

After 12 h of transportation, the gill filament diameter of juvenile Culter alburnus showed an upward trend with increasing taurine concentration, peaking in the TAU 1.5 group. Regarding the secondary gill lamellae, compared with the control group, the lamellar length in the TAU 0.5 and TAU 1.5 groups increased significantly (p < 0.05), while the lamellar spacing was significantly reduced (p < 0.05) in all groups except the TAU 2.0 group (Table 4). The histological changes in gill tissue after 12 h of transport were classified into six categories: normal structure, necrosis, epithelial bulging, lamellar fusion, hypertrophy, and hyperplasia. The gill tissue structure of the taurine-supplemented groups was basically consistent with that of the control group. Under H.E. staining, all groups exhibited clear and intact gill tissue structures, with lamellar fusion observed in both the taurine-supplemented groups and the control group. Compared with the control group, the degree of secondary gill lamellae curvature in the TAU 1.5 and TAU 2.0 groups was significantly reduced (Figure 2). Mucous cells, exocrine cells, and epithelial cells on the gill lamellae showed varying degrees of vacuolization, which was alleviated with increasing taurine concentration. The lowest degree of vacuolization was observed in the TAU 2.0 group.

3.4. Antioxidant Immune Ability

Dietary taurine supplementation significantly enhanced immune and antioxidant responses in C. alburnus following transport. Specifically, ACP and AKP activities were significantly elevated in the TAU 1.0, TAU 1.5, and TAU 2.0 groups compared to the control (p < 0.05) (Figure 3d, f). As shown in Figure 3e, T-NOS activity was significantly reduced in the TAU 0.5 and TAU 2.0 groups (p < 0.05). MDA levels were significantly lower in the TAU 1.0 and TAU 2.0 groups (p < 0.05), indicating reduced oxidative damage (Figure 3a). Notably, GSH-Px and CAT activities were significantly increased in the TAU 2.0 group (p < 0.05), with GSH-Px reaching its highest level in the TAU 0.5 group (Figure 3b, c).

3.5. Transcriptome Analysis

The Q30 base distribution of each sample was 92.76%~93.68%, and the average GC content was 47.31% (Table S3).

Through principal component analysis (PCA), the overall metabolic differences between each group of samples can be fully reflected, and the degree of variation between samples in the group can be measured. As shown in Figure 4, PCA revealed significant differences between the T-0h and T-12h groups (p < 0.05). The T-12h group was mainly distributed in the second quadrant, while the T-0h group was concentrated in the fourth quadrant. These results confirm the rationality of the sample processing method and provide a reliable basis for subsequent analyses.

After differential expression analysis of cDNA libraries of T-0h and T-12h, 1478 DEGs were screened, including 619 upregulated genes and 859 downregulated genes, as shown in Figure 5a.

As shown in Figure 5b, GO enrichment analysis was performed on the T-0h group and the T-12h group. The GO terms mainly enriched in the biological process category were extracellular matrix organization, antimicrobial humoral immune response mediated by antimicrobial peptide, and proteolysis, etc. The GO terms mainly enriched in the cellular component category were extracellular matrix, extracellular region, and extracellular space, etc. The GO terms mainly enriched in the molecular function category were serine-type endopeptidase activity, chemokine receptor binding, and extracellular matrix structural constituent, etc. In summary, most of the DEGs in the gills after transport stress were enriched in immune- and protein-synthesis-related pathways.

After KEGG enrichment analysis of T-0h group and T-12h group, a total of 340 KEGG signaling pathways were obtained, and 23 pathways were significantly enriched (p < 0.05). The main enriched signaling pathways were immune and metabolic pathways such as Cytokine–Cytokine Receptor Interaction, HIF-1 signaling pathway, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, protein digestion and absorption and Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (TLRs) (Figure 5c).

In order to verify the results of transcriptome sequencing, six immune-related DEGs with high expression were randomly selected for qRT-PCR assay. The qRT-PCR verification showed that the expression levels of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) such as CXCR4, cldn7a, CDH1, H2-Aa, OCLN, Il12b and TLR5 were significantly upregulated, which were consistent with the RNA-seq data, indicating that the transcriptome sequencing results were reliable (Figure 6).

4. Discussion

Transport stress induces significant disturbances in physiological and biochemical processes, including hormone secretion and material-energy metabolism, directly affecting fish health and even threatening their survival. As an antioxidant, taurine not only promotes fish growth performance to a certain extent, but also enhances their antioxidant and immune functions. Furthermore, it regulates the activity of intestinal digestive enzymes (e.g., amylase, lipase, protease) in fish, thereby facilitating the breakdown and absorption of dietary carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins [22]. However, the current application of taurine for transport stress is mainly focused on water additives and is less used as feed additives. After 56 days of feeding with taurine-supplemented diets, the WGR and SGR in the high-taurine groups (TAU 1.5 and TAU 2.0) significantly increased, while the FCR significantly decreased (p < 0.05). These results suggest that dietary taurine supplementation enhances intestinal function, thereby promoting the digestion, absorption, and utilization of feed nutrients, which ultimately improves growth performance in juvenile Culter alburnus [23]. Although Liu [24] reported no significant effect of taurine on the growth of C. alburnus, we speculated that this discrepancy stems from the longer 8-week feeding period in the present study. Combined with species-specific differences and variations in feed types, juvenile C. alburnus had more sufficient feeding opportunities, increasing their total nutrient intake and ultimately leading to higher WGR and SGR. In addition, no significant differences in WGR, SGR, or FCR were observed between the low-taurine groups (TAU 0.5, TAU 1.0) and the control group (p > 0.05), indicating that taurine’s physiological regulation is concentration-dependent.

During transportation, the deterioration of water quality, particularly the accumulation of ammonia nitrogen originating from fish metabolism, is a major factor inducing physiological stress in fish [25]. Ammonia (NH3) is the main final product of protein catabolism in teleosts, and its production rate is directly related to protein oxidation rates [26]. Studies have shown that taurine can effectively alleviate ammonia-induced acute stress response in species like the large yellow croaker [27], crucian carp and grass carp [28] during transportation. Although no statistically significant difference in NH3-N content was observed between all taurine-supplemented groups and the control group (p > 0.05), the TAU 2.0 group showed a 14.24% reduction in NH3-N content. The higher survival rates observed in taurine-supplemented groups suggest that dietary taurine may help inhibit endogenous protein breakdown, reduce nitrogen excretion, and alleviate the adverse effects of transport stress on metabolic homeostasis, which, in turn, may contribute to the observed improvement in transport water quality. Given that water temperature and pH remained stable across all groups, environmental factors are unlikely to have influenced ammonia levels, which lends support to the possibility that the reduction in NH3-N concentrations is at least partially associated with the regulatory effects of taurine.

The gills are a critical organ responsible for respiration, osmoregulation, nitrogenous waste excretion, and acid-base balance in aquatic organisms. As the first organ to respond to adverse environmental conditions, they have been frequently used as a biomarker for stress responses [29]. Therefore, in this study, the morphological changes in gill tissue structure were used to directly evaluate the alleviation effect of dietary taurine on transport-stress-induced gill injury in Culter alburnus. H.E staining results showed that dietary taurine increased gill filament diameter and secondary lamella length in juvenile C. alburnus after transportation compared with the control group. This indicates that taurine contributes to enhancing the mucosal immune barrier, thereby reducing stress-induced gill damage. Additionally, taurine-supplemented groups exhibited reduced lamella spacing, which likely arises from structural optimization under transportation stress and contributes to improved gill tissue stress resistance. Cengiz et al. [30] proposed that gill damage in fish falls into two categories: the first is defensive damage, including proliferation of filament epithelial cells, swelling of gill lamellar respiratory epithelium, and excessive mucus secretion; the second is damage directly induced by toxic substances (e.g., epithelial cell necrosis, sloughing). In this experiment, gill tissue exhibited cell vacuolization, but no obvious abnormalities in epithelial cells or other severe histological damage (e.g., necrosis, sloughing) were observed. These results indicated that dietary taurine could protect the ultrastructure of gill cells to a certain extent, alleviate cell vacuolization, and inhibit necrosis. This observation is consistent with the findings in Carassius auratus [31], where dietary taurine has been shown to play a certain role in maintaining the stability of gill epithelial cells.

The accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during transport overwhelms the antioxidant defense system, inducing oxidative stress in fish. Elevated CAT activity and glutathione (GSH) levels can mitigate this stress [32,33]. Malondialdehyde, a key biomarker of lipid peroxidation, signals biomolecular damage when its levels rise [34]. CAT activity was significantly increased in the TAU 2.0 group (Figure 3b). Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity peaked in the TAU 0.5 group and remained elevated in TAU 2.0 (Figure 3c). MDA level, a key marker of lipid peroxidation, was significantly decreased in TAU 1.0 and TAU 2.0 (Figure 3a). The increased CAT and GSH-Px activities reflected the enhanced enzymatic antioxidant defense, and the synergistic action of these two enzymes reduced the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The decrease in MDA level indicated the decrease in lipid peroxidation, indicating that taurine supplementation alleviated the oxidative damage to cell membranes during transport stress. These findings were consistent with previous studies on yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco) [35] and turbot (Scophthalmus maximus) [36], where dietary taurine supplementation has been shown to effectively scavenge reactive oxygen species, mitigate lipid peroxidation, and enhance cellular antioxidant defense to alleviate inflammatory stress. Notably, studies have demonstrated that taurine improves antioxidant capacity by activating the Nrf2-Keap1 signaling pathway, thereby regulating the expression and activity of antioxidant enzymes, which may explain why taurine-induced upregulation of CAT and GSH-Px was observed in our experiment [23]. Compared with the control group, ACP and AKP activities were significantly elevated in the TAU 1.0, TAU 1.5, and TAU 2.0 groups (Figure 3d,f), while T-NOS activity was significantly reduced in the TAU 0.5 and TAU 2.0 groups (Figure 3e). ACP and AKP are indicators for evaluating the immune status of fish and are involved in fish non-specific immunity [37]. The increase in their activities reflects the enhancement of non-specific immune function, especially in lysosomal degradation and mucosal barrier defense. However, although nitric oxide (NO) is involved in physiological processes such as immune defense and angiogenesis, excessive NO production in the body mediates the pathological effects of endotoxins and pro-inflammatory factors, causing inflammation and tissue damage [38]. The decrease in T-NOS activity indicated that taurine inhibited the excessive activation of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) induced by transport stress, thereby reducing T-NOS activity and excessive NO production. This maintained the normal physiological function of the fish during transportation and alleviated immune-mediated oxidative stress. These findings are consistent with previous studies on Culter alburnus [24] and Chinese mitten crab (Eriocheir sinensis) [39], in which dietary taurine supplementation increased ACP and AKP activities in the liver and intestine by balancing immune activation and reducing oxidative damage.

As fish gills directly interact with the external environment, they serve as the first barrier against external stressors (e.g., pathogens and chemicals) and play a crucial role in fish development and disease resistance [40]. Therefore, transcriptome sequencing was used to analyze the transcriptomic gene expression profiles of gill tissue in juvenile C alburnus subjected to 12 h of transport stress, following dietary supplementation with 2.0 g/kg taurine. In this study, a total of 1478 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the gill tissue of C. alburnus under transport stress, comprising 619 upregulated and 859 downregulated genes compared to the control group. GO/KEGG enrichment analyses showed that these DEGs were mainly involved in metabolic pathways, including proteolysis and protein digestion and absorption. This suggests that taurine may alleviate transport-stress-induced metabolic disturbances in gill tissue through the modulation of protein catabolism. Further GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of immune signaling and cell-death-related pathways under transport stress. This implies that taurine could enhance stress tolerance precisely by modulating these enriched pathways associated with oxidative stress and immune responses.

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed a significant activation of the Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway in the T-12h group compared to the T-0h group. In fish, the TLR signaling pathway has been shown to play a key role in the response to bacterial and viral infections [41,42]. The upregulation of TLR5 can activate flagellin-mediated NF-κB signaling via the MyD88-dependent pathway at the cell membrane [43], thereby promoting the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., Il12b) and immune effector molecules [44]. This response efficiently removes the invading pathogens, breaking the vicious cycle of “immune overreaction—reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst” caused by pathogen infection, thereby reducing the risk of oxidative damage. This result was consistent with a study on grass carp [45], which demonstrated that taurine-mediated upregulation of TLR5 enables the recognition of invading pathogens and promotes downstream immune responses to eliminate them, thereby protecting the organism from damage. While the present study only compared changes before and after transportation, when combined with our previous observations of enzyme activity responses, it can be hypothesized that taurine may prime the TLR signaling pathway to better cope with tissue damage and inflammatory stress during transportation. This was consistent with a previous study showing that dietary taurine supplementation regulates Toll-like receptors in Seriola rivoliana to improve stress resistance [46].

In this study, compared with the T-0h control group, the Cytokine–Cytokine Receptor Interaction pathway was significantly activated, and the cytokine Il12b was significantly upregulated in C. alburnus after 12 h of transport following dietary taurine supplementation. These results confirmed the potential immunomodulatory role of taurine in alleviating immune stress under transport conditions. Cytokine is a soluble small-molecule protein secreted by immune cells or tissue cells that can regulate the interaction between cells and participate in the inflammatory response. They are key mediators for information transmission between immune cells [47]. Il12b activates natural killer (NK) cells, which kill virally infected cells [48] and secrete interferon-γ (IFN-γ) to facilitate viral clearance [49]. The observed upregulation of Il12b in our study reflects a stress-induced defensive inflammatory response rather than excessive inflammation, which may help balance immune activation and stress tolerance in fish, thereby avoiding immune paralysis caused by prolonged transport stress. This was consistent with previous findings in Larimichthys crocea [50] that taurine modulated pro-inflammatory factor expression to enhance disease resistance under stress. Collectively, these results indicate that, under the conditions of dietary taurine supplementation, transport stress triggered the activation of the Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathway and Cytokine–Cytokine Receptor Interaction pathway via the upregulation of immune-related genes (TLR5, Il12b) in C. alburnus. This stress-induced response likely supports tissue repair and immune homeostasis, potentially reducing oxidative damage and thus mitigating the adverse effects of transport stress on the fish.

5. Conclusions

In summary, dietary taurine supplementation was associated with improved antioxidant capacity and immune function in C. alburnus, which may be linked to optimized nutritional metabolism, reduced transport-induced gill damage, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity, and balanced immune gene expression. And, the effect was the best when the taurine supplementation was 2.0 g/kg. This study also systematically revealed the transcriptome response characteristics of the gill tissue of C. alburnus under transport stress and clarified the molecular pathway of taurine, which provided basic data and mechanism reference for the transcriptome study of C. alburnus stress physiology.
