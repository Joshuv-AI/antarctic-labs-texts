Factors associated with utilisation of antenatal care visit services in Nepal: evidence from Demographic and Health Survey 2016–2022
Authors: Om Chandra Thasineku, Sudesh Pandit, Yogendra Bahadur Gurung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveAntenatal care (ANC) visits play a vital role in protecting women’s health and their unborn babies. This study aimed to examine the associated factors with utilisation of ANC visits by using Andersen’s behavioural model based on predisposing, enabling and need factors.DesignCross-sectional study using secondary datasets from the Nepal Demographic and Health Survey 2016 and 2022 was appended for analysis. The analysis accounted for the stratified two-stage cluster sampling design of the survey by applying sampling weights and adjusting for clustering and stratification. A multivariable ordinal logistic regression analysis was conducted, reporting results in an adjusted OR (AOR) at a 95% CI.SettingNepal.ParticipantsThis study included 3909 women aged 15–49, who were pregnant and possibly used ANC visit services within the 2 years preceding the survey.Outcome variableANC visits.ResultsAmong the 3909 women aged 15–49, 3.18% never attended ANC, 21.20% did not complete the recommended ANC visits, while 75.63% completed the recommended ANC visits. In predisposing factors, women from the Madhesi ethnic group (AOR=0.397, p<0.001), with no education (AOR=0.528, p<0.010), non-internet user (AOR=0.451, p<0.001) and no exposure to health programme (AOR=0.601, p<0.050) had lower odds of completing recommended ANC visits. Among enabling factors, women from the Terai region (AOR=0.163, p<0.001), Karnali province (AOR=0.277, p<0.001), poorest households (AOR=0.139, p<0.001) and those without health insurance enrolment (AOR=0.484, p<0.050) showed lower odds of completing recommended ANC visits. Regarding need factors, women who visited a health facility for the last 12 months (AOR=0.414, p<0.001) and multiparous women with parity 6+ (AOR=0.143, p<0.001) were less likely to complete recommended ANC visits compared with the reference category.ConclusionsEnhancing women’s education, access to internet use and health programme exposure, as well as addressing caste-based, provincial and economic disparities, are crucial for improving ANC utilisation. Furthermore, promoting health insurance enrolment and raising awareness of pregnancy-related risk among multiparous women are essential for the utilisation of recommended ANC visits to enhance the health of mothers and newborn babies.Keywords: Nepal, Pregnancy, Pregnant Women, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) is a key maternal healthcare service that supports the well-being of both the pregnant mother and the unborn baby during pregnancy.1 2 It includes preventive, diagnostic and educational measures aimed to ensure optimal outcomes during pregnancy, delivery and the postpartum period.3 ANC provides a unique opportunity for the anticipation and management of complications arising from pregnancy, advocacy of good health practices and immunisation, and information dissemination on essential health services.4 5 Globally, increased access and usage of ANC services reduces morbidity and mortality, highlighting their key role in an integrated reproductive healthcare system.6,8

Realising the importance of ANC, WHO initially recommended a minimum of four ANC visits during pregnancy. Emerging empirical evidence showed that increasing the number of ANC contacts could significantly improve maternal and perinatal outcomes.3 9 Thus, WHO revised its recommendation guidelines in 2016 and now recommends a minimum of eight ANC contacts for pregnant women, emphasising the need for earlier initiation and continuity of care throughout the pregnancy.3 ANC uptake varies globally across contexts due to differences in health systems, socioeconomic status, culture and policy frameworks.10 In high-income countries, coverage of ANC is nearly universal, facilitated by well-established healthcare infrastructure and high quality of care. Conversely, many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face substantial barriers preventing their population from using ANC services.11 Such barriers include limited access to health facilities, shortages of skilled healthcare providers, financial constraints and sociocultural constraints that may limit women’s agency and decision-making autonomy.12 13 Consequently, a significant proportion of women in LMICs still fail to attain the recommended number of ANC contacts,14 resulting in missed opportunities for early detection and prevention of obstetric complications.15

Nepal, undergoing demographic and epidemiological transitions, has made significant progress in maternal health over the past two decades.16 The maternal mortality ratio (MMR) has markedly declined from 539 per 100 000 live births in 199617 to 151 in 2021,18 reflecting sustained improvements in maternal health outcomes. This achievement has been supported by national efforts such as the Safe Motherhood Policy,19 the Newborn Health Long-Term Plan (2006–2017), National Safe Motherhood20 and the Nepal Health Sector Strategy.21 These frameworks have collectively prioritised the expansion of skilled maternal healthcare services and community-based interventions. Additionally, the Maternal Health Roadmap (2018–2030)22 aligns with global health goals and enhances the quality, accessibility and equity of ANC services across the nation. Four ANC visits for women will be incentivised with a cash payment of NPR800 to the women on completion of the four ANC visits at 4, 6, 8 and 9 months of pregnancy, delivery at the health facility and postnatal care.23

The Nepal Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) 2022 offers a recent, nationally representative dataset that enables a re-evaluation of the current state of ANC utilisation and its associated factors.16 Given the changes in health policy, service delivery and population dynamics since the last survey, it is important to examine the recent patterns and determinants of ANC contact. This study aims to identify the associated factors with ANC utilisation in Nepal using nationally representative DHS data from 2016 to 2022. We applied complex sample-adjusted ordinal logistic regression to capture gradients in ANC visits while ensuring methodological rigour, offering a novel approach integrating Andersen’s behavioural model. By addressing persistent sociodemographic, economic, geographical and health system disparities into predisposing, enabling and need factors, this study provides critical insights that influence utilisation of ANC, thereby providing evidence to support targeted interventions and policy measures to improve maternal health outcomes and reduce inequalities in access to utilisation of ANC.

Methods

Setting

Nepal, located in South Asia, is administratively divided into seven provinces.24 It has greatly diversified ecological regions stretching from the Himalayan Mountains to the Terai plains.25 Health services comprise both public and private hospitals, but access to health services is limited in rural areas. Nepal faces several socioeconomic challenges, with the majority of the population living in poverty.26 Although progress has been made, disparities in access to education and healthcare services still remain uneven.

Study design

This study used secondary data from nationally representative data from NDHS 2016 and 2022 and employed a quantitative research design.27 The survey was conducted under the supervision of the Ministry of Health and Population and New ERA with technical support from ICF International.16 17 Details regarding methodology can be sourced from the official NDHS 201617 and NDHS 202216 reports.

Sampling and sample size

The updated sampling frame adopted in the NDHS 2016 and NDHS 2022 was obtained from the Nepal Population and Housing Census of the National Statistical Office.16 17 For national representation, the survey included the three ecological regions and seven provinces of Nepal. The stratification of all the seven provinces in Nepal created 14 sampling strata according to urban and rural areas. Thereafter, the selection of the sample was done through a two-stage stratified sampling method.

The NDHS 2016 and NDHS 2022 datasets were pooled28 29 to increase sample size and ensure the sufficient number of observations in the ‘no ANC visits’, ‘1–3 ANC visits’ and ‘4 or more visits’ categories for reliable analysis and robust statistical analysis.

Of all the interviewed households, 13 089 (in 2016) and 15 238 (in 2022) women, aged between 15 and 49, were eligible for the survey. A total of 12 862 (in 2016) and 14 845 (in 2022) women were interviewed, with 98.3% (in 2016) and 97.4% (in 2022) response rates. In accordance with the study’s objective, the analysis included 1978 (in 2016) and 1931 (in 2022) women aged between 15 and 49 who had live births in the 2 years preceding the survey presented in figure 1.

Data collection and processing

In Nepal, DHS 2016 data were obtained from 19 June 2016 to 31 January 2017, and DHS 2022 data were obtained from 5 January to 22 June 2022. After the completion of data collection in each cluster, the data files were registered. Then, data files would be screened for consistency, completeness and presence of any outliers.

Outcome variables

The study used ANC visits as the outcome variable. It was recoded into an ordinal variable ‘0’ for no ANC visits, ‘1’ for one to three ANC visits and ‘2’ for four or more visits. This categorisation allows for ordinal logistic regression analysis, distinguishing between women with no ANC visits, those with incomplete visits (one to three ANC visits) and those who completed the recommended number of visits (4+ ANC visits).

Independent variables

According to Andersen’s behavioural model, this study classified 17 independent variables into three categories: predisposing factors, enabling factors and need factors,30 as presented in figure 2. Sex of the household head, ecological zone, province, place of residence, wealth index and health facility visit during the last 12 months were included as independent variables, retaining their original classifications from the DHS dataset. Moreover, if women’s reported frequency of reading newspaper or magazine, frequency of listening to radio and frequency of watching television were categorised as almost every day, at least once a week and less than once a week, at least one category was considered as indicating participation in mass media exposure; otherwise, it was not exposure to media. These programmes consisted of eight different health-related programmes that were aired on radio and television.

Conceptual framework

Figure 2 presents the framework based on Andersen’s behavioural model, which describes factors associated with ANC visits and service utilisation. According to the theoretical constructs of Andersen’s behavioural model, variables are divided into three categories: predisposing, enabling and need factors.31 Predisposing factors encompass sociodemographic characteristics that shape an individual’s disposition to use healthcare services32; enabling factors refer to the resources and accessibility to the utilisation of healthcare facilities31 32; and need factors reflect both perceived and evaluated health needs that motivate individuals to seek and use healthcare services.33

Data and statistical analysis

Stata V.15.1 was used for data processing and statistical analyses.34 Complex sample analysis using Stata’s ‘svyset’ and ‘svy’ commands accounted for sampling weights, clustering and stratification to ensure accurate estimates and reliable significance testing. Variables significantly associated with the outcome variable in χ2 tests were further assessed by ordinal logistic regression for multivariable analysis. Multivariate ordinal logistic regression analyses were employed to identify factors associated with utilisation of ANC visits. The results were reported as adjusted ORs (AOR) with 95% CIs. A p value <0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

The study employed a multivariable logistic model to estimate AORs at a 95% CI. We employed two models to estimate the AORs and were guided by Andersen’s behavioural model, categorising predictors into predisposing, enabling and need factors.

Model I (domain-specific model)

Separate multivariable logistic regression models were fitted for each domain of Andersen’s behavioural model (predisposing, enabling and need). Within each domain, all relevant variables were simultaneously entered into the model for estimation of AORs for potential confounding among variables within the same domain. This approach allowed for the assessment of the independent contribution of factors within each conceptual group.

Model II (full multivariable model)

A final multivariable logistic regression model was constructed by including all of the predictors from the three domains of Andersen’s behavioural model (predisposing, enabling and need) at the same time. This model provided fully adjusted AORs, accounting for potential confounding across domains at the same time.

Before performing ordinal logistic regression, variance inflation factors were assessed to detect multicollinearity. No issues were found among independent variables within predisposing, enabling and need factors.35

The outcome variable, ANC visits, was categorised into three ordered levels: no ANC (0), one to three ANC visits (1) and four and more ANC visits (2), based on the NDHS data structure and four-visit benchmark used in national monitoring in Nepal during the survey periods, reflecting the WHO-recommended threshold for adequate ANC visits, although the WHO currently recommends eight ANC contacts.3 Because the dependent variable is inherently ordinal rather than nominal or continuous, an ordinal logistic regression was the most suitable statistical modelling strategy.36 In the initial phase of analysis, a regular ordinal logistic regression without the survey adjustment indicated the violation of the assumed proportional odds (χ²(14)=25.73, p=0.028). However, such tests ignore the complex DHS design and could mislead inferences, ignoring stratification, clustering and sampling weights. When the complex sample design was properly specified, the test for proportional odds assumption was no longer violated, as indicated by the likelihood ratio test (χ²(14)=22.23, p=0.074) and further confirmed by the generalised ordered logit model with survey adjustment (gsvy: gologit2, autofit), which yielded F(27, 415)=1.25 (p=0.1813). The proportional odds assumption was satisfied once the complex sample design was incorporated; there was no need to employ multinomial or generalised models. Thus, the final analysis was done using survey-adjusted ordinal logistic regression (svy: ologit), which reflects the ordered nature of ANC visits36 and ensures valid SE estimation under the DHS complex sample framework.

Patient and public involvement

The data used for this study are publicly available and fully anonymised, containing no personally identifiable information, with secondary data analysis without direct involvement of patients.

Results

The trend of ANC visits shows an improvement over time; the proportion of women with no ANC visits decreased from 3.64% in 2016 to 2.70% in 2022. Similarly, the percentage of women with one to three visits declined from 25.52% in 2016 to 16.77% in 2022. On the contrary, the proportion of women completing four or more (recommended) ANC visits increased substantially from 70.85% in 2016 to 80.53% in 2022. Overall, in the pooled sample, 75.63% of women had completed the recommended four or more ANC visits, indicating a positive trend in ANC utilisation in Nepal over the survey periods, as presented in figure 3.

Geographical and economic inequalities within enabling factors were also evident for ANC service utilisation. Women from Bagmati and Sudurpaschim provinces, urban residents and higher wealth quantiles had four or more ANC visit utilisations compared with those from Karnali province, rural residents and lower wealth quantiles presented in table 2.

Health facility system and behavioural factors as need factors also showed strong association with ANC service utilisation. Women who visited a health facility in the past 12 months and those with a lower birth order number were more likely to complete four or more ANC visits in comparison to those who did not visit a health facility in the past 12 months and those with a higher birth order number, as shown in table 2.

Table 3 showed various factors were significantly associated with ANC utilisation. Among predisposing factors, women from the Dalit ethnic group (AOR=0.440, 95% CI 0.272 to 0.711, p<0.010) and Madhesi ethnic group (AOR=0.397, 95% CI 0.240 to 0.659, p<0.001) had lower odds of four or more ANC visits compared with the Brahmin/Chhetri ethnic group in model I only. Women with no education (model I: AOR=0.528, 95% CI 0.367 to 0.760, p<0.010) in model I only and basic education (model I: AOR=0.603, 95% CI 0.439 to 0.829, p<0.010; model II: AOR=0.686, 95% CI 0.488 to 0.963, p<0.050) in both models were less likely to complete four and more ANC visits. Lack of internet use remained strongly associated with lower ANC utilisation in both models (model I: AOR=0.451, 95% CI 0.342 to 0.596, p<0.001; model II: AOR=0.457, 95% CI 0.334 to 0.626, p<0.001). Similarly, women with no health programme exposure had lower odds (AOR=0.601, 95% CI 0.399 to 0.904, p<0.050) of attending higher ANC visits compared with women with health programme exposure in model I only.

Among enabling factors, urban residence was significant only in model II (AOR=0.646, 95% CI 0.475 to 0.879, p<0.010), and the Terai region (model I: AOR=0.163, 95% CI 0.085 to 0.315, p<0.001; model II: AOR=0.218, 95% CI 0.103 to 0.460, p<0.001) had lower odds of four and more ANC visits. While analysing provincial disparities, it was significant in both models, particularly for Koshi, Madhesh, Bagmati, Gandaki and Karnali. Women categorised within the poorest wealth category (model I: AOR=0.139, 95% CI 0.070 to 0.274, p<0.001; model II: AOR=0.320, 95% CI 0.156 to 0.655, p<0.010) and poorer wealth category (model I: AOR=0.297, 95% CI 0.158 to 0.557, p<0.001; model II: AOR=0.514, 95% CI 0.271 to 0.973, p<0.050) exhibited significantly lower odds compared with women in the richest wealth category. Moreover, women who were not covered by health insurance coverage showed lower odds (AOR=0.484, 95% CI 0.271 to 0.865, p<0.050) of attending higher ANC visits compared with women with insurance coverage in model I only.

The analysis of need-related factors showed that women who did not visit a health facility in the last 12 months exhibited a significantly lower association with ANC utilisation in both models (model I: AOR=0.414, 95% CI 0.329 to 0.520, p<0.001; model II: AOR=0.578, 95% CI 0.388 to 0.862, p<0.010) compared with those who did visit a health facility in the last 12 months. Women with a birth parity were inversely associated with ANC visits. Compared with first parity, women with second to third parity had lower odds (AOR=0.583, 95% CI 0.477 to 0.713, p<0.001); those with fourth to fifth parity had lower odds (AOR=0.256, 95% CI 0.196 to 0.344, p<0.001); and women with 6+ parity had lower odds (AOR=0.143, 95% CI 0.089 to 0.231, p<0.001) for higher ANC visit utilisation in model I only.

Discussion

Nepal has committed to achieving the Sustainable Development Goals target of reducing the MMR to 70 per 100 000 live births and the neonatal mortality ratio to 12 per 1000 live births by the year 2030.37 Some important health-related policy frameworks38,41 in Nepal emphasised the completion of recommended ANC visits as a strategy for achieving maternal health-related targets. Such as after completing the recommended ANC visits, cash payment as an incentive of NPR800 is given to women after health facility delivery.42 Although the WHO recommends a minimum of eight ANC contacts,3 the use of four and more visits in this study aligns with national monitoring indicators in Nepal during the NDHS survey periods, ensuring comparability and policy relevance. These frameworks appear to have attempted to promote maternal and neonatal health by adopting relevant strategies. However, disparities in the utilisation of ANC visit services and the prevalence of not completing the recommended ANC visit still exist in Nepal. This policy framework emphasises the need to strengthen the utilisation of ANC visits. According to the study, the aim was to examine the associated factors with utilisation of ANC visits in Nepal in terms of predisposing, enabling and need-related factors. Some variables may partly reflect underlying health-seeking behaviour (visiting a health facility in the last 12 months, health programme exposure and internet use) rather than acting as purely independent determinants of ANC utilisation; therefore, results are interpreted with caution.

Predisposing factors

Caste and ethnicity are significant determinants of maternal health service utilisation in Nepal, particularly in the context of ANC, as evidenced by the findings from the study.43 Despite national efforts to promote equitable access to maternal health services, women from disadvantaged caste and ethnic groups continue to face systemic barriers that limit their ability to receive timely and adequate ANC.44 According to a study based on the Nepal Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey of 2014 and 2019, women from Dalit, disadvantaged Janajati and other Madhesi caste groups were significantly less likely to complete the recommended four or more ANC visits when compared with women belonging to advantaged caste groups.45

A cross-sectional study conducted in the Mahottari district, focusing on the Dalit population, revealed that only 42% of Dalit women completed four or more ANC visits. The study emphasised that discriminatory attitudes and lack of culturally sensitive care were major barriers for Dalit women.46 These findings suggest that caste-based inequities in ANC utilisation are not solely due to economic disadvantage but are also rooted in social exclusion, discrimination and lack of culturally appropriate services. Women from marginalised castes may avoid or delay ANC visits due to fear of mistreatment, lack of trust in the health system or limited awareness of their rights and entitlements. These findings indicate that economic deprivation is not the only cause of caste-based inequities in ANC utilisation, but that they are also rooted in social exclusion, discrimination and lack of culturally appropriate services. In fear of being mistreated and lacking trust in the health system and not having awareness of her rights, women from marginalised castes would delay or avoid using ANC visits.

Findings indicated that women with no education are less likely to complete recommended ANC visits than women with secondary and higher education. These findings align with previous studies: women with higher education levels are significantly more likely to make an early start with ANC and complete the recommended ANC visits.47 ANC visits are widely recognised as a crucial pathway to promoting institutional delivery48 and maternity care. Women with secondary or higher education were significantly more likely to complete four or more ANC visits compared with women who had no education.3149,53 Expanding educational opportunities, along with health literacy initiatives, remains crucial for improving utilisation of ANC visits. So, maternal education is consistently identified as one of the most influential determinants of ANC utilisation.

Internet use was a significant determinant of ANC utilisation, and a similar study also shows the same findings.51 The use of internet-based technologies is promising to enhance maternal healthcare as well as ANC utilisation.54 A comprehensive scoping review found that digital health technologies substantially improved ANC access and utilisation.55 With access to timely information, reminders and virtual support, digital technologies enable women to make informed choices and comply with suggested schedules for ANC.54 This study shows that internet use and digital communication tools can bridge critical gaps to complete recommended ANC visits, especially in resource-constrained settings. These findings showed the significance of designing inclusive and culturally sensitive health programmes that reach underserved populations. Exposure to health programmes is an important factor in ensuring the completion of recommended ANC visits. Similar findings were also found in a multilevel analysis conducted across 16 sub-Saharan African countries, which found that exposure to structured health programmes significantly influenced the timing and frequency of ANC visits.56 Health programmes are an important entry mechanism through which women are educated, reducing barriers to care and promoting timely ANC attendance.

Enabling factors

Urban women had significantly lower odds of ANC utilisation, reflecting a proximity effect, where easy access creates confidence that services can be sought when needed, reducing recommended ANC visits. Urban women may also be discouraged from visiting ANC regularly for other reasons, such as the high costs44 57 of private services,58 perceived poor quality of care,59 poor attitudes of healthcare providers57 60 and weak community social support61 62 in urban settings. Evidence indicates that financial barriers, dissatisfaction with care and limited social support are key determinants of adequate maternal healthcare utilisation in urban contexts. These findings suggest that beyond physical access, affordability, quality and social factors critically shape ANC visits among urban women in Nepal.

The ecological setting in Nepal is another important factor governing the use of ANC services. The country’s three major ecological zones—Mountain, Hill and Terai—differ in terms of geography, infrastructure and healthcare accessibility, all of which shape maternal health behaviours. In this study, findings suggest that women from the Terai region are less likely to complete the recommended ANC visits compared with those in the Mountain and Hill regions. Similarly, a study also found that residents of the Terai region had significantly lower odds of completing four or more ANC visits.63 One reasonable explanation is behavioural: women in the Terai may delay or skip ANC visits due to the perception that services are easily accessible at any time, leading to complacency.50 These findings suggest and highlight the need for region-specific interventions. In the Terai, health programmes should focus not only on improving access but also on raising awareness, promoting early initiation of ANC and addressing cultural and behavioural factors that may hinder timely care.

This study found that province-level differences significantly affect the likelihood of completing four or more ANC visits. A similar study also revealed that women in Madhesh and Karnali provinces were significantly less likely to attend the recommended ANC visits. These provinces, which include parts of the Terai and Karnali regions, respectively, face challenges such as poverty, limited health infrastructure and cultural barriers.31 Further supporting this, Pandey et al emphasised that provinces were significant determinants of ANC utilisation. Their study indicated that provincial disparities contribute to gaps in the continuum of care, encompassing ANC, institutional delivery and postnatal care services.51

Women who belonged to the poorest category of the wealth index were more likely to miss the recommended ANC visits as compared with the richest wealth category. The same group also exhibited a reduced likelihood of completing the recommended ANC visits, thus enhancing the already existing disparity. These findings are coherent with those from studies in sub-Saharan Africa, where economic capability is acknowledged as a key determinant of adequate ANC uptake.53 Likewise, many studies reported that optimal ANC utilisation was significantly associated with wealth status, with poorer women being less likely to access timely and frequent care.64,68 This suggests that poverty remains a very important barrier to accessing essential maternal health services, especially recommended ANC visits.

This study found that health insurance enhanced significantly a woman’s likelihood of completing the recommended number of ANC visits. Women enrolled in health insurance were significantly more likely to attend at least four ANC visits compared with those without insurance.56 Similarly, in Cambodia, women with health insurance were also significantly more likely to use ANC services.69 These findings align with the literature, suggesting that health insurance mitigates financial barriers, reduces out-of-pocket expenditures and encourages timely and adequate use of maternal health services. Insurance coverage facilitates access to care and promotes continuity, financial risk protection mechanisms and quality of care by reducing the economic burden on households.

Need factors

The ANC visits are also associated with need factors. Women who had visited a health facility in the past 12 months were probably able to complete their recommended ANC visits due to the strong association that exists between recent utilisation of health services and ANC visits. Women who had recently visited a health facility were more likely to use ANC, consistent with evidence that regular interaction with health workers increases trust, awareness and continuity of care.70 These findings align with previous studies emphasising the role of healthcare access and contacts in completing adequate ANC utilisation, whereby a lack of recent facility visits may reflect barriers to service or reduced pregnancy risk perception.67 71 72 Such evidence resonates with established research highlighting that timely and repeated interaction with health services is pivotal in completing recommended ANC visits, particularly in low-resource settings.67 68 72 73

ANC visits during pregnancy seem to be less of a priority for women who have given birth more than six times compared with women who have just experienced their first birth. Thus, it indicates a negative relationship between high parity and recommended ANC visits.71 These findings also align with extensive evidence indicating that higher parity negatively affects ANC utilisation due to factors such as increased maternal confidence from prior pregnancies, competing household responsibilities, economic barriers and limited perceived need for repeated care. Furthermore, high parity is often intertwined with socioeconomic and demographic variables that collectively lower the involvement with formal maternal health services.65 68 71 74

The utilisation of recommended ANC visits in Nepal was significantly associated with predisposing, enabling and need factors. Women from disadvantaged ethnic groups with lower levels of education, limited internet access and poor exposure to health programme were less likely to complete recommended ANC visits. Moreover, geographical disparities, poor economic status of households and lack of health insurance remained important barriers for utilisation of recommended ANC visits. Similarly, higher parity and limited healthcare-seeking behaviour are also associated factors of ANC visits and service utilisation among need factors. These findings highlight the need for multisectoral targeted and equitable maternal health interventions for socioeconomically and geographically vulnerable women to improve utilisation of ANC visit services.

This study has several strengths and limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The study used two consecutive nationally representative DHS datasets of Nepal, employed a large sample size and used survey-weighted analyses guided by Andersen’s behavioural model, which strengthens the validity and national generalisability of the findings. However, the cross-sectional design does not permit causal interpretation, and reliance on self-reported ANC information may be affected by recall bias. In addition, the datasets did not include several relevant health system and service quality variables, such as quality of care, service provider attitudes and facility readiness. Despite these limitations, the study offers nationally representative evidence on factors associated with ANC visit utilisation in Nepal and provides important insights for maternal health policy and programme planning.

Conclusions

Policy frameworks in Nepal appear to have attempted to promote maternal and neonatal health by adopting different strategies; however, some mothers in Nepal still did not complete the recommended ANC visits. Enhancing women’s education, caste-based awareness and information through the internet (such as social media), together with health programme exposure; addressing ecological regions, especially in the Terai region, and provincial disparities; addressing economic barriers for low-income households; expanding financial protection through social health insurance along with engagement in health facilities and health workers; and eradicating misconceptions and tailored counselling about repeated ANC visits, especially among multiparous women, are essential to completing recommended ANC visits and targeted interventions for aligning with Andersen’s behavioural model to mitigate risks and improve safe delivery for maternal and neonatal health outcomes. Together, these strategies can contribute to universal ANC coverage and accelerate Nepal’s progress towards maternal and health goals.
