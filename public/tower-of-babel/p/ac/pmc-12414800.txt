Black Hairy Tongue as a Rare Adverse Effect of Linezolid in Multidrug‐Resistant Tuberculosis: A Case Report
Authors: Dipendra Adhikari, Salina Paudel, Munna William, Manoj Kumar Bhatt, Sanju Dahal, Abishkar Gautam, Binay Adhikari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBlack hairy tongue (BHT), or lingua villosa nigra, is a rare adverse effect of linezolid, an antibiotic frequently used in the treatment of multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis (MDR‐TB). We present a case of a 24‐year‐old female who developed BHT while receiving linezolid as part of a longer regimen for MDR‐TB. The patient exhibited a typical BHT presentation, with painless brown‐to‐black discoloration on the posterior dorsal surface of her tongue, appearing 25 days after initiating linezolid therapy. There were no other identifiable contributing factors. Upon discontinuation of linezolid and a shift to a modified regimen, the BHT completely resolved within 10 days. A Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale score of 5 suggested a probable causal relationship between linezolid and BHT. This report represents the first documented case of linezolid‐associated BHT from Nepal, highlighting the importance of clinicians' awareness of this rare but clinically significant side effect, especially within the context of MDR‐TB treatment, to provide prompt diagnosis, reassurance, and appropriate management.

Summary Clinicians managing multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis (MDR‐TB) with linezolid should recognize black hairy tongue (BHT) as a rare, benign, and self‐limiting adverse effect.Early identification can prevent unnecessary investigations, provide timely reassurance to patients, and guide temporary management decisions without compromising regimen efficacy.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global public health concern, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Despite advances in diagnosis, prevention, and treatment, the emergence of multidrug‐resistant/rifampicin‐resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR‐TB) poses a major challenge to global TB control efforts. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates approximately 400,000 new MDR/RR‐TB cases in 2023 [1]. Managing MDR/RR‐TB requires second‐line anti‐tuberculosis drugs. The WHO Consolidated Guidelines on Tuberculosis (Module 4: Treatment—Drug‐Resistant Tuberculosis Treatment, 2022 update) recommend either a 6‐month BPaLM regimen (bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, and moxifloxacin) or a 9‐month all‐oral regimen (bedaquiline, levofloxacin or moxifloxacin, ethionamide or linezolid, ethambutol, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and clofazimine) [2]. The 9‐month regimen is suitable for all ages, while BPaLM is preferred for individuals aged 14 years and older. A longer regimen (18–20 months) is recommended if BPaLM and the 9‐month all‐oral regimens are unsuitable [2].

Linezolid, an oxazolidinone antibiotic, is included in all three WHO‐recommended regimens. It inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 50S ribosomal subunit, preventing the formation of a functional initiation complex. While highly effective against MDR/RR‐TB, linezolid is associated with several adverse drug reactions. Common side effects include headache, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. Prolonged use can lead to dose‐dependent toxicities such as myelosuppression, peripheral neuropathy, and optic neuropathy, potentially requiring dose adjustments or discontinuation [3]. Black hairy tongue (BHT), also known as lingua villosa nigra, is a rare side effect, with an incidence of only 0.2% [4]. This case report details a 24‐year‐old female who developed BHT while receiving linezolid as part of a longer regimen for MDR/RR‐TB, the first such case reported from Nepal to our knowledge.

Case Presentation

Case History and Examination

A 24‐year‐old female from a refugee camp in Nepal presented with a prolonged cough, purulent sputum, weight loss, lethargy, and intermittent fever. She reported no known TB contact history or prior illness. Initial investigations included chest X‐ray (revealing bilateral opacities), AFB smear microscopy (2+ on both slides I and II), and GeneXpert testing (detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis with high bacterial load and rifampicin resistance). Further testing for drug sensitivity, culture, and Line Probe Assay (LPA) was undertaken, and she commenced a longer regimen (LR1‐ Linezolid, Clofazimine, Pyrazinamide, Levofloxacilin, Bedaquiline) following completion of additional necessary laboratory work. After 23 days, second‐line LPA results indicated resistance to fluoroquinolones, prompting a change to an LR2 regimen (Linezolid, Clofazimine, Pyrazinamide, Cycloserine, Bedaquiline). Black discoloration of her tongue appeared after 25 days of anti‐tubercular treatment. She reported no burning sensation, itching, pain, discharge, or altered taste. She denied smoking, alcohol use, tea/coffee consumption, or illicit drug use. She had no history of chronic illness. Examination revealed brown‐to‐black discoloration on the dorsal surface of her tongue, extending from the middle to the posterior region, with elongated filiform papillae creating a “carpet‐like” appearance (Figure 1). Vital signs were normal.

Investigations

Renal function tests, liver function tests, and serum uric acid levels were within normal limits. Her complete blood count showed only mild anemia (hemoglobin 10.6 g/dL); other parameters were normal. A literature review indicated that linezolid was the only medication she was taking that could cause BHT.

Treatment and Follow‐Up

As the patient was experiencing significant cosmetic distress and anxiety, linezolid was held after a shared decision‐making discussion and with her informed consent. Linezolid was discontinued and delamanid was added, while she continued the other LR2 medications. The BHT gradually improved and resolved completely within 10 days (Figure 1). The patient tolerated re‐challenge well; delamanid was stopped, and there was no recurrence of BHT. A Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale score of five indicated a probable causal relationship between linezolid and the BHT (Table 1). She subsequently achieved sputum culture conversion at 3 months and was doing well with ongoing treatment and regular follow‐up.

Discussion

BHT is a rare, benign, and self‐limiting reaction to linezolid, usually limited to cosmetic concerns and resolving with supportive care [6]. It is characterized by elongation and enlargement of the filiform papillae on the dorsal surface of the tongue, resulting in discoloration (typically black, but can be brown, yellow, green, or blue) [7]. The exact pathophysiology is not fully understood, but proposed mechanisms include decreased papillae desquamation due to lack of friction, leading to elongation and enlargement. Discoloration is thought to result from the accumulation of bacteria, fungi, food particles, coffee, tea, tobacco, and other substances, or from porphyrins produced by chromogenic bacteria or yeast [8].

Known risk factors for BHT include poor oral hygiene, excessive consumption of tea or coffee, smoking, xerostomia, trigeminal neuralgia, general debilitation, and certain medications. Antibiotics, including erythromycin, linezolid, doxycycline, penicillin, minocycline, and metronidazole, are well‐documented causes of BHT [8]. In our patient, the only identifiable risk factor was linezolid 600 mg orally once daily, used as part of a longer regimen (LR1/LR2) for multidrug‐resistant TB. The Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale score of 5 (Probable Adverse drug reaction) further supports linezolid as the likely causative agent [5]. Patients typically present with painless, dark brown‐to‐black discoloration on the dorsal surface of the tongue, often concentrated in the midline or posterior region. Some patients experience mild irritation, burning, tickling, dysgeusia, halitosis, or dry mouth [8, 9]. Our patient experienced none of these additional symptoms.

Diagnosis of BHT is based on visual inspection and a thorough history to rule out other causes and drug interactions. In reported cases, linezolid‐induced BHT typically appears within a median of 2 weeks (range 2–14 days) [10]. In our patient, black discoloration was noted after 25 days of linezolid use. Treatment primarily involves discontinuation of the causative agent and maintenance of good oral hygiene. Discontinuation of linezolid has been reported to resolve discoloration within a median of 7 days (range 3 days to 6 months) [10]; in our case, resolution occurred after 10 days. Topical baking soda application or rinsing with diluted hydrogen peroxide (1.5%–3%) might aid exfoliation and reduce pigmentation. If discoloration persists despite discontinuation of linezolid and improved oral hygiene, other conditions such as fungal infections or systemic diseases should be considered [8, 11].

Linezolid is classified as a Group A priority medicine in WHO guidelines and is considered an essential component of MDR‐TB regimens, particularly in cases with fluoroquinolone resistance, where its continuation is recommended unless grade 3 or 4 adverse events such as severe myelosuppression, optic neuritis, or peripheral neuropathy occur [12, 13]. In our patient, linezolid was temporarily withheld for cosmetic distress, substituted with delamanid, and later reintroduced without recurrence, underscoring the need for individualized, patient‐centered management. The dose dependence of this reaction remains unclear and warrants further study.

Conclusion

This case report highlights the rare but clinically significant complication of BHT associated with linezolid use in MDR‐TB treatment. Although alarming in appearance, BHT is self‐limiting and improves with good oral hygiene or discontinuation of the drug. Clinicians should be aware of this potential side effect to avoid unnecessary investigations and provide appropriate reassurance to affected patients.
