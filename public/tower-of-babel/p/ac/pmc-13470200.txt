Mesenchymal transitions reduce lamin A expression and nuclear stiffness to enhance confined migration in glioblastoma
Authors: Landon Teer, Neha Anil, Dominic Armagno, Bradley Mahaffey, Sihan Sun, Charles Froman-Glover, Akshitkumar Mistry, Kavitha Yaddanapudi, Joseph Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The evolution of glioblastoma (GBM) is coupled with a proneural-to-mesenchymal transition (PMT) that exacerbates disease in part through heightened cell invasiveness and spread. PMT follows similar molecular and phenotypic features with epithelial to mesenchymal transitions (EMT) and promotes rapid GBM invasion despite tight physical barriers in the dense brain parenchyma. Recent studies suggest that efficient confined migration is key to navigating these physical constraints; however, the role of PMT in mediating rapid confined migration remain unclear. Using microchannel platforms, AFM, and molecular assays, we explore this question through a biophysical lens to investigate the impact of GBM mesenchymal transformation on cellular mechanics and confined migration. We found that TGF-β1 induced PMT decreased expression and organization of Lamin A, leading to a reduction in nuclear stiffness, especially in U251 cells (1278 Pa → 682.6 Pa). Mesenchymal U251s migrated more quickly through confined microchannels though mesenchymal U87s migrated more slowly. Notably, nuclear stiffening via RO-3306 dramatically reduced confined migration speed in TGF-β1 treated U251 cells. Our study reveals that TGF-β1 induced PMT reduces Lamin A expression and softens the nucleus to support efficient confined migration in a cell-type dependent manner. These findings indicate that PMT alters the biophysical properties of the cell, opening new directions for developing therapeutic strategies for this deadly disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56141-0.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive, stage 4 glioma with a dismal 15-month median patient survival1–4. Current treatment consists of surgical resection followed by radiotherapy and concomitant chemotherapy5–7,but these strategies have done little to improve patient survival8–10. This lack of therapy efficacy is due in part to the evolving tumor microenvironment (TME), which limits therapy transmission and induces phenotypic changes in GBM cells that confer therapy resistance and promote tumor evolution11–13.

GBM is characterized by a highly mutable genome (TERT, TP53, EGFR, PTEN, etc.1,14, which enables cell plasticity and the acquisition of pro-malignant changes including the development of therapy resistant mechanisms such as MGMT mediated DNA repair15–17, alterations in tumor metabolism, and enhanced cell invasiveness. Many of these pro-tumorigenic mechanisms are activated in response to changes in the TME, which include signaling molecules, oxygenation, and biophysical changes18–22. Notably, many of these pro-malignant changes correlate with a highly plastic phenotypic transition known as proneural-to-mesenchymal transition (PMT), which is similar to the framework of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in other cancers23,24. Although GBM is not an epithelial tumor, PMT shares common molecular and phenotypic features to EMT including increased invasiveness, a greater resistance to standard therapy, and enhanced secondary tumor formation25–28.

Although GBM cells do not generally metastasize, they rapidly infiltrate other regions in the brain, migrating along white matter tracks, vascular components, and through the hyaluronic acid (HA) rich parenchyma29–33. Notably, the brain parenchyma is extremely dense as pore size exists on the nanometer scale, yet GBM cells invade rapidly nonetheless while other cells and therapeutics struggle to penetrate the tissue29,34–37. Intriguingly, recent studies reveal that the most aggressive and invasive cancer cell lines exhibited the softest mechanical profile, identifying a link between cancer malignancy and cell mechanics38. This phenomenon has been observed in many cancer types, including GBM, which suggests that cancer cells can adopt mechanically softer, compliant phenotypes to bypass the tight, physical barriers and facilitate enhanced cancer invasion and spread39–43. Specifically, research has suggested that nuclear mechanics is a key mediator of this infiltration with lamin A and C mediating these properties by regulating intermediate network formation within the inner nuclear membrane44. However, it remains unclear whether PMT regulates biophysical changes and if these alterations lead to enhanced infiltration potential.

Here, we investigate the impact of mesenchymal transitions on cytoskeletal and nuclear mechanics and its role in confined migration by leveraging traditional molecular biology approaches, biophysical tools, and bioengineered platforms. We hypothesize that TGF-β1 induced PMT enhances confined migration through lamin-dependent reductions in nuclear stiffness. Using human GBM U251 and U87 cell lines, we find that mesenchymal transitions alter nuclear and cytoskeletal expression, leading to dramatically softer nuclei and efficient confined migration in a cell-type dependent manner. Further, nuclear stiffening via lamin depolymerization inhibitor RO-3306 significantly reduced confined migration in mesenchymal GBM cells. Our work reveals a mechanistic link between mesenchymal activation and reduced nuclear lamin A expression, prompting further exploration of this axis as a potential therapeutic target.

TGF-β1 induces mesenchymal transitions in GBM

As a first step in assessing the biophysical alterations that occur during PMT, we wanted to verify that incubation with TGF-β1, a well-known inducer of mesenchymal transitions, is sufficient to drive mesenchymal transitions in GBM cells45,46. We treated human GBM cell lines U251 and U87 with 10 ng/ml of TGF-β1 and measured indicators of TGF-β1 activation and mesenchymal transitions including pSMAD2 and pSTAT3 activation and ZEB1 expression. With TGF-β1 treatment, both cell lines displayed significant increases in SMAD2 phosphorylation (Fig. 1A, D) indicating activation of the canonical TGF-β1 pathway. Further, cells treated with TGF-β1 showed significant increases in STAT3 phosphorylation (Fig. 1B, E), a well-established driver of mesenchymal pathway activation. We then probed for mesenchymal transcription factor ZEB1 and found that it was indeed upregulated in both cell lines (Fig. 1C, F). Taken together, these data confirm that TGF-β1 is sufficient to induce mesenchymal transitions in U251 and U87 cell lines.

Mesenchymal transitions alter cytoskeletal organization and reduce nuclear lamin A expression

Next, we wondered how structural components of the cell are influenced by PMT and examined changes in protein expression and organization of the actin cytoskeleton and the nuclear lamina, key regulators of cytoskeletal and nuclear rigidity, respectively. We first explored changes in F-actin/G-actin ratios and found that U251s exhibited similar levels of F-actin but an increase in G-actin (Fig. 2A) while U87s had minimal changes (Fig. 2F). Immunostaining showed pronounced F-actin fibers around the cell in NT and TGF-β1 treated U251 cells; however, TGF-β1 treated U251s had more G-actin pools within the interior of the cell (Fig. 2A). In U87 cell lines, minimal changes were observed via immunostaining with similar staining intensity and organization in both cohorts (Fig. 2F). Cell-type specific differences were also noted when evaluating changes to α-tubulin and vimentin. U251s displayed higher basal level α-tubulin expression than U87s, but U87s exhibited higher levels of vimentin expression (Supplementary Fig. 6B, C; F, G). TGF-β1 treatment did not affect α-tubulin and vimentin expression in both cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 12H-K). We then examined changes in the nuclear lamina during PMT and by evaluating the expression and organization of lamin A and lamin C, given their known role in regulating nuclear mechanics. We found that TGF-β1 treated reduced lamin A in both U251s and U87s (Fig. 2B, G); however, lamin C was unchanged in U251s but reduced in U87s (Fig. 2C, H). Given the more prominent reduction in lamin A compared to lamin C, we further explored alterations in lamin A organization following TGF-β1 treatment. Notably, lamin A fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced in both cell lines with thinner and more disrupted lamin stain around the nucleus (Fig. 2D, I). This alteration in lamin A expression also leads to changes in nuclear shape with reduced nucleus circularity with TGF-β1 treatment for both U87 and U251s (Fig. 2E, J). From these data, we reveal that TGF-β1 induces cell-type specific significant changes to the cytoskeletal and nuclear structural proteins with lamin A reductions being consistent between the cell lines.

Mesenchymal transitions lead to reductions in cytoskeletal and nuclear stiffness

After observing changes in the expression of structural proteins in the cell, we were interested in how cellular rigidity might change. Using AFM, we probed the cytoskeleton and nucleus by generating 12 × 12 force maps at 3 positions in the cell body around the nucleus and on top of the nucleus. We first found that U251 cells were stiffer than U87s with non-treated U251s displaying a stiffness of ~ 1700 Pa for the cytoskeleton and ~ 1300 Pa for the nucleus, while U87s exhibited a stiffness of ~ 900 Pa for the cytoskeleton and ~ 400 Pa for the nucleus (Fig. 3A, B; G, H). U251s appeared to have more heterogeneity across cells as evidenced by the histogram distribution (Fig. 3B, H). Additionally, for both cell lines, the cytoskeleton was stiffer than the nucleus. When treated with TGF-β1, cytoskeletal and nuclear stiffnesses were significantly reduced in both cell lines with treated U251s displayed stiffnesses of ~ 1300 Pa and ~ 600 Pa and U87s having stiffnesses of ~ 500 Pa and ~ 300 Pa in the cytoskeleton and nucleus, respectively (Fig. 3D, E; J, K). For both cell lines, reductions in stiffness were statistically significant (Fig. 3C, F; I, L). Interestingly, the softening trend differed between the cell lines with U251 exhibiting more pronounced relative reductions in the nucleus (46.57%) when compared to the cytoskeleton (33.24%), while U87 softening was more pronounced in the cytoskeleton (46.23%) than in the nucleus (11.17%). In summary, both U251 and U87 exhibited reductions in mechanical rigidity following structural protein changes; however, U251s showed dramatic losses in nuclear stiffness while U87s exhibited large reductions in cytoskeletal stiffness.

TGF-β1 induced mesenchymal transition promotes confined migration in U251s but not U87s

Mesenchymal GBM is the most invasive subtype of GBM and rapidly infiltrates through the brain parenchyma despite its dense ultrastructure and tight pores. Thus, we investigated how TGF-β1 induced changes might support this rapid infiltration by evaluating migration in 2D and under confinement. In 2D motility assays, we found that TGF-β1 treatment increases mean cell speed in U251s but had no effect on cell persistence (Fig. 4A, B), and TGF-β1 incubation led to no changes in U87 mean cell speed and persistence (Fig. 4D, E). Although traditional 2D free motility experiments provide some insight into cell migration, they fail to recapitulate the biophysical constraints seen in vivo thus limiting insights gained. Thus, we investigated confined migration using a PDMS microchannel device of varying channel widths (50 μm, 20 μm, 10 μm, and 5 μm) to simulate the tight pores seen in the brain parenchyma. As expected, NT cells for both cell lines showed a decrease in mean cell speed with decreasing channel widths, indicating that tighter confinement impedes efficient migration (Fig. 4C, F). With TGF-β1 treatment, U251 cells migrated significantly faster, even under tight confinement in the 5 μm channels (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, TGF-β1 did not increase mean cell speed in U87s but instead dampened speeds in all widths except in the 5 μm channel, which exhibited no significant differences (Fig. 4F). We wondered if differences in actin dynamics may be responsible for this result, and we examined cofilin activity due to its established role in regulating actin polymerization/depolymerization. Interestingly, TGF-β1 treatment led to a significant reduction in cofilin phosphorylation, indicating more actin depolymerization and a more dynamic cytoskeleton (Supplementary Fig. 14A, D). Increased actin dynamics is known to affect cell polarization, which is crucial for confined migration and thus may be responsible for the reductions we observe. However, it is important to note that despite this reduction in confined migration speed in TGF-β1 treated U87s, U87s migrated faster than U251s across channel widths, showing cell-type dependent differences in innate invasion capacity and sensitivity to TGF-β1. These data provide evidence that the pro-invasive impact of TGF-β1 is cell type dependent and that changes to the structural proteins and cell rigidity may underlie these effects.

Nuclear stiffening via RO-3306 reduces confined migration of TGF-β1 treated cells

Because of the consistent reduction in lamin A and the reduction in nuclear stiffness with TGF-β1 treatment, we explored whether stiffening the nucleus by preventing lamin disassembly would limit confined migration in TGF-β1 treated cells. To investigate this relationship, we incubated TGF-β1 treated cells with RO-3306, a CDK1 inhibitor that inhibits lamin phosphorylation and depolymerization. In both U251 and U87, RO-3306 treatment increased nuclear stiffness to non-treated stiffness ranges despite the continuous presence of TGF-β1 (Fig. 5A, C). However, RO-3306 treatment did have a cell-type specific effect with treatment, reducing 2D cell migration speeds in U87s but not in U251s (Supplementary Fig. 11). Treatment with RO-3306 decreased confined migration speed for TGF-β1 treated groups across channel widths for both cell lines (Fig. 5B, D). Importantly, the increased confined migration observed with TGF-β1 treatment in U251s was lost with nuclear stiffening via RO-3306 (Fig. 5B). Although U87 responses to RO-3306 may confound the impact of nuclear stiffening on U87 confined migration, U251 data show that nuclear stiffening via RO-3306 can inhibit the enhanced confined migration conferred by TGF-β1, linking mesenchymal transition induced reductions in nuclear stiffness with GBM cell invasion potential.

Discussion

Increased invasion is a well-known effect of mesenchymal transitions in cancer; however, comparatively little is known about the impact of mesenchymal transitions on biophysical changes and their influence on invasion, especially in the context of confined migration, a mode of motility critical for infiltration in 3D environments. Models of confined migration include the action of the cytoskeleton (protrusion and contraction) as well as the deformation of the nucleus as parameters for successful squeezing through constrictions47,48. Additionally, overall reductions in cellular stiffness support successful transit through confinement. Following this paradigm, our data show that U251 and U87s display different basal level expression of nuclear and cytoskeletal proteins and that TGF-β1 induced PMT alters the expression and organization of the actin cytoskeleton and nucleus in cell type dependent manner. Due to the complex interconnected nature of cytoskeletal elements, it is unsurprising that cells may remodel their cytoskeletal and nuclear structure differently. Our data shows that basal level expression of cytoskeletal and nuclear proteins vary significantly and provides a basis for cell stiffness. For example, U251s have higher expression of Lamin A/C and α-tubulin, but lower expression of vimentin when compared to U87s (Supplementary Fig. 12). Given the role of microtubules in supporting compressive stresses, U251s exhibited stiffer cytoskeleton, and the elevated Lamin A/C expression led to stiffer nuclei. TGF-β1 induced mesenchymal transitions softened both cell lines in cytoskeletal and nuclear stiffness, but the underlying mechanism differs. Whereas reductions in cytoskeletal stiffness may be attributed to a change in F-actin/G-actin ratio in U251s, U87s experience a change in cytoskeletal dynamics with TGF-β1 decreasing cofilin phosphorylation, leading to more actin depolymerization and a reduction in cytoskeletal stiffness (Supplementary Fig. 14). Notably, there is a consistent reduction in Lamin A across both U251s and U87s, leading to a decrease in nuclear stiffness for both U251s and U87 and a more substantial reduction in U251 cells. Our data matches recent reports from other cancers that identify Lamin A specifically as a key regulator of nuclear mechanics49–51. It has been long thought that Lamin A and C have redundant roles within the nucleus, supporting nuclear integrity through reinforcement of the inner nuclear membrane and participating in gene transcription regulation; however, a growing body of research is illuminating Lamin A and C specific functions51,52. With respect to nuclear mechanics, Lamin A appears to play more vital roles in regulating nuclear shape and integrity and is more sensitive to mechanical environments and facilitating mechanosensitive pathways53–55. Lamin A is also more rapidly integrated into the nuclear lamina and governs a strain stiffening phenomenon during nuclear deformation54. Moreover, in stiffer tissues such as bone, enhanced Lamin A expression is noted, presumably to bear the increased loads within the stiffer microenvironment55. In cancer, reduced Lamin A has been observed and associated with efficient confined migration potential51. Lamin A alterations have also recently been found to be influenced by EMT through an Akt dependent pathway56. Consistent with these findings, our study connects mesenchymal transitions in GBM with reduced Lamin A expression.

TGF-β1 induced reductions in Lamin A and nuclear stiffness in U251s led to more efficient confined migration in all channel widths (Fig. 4C); however, this was surprisingly not observed in U87s (Fig. 4F), with mean cell speed reduced in all channel widths except the 5 μm channel. These cell-type differences in confined migration may be attributed to differential responses to TGF-β1, cell mechanical profiles, F-actin/G-actin expression and actin dynamics. Although bulk tumor cells U87s and U251s have not been subtyped, there is evidence that these cell lines display markers that are associated with different GBM phenotypes. For example, U87s have been associated with a more mesenchymal phenotype whereas U251s have been linked to the proneural subtype, which is less migratory and more prone to phenotypic transitions57,58. This is evidenced by the characteristics of NT U87s in our data, which are softer and more migratory than NT U251s. These differences in phenotypic background may influence their sensitivity to the TGF-β1 response, leading to a divergence in functional migration. Additionally, U87 and U251s had distinct mechanical signatures after TGF-β1 stimulation with U87 softening being more pronounced in the cytoskeleton (46.23%) than in the nucleus (11.17%), while U251 exhibiting more pronounced relative reductions in the nucleus (46.57%) than in the cytoskeleton (33.24%). These unique mechanical profiles may influence efficient confined migration, which requires a balance of cytoskeletal integrity and nuclear softening59. Moreover, the function of the actin cytoskeleton is critical for successful confined migration as cells must be able to polarize and generate sufficient contractile force60,61. In this regard, TGF-β1 significantly reduced cofilin phosphorylation in U87s, leading to increased actin cleaving, which may perturb cell polarization (Supplementary Fig. 14)62. Importantly, cofilin phosphorylation was unaffected in U251 cell lines. Additionally, TGF-β1 treatment led to differences in F-actin/G-actin expression with U87s exhibiting minimal changes to actin cytoskeletal expression while U251s had much higher levels of G-actin with no change in F-actin expression (Fig. 2A, F). Increased G-actin pools are often associated with increased cell migration as high levels of G-actin are necessary to sustain rapid F-actin polymerization at the leading edge63. In models of confined migration, rapid cytoskeletal reorganization is required and facilitated by the presence of a sufficient G-actin pool to enable proper polarization of the cell64. Thus, these differences in G-actin expression and cofilin phosphorylation may underlie the functional differences observed in U87s and U251s. Collectively, these data suggest that cells from different phenotypic backgrounds within the tumor may have variable sensitivity and response to TGF-β1, and the extent of functional changes that can occur in mesenchymal-like cells may be limited. Further investigations into subtype specific differences in cytoskeletal and nuclear structural make up will also provide more insight into these differences.

Our report also shows that nuclear stiffness is a key regulator of enhanced confined migration in PMT-induced cells. In U251s, TGF-β1 induced mesenchymal transitions led to increased confined migration speed that was ablated with the introduction of nucleus stiffening agent RO-3306, an inhibitor of lamin phosphorylation and disassembly. Although TGF-β1 treatment did not increase confined migration speed in U87s, treatment with RO-3306 did increase nuclear stiffness and reduce overall confined migration potential. As mentioned above, efficient confined migration occurs through the balanced interplay between cytoskeletal activity (polarization and contraction) and nuclear mechanics, and our data indicate that TGF-β1 influences the activity and properties of the cytoskeleton and nucleus, which impact its confined migration potential. Importantly, our study highlights the significance of nuclear mechanics in facilitating confined migration, as both cells are drastically impacted by a stiff nucleus. This provides evidence that PMT-induced reductions in nuclear stiffness via decreased lamin A is a major biophysical change that supports efficient and rapid confined migration in GBM.

Our report adds to the growing body of evidence that identify cellular biophysical characteristics as an important mediator of cancer invasiveness and spread. Our findings provide a mechanism by which cells within the TGF-β1 rich TME can undergo PMT to acquire reduced nuclear stiffness via decreased Lamin A to navigate the dense brain parenchyma more efficiently. Our data suggests that this mechanism proceeds in cells that maintain a balance in cytoskeletal dynamics that enable proper cell polarization. Limitations include the use of bulk GBM cells, which do not represent tumor heterogeneity and cannot recapitulate GBM subtypes accurately, and the model of PMT, which does not account for the plasticity of this transition. Although identifying nuclear stiffness as a central mediator of PMT induced confined migration efficiency, targeting this axis is much more challenging due to potential effects on resident cell populations, and will require novel drug targeting approaches and/or unique pharmacological targets identified through deeper mechanistic studies. Thus, future work includes elucidating the mechanism of TGF-β1 dependent regulation of Lamin A expression and organization as well as investigating glioma stem cell populations for potential mechanistic overlap. Recent reports have begun to tease out these pathways with evidence of a Smad3/AKT2 dependent phosphorylation of Lamin A that increases nuclear deformation in both lung and breast cancer cells51,56. Focused interrogation of PMT induced changes to actin cytoskeletal dynamics and actin binding proteins (formins, Arp2/3) is warranted, especially in the context of confined migration65. As deeper mechanistic insights into the relationship between PMT and biophysical changes are gained, new pharmacological targets may be identified to disrupt confined migration and improve prognosis for this deadly disease.

Materials and methods

Continuous cell line culture

U251 and U87 human GBM cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Corning, 10–017-CV) with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning, 35-010-CV), 1% (vol/vol) sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360-070), 1% (vol/vol) non-essential amino acids (Gibco, 11140-050), and 1% (vol/vol) penicillin plus streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were tested for Mycoplasma (InvivoGen rep-mys-10) every 3 months with media changes every 3 to 4 days. All experiments were performed within 5 passages from the frozen stock. Additionally, cells were starved of serum before experiments assaying phosphorylated proteins in a solution comprised of DMEM with 0.5% (vol/vol) FBS, 1% (vol/vol) sodium pyruvate, 1% (vol/vol) non-essential amino acids, and 1% (vol/vol) penicillin plus streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) was added to the culture medium at a concentration of 10 ng/mL for 30 min, 8 h, or 72 h, depending on the experiment45. Nuclear stiffening agent RO-3306 was added to the culture medium at a concentration of 20 µM for 4 h preceding experiment initiation. TGF-β1 was purchased from R&D SYSTEMS (240-B-002/CF). RO-3306 was purchased from TOCRIS (4181/10).

BCA & western blots

Following treatments, cells were lysed for western blots using a solution of Pierce™ RIPA Buffer (Thermo Fischer Scientific 89900) with a 1:100 dilution of Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 1861281). Lysates were collected and frozen at −80 °C for 24 h before centrifugation at 16 G’s for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was collected and the pellet discarded. 10 µL of protein supernatant from each lysate was placed in a 96 well with albumin protein standards (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 23209). Pierce™ BCA Protein Assasy Reagent A (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 23228) and Reagent B (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 859078) were mixed into a 50:1 solution. 200 µL of solution was added to each well containing protein, and the 96 well was covered and placed into an incubator at 37 °C for 15 min. Afterward, the 96 well was placed in a BioTek Epoch Microplate Spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies,1612138). Protein concentrations were normalized to the lowest concentration using a standard curve generated from albumin standards. NuPAGE® LDS Sample Buffer (Invitrogen, NP0007) and NuPAGE® Sample Reducing Agent (Invitrogen, NP0009) were added to protein solutions at a 1:6.5 and 1:16.25 dilution respectively. Lysates were then stored at −80 °C until needed.

For F-actin/G-actin ratios, samples were processed as previously described66. Cells were collected and pelleted and resuspended in 250 µL of extraction buffer (0.1% triton X-100 in PBS and complete protease inhibitor) and incubated for 5 min with slight agitation. The samples were then centrifuged at 15,000 g at 4 °C for 5 min. The soluble portions (supernatant) containing globular actin was collected, and the triton-insoluble (pellet) containing predominantly F-actin was resuspended in 250 µL of RIPA buffer and vortexed for 30 min. Similar to other lysates, samples were prepared with LDS sample buffer and reducing agent prior to gel electrophoresis.

Lysates were injected into NuPAGE™ 4–12% Bis-Tris Gels (Invitrogen, NP0321BOX/NP0323BOX) alternating treatment groups and having Precision Plus Protein Kaleidoscope (BIO-RAD, 1610375) in one well. Electrophoresis was used to separate proteins by implementing 200 V and 450 mA for 30 min from a PowerEase Touch 350 W (Invitrogen, PS0350). Gels were then exposed to 15 V and 450 mA for 2 h to induce protein transfer onto the Odyssey® Nitrocellulose Membrane (LI-COR Biosciences, 926–31092). After transfer, membranes were rinsed three times with TBST and blocked for 1 h with Intercept® Blocking Buffer (LI-COR Biosciences, 927–60001). Primary antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology and added to blocking buffer in a 1:1000 dilution overnight at 4 °C for P-Smad2 (3108 S), Smad2 (3103 S), P-Stat3 (9145 S), Stat3 (9139 S), ZEB1 (70512 S), P-Lamin A/C (13448 S), Lamin A/C (4777 S), Pan-Actin (8456 S), and GAPDH (97166 S). Secondary antibodies were purchased from LI-COR Biosciences and added to blocking buffer in a 1:5000 dilution for 1 h at 22 °C of rabbit targeting (926–68071) and mouse targeting (926–32210) secondary antibodies. Membranes were rinsed three times with TBST following secondary antibody incubation and scanned using a LI-Cor Odyssey CLx western blot imager. Using Image Studio (Li-Cor) initial low-resolution scans are conducted to identify bands, and ROIs are then drawn around the proteins of interest for higher resolution scans. Because of this, full length blots were not scanned in this report. Original blots are included in the Supplementary Figs. 1–10; 13 and 15. Scans were input into ImageJ and protein band intensity normalized to the nontreated group average.

Immunostaining and fluorescence microscopy

Standard protocols for immunostaining were conducted. Samples were fixed in 3.7% paraformaldehyde, washed with PBS, and then blocked for one hour. After blocking, samples were incubated with primary antibodies for lamin A at 1:200 dilution for 3 h at room temperature. After rinsing with PBS, secondary antibodies at a 1:1000 dilution were added along with phalloidin stain for actin at 1:200 for one hour. Samples were then mounted with anti-fade mounting media overnight before imaging using a Nikon TE2000 Epifluorescence Microscope (Nikon Instruments, Melville, NY). Images were analyzed from MAX project Z-stacks and fluorescence intensity was calculated as the corrected total fluorescence: CTF = Integrated Density—Total Fluorescent Area/Background Fluorescence via ImageJ. For analysis of nuclear staining intensity, nuclear ROIs were identified via thresholding strategies and total fluorescent area was obtained through the measure function in ImageJ. For quantification of images, uniform exposure was maintained across experimental groups.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) force mapping

A Bruker NanoWizard 4XP Bio-AFM was used to collect all measurements. Cells were seeded on TCP glass bottom plates. MLCT-BIO cantilevers (Bruker) were calibrated in air and liquid to acquire a spring constant and deflection sensitivity before testing. For data acquisition, 2 μm x 2 μm square area were imposed on the cytoskeletal and nuclear regions of the cells and divided into 12 × 12 force maps, which creates a 144-force curve grid equally distributed over the 2 μm x 2 μm scan area. Brightfield images during scanning were used to identify nuclear and cytoskeletal mapping regions (Supplementary Fig. 18). Force curves were collected by utilizing an indentation depth of 1.0 μm at a ramp rate of 1hz. Cell nuclei were located using light microscopy and cytoskeletal regions were defined as regions being ~ 1 nuclear diameter removed from the nucleus center. For all cells, a minimum of 3 2 μm x 2 μm scans were conducted on each region (nuclear and cytoskeletal) and a minimum of 5 cells were analyzed across all groups. Bruker JPK Data Processing SPM software was used to fit the curves according to the Hertz/Sneddon model and triangular pyramid tip geometry with an angle of 35° and a Poisson ratio of 0.5. Curves with irregular, sinusoidal outputs indicative of failed contact maintenance were removed before statistical analysis, and outliers were identified via the ROUT method, which detects outliers through nonlinear regression fitting. Normality and Lognormality test determined that our data was not normally distributed and thus Mann Whitney U tests were utilized to determine statistical significance, as previously described67.

PDMS microchannel device preparation

Silicon masters (University Wafers) were spin-coated with SU-8 2010 (KAYAKU) and given a preexposure soft bake at 115 °C. Wafers were then photopatterned with the microchannel designs (a x 2000 μm x 8 μm) where a represents the variable channel width and given a postexposure bake at 115 °C. The wafers were developed using Baker BTS-220 then given a hard bake at 115 °C. Sylgard 184 base (DOW Silicones Corporation, 000000840559) and curing agent (DOW Silicones Corporation, 000000840559) were mixed in a 10:1 ratio, degassed, and then poured onto the wafer to a thickness of approximately 5 mm. The PDMS devices were placed at 60 °C for 2 h to cure. After curing, the devices were cut out with a razor blade and plasma treated for 30 s. Devices were then inverted onto a PEG coated coverslip for 15 min to allow for passivation on the channel tops. After passivation, devices were removed and placed in fibronectin to functionalize in the incubator for 3 h at 37 °C. Finally, the PDMS microchannels were adhered to tissue culture polystyrene dishes channel side up with sterile vacuum grease to allow for cell seeding and time-lapse microscopy. PDMS device parameters and cell morphology under confinement are presented in Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17.

2D and microchannel motility assays

Time lapse microscopy was conducted with a Nikon Eclipse TE2000-U microscope by capturing images every 15 min for at least 16 h. ImageJ manual tracking was used to follow cell motility. Cells that were dividing, in contact with other cells, or in the process of apoptosis were not included in the analysis. Additionally, microchannel motility analysis was constrained to cells that migrated within the PDMS walls throughout the selected timeframes. Cell motility was characterized via mean cell speed (µm/hour) and cell persistence (distance/displacement)68.

Statistics

The data are reported as the means of all replicates, and error bars are SEM. GraphPad Prism 10 software was used to create figures, and statistical significance between sample groups was determined by Welch’s unpaired t-test, one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, and Tukey’s HSD tests. Data outliers were removed according to the ROUT method with a coefficient (Q) equal to 1%. Details of comparisons and replicates are provided in the appropriate figure legend.
