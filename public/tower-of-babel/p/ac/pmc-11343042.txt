Neurotox Screen? Zebrafish Study Points to PFOS Early-Life Exposure Effects
Authors: Nate Seltenrich
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A translucent grid of squares, each one holding a thin black larval fish.

Over the last couple of decades, zebrafish have gained attention as a valuable model organism and research tool across the biomedical sciences.1,2 A newly published study in Environmental Health Perspectives underscores the potential for zebrafish to serve as a screening tool for pollutants and neurodevelopmental outcomes in humans. In this case, evaluating the effects of exposure to two chemicals among thousands of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) also helped illuminate the molecular mechanisms through which such outcomes can occur.3

The study was performed by researchers in Germany, Sweden, Canada, and the United States. Together, they sought to better understand previously observed behavior suggestive of neurotoxicity in zebrafish following developmental (days 1 through 4 postfertilization) and acute (5 days postfertilization) exposure to either of two related chemicals within the broader PFAS class: perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) or perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS).4–6

The group was co-led by first author Sebastian Gutsfeld, a PhD candidate at the Helmholtz Centre for Environmental Research (UFZ) in Leipzig, Germany, and Tamara Tal, group leader in mechanistic toxicology at UFZ and a professor of integrated systems toxicology at Leipzig University. Tal notes that today, only one method is approved internationally and endorsed by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development to assess whether a chemical is developmentally neurotoxic.7 “It requires a rodent study, in which you expose the pregnant dam to chemicals and you assess effects in offspring,” Tal says. “To carry out one of these studies takes more than a year and more than a million dollars.”

Researchers studied neurotoxicity of developmental exposures to PFOS and PFHxS in zebrafish larvae, shown here. Their work suggested nuclear receptors in the PPAR family as mechanisms mediating the observed visual startle response. Image: Courtesy of the Mechanistic Toxicology Group/UFZ.

That is why the zebrafish behavioral model holds so much promise, she says. “It’s incredibly inexpensive. It's a single assay that can be done in 5 days. And it basically accounts for nearly every necessary developmental process in a single test.”

Multiple studies in the last 5 years have revealed that zebrafish exposed to certain PFAS exhibit what is referred to as dark-phase hyperactivity, or increased movement during the dark phase of a controlled light/dark cycle in a laboratory.4,5 Tal led one of these studies6 when she was a principal investigator within the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Integrated Systems Toxicology Division. Because zebrafish are naturally more active during the day, hyperactivity in the dark may reflect a heightened anxiety state and is considered an indicator of altered neurobehavioral function.8

In the new study, the researchers confirmed that dark-phase hyperactivity occurred after exposure to each of the two chemicals. But they also introduced and measured a second, more novel outcome called visual startle response hyperactivity, defined as rapid, dart-like movements by the fish within the first three seconds of a sudden switch from either light to dark or dark to light. Here, they found that developmental exposure to PFOS triggered persistent visual startle response hyperactivity that was irreversible up to 8 days postfertilization. This behavioral change is also believed to predict neurotoxicity in fish, Tal says.

Zebrafish and human brains share basic structures, functional regions for sensory processing and motor control, and many receptors and neurotransmitters,1 so it is possible that the chemicals exert similar neurological effects on both species. Therefore, the researchers sought to identify molecular mechanisms mediating the observed behavioral effects. After extracting zebrafish DNA, they performed a comprehensive gene–activity analysis that identified a small group of nuclear receptors known as peroxisome proliferator–activated receptors (PPARs) as likely candidates.

The researchers used clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) gene-editing techniques and administered a PPAR antagonist (which blocks the effect of other signaling mediators, or ligands, at the receptors). They confirmed that pparda and ppardb, two of several PPAR genes that together are referred to as ppard, were associated with visual startle response hyperactivity following larval exposure to PFOS. This finding reinforces potential links with human biology and chemical response, given that ppard is widely expressed in the brains and other tissues of both zebrafish and humans (where it is known as PPAR∂).

Lisa Truong, an Oregon State University associate professor of environmental and molecular toxicology who was not involved in the study, says that this approach provides a robust foundation for future chemical screening and bolsters the zebrafish behavior model as a valuable tool to reveal potential developmentally neurotoxic effects quickly and efficiently—in days rather than the many months required for rodent studies.

That’s “a big deal,” given the nearly 15,000 distinct PFAS9 in existence today, she says. “The strength of this paper is really showing that the zebrafish [behavioral] model itself is going to be able to help with the daunting task of understanding how these compounds are impacting human health.”

Pinpointing a molecular mechanism is another important contribution, says Jennifer Freeman, a professor at Purdue University who also researches health effects of PFAS using zebrafish.10 But even more significantly, the researchers’ innovation of measuring visual startle response alongside dark-phase hyperactivity expands the field’s methods, says Freeman, who was not involved in the study. “The novel approach that they added was looking at that transition phase [from light to dark or dark to light],” she says. “It’s a newer outcome that hasn’t really been included in assays yet for this [zebrafish behavioral model].”
