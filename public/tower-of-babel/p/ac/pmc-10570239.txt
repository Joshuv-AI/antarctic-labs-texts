Introducing Molecular Chaperones into the Causality and Prospective Management of Autoimmune Hepatitis
Authors: Albert J. Czaja
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Molecular chaperones influence the immunogenicity of peptides and the activation of effector T cells, and their pathogenic roles in autoimmune hepatitis are unclear. Heat shock proteins are pivotal in the processing and presentation of peptides that activate CD8+ T cells. They can also induce regulatory B and T cells and promote immune tolerance. Tapasin and the transporter associated with antigen processing-binding protein influence the editing and loading of high-affinity peptides for presentation by class I molecules of the major histocompatibility complex. Their over-expression could enhance the autoimmune response, and their deficiency could weaken it. The lysosome-associated membrane protein-2a isoform in conjunction with heat shock cognate 70 supports the importation of cytosolic proteins into lysosomes. Chaperone-mediated autophagy can then process the peptides for activation of CD4+ T cells. Over-expression of autophagy in T cells may also eliminate negative regulators of their activity. The human leukocyte antigen B-associated transcript three facilitates the expression of class II peptide receptors, inhibits T cell apoptosis, prevents T cell exhaustion, and sustains the immune response. Immunization with heat shock proteins has induced immune tolerance in experimental models and humans with autoimmune disease by inducing regulatory T cells. Therapeutic manipulation of other molecular chaperones may promote T cell exhaustion and induce tolerogenic dendritic cells. In conclusion, molecular chaperones constitute an under-evaluated family of ancillary proteins that could affect the occurrence, severity, and outcome of autoimmune hepatitis. Clarification of their contributions to the immune mechanisms and clinical activity of autoimmune hepatitis could have therapeutic implications.

Introduction

Autoimmune hepatitis is mainly a consequence of antigen-activated CD4+ T helper cells that generate poorly modulated T helper 1 (Th1) and T helper 2 (Th2) immune responses [1–5]. Liver damage can also be caused by antigen-activated CD8+ T cells that differentiate into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [6, 7]. The CTLs can infiltrate the liver [8], resist suppression by regulatory T cells (Tregs) [9], and destroy hepatocytes [10, 11]. Undiscovered immunogenic peptides from the environment are possible triggers of both pathological processes [12–15].

The immunogenicity of a peptide requires its presentation by class I [16, 17] or class II [12] molecules of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) and activation of antigen-specific effector T cells (CD4+ helper T cells or CD8+ T cells) [18]. Pathogenicity requires differentiation of the activated CD4+ T cells into Th1 and Th2 lymphocytes [19–22] and differentiation of the activated CD8+ T cells into CTLs [7, 10, 23]. Genetic factors influence the predisposition for autoimmune hepatitis [24–26], but they have not been disease-specific or required [24–27].

Molecular chaperones may be key determinants of peptide immunogenicity, and they may in turn affect the pathogenic mechanisms of autoimmune diseases [28–30]. Molecular chaperones can facilitate the processing of environmental antigens that could trigger autoimmune hepatitis [14, 15, 31, 32], and they can become targets themselves of the immune response [31, 33].

Molecular chaperones select, process, edit, and translocate immunogenic peptides in the cell [28–30, 34–36]. They reside in all cellular compartments [37–39], and they assist in the proper folding of native and non-native proteins [40, 41]. Molecular chaperones prevent protein aggregation and interaction [30], direct protein deposition (maturation or degradation) [35, 42, 43], and facilitate the assembly and stabilization of polypeptides [28]. They promote the actions of key proteins without performing the action themselves [28].

Molecular chaperones can also affect antigen presentation by class I MHC [44–52] and class II MHC [53–57] molecules. They can influence dendritic cell function [58], facilitate antigen cross-presentation [59–65], and promote an autoreactive T cell response [66, 67]. Certain molecular chaperones can modulate the immune response by favoring the development of Tregs [68, 69] or down-regulating the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines via the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway [70, 71]. Chaperonopathies of a genetic or acquired nature have been associated with diverse degenerative human diseases [36, 72–75], and the impact of chaperone dysfunction or overload on the occurrence and outcome of autoimmune diseases is unclear. Clarification of the role of molecular chaperones in the occurrence and outcome of autoimmune hepatitis could have therapeutic implications [37, 72, 74–77].

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are a pivotal family of molecular chaperones involved in the immune response [29, 31, 32, 34, 38, 39, 78]. Other key participants are tapasin [79–81], the transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP)-binding protein (TAPBPR) [44–52], the lysosome-associated membrane protein-2a isoform (LAMP-2a) [53–56], and the human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-B-associated transcript 3 (Bat3) [66, 67]. These molecules can facilitate immune reactivity to environmental stimuli and contribute to the causality of autoimmune hepatitis via an epigenetic pathway [15, 82, 83] or molecular mimicry [14]. They can also maintain or enhance the activated T cell response by degrading negative regulatory factors within T cells [84] or preventing T cell exhaustion [66].

The goals of this article are to describe the properties of key chaperone molecules that help generate and modulate the immune responses, indicate how these molecules could transform environmental factors into triggers of autoimmune hepatitis, and encourage their investigation as pathogenic factors, counter-regulatory immunosuppressive agents, and therapeutic prospects. Molecular chaperones could expand concepts of causality and management of autoimmune hepatitis.

Methods

English abstracts were identified in PubMed using the search phrases, “molecular chaperones,” “molecular chaperones in autoimmune diseases,” and “molecular chaperones in autoimmune hepatitis.” Pertinent abstracts were selected, and key aspects were noted. Full-length articles that expanded principal concepts were reviewed, and they constituted the primary bibliography. Key references cited in the selected full-length articles were expanded into full-length articles, and they constituted a secondary bibliography. A tertiary bibliography of full-length articles was developed from references cited in the secondary bibliography. Several hundred abstracts and 121 full-length articles were examined.

Heat Shock Proteins and the Processing of Environmental Antigens

HSPs are constitutive and inducible proteins that are distributed in the cytosol, nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, and mitochondria of human cells [29, 37, 38]. Cell distress associated with heat shock, hypoxia, infection, and inflammation can trigger the disassociation of heat shock factor-1 (HSF-1) from heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) [37, 38, 85–89]. HSF-1 can then translocate from the cytoplasm to the nucleus where it can activate genes that encode the HSPs [90, 91]. As concentrations of HSP increase, HSP70 can terminate gene activity by a feedback mechanism that dissociates HSF-1 from the nuclear deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) [87–89]. HSP90 can also sequester HSF-1 and prevent its effect on DNA transcription [92, 93].

HSP Classifications and Functions

HSPs are classified by molecular weight [94], and they may be variously dependent on adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [38]. Small HSPs (molecular weight, 15–30 kilodaltons) are ATP-independent, and they are located in the cytosol, nucleus, and mitochondria [38, 95–97]. Small HSPs prevent protein aggregation, and they sequester denatured and misfolded proteins [38, 98, 99] (Table 1). They can also inhibit the apoptosis of inflammatory and immune cells by interfering with caspase activation and the mitochondrial pathway of programmed cell death [95, 100–103]. HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 (molecular weight, 40–90 kilodaltons) are protein families [94] that differ from the small HSPs by their dependence on ATP [38] (Table 1). These HSPs maintain the integrity and function of intracellular nascent proteins by ensuring their correct folding, conformation, preservation, and transport [29, 32, 90, 104]. They aid in cell recovery after injury [29], and they chaperone the degradation of stress-damaged proteins by proteases [102].

HSP40, HSP70, and HSP90 have also been implicated in the processing and transport of immunogenic peptides to MHC class I molecules [32, 34, 59, 105, 106] (Table 1). They are instrumental in the differentiation of CD8+ CTLs via the cross-presentation of foreign antigens [34, 59, 62, 63], and they can prolong the survival of inflammatory and immune cells by inhibiting their apoptosis [95, 101, 102]. HSPs can also counter-regulate the immune response by inducing the proliferation of interleukin (IL)-10 secreting Tregs [22, 107–110]. Immunization with HSP60 has increased the number of Tregs and induced immune tolerance in experimental models of autoimmune arthritis [38, 68, 111–113].

Large HSPs (molecular weight, > 100 kilodaltons) belong to the HSP70 superfamily [114] (Table 1). They are characterized by a large loop component that enables them to bind extracellular polypeptides and molecules [114]. This attribute may facilitate an immune response to tissue injury by binding large proteins and peptides [114, 115]. It may also enhance their ability to process environmental antigens. Large HSPs promote antigen cross-presentation by dendritic cells [116, 117], and they prime CD8+ T lymphocytes by loading MHC class I molecules with processed extracellular antigen [117]. The large HSPs may also participate as co-chaperones of HSP70 to correct misfolded polypeptides, untangle protein aggregates, and promote refolding of native proteins [38, 114, 118, 119].

HSPs and the Immune Response

HSPs optimize the machinery of the immune response [32, 38, 59, 105], and their over- or under-expression can promote or impair immune reactivity [31, 32]. Multiple HSPs can share similar actions or have opposing actions that modulate the net effect (Table 1). Some HSPs can influence the survival of activated T cells by having a pro-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic effect [95, 100–102], whereas others can suppress the immune response by inducing the proliferation of Tregs and promoting immune tolerance [22, 31, 120–122]. Certain HSPs may also down-regulate the transcriptional activity of NF-κB and alter cytokine production and the inflammatory response [70, 71]. The salient HSPs involved in the immune response span all molecular weights, and they can participate in a network of co-chaperones and regulatory molecules that have a composite effect [38, 99, 120].

HSPs are also highly immunogenic [122]. Anti-HSP antibodies have been detected in normal individuals [123, 124], and they have been present more frequently in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [125], celiac disease [126], and autoimmune hepatitis [127, 128] than in normal individuals. The pathological consequences and the clinical implications of anti-HSPs remain uncertain, but a molecular mimicry between human and bacterial HSPs may generate cross-reacting antibodies [129]. Self-produced or administered antibodies to select HSPs have attenuated the immune response in animal models of RA by blocking pivotal HSP epitopes and inducing IL-10 production [130–132].

The cross-presentation of environmental antigens is an action that is chaperoned by HSPs [59, 116], and it is a pivotal under-evaluated mechanism by which environmental antigens could trigger autoimmune hepatitis [14]. Environmental stimuli could also promote autoimmune hepatitis by inducing epigenetic changes [15, 83] that alter the constellation of HSP chaperones involved in the maintenance of immune tolerance [15, 133, 134]. Autoimmune hepatitis could be a consequence of acquired perturbations in HSP production and function.

Cross-Presentation of Environmental Antigens

A pivotal action of several immunomodulatory HSPs is to facilitate the cross-presentation of exogenous (foreign) antigens [14] (Table 1). Antigen cross-presentation is a process in which dendritic cells can present processed extracellular antigens to CD8+ T cells by MHC class I molecules [60, 116]. The antigen-activated CD8+ T cells can then differentiate into CTLs. Liver-infiltrating CD8+ T cells are abundant in the portal tracts and areas of interface hepatitis in patients with autoimmune hepatitis [8].

Antigen-activated CD8+ T cells increase the production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) [11], promote apoptosis of hepatocytes [1, 135], and resist suppression by Tregs [9]. The intensity of IFN-γ production and cytotoxicity by the activated CD8+ T cells has correlated with clinical manifestations of hepatic damage [11]. Furthermore, the peripheral blood levels of CD8+/CD25+ lymphocytes have been positively associated with the serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in patients with acute-onset autoimmune hepatitis [136]. These findings have indicated that antigen-activated CD8+ T cells are key contributors to the pathogenic immune process in autoimmune hepatitis [2, 5, 10, 137].

Antigen cross-presentation complements the canonical pathway by which processed foreign antigen is loaded into MHC class II molecules [138] (Table 1). Activation of CD4+ helper T cells by this pathway can generate Th1 and Th2 immune responses [19, 22, 138, 139]. Antigen cross-presentation by MHC class I molecules and CD4+ T cell activation by MHC class II molecules may allow environmental antigens that mimic critical epitopes of autoimmune hepatitis to overcome immune tolerance and cause disease [14]. HSPs can promote antigen cross-presentation by enhancing dendritic cell maturation and function [34, 140–144], chaperoning the transformation of selected antigen into a substrate for presentation by MHC class I molecules, and supporting the activation of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells [14, 32].

Environmentally Induced Epigenetic Effects on HSP Production and the Immune Response

Epigenetic changes in the genes encoding the HSPs can alter their transcription and affect cellular responses to environmental antigens [39, 133, 134, 145] (Table 1). HSP60 is encoded by a single-gene, but it participates in multiple cellular functions affecting inflammation, immune response, cell replication, and carcinogenesis [146]. This diversity of potentially counter-regulatory actions may relate to post-translational modifications of its gene by environmental factors [15, 83, 146].

Many viruses depend on HSP90 for the nuclear translocation of polymerases essential for their replication [147]. Histone deacetylation of the gene encoding HSP90 increases its transcription and promotes viral replication and infectivity [39]. Other HSPs may facilitate the immune response generated by viral antigens that resemble self-antigens (molecular mimicry) [14, 31, 32, 148], or the HSPs themselves may share amino acid sequences with the virus and promote immune reactivity [33, 39] or immune tolerance [69, 76, 110]. These changes could in turn enhance the risk of autoimmune hepatitis.

Multiple environmental factors, including diet [149, 150], alcohol [151, 152], tobacco [153, 154], heavy metals [155], and ionizing radiation [156, 157], can induce epigenetic changes that alter determinants of the immune response [15, 158]. The nature and impact of these environmental stimuli on epigenetic modifications that influence HSP production, function, processing of environmental antigens, and risk of autoimmune hepatitis are unclear [14, 159, 160].

Tapasin/TAPBPR and the Presentation of MHC Class I Peptides

Tapasin Properties and Actions

Tapasin is a component of the peptide-loading complex (PLC) in the endoplasmic reticulum, and it contributes to the loading and editing of peptides that are presented by MHC class I molecules [6, 79, 80] (Table 2). The MHC class I molecules present peptides derived mainly from the endogenous proteome, but the peptides can also be derived from exogenous sources and activate CD8+ T cells by antigen cross-presentation [6]. The PLC is a membrane complex that consists of the transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP), the MHC class I molecule, the endoplasmic reticulum resident protein 57 (ERp57), calreticulin, and tapasin [161, 162]. Tapasin is a co-chaperone with ERp57 [164–167] and calreticulin [168, 169] in the PLC (Table 2). The three co-chaperones constitute an interactive network that edits the peptides for MHC class I presentation [170, 171]. Tapasin ensures the loading of high-affinity peptides by systematically displacing bound peptides of low affinity from the MHC class I molecule [171–174]. The disulfide-linked heterodimer of tapasin and ERp57 recruits MHC class I molecules and maximizes the selection of high-affinity peptides more effectively than tapasin alone [162]. Calreticulin enhances weak tapasin/MHC class I interactions and increases the affinity requirement for peptides to load [175]. Tapasin deficiency in experimental models has greatly reduced the cell surface presentation of peptides by MHC class I molecules [80, 176, 177].

Tapasin edits peptides for presentation by all the MHC class I molecules, but it preferentially affects peptides presented by the HLA-B molecule [174, 178, 179] (Table 2). The MHC class I molecules consist of a highly polymorphic heavy chain and an invariant light chain (β2-microglobulin) [178], and they are designated as human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C molecules. The genes for the MHC class I molecules encode the heavy chain, and polymorphisms of these genes affect peptide binding to the MHC class I molecules. Some allelic variations of the HLA-B molecule may bind peptides independently of tapasin [179], but the peptides are typically suboptimal for presentation [174].

The PLC controls the responses to immunogenic peptides in a hierarchical fashion based on peptide affinity for the MHC class I molecules [161]. Tapasin and the other co-chaperones in the PLC can influence these affinity requirements by their interactions [162, 175]. Tapasin stabilizes the peptide-free MHC class I molecules, enhances their peptide-binding capacity, and favors the selection of high-affinity peptides [170, 178]. Its degree of success relates to its interactions with ERp57 and calreticulin [162, 175]. The over- or under-expression of tapasin could affect the selection and presentation of environmental antigens by MHC class I molecules and the propensity for autoimmune hepatitis.

TAPBPR Properties and Actions

TAPBPR is an intracellular chaperone that is tapasin-related (22% homology) [45, 180, 181]. Similar to tapasin, TAPBPR resides in the endoplasmic reticulum [44], is induced by IFN-γ [45], and edits peptides that bind to MHC class I molecules [45, 49] (Table 2). Unlike tapasin, TAPBPR exists outside the PLC [44–48], does not interact with ERp57 or calreticulin [45], preferentially edits peptides bound to HLA-A [49], moves with the MHC class I complex through the Golgi apparatus [45], and is not required for peptide loading [44]. The binding of TAPBPR and tapasin to MHC class I molecules is mutually exclusive [44].

TAPBPR mainly dissociates suboptimal peptides from MHC class I molecules after their selection and quality control by the PLC [45–47, 49] (Table 2). This downstream effect can refine the peptides presented by the MHC class I molecules on the cell surface [182]. TAPBPR can also facilitate peptide binding in conditions of reduced peptide concentration by selecting peptides with less affinity [51]. TAPBPR depletion does not affect surface expression of MHC class I molecules or prevent peptide loading [45], but it does increase peptide diversity [46]. TAPBPR deficiency or malfunction may decrease the strength and specificity of the immune response.

Tapasin/TAPBPR Interactions and the Quality of the Immune Response

Tapasin and TAPBPR act independently of each other, and they continue to function in the other’s absence [44]. Whereas the interaction of TAPBPR with MHC class I molecules increases in the absence of tapasin, the interaction of tapasin with MHC class I molecules is unaffected by the absence of TAPBPR [44] (Table 2). A deficiency or dysfunction of either tapasin or TAPBPR cannot be compensated by the other, and the overall quality of antigen presentation diminishes [44–46]. The cell surface presentation of peptides by MHC class I molecules decreases after the loss of tapasin [176, 177], and peptide-depleted MHC class I molecules can rebind released peptides or bind low-affinity peptides after the loss of TAPBPR [46, 50, 51]. The reduction in antigen presentation by tapasin deficiency and the decrease in peptide selectivity by TAPBPR deficiency may impair the processing of exogenous peptides and decrease the precision and vigor of the immune response.

Tapasin/TAPBPR and Autoimmunity

Tapasin and TAPBPR could contribute to the occurrence of an autoimmune disease by processing environmental antigens that mimic critical self-antigens [14, 148] (Table 2). The immunogenic pathway of molecular mimicry via MHC class I molecules has not been recognized as a primary basis for pathogenicity in autoimmune hepatitis [14]. Nevertheless, it is a mechanism by which environmental antigens could activate liver-infiltrating CTLs via antigen cross-presentation [14, 61, 65]. In contrast, deficiencies in tapasin and TAPBPR function could promote immune tolerance by impairing the expression and function of MHC class I molecules and limiting the differentiation of CD8+ T cells [46, 50, 51, 176, 177]. Variations in the actions of tapasin and TAPBPR may modulate immune tolerance and have therapeutic implications in autoimmune hepatitis.

LAMP-2a and the Presentation of MHC Class II Peptides

LAMP-2a Properties and Actions

LAMP-2a is one of three protein isoforms of LAMP-2 that are distributed between intracellular compartments and the cell surface [54]. LAMP-2a is a heavily glycosylated transmembrane protein in endosomes and lysosomes [54, 183], and it is a highly selective receptor that imports cytosolic proteins into lysosomes [53, 55, 184, 185] (Table 3). The proteins can then be processed by chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) into substrates that are degraded, recycled, or delivered to MHC class II molecules for presentation to naïve CD4+ T cells [53, 55, 56, 184–187]. CMA is one of three types of autophagy (macroautophagy, microautophagy, and CMA) [187–189], and the proteins chosen for CMA express a pentapeptide motif that binds in the cytoplasm with the heat shock cognate 70 protein (HSC70) [184, 190, 191] (Table 3). The amino acid motif, lysine-phenylalanine-glutamic acid-arginine-glutamine (KFERQ), characterizes the proteins destined for CMA after binding with HSC70 [192, 193]. HSC70 and HPS70 have 85% structural homology [194], but HSC70 is a constitutive cellular protein that is preferred over the inducible HSP70 in CMA [184, 195, 196].

Multiple other molecules can co-chaperone the transport of cytosolic proteins into lysosomes, including HSP90, HSP40, HSP70-interacting protein (HIP), and the Bc12-associated athanogene 1 protein (BAG-1) [184] (Table 3). The protein-chaperone complex interacts directly with LAMP-2a for import into the lysosome [185, 186, 189]. LAMP-2a acts independently of TAP which is the transporter of endogenous antigens processed in the cytosol and delivered to MHC class I molecules [197, 198]. The LAMP-2c isoform is an endogenous negative regulator of CMA, and it can selectively alter the presentation of cytoplasmic antigens [199].

Danon disease is an inherited deficiency of LAMP-2 [200, 201], and the presentation of exogenous antigens by MHC class II molecules has been impaired in B cells obtained from a patient with Danon disease [202]. Danon disease is characterized by the accumulation of intracytoplasmic vacuoles containing diverse substrates and glycogen in skeletal and cardiac myocytes [200, 201].

LAMP-2a and the Loss of Self-Tolerance

LAMP-2a can facilitate the selection and processing of exogenous antigens for presentation by MHC class II molecules by supporting CMA [56] (Table 3). It may also affect the activity of CD4+ T cells by modulating CMA within T cells [84, 203–205]. Over-expression of LAMP-2a and increased activity of CMA could enhance the processing of environmental antigens, promote T cell reactivity, and increase the risk of autoimmune hepatitis. Counter-regulatory factors, such as LAMP-2c, might modulate this risk [199].

LAMP-2 Levels in Autoimmune Liver Disease

The intrahepatic expression of LAMP-2 has been increased in primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), and the increased expression has correlated with disease severity [203]. Furthermore, treatment with ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) has partially reduced the over-expression of LAMP-2 in the liver [203]. Similar findings have been demonstrated by assessing serum levels of LAMP-2 in patients with PBC [204] and LAMP-2a levels in circulating CD4+ T cells [205].

Basal serum LAMP-2 levels have been increased in patients with PBC, especially in those patients with histological stages III-IV or total serum bilirubin levels greater than 1 mg/dL [204]. Serum LAMP-2 levels during the first 3 months of UDCA therapy have also predicted a treatment response sooner than other indices [204]. Similarly, the CD4+ T cells from patients with PBC have had higher levels of LAMP-2a than CD4+ T cells from healthy individuals and patients with chronic hepatitis B [205]. The CD4+ T cells from patients with PBC have also exhibited greater responses to stimulation in vitro than the CD4+ T cells of healthy individuals [205]. LAMP-2a expression in the CD4+ T cells has been associated with disease activity and the response to UDCA, and the over-reactions of the CD4+ T cells have been prevented in vitro by inhibiting LAMP-2a activity [205].

LAMP-2a and Enhanced T Cell Function

CMA is activated in T cells after engagement of the T cell antigen receptor (TCR) with peptide presented by the MHC class II molecules [84]. LAMP-2a is induced on the lysosomal membrane, and intracellular proteins that could impair T cell activation are degraded by CMA. The key negative regulators targeted by CMA in T cells are ubiquitin ligase (Itch) and calcineurin inhibitor (Rcan-1) [84] (Table 3). Preservation of the LAMP-2a and CMA activities is pivotal in maintaining T cell-mediated immune responses. These responses have been reduced by experimental deletion of the LAMP-2a gene and by spontaneous age-related reductions in CMA activity [84, 206]. The loss of negative regulators of T cell activity via over-expression of CMA could impact on the risk and severity of autoimmune hepatitis. Studies that clarify the role of LAMP-2a and CMA in the liver and activated CD4+ T cells of autoimmune hepatitis could suggest targeted therapies.

BAT3 and Preservation of the Immune Response

Bat3 Properties and Actions

Bat3 (also called scythe and BAG6) is a cytoplasmic protein that chaperones multiple protein interactions. Bat3 inhibits cellular apoptosis in osteosarcoma cells [207] and regulates the apoptosis inducing factor (AIF) in other cell lines [208, 209]. Bat3 helps preserve chromosomal function and cell mitosis [210], and it increases the activity of IFN-γ-inducible class II transactivator (CIITA) [57] (Table 4). Bat3 also interacts with transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) [211], enhances type 1 collagen expression [211], promotes the intracellular accumulation and degradation of HSP70 [212], and prevents the exhaustion of T cells [66, 67]. Bat3 is essential for the selective, proteasome-mediated, degradation of defective polypeptides, and it may supply antigenic peptides for presentation by MHC class I molecules [42]. Bat3 and HLA Class II Gene Expression

CIITA governs the expression of the genes that encode the HLA class II peptide receptors, and Bat3 chaperones CIITA activity [57] (Table 4). Both Bat3 and CIITA are induced by IFN-γ, and they are translocated from the cytosol to the nucleus of antigen presenting cells (APCs) [57]. Activation of the HLA class II genes encodes the expression of the molecules that present exogenous peptides to CD4+ T cells. The activated CD4+ T cells could in turn generate an immune response that culminates in autoimmune hepatitis [1–5].

Bat3 and T Cell Exhaustion

Bat3 inhibits the function of T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing 3 protein (Tim-3) [66] (Table 4). Tim-3 is expressed on activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, Tregs, dendritic cells, and natural killer (NK) cells [213, 214]. Its natural ligand is galectin-9 [215], and the engagement of Tim-3 and galectin-9 induces cell death [216]. The constitutive expression of Tim-3 on dendritic cells inhibits their activation and T cell priming [58, 217], and the expression of Tim-3 on terminally differentiated T cells signals their exhaustion and the loss of T cell-mediated immune responses [216]. The Tim-3/galectin-9 axis can also promote immune tolerance by expanding the population of granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells [66, 214, 218, 219].

T cell exhaustion is characterized by progressively reduced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, expression of inhibitory receptors on the cell surface, and impaired immune responses [214, 220–223] (Table 4). Progressive immune tolerance may also be accompanied by the production of IL-10. Exhausted CD8+ T cells expressing both Tim-3 and the programmed death 1 receptor (PD-1) have been shown to secrete the anti-inflammatory cytokine, IL-10, in a murine model of chronic viral infection [224]. T cell exhaustion could improve the outcomes of autoimmune disease by limiting T cell activity [221–223]. Bat3 could counter this salutary effect by repressing Tim-3 function [66].

Bat3 binds to the cytoplasmic tail of Tim-3, inhibits Tim-3 ligation with galectin-9, and prevents T cell exhaustion [66] (Table 4). The expression of Bat3 is reduced in exhausted T cells, and the targeted deletion of Bat3 in T cells promotes their exhaustion [66]. The lack of Bat3 expression in dendritic cells promotes immune tolerance by decreasing the activation of Th1 and Th17 lymphocytes and increasing the population of Tregs [58].

Polymorphisms of Bat3 have been associated with an increased odds ratio for type 1 diabetes in patients [225]. In 43 sequential patients with type 1 diabetes, the odds ratio for sharing a polymorphism of Bat3 was 4.8 (P < 0.01), and the odds ratio increased to 6.9 for sharing HLA-B8 and Bat3 (P < 0.001) [225]. Bat3 polymorphisms may have variable functionality and increase the risk of autoimmune disease, especially in genetically susceptible individuals. Bat3 is an attractive therapeutic target for preventing or ameliorating autoimmune processes [221–223].

Molecular Chaperones and the Causality of Autoimmune Hepatitis

Molecular chaperones are under-evaluated, potentially pivotal pathogenic factors in the causality of autoimmune hepatitis. The roles of HSPs, tapasin, TAPBPR, LAMP-2a, and Bat3 must each be determined in autoimmune hepatitis to fully clarify the pathogenic mechanisms of this disease. HSP40, HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 are key modulators of the immune response [32, 34, 226], and they should have investigational priority over the other molecular chaperones.

HSP70 as a Prime Pathogenic Factor for Investigation

HSP70 may be a particularly relevant contributor to autoimmune hepatitis since it can enhance antigen presentation by dendritic cells [105], overcome the immune tolerance of T cells [227], and serve as an self-antigen that generates an autoimmune response [228] (Table 1). HSP70 and HSP70-derived peptides also have immunosuppressive effects, and they could impact on the susceptibility and outcome of autoimmune hepatitis by increasing IL-10 production [229], promoting the activity of Tregs [69], and reducing the severity of immune-mediated disease [69]. Furthermore, HSP70 is secreted by B regulatory cells (Bregs), and it is critical for Breg suppression of effector T cell activity [230]. Serum levels of antibodies to HSP70 have been associated with inflammatory activity in autoimmune hepatitis and PBC, and the reactivity, directed mainly against HSC70, could prove to be a useful biomarker of disease severity or treatment response [127, 231, 232]. The diverse, abundant, and counter-regulatory immune effects of HSP70 suggest that it is a critical pathogenic factor whose over- or under-expression in autoimmune hepatitis should be rigorously evaluated [132].

Other HSPs for Investigation as Pathogenic Factors

HSP40 can increase production of IL-10 and induce Tregs in patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis [32, 233], and it could have an immune modulatory effect in autoimmune hepatitis (Table 1).

HSP60 can induce T cell activation or tolerance in experimental models of arthritis depending on the source of the HSP60 [32, 111] (Table 1). Peptides derived from HSP60 have induced Treg function and suppression of experimental arthritis [234], but they have also increased the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in children with Crohn’s disease [235]. Preliminary assessments of HSP60 in autoimmune hepatitis have demonstrated its association with histological inflammation [236], structural mimicry with liver-related antigens [237], and lack of diagnostic specificity and predominance [238].

HSP90 facilitates the activation of diverse signaling molecules and kinases that can influence the activity of inflammatory (neutrophils, natural killer [NK] cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells) and immune cells (T and B lymphocytes) [78, 239–241] (Table 1). Immunization with high doses of an isoform of HSP90 (grp96) has had immunosuppressive effects in murine models of diabetes and encephalomyelitis [242]. In contrast, pharmacological inhibition of HSP90 activity in human T cells, NK cells, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells has down-regulated the proliferation and function of T lymphocytes, inhibited NF-κB activity, decreased the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and up-regulated the expression of HSP70 [240, 241]. The immune stimulatory and pro-inflammatory effects of HSP90 have made it an attractive therapeutic target for autoimmune disease [239]. The expression and consequences of HSP90 activity warrants evaluation in autoimmune hepatitis.

Molecular Chaperones and the Treatment of Autoimmune Hepatitis

Autoimmune hepatitis has an unmet clinical need for treatments that improve the outcomes of current glucocorticoid-based regimens [243–245]. The molecular chaperones are attractive therapeutic targets because their effects on antigen selection [30] and effector T cell activation [47, 50, 51], survival [95, 102], and function [66] can sustain a deleterious immune response. Identification, isolation, and neutralization of the pivotal promoter of these deleterious effects would have an obvious therapeutic advantage.

Molecular chaperones may also have key counter-regulatory, immunosuppressive [68, 69, 121] and anti-inflammatory effects [70, 71], and therapeutic interventions that enhance these salutary actions could mitigate liver injury [37, 76]. Molecular chaperones commonly exist in networks with counter-balancing opposite effects [31, 99], and the major downside to their therapeutic manipulation is the uncertain effect of network disruption or imbalance on overall immune homeostasis. The molecular chaperones that have been most actively evaluated as therapies for autoimmune disease have been the HSPs [37, 76, 122]. They have been used mainly to migrate a deleterious immune response by strengthening protective immunosuppressive mechanisms.

HSPs and Therapeutic Immunization

Therapeutic administration of select HSPs has improved disease manifestations in experimental models of collagen/adjuvant-induced arthritis [68, 71, 121, 234, 246], type 1 diabetes [247–249], and colitis [131], and immunization with select HSPs has also had promising results in early clinical trials involving patients with RA [37, 250–254] (Table 5). Bacterial and murine full-length HSP60 [68, 111, 122], HSP70 [69, 256, 257], and key conserved epitopes and isoforms of HSP70 [69, 121, 229] have attenuated experimental forms of arthritis and type 1 diabetes [37]. HSP10 [252], HSP40-derived peptide [250, 251], HSP60-derived peptide [254], and HSP70-derived peptide [253] have increased production of IL-10, decreased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, reduced T cell proliferation, and improved activity scores in phase I/II clinical trials involving patients with RA (Table 5). Current immunization strategies in the management of autoimmune disease have not focused on a single HSP that is disease-specific, and the beneficial consequences of the immunization trials have not been HSP-specific. Furthermore, improvements in the clinical outcomes have been inconsistent or absent. The emergence of HSP immunization as an effective management option in autoimmune hepatitis will require the selection of an immunogen that induces the strongest immunosuppressive responses in this disease.

HSPs and Pharmacological Induction

HSP expression can also be enhanced by pharmacological agents [37] (Table 5). The principal mechanism of pharmacological induction is to activate HSF-1 by releasing it from sequestration by HSP90 [260–262] or HSP70 [263], lowering the temperature threshold for HSF-1 activation [264], or enhancing its binding to nuclear DNA by inducing kinases that increase its phosphorylation state [264]. The net effect is to promote gene transcription of unselected HSPs.

Multiple drugs and compounds have induced HSF-1 activity [37], and HSP90 inhibitors, especially geldanamycin and its analogs, have emerged as prominent agents being evaluated in the treatment of RA [265, 266] and cancer [267–269] (Table 5). An effort to repurpose drugs by matching them with relevant pathogenic pathways have also recognized geldanamycin as a therapeutic candidate in PBC [270]. Pharmacological modification of HSP expression in autoimmune hepatitis awaits determination of the pivotal HSP, demonstration that its transcription can be effectively stimulated by the drug, and assurances that dose-limiting toxicities are absent [269, 271].

Monoclonal HSP Antibodies and Immune Tolerance

Humanized monoclonal antibodies to HSP60 (prozumab) bind to HSP60 and down-regulate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-6 and IFN-γ, increase secretion of IL-10, and improve the manifestations of arthritis and colitis in murine models [131] (Table 5). Resistance to adjuvant-induced arthritis has been associated with natural and acquired antibodies to HSP65 that are directed against certain epitopes of HSP65 [130]. Antibodies non-reactive to these epitopes have been associated with susceptibility to arthritis, whereas active vaccination with the protective epitopes or passive administration of antibodies against the epitopes have ameliorated the arthritis [130].

Increased serum levels of naturally occurring antibodies to HSP60, HSP70, and HSP90 in patients with RA have not reflected disease activity or outcome, but they have been associated with the production of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines that may modulate the inflammatory response [125] (Table 5). The advantages of therapy with monoclonal antibodies over HSP immunization are unclear. The induced generation of self-antibodies by HSP immunization rather than delivery of a monoclonal antibody has been preferred in experimental models and clinical trials [76, 122], and it is the strategy best supported for evaluation in models of autoimmune hepatitis.

Prospective Strategies for Inducing Immune Tolerance

T cell exhaustion has been associated with an improved clinical outcome in immune-mediated disease [221–223], and the therapeutic promotion of T cell exhaustion by inhibiting the ligation of Bat3 with Tim-3 is a theoretical consideration [66] (Table 5). Bat3 regulates the functional state of dendritic cells [58]. Dendritic cells deficient in Bat3 have increased substrates (acetyl-coenzyme A, citrate, and cholesterol) that are precursors for steroid synthesis [58]. The generated glucocorticoids can induce a tolerogenic phenotype in dendritic cells and suppress T cells responses by promoting the expression of Tim-3 and exhaustion of T cells [58]. The release of glucocorticoids from the dendritic cells could also promote the formation of Tregs [272].

Tolerogenic dendritic cells are characterized by their ability to induce Tregs from naïve T cells [273–275], and the induction of a Bat3 deficiency would be one mechanism to induce this tolerogenic phenotype [58]. Tolerogenic dendritic cells can also be induced by pharmacological manipulations that are not specifically directed at Bat3 expression [275–277]. Glucocorticoids [278, 279], rapamycin [280–282], and certain cytokines (IL-10, tissue necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], and TGF-β) [283–286] have been key components of diverse pharmacological strategies for inducing tolerogenic dendritic cells in experimental models [275]. Investigations that clarify the role of Bat3 in affecting the severity, relapse frequency, and outcome of autoimmune hepatitis could support investigations into the therapeutic induction of T cell exhaustion and the pharmacological creation of tolerogenic dendritic cells as prospective management strategies for autoimmune hepatitis [214, 223].

Conclusions

Molecular chaperones are instrumental in developing and sustaining the deleterious autoimmune responses associated with the causality of autoimmune hepatitis. They can also mitigate these responses by promoting the production of immunosuppressive Tregs, Bregs, IL-10, and tolerogenic dendritic cells. Investigations that define the roles of HSPs, tapasin, TAPBPR, Lamp-2a, and Bat3 are warranted to fully understand the pathogenic and counter-regulatory mechanisms of autoimmune hepatitis. These insights may direct management strategies that promote immune tolerance by HSP immunization, T cell exhaustion, or altered dendritic cell phenotype.
