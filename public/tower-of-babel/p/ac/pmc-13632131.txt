Performance of the PREVENT heart failure risk score and correlation with microvascular dysfunction and coronary plaque burden in patients with rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Ilana M Usiskin, Katherine Zhong, Ying Qi, Lauren Rusnak, Andrew Cagan, Yumeko Kawano, Gregory C McDermott, Tianrun Cai, Sara M Sweet, Vidul Panickan, Alexandra A Josephson, Tianxi Cai, Damini Dey, Marcelo Di Carli, Daniel H Solomon, Jennifer E Ho, Brittany Weber, Katherine P Liao
Published: 2026

Introduction

Patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) have a 1.5-fold increased risk of heart failure (HF) compared to the general population [1]. The 2023 Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events risk score for HF (PREVENT-HF) was developed to identify at-risk patients in the general population earlier for prevention and management [2]. However, there are limited data on the performance of PREVENT-HF in RA to inform use in clinical care and research.

In RA, systemic inflammation is associated with excess HF risk, potentially due to coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) and accelerated atherosclerosis [3]. Advanced CV imaging methods that measure CMD and atherosclerotic burden provide an opportunity to understand the correlation between thse imaging markers and predicted HF risk.

The objective of this study was to assess the performance of the PREVENT-HF risk score in RA and to gain mechanistic insight into HF risk captured by PREVENT-HF by assessing measures of coronary microvascular dysfunction and coronary artery plaque volume.

Methods

Participants and study design

We utilized an electronic health record (EHR)-based longitudinal RA cohort in a large, multihospital healthcare system. The cohort was defined using a validated algorithm for RA (positive predictive value (PPV) 90%) [4]. The index date was date of RA diagnosis. We studied patients aged 30 to 79 years with EHR encounters ≥1 year prior to and 90 days after the index date. Subjects with prevalent HF, myocardial infarction, or stroke were excluded. Subjects missing the required PREVENT-HF components available between 1 year prior to and 90 days after the index date were excluded.

Incident HF and HF date were defined using a previously published algorithm using a combination of ICD codes and EHR data extracted using natural language processing (PPV 90% for HF diagnosis) [4]. Subjects were censored at incident HF, loss to follow-up, or 10 years after the index date, whichever occurred first.

The PREVENT-HF score was calculated using the published base model at the index date. The components of PREVENT-HF were extracted from the EHR from 1 year prior to 90 days after the index date; diabetes history was determined from EHR entry to 90 days after index date. Antihypertensive medication use was defined using prescription data. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 creatinine equation [2]. RA-related covariates including serostatus and RA-specific medications were extracted ± 90 days of the index date.

Imaging correlates

A subset of RA patients from the same population as the EHR RA cohort were enrolled in a research study, the Lipids, Inflammation and Cardiovascular Risk in RA (LiiRA) study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02714881) and a long-term extension study [5]. LiiRA enrolled RA patients about to initiate a tumor necrosis factor inhibitor (TNFi) as directed by their rheumatologist. Inclusion criteria were individuals age >35, without a history of CVD and who were not on a statin. The PREVENT-HF score was calculated for all LiiRA patients at baseline. All LiiRA subjects underwent a myocardial perfusion PET study to calculate their myocardial flow reserve (MFR) and detect presence of CMD (defined by MFR<2.5) [5]. A subset of LiiRA subjects were enrolled in a long-term extension study and underwent an additional cardiac perfusion PET and coronary CT angiogram (CCTA) to quantify coronary plaque volume >2 years after their baseline LiiRA visit and MFR assessment [6]

Statistical analysis

The clinical characteristics of the RA cohort at baseline were compared for those who eventually developed HF vs no HF using paired t-tests or the Wilcoxon signed rank test. We categorized subjects into 10-year estimated CVD risk categories based on the American Heart Association CVD risk categories: low (<5%), borderline (≥5% to <7.5%), intermediate (≥7.5% to <20%), and high (≥20%).

We assessed discrimination by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). Calibration was assessed by plotting the observed percentage of subjects who had incident HF within 10 years versus the predicted incident HF percentage based on PREVENT-HF scores for each risk category. We additionally assessed calibration with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and calculated the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio for each risk category. The O/E ratios in each category were further stratified by CRP above vs below 3 mg/L (a threshold for elevated CV risk) among individuals with available CRP. We used a Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate hazard ratios (HR) for incident HF for each risk category compared with the low-risk category (reference).

For individuals with imaging results from LiiRA, we calculated the Spearman rank correlation between the baseline PREVENT-HF score and baseline MFR, and between baseline PREVENT-HF score and follow-up MFR. For subjects in the follow-up study, we calculated the Spearman rank correlation between the baseline PREVENT-HF score and follow-up plaque volume.

This study was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

Results

We studied 2064 RA subjects, mean age 55, 75% female, 46% seropositive, with a median 10-year PREVENT-HF score of 2.4%; 125 (6.1%) developed HF within 10 years (Panel A).

Performance characteristics of the PREVENT-HF equation in RA

The AUC of the PREVENT-HF score was 0.78 (95% CI 0.74–0.82). Based on the calibration plot and O/E ratios, the PREVENT-HF score underestimated HF risk among RA patients in the low, borderline and intermediate risk categories and overestimated risk in the high risk category (Panel B). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test suggested that the PREVENT-HF equations are limited in modeling HF risk in the RA cohort (p-value <0.001). When stratified by CRP levels, the underestimation of risk was driven by RA patients with low or intermediate predicted HF risk and CRP ≥3mg/L (O/E: 1.6 low, 1.7 intermediate). The HR for incident HF was 4.1 (95% CI 2.5, 6.7) for borderline risk, 5.3 (95% CI 3.5, 8.0) for intermediate risk, and 8.8 (95% CI 5.1, 15.1) for high risk.

Imaging correlates of PREVENT-HF risk

Among subjects in the LiiRA study, which enrolled 73 RA subjects with mean age 55, 82% female, mean BMI 30, and 68% seropositive, median hsCRP was 4.1 mg/L and mean clinical disease activity index [CDAI] was 24 (moderate-high disease activity). The median PREVENT-HF score was 2.1. As previously published, mean baseline MFR was 2.65 (SD 0.48) [5].

At baseline, higher PREVENT-HF scores were cross-sectionally correlated with lower MFR, indicating a higher degree of CMD (rs −0.31 (95% CI −0.49, −0.10), p = 0.008) (Panel C). In the extension study (>2 years after baseline cardiac PET, n = 18), a higher baseline PREVENT-HF score was correlated with lower (worse) MFR, higher total plaque volume and higher non-calcified plaque volume, but not with total calcified plaque (Panel C).

Discussion

In summary, in this RA cohort, the PREVENT-HF score had reasonable discrimination but may underestimate HF risk among individuals with lower estimated risk and higher CRP levels, highlighting a clinical subgroup where additional attention may be required during clinical evaluation or for future efforts to improve risk stratification. A higher PREVENT-HF score was correlated with a higher degree of CMD cross-sectionally and in follow-up, with a signal for higher future non-calcified plaque burden.

The results from this study corroborate findings from a large, predominantly male cohort of RA patients where PREVENT-HF generally underestimated HF [7]. In our study, the PREVENT-HF score was correlated with a higher degree of CMD, consistent with data that CMD is a feature of HF in the general population [3]. These data lend support to the hypothesis that in RA, higher levels of inflammation, correlated with a higher degree of CMD, are possible mechanisms for excess HF risk.

This study should be viewed within the context of several limitations. We used data from a single healthcare system, and HF diagnoses managed outside of the system may have been missed. However, the proportion of RA subjects who developed HF is similar to other studies [1]. Since we used a retrospective cohort for our main analysis, inflammatory markers were not available for about a third of the cohort, and we did not have standardized measures of disease activity. Similarly, most patients did not have the optional components of the PREVENT-HF score measured; 79% did not have a hemoglobin A1c and 94% a urine albumin:creatinine. Thus, we were unable to evaluate if these components might improve HF risk stratification over the base model. In this cohort without prior known CV risk, N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and cardiac troponin were not available for study, and the role of these markers for improving HF risk stratification is an important area for future study. The PREVENT-HF scores were determined at the time of RA diagnosis and future directions include assessing the performance of HF risk in patients with longer RA duration. The LiiRA study included patients with moderate-high disease activity initiating a TNFi, and the results may not generalize to patients already on biologic therapy or with lower disease activity. Finally, the longitudinal imaging sub-study had a small sample size and the results require replication in a larger cohort.

The PREVENT-HF score is a promising tool for assessing HF risk in RA but should be interpreted with caution among RA patients with low predicted risk, especially in those with elevated CRP. Cardiac imaging biomarkers of microvascular dysfunction and non-calcified plaque burden were correlated with increased HF risk and may improve risk HF risk stratification. Future directions include applying the PREVENT-HF equations to identify individuals with RA at elevated risk for HF, enabling studies of the inflammatory pathways associated with microvascular dysfunction and accelerated atherosclerosis to inform preventative strategies.
