Regional Differences in Characteristics, Service Utilization, and Outcome of Patients Receiving Physician‐Led Home Visit Care in Japan: A Nationwide Study
Authors: Yu Sun, Jun Komiyama, Masao Iwagami, Nobuo Sakata, Satoru Yoshie, Tomoko Ito, Takahiro Sugiyama, Kimikazu Kashiwagi, Nanako Tamiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimPhysician‐led home‐visit care has become essential in the super‐aged society of Japan. To develop region‐specific service systems, understanding regional differences in patient profiles is essential. This study aimed to describe regional differences in patient characteristics, service utilization, and outcomes, and to assess the associations between regional healthcare resources and patient outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective study used linked data from the National Database of Health Insurance Claims and Specific Health Checkups and the Japanese Long‐term Care Database (April 2020–March 2021). Patients aged ≥ 65 years receiving regular physician‐led home visits in October 2020 were included. Analyses were conducted by prefecture and region. Associations between regional healthcare resources and patient outcomes were assessed using Spearman's correlation coefficients (ρ).ResultsAmong 337 863 patients, regional variations were observed. Western Japan had a higher prevalence of cancer, heart disease, respiratory disease, and orthopedic disorders, whereas Tohoku showed the highest rates of high care‐need levels and home nursing utilization. Hospitalization risk was highest in western Japan, whereas in‐home deaths were more frequent in eastern Japan. Hospital bed density was positively correlated with hospitalization risk (ρ = 0.62) and negatively with 6‐month mortality risk (ρ = −0.44). Utilization of enhanced home care support clinics/hospitals was moderately associated with higher in‐home death risks (ρ = 0.49).ConclusionsSubstantial regional differences exist in patient characteristics, service utilization, and outcomes among older adults receiving physician‐led home‐visit care. Healthcare resources were strongly associated with hospitalization and end‐of‐life care, highlighting the need for region‐specific strategies to optimize healthcare delivery and resource allocation for the super‐aged society of Japan.Keywords: home healthcare, home visit, long‐term care, older people, regional disparity

Introduction

Healthcare systems worldwide are shifting from hospital‐centered care to community‐ and home‐based services, which are driven by the need to manage complex chronic conditions, reduce healthcare costs, and support individuals unable to access outpatient services [1, 2]. Japan, the most rapidly aging society globally [3], faces a growing demand for home healthcare. Home‐visit medical care has become an essential component of the healthcare system, reducing the strain on acute care hospitals.

The delivery of home healthcare services varies across regions owing to differences in population size, aging rates, availability of home‐visit care services, and the number of long‐term care facilities. Previous studies have shown that areas with higher population densities and more home care support clinics/hospitals (HCSCs) tend to have more physician‐led home visits, whereas areas with more long‐term care facilities have fewer home visits [4]. Additionally, regional variations in physician‐led home visits have been observed, with higher rates in the Kanto, Chubu, Kinki, and Chugoku regions, and lower rates in the Hokkaido and Tohoku regions [4]. However, these studies primarily focused on visit rates, without exploring differences in patient characteristics across regions. A previous study of three municipalities revealed variations in patient profiles, such as underlying health conditions and types of long‐term care services used [5], suggesting that similar differences may exist nationwide. Furthermore, although differences in healthcare resources, including hospital bed availability, may influence outcomes such as hospitalization and in‐home death among patients receiving physician‐led home care, these associations remain unexplored.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has comprehensively examined regional variations in patient characteristics, service utilization, and outcomes as well as their associations with healthcare resource distribution using nationwide data. To address these gaps, this study used nationwide data to explore regional differences in patient characteristics, service utilization, and outcomes, and to examine their associations with regional healthcare resource distribution. These findings may inform the development of region‐specific strategies for optimizing home healthcare delivery and resource allocation in the super‐aged society of Japan.

Methods

Home Healthcare in Japan

In Japan, home healthcare involves regular physicians‐led regular home visits covered by health insurance. In 2006, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) introduced HCSCs [6, 7, 8] to provide 24‐h home visit care upon patient request. In 2012, enhanced HCSCs were introduced to provide higher‐quality home healthcare by focusing on emergency home visits and end‐of‐life care [6, 7, 8]. Enhanced HCSCs must meet additional requirements: employ three or more full‐time physicians, have conducted at least 10 emergency home visits in the past year, and have provided home‐based end‐of‐life care at least four times in the past year [7, 8]. Conventional HCSC that meet these criteria can be upgraded to a higher reimbursement category [6, 7, 8]. Additionally, patients may also receive home‐visit nursing care [9].

Long‐Term Care in Japan

In 2000, Japan introduced a long‐term care insurance system distinct from the national medical insurance system [10, 11, 12]. Residents aged ≥ 65 years were eligible to receive long‐term care services, including home‐, community‐, and facility‐based care. Eligibility is determined by municipalities according to standardized national assessments of physical or mental disability. The care‐need‐level certificate consists of seven categories, ranging from support level 1 (less disabled) or 2 to care‐need levels 1 to 5 (most disabled) [13].

Data Source and Study Participants

This retrospective study used linked data from the National Database of Health Insurance Claims and Specific Health Checkups of Japan (NDB) and the Japanese Long‐term Care Database between April 2020 and March 2021. The data were provided by the MHLW. The NDB contains claims data on approximately 98% of healthcare services provided in Japan [14], including anonymized individual identifiers, age, sex, disease codes, medical procedures, and prescriptions. Procedures and medications were coded using the original Japanese codes. The diagnoses were linked to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes. The Japanese Long‐term Care Database comprises: (1) Certification data of Long‐term care from municipalities; (2) Long‐term care insurance claim data submitted by service providers; and (3) Long‐term care Information system for Evidence (LIFE) data provided by service providers [15]. However, LIFE data was not used in this study. All datasets shared the same anonymized IDs, enabling integrated analysis.

The study participants included patients aged ≥ 65 years who received physician‐led regular home visit care in October 2020. Eligible visits were identified through claims for the “Home Medical Care Management Fee” or the “Home Cancer Care Management Fee.”

Patient Characteristics

We extracted patient demographics, including age (≥ 85 or < 85 years), sex, medical diagnoses (cerebrovascular disease, cancer, fracture, joint disease, respiratory disease, heart disease, joint disease, and dementia), and long‐term care need levels (classified as care support level, care need level 1–3, care need level 4–5, and no certification). Diagnoses were identified using ICD‐10 codes recorded between April and October 2020 (Appendix S1), excluding those marked as suspected. Long‐term care need levels were determined using either long‐term care certification data or service utilization records as of October 2020.

Service Utilization

Data on service utilization included the type of healthcare facilities providing home‐visit care (any HCSCs, enhanced HCSCs) and use of home‐visit nursing services. Facility type was determined based on detailed codes associated with the Home Medical or Cancer Care Management Fees. Use of home‐visit nursing services was determined from physician‐issued orders between April and October 2020, or from the presence of relevant service codes in October 2020 claims data.

Outcome

Patient outcomes were tracked over a 6‐month period (until March 2021) and included at least one hospitalization, death, and in‐home death (among deceased patients). Death was defined based on either claims data indicating death as the discharge/disease status, or surcharges for the death certificate or end‐of‐life care at home. A previous validation study showed high sensitivity (94.3%) and specificity (98.5%) for identifying inpatient deaths using claims data [16]. In‐home death was defined based on the presence of a death certificate or home‐based end‐of‐life care surcharges.

Statistical Analysis

First, we described and visualized patient characteristics, service utilization, and outcomes by prefecture, and aggregated them by eight regions of Japan (Hokkaido, Tohoku, Kanto, Chubu, Kinki, Chugoku, Shikoku, and Kyushu). The Kanto, Chubu, and Kinki regions were considered urban because of the presence of major cities (Tokyo, Nagoya, and Osaka), whereas the remaining regions were considered middle or depopulated areas. Given the substantial variation in care need certification rates across prefectures, we visualized the proportion of individuals with care levels 4–5 among those certified as needing care.

Second, to account for differences in patient profiles, we used multivariable logistic regression models to estimate adjusted outcome risks by prefecture and region, adjusting for age, sex, medical conditions, and care need level.

Finally, we analyzed prefectural‐level associations of adjusted outcome proportions with healthcare resources. (1) The number of hospital beds per 1000 residents aged ≥ 65 years (excluding psychiatric beds, based on 2020 data from the Survey of Medical Institutions) [17] and (2) the proportion of patients receiving care from enhanced HCSCs were used in the analyses. The number of people aged ≥ 65 years by prefecture was extracted from the publicly available results from the Basic Resident Register [18]. In addition, to account for the potential impact of caregiver presence on patient outcomes, (3) the proportion of single‐person households among older adults aged ≥ 65 years, based on data from the 2020 Population Census of Japan [19] was also included as the prefectural indicator. Spearman's rank correlation coefficients (ρ) were used to assess associations between healthcare resource indicators and adjusted outcome rates.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and STATA (version 17; Stata Corp., Texas, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Tsukuba (approval number 1729). The requirement for informed consent was waived because the data were anonymized prior to the researcher's access.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first national‐level study to examine regional differences in the characteristics, service utilization, and outcomes of patients receiving physician‐led home‐visit care. Our results revealed substantial regional variations. In western Japan, patients more frequently had cancer, respiratory and heart diseases, and orthopedic disorders, whereas the Tohoku region had the highest prevalence of cerebrovascular disease, greater care needs, and the highest rates of home nursing utilization. Hospitalization rates were elevated in western Japan, whereas end‐of‐life care at home was more common in eastern Japan. Furthermore, regional healthcare resources were associated with patient outcomes, with a strong positive correlation observed between hospitalization rates and hospital bed density.

Previous studies have shown higher utilization of physician‐led home‐visit care in regions with high population density [4]. Despite its lower population density, the Chugoku region demonstrates high utilization of home‐visit care [4], which has been attributed to a relatively large number of general clinics and conventional HCSCs offering these services, despite the low density of enhanced HCSCs per older adult. In our study, the utilization rate of any HCSCs and that of enhanced HCSCs was the lowest in the Chugoku region, supporting the notion that home‐visit care there is primarily provided by general clinics and conventional HCSCs. This aligns with another nationwide survey wherein respondents from the Chugoku region frequently reported, “We conduct home visits ourselves when our outpatient patients require them,” [20], suggesting the presence of coordinated systems, possibly led by regional medical associations, that facilitate home‐visit care delivery even without a high concentration of enhanced HCSCs.

In contrast, although previous research has shown lower utilization of physician‐led home visits in the Tohoku region [4], our findings indicate that among recipients, there is a higher prevalence of cerebrovascular disease, greater care needs, and more frequent use of home nursing services. Given that cerebrovascular disease‐related mortality is highest in the Tohoku region [21], regional differences in disease prevalence may partly explain these patterns. Moreover, with fewer home healthcare providers and limited resources [4], patients with reduced activities of daily living who cannot attend outpatient clinics may be prioritized for home visit care.

In terms of 6‐month outcomes, hospitalization risk and home death proportions among decedents exhibited an inverse relationship; hospitalization risk was highest in western Japan (Kyushu, Chugoku, and Kinki regions), whereas home death risk was highest in eastern Japan (Kanto, Tohoku, and Hokkaido). This reflects the previously reported “high west, low east” distribution of hospital bed density [22]. In our study, hospital bed density was strongly positively correlated with hospitalization risk, suggesting that greater bed capacity in western Japan may facilitate admissions upon clinical deterioration. This finding is consistent with prior surveys of clinics providing home visit care, in which respondents in the Tohoku and Hokkaido regions most frequently reported challenges in securing beds for emergency admissions, whereas those in western Japan reported such challenges comparatively less often [20]. The observed positive correlation between hospital bed density and hospitalization risk may reflect several possible mechanisms. In regions with greater bed availability, patients may be more readily admitted upon clinical deterioration, indicating improved access to inpatient care. Moreover, higher hospital bed density may result in the substitution of hospital‐based care for end‐of‐life care that could otherwise be provided at home. Conversely, increased hospitalization rates may also reflect insufficient resources to support home‐based end‐of‐life care. Given the cross‐sectional nature of this study, causal relationships cannot be determined, and longitudinal studies are warranted to elucidate these pathways.

Our study revealed that the 6‐month mortality risk was highest in Tohoku region. Previous reports have documented that this region has the lowest life expectancy [23] and highest overall mortality rate [21] in Japan, which may also apply to patients receiving physician‐led home‐visit care. A moderate negative correlation was observed between hospital bed density and 6‐month mortality, suggesting two possibilities: (1) in regions with fewer beds, home‐visit care may be more commonly provided to patients near the end of life; and (2) limited bed availability may restrict admissions, resulting in more patients remaining at home until death, thereby increasing observed mortality risks.

Our study demonstrated a moderate correlation between enhanced HCSC utilization and home death proportion among decedents. Prior research has shown that patients treated by enhanced HCSCs are more likely to die at home [8], which is consistent with our findings.

We found a moderate negative correlation between the proportion of single‐person households among older adults and 6‐month mortality among patients receiving physician‐led home visit care. This finding suggests that in regions with a higher proportion of older adults living alone, patients receiving home care may be less severely ill or receive services earlier in the disease trajectory. It is also possible that such regions have more accessible home care systems that allow for earlier intervention. Conversely, in regions with higher mortality rates, patients receiving physician‐led home visit care may be more severely ill, possibly because family caregiving capacity allows home care to be reserved for those with greater needs.

Our findings may help inform region‐specific strategies to support dignified care for the final stage of life in line with patient preferences. To ensure that patients can receive care according to their wishes, healthcare systems should support hospital‐ and home‐based care options. For example, in regions with low hospitalization rates, increasing hospital bed capacity may be necessary to accommodate emergency admissions and inpatient care. Conversely, in regions with low rates of in‐home death, expanding the availability of enhanced HCSCs may facilitate appropriate home‐based care. Furthermore, in regions where hospital admission is difficult because of limited bed availability—such as Tohoku and Hokkaido—strengthening home care through telemedicine may be an effective strategy. In Western countries, “hospital‐at‐home” models, which deliver hospital‐level treatment at home even during emergencies, have demonstrated outcomes equal to or better than inpatient care [24]. Notably, studies report no significant differences in patient outcomes or satisfaction between physician home visits and remote consultations supported by visiting nurses [25]. These findings suggest that enhancing nurse–physician collaboration and using telemedicine could reduce physician burden and expand access to timely home visit care in Japan.

This study had some limitations. First, the analysis was conducted during the Coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, during which the number of patients receiving physician‐led home‐visit care and the rate of in‐home deaths increased [26, 27, 28]. Therefore, the findings may differ from non‐pandemic conditions. However, previous studies have found nationwide increases in home‐visit care and in‐home deaths, regardless of municipal size or region [10, 11], suggesting that the impact of regional differences may be limited. Second, information on socioeconomic status, the presence of family caregivers, advance care planning, and end‐of‐life preferences was unavailable and therefore not considered. Third, because of limitations in the available data, we were unable to distinguish between different types of hospital beds or assess whether they were being used as intended. Despite these limitations, this study is the first to analyze regional differences among patients receiving physician‐led home‐visit care, using data from the NDB and Japanese Long‐term Care Database. Our findings provide important insights for designing region‐specific home healthcare systems.

In conclusion, using linked data from the NDB and Japanese Long‐term Care Database, we identified regional variations in patient characteristics, service utilization, and outcomes. These outcomes were associated with regional healthcare resources and service use, with a strong positive correlation between hospital bed density and hospitalization risks. These findings suggest that the distribution of medical resources influences healthcare utilization and place of death. Our results highlight the importance of considering regional characteristics when developing home healthcare delivery systems and allocating medical resources to address local population needs.
