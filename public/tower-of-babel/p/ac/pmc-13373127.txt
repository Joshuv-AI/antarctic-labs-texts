Successful Introduction of Lorlatinib After Drug Eruption With Alectinib in ALK‐Positive Lung Adenocarcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Shunsuke Omura, Keeya Sunata, Tetsuo Tani, Koyuru Uchibori, Shun Shinomiya, Saeko Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusion genes are detected in approximately 3%–5% of non‐small cell lung cancers (NSCLC). Alectinib and lorlatinib are ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors with established efficacy; however, their adverse event profiles differ. We report a 61‐year‐old man with recurrent ALK‐positive lung adenocarcinoma who developed a grade 2 drug eruption consistent with fixed drug eruption during alectinib therapy. The rash resolved following drug discontinuation and corticosteroid treatment. Lorlatinib was subsequently initiated without recurrence of cutaneous toxicity. Apart from Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) Grade 2 dyslipidemia, no significant adverse events occurred, and partial response has been maintained for over 1 year. This case suggests that lorlatinib may be a feasible alternative in patients who develop cutaneous adverse reactions to alectinib, enabling continuation of targeted therapy.Keywords: Alectinib, ALK fusion gene, drug eruption, Lorlatinib, non‐small cell lung cancer

Introduction

Anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) fusion genes are identified in approximately 3%–5% of non‐small cell lung cancers (NSCLC), particularly, in adenocarcinoma histology. Alectinib and lorlatinib are ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) that suppress constitutive ALK signalling and have demonstrated significant clinical efficacy. Although both agents are recommended as first‐line therapies, their toxicity profiles differ. We report a case in which lorlatinib was successfully introduced after discontinuation of alectinib due to drug eruption.

Case Report

A 61‐year‐old man presented with a 12‐mm nodule in the left lingular segment. He underwent left upper lobectomy, and pathological examination revealed adenocarcinoma, pT1bN0M0, stage IA2 (Molecular testing for ALK rearrangement was not conducted).

Four years postoperatively, follow‐up CT imaging (Figure 1A) and FDG PET‐CT (Figure 1B) revealed a 90‐mm anterior mediastinal mass and multiple bone metastases, consistent with postoperative recurrence. Molecular testing using the Oncomine Dx Target Test Multi‐CDx System confirmed ALK fusion positivity.

Chest radiograph, CT and PET‐CT findings at the time of recurrence. (A) Chest CT revealing a necrotic‐prone mass lesion, measuring 89 mm in its longest diameter, in the anterior mediastinum. (B) PET‐CT demonstrating increased FDG uptake in the anterior mediastinum, pleura and multiple bones.

Alectinib therapy was initiated. On Day 6, a Grade 2 erythematous papular rash developed on the dorsal aspect of both lower legs, involving approximately 18% of the body surface area (BSA), according to CTCAE version 6.0 (Figure 2). No mucosal lesions were observed. A dermatology consultation was performed, and the patient was diagnosed with a drug eruption clinically consistent with fixed drug eruption (skin biopsy and drug‐induced lymphocyte stimulation testing were not performed).

Skin rash on Day 6 of alectinib administration. Drug eruption presenting as disseminated erythematous papules desensitization. Lorlatinib on the posterior aspects of both lower legs.

Alectinib was immediately discontinued. Treatment was initiated with oral prednisolone (20 mg/day for 4 days, followed by 10 mg/day for 5 days), along with topical corticosteroids and antihistamines. The rash resolved completely without sequelae.

Although the rash was limited to CTCAE Grade 2 and no mucosal involvement was observed, the consulting dermatologist considered the eruption to be drug‐related and recommended avoidance of re‐exposure to alectinib because of the potential risk of recurrence. Given the patient's high tumour burden, including a 90‐mm mediastinal mass and multiple bone metastases, rapid disease control was prioritized. Therefore, lorlatinib was selected rather than alectinib dose reduction or desensitization. Lorlatinib (100 mg/day) was initiated 9 days after the discontinuation of alectinib. No recurrence of rash was observed. Apart from CTCAE grade 2 dyslipidaemia requiring lipid‐lowering therapy, no significant adverse events occurred.

Within 3 months, the anterior mediastinal mass markedly decreased and subsequently became undetectable on CT imaging (Figure 3A). Partial response has been maintained for approximately one and a half years (Figure 3B) with ongoing lorlatinib therapy.

Contrast‐enhanced CT after initiating ALK inhibitors. Chest radiograph showing remarkable regression of the recurrent anterior mediastinal mass with ongoing lorlatinib therapy for (A) 3 months after initiation of lorlatinib and (B) 18 months after initiation of lorlatinib.

Discussion

The ALK gene encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase. Chromosomal rearrangements, most commonly EML4–ALK fusion, result in constitutive kinase activation and downstream signalling through pathways such as RAS/MAPK, JAK–STAT and mTOR, thereby promoting tumorigenesis [1]. ALK rearrangements are identified in approximately 3%–5% of NSCLC, predominantly in adenocarcinoma histology and frequently in younger or never‐smoking patients [2].

Alectinib has demonstrated superior efficacy compared with crizotinib in the first‐line setting, as shown in the ALEX trial, with significantly prolonged progression‐free survival and improved central nervous system control [3, 4]. More recently, lorlatinib has also shown substantial efficacy in untreated ALK‐positive NSCLC, with durable responses reported in the CROWN trial [5]. Accordingly, current clinical guidelines recommend both agents as preferred treatment options in ALK‐positive NSCLC [6].

Although both alectinib and lorlatinib are highly effective ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors, their adverse event profiles differ. Skin rash has been reported more frequently with alectinib than with lorlatinib according to prescribing information and clinical data [4, 5]. Hypersensitivity reactions may not be shared among different ALK inhibitors, suggesting that switching to another ALK inhibitor may be feasible in selected patients [7]. The underlying mechanism of this difference remains unclear. However, the two agents possess distinct chemical structures and pharmacokinetic properties. Alectinib is a second‐generation ALK inhibitor with a benzocarbazole‐based scaffold and is primarily metabolized by CYP3A4 [8, 9]. In contrast, lorlatinib is a macrocyclic third‐generation ALK inhibitor designed to overcome resistance mutations and is metabolized mainly by CYP3A4 and UGT1A4 [10, 11]. These pharmacologic differences may contribute to variability in hypersensitivity reactions among ALK inhibitors and may partly explain the absence of cross‐reactivity observed in the present case.

Several reports have described successful re‐administration of alectinib after severe drug eruption using desensitization protocols [12, 13]. In those reports, switching to crizotinib was considered as an alternative strategy; however, concerns remained regarding its comparatively lower efficacy than alectinib. Consequently, desensitization and re‐challenge with alectinib were attempted to maintain effective ALK inhibition. Since the introduction of lorlatinib, another highly effective ALK inhibitor has become available. In the CROWN trial, lorlatinib demonstrated substantial and durable efficacy in untreated ALK‐positive NSCLC [5]. Therefore, when clinically significant cutaneous toxicity develops during alectinib treatment, switching to lorlatinib may represent a reasonable alternative to desensitization in selected patients. In the present case, although the rash was limited to CTCAE Grade 2 and no mucosal involvement was observed, the consulting dermatologist recommended avoiding re‐exposure to alectinib because of the potential risk of recurrent hypersensitivity. Furthermore, the patient had a substantial tumour burden, including a 90‐mm mediastinal mass and multiple bone metastases, requiring prompt and uninterrupted disease control. Considering these factors, switching to lorlatinib was considered a more practical strategy than attempting alectinib dose reduction or desensitization. Notably, lorlatinib was introduced without recurrence of cutaneous toxicity, and the patient has maintained a durable partial response for more than 1 year. This case suggests that switching from alectinib to lorlatinib may be a feasible therapeutic option when cutaneous adverse reactions preclude continued alectinib treatment.

This report has several limitations. First, this is a single case report, and therefore the generalizability of our findings is limited. Second, the immunological mechanism underlying the drug eruption was not fully investigated. No drug‐induced lymphocyte stimulation test or skin biopsy was performed to confirm the causal relationship between alectinib and the rash. Therefore, the diagnosis of fixed drug eruption could not be established definitively and was based primarily on clinical findings. Third, because lorlatinib was introduced shortly after discontinuation of alectinib, the possibility of spontaneous resolution of the cutaneous reaction independent of drug switching cannot be completely excluded. Further studies and accumulation of additional cases are required to clarify the safety of switching between ALK inhibitors after cutaneous adverse reactions.

In conclusion, we report a case of ALK‐positive NSCLC in which lorlatinib was successfully administered after discontinuation of alectinib due to drug eruption. Switching to another ALK inhibitor with a distinct chemical structure and safety profile may represent a practical therapeutic option when hypersensitivity reactions prevent continuation of alectinib. Further clinical experience is needed to determine the optimal management strategy for ALK inhibitor–associated cutaneous toxicity.
