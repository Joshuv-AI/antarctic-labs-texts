Impact of preoperative dual antiplatelet therapy vs. aspirin monotherapy on perioperative outcomes in carotid endarterectomy patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Wenjun Yang, Wei Hu, Yifeng Pan, Xiangkun Meng, Leilei Tang, Jie Chen, Huimin Xu

Introduction

Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) remains a cornerstone intervention for stroke prevention in patients with significant symptomatic or asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis [1]. Antiplatelet therapy is fundamental to the perioperative management of CEA and serves to reduce the risk of thromboembolic events. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) is increasingly used in patients undergoing CEA due to concurrent cardiac or cerebrovascular conditions such as coronary artery disease or ischemic stroke [2]. Recent data indicate that approximately 35% of these patients receive preoperative DAPT [3]. This trend places surgeons in a clinical dilemma [4]: whether DAPT should be continued before CEA. Substantial evidence suggests that continued DAPT may provide benefits, such as reduced restenosis and perioperative stroke [5, 6]; however, other studies have reported increased risks of bleeding [7, 8], prolonged length of stay [9], and even higher mortality [10]. These conflicting findings highlight the critical trade-off between thrombotic and hemorrhagic complications.

Despite its widespread clinical use, no definitive consensus exists regarding the optimal preoperative antithrombotic management strategy for patients undergoing CEA [11–13]. The Society for Vascular Surgery recommends individualizing perioperative DAPT decisions [14]. Reflecting this uncertainty, a survey of European vascular surgeons showed that although nearly all surgeons would continue aspirin prior to CEA, 43% and 55% would discontinue clopidogrel before CEA in symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, respectively [15].

Against this backdrop of clinical uncertainty and practice variability, targeted research on preoperative antiplatelet strategies in patients undergoing CEA has become increasingly important. However, most studies have been conducted in Western populations. There are well-documented racial disparities in antithrombotic treatment responses, particularly the higher susceptibility to bleeding complications observed in Asian populations [16–18], which limits the generalizability of the existing evidence. Consequently, the risk–benefit balance of continued DAPT versus aspirin monotherapy in Asian patients undergoing CEA remains unclear. To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients undergoing CEA to evaluate the perioperative outcomes associated with preoperative DAPT compared with aspirin monotherapy in this population.

Aim

This study aimed to compare perioperative major bleeding and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) between patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy receiving aspirin monotherapy and those receiving dual antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel plus aspirin).

Method

Study design and subjects

This retrospective cohort study included inpatients who underwent CEA for carotid artery stenosis between January 2020 and December 2024 at a Chinese tertiary-care hospital. The study protocol adhered to the STROCSS guidelines [19]. Clinical data were obtained from the electronic medical records database. Patients who underwent CEA for asymptomatic stenosis ≥ 70% or symptomatic stenosis ≥ 50% and who received antiplatelet therapy within 2 weeks prior to CEA were included. Patients meeting any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) those who underwent concomitant procedures with CEA; (2) those receiving clopidogrel monotherapy or ticagrelor therapy before CEA; and (3) those lost to follow-up within 30 days after CEA.

The decision to continue or discontinue clopidogrel before surgery and the duration of discontinuation were at the surgeon’s discretion. Patients were categorized into two groups based on antiplatelet therapy status 5 days prior to the CEA procedure: (1) aspirin group—patients receiving aspirin alone and (2) DAPT group—patients receiving aspirin plus clopidogrel. The 5-day cutoff was selected based on the pharmacodynamic profile of clopidogrel, whose antiplatelet effects persisted for the lifespan of platelets. Discontinuation for ≥ 5 days prior to surgery is widely considered sufficient to allow recovery of platelet function, according to clinical guidelines [20, 21].

Data collection and definitions

A chart review was conducted for each patient included in this study. Demographic characteristics (sex, age, weight, and height) and baseline laboratory values (hemoglobin, platelet count, and serum creatinine) were recorded for each patient. The clinical data included symptomatic status (ipsilateral amaurosis fugax, transient ischemic attack, or stroke within 6 months) [5], degree of carotid stenosis, smoking history and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and renal insufficiency (defined as creatinine clearance < 60 mL/min using the Cockcroft–Gault equation). Preoperative medications, including β-blockers, oral anticoagulants, proton pump inhibitors, statins, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), were documented, along with prior revascularization procedures (coronary artery bypass grafting [CABG], percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI], prior CEA, or carotid artery stenting [CAS]). Operative variables, including American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class, surgery type (conventional or eversion), patch use, and protamine administration, were also collected.

The degree of carotid stenosis was categorized according to the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial criteria and classified as > 70% or 50–70% [22]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2) and categorized as low (< 24 kg/m2) or high (≥ 24 kg/m2).

All patients were operated underwent general anesthesia. Systemic intra-operative heparinization was given routinely as 50 IU sodium heparin per kilogram body weight, up to a maximum of 5000 IU. Protamine use was as per surgeon preference. Activated clotting time was not measured routinely. Drain was placed in every case. All patients were monitored in the intensive care unit for a minimum 12 h before transfer to the surgical ward.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of major bleeding events within 30 days following CEA, defined as neck hemorrhage requiring reoperation or intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). The secondary outcome was the occurrence of MACEs within 30 days following CEA, defined as a composite of ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction (MI), and all-cause death. Detailed definitions of the study outcomes are provided in Supplementary Table 1. All events were identified from subsequent outpatient and inpatient electronic medical record reviews.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages). Covariate imbalance between the two groups was addressed using stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, including the following baseline variables: sex, age, BMI, symptomatic status, degree of carotid stenosis, comorbidities, smoking history, preoperative medications, prior cardiovascular procedures, hemoglobin, platelet count, ASA class, and operative factors. Individual weights were calculated and stabilized by multiplying by the marginal probability of treatment.

Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to assess the balance of baseline characteristics, with an SMD of < 0.1 indicating a negligible imbalance. Univariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between preoperative antiplatelet therapy (DAPT vs. aspirin alone) and clinical outcomes, with odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported before and after IPTW.

Subgroup analyses for major bleeding were performed stratified by age (< 75 and ≥ 75 years), symptomatic status, degree of carotid stenosis (50–70% and > 70%), prior CEA/CAS, and statin use. For subgroup analyses, multivariable logistic regression and propensity score–based methods (IPTW) were applied. A P-value for interaction < 0.1 was used to assess potential interactions between the treatment groups and subgroups.

To identify the predictors of major bleeding, multivariable analysis was conducted using stepwise logistic regression. The covariates included in the univariable analysis were sex, age, BMI, symptomatic status, degree of carotid stenosis, comorbidities, preoperative medications, prior cardiovascular procedures, and operative factors. Variables with a significance level ≤ 0.20 in the univariate analysis were entered into the final multivariable model. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.3). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethics approval

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine on 20 Dec 2024 (NO. 2024-1490).

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, between January 2020 and December 2024, 905 inpatients underwent CEA. Patients who were not receiving antiplatelet therapy before CEA (n = 60), underwent concomitant procedures (n = 10), received clopidogrel monotherapy (n = 60) or ticagrelor therapy (n = 26), or were lost to follow-up (n = 8) were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 741 patients were included in this analysis.

Study population and flow chart. Abbreviations: CEA, carotid endarterectomy

The mean age of the study population was 68.32 ± 7.79 years, and 616 patients (83.1%) were men. Ipsilateral stenosis > 70% was present in 80.3% of the patients, and 24.7% were symptomatic. Proton pump inhibitors were used in 89.1% of patients (pantoprazole, 80.2%; lansoprazole, 5.4%; rabeprazole, 3.5%), and 97.3% received statins. The detailed distribution of statin medications is presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Compared with the aspirin group, patients in the DAPT group were more likely to be symptomatic (30.4% vs. 22.0%) and had higher rates of prior stroke (53.6% vs. 39.5%), coronary artery disease (40.9% vs. 17.7%), hyperlipidemia (19.8% vs. 13.9%), prior PCI or CABG (32.5% vs. 9.7%), and prior CEA/CAS (14.8% vs. 6.9%). No significant differences were observed between the groups in other comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, renal insufficiency, CHF, and COPD.

Preoperative β-blocker use was more common in the DAPT than in the aspirin group (36.3% vs. 25.4%). Patients in the DAPT group were also more likely to undergo eversion CEA (44.7% vs. 25.2%) and receive protamine (42.2% vs. 22.6%). After IPTW adjustment, the baseline characteristics of the two groups were well balanced (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Clinical outcomes

The overall incidence of major bleeding and MACEs within 30 days after CEA was 3.0% and 2.3%, respectively. Compared with the aspirin group, the DAPT group had a significantly higher risk of major bleeding (5.5% vs. 1.8%; OR 3.19, 95% CI 1.34–7.58), primarily driven by an increased incidence of neck hemorrhage (4.2% vs. 1.6%; OR 2.73, 95% CI 1.06–7.01). The risk of MACEs did not differ significantly between the DAPT and aspirin groups (2.1% vs. 2.4%; OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.31–2.54). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of ICH, ischemic stroke, MI, or death between the two groups (Supplementary Table 3).

After IPTW adjustment, DAPT remained significantly associated with a higher risk of major bleeding (OR 4.21, 95% CI 1.81–9.78) and neck hemorrhage (OR 3.93, 95% CI 1.54–10.01). No significant difference in MACEs was observed between the groups (OR 1.49, 95% CI 0.57–3.85) (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis

In subgroup analyses stratified by age, symptomatic status, degree of carotid stenosis, prior CEA/CAS, and statin use (Supplementary Fig. 2), DAPT was generally associated with an increased risk of major bleeding in most subgroups.

Using a cutoff of 75 years, DAPT was associated with an increased bleeding risk in both age groups (OR 3.06, 95% CI 1.07–8.76 vs. OR 13.17, 95% CI 1.35–128.41; p for interaction = 0.254). Similarly, an increased bleeding risk was observed regardless of stenosis severity (OR 9.67, 95% CI 0.98–95.87 vs. OR 3.54, 95% CI 1.24–10.08; p for interaction = 0.434).

Among asymptomatic patients, DAPT was associated with a higher risk of major bleeding (OR 5.51, 95% CI 1.98–15.35), whereas this association was not observed in symptomatic patients (OR 1.35, 95% CI 0.16–11.64). A similar pattern was observed in patients without prior CEA/CAS (OR 4.65 [95% CI 1.75–12.32]). In addition, statin users receiving DAPT had a higher risk of major bleeding than those receiving ASAA (OR 3.96, 95% CI 1.46–10.73). No significant differences in major bleeding were observed between high- and moderate-intensity statins (p = 0.546) or between hydrophilic and lipophilic statins (p = 0.655) (Supplementary Table 4).

Overall, no statistically significant interactions were observed between antiplatelet therapy and the subgroup variables, and the subgroup findings were consistent with the overall results.

Factors associated with postoperative 30-day major bleeding

Multivariable analysis identified DAPT as an independent risk factor for postoperative 30-day major bleeding (OR 3.40, 95% CI 1.42–8.19, p = 0.006), whereas statin use was an independent protective factor (OR 0.13, 95% CI 0.03–0.51, p = 0.003) (Table 2).

Discussion

In the present study, we assessed the perioperative impact of preoperative DAPT in an Asian population undergoing CEA. Notably, our results demonstrated that preoperative DAPT was associated with a higher incidence of major bleeding complications, with no significant difference in the incidence of MACEs.

Bleeding is the dominant perioperative hazard of antiplatelet therapy in patients undergoing CEA; however, the specific contribution of DAPT to this risk remains controversial. We found that DAPT was associated with a higher incidence of major bleeding, primarily driven by neck hemorrhage. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting an increased risk of neck hemorrhage with DAPT [7, 23]. Conversely, Donners et al. analyzed 12,317 patients from the Dutch Carotid Intervention Audit Registry and reported no significant difference in the 30-day rates of neck hemorrhage requiring reintervention or ICH between patients receiving DAPT and those receiving single antiplatelet therapy [24]. Notably, the single antiplatelet therapy group in that study included both aspirin and clopidogrel monotherapies, whereas our comparator group was restricted to aspirin monotherapy, making the incremental risk associated with clopidogrel more apparent.

From a mechanistic perspective, clopidogrel irreversibly inhibits the P2Y12 receptor, reducing platelet aggregation, whereas aspirin inhibits the cyclooxygenase-1 pathway, decreasing thromboxane A2 synthesis. Their synergistic antiplatelet effects may prolong bleeding time and impair hemostasis, thereby increasing the risk of surgical bleeding [25]. Importantly, neck hemorrhage is not a trivial complication; it is associated with prolonged hospital stay, increased incidence of cranial nerve injury, and higher morbidity and mortality rates [8, 9]. Therefore, the use of DAPT at the time of CEA should be carefully weighed against the clinically significant risk of bleeding, as highlighted by our findings. These findings highlight the potential role of clinical pharmacists in optimizing perioperative antiplatelet strategies, including individualized risk assessment and medication management in patients undergoing CEA.

Preoperative antiplatelet therapy requires careful balancing of bleeding risk and cardiovascular protection. It is well established that the continuation of aspirin monotherapy reduces the stroke risk after CEA [26]. However, the impact of DAPT on MACEs remains unclear and conflicting. Jones et al. reported that preoperative DAPT was associated with fewer strokes and a lower composite endpoint of stroke/death in patients with asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis [27]. In contrast, Manunga et al. found no differences in postoperative stroke, MI, or 30-day mortality [3]. In our study, DAPT was not associated with a significant difference in the 30-day composite endpoint of ischemic stroke, MI, or death, or in any individual component. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because of the relatively small sample size and low event rates, which may limit the statistical power. Larger multicenter studies are needed to clarify the cardiovascular benefits of preoperative DAPT.

Notably, multivariate analysis indicated that preoperative statin use was independently associated with a reduced risk of postoperative major bleeding. Current evidence regarding the relationship between statin use and bleeding risk remains inconclusive [28, 29]. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of potential residual confounding, particularly healthy user bias. Subgroup analyses by statin intensity and lipophilicity showed no significant differences in bleeding risk; however, the very small number of events in the high-intensity group (n = 1) limits the reliability of these comparisons. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in future studies with larger sample sizes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center retrospective study, it is subject to inherent limitations, including residual bias and unmeasured confounding. Although we used a comprehensive covariate-adjusted IPTW approach, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly from factors such as institutional practice patterns and surgeon preferences. The decision to continue or discontinue clopidogrel was made at the surgeon’s discretion, introducing potential confounding by indication. Second, CYP2C19 polymorphisms were not assessed, as genotyping is not routinely performed in clinical practice. These genetic variants significantly influence clopidogrel response and are highly prevalent in Asian populations [30], potentially affecting both bleeding and thrombotic outcomes [31]. We suggest that clinicians consider CYP2C19 genotyping when prescribing clopidogrel, particularly in Asian patients. Finally, the low incidence of MACEs limits the ability to detect meaningful differences and raises the possibility of type II error.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important evidence regarding the perioperative impact of DAPT use in Asian patients undergoing CEA. Our findings suggest that the increased risk of major bleeding associated with DAPT may outweigh its potential benefits in this patient population. Further large-scale, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to confirm these results and define optimal antiplatelet strategies for patients undergoing CEA.

Conclusion

Compared with aspirin monotherapy, preoperative DAPT was associated with an increased risk of perioperative major bleeding without a reduction in perioperative MACEs in patients undergoing CEA. Caution is warranted when DAPT is administered within 5 days before CEA. Larger multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.
