Effects of albendazole treatment on sperm parameters of ram experimentally infected with cystic echinococcosis
Authors: Figen Celik, Ibrahim Halil Gungor, Muhammet Uslug, Aslıhan Cakır-Cihangiroglu, Mustafa Sonmez, Sami Simsek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cystic echinococcosis (CE) is a chronic zoonotic disease caused by Echinococcus granulosus, primarily affecting the liver and lungs of livestock but also capable of inducing systemic alterations through oxidative stress and metabolic imbalance. These systemic effects may impair reproductive physiology in male animals. This study aimed to investigate the effects of CE infection and subsequent albendazole (ABZ) treatment on sperm quality parameters in rams. Fourteen male lambs were experimentally infected with E. granulosus eggs, and after the establishment of infection, six were treated orally with ABZ (7.5 mg/kg) every 30 days for three months, while others served as untreated controls. Semen samples were evaluated using computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) for motility and kinematic parameters, as well as for sperm concentration, viability, and abnormality rates. CE infection alone led to decreased motility, sperm concentration, and alterations in kinematic parameters such as linearity (LIN), straightness (STR), and amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH), reflecting impaired sperm trajectory and coordination. ABZ-treated animals exhibited a more pronounced decline in total and progressive motility and in kinematic parameters (straight-line velocity-VSL-, average path velocity-VAP-), suggesting potential microtubule- or mitochondria-associated functional alterations. No significant changes were detected in sperm viability. These findings indicate that both CE and ABZ may adversely influence sperm function, possibly through oxidative and cytoskeletal mechanisms. However, the absence of an uninfected ABZ -only control group limits the ability to distinguish independent drug-related effects from infection-associated alterations.

Introduction

Cystic echinococcosis (CE) remains one of the most widespread zoonotic helminth infections affecting both humans and herbivores. The life cycle typically revolves around dogs, which act as the principal definitive hosts, and various livestock species that serve as intermediate hosts. Transmission to humans and animals occurs through accidental ingestion of infective Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato eggs shed in the faeces of infected dogs or carried on their fur, as well as via contaminated water or food. When carnivores, most commonly dogs, but also foxes, jackals, or wolves consume infected visceral organs such as liver or lungs containing viable hydatid cysts, the parasite develops into its adult form in the small intestine, usually within 45–50 days. While CE primarily impacts the liver and lungs, systemic effects on various biochemical and physiological functions have been increasingly recognized (Borhani et al. 2021; Bozukluhan et al. 2024). CE has been associated with chronic inflammation, metabolic disturbances, and oxidative stress, all of which may contribute to impaired reproductive function. However, research on the specific impact of CE on ram fertility, particularly sperm quality, remains limited. Understanding the relationship between CE and semen parameters such as motility, concentration, and morphology is essential for assessing its implications on breeding performance (Sagkan-Ozturk et al. 2015; Cancela et al. 2019).

Albendazole (ABZ), a benzimidazole-group, broad-spectrum anthelmintic, is widely used in the treatment of CE and other helminth infections. Its primary mechanism of action involves inhibition of microtubule polymerization, resulting in structural and functional disruption of the parasite. However, prolonged ABZ exposure has also been associated with potential adverse effects on host metabolism, liver function, and testicular physiology (Tabl et al. 2018). Despite its widespread use, relatively limited attention has been paid to its potential effects on male reproductive parameters, particularly sperm motility, concentration, viability, morphology, and DNA integrity in small ruminants. Experimental studies have reported that ABZ administration may negatively affect spermatological parameters, especially during the reproductive period. For instance, treatment of Saanen bucks resulted in significant reductions in sperm motility, viability, and concentration, accompanied by increased sperm DNA damage (Yucel et al. 2021). Similarly, in rams infected with gastrointestinal nematodes, ABZ treatment was associated with a transient decline in testosterone levels and sperm quality. However, these parameters gradually returned to baseline following treatment cessation (Hussein et al. 2015). Consequently, caution has been advised when administering ABZ during the breeding season. In contrast, earlier studies in bulls reported no significant adverse effects on testicular weight, sperm production, or semen quality following ABZ treatment (Berndtson et al. 1980), suggesting that the reproductive effects of ABZ may be species-dependent. Collectively, these findings highlight the need for careful risk-benefit evaluation when ABZ is used in breeding animals, particularly during sensitive reproductive periods.

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of ABZ treatment on specific sperm parameters in rams experimentally infected with E. granulosus eggs. Given the potential impact of CE on systemic physiology, including reproductive function, this study aims to evaluate changes in sperm motility, sperm concentration, viability and morphology following ABZ administration. Therefore, this study aims to gain a better understanding of the effects of both CE and ABZ treatment on some spermatologic features in rams.

Materials and methods

Study location and experimental setting

The study was conducted at Fırat University, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine (Elazig, Türkiye). Experimental infection procedures, animal monitoring, and necropsy examinations were performed at the university’s animal research facilities. Spermatological analyses were carried out at the Department of Artificial Insemination laboratory under standardized conditions.

Animals and experimental design

At the beginning of the study, a total of 14 Akkaraman rams, aged 1 to 1.5 years and weighing approximately 50–55 kg, were included. All animals underwent general clinical and genital examinations prior to enrollment, and only rams showing no pathological findings were selected. Throughout the experimental period, animals were housed under standard husbandry conditions, fed with high-quality roughage and concentrate feed, and provided with drinking water ad libitum. Of the 14 rams, three animals served as uninfected negative controls, whereas 11 rams were experimentally infected with E. granulosus eggs (Celik et al. 2025). The experimental design followed a longitudinal model, allowing assessment of spermatological parameters across different stages of infection and treatment. During long-term follow-up, four rams were lost due to severe CE-related complications. Two animals died prior to treatment initiation at approximately day 210 post-infection (week 30). Albendazole treatment was initiated at day 224 post-infection (approximately 32 weeks after experimental infection), allowing sufficient time for the establishment and progression of CE prior to treatment. Two additional deaths occurred during the first week following treatment initiation, corresponding to approximately days 232–234 post-infection. All deceased animals were subjected to systematic necropsy examination, which revealed extensive hepatic and pulmonary lesions consistent with advanced CE. No gross pathological findings suggestive of acute ABZ-related toxicity were observed. Following mortality-related losses, seven infected rams remained available for follow-up. However, sufficient semen could not be consistently collected from one ram, therefore experimental groups were finalized using six infected rams. These animals were equally allocated into two balanced groups: three rams received albendazole (ABZ) treatment and three remained as infected untreated controls. ABZ treatment was initiated on the days 224 post-infection, and spermatological evaluations were performed approximately 30, 60, and 90 days after treatment initiation, corresponding to the initial, middle, and final phases of the study, respectively. Thus, animals had been infected for approximately 224 days at treatment initiation and for nearly 314 days by the end of the observation period. Semen samples were collected from the infected rams throughout this three-month period at regular intervals, and a phase-based analytical approach was adopted accordingly. In addition, semen samples had been collected repeatedly from three uninfected control rams prior to necropsy and were used as non-infected controls for comparison. Thus, despite the reduced number of infected animals, balanced group allocation was maintained, albeit with a limited sample size.

Experimental infection and ABZ treatment

A hydatid cyst-infected liver was obtained from a slaughtered sheep at a local abattoir in Elazig, Türkiye, and transported to the laboratory under sterile conditions. Cyst fluid was aspirated and centrifuged to isolate protoscoleces, which were examined microscopically for viability. Protoscolex viability was assessed prior to infection using a combination of microscopic evaluation of motility and vital staining. Motility and flame cell activity were examined under light microscopy, and viability was further confirmed using eosin exclusion, where viable protoscoleces remained unstained while non-viable ones took up the dye. Only preparations with a viability rate of approximately 90% or higher were used for experimental infection (Diker et al. 2007). A stray dog was clinically examined, dewormed, and confirmed to be free of intestinal parasites prior to infection. Prior to experimental infection, the dog was confirmed to be free of gastrointestinal helminth infections by repeated coprological examinations using flotation techniques, performed using multiple fecal samples collected over consecutive days. No parasite eggs were detected in any of the examined samples. The dog was orally infected with approximately 20,000 viable protoscoleces under veterinary supervision (ethical approval No. 2022/15, dated 07.09.2022). For experimental infection of lambs, approximately 20,000 E. granulosus eggs per animal were administered. This dose was selected to ensure consistent establishment of infection and to reduce variability in cyst development across animals, as lower doses may result in heterogeneous infection outcomes. Mortality observed in some animals was considered within the expected progression of long-term experimental CE infection. After approximately 50 days, adult E. granulosus worms were expelled following administration of arecoline hydrobromide, and parasite eggs were recovered from the feces. Egg purification was performed using standard flotation and washing procedures as previously described (Eckert et al. 2001; Deplazes et al. 2017). Experimental infection of lambs was performed by oral administration of approximately 20,000 E. granulosus eggs per animal. Prior to infection, egg viability was assessed based on morphological integrity and embryonation status under light microscopy, and only fully developed, morphologically intact eggs were used. To ensure standardization, the infection dose was prepared from homogenized egg suspensions with consistent egg counts. Experimental infection of sheep was performed via oral administration using an intragastric gavage technique under veterinary supervision. Infection establishment was confirmed through a combination of longitudinal clinical monitoring, ultrasonographic detection of cystic lesions, and serological findings, as previously described (Celik et al. 2025). ABZ treatment was initiated on day 224 post-infection (week 32), once infection had been confirmed by longitudinal clinical and monthly ultrasonographic monitoring, in order to assess treatment-associated changes under established infection conditions. Infected rams allocated to the treatment group received ABZ orally via intragastric dosing at 7.5 mg/kg body weight, administered once every 30 days for three consecutive months (approximately days 30, 60, and 90 after treatment initiation). This intermittent dosing regimen was selected to reflect a practical veterinary treatment approach while allowing assessment of temporal effects on spermatological parameters. Infected untreated animals (n = 3) were maintained as controls throughout the study period. Three uninfected rams were included as non-infected reference controls. Beginning at approximately 7 months of age, semen samples were repeatedly collected from these animals on at least six occasions prior to necropsy, providing baseline control data for comparison. These control animals were slaughtered at approximately 11 months of age and were not subjected to infection or ABZ treatment. The overall duration of the study was approximately 314 days, encompassing the experimental infection period, ABZ treatment, and follow-up semen sampling. Spermatological assessments in infected animals were performed within a phase-based sampling design (initial, middle, and final phases), allowing evaluation of temporal changes associated with chronic infection and treatment rather than immediate short-term drug effects. Artificial vagina training period

Prior to semen collection, all rams were trained for semen collection using an artificial vagina. For this purpose, rams were housed with estrus-induced teaser ewes for approximately two months to facilitate adaptation. Estrus in ewes was induced by intramuscular administration of 17β-estradiol (5 mg/2 mL per ewe) dissolved in sesame oil. During mating attempts, the erect penis was guided into the artificial vagina until the rams became fully accustomed to the procedure.

Semen collection

For analytical purposes, semen quality parameters were evaluated across three predefined experimental phases corresponding to the 90-day ABZ treatment and follow-up period. Baseline semen parameters were obtained from both non-infected control rams (n = 3) and infected rams (n = 6) prior to treatment allocation. Following baseline sampling, infected rams were allocated to either the ABZ-treated group (n = 3) or the infected untreated control group (n = 3). In the treated group, ABZ administration was initiated at day 224 post-infection and repeated at 30-day intervals for three consecutive months. Spermatological evaluations were organized according to a phase-based design: the initial phase corresponded to the start of ABZ treatment at day 224 post-infection, whereas the middle and final phases corresponded to days 254 and 284 post-infection, respectively. Within this treatment-observation period, semen samples were collected at regular intervals, approximately every 10 days, allowing temporal evaluation of sperm parameters across the defined phases. Semen collection was performed using an artificial vagina during three predefined experimental phases corresponding to the initial, middle, and final stages of infection and treatment. During each phase, semen was collected at regular approximately 10-day intervals (around days 10, 20, and 30), yielding three semen collections per phase for each ram. Immediately after collection, semen samples were diluted at a 1:1 ratio with a pre-warmed Tris-egg yolk extender and transported to the laboratory in a water bath maintained at 37 °C. Semen quality parameters were evaluated both immediately after dilution (0 h) and after 24 h of storage at 5 °C.

Spermatological analyses

Sperm quality was comprehensively evaluated based on motility characteristics, CASA-derived kinematic parameters, viability, morphological abnormalities, and sperm concentration.

Motility and kinematic parameters

Sperm motility and kinematic parameters were evaluated using a computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) system (ISAS v1, Proiser, Spain). For CASA analysis, semen samples were diluted to a final concentration of approximately 1 × 106 sperm/mL, and 3 µL of the sample was placed onto a specialized CASA slide (Spermtrack, 20 μm). Analyses were performed under a phase-contrast microscope equipped with a heated stage maintained at 37 °C. The following parameters were recorded: total and progressive motility (%), proportions of rapid, medium, slow, and immotile spermatozoa (%), curvilinear velocity (VCL), straight-line velocity (VSL), average path velocity (VAP), linearity (LIN), straightness (STR), wobble (WOB), amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH), and beat-cross frequency (BCF). CASA settings were standardized for ram semen as previously described (Kaya et al. 2021).

Sperm concentration

Sperm concentration (×106/mL) was automatically determined using the CASA concentration module from the same motility-prepared samples.

Abnormal spermatozoa ratio

Abnormal spermatozoa were evaluated using Diff-Quick staining. For each sample, 200 spermatozoa were examined under a phase-contrast microscope, and head, tail, and total abnormalities were recorded as percentages (Gungor et al. 2022).

Viability assessment

Sperm viability was assessed using the eosin-nigrosin staining method. A total of 200 spermatozoa per slide were evaluated, and live-dead ratios were calculated based on staining characteristics (Gungor et al. 2022).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0). The individual animal was considered the experimental unit. Because semen samples were repeatedly collected from the same rams, individual ejaculates were not treated as independent observations. To account for repeated sampling, semen quality parameters were summarized for each animal within three predefined experimental phases (initial, middle, and final). All statistical comparisons were performed using a predefined phase-based, animal-level analytical approach aligned with the 90-day ABZ treatment schedule. For each phase, the mean value of repeated semen collections was calculated and used for statistical comparisons. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For normally distributed variables, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. For non-normally distributed variables, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used, followed by pairwise comparisons using the Mann-Whitney U test. To control for multiple comparisons, Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for parametric data, while Bonferroni correction was used for non-parametric pairwise comparisons to adjust the significance threshold. Results are presented as mean ± SEM, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the limited number of animals per group, findings were interpreted cautiously and regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Although animals were followed for different post-treatment durations, these time frames were not considered independent treatment groups.

Ethical approval and animal welfare compliance

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with national regulations for the protection of animals used for scientific purposes in Türkiye. The study was approved by the Fırat University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2023/20 − 12 for sheep experiments and 2022/15 for dog experiments). The design, conduct, and reporting of the study were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines to ensure transparency, reproducibility, and adherence to animal welfare standards.

Results

Motility and kinematic parameters of diluted (0 h) semen

Phase-based mean values for sperm motility and kinematic parameters of diluted semen (0 h) are presented in Table 1. Statistical comparisons were performed using animal-based mean values calculated for each experimental phase. During the initial phase, progressive motility was significantly lower in both the infected untreated and ABZ-treated groups compared to the control group (P < 0.05), with no significant difference between the two infected groups (P > 0.05). Total motility did not differ significantly among control, treated, and infected untreated groups (P > 0.05). In the middle phase, progressive motility remained significantly lower in both infected untreated and treated groups compared to the control group (P < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed between the treated and infected untreated groups (P > 0.05). Total motility did not differ significantly among the groups (P > 0.05). In the final phase, total motility was significantly lower in the treated group compared to the control group (P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between the untreated and control groups (P > 0.05). With respect to kinematic parameters, VSL and VAP values were significantly lower in the treated group compared to both the control and infected untreated groups during the initial phase (P < 0.05). LIN and STR values were consistently lower in both treated and infected untreated groups compared to the control group across all phases (P < 0.05), with no significant difference between the two infected groups (P > 0.05). ALH values were significantly higher in both treated and infected untreated groups compared to controls during the initial phase (P < 0.05), and remained significantly higher in the infected untreated group compared to the control group during the middle phase (P < 0.05). BCF values were significantly lower in both treated and infected untreated groups compared to the control group during the middle and final phases (P < 0.05), with no significant difference between the two infected groups (P > 0.05). Within the ABZ-treated group, phase-based evaluation indicated temporal changes in sperm motility and kinematic parameters across the initial, middle, and final phases; however, parasitological cure was not assessed, and treated animals were not considered infection-free.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that chronic parasitic infections can negatively affect male reproductive function through systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic alterations, which may impair spermatogenesis and sperm quality (Aitken and Roman 2009; Fijak et al. 2016). Although specific data on CE in rams are limited, similar mechanisms have been reported in other parasitic and chronic disease models. In addition, benzimidazole compounds, including ABZ, have been shown to interfere with microtubule formation and cellular division processes, which are essential for spermatogenesis (Lacey 1990; Hennessy et al. 1989). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first experimental study to simultaneously evaluate the effects of CE infection and ABZ treatment on spermatological parameters in rams. The findings demonstrate that both CE and ABZ may negatively influence male reproductive performance. CE is known to induce systemic effects such as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic imbalance (Anteplioglu et al. 2024). These systemic stressors have been widely reported to disrupt spermatogenesis and negatively affect sperm morphology, motility, and concentration (O’Flaherty and Scarlata 2022). In particular, oxidative stress damages sperm membranes, axonemal proteins, and DNA, leading to impaired motility, reduced progression, and diminished fertilization capacity, while also interfering with capacitation and the acrosome reaction (Agarwal et al. 2014; Aitken 2022). Consistent with this pathophysiological background, the reductions in sperm motility and concentration observed in the untreated infected group in the present study are in line with the systemic burden of CE. It should be noted that a single dose of ABZ is generally not expected to result in overt systemic toxicity under standard veterinary use conditions (Dayan 2003; Hennessy et al. 1989). Therefore, the alterations observed in sperm parameters following treatment are unlikely to reflect acute toxic effects of the drug alone. Instead, these findings may be explained by indirect or delayed mechanisms, including the impact of benzimidazoles on microtubule-dependent processes involved in spermatogenesis (Lacey 1990), as well as immune-mediated responses triggered by parasite damage and antigen release (Horton 2000). Furthermore, the long duration of infection prior to treatment may have contributed to the observed impairments in sperm quality through chronic inflammatory and metabolic effects. The dosing regimen applied in this study differs from prolonged or repeated treatment protocols that may be used in clinical or field settings for CE. Therefore, the observed effects should be interpreted within the context of an intermittent dosing strategy rather than long-term therapeutic administration. In addition, ABZ has been reported to induce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), contributing to oxidative stress. Oxidative stress is a well-established factor in male reproductive dysfunction, as it damages sperm plasma membranes, axonemal proteins, and DNA, leading to impaired motility, reduced progressive movement, and decreased fertilization capacity. Furthermore, excessive ROS levels can interfere with key functional processes such as capacitation and the acrosome reaction (Agarwal et al. 2014; Aitken 2022). Therefore, the observed deterioration in sperm parameters in the treated group may be partially explained by oxidative stress mediated mechanisms in addition to the direct pharmacological effects of albendazole.

Previous studies have reported that CE infection may negatively affect the physiological condition and productivity of livestock, potentially influencing reproductive performance through systemic and metabolic alterations (Eckert and Deplazes 2004; Torgerson and Budke 2003). In addition, benzimidazole compounds, including ABZ, have been associated with adverse effects on spermatogenesis and sperm motility in different animal models, likely due to their effects on microtubule formation and rapidly dividing germ cells (Ramalingam et al. 1983; Yücel et al. 2021). While the present study focuses on male reproductive parameters, CE may also have broader implications for overall reproductive performance in livestock. In female animals, parasitic infections and systemic inflammatory responses have been associated with altered hormonal balance, reduced fertility, and impaired pregnancy outcomes, contributing to decreased productivity and economic losses (Eckert and Deplazes 2004; Torgerson and Budke 2003). In addition, benzimidazole compounds are more commonly discussed in the context of female reproductive safety, where concerns regarding teratogenicity and reproductive toxicity have been reported, particularly during early gestation (Dayan 2003). Therefore, the combined effects of parasitic infection and treatment on both male and female reproductive functions should be considered when evaluating their overall impact on herd fertility and livestock production systems. However, studies evaluating the combined impact of CE infection and ABZ treatment on spermatological parameters are lacking. Therefore, the present study provides novel insights into the combined effects of infection and treatment on male reproductive parameters. It should also be noted that the dosing regimen applied in this study represents an intermittent treatment protocol rather than continuous administration, which may influence the magnitude and duration of observed effects on sperm parameters. Although albendazole and its active metabolite have relatively short plasma half-lives, their effects on spermatogenesis may persist beyond drug clearance due to their impact on microtubule-dependent cellular processes. Benzimidazole-induced alterations in sperm parameters are generally considered reversible; however, recovery may require the completion of one or more spermatogenic cycles. In rams, spermatogenesis lasts approximately 47–50 days, suggesting that normalization of sperm parameters may take several weeks to months following drug exposure (Amann and Hammerstedt 1993; Hennessy et al. 1989). Furthermore, while benzimidazoles are often administered in prolonged or repeated regimens in clinical practice, the intermittent dosing protocol applied in this study reflects a controlled experimental design and should be interpreted within this context. In addition, Echinococcus spp. are known to modulate host immune responses, often inducing a state of immune tolerance that facilitates long-term parasite survival (Siracusano et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2003). The therapeutic efficacy of ABZ is partly associated with parasite damage leading to the release of antigenic components, which can subsequently stimulate host immune responses (Horton 2000). Therefore, the effects observed following albendazole treatment may not be solely attributable to direct pharmacological action, but could also involve immune-mediated mechanisms triggered by parasite degeneration. In this context, the absence of a non-infected treated control group represents a limitation, as it prevents full differentiation between direct drug effects and immune-mediated responses. It is also plausible that increased immune activation following treatment may contribute to the observed alterations in sperm parameters.

The present study provides exploratory evidence that CE infection and subsequent ABZ treatment are associated with alterations in sperm functional quality in rams. Using an animal-based, phase-oriented analytical approach, consistent reductions in progressive motility and sperm concentration were observed in infected animals across experimental phases, indicating a negative impact of CE on male reproductive performance. These findings are in line with previous reports demonstrating that chronic helminth infections induce systemic inflammation, metabolic imbalance, and oxidative stress, all of which may adversely affect spermatogenesis and semen quality (Sagkan-Ozturk et al. 2015; Borhani et al. 2021; O’Flaherty and Scarlata 2022). From a practical perspective, the observed negative effects of both CE infection and ABZ treatment on sperm parameters may have implications for breeding management, particularly in endemic regions. Although temporary impairment of sperm quality following ABZ administration may suggest avoiding treatment during mating periods, control of CE infection remains a priority in veterinary practice. Therefore, treatment strategies should be carefully timed to balance infection control and reproductive performance.

Chronic helminth infections, including CE, are known to induce prolonged systemic inflammation and immune modulation, which may adversely affect reproductive physiology. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress can disrupt testicular function by impairing spermatogenesis, altering Sertoli and Leydig cell activity, and compromising the blood-testis barrier (Aitken and Roman 2009; Fijak et al. 2016). In addition, chronic parasitic infections are often associated with metabolic stress and hormonal imbalances, which may further contribute to reduced sperm quality. Although direct data on fertility outcomes in sheep with cystic echinococcosis are limited, studies in livestock and other chronic disease models suggest that sustained inflammatory burden and reduced body condition can negatively impact reproductive performance and breeding efficiency (Diskin and Kenny 2014). Therefore, the alterations in sperm parameters observed in the present study may reflect broader infection-associated effects on male reproductive capacity. The timing of treatment in relation to the breeding season is also an important consideration. In sheep, the breeding season typically extends over several weeks to a few months, depending on breed and management conditions. Although temporary avoidance of albendazole administration during this period may be considered to minimize potential adverse effects on sperm quality, interruption of treatment in animals with established infection may allow continued parasite development and disease progression. Therefore, treatment decisions should be carefully balanced, taking into account infection severity, transmission risk, and reproductive management priorities.

Phase-based analysis of CASA-derived kinematic parameters revealed that reductions in LIN and STR were persistent features in infected animals, suggesting impaired sperm trajectory and directional movement rather than simple reductions in overall motility. Such alterations are biologically relevant, as directional motility is essential for successful oocyte approach and fertilization (Mortimer et al. 2015). The observed increase in ALH, particularly during the initial and middle phases, may reflect compensatory or dysregulated flagellar motion associated with membrane instability or axonemal dysfunction, phenomena commonly linked to oxidative stress-mediated damage in spermatozoa (Agarwal et al. 2014; Aitken 2022).

In treated animals, a more pronounced decline in total and progressive motility was evident during the final experimental phase. Additionally, reductions in VSL and VAP during the initial phase suggest impaired straight-line and average path velocities following ABZ administration. These findings are consistent with the known mechanism of action of ABZ, which involves inhibition of β-tubulin-dependent microtubule polymerization, a process essential not only for parasite survival but also for sperm flagellar structure and mitochondrial energy regulation (Hennessy et al. 1989; Hussein et al. 2015; Yucel et al. 2021). ABZ is characterized by a relatively short plasma half-life; however, its primary active metabolite, ABZ sulfoxide, exhibits prolonged systemic availability and is considered responsible for most of the drug’s biological activity (Dayan 2003; Marriner et al. 1986). In addition, benzimidazoles are known to affect microtubule-dependent processes in rapidly dividing cells, including spermatogenic cells, which may result in delayed or cumulative effects on sperm production and function (Hennessy et al. 1989; Lacey 1990). Therefore, the alterations observed in sperm parameters in the present study are more likely to reflect subacute or delayed effects on spermatogenesis rather than immediate pharmacological effects following drug administration.

Importantly, sperm viability was not significantly affected across any experimental phase, indicating that both CE infection and ABZ treatment primarily influenced functional sperm parameters rather than inducing direct spermatocyte death. This observation supports previous reports suggesting that benzimidazole compounds may impair sperm motility and kinematics without necessarily causing overt cytotoxicity, possibly through subcellular structural and metabolic disturbances (Tabl et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2021).

The absence of significant differences in total motility and abnormality parameters in diluted-cooled semen further suggests that infection- and treatment-related effects are more pronounced in fresh semen and may interact differently with storage-related stress conditions. Similar phase- and condition-dependent responses in CASA parameters have been reported in previous studies evaluating semen under standardized laboratory settings (Contri et al. 2010; Kaya et al. 2021). From a practical perspective, moderate reductions in sperm viability may be biologically tolerable and may not necessarily translate into reduced fertility, as fertilization can still occur with a proportion of non-viable spermatozoa. However, more pronounced decreases in viability particularly when accompanied by impairments in motility and kinematic parameters are more likely to compromise reproductive success. In rams, fertility outcomes are determined by the combined effects of multiple sperm quality parameters rather than a single variable (Amann and Hammerstedt 1993). In addition, the observation that treatment-related effects were more pronounced in fresh semen suggests that drug-associated alterations may be more readily detectable under near-physiological conditions. In contrast, cooled semen is subject to additional storage-related stressors, including temperature shifts, membrane destabilization, and oxidative stress, which may obscure or interact with treatment-related effects, thereby complicating direct comparisons between fresh and stored samples (Watson 2000; Holt and North 1991).

From a reproductive perspective, alterations in sperm motility, concentration, and kinematic parameters are known to be closely associated with reduced fertilization capacity and breeding success in ruminants (Amann and Hammerstedt 1993; Mortimer 2000). In particular, progressive motility and velocity-related parameters such as VSL and VAP are critical determinants of the ability of spermatozoa to reach and fertilize the oocyte. Therefore, the reductions observed in these parameters in both CE-infected and ABZ-treated animals may have practical implications for reproductive performance, especially in breeding males. Although the present study did not directly evaluate fertility outcomes, the magnitude and consistency of the observed changes suggest that both infection and treatment-related effects could potentially compromise breeding efficiency under field conditions.

The observed mortality is consistent with previously reported long-term experimental models of CE in sheep and reflects disease severity rather than experimental manipulation. Interpretation of the present findings is nevertheless limited by the small number of animals per group and the absence of an uninfected ABZ-treated control group, which precludes definitive separation of infection- and treatment-related effects. In addition, parasitological clearance following ABZ administration was not assessed. Accordingly, any inference regarding the specific reproductive effects of ABZ should be regarded as assumptive rather than definitive, as the observed phase-dependent changes in sperm parameters may reflect infection-related pathology, treatment-associated effects, or their combined influence. Despite these limitations, the phase-based patterns documented in this study provide valuable exploratory insight into the temporal dynamics of sperm functional alterations during chronic CE and subsequent anthelmintic intervention. These limitations should be taken into account when interpreting group comparisons, particularly in the later experimental phases where reduced sample size may have affected statistical power. Another limitation of the present study is the absence of a non-infected ABZ-treated control group, which could have helped to distinguish the direct effects of ABZ from those associated with CE infection. However, benzimidazole compounds are not typically administered prophylactically to uninfected animals under field conditions. Therefore, the experimental design was focused on evaluating treatment-associated changes in animals with established infection, which reflects a more clinically relevant scenario. From a practical perspective, the observed negative effects of both CE infection and ABZ treatment on sperm parameters may have implications for breeding management in endemic regions. Benzimidazole compounds, including ABZ, are widely used for the control of helminth infections in livestock; however, their potential impact on male reproductive performance should be considered when treatment is planned during mating periods (Dayan 2003; Lacey 1990). Although temporary impairment of sperm quality following ABZ administration may suggest avoiding treatment during breeding seasons, infection control remains a priority in endemic areas. Therefore, treatment strategies should be carefully scheduled to balance reproductive performance and parasite control.

This study provides exploratory evidence that chronic CE is associated with alterations in sperm motility, concentration, and selected kinematic parameters in rams. Phase-based analysis indicated that ABZ administration was accompanied by temporal changes in sperm functional quality, although definitive attribution of these effects to drug action alone is not possible within the present study design. Despite these limitations, the findings offer valuable preliminary insight into the reproductive consequences of chronic CE and anthelmintic intervention in breeding rams.
