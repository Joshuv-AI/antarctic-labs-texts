Assessing the consistency of simultaneous tripling of nuclear and renewable energy capacity in Korea: Evidence from the IPCC AR6 scenarios
Authors: Jiseok Ahn, Sangyong Park, Son H Kim, Haewon McJeon, Jiyong Eom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAt COP28, the international community, including Korea, committed to tripling renewable energy capacity by 2030 and nuclear capacity by 2050. These pledges are embedded within Korea’s carbon neutrality framework and the 11th Basic Plan for Long-Term Electricity Supply and Demand. This study examines the consistency of Korea’s clean energy policies with Paris-compliant trajectories derived from the IPCC AR6 scenario database. Our analysis demonstrates that Korea’s current electricity plan exhibits a slower phase-out of fossil fuel capacity relative to Paris-aligned pathways. Moreover, while the renewable energy tripling target demonstrates strong alignment with global decarbonization scenarios, the majority of IPCC AR6 models project constrained nuclear expansion, rendering the nuclear tripling pledge inconsistent with prevalent long-term pathways. As Korea advances its energy transformation, enhanced science-based modeling and rigorous scenario analysis are imperative to ensure policy coherence with global decarbonization objectives and support evidence-informed decision-making.Subject areas: environmental policy, energy policy, economics

Introduction

The 2015 Paris Agreement established an international consensus to limit global warming to well below 2°C, requiring deep transformations in energy systems. Building on this ambition, 133 countries, including Korea, committed at the 28th Conference of the Parties (COP 28) to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) to tripling renewable energy capacity by 2030 and nuclear capacity by 2050.1,2 These international pledges signal a critical shift toward clean energy, yet raise questions about the technical feasibility and consistency of national energy transition strategies within global mitigations scenarios.3,4,5

Scenarios have long served as a key tool in global change research, helping to characterize uncertainties in the complex interactions between human and environmental systems.6,7 Scenarios aiming to limit global warming to 1.5°C require major transformations across both energy supply and demand sectors. According to a recent study,8 achieving this ambitious target requires not only a fundamental restructuring of existing energy systems but also a large-scale transition toward renewable energy sources, alongside substantial improvements in energy efficiency to mitigate the ongoing rise in global energy demand. These changes involve phasing out fossil fuels, accelerating the deployment of low-carbon technologies, and implementing stringent policy measures to support deep decarbonization. Additionally, the role of renewable energy in climate stabilization has been a central theme in scenario comparison studies,9,10 highlighting the critical need for scaling up renewable capacity, optimizing grid integration, and ensuring system resilience to meet the stringent emission reduction targets.

Rapid cost declines in renewables are accelerating decarbonization and may reduce the economic appeal of other low-carbon technologies. This trend raises questions about how cost reductions in wind and solar power influence the role and perceived value of CCS (carbon capture and sequestration) for decision-makers.11 The feasibility of large-scale CDR (carbon dioxide removal) deployment is highly uncertain, and existing scenarios have been criticized for over-reliance on CDR.12 These uncertainties underscore the importance of diversifying mitigation strategies, balancing technology portfolios, and ensuring that near-term actions do not depend excessively on future, uncertain technological breakthroughs.

Numerous studies have examined long-term energy transition pathways for Korea, providing valuable insights into the challenges and opportunities of decarbonization. For example, Park et al.13 used the long-range energy alternatives planning system (LEAP) model to assess three electricity scenarios for Korea by 2050, demonstrating that demand-side management and renewable energy expansion can achieve significant Greenhouse gas (GHG) reductions at an affordable cost. Similarly, Hong et al.14 developed transition roadmaps for Korea’s energy system, showing that increasing renewable generation while reducing overall energy demand enhances energy security, creates jobs in the electricity sector, and lowers emissions. In the context of Korea’s carbon neutrality goals, Joo et al.15 examined the 2050 target-setting process and its role in accelerating the transition to a carbon-neutral society, while Cho et al.16 conducted a scenario-based comparative analysis of Korea’s building sector, highlighting the need for effective sectoral policies to reduce emissions.

An extensive literature conducts multi-model comparison frameworks in order to assess the countries’ decarbonization in major economies or countries, which include European Union (EU),17 USA,17,18,19China,20 and Japan.21,22 However, few multi-model studies explored the role of the power sector in achieving carbon neutrality Korea. Park23 conducted a literature review study by synthesizing eight independent scenario studies on Korea’s decarbonization pathways. Yet, it did not conduct comparative assessment among scenarios which are generated by multiple models that follow a common set of protocols. To the best of our knowledge, a recent study from Cho et al.24 is the first systematic multi-model comparison study on Korea’s climate policy of carbon neutrality, enhancing the understanding of the potential implications of the carbon neutrality target on the role of the power sector and its low-carbon transformation strategy. However, none of the existing studies have explicitly analyzed Korea’s alignment with the tripling renewable and nuclear energy pledge, as emphasized in the latest international policy discussions in COP 28.

While these studies offer important national-level insights, they primarily rely on domestic modeling frameworks or focus on sector-specific analyses, with limited integration of global climate scenario databases. In contrast, global scenario studies—such as those based on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) scenarios database25—have been widely used to analyze regional and global decarbonization pathways, but applications to individual countries, particularly Korea, remain scarce. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to conduct a detailed, Korea-specific assessment using scenarios from the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) database.

Scenarios database have been central to IPCC decarbonization assessments for more than two decades, making it possible to collect and compare large ensembles of modeled future.26 They have also been used to gather and assess the existing knowledge to provide emission pathways for climate modeling.27 The past two decades have seen an explosion of climate change mitigation scenarios from different disciplinary communities, including energy, land use, and integrated assessment modeling.28,29

In this study, we use the global mitigation scenarios from IPCC AR6 scenarios database to quantitatively assess the consistency of global climate actions such as the tripling of nuclear and renewable energy capacity pledge (hereafter, the tripling pledge). These scenarios provide country-specific mitigation potential and decarbonization pathways consistent with global climate goals. One approach to evaluating the tripling pledge is to benchmark it against national-level data derived from a subset of Paris-compliant global decarbonization scenarios. Out of 2,344 scenarios the IPCC AR6 scenarios database, we used a subset of 198 scenarios that explicitly model Korea as a region to assess the tripling pledge and the country’s energy transition targets (Figure 1). The analysis evaluates the consistency of Korea’s tripling targets for renewables and nuclear with Paris-compliant pathways, and compares Paris-compliant and non-compliant scenarios to understand their positioning within Korea’s broader energy system transformation.

Background on electricity generation for Korea: historical trends and future directions under the national plan

Understanding the historical evolution of Korea’s electricity generation is essential for planning future power sector decarbonization. Figure 2 present historical and projected changes in Korea’s electricity generation mix, including total generation in terawatt-hours (TWh), five-year average fuel shares from 1961 to 2023, and future targets outlined in the 11th Basic Plan for Long-Term Electricity Supply and Demand (hereafter the 11th Basic Plan).30,31 Over the past six decades, Korea’s electricity generation has expanded significantly—from under 10 TWh in the early 1960s to over 580 TWh by 2023 (Figure 2A). According to the 11th Basic Plan, electricity generation is projected to increase by approximately 11% by 2030 (643 TWh) and 21% by 2038 (704 TWh) relative to 2023 levels (Figure 2B).

Oil-fired generation dominated Korea’s energy mix during its period of rapid industrialization; however, the oil crises of the 1970s prompted a strategic shift toward nuclear and coal to enhance energy security. By the 1990s, nuclear had become the dominant source of electricity generation, with coal following as the second-largest contributor. Introduced in the 1980s, natural gas has become one of the three main sources of Korea’s power generation system, contributing over 20% of total electricity generation in the 2020s.

Since 2010, Korea has initiated renewable energy deployment, although its share remained modest—averaging just 9% between 2021 and 2023 (Figure 2C). The 11th Basic Plan targets a renewable energy share of 22% by 2030 and 33% by 2038, alongside increased reliance on nuclear power, the introduction of hydrogen co-firing in gas-fired plants, and a phased reduction of dependence on unabated coal and natural gas. These developments signal a transition toward a more diversified and low-carbon electricity system in Korea (Figure 2D).

Results

Decarbonization pathways: comparing Korea’s national power sector plans with Paris-compliant scenarios

To assess how Korea’s energy system transformation aligns with the targets set under the Korea’s 11th Basic Plan, we analyze electricity capacity projections across key generation sources (Figure 3). Scenario comparisons based on climate ambition show clear differences between Paris-compliant (C1-C3) and non-compliant (C4-C8) pathways, highlighting the pivotal role of policy ambition in shaping the pace and direction of Korea’s energy transition. We classify all scenarios in either the C1-C3 ensemble or C4-C8 ensemble (Figure 4), the former is considered to be relevant to the Paris Agreement’s temperature limits,32 even though some scenarios may not be considered to be fully Paris-compliant depending on the interpretation of its temperature goal and of other objectives, as well as judgment on the probability, overshoot, and timing of the temperature change.33 Projected capacity trajectories for coal, gas, nuclear, solar, and wind vary significantly across these scenario categories (Figure 3A). Coal capacity declines in both scenario categories, but more rapidly in Paris-compliant scenarios, which project a median projection of 3 GW by 2040, compared to 7.2 GW in non-compliant scenarios. Regardless of climate ambition, most scenarios reflect a broad consensus on the need to phase out unabated coal. In contrast, natural gas shows the greatest divergence. Paris-compliant scenarios anticipate a gradual decrease post-2030, reaching a median of 29.4 GW by 2050, suggesting a limited transitional role. Non-compliant scenarios, however, project substantial increases, reaching a median of 63.9 GW in 2050, highlighting its continued role as a major energy source under weaker climate targets.

Nuclear capacity remains relatively stable across both scenario groups through 2030. By 2050, Paris-compliant scenarios show a median capacity of 16.3 GW (IQR: 13.2–38.9 GW), reflecting uncertainty regarding its future role. Non-compliant scenarios generally project a decline, albeit with considerable variation in the extent of reduction.

Solar capacity exhibits strong growth under Paris-compliant scenarios, with a median of 172.5 GW by 2050 (IQR: 16–191.3 GW), driven by ambitious decarbonization targets. Non-compliant scenarios also show expansion, though to a slightly lesser extent, with a median of 137.5 GW (IQR: 14.2–172.3 GW).

Wind capacity follows a similar trajectory to solar, with Paris-compliant scenarios projecting steady and robust growth—reaching a median capacity of 93.7 GW (IQR: 87.2–110 GW) by 2050. In contrast, non-compliant pathways foresee slower and more uncertain deployment, with a median of 61.7 GW (IQR: 18.8–86.4 GW), highlighting the critical role of policy ambition and regulatory support in enabling large-scale deployment.

When compared with Korea’s national targets outlined in the 11th Basic Plan (Figure 3B), key differences emerge. Coal capacity under the 11th Basic Plan exhibits a declining trend that is broadly consistent with Paris-compliant scenarios, though the reduction occurs on a more delayed timeline. In contrast, natural gas capacity projections diverge substantially. While Paris-compliant scenarios anticipate a gradual decline in gas-fired capacity after 2030, the 11th Basic Plan projects a continued expansion of gas generation, suggesting a sustained reliance on natural gas that is misaligned with more ambitious climate pathways. For nuclear energy, the 11th Basic Plan indicates a policy-driven expansion, positioning it above the IQR range of Paris-compliant scenarios and reflecting a more proactive national approach. However, this does not necessarily diverge from global mitigation scenarios, as nuclear expansion is also featured in several scenarios aligned with global mitigation scenarios. Meanwhile, renewable energy projections under the 11th Basic Plan generally align with the median trajectory of Paris-compliant scenarios, indicating consistency in the planned scale of solar and wind deployment.

Overall, Korea’s electricity strategy under the 11th Basic Plan aligns with global mitigation scenarios—particularly in the planned phase-out of coal and the scaling up of renewables. However, it diverges through continued expansion of gas capacity and a more ambitious nuclear deployment strategy. These findings suggest that Korea’s transition pathway will not only be affected by global norms, but also be strongly shaped by the clarity, ambition, and consistency of national policies.

The observed divergence across technologies reflects more than just economic and technological uncertainties; it underscores the decisive influence of domestic strategic choices in steering the pace and direction of the transition. As such, aligning Korea’s long-term electricity planning with Paris-compliant trajectories will require sustained policy commitment and adaptive governance to respond to both international expectations and evolving domestic conditions.

Tripling energy goals in Korea: alignment with IPCC scenarios

While the COP28 agreement calls for tripling of renewable and nuclear energy capacity, its consistency with Korea’s long-term energy transition pathway has not been systematically evaluated. To address this gap, we examine projected nuclear and renewable capacity trajectories under Paris-compliant and non-compliant scenarios, using Korea’s 2023 actual capacity as the reference point. Although the target years are defined, the agreement does not specify a baseline year from which the tripling should be measured. For this analysis, we adopt 2023 as the baseline year. As most scenarios in the IPCC AR6 database do not report value for 2023 data, we estimate interim capacity levels using linear interpolation between 2020 and 2025.

Figure 5A compares projected nuclear and renewable energy capacities under Paris-compliant and non-compliant scenarios with Korea’s tripling targets, using 2023 capacity levels as the baseline. Nuclear energy capacity (Figure 5A, left) shows limited growth, even in Paris-compliant pathways. Only 4% of scenarios (9 out of 198) achieve the tripling target by 2050. The median nuclear capacity remains at 19.5 GW, which is below Korea’s 2023 actual capacity of 24.7 GW—and even the upper bound of the IQR (13.2–38.9 GW) fall short of the target, indicating that substantial nuclear expansion is not dominant assumption in most global mitigation scenarios.

In contrast, renewable energy (Figure 5B, right) displays robust capacity growth. Approximately 29% of scenarios (57 out of 198) reach the tripling target by 2030. The median renewable capacity in Paris-compliant scenarios reaches 63.8 GW in 2030, approximately 2.4 times the 2023 level. Paris-compliant scenarios also exhibit higher interquartile values than non-compliant ones, reinforcing the central role of renewables in high-ambition decarbonization pathways.

To further explore structural shifts, Figure 5B presents ternary plots of Korea’s electricity capacity mix in 2023, 2030, and 2050. In 2023, scenarios cluster around fossil fuel dominance. By 2030, the downward-right shift in the ternary plot indicates a transition away from coal, primarily driven by declining coal capacity and a substantial increase in renewables. By 2050, Paris-compliant scenarios converge toward renewable-dominant mixes, with fossil fuels sharply reduced and nuclear remaining secondary. Projected shares of fossil fuels in electricity generation are presented in Figure S2, with details provided in Table S7.

These 2050 projections underscore a clear structural shift: in Paris-compliant scenarios, renewables emerge as the core of Korea’s future power system. While fossil fuels experience a sharp decline, nuclear plays a comparatively limited role, even in ambitious pathways—reflecting persistent uncertainty regarding its scalability and deployment potential, despite global tripling commitments. Collectively, these trends emphasize the centrality of renewables in Korea’s decarbonization strategy and raise critical questions about the practical role of nuclear energy in the clean energy transition.

Discussion

Scenario-based analysis has become a critical tool for assessing national decarbonization pathways consistent that are consistent with global climate objectives.33,34 The scientific community has undertaken extensive research to outline pathways aligned with the Paris Agreement, outlining the key characteristics of emission trajectories needed to meet its temperature goal.35 However, global models often face limitations in representing national and subnational contexts, including differences in policy frameworks market structures, technological capabilities, and societal preferences.34 As a result, effective decarbonization requires heterogeneous, context-specific approaches, rather than reliance on a uniform global pathway.19,36,37 While global mitigation scenarios provide a useful foundation and directional guidance, national-level analysis remains indispensable for developing realistic and actionable strategies tailored to local conditions.29,38

In this study, we evaluated the consistency of Korea’s recent Basic 11th Plan and its tripling pledge for nuclear and renewable energy using Korea-specific scenarios from the IPCC AR6 database. We identified both areas of alignment and key gaps. Our analysis reveals a case of partial consistency: while the planned scaling up of renewable capacity aligns broadly with global Paris-compliant trajectories, other aspects of the plan—such as the continued expansion of gas capacity and a slower-than-required coal phase-out—diverge from the patterns observed in low-warming scenarios. According to Korea’s Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), the country aims to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 40% from 2018 levels by 2030 (see Figure S3), reflecting a significant near-term mitigation commitment. However, comparisons with international pledges show that Korea’s plan may fall short of meeting global expectations, particularly in the areas of coal phase-out and natural gas reliance. Aligning Korea’s strategy with global pathways will require re-evaluation of fossil fuel policies and accelerated deployment of renewable and nuclear energy.

This study is among the first to systematically compare Korea-specific scenarios in the IPCC AR6 database with national energy policy. The AR6 database provides a transparent framework for benchmarking national trajectories against global mitigation pathways. However, its limited representation of smaller regions—including Korea restricts its ability for detailed policy alignment. Greater investment in domestically tailored scenario development is needed—not only to guide national energy and climate strategies but also to strengthen Korea’s engagement in international climate science and policy dialogues.

Looking ahead, Korea faces an increasingly complex and uncertain path toward deep decarbonization. The partial consistency between national planning and global decarbonization pathways suggests that Korea’s current strategies may not be sufficient to long-term climate targets. Addressing these challenges will require the development of advanced modeling frameworks that integrate evolving technologies, such as hydrogen or ammonia blending in power plants and the blending of hydrogen or ammonia with LNG or coal. Beyond technical modeling, a more institutionalized collaboration between academia, government, and industry will be essential to ensure that scenario design supports policy relevance, credibility, and public accountability. Equally important is the adoption of ambitious yet evidence-based policies that are informed by realistic system constraints and international best practices. By aligning modeling tools, technological pathways, and climate governance, Korea can move from partial consistency to full alignment with its net-zero goals—advancing both national interests and its credibility as a contributor to global climate leadership.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of this study lies in the representation of emerging technologies in the global scenarios used for comparison. Korea’s 11th Basic Plan includes technologies not represented in AR6 scenarios such as ammonia co-firing in coal-fired power plants and hydrogen blending or co-firing in LNG units, which are not explicitly captured in the IPCC AR6 scenario database. This mismatch between national planning and scenario assumptions may bias the assessment of consistency between Korea’s energy transition pathway and global Paris-aligned trajectories. Future scenario frameworks should incorporate such transitional technologies to more accurately reflect evolving national strategies and to improve the robustness of comparative alignment assessments. Furthermore, we acknowledge that national scenarios should not be assessed solely on the basis of global climate outcome categories, such as those used in the IPCC scenario classification. While these categories are useful for providing a standardized global benchmark, national energy pathways are shaped by a broader set of factors—including country-specific emissions allocation rules, sectoral reduction priorities, and differences in the treatment of non-CO2 greenhouse gases. These dimensions are not fully reflected in the global categorization framework. Therefore, we emphasize that our analysis provides a directional assessment rather than a normative judgment, and future work should incorporate several factors such as emission allocation schemes and different emission reduction levels among various GHGs. Lastly, we recognize the importance of integrating broader system-level considerations—such as demand-side dynamics, fuel import dependency, and energy security—particularly for countries like Korea that have limited domestic fossil fuel resources. Future research should build on this work by incorporating these critical dimensions into national scenario assessment frameworks.

Method details

Scenario selection criteria

This study draws on ISO-level scenarios from the IPCC AR6 Scenario Database to assess long-term electricity capacity trajectories in Korea. Among the full AR6 database, a total of 767 scenarios explicitly report Korea as a distinct regional unit. From this initial pool, we first restricted the sample to scenarios classified into climate assessment categories (C1–C8) based on the AR6 metadata, which indicates that these scenarios satisfy the core methodological and internal consistency requirements applied in the Working Group III assessment. This step resulted in 273 scenarios eligible for further screening.

To ensure comparability across technologies and to avoid distortions arising from incomplete sectoral reporting, we applied an additional variable-consistency filter. Only scenarios that simultaneously reported capacity trajectories for all major electricity generation technologies were retained. Specifically, the analysis required the availability of the following variables:

Capacity|Electricity|Coal|w/o CCS, Capacity|Electricity|Gas|w/o CCS, Capacity|Electricity|Nuclear, Capacity|Electricity|Solar|PV, Capacity|Electricity|Wind.

Scenarios missing any of these variables were excluded, as incomplete technology coverage would undermine cross-technology comparisons and system-level interpretations of Korea’s power sector transition. After applying this consistency criterion, 239 scenarios remained.

Data exclusion rationale and final scenario set

In addition to variable completeness, we conducted a model-level plausibility assessment to ensure consistency with historical national energy statistics and established characteristics of Korea’s electricity system. During this review, scenarios generated by the GEM-E3 model family exhibited an implausible surge in coal-fired power capacity, increasing from approximately 28.5 GW in 2015 to 81.8 GW in 2020. Such a rapid expansion contradicts observed capacity trends and documented policy developments in Korea, which indicate stagnation or decline in coal capacity over this period.

Given the magnitude and timing of this deviation, GEM-E3 scenarios were excluded from the analysis. This exclusion was methodological in nature, reflecting concerns related to data reliability, historical consistency, and sectoral plausibility, rather than a value judgment on the model itself. Following this exclusion, the final analytical dataset consisted of 198 scenarios, which form the basis for all subsequent analyses presented in this study (Figures 1 and 4).

Consistent with prior literature33,39 and established AR6 analytical practices,32 scenarios in categories C1–C3 were grouped as Paris-compliant, as they align most closely with the Paris Agreement’s long-term temperature goals. Scenarios classified as C4–C7 were treated as non-compliant, reflecting pathways associated with higher warming outcomes or insufficient mitigation ambition. This classification provides a transparent and policy-relevant framework for benchmarking Korea’s electricity transition against global decarbonization pathways. This classification enables a structured comparison of national energy system trajectories under different levels of global climate ambition. The validity of this classification was confirmed by the close correspondence between scenario warming categories and projected national GHG emission levels in Korea (see Tables S8 and S9; Figures S4–S6).

Socioeconomic and technological assumptions

To verify the comparability of scenarios, the underlying socioeconomic and technological assumptions across warming categories were examined. Population trajectories were found to be effectively identical across all scenario categories, declining gradually from 51.3 million in 2020 to 48.1 million in 2050, with negligible IQRs (Table S1). This consistency ensures that differences in energy system outcomes between groups are not attributable to divergent demographic assumptions.

GDP assumptions likewise showed high consistency across both Paris-compliant and non-compliant scenarios, with median values starting at approximately 1,527 billion USD PPP in 2020 and increasing to a range of 2,318–2,979 billion USD PPP by 2050 (Table S2). Overlapping interquartile ranges across warming categories confirm that macroeconomic differences do not explain the structural divergence observed in power sector outcomes.

Capital costs for key renewable technologies (offshore wind, onshore wind, and solar PV) were also consistent across scenario categories (Table S3). Carbon prices in the most stringent category (C2) rise substantially over time, reaching a median value of $6,146 USD2010/tCO2 by 2050, with a widening interquartile range reflecting increasing policy uncertainty, while non-compliant scenario categories exhibit comparatively flat and lower carbon prices (Table S4).

As a policy reference point, Korea’s 11th Basic Plan for Electricity Supply and Demand projects a gradual decline in coal-fired capacity from 39.8 GW in 2024 to 21.8 GW in 2038, an increase in gas capacity to 69.2 GW, and modest nuclear growth from 26.1 GW to 35.2 GW (Table S5).

Exclusion of GEM-E3 model scenarios

Scenarios from the GEM-E3 model family were excluded from the primary analysis due to a model-specific anomaly: an implausible increase in Korean coal capacity from 28.5 GW in 2015 to 81.8 GW in 2020, which is inconsistent with empirical energy statistics. To transparently assess the impact of this exclusion decision, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by re-running the relevant analyses with the GEM-E3 scenarios included (n = 41 additional scenarios). Results of this sensitivity analysis are presented in Supplementary Section S2–S5 (Figure S7; Table S10).

2038 target interpolation

To enable direct comparison between scenario projections and Korea’s 11th Basic Plan for Electricity Supply and Demand (which covers the period 2024–2038), scenario data points between the years 2035 and 2040 were linearly interpolated to estimate solar and wind power generation capacity for the year 2038. The 2038 capacity targets specified in the 11th Basic Plan are 77.2 GW for solar PV and 40.7 GW for wind power. For solar PV, 71% (46 out of 65) of Paris-compliant scenarios exceed the 2038 target, compared to only 41% (54 out of 133) of non-compliant scenarios. For wind power, 65% (42 out of 65) of Paris-compliant scenarios reach or surpass the 2038 target, while only 28% (37 out of 133) of non-compliant scenarios do so. These comparisons are visualized in Figure S8, which shows the distribution of interpolated 2038 scenario values relative to the national targets.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

Scenario outcomes are summarized using descriptive statistics, specifically the median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentile), computed across all scenarios within each classification group (Paris-compliant vs. non-compliant, or C1–C7). These statistics are reported for power generation capacity by technology (GW) (Table S6), fossil fuel share in electricity generation (%) (Table S7), and national GHG emissions (MtCO2eq/yr) (Table S8) at five-year intervals from 2015 to 2050. The number of scenarios included in each statistic may vary by variable due to differences in reporting completeness across IAMs. Disaggregated capacity trajectories by Paris-compliant category are shown in Figure S1, and historical and projected fossil fuel shares are illustrated in Figure S2.

Validation of scenario classification via GHG emissions

The validity of applying global IPCC warming-level categories as a proxy for national mitigation stringency was evaluated by examining projected national GHG emissions under each category. As shown in Figure S6, Korea’s GHG emission trajectories show a clear gradient across warming categories: C1 scenarios exhibit the lowest emissions through 2050, while C7 scenarios project the highest. Figures S4 and S5 presents these results disaggregated by Paris-compliant and non-compliant groups, with corresponding numerical values in Tables S8 and S9. This gradient pattern supports the use of IPCC warming levels as a meaningful benchmark for classifying national-level scenario pathways.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

To quantitatively validate the binary classification framework (Paris-compliant vs. non-compliant), Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted on the power generation capacity mixes of all 198 selected scenarios across the period 2030–2050. Each scenario was represented as a vector of technology-specific capacities at five-year time steps. PCA was applied to the standardized data matrix to extract the principal components (PCs) and their associated variance explained. The proportion of variance explained by each PC is shown in the scree plot (Figure S9).

PC1 explains 52% of total variance and primarily reflects the intensity of decarbonization, capturing the structural trade-off between accelerated solar PV deployment during 2030–2045 (positive loadings) and continued reliance on nuclear capacity in 2035 (negative loadings). PC2 explains an additional 15.9% of variance and further distinguishes scenarios by the trade-off between continued solar growth and gas dependence during 2040–2050 (Figure S10).

Confidence ellipses at the 50%, 75%, and 95% levels were plotted in the PC1–PC2 space to visualize the separation between Paris-compliant and non-compliant scenario clusters (Figure S11). Non-overlapping ellipses at the 95% level confirm that the two scenario groups follow structurally distinct decarbonization pathways, supporting the validity of the binary classification approach used in this study.
