Exploring the new characteristics of professionalism in nursing from the perspective of experts: A qualitative Study
Authors: Sakineh Parastesh, Mohammadali Hosseini, Farahnaz Mohammadi-Shahbolaghi, Sadat S.B. Maddah, Abbas Ebadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeRecently, professionalism has been identified as one of the most crucial issues in the nursing profession. Nurses consider that the primary problem that affects professionalism is the neglect of experts in defining and developing indicators of professionalism in nursing. This study examined the characteristics of professionalism in nursing from the perspective of experts.MethodsThis study is the second part of a conceptual analysis of professionalism in nursing (semi-structured interview), which was conducted using a qualitative content analysis method. The study participants consisted of 17 nursing faculty experts, three nursing managers, two head nurses, two experienced clinical nurses, two general physicians, and two hospital managers, who were recruited from six medical science universities. They were selected using purposive and snowball sampling methods, and interviewed. Data were analyzed using a deductive and directed content analysis approach.ResultsAfter data analysis, 954 primary codes, 22 subcategories, and nine main categories were obtained. The main categories (characteristics of professionalism in nursing) included: “academic specialized knowledge,” “continuous pursuit of competence,” “adherence to ethical codes and values,” “professional commitment,” “accountability,” “autonomy,” “advocacy,” “professional interaction,” and “professional self-awareness.” The inclusion of the “professional self-awareness” category in the literature review matrix is an innovative contribution of the present study. This addition served as the foundation for developing a new conceptual model of professionalism in nursing.ConclusionsThe conceptual framework developed in this study can inform policies aimed at attracting qualified individuals to the nursing profession. A clear understanding of the attributes of professionalism, particularly professional self-awareness, is essential for entering the profession. This awareness can foster persistence and job satisfaction among nurses as well as contributing to improved quality of care and enhanced client satisfaction.

Introduction

Professionalism is a fundamental concept in nursing, requiring the development of a distinct professional identity and specific characteristics that enable individuals to enter and excel in the profession.1,2 A “professional nurse” is generally defined as someone who has completed formal education, possesses comprehensive clinical skills, holds a license or certification to practice, and consistently demonstrates adherence to recognized behavioral norms, high professional recognition, and sustained commitment to the profession.2,3 In addition to these core attributes, researchers have emphasized qualities such as inquiry, autonomy, creativity, research orientation, collaboration, service to society, and active participation in professional organizations.4, 5, 6

Given the rapid pace of technological advances, evolving patient needs, and the expansion of interprofessional collaboration, nursing professionalism should be viewed as a dynamic construct that continually evolves. Without a clear and updated definition of its core characteristics, the profession risks misalignment between educational preparation and clinical realities, which could lead to reduced patient satisfaction, decreased nurse retention, and an increased risk of burnout. Therefore, recognizing and understanding these evolving characteristics is not simply an academic exercise, but instead it should directly inform curriculum development, policy making, workforce planning, and strategies to ensure safe and high-quality patient care.2,7

Historically, nursing was considered a semi-profession due to its limited academic foundations, dependence on other disciplines, and insufficient theoretical and research evidence. In recent decades, the elimination of these deficiencies as well as advances in healthcare technology and the establishment of specialized care units have shifted nursing toward full professional status.8 Nonetheless, persistent challenges such as limited professional autonomy, gaps between theory and practice, low public recognition, and enduring societal misconceptions continue to hinder full professional realization,2,8, 9, 10 and they have direct implications for nurse retention, quality of care, and public trust in the profession.2,11

Evidence from various countries indicates that professionalism among nurses often remains at low to moderate levels, and it is associated with limited clinical competence, minimal involvement in decision making, and a sense of professional powerlessness.4,12, 13, 14 Many of these issues derive from the lack of well-defined, culturally grounded indicators of professionalism developed by nursing researchers and validated for specific healthcare contexts.2 Several conceptual models and measurements have been introduced to assess nursing professionalism, such as Miller's model, Hall's Professionalism Inventory, Kramer's Professional Nursing Practices Instrument, and the American Nurses Association standards,3,15, 16, 17 but most were developed in Western contexts and have rarely been validated for Middle Eastern healthcare systems. Professionalism in nursing is inherently influenced by sociocultural values and legal frameworks that are unique to each context, necessitating a localized understanding of its defining characteristics.21 This mismatch between existing frameworks and the realities of contemporary nursing practice underscores an urgent need for updated, culturally adapted assessment tools, and this need is even acknowledged by the original model developers.2 As emphasized by Hall, defining a profession and its core characteristics should be achieved through consensus among members of that profession,15 reinforcing the importance of locally informed frameworks.22 Moreover, the dynamic nature of nursing professionalism, which is shaped by social, cultural, scientific, technological, and contextual changes, requires continuous re-examination to ensure relevance and applicability.2

In this regard, the perspectives of experienced nursing experts are uniquely valuable because they integrate extensive clinical practice, advanced theoretical knowledge, and leadership experience, allowing them to identify nuanced, culturally embedded characteristics of professionalism that may be invisible to novice nurses, patients, or managers. Their insights are essential for bridging the theory–practice gap and uncovering systemic and cultural influences, as well as for defining competencies and values that resonate with the realities of local practice, which other groups cannot fully capture. This expert lens provides actionable insights for designing locally relevant assessment frameworks.1,23 Several studies conducted in Iran assessed nursing professionalism using general instruments,5,8,24 but none specifically explored the characteristics of professionalism among Iranian clinical nurses. By directly engaging experts, the present study addresses a critical gap in the literature, generates empirical evidence for cultural validation, and supports the development of reliable measurement instruments tailored to the Iranian healthcare context.19,23 Our findings can inform nursing education, guide strategies for recruitment, retention, and professional development, and ultimately improve the quality of care, satisfaction of nurses, and public trust. They also provide a foundation for curricular reform, targeted professional training, and enhancing therapeutic communication, patient safety, and satisfaction.25,26

The present study is the second phase of a larger, sequential, exploratory, mixed-methods project. The first phase employed the Whittemore and Knafl integrative review method, which is a modified version of Cooper's five-stage approach,27 to synthesize the literature and identify eight key characteristics of nursing professionalism: specialized academic knowledge, continuous pursuit of competence, autonomy, professional commitment, professional interaction, accountability, adherence to ethical principles and values, and advocacy.2 In the second phase, qualitative interview findings were integrated with the literature review to confirm that the identified attributes were both evidence-based and culturally relevant to the Iranian healthcare context. This methodological integration enhances the credibility and transferability of our findings, ensuring that they address both the theoretical and practice-based gaps identified earlier.28 Ultimately, this study aims to explore and describe the characteristics of nursing professionalism from the perspective of experienced nursing experts, thereby contributing to the development of a culturally informed conceptual framework and offering practical implications for advancing the professionalism of nursing in Iran.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This qualitative study was the second part of a qualitative content analysis study (semi-structured interviews) conducted in a deductive manner as a directed content analysis approach. The research environment was suitable for qualitative research, i.e., the workplaces of the interviewees were real and natural environments.

Participants and setting

The participants included nursing faculty members, members of the national nursing board, officials from the nursing organization, nursing managers, and experienced clinical nurses. The inclusion criteria were: willingness to participate in the study, provision of informed consent, being a nursing professor with experience in teaching or researching professionalism, or being a nurse with substantial experience in clinical practice, management, or recognized expertise in nursing. The exclusion criterion was: novice nurses without sufficient professional experience. Purposeful and snowball sampling strategies were employed to recruit participants with maximum diversity from six universities of medical sciences. To broaden perspectives and enhance the study's rigor, additional data were collected through interviews with two hospital managers and two general physicians from different hospitals, as well as from a data matrix derived from a previous literature review. These non-nurse participants were included due to their extensive professional interactions with nurses making them familiar with nursing roles and responsibilities, offering complementary external viewpoints that enriched and validated the nursing experts' perspectives, consistent with the qualitative research principle of triangulation.29

Following approval from the ethics committee, nursing experts were initially contacted by telephone. After obtaining informed consent and arranging the interview time and location, interviews were conducted. At the end of each session, participants were invited to recommend other eligible experts for subsequent interviews. Data collection

Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, data were gathered through detailed, semi-structured interviews conducted in two phases: March–November 2019 and May–August 2022. Nursing experts were identified through the primary researcher's extensive experience, spanning over 29 years in education, clinical practice, university administration, and nursing organizations, as well as input from four research team members with expertise in nursing and qualitative content analysis. To maintain consistency, all participants were asked similar semi-structured interview questions. Before starting the interview, the purpose of the research was explained to the participants. Interviews were recorded, and field notes and memos were used to supplement the data collection process.

Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached, which occurred after 28 interviews. All interviews were audio-recorded using a digital recorder and independently transcribed verbatim by two members of the research team, with each interview lasting between 21 and 86 min.

Data analysis

The data were manually analyzed using a deductive approach following directed content analysis, as outlined by Elo and Kyngäs.30 Consistent with their methodology, a data integration matrix derived from the integrative literature review2 was employed as a guidance framework to systematically organize, compare, and synthesize codes and themes. This strategy enhanced the rigor of data triangulation and strengthened the trustworthiness and credibility of the findings. Deductive content analysis employs a structured or unstructured matrix based on previous research and involves three phases: preparation, organization and summarization, and reporting.30 During the preparation phase, interview transcripts were typed and read repeatedly to gain an overall understanding. In the organization and summarization phase, two members of the research team coded and condensed the data into thematic units. These codes were then mapped onto the matrix derived from the integrative literature review according to their similarities. New subcategories and categories were created to accommodate novel codes. Reliability and validity

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data, Lincoln and Guba's four trustworthiness criteria were employed, i.e., credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, corresponding to validity and reliability in quantitative research.31,32 Credibility was strengthened by recruiting participants with maximum diversity and providing interview transcripts for their review and feedback. Interview texts were read repeatedly to enhance their understanding. Dependability was ensured through consultation with four experts, consistent questioning, and the use of field notes and memos. Confirmability was achieved by allocating sufficient time for data collection until saturation, clearly communicating the study objectives, thoroughly documenting research process, incorporating participants' quotations, and conducting team discussions about the analysis. Transferability was addressed by seeking input from four additional nursing experts on the findings and by providing a comprehensive description of research procedures in the final report.

Results

Demographic details of participants

Of participants, 18 were women and 10 were men, with a mean age of 49 years. Overall, 53 % held a PhD, 11 % a BSN, and 50 % were faculty members. In addition, 82 % had more than 20 years of work experience. Table 2 presents the detailed individual characteristics of the participants.

Main results

Based on the data analysis, 954 primary codes, 22 subcategories, and nine main categories were obtained (Table 4).

Category 1: possession of specialized knowledge

From the participants’ perspective, one of the key characteristics of professionalism was holding a university degree and possessing specialized nursing knowledge. The codes derived from this category were organized within the literature review matrix under two subcategories.

Possession of academic education

Most participants emphasized that formal academic education before entering the profession and completing postgraduate education during service are essential for becoming a professional nurse. “The first criterion of professionalism is academic education” (P6). “When a job is called a profession, it must, first and foremost, be grounded in academic education, and this academic education should be able to be continued” (P15).

Possession of a body of specialized knowledge

According to this subcategory, the participants believed that a professional nurse must acquire and apply specialized knowledge to provide effective responses to patients’ care needs. As one participant noted, “Professionalism has a dimension of knowledge and understanding. We should possess and apply this scientific body and specialized knowledge” (P4). Another emphasized, “To be a scientific nurse and to act based on nursing science is a sign of professionalism” (P17).

Category 2: continuous pursuit of competence

Most participants emphasized the need to continuously maintain professional competence by updating specialized nursing skills, clinical reasoning, and creative thinking. The data analysis for this category was organized within the data matrix under the following three subcategories.

Maintaining professional competence for quality care

According to the experiences of most participants, another characteristic of a professional nurse is possessing the necessary qualifications to deliver high-quality and evidence-based care within their respective field. As one participant stated, “Regarding the specific indicators of professionalism, the question of competence arises. For example, a nurse must have the required skills relevant to the department to provide proper care for an emergency patient in the emergency department” (P13).

Participation in continuing education programs

This subcategory highlights the need for professional nurses to participate in in-service training courses to maintain their competence. As one faculty member noted, “One of the characteristics of a professional nurse should be the willingness to learn. Requesting to participate in continuous education reflects a person's desire to learn and improve their profession” (P6). Another participant emphasized, “Nurses must receive education specific to the department they work in to remain up-to-date in both skills and knowledge” (P18).

Development of clinical reasoning and creative thinking

The participants believed that a professional nurse is wise and intelligent, and should evaluate physicians' orders in terms of scientific accuracy before implementation. As one participant explained, “A professional nurse must have reasoning knowledge; that is, a professional person is wise, intelligent, and thoughtful” (P3). Another emphasized, “When a doctor orders a vial of medicine, many nurses simply administer it; these individuals are not professionals. A professional nurse possesses clinical thinking and reasoning and does not follow a physician's orders without critical evaluation” (P16).

Category 3: adherence to ethical principles and values

All participants emphasized the importance of delivering nursing care based on professional ethics, values, standards, and relevant laws. The resulting codes were organized within the literature review matrix under the following two subcategories.

Adherence to ethical principles

Data analysis for this subcategory indicated that, from the experts’ perspective, delivering nursing care while upholding the principles of professional ethics and spiritual values is a vital attribute of an experienced nurse. As one participant explained: “In the intensive dialysis unit, I told my colleagues, your work here is not only about providing specialized services; your performance must also be ethical and scientific because we are dealing with human beings. Our professional nursing is inherently ethical—so where does caring, powered by love, originate? It stems from the application of ethics and professional practice” (P1). Another emphasized, “First, regarding ethics, it is not just the principles of professional ethics, there must be spirituality in professionalism; a merely materialistic nurse cannot act professionally, because her clients, i.e., patients, also have spiritual needs” (P2).

Adhering to legal and cultural values

In addition to being aware of professional rules and cultural values, some experts believe that ethical care requires professional nurses to adhere to their implementation. As one participant stated, “In ethical care, one critical aspect is that the nurse knows the nursing rules—having full knowledge of how to perform procedures and implementing them correctly” (P18). Another noted, “In previous years, we followed a standard scientific reference; now many practices are based on policies and guidelines” (P26). Moreover, nurses must respect patients' cultural values. One participant shared, “The first question the nurse asked my patient's companion was: What are his sacraments? What book does he like to have next to him?” (P6).

Category 4: professional commitment

More than half of the participants emphasized the importance of professional commitment. The codes derived from the thematic units were categorized under two subcategories (see Table 4).

Membership in professional organizations

Most participants stated that membership in professional organizations is an indicator of nurses’ professionalism. As one participant remarked, “It is very concerning that many of our clinical nurses do not actively participate in nursing organization elections. Many are not members of the nursing system or fail to renew their membership; well, it means they are not professionals” (P3).

Development of positive professional identity and commitment

Some participants believed that by delivering competent performance, professional nurses establish nursing as a discipline defined by expertise and skill, thereby fostering a positive professional identity, gaining public trust, and demonstrating a strong commitment to their work and service for the health of individuals and the community. This professional commitment encompasses respect for the role and a genuine concern for patient care and the community's well-being. As one participant explained, “Nurses must create social prestige for the profession and introduce nursing to society only by providing professional work and service” (P1). Another stated, “Now, the great skill of nurses has significantly changed people's perception of nursing compared with before” (P15). Furthermore, one participant emphasized, “If you don't hear the people in a nurse's words or see any sign of it, this nurse is not a professional; therefore, the people and clients must be reflected in our words as we work for them” (P3). Another remarked, “If a nurse wants to be professional, she says that I chose the nursing profession because I want to have a role in society and fulfill my duty as a human being” (P2).

Category 5: accountability

The participants’ experiences related to accountability were categorized into the following two subcategories.

Responsibility

The analysis of participants’ experiences indicated that nurses are accountable for fulfilling their professional duties. As one participant stated, “In my opinion, having a sense of responsibility and morality is inherent to being human; with someone who possesses responsibility, a supervisor does not need to monitor her work—she is her supervisor. In short, we accept responsibility and commit to it, and must adhere to it ourselves” (P27).

Being accountable

The analysis of experts' experiences revealed that professional nurses are expected to respond promptly to patients' care needs and be accountable for the outcomes of their performance. As one participant explained, “For example, when a child's mother says that my child's vein is damaged, several nurses respond by asking her to wait a moment. If a nurse acts immediately and addresses the patient's need, she can satisfy the family and this reflects professionalism” (P20). Another participant stated, “Every person is responsible for every action they perform from the moment they wake up. Considering the responsibility and commitment they hold, if an error occurs, they must be accountable” (P17).

Category 6: autonomy

Based on the data obtained from the interviews, participants believed that a professional nurse is capable of independently assessing patients, planning, and implementing appropriate nursing care grounded in current research theories and evidence. In addition, the nurse can perform self-evaluation and take corrective actions to enhance her performance. The codes within this category were organized into the following three subcategories.

Application of theory and research in practice

Analysis of participants' experiences revealed that the application of theoretical knowledge, nursing theories, and research evidence in patient care is considered a professional behavior. As one participant stated, “I worked as a staff member in a foreign country for three years. We were required to write and implement the nursing process at the bedside; we also had to teach and document. This is professional behavior” (P6). Another participant emphasized, “We can only claim professionalism when we can apply theories in practice” (P15). Some experts highlighted that a professional nurse should utilize scientific evidence from research in clinical settings. One participant noted, “We do not consistently practice evidence-based care; for example, many master's theses have been completed, and their findings should be implemented in the clinic. Applying research results in clinical practice is a hallmark of professionalism” (P13).

Possession of independence of performance

From the participants, perspective, a nurse's ability to independently provide care based on her nursing knowledge indicates professional power, authority, and autonomy within the scope of her role. As one participant stated, “Another crucial aspect of professionalism is independence; therefore, if someone wants to act professionally, she must be able to perform independently” (P14).

Self-regulation

From the participants’ perspective, a professional nurse is capable of evaluating her performance as well as that of her colleagues and taking corrective actions based on the results. As one participant stated, “To become professional, we must periodically self-evaluate. Nurses should assess their knowledge, attitudes, and performance in key professional areas and transform the outcomes of this evaluation into improvement projects, thereby achieving professional accreditation” (P3).

Category 7: advocacy

The experts stated that nurses advocate for the rights of their patients, colleagues, and themselves by utilizing their legal knowledge. Thus, in addition to supporting patients, professional nurses must protect themselves and colleagues given their potentially high occupational vulnerability. The participants’ experiences regarding advocacy were categorized into the following three subcategories.

Patient support

Nursing experts emphasized that a nurse's supportive role involves striving to meet the care needs of patients and preventing harm. As one participant stated, “Supporting the patient is one of the crucial roles of nursing, encompassing maintaining patient safety and providing high-quality care; a professional nurse is someone who embodies the role of a mother as much as possible” (P3). Another participant noted, “Patient support is an integral part of our professionalism” (P9).

Self-support

The subcategory of self-support emerged as an innovative finding in the integrated literature review phase and was further strongly emphasized by participants during the qualitative interview phase. The participants stated that professional nurses should strive to maintain their physical, mental, and spiritual well-being. As one participant expressed, “In my opinion, a professional nurse is someone who is highly attentive to her physical health … possesses social well-being, and also prioritizes mental health, which includes undergoing periodic psychological check-ups” (P3). Another participant emphasized, “I consider attention to spiritual health as one of the crucial components of professionalism for a nurse. It is essential to care for one's spiritual well-being” (P20).

Colleague support

The analysis of participants' experiences revealed that professional nurses play a supportive role for their colleagues through training, monitoring, and guidance. As one participant recounted, “When I administered the wrong medication, my colleague supported me without hesitation. He inquired about the patient's condition, adjusted his position, and advised me to monitor the patient's vital signs every 15 min. I felt supported, and I also learned that if a similar situation occurs with my student, I should not blame her, but instead calm the environment and prioritize the patient's care” (P10).

Category 8: professional interaction

Participants emphasized various aspects of professional interaction, which were categorized into three subcategories within the data matrix: “patient communication,” “interprofessional communication,” and “interdisciplinary communication” (Table 4).

Communication with the patient

Effective communication with patients and their families was one of the subcategories that emerged from the data analysis, and it was emphasized by most participants. Indeed, effective communication was identified as one of the most crucial characteristics of a professional nurse. As one participant stated, “An essential aspect of nursing professionalism is communication; a nurse must establish proper and effective communication with the patient. Without establishing appropriate communication and obtaining comprehensive and accurate information from the patient, the quality of care will be compromised” (P19).

Interprofessional communication

This subcategory refers to effective communication with nursing colleagues and the maintenance of their privacy as a component of professional behavior. As one participant described, “A proper relationship with a colleague means valuing the colleague and the student from the very beginning of their entry into the department. The individual feels respected simply because she is a nurse, and this instills a sense of sacredness toward the profession” (P10).

Interdisciplinary communication

Most participants regarded teamwork skills and the ability to interact effectively with physicians, medical staff, and support personnel as essential professional characteristics. As one participant stated, “Communication with other clinical staff, laboratory colleagues, doctors, and other team members is vital. A nurse with strong communication skills manages patients more effectively. They can coordinate timely physician visits by communicating well. Establishing proper communication with all members of the healthcare team is a crucial professional behavior” (P18).

Category 9: professional self-awareness

One of the novel findings of the current study, and acknowledged by more than half of the participants, was the emergence of a new category called professional self-awareness. This category comprises two subcategories: personal self-awareness and professional nursing awareness. These subcategories were not identified in the previous review study and were added to the existing matrix (Table 4).

Self-awareness

Regarding this subcategory, participants emphasized that a professional nurse should be aware of the personal characteristics required for nursing practice and actively strive to strengthen these attributes. As one participant explained, “It means having self-awareness; a professional person is someone who has achieved self-awareness. In my opinion, a professional nurse is extremely honest with herself, does not deceive herself, and can withstand stress. The most significant aspect of unprofessionalism is the inability to understand oneself and one's public persona” (P3). Another participant stated, “A professional is someone who knows herself—that is, understands what qualities she possesses, which traits help her become professional, and which obstacles hinder her professionalism” (P7).

Professional nursing awareness

In addition to self-awareness, the participants stated that one of the characteristics of a professional nurse is professional nursing awareness, which involves a thorough understanding of working conditions, job descriptions, and the essential attributes of a professional nurse. As one participant explained, “A nurse at various levels should know their role. Therefore, professional self-awareness becomes a component. Know her professional self. I cannot perform my professional role well until I know my duties” (P5). Another participant added, “Now, if you ask a nurse to define nursing, see, many cannot define her profession, and she is unfamiliar with her seven roles; that is, we must be aware of the description of my duties and refer to the description of general and specific duties. This is professional behavior” (P3).

Discussion

This study explored nursing professionalism through the perspectives of recognized nursing experts, revealing nine core characteristics. All except professional self-awareness aligned with our literature review matrix,2 highlighting both the consistency of foundational professional attributes and the emergence of novel, context-sensitive dimensions shaped by local expertise. Gathering insights from experts with extensive clinical, academic, and managerial experience who provided a nuanced understanding enabled a deeper conceptualization of professionalism beyond existing models.

Characteristics such as possession of specialized knowledge, continuous competence development, accountability, advocacy, and professional interaction were recognized in previous studies,2,6,10,33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39 but our expert interviews highlighted notable variations in emphasis according to role, education, and experience. Nursing professors, particularly those with advanced academic and clinical backgrounds, emphasized ongoing competence, ethical accountability, self-advocacy, advocating for colleagues and patients, effective nurse-to-nurse communication, and mentoring as central to sustaining professionalism. Nurse managers focused on accountability, institutional governance, and promoting supportive practice environments, whereas clinical nurses highlighted patient-centered advocacy, autonomous practice, and the development of professional identity through reflective engagement. These role- and experience-sensitive perspectives demonstrate that the interpretations and applications of these core professional attributes are contextually shaped, reinforcing the importance of integrating expert insights to refine and contextualize the conceptualization of nursing professionalism.

By contrast, the “adherence to ethical principles,” “professional commitment,” and “autonomy” attributes were refined during the qualitative interview data analysis process through the merging of certain subcategories, resulting in a more streamlined and conceptually coherent structure. Role-, experience-, and education-sensitive variations also emerged across key characteristics, which are further discussed for each attribute concerning professional role, years of experience, and educational degree in the following.

The “adherence to ethical codes and values” characteristic was described as complex and context-dependent, integrating ethical principles with legal, cultural, and spiritual norms.2 Nursing educators with extensive experience conceptualized this characteristic as a commitment to justice, beneficence, autonomy, and professional fidelity, bridging codes, laws, and cultural expectations. By contrast, clinical nurses emphasized patient-centered behaviors, such as respect, honesty, and trust, whereas nurse managers focused on fairness, transparency, and policy-based equity. General practitioners and hospital administrators tended to prioritize regulatory compliance and risk mitigation. Senior and highly educated staff tended to favor abstract, universal ethical concepts, whereas less experienced nurses relied on procedural rules, reflecting differences in socialization, role expectations, and exposure to ethical discourse.40, 41, 42 These variations illustrate how academic and managerial roles align with formal ethical frameworks, whereas frontline practice anchors ethics in patient-centered care. Principles and values in nursing ethics are culturally situated and socially constructed, emphasizing the importance of context-sensitive ethics education and supportive institutional environments.43, 44, 45

Professional commitment reflects a dual pathway comprising organizational engagement, which empowers nurses, and an internalized professional identity that fosters societal trust.2,46,47 Emphasis on each subcategory varied by expert role, academic degree, and years of experience, where clinical nurses saw membership in professional organizations as a path to autonomy and identity development through patient care, educators emphasized both membership and identity formation in mentoring novice nurses, and nurse managers linked these dimensions to institutional credibility, policy influence, and strategic governance. It was notable that general physicians and hospital administrators did not provide specific codes regarding this characteristic. Professional identity was seen to emerge during formal education, expand through clinical exposure, and strengthen with experience gained and reflective practice.35,44,48 Within the sociocultural context, nurses’ commitment was further shaped through enhancing the public image of nursing and demonstrating dedication to professional values.5,49

Participants described autonomy in various ways. Clinical nurses applied knowledge and judgment to act independently, evaluate outcomes, and guide themselves and their colleagues. Nursing educators, particularly those with advanced academic and clinical backgrounds, emphasized that autonomy is considered essential for fostering critical thinking, evidence-based practice, and mentoring. Nurse managers framed autonomy in terms of independent professional decision making within organizational protocols. Again, general physicians and hospital administrators did not provide specific codes regarding this characteristic. These role-, education-, and experience-sensitive perspectives indicate that professional autonomy relies on both theoretical knowledge and practical judgment, although hierarchical constraints may limit its full expression in clinical practice.2,10,46,50,51 Similarly, Konukbay emphasized that applying theory and research is essential for maintaining independence in professional duties.52

The ninth characteristic, “professional self-awareness,” was added to the literature review matrix,2 and it represents an innovative finding that is absent from existing nursing professionalism models and tools. This characteristic encompasses two subcategories: “personal self-awareness” and “professional nursing awareness.” Clinical nurses highlighted personal self-awareness by recognizing traits such as honesty, optimism, stress and criticism management, patient-centeredness, and adaptability to work conditions and shifts. These findings are consistent with the studies by Azemian et al.,46 and Younas et al.53 who highlighted the importance of personal qualities and awareness of one's abilities and limitations for effective stress management.

In the professional awareness subcategory, participants stressed conscious choice of the nursing profession, understanding its societal role, duties, and career advancement criteria. Nursing educators with extensive academic and clinical experience emphasized the importance of this awareness for mentoring and shaping novice nurses. By contrast, nurse managers associated it with role clarity, accountability, and organizational expectations. General physicians and hospital administrators did not provide specific codes regarding this characteristic. Overall, these findings suggest that professional self-awareness develops through personal reflection, formal education, clinical exposure, and reflective practice, playing a critical role in fostering competent, ethical, and adaptive professional behavior. Previous studies indicate that nurses who lack adequate professional knowledge may consider leaving the field.49 Similarly, Balogunt54 and Younas et al.53 emphasized the importance of understanding professionalism and professional duties for effective care delivery.

In the present study, the key characteristics of professional nursing were identified, highlighting a novel emphasis on professional self-awareness, with clear implications for clinical practice. Promoting these attributes strengthens ethical judgment, autonomy, accountability, and patient-centered care. Professional self-awareness allows nurses to recognize their strengths and limitations, fostering reflective practice, adaptability, and effective stress management. When integrated through targeted education, mentorship, and ongoing professional development, these attributes can enhance clinical performance, patient safety, and team collaboration, thereby reinforcing a culture of professionalism in healthcare.2,40, 41, 42,53

Another important result obtained in the present study was the development of a new conceptual framework comprising nine characteristics of professionalism in nursing, organized into three main dimensions: cognitive, behavioral, and affective (Figure 1), based on Mottian's (2014) framework,55 which has been widely supported by recent studies.34,35 We conceptualize nursing professionalism as a multidimensional construct where the cognitive domain encompasses specialized knowledge, continuous competence development, and the innovative characteristic of professional self-awareness, which is not explicitly examined or highlighted in existing models.2,38,50,51 The behavioral domain includes adherence to ethical principles, accountability, autonomy, advocacy, and professional interaction, reflecting observable professional conduct. The affective domain, exemplified by professional commitment, captures the underlying attitudes and motivations that sustain long-term engagement and ethical dedication. Importantly, our findings show that the expression of these characteristics is shaped by sociocultural and organizational contexts, demonstrating that professionalism is dynamic and socially constructed.56

Limitations

This study is the first to explore the perspectives of nursing experts and healthcare colleagues who are familiar with nursing roles and responsibilities regarding professionalism criteria in nursing within Iran. A primary limitation is that the interviews were limited to experts affiliated with universities in only two provinces. However, efforts were made to include nursing experts from diverse ethnic backgrounds across all relevant medical universities in Tehran and Rasht. Future research should expand to include nursing experts from additional provinces to improve the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study explored the characteristics of a professional nurse from the perspective of nursing experts. The findings highlight eight key attributes of nursing professionalism that are commonly recognized in the literature, which can be categorized into cognitive, behavioral, and attitudinal dimensions, as well as a novel emphasis on professional self-awareness. The participants expressed concerns that many nurses enter the profession primarily for financial reasons, often without sufficient evaluation of essential personal qualities or an adequate understanding of the profession, which highlights the need for targeted interventions by policymakers and stakeholders. In particular, crucial steps include assessing critical personal characteristics during university admission and ensuring comprehensive education about the profession.
