Near-complete telomere-to-telomere genome assembly of a male barbel steed (Hemibarbus labeo)
Authors: Wei Liu, Ming Qi, Wenrui Shi, Xiaofeng Zhang, Gaohua Yao, Qinghui Meng, Chen Chen, Tianlun Zheng, Yongyi Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe barbel steed (Hemibarbus labeo), an economically important freshwater fish in China, belongs to the order Cypriniformes and the family Cyprinidae. At present, research has been conducted on the basic biology, artificial breeding, aquaculture, and nutritional requirements of H. labeo. However, genome assembly and exploration of the unique genomic composition of the barbel steed are still in their infancy. Previously available genome assemblies were fragmented and incomplete, hindering advances in genomic research and breeding applications. In this study, we generated a near complete telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assemblies for a male H. labeo using PacBio HiFi, ONT ultra-long, and Hi-C data. The resulting assemblies span 1,040.46 Mb with contig N50 of 41.15 Mb, and 25 chromosomes were assembled. The quality value (QV) scores were 60.02, and Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) completeness scores were 98.3%. The genome contains a total of 37,023 coding genes, of which 35,589 (96.13%) were functionally annotated. The high-quality near-T2T assemblies provide invaluable resources for molecular breeding and advancing biological and evolutionary research in H. labeo.

Background & Summary

Barbel steed (Hemibarbus labeo) (Fig. 1) is a freshwater bottom dwellers cyprinid living in streams and feeding on aquatic insects and mollusks, and also ingest algae, plant detritus, small shrimp, and smaller fish. Distributed in major drainage of East Mainland China, Japan, and Korea. It has both ecological and economic value and is an important target for the conservation of river fish diversity. It belongs to the family Hemibarbus, the body is laterally compressed and rod-shaped, with a large head, pointed snout and rounded abdomen. H. labeo has often been misidentified as its closely related sister species, H. maculatus. Adult H. labeo have no spots on their sides, only small individuals have inconspicuous black spots, while H. maculatus have 8–14 round dark brown spots on their body sides1. Because of H. labeo are tender and delicate, with a delicious flavor, the content of unsaturated fatty acid DHA reaches the level found in deep-sea fish, offering high nutritional value and health benefits, the species has been an important farmed fish in China2–4.

Barbel steed, H. labeo collected from Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China.

At present, several genome assemblies of H. labeo have been previously reported, but they are all limited to the chromosome level and contain numerous gaps and incomplete telomeric sequences5,6. Incomplete genome data is like a ‘treasure map’ with missing key areas, which can seriously mislead or even hinder the progress of scientific research. In recent years, the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technology and their gradual reduction in costs has made large-scale genome sequencing and assembly feasible in non-model taxa7–9. In this study, we used a combination of PacBio HiFi, ONT ultralong, Hi-C reads and RNA-Seq sequencing data to assemble and annotate the H. labeo genome. The results revealed an assembled genome size of 1,040.46 Mb with 25 chromosomes were assembled. The completeness of the genome assembly was assessed using a number of parameters, which include scaffold N50 score (41.15 Mbp), BUSCO score (97.5%), QV score 60.02. In addition, a total of 22,518 high-confidence protein-coding gene loci were identified, among which 20,143 (89.5%) were successfully annotated in public functional databases. The assembled chromosome-level genome offers invaluable resources for molecular breeding, functional genomics, and evolutionary studies of H. labeo and other Cyprinidae species.

Methods

Ethics statement

The experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Science Technology Department of Zhejiang Province for Zhejiang Fisheries Test and Aquatic Disease Prevention Center [SYXK (Zhe) 2025–0015, 13 Jun 2025].

Sampling and sequencing

A sexually mature male individual of H. labeo was collected from Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China. Tissue specimens, including muscle, liver, and intestine, were dissected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for nucleic acid extraction. Genomic DNA was isolated from muscle using the Blood & Cell Culture DNA Kit (Qiagen, 13323). DNA quality was evaluated via NanoDrop (Thermo Fischer Scientific, ND2000), Qubit(Invitrogen, Q33238), and agarose gel electrophoresis. Total RNA was extracted from six tissue types using the RNAprep Pure Tissue Kit (TIANGEN, DP431). RNA integrity, concentration, and purity were assessed using the NanoDrop (Thermo Fischer Scientific, ND2000) and Angilent 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument (Agilent Technologies, G2939BA).

Library construction, and sequencing

A multi-platform sequencing strategy was adopted for genome analysis of H. labeo.

For PacBio HiFi sequencing, a SMRT bell library was constructed using a SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA). The library preparation process involved DNA shearing, purification with AMPure PB Beads, removal of single-stranded DNA overhangs, damage repair, end repair, ligation of hairpin adapters, and final bead-based purification. After passing quality control, the library was loaded onto a PacBio platform (Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA). HiFi reads were generated using SMRTLink software (https://www.pacb.com/support/software-downloads). The final dataset consisted of 2,370,484 HiFi reads, totaling 40.36 Gb of sequence data, with an average read length of 17.03 kb and an N50 of 16.58 kb (Table 1).

For ONT ultra-long sequencing, libraries were prepared with the SQK-LSK109 Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) and sequenced on Nanopore PromethION sequencer. Raw data were processed using Porechop (version 0.2.4, https://github.com/rrwick/Porechop) to remove adapter sequences, followed by Filtlong (version 0.2.1, https://github.com/rrwick/Filtlong) with parameters “-min_length 30000–min_mean_q 90” to filter out low-quality reads. This yielded 71.88 Gb of high-quality ultra-long reads with an average length of 61.50 kb (Table 1).

The Hi-C library were generated through the following steps: crosslinking cells with formaldehyde, digestion with the restriction enzyme MboI, end-filling and biotin labeling, ligation of blunt-ended fragments, purification, and random shearing to 300–500 bp fragments10,11. Library quality was checked using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, an Agilent 2100 instrument (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA), and q-PCR. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was subsequently performed on an Illumina Novaseq platform. To eliminate reads with artificial bias (e.g., low-quality paired reads resulting from base-calling errors, duplicates, or adapter contamination), we filtered out reads containing >3 unidentified nucleotides (N), reads aligning to adapters, and reads with ≥20% of bases having a Phred quality score ≤5. A total of 397,797,464 raw reads were generated, resulting in 119.20 Gb of clean data for pseudochromosome assembly (Table 1).

Genome assembly

Whole-genome assembly was performed using Hifiasm (version 0.25.0, https://github.com/chhylp123/hifiasm)12, integrating HiFi, ONT, and Hi-C data. The initial ONT assembly was polished by PacBio HiFi reads using Racon (version 1.4.3, https://github.com/isovic/racon)13. The draft assembly was then processed with purge_dups (version 1.2.5, https://github.com/dfguan/purgedups)14 to remove haplotigs and overlaps based on read depth. Remaining contigs were aligned against the NT database using BLAST (version 2.12.0, https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi)15 to identify and remove potential contaminant sequences. A GC-depth plot was used to filter out contigs exhibiting unusually high GC content or low coverage. The preliminary assembly achieved a genome size of 1.06 Gb, consisting of 29 contigs with a contig N50 of 40.80 Mb (Table 2).

Filtered Hi-C reads were aligned to the initial assembly using HiC-Pro (version 3.1.0, https://github.com/nservant/HiC-Pro)16. Valid paired-end reads were used for pseudochromosome construction with 3D-DNA (180922, https://github.com/theaidenlab/3d-dna)17. Manual correction of pseudochromosomes was carried out in Juicebox, guided by Hi-C contact maps. The final contact map was visualized using HiCExplorer (version 3.7.2, https://hicexplorer.readthedocs.io/en/latest/)18,19. To obtain a telomere-to-telomere (T2T) gap-free genome, we applied a custom gap-filling pipeline using Auto Gap Filling software. Gap filling was performed based on synteny analysis between the supplemental genome and the target genome. For each gap, a sliding window (step size: 10 kb) was used to extract 10–100 kb upstream and downstream flanking sequences. Syntenic relationships were identified by aligning these flanking regions to the supplemental genome. Subsequently, the target chromosome and the corresponding candidate contig from the supplemental genome were aligned using MUMmer for each gap to determine the exact filling position. Based on the alignment-derived positional information, the genome was reconstructed to obtain a gap-filled assembly. Contigs from NextDenovo (version 2.5.2, https://github.com/Nextomics/NextDenovo) were aligned to Hifiasm-derived contigs using Winnowmap2 (version 2.03, https://github.com/marbl/Winnowmap)20. ONT contigs located near or spanning gaps were selected for gap filling at the chromosome level. These selected ONT contigs were aligned to the corresponding chromosome sequences using MUMmer (version 4.0.0, https://github.com/mummer4/mummer)21, and gap sequences were filled based on the ONT contig alignments. The final assembly contained 25 chromosomes with a scaffold N50 of 41.15 Mb. Although not all chromosomes have telomeric repeats at both ends, all of them are gap-free, achieving near T2T completeness (Fig. 2; Tables 3, 4). The 25 chromosomes were clearly distinguishable in the heatmap (Fig. 3), and the strong interaction signals along the diagonal further confirmed the high quality of the assembly.

Telomere evaluation, extension, and centromere analysis

Following gap filling, telomeric and centromeric regions were identified. Telomere analysis was performed by scanning the genome for the telomeric repeat motif “TTAGGG”. For chromosomes lacking telomeres or having excessively short telomeric regions, we extended them sequentially using ONT assembly sequences and HiFi reads, followed by re-evaluation. Obtain the specific positional distribution of telomeres on chromosomes (Table 5). Centromeric regions were characterized by identifying continuous clusters with high densities of short tandem repeats and low gene densities. Centromics(https://github.com/ShuaiNIEgithub/Centromics) software was used for centromere prediction based on HiFi reads and the final assembled genome. For some chromosomes, no centromeric signals were detected. These instances may correspond to non-canonical satellite repeat regions often containing complex insertions of multiple satellite families, long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons, or other uncharacterized transposable elements that fall beyond the detection capability of tandem repeat–based tools. Presumed centromeres were successfully identified on 20 chromosomes, whereas five chromosomes lacked definitive centromeric signals (Fig. 4; Table 6).

Assessment of the genome quality

To evaluate the accuracy of the final genome assembly, HiFi reads were aligned back to the assembly using minimap2 (version 2.26, https://github.com/lh3/minimap2)22. Depth and GC content were calculated in 10-kb windows using SMAtools (version 1.17, http://www.htslib.org/)23 and bedtools (version 2.2.29, https://github.com/arq5x/bedtools2)24, and a GC-depth scatter plot was generated using R. Assembly completeness was assessed using the BUSCO (version 5.2.2, https://busco.ezlab.org/)25 pipeline with the appropriate conserved gene database. In addition, merqury (version 1.3, https://github.com/marbl/merqury)26 was used to compute the consensus quality value (QV).

Read mapping rate: A total of 2,369,837 HiFi reads (99.97%) were successfully mapped to the assembly (Table 7).

Repeat sequence annotation

A combined strategy using both de novo and homology-based approaches was employed for repeat element prediction. For de novo annotation, MITE-Hunter27 was used to identify MITEs (miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements), which belong to class II transposable elements and are widely distributed in the genome. LTRharvest28 and LTR Finder29 were used to detect long terminal repeats (LTRs) and LTR retriever30 was used to integrate the predictions from both tools. For homology-based annotation, RepeatMasker31 was used to search the genome against the RepBase database RepBase (http://www.girinst.org/repbase) to identify known repeat sequences. Combining de novo and known repeats, RepeatMasker was applied to mask repetitive regions. Additionally, RepeatModeler32 was used to identify other repetitive elements de novo from the repeat-masked genome. A total of 595.19 Mb (57.20%) of dispersed repeats were identified, including DNA transposons (28.81%), LINEs (3.35%), SINEs (0.17%), and LTRs (8.75%) (Table 10).

Gene prediction and functional annotation

Protein-coding genes were predicted using Augustus (v3.3.2)33 and Genscan34. Homology-based prediction was performed using tblastn, miniprot (v0.11-r237), and liftoff (v1.6.3)35. Protein sequences from closely related species—Danio rerio (GCF_049306965.1), Ctenopharyngodon idella (GCF_019924925.1), Cyprinus carpio (GCF_018340385.1), Pseudorasbora parva(GCF_024679245.1), and Ictalurus punctatus(GCF_001660625.3) —were downloaded from NCBI for homology-based evidence. All gene models were merged using MAKER2 (v2.31.10)36. Functional annotation was conducted against NCBI NR, Swiss-Prot, KEGG, Gene Ontology (GO), and eggNOG databases37,38. A total of 22,518 high-confidence protein-coding gene loci were identified, among which 20,143 (89.5%) were successfully annotated in public functional databases. (Table 11).

Quality evaluation of the final assembly

The quality of the final H. labeo genome assembly was evaluated through multiple metrics, including transcript mapping rate, GC-depth distribution, BUSCO completeness scores, QV assessment, and re-mapping of Hi-C data. Collectively, these results confirm the high quality of the assembly. The high contiguity makes it suitable for large-scale genomic analyses. Importantly, the high BUSCO completeness score indicates that nearly all essential eukaryotic genes are present and largely single-copy, a critical requirement for downstream gene annotation and comparative genomics. The consistently high mapping rates observed across different data types (genomic and transcriptomic) not only serve as technical quality indicators but also functionally validate the assembly by demonstrating its ability to accurately represent both raw sequencing reads and expressed gene structures. Thus, this high-quality near-T2T assembly provides a reliable foundation for future studies of gene function, genome evolution, and other downstream analyses.

Technical Validation

The near-complete telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assembly of H. labeo was validated through multiple approaches. Transcripts mapping ratio showed that a total of 2,369,837 reads were mapped to the genome, with a mapping rate of 99.97%. BUSCO (actinopterygii_odb10) assessment showed that 7,028 (97.5%) of the complete orthologs, indicating high genic completeness. All PacBio HiFi reads (40.36 Gb) using Merqury (k-mer size = 21) were used to evaluate the consensus quality value (QV) of the final genome assembly. The genome-wide QV was calculated to be 61.02, corresponding to an estimated per-base error rate of 7.91 × 10−7. GC-depth analysis showed that there are no separate clusters of scattered points in the GC base coverage depth distribution plot, indicating that the sequencing data is free of contamination. These results confirm the high quality and reliability of the assembly.
