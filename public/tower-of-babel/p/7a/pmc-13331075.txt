Predicting health and disease: a conceptual framework for AI in preventive and precision medicine
Authors: Anis Barmada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe conventional medical approach of treating symptoms as they appear with restricted screening often limits intervention to slowing disease progression rather than fully reversing it. A new approach leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) and computational technologies across expanding multimodal biomedical datasets holds the promise to enable predicting actionable future changes in health before symptom onset. This article presents a conceptual framework for a preventive paradigm in precision medicine and healthcare, integrating recent advancements in AI and biomedical datasets. Key remaining challenges facing computational systems, real-world clinical validation and implementation, and preventive interventions together with recommendations and prioritised future directions are highlighted. Such an approach could pave the way for more proactive and preventive medical interventions to effectively address the growing burden of chronic disease.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Data Science, Disease Management, Electronic Health Records, Preventive Medicine

Introduction

The conventional medical approach often relies on recognising presenting symptoms and signs towards the goal of disease management.1 2 Over the years, this approach has undoubtedly ameliorated much suffering. However, such ‘macroscopic’ phase of pathogenesis in many cases reflects accumulating molecular and cellular changes that have resulted in imbalanced and likely irreversible tissue dysfunction manifesting as observable clinical and/or sustained laboratory characteristics.3 4 At such a stage, medical intervention is frequently inherently limited to slowing disease progression rather than reversing its course, translating into a growing burden of incurable chronic conditions. For example, 75% of adults in the USA have at least one chronic condition5 with a similarly increasing disease burden globally.6

Interpreting medical findings and determining the need for treatment based primarily on the presence of clinical symptoms can overlook valuable opportunities for early intervention and disease prevention.7 Further, clinical interpretation of laboratory results that rely on population-based reference values poorly reflects individual characteristics and disease risk.8 As a result, rich data with the potential to reveal reversible preclinical disease states often remain underused, particularly in terms of integration across modalities.9 Indeed, evidence suggests that many disease states exist on a continuum rather than being strictly dichotomous (ie, healthy vs diseased).4 10

To more effectively address persistent and emerging incurable conditions, a new paradigm leveraging multimodal artificial intelligence (AI) may enable prediction of future health changes and facilitate intervention on clinically silent yet actionable processes to halt disease onset. Despite the promise of AI and prior work on applications in precision medicine, the conceptual, clinical and operational considerations for use in preventive healthcare remain comparatively limited, which is particularly relevant in light of recent enabling developments in predictive capabilities. A new approach entails extending and reframing AI applications from personalised treatment, disease stratification and management to preclinical intervention and preventive care. Here, a conceptual framework is proposed for predictive and preventive medicine building on recent advancements in AI (including classical machine learning and deep learning) and multimodal biomedical datasets. Evidence is integrated from representative recently published studies selected as illustrative examples along with clinical applications. Key remaining challenges facing computational systems, clinical validation and implementation, and preventive interventions are highlighted along with recommendations and prioritised future directions.

The conventional medical approach

Since its early days, the practice of medicine has revolved around the diagnostic process1—which condition or disease most consistently explains the patient’s symptoms and signs? For Hippocrates, it is claimed that diagnosing diabetes mellitus meant meeting a few criteria: a history of polyuria, polyphagia and polydipsia coupled with an examination of the patient’s urine, including its sweet taste.11 Today, from the chief concern to the assessment and plan, the comprehensive history and physical exam—together with advanced laboratory, microbiology, imaging, tissue and other studies—equip the physician with rich insights into the patient’s condition as well as overall life.1 This knowledge facilitates formulating evidence-based decisions regarding diagnostic and therapeutic measures to most optimally manage the patient’s condition.

Yet for the urine to taste sweet with classic presenting symptoms (or for blood glucose levels to have a pronounced and/or sustained elevation by today’s diagnostic standards12), the underlying homeostasis must have undergone prior “microscopic” molecular and cellular shifts that are no longer easily reversible. In type 1 diabetes (T1D), for example, these changes include asymptomatic autoimmunity, progressive impaired metabolism and early pancreatic β-cell destruction that can long precede the transition from normoglycaemia to clinical hyperglycaemia.10 Additionally, screening individuals using these same simplistic ‘lagging’ markers (eg, blood glucose or haemoglobin A1C) and restricting testing to those with limited conditions or associations (eg, first-degree relative with diabetes) will miss many patients while diagnosing the rest too late. With this approach, most of our medical interventions become limited to slowing later stages of disease—no longer able to reverse the primary source of pathogenesis nor achieve a curative state. Evidently, soon after being clinically diagnosed, patients with T1D often become dependent on exogenous insulin to maintain their altered homeostasis.10 12

Such a scenario is common to most chronic diseases. Indeed, many of the currently widely used clinical laboratory markers reflect later disease stages after tissue injury and cell death have already occurred.13 For example, such markers include tissue-specific (or tissue-enriched) proteins that are released on cell damage into the blood where their elevation is detected, such as troponin in cardiomyocytes, lipase and other digestive enzymes in pancreatic acinar cells and creatine kinase in skeletal muscle myofibers to name a few. While these tests remain critical in the acute setting, their elevation alone indicates progression to cell damage by an underlying dysfunction that can commonly dispose affected individuals to significant morbidity throughout their lifetime. Instead, recent findings from many studies across medical conditions demonstrate the existence of measurable molecular and cellular changes preceding the onset of symptomatic or acute disease14 15—which should be more optimally leveraged to inform and preempt early medical intervention, thereby better harnessing the body’s natural resilience to maintain health.

The predictive medical approach

To address this gap in the medical approach, clinically predictive tools that can enable defining and monitoring early changes preceding disease onset with opportunities for actionable medical intervention need to be developed and validated (figure 1). Such markers may now be within reach given recent developments in multimodal AI and datasets, and further advancing them into the clinic beyond retrospective testing would mark a paradigm shift in precision16 and preventive medicine.

Particularly over the past few years, there has been an explosion of AI-enabled clinical applications demonstrating the potential utility of such tools to accurately predict various actionable health outcomes (table 1).1417,26 For example, this includes predicting T1D onset from electronic health record (EHR) data in the primary care setting.17 Specifically, the study used 26 EHR predictors—including demographics, clinical symptoms and information, blood tests and healthcare utilisation patterns—from participants younger than 15 years old in the UK followed from their first primary care record. The developed machine learning algorithm (ensemble SuperLearner) predicted nearly 72% of those who would develop T1D in the 90 days before clinical diagnosis (area under the curve, AUC=0.841).17 Given that delayed diagnosis is a main risk factor for life-threatening diabetic ketoacidosis—seen at onset in up to 80% of T1D cases globally27 28 including ~25% in the UK17 28 29 and 40%–60% in the USA12 30 31—predictions enabling preemptive intervention are suggested to reduce associated morbidity and mortality,32 although further studies are needed to confirm direct benefits on clinical outcomes.

In addition to diabetes, AI tools have shown potential to predict a wide range of diseases years before clinical onset. A study on Alzheimer’s disease prediction leveraged retrospective longitudinal EHR clinical data, including medical conditions, symptoms, interventions and laboratory tests.18 Random forest models predicted disease onset up to 7 years before diagnosis (AUC=0.72, which improved to 0.86 after addition of demographics and visit-related features), which may support prevention strategies in the long preclinical period.33 34 In particular, nearly half of dementia cases are potentially preventable based on modifiable risk factors,35 supporting the utility of informed, targeted lifestyle modification. For pancreatic cancer, deep learning algorithms (ie, sequential neural networks, including the transformer model36 applied to time-sequenced disease codes in longitudinal clinical records) predicted cancer occurrence 3 years before diagnosis (AUC=0.71–0.88).19 Such predictions may help address the particularly poor clinical outcomes of pancreatic cancer, which is highly aggressive and typically presents late due to lack of early symptoms.19 37 For instance, patients identified early can be cured through surgical resection of precursor lesions37 38 and emerging personalised targeted therapies hold significant promise.39

Beyond single-disease prediction, a model (termed Delphi-2M) developed recently by modifying the generative pretrained transformer architecture was able to accurately predict future rates for over 1000 diseases simultaneously at any given timepoint up to 20 years in advance.20 The model was trained on data from over 400 000 UK Biobank participants (including previous health diagnoses in EHRs in addition to lifestyle and other information like sex, body mass index, smoking and alcohol use) and further validated on external data of 1.9 million individuals from Danish population registries. The tool’s performance varied, including by disease and time point, with an average AUC of 0.76, which decreased to 0.70 for predictions after 10 years. This performance was comparable to several current single-disease risk tools, including for dementia and cardiovascular disease, presenting an important advancement in multidisease modelling. Direct benefits of AI predictive tools to clinical care across diseases may become increasingly attainable with the continuing rapid advancements in computational technologies and expansion of biomedical datasets; however, prospective validation remains needed to support implementation and establish clinical utility.

These powerful tools can be trained using large multimodal datasets (eg, blood biomarkers and laboratory tests, multiomics including genomic data, microbiome and immune findings, various types of imaging across organs, tissue and histology analyses, clinical observations and notes, as well as environmental, social and lifestyle factors including from wearable biosensors), ideally in representative and diverse longitudinal cohorts encompassing well-documented parameters, such as the NIH All of Us Research Program, UK Biobank and others internationally (online supplemental file 1).

By integrating these high-dimensional data, predictive tools may be prospectively validated to relate indicative subclinical disturbances to subsequent changes in health with a resolution surpassing that of traditional methods and screening.14 25 26 Based on an individual’s past and current data (eg, linked in their personalised EHR), clinically silent changes may be regularly monitored to recognise predictive deviations possibly years prior to the onset of irreversible disease, thereby informing actionable clinical intervention and/or risk factor modification. A ‘funnel’ framework could facilitate broad regular health monitoring and prioritisation of higher risk individuals for advanced testing and timely preventive treatment by leveraging existing and new healthcare systems (figure 2A).

For instance, recent findings have suggested that routine complete blood count (CBC) values at periodic exams might be leveraged as a screening tool for disease onset risk in apparently healthy adults, including conditions such as heart disease, stroke, diabetes, kidney disease and osteoporosis.8 Further, these CBC values define patient-specific reference intervals,8 supporting their use for personalised longitudinal health monitoring and prioritisation. Biosensors can also serve as a valuable source of data for regular health monitoring, particularly given their ability to provide continuous input and capture environmental factors. Notably, recent technological advancements in continuous protein sensing have unlocked new possibilities for real-time biomarker monitoring.40,42 A recent study demonstrated the utility of wearables combined with demographics and routine blood markers for insulin resistance prediction.43 Further, a recently developed sleep foundation model has shown the utility of sleep study data (polysomnography) for accurate prediction of future disease risk, including dementia, stroke, cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease.23 Integration with other modalities such as imaging and omics data may provide complementary insights and increase prediction accuracy. Overall, this approach can promote health maintenance instead of chronic disease management—with potential healthcare economic, social and clinical benefits.

Opportunities for enhanced preventive medical interventions

In order to improve clinical outcomes using such predictive knowledge, medical interventions, both optimised and newly developed, that can restore homeostasis once indicated must also be rigorously tested. Take diabetes again as an illustrative example: on 17 November 2022, the Food and Drug Administration approved the drug teplizumab, an Fc receptor–nonbinding anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody, for asymptomatic stage 2 T1D. In a randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blind clinical trial, the treatment administered to 44 individuals was shown to delay the progression to clinical T1D compared with placebo by a median of 2 years in those at risk for, but who at the time did not have, clinical disease.44 These enrolled high-risk participants were relatives of T1D patients and already with at least two diabetes-related autoantibodies and existing dysglycaemia. By the end of trial, the percentage of diabetes-free individuals in the treatment group was double that in the placebo group.44

This early success is a testament to the promise of presymptomatic treatment to enhance clinical outcomes, but additional steps remain needed to move from disease delay to true prevention. For example, screening only those with immediate T1D relatives will miss the majority—nearly 90%—of people who end up developing the disease.45 Currently, T1D staging system is based on presymptomatic autoantibodies (stage 1) and dysglycaemia (stage 2), followed by symptomatic disease (stage 3). However, even presymptomatic stages can be associated with significant irreversible loss of functional β-cell mass. Further, progression between stages 1 and 2 to clinical stage 3 is non-linear, relying on simplistic markers (autoantibodies and glucose) that limit the ability to estimate true risk—with autoantibodies indicating a bystander immune reaction as opposed to autoreactive T cells that drive pathogenesis.46 Instead, reconsideration of this staging framework has been recently proposed to emphasise T1D risk prediction based on longitudinal multimodal data, including demographic, genetic, metabolic, autoantibody and immune analyses.46 Machine learning models leveraging such biomarkers, which are present up to years before clinical onset, can enable better estimation of progressive risk to determine the optimal window for preventive treatment directly targeting pathogenic T cells (figure 2B).

Further research into such early disease therapies across medical conditions may potentially enable approaches for reversal of disease onset by targeting precise molecular and cellular changes at the root of the disease. Currently, many chronic diseases do not have proven preventive interventions. This partly reflects our historical inability to identify presymptomatic disease, not necessarily the absence of modifiable targets. With ongoing predictive advancements (including AI use in drug discovery,47,49 see online supplemental file 1), future work is better positioned to expand our biological understanding of these processes and potentially accelerate preventive interventions. Yet it is important to acknowledge biological constraints on prevention, including potentially irreversible processes related to ageing, genetic factors and cumulative damage. Accordingly, to maximise the benefits of early detection and preventive intervention, clinical applications must be focused on diseases with presymptomatic stages in which reversible, modifiable targets can be identified.

Outlook, challenges and future directions

The advantages and strengths of AI-powered preventive medicine are clear: these tools may finally enable enhanced synthesis of rich multimodal data to reveal asymptomatic but actionable/reversible changes in health for timely lifestyle and medical interventions. This includes potentially more comprehensive, quantitative and personalised risk prediction coupled with earlier and more effective prevention and treatment. Such a shift from reactive to proactive healthcare has the promise to address the growing burden of chronic disease and need for lifelong management.

At this time, however, these predictive and preventive approaches are still in their infancy. Key remaining challenges must be overcome to fully harness the potential of AI in preventive medicine (figure 3), including those facing (1) computational systems including ethical considerations (online supplemental file 1), (2) clinical validation and implementation and (3) preventive medical interventions.

With respect to computational systems, advancements in multimodal integration are still needed to maximise insights from biomedical datasets. For example, a new contrastive learning approach developed recently to accommodate multiple polysomnography (sleep study) configurations demonstrated the utility of multimodal sleep recordings in disease prediction.23 Yet most AI applications for disease prediction to date have been limited to a single or few modalities (table 1). Additionally, it is important to identify specific disease contexts and associated multimodal data that will enable additive value over single modalities. For instance, in Parkinson’s disease prediction, a model combining several modalities—including accelerometry, genetics, lifestyle, blood biochemistry and prodromal symptoms—was not found to outperform the single accelerometry model.22 This might indicate that these individual modalities are capturing an overlapping set of information to varying degrees, emphasising the need for careful selection of context-specific synergistic data types. Particularly, the advantage of multimodal integration lies in combining data types that capture distinct axes of information (rather than highly correlated ones), as demonstrated across several studies.43 50 Given that combinations can be highly context-dependent, the disease and prediction circumstances, modality complementarity as well as the cost–benefit considerations of data collection must be explicitly evaluated (eg, via ablation or incremental modelling) for added predictive value during model development and validation.

To enable true primary prevention (ie, stop initial disease onset rather than simply prevent related comorbidities), prediction approaches must incorporate indicative biological markers beyond descriptive information of prior conditions in EHRs. To enable such integration across modalities, including active and passive systems spanning high-dimensional structured and unstructured data, standards for clinical infrastructure, data quality and access, storage capacity as well as interoperability must be established.

Despite the accuracy of various AI prediction models, understanding how these tools reach their conclusions remains challenging. Prioritising work on interpretable and explainable approaches might support a stronger foundation to guide health decision-making. For example, explainable AI methods for the multidisease model Delphi-2M provided insights into predictions by revealing comorbidity clusters with time-dependent consequences on future outcomes.20 Lastly, another important consideration for clinical application concerns ethical implications such as bias, generalisability and privacy (see online supplemental file 1). Currently, the majority of AI disease prediction studies were based on US or European cohorts with limited diversity and generalisability (table 1).

Second, although AI tools have shown strong predictive performance, clinical validation and implementation remain at early stages. For instance, multidisease modelling had a performance comparable to routinely used clinical risk scores for dementia and cardiovascular disease.20 However, the predictive performance itself may not necessarily translate into clinical utility or improved outcomes.51 Clinical validation requires model outputs to further correlate with clinically meaningful outcomes across relevant demographic groups. To this end, beyond retrospective analyses, prospective real-world validation will be crucial to establish the clinical utility of these tools, including improving patient outcomes. Barriers to clinical implementation and potential solutions have been discussed recently,52 relating to broad integration within the healthcare ecosystem beyond merely the AI algorithms themselves. While relatively few studies have evaluated these tools in real-world clinical settings, early implementation efforts have yielded encouraging results. For example, real-world studies of AI-supported mammography screening in Europe and the USA have shown improved breast cancer detection at the population level.53,55 Looking ahead, future work must prioritise prospective evaluation to demonstrate the clinical utility of AI prediction tools across diseases. Economic and operational challenges for clinical implementation are further discussed in the online supplemental file 1.

Importantly, for rare diseases and presymptomatic states, contextualisation of model performance in the setting of positive predictive values at different disease prevalence levels is essential to ensure acceptable false positive rates. Recent strategies to reduce false positives include leveraging longitudinal data and dynamic disease risk through context-sensitive screening.56 This approach allows estimating risk based on personalised EHR trajectories to determine when further screening is likely to be informative, enabling more targeted, sequential deployment that improves precision and reduces false positives. Additionally, the proposed framework herein offers advantages of longitudinal, multistep screening to quantify progression risk and certainty during a prevention window (figure 2B).

Lastly, research on early disease processes and targets is essential to advance novel preventive interventions, particularly given that the majority of basic and clinical research efforts currently focus on late disease biology and subsequent manifestations. Importantly, evidence must be generated, including through longitudinal data, identifying reversible (vs irreversible) presymptomatic disease processes to establish prevention feasibility and guide target discovery and testing. To facilitate the development and approval of such preventive strategies, government agencies and biopharmaceuticals need objective biomarkers and targets from AI predictions that are specific and directly associated with early disease progression. These should ideally rely on routine measures to enable population-based health monitoring and prioritisation. Preventive interventions can take many forms, including informed lifestyle modification as well as pharmacologic and biologic approaches. For instance, these may target both extrinsic factors (eg, viral infections implicated in autoimmunity57 or dementia58 in at-risk individuals) or intrinsic factors—either directly by reversing and eliminating early pathological changes, or indirectly by counteracting their effects. Addressing lead-time bias in study designs and minimising the risk of overtreatment as well as the psychological burden of disease prediction are important considerations (online supplemental file 1).

From Hippocrates to medical AI, re-imagining the traditional clinical paradigm is needed for medicine to better match today’s capabilities and to fully capitalise on ongoing technological advancements for improving health. Cutting-edge computational and AI tools together with growing multimodal biomedical datasets have the promise to enable predicting future changes in health with opportunities for preventive medical intervention to effectively address the growing burden of chronic disease.
