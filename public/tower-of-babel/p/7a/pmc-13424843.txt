Socioeconomic factors and time to diagnosis in hidradenitis suppurativa
Authors: Aileen Y Chang, Krittin J Supapannachart, John Boscardin, Jinoos Yazdany, Haley B Naik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiagnostic delay is common in hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), yet the influence of socioeconomic status remains poorly understood. In this cross-sectional study of patients with HS enrolled in a single-institution longitudinal cohort, time to diagnosis was 53% shorter among patients with less than a bachelor’s degree and 73.1% longer among those living in nonmetropolitan or smaller metropolitan areas. Time to diagnosis was not associated with Hurley stage. Larger studies are needed to clarify socioeconomic drivers of delayed HS diagnosis and disease outcomes.

Dear Editor, Patients with hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) face long delays in diagnosis. Educational attainment, income and insurance type shape access to dermatological care,1 and dermatologists are concentrated in population-dense areas.2 Yet, no studies have adequately examined whether socioeconomic factors may influence time to diagnosis of HS, and it is unclear whether diagnostic delay is associated with disease outcomes.3–5 Socioeconomic status (SES) has multiple dimensions and can be measured at the level of the individual, neighbourhood or larger geographical areas. In the few studies that have examined SES, only one dimension of SES was included.6–8 We sought to build on our prior work,7 to evaluate if SES, measured using four different dimensions, is associated with time to diagnosis in patients with HS. In addition, we examined the association of time to diagnosis with Hurley stage.

We conducted a cross-sectional study of baseline visits for participants with HS enrolled in a longitudinal cohort at a single institution with baseline visit between 18 July 2016 and 19 July 2020, and residing in California, USA. We included four SES variables of interest: educational attainment; insurance; census-tract level neighbourhood SES;9 and county-level rural–urban continuum code. Residential address was used to determine the county-level 2023 rural–urban continuum code and census tract-level neighbourhood SES using an index measure of neighbourhood SES that incorporates income, poverty, housing cost, rental cost, education, occupation and employment data.9 We assigned each ­census tract a quintile of neighbourhood SES based on the distribution of index values in California. Time to diagnosis was a continuous variable calculated by subtracting participant-reported age of symptom onset from participant-reported age of diagnosis. Where the difference in time to diagnosis was 0 (i.e. age of symptom onset was the same as age of diagnosis) in 45 patients, 0.5 was used as the time to diagnosis.

Of 269 participants with a baseline visit, 216 (80.3%) had data available to calculate time to diagnosis. Of the 216 participants, we excluded 2 participants with age of onset reported at 2 and 6 years old, due to concerns regarding reporting error. Due to minimal missingness of covariate data, we performed a complete case analysis, leading to an additional 18 participants being excluded. We used Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests to compare time to diagnosis between levels of each covariate. For our analysis evaluating socioeconomic factors and time to diagnosis, we report the exponentiated coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from multivariable linear regression models with the log-transformed outcome, each SES variable as the independent variable in separate models and confounders added sequentially, adjusting first for age at onset, sex, and race and ethnicity, and then adding each measure of SES.

For our analysis evaluating time to diagnosis and Hurley stage at presentation, we report coefficients with 95% CIs from logistic regression models where the binary outcome was Hurley stage [stage I (ref.) vs. stage II–III], the primary exposure was time to diagnosis (log transformed) and confounders were added sequentially.

We assessed for multicollinearity using variance inflation factors to ensure stability of models. We analysed data from 18 November 2024 to 6 November 2025, using Stata 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). STROBE reporting guidelines were followed.

Our study included 196 participants with a median age at baseline visit of 34.5 [interquartile range (IQR) 26.0–45.5] years, of whom 75.0% (n = 147/196) were female patients (Table 1). Median (IQR) time to diagnosis was longer for patients who were younger, female, had a higher educational attainment, and were living in a nonmetropolitan area or metropolitan area with <1 million people (Table 1). In unadjusted ­models, lower educational attainment was associated with longer time to diagnosis. In fully adjusted models, higher educational attainment and living in a nonmetropolitan area or metropolitan area with <1 million people was associated with longer time to diagnosis. Time to diagnosis for patients with less than a bachelor’s degree was 53% shorter than for patients with higher levels of educational attainment, and 73% longer for patients living in a nonmetropolitan area or metropolitan area with <1 million people [Table 2; Table S1 (see Supporting Information)]. A greater proportion of participants without time to diagnosis data were living in a nonmetropolitan area or metropolitan area with <1 million people (Table S2; see Supporting Information). Longer time to diagnosis was not associated with Hurley stage (Table 3).

In this study, participants living in nonmetropolitan areas and metropolitan areas with <1 million people had a 73.1% longer time to diagnosis. This may reflect fewer dermatologists and less HS awareness among generalists outside of metropolitan areas with >1 million people. Alongside efforts to expand dermatology access outside of highly dense urban areas, augmenting HS disease awareness among generalists in dermatology access-limited communities may reduce diagnostic delays. Median time to HS diagnosis was lower than that reported in the literature, which may reflect increased disease awareness since the approval of biologic therapies for HS in 2015.

In contrast with a Spanish study,8 we observed a longer time to diagnosis among participants with higher educational attainment and found an independent association between lower educational attainment and shorter time to diagnosis. This unexpected finding contrasts with prior literature. It may reflect unmeasured factors such as distance to dermatological care if participants with higher educational attainment tended to be in areas with less ­dermatology access, as an HS diagnosis commonly relies on dermatological expertise.10 Another possible explanation is that individuals with higher educational attainment have higher health literacy, which may enable an individual to generate alternative explanations for their symptoms. This could lead some individuals to delay care for HS symptoms.

Time to diagnosis was not associated with Hurley stage. Our findings align with another single-centre study from the USA5 but contrast with a multisite German study and a multisite global study.3,4 This may reflect that Hurley stage was not measured at diagnosis but at baseline study visit, similar to the other study from the USA that assessed Hurley stage at presentation. Our study included some participants who had been diagnosed prior to their baseline study visit and had already received some form of HS treatment.

This study has limitations. Time to diagnosis relied on participant recall, consistent with prior studies,3,4 and 23% of included participants had a time to diagnosis of zero as integer years was collected for ages. The four SES variables and Hurley stage were measured at baseline study visit. There is temporal mismatch in the rural–urban continuum code (2023) and baseline visits (2016–20). The generalizability of the study findings may be limited: ­participants were recruited from dermatology clinics at a single institution, potentially excluding individuals with the longest diagnostic delays; the study population skewed towards higher SES (45% held a Bachelor’s degree or higher, 62% resided in the highest SES neighbourhoods); and uninsured patients were not included.

We found that higher educational attainment and residing outside of the largest metropolitan areas were associated with longer time to HS diagnosis in a racially and ethnically diverse study population with higher SES. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more balanced representation across the socioeconomic spectrum are needed to better understand the influence of SES factors on time to HS diagnosis and how such factors may affect disease severity.
