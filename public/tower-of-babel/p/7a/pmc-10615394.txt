Effects of hip brace on coxa valga in nonambulatory children with cerebral palsy: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hyeonseong Woo, Bo Ryun Kim, Jin A. Yoon, Hyun Jung Han, Young Il Yoon, Seung U. Lee, Seon Cho, Yong Beom Shin, Hyun Jung Lee, Jee Hyun Suh, Jiwoon Lim, Jaewon Beom, Yulhyun Park, Ju Seok Ryu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Coxa valga, measured as the neck-shaft angle (NSA) or head-shaft angle (HSA), is regarded as a potential risk factor for hip dislocation in patients with cerebral palsy. We investigated the effects of a novel hip brace on coxa valga.Methods:A prospective, multicenter, assessor-blinded, randomized controlled trial was conducted from July 2019 to November 2021. Children with cerebral palsy aged 1 to 10 years with Gross Motor Function Classification System levels IV and V were recruited. The study group wore a hip brace for at least 12 hour a day. A lower strap of the hip brace was designed to prevent coxa valga biomechanically. The effectiveness of the hip brace on coxa valga was assessed by measurement of the NSA and head-shaft angle at enrollment and 6 and 12 months after enrollment.Results:Sixty-six participants were enrolled, and 33 patients were assigned to each group. Changes in the mean NSA of both sides and the NSA of left side showed significant differences between the 2 groups over 12 months (mean NSA of both sides, −1.12 ± 3.64 in the study group and 1.43 ± 3.75 in the control group, P = .023; NSA of the left side, −1.72 ± 5.38 in the study group and 2.01 ± 5.22 in the control group, P = .008).Conclusions:The hip brace was effective in preventing the progression of coxa valga and hip displacement, suggesting that the prevention of coxa valga using hip brace is a contributing factor in prevention of hip displacement.

1. Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a heterogeneous group of disorders affecting the fetal or infantile brain and resulting in impaired motor function and posture.[1] Although the recent global prevalence of CP in high-income countries has declined to 1.6 per 1000 live births, CP is still associated with substantial socioeconomic costs to the national health service.[2,3] The estimated lifetime medical cost of spastic CP in Korea was 34,640 US dollars, which was 2.4 times the lifetime medical cost of the general population (14,579 US dollars), in a previous study.[4]

More medical costs are incurred in patients with higher Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) levels.[2] From the viewpoint of the musculoskeletal system, many skeletal deformities and neuromuscular abnormalities develop in nonambulatory patients with CP (GMFCS level IV or V). Pes equinus, acetabular dysplasia, hip displacement, coxa valga, scoliosis, and joint contractures are well-known musculoskeletal problems associated with CP.[5] Although encephalopathy is static and nonprogressive in children with CP, musculoskeletal deformities develop and progress, eventually resulting in pain and decreased quality of life.[6]

Hip displacement is the second most common musculoskeletal deformity, after pes equinus, affecting nearly 35% of the population with CP.[7] The risk of hip displacement is linearly correlated with the GMFCS level, with a high incidence of hip dislocation in severe CP (69% and 90% in patients with GMFCS levels IV and V, respectively).[8] Progressive hip displacement leads to hip dislocation and requires surgery. A census in Scotland showed that 8.4% (138/1646) of patients with CP had undergone hip surgery, and the majority of them had GMFCS levels IV and V (88%, 121/138).[9] Additionally, the risk of postoperative complications after hip surgery is nearly 2-fold higher in patients with nonambulatory CP (GMFCS levels IV and V) than in patients without CP.[10] Conservative management strategies to prevent hip dislocation and surgery have been studied, but previous studies investigating conservative management have shown inconsistent results.[11–13] A recent study showed that a newly designed hip protection brace consisting of 3 straps significantly decreased the Reimer hip migration index (MI) by −3.3% after application for 12 months.[14] Compressing the hip capsule by the brace pushes the femoral head into the acetabulum and compensates for the laxity of the capsule. A decrease in the laxity of the hip ligaments and capsule may be the main contributor to the significant decrease in hip displacement, but other contributors were not investigated yet.

Coxa valga, which is radiologically measured as a high neck-shaft angle (NSA) or head-shaft angle (HSA), is considered a predisposing factor for hip displacement.[15] The mean NSA and HSA showed a stepwise increase according to the GMFCS (NSA, 145.6 ± 7.7; HSA, 156.1 ± 8.4 in GMFCS level IV; NSA, 147.2 ± 8.8; HSA, 161.4 ± 11.0 in GMFCS level V).[16] Another large population-based study suggested that high NSA and HSA are potential risk factors for hip displacement in patients with CP.[15,17]

The new hip brace consists of 3 straps designed to counteract hip dislocation, coxa valga, and hip adduction. We hypothesized that a hip brace can reduce the progression of coxa valga, which is represented by the NSA and HSA. The purpose of this study was to determine the effects of a hip brace on coxa valga and the mechanism for improving hip displacement using a hip brace.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

This manuscript was written in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 guidelines. This study was designed as a prospective, multicenter, assessor-blinded, randomized controlled trial to investigate the efficacy of a new hip brace in preventing the aggravation of hip displacement in patients with CP. This study was conducted from July 26, 2019, to November 30, 2021, at 4 teaching hospitals in Korea (Seoul National University Bundang Hospital, Pusan National University Hospital, Korea University Anam Hospital, and Jeju National University Hospital). The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating institutions, and written informed consent was obtained from the patients or their primary caregivers.

2.2. Participants and randomization

We prospectively recruited patients with CP who visited the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine of the 4 teaching hospitals from July 2019 to October 2020. Patients who were (1) diagnosed with CP, (2) classified to have GMFCS level IV or V, (3) aged 1 to 10 years, and (4) diplegic or quadriplegic were included. Patients who had undergone hip joint surgery or were scheduled for hip joint surgery during the trial period were excluded. Patients who had undergone botulinum toxin injection to the hip muscles within the 3 months before the trial or during the trial period were excluded. Patients who changed antispastic medications during the trial period were also excluded.

As described in a previously published paper,[14] the same number of participants were allocated to both the study and control groups via blocked randomization (random block size 4). The randomization process was conducted by a statistician, and the two outcome assessors were blinded to the group allocation.

2.3. Intervention: hip brace

The hip brace, which consists of inner pants and outer elastic fabric bands, was designed to compress the capsule and ligaments of the hip joints, with the aim to eventually prevent hip dislocation. The fabric bands consisted of upper, lower, and thigh straps, which were designed to prevent hip dislocation, coxa valga, and hip adduction, respectively. Figure 1 shows the direction of the forces exerted by each strap. The upper strap exerts downward and medial compression forces on the hip joints to tighten ligaments and capsules, while the lower strap exerts upward and medial forces immediately below the greater trochanter of the femur. The greater trochanter of the femur was located between the lower margin of the upper strap and the upper margin of the lower strap to maximize the effectiveness of the brace in preventing hip displacement. The upper strap exerts force in the direction of pushing the femoral head into the acetabulum and also reduces the laxity of the hip capsule. And the combination of forces exerted by the upper and lower straps was expected to prevent coxa valga. The thigh strap exerted forces in the direction of hip abduction.

The front and rear part of the hip brace. White arrows represent the direction of the forces exerted by each strap, in the order of upper, lower and thigh strap. GT = greater trochanter; L = lower strap; T = thigh strap; U = upper strap.

The precise clothing pressure was measured using air-injected clothing pressure measurement system (AMI 3037, AMI Techno, Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Clothing pressure was measured at 7 key locations of the several prototypes of the hip brace, and the optimal one was used in this study (see Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K454, which showed the locations of clothing pressure measurement). The measurements were conducted based on a brace prototype for 5 year-old. Three repetitive measurements were taken at each location, and the average value was calculated. The clothing pressure was classified into 4 grades (I, II, III, and IV) according to the compression classes proposed by the European Committee for Standardization (2001) (A higher class exerts higher compression pressure, and Class III or IV can be used as medical devices).[18] In the final prototype, the clothing pressure of the upper and lower strap were class III, while the pressure of the thigh strap was class IV (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K455, which showed results of clothing pressure measurement of the brace).

After randomization, patients in both the study and control groups underwent conventional physical therapy designed for patients with CP. In addition, the study group wore a hip brace for at least 12 hour a day.

2.4. Outcome measures and follow-up assessments

Demographic variables, including age, sex, height, body weight, and GMFCS levels, were collected at enrollment. Hip anteroposterior (AP), bilateral femur AP/lateral radiographs were obtained for the patients in the supine position with bilateral hip and knee maximally extended, without wearing hip braces at enrollment. The same radiographs were obtained 6 and 12 months after enrollment.

The effectiveness of hip brace treatment in the prevention of deformity in coxa valga was assessed by measuring the NSA and HSA. All angles were measured from the radiographs taken at enrollment and 6 and 12 months after enrollment by 2 blinded outcome assessors.

2.5. Radiographic measurement of the NSA and HSA

Measurements of the NSA and HSA were performed using the INFINITT Picture archiving and communication system (PACS) software (INFINITT Healthcare Co., Seoul, South Korea), as shown in Figure 2. The NSA was determined as the angle between the femoral neck axis and the femoral shaft axis in hip AP radiographs.[16] The femoral neck axis was defined as the line joining the center of the femoral head and the midpoint of the narrowest line across the femoral neck. The center of the femoral head was determined using the best-fitting circle of the femoral head. The femoral shaft axis was defined as the line joining the midpoint of the 2 horizontal line segments connecting the medial and lateral borders of the femoral shaft. One was horizontally drawn 25 mm distal to the caudal end of the lesser trochanter, and the other was horizontally drawn caudally at the diaphysis. The HSA was defined as the angle between the femoral shaft axis and the line perpendicular to the proximal physis of the femur.[16]

2.6. Statistical analysis

As we reported in the previous study, the sample size was determined to investigate the effects of hip braces on the MI, which was the primary outcome in the previous study.[14] Considering a dropout rate of 5%, the sample size was estimated as 34 patients in each group (see Data S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K456, which described the statistical analysis plan and sample size determination).

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 27.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and R software (version 4.2.2.; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. To estimate the inter-rater reliability of the measurements of the NSA and HSA, the intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) in the definition of absolute agreement were calculated. The ICC for each variable between the two assessors is presented in Table 1. There were good agreements for the measurements of the NSA and HSA among the 2 outcome assessors (ICC: 0.978 for NSA, and 0.882 for HSA).

Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of the distribution were performed to assess the normality of the continuous variables. Student t test or the Mann–Whitney test was used to investigate the baseline group differences according to the results of the normality tests. A P-value of <.05 indicated statistical significance.

Analysis of the sequential progression of the NSA and HSA was performed using linear mixed-effects models. The baseline angle, age, group, time, and their interaction were used as fixed variables, and patients and centers were used as random effects in the model. All available data from baseline to the 12-month follow-up were used for the intention-to-treat analysis. Differences in the angles of coxa valga between the two groups were estimated using this model. A P-value of <.05 indicated statistical significance. In a post hoc analysis of the model, a Bonferroni-corrected P-value of <.05/3 = .0167 was used to determine statistical significance.

2.7. Ethics statement

This prospective, assessor-blinded randomized-controlled trial (registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under registration number NCT04033289) was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the tertiary university hospital (E-1906-546-002). This study was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

Between July 2019 and October 2020, 67 patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled in the study (Fig. 3). At initial allocation, 33 and 34 patients were randomly assigned to the study and control groups, respectively. Among them, one patient in the control group refused to undergo baseline assessment and decided not to participate in the trial. Consequently, 33 patients were assigned to each group during the final allocation. In the study group, 8 patients ceased to participate at the 6-month follow-up for reasons including brace size problems (3), visit problems (3), and surgery (2), while 5 patients dropped out at the 12-month follow-up because of brace size problems (2), visit problems (1), surgery (1), and improperly obtained radiographs (1). In the control group, one patient discontinued the therapy owing to a visit problem at 6 months, and 3 patients additionally discontinued at 12 months owing to a visit problem (1) and refusal (2). There was no serious adverse events during the trial.

4. Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effect of a hip brace on proximal femoral geometry in nonambulatory patients with CP. The hip brace showed preventive effects on the progression of coxa valga, as measured by the NSA. We previously revealed the effect of braces on the prevention of hip displacement, but the factors contributing to this result were not further investigated.[14] A variety of mechanisms, including increased adductor spasticity, weakness of the hip abductors, and nonambulatory function, are related to coxa valga in patients with CP. These complex mechanisms contribute to coxa valga, femoral anteversion, and eventually hip displacement. The hip brace was designed to target these pathophysiological mechanisms. The upper strap compresses and tightens the hip ligaments and capsules, the lower strap exerts a compression force to prevent coxa valga, and the thigh strap exerts force to prevent hip adduction. We verified that the hip brace was significantly effective in improving the NSA and HSA, indicating its effectiveness in preventing both coxa valga and hip displacement. This study is meaningful, as to the best of our knowledge, it is the first to show improvement in the coxa valga by hip braces.

The degree of hip displacement is highly associated with hip pain in patients with CP, particularly in nonambulatory patients (GMFCS levels IV and V).[19] Coxa valga, represented by high NSA and HSA, is a known risk factor for hip displacement in patients with CP.[16,17] Previous studies have shown that children with CP with higher HSA values have a 1.6-fold higher risk for hip displacement than children with CP with the same GMFCS level, the same age, and the same hip migration percentage.[17] Even in cases of hip reconstruction surgery for children with CP, patients with a higher preoperative NSA showed significantly increased failure rates, resulting in revision surgery.[20] Therefore, intervention to prevent coxa valga is important to prevent hip displacement and further redislocation in patients with CP.

Few studies have investigated the natural history of coxa valga in patients with CP. Non-CP children with high NSA and HSA at age 3 years old showed a continuous decrease in angles (average decrement in NSA, −1.1°/year [95% CI: −1.5 to −0.8°/year]; in HSA, −1.1°/year [95% CI: −1.5 to − 0.7°/year]).[21] One study comparing the evolution of the HSA in CP and non-CP children showed that the HSA of non-CP children and ambulatory CP children (GMFCS levels II and III) decreased between 2 and 8 years of age. In contrast, the HSA of nonambulatory children with CP (GMFCS levels IV and V) did not show a decrement as they grew over the same period.[22] Although not statistically significant, there was an observed trend of increased HSA with advancing age among individuals classified at GMFCS level V. In another study, it was observed that nonambulatory patients with CP demonstrated an increasing trend in NSA, contrasting with ambulatory patients with CP.[23] In proportion to the GMFCS level, children with CP showed more severe coxa valga, indicating that management of coxa valga is important in nonambulatory children with CP (GMFCS levels IV and V).

Several studies showed the effect of hip-abducted standing programs on the development of stable hips.[24,25] However, in nonambulatory children with CP, who mostly show severe adductor spasticity, the use of a hip abduction bar worsens hip dislocation by acting as a first-class lever and pushing the femoral head out of the acetabulum.[26] Additionally, the use of a seating device with medial knee support in nonambulatory children with CP showed aggravation of hip displacement in a previous study.[27]

Meanwhile, a previous study reported that the use of an abduction bar with hip compression bandage showed the lowest net adduction index on surface electromyography (EMG) scans.[26] The net adduction index is the weighted summation of muscle strength and moment arm length of each adductor and abductor muscle, which implies the net rotational torque on the femoral head in the direction of hip dislocation. To better understand the biomechanics of the hip adductor and abductors based on surface EMG, we invented a hip brace consisting of 3 straps, which were designed to prevent loosening of the hip joint capsule, the coxa valga, and hip adduction.

In this study, patients wearing this hip brace for 12 months showed a significant difference in coxa valga (mean between-group difference in the change of the mean NSA of both sides, −3.50 [95% CI: −5.92 to 1.09]). Although the time-group interaction effect was not statistically significant, the HSA of both sides was decreased in the study group compared with that in the control group (mean change in the mean HSA of both sides at the 12-month follow-up: −0.79 ± 3.95 in the study group vs 0.97 ± 4.37 in the control group, P = .099). Taking into account the previous study that has shown an increasing line in NSA in nonambulatory patients with CP, as well as the fact that NSA increased in our control group, even though the decrement was small in our study group, it is considered an important finding that NSA did not further increase.[23] In the previous study, the migration percentage showed remarkable improvement, while there were only minimal changes in NSA in this study.[14] The reason for this discrepancy is believed to be that inducing skeletal changes is challenging, but correcting migration percentage can be relatively easier by reinforcing the laxity of the hip capsule. The brace would be helpful in preventing the progression of coxa valga and correcting hip displacements in nonambulatory children with CP; this would reduce the number of hip surgeries, which increase the likelihood of postoperative complications and failure rates in nonambulatory children with CP.

Although both the NSA and HSA are useful parameters for measuring coxa valga, only the NSA showed improvement during hip brace intervention in this study. This result may be due to the lower reliability of HSA than the other parameters. As reported in a previous study, demarcation of the proximal femoral physis is sometimes difficult in patients with CP, which often leads to low inter-rater reliability.[16] For this reason, the HSA showed no statistically significant improvement, although it showed favorable trends during the intervention period.

This study has several limitations. First, this study was based on a hypothesis that is widely accepted but has not yet been proven. The hypothesis that muscle imbalance around the hip joint and adductor spasticity are the main contributing factors to hip dislocation should be further investigated and proved by biomechanical approach. However, due to the limited clinical utilization of surface EMG, this hypothesis has not been clearly substantiated so far.[28] While there are some indirect clues that support this hypothesis, such as the preventive effects of botulinum toxin injection into hip adductor muscles on hip dislocation, it is evident that further research is needed to prove the hypothesis. Second, nearly one-third of the participants in the study group discontinued brace interventions during the trial. Many of these resulted from the small size of the brace, as the patients showed faster growth than expected. Moreover, 3 patients underwent hip surgery during the trial period. However, as we highlighted in the previous paper,[14] the MI of the 3 patients who underwent surgery did not show any changes during that period, and the decision for surgical intervention was based on the choice of the parents. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed, considering the considerable dropout rates in nonambulatory patients with CP. Third, we measured the NSA on the projection image rather than the actual NSA that can be measured on computed tomography. Despite the need to measure true angle, projection images were used due to the radiation hazard in young patients with CP. Further studies with hip computed tomography or three-dimensional computer reconstruction are needed to investigate the effects of hip braces on actual NSA. Fourth, the participants’ ages were heterogeneous and ranged from 1 to 10 years. A recent longitudinal study from the Swedish Surveillance Program for CP showed that the mean migration percentage increases with decreasing rates until the age of 6 years, before eventually reaching a stable plateau.[29] Therefore, further research should attempt an age-stratified analysis.

In conclusion, this prospective, multicenter, assessor-blinded, randomized controlled trial revealed that the newly designed hip brace is effective in preventing the progression of coxa valga as well as hip displacement. The lower strap of the brace, which exerts upward and medial forces, effectively counteracts coxa valga. This suggests that the prevention of coxa valga by hip brace intervention is a contributing factor to the prevention of hip displacement.
