Examining the daily experiences of parents caring for children with rare neurogenetic conditions using over 18,000 ecological momentary assessments
Authors: Lyndsey N. Graham, Heming Li, Stephen Tueller, Anne Wheeler, Dan Foti, Bridgette Kelleher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parent caregivers of children with rare neurogenetic conditions experience daily challenges in meeting the needs of their child and family, though studies examining the daily experiences of caregivers are extremely limited. Here, we present pre-intervention ecological momentary assessment data from 495 parent caregivers of children with rare neurogenetic conditions who were enrolled in a clinical trial focused on caregiver well-being. We aimed to both describe typical daily routines across the sample, and to examine how daily experiences, context, and well-being interrelated over time. Across the two-week period, caregivers spent substantial time attending to family needs, often with limited social support and without also engaging in leisure and social activities that could ease burden and support well-being. Autoregressive cross-lagged models indicated a number of dynamic relationships among daily activities, social support, and indicators of well-being. Findings highlight potential targets for intervention to bolster caregiver support and promote healthy family dynamics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49943-9.

Introduction

Of the 30 million individuals with rare conditions in the United States, approximately half are children1 who often require substantial caregiving support to manage their condition. Caregiving for a child with a rare condition impacts nearly all aspects of a parent caregiver’s life, including physical health, finances, mental health, socioemotional well-being, and quality of life2. In the general population, the prevalence of parents experiencing mental health and quality of life challenges is staggering, such that the US Surgeon General has referred to parent well-being as a critical public health priority3. The health and well-being of parent caregivers is generally poorer than those parenting otherwise-healthy children4,5 yet the challenges of their day-to-day life are often shrouded from the public’s view. Indeed, empirical research focused on the day-to-day challenges of caregivers has tended to focus on other caregiving populations, such as general familial care6,7, adult cancer patients8, and individuals with dementia9–11. Although nearly 15 million children experience rare conditions in the U.S., studies focused specifically on the daily life experiences of caregivers for children with rare neurogenetic conditions (NGCs) are limited5,12,13.

A number of logistical hurdles make it challenging to study the daily lives of NGC caregivers – namely, caregivers are busy doing the work of caregiving, generally without respite support or flexibility necessary to travel for in-person research studies. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) has emerged as a promising method for monitoring and assessing the daily experiences of NGC caregivers, without requiring in-person assessment. EMA is an ecologically sensitive approach to measurement that utilizes brief, repeated assessments to obtain temporally dense information about moment-to-moment experiences14. EMA boasts a number of benefits for caregiver-focused research, including by minimizing recall bias, reducing reporting demands, and eliminating travel burden all via flexible, remote methods that take <5 minutes per instance. Although using EMA to study parent caregivers to children with disabilities is a recent development12,13,15–17, EMA is already established as a useful method in other caregiving populations (e.g., adults with dementia18–22, adults with cancer23).

The resilience cascade model24,25 frames resilience as the capacity to adapt to challenges, acknowledging that an individual’s capacity and the challenges they must adapt to are dynamic and spread across multiple systems of influence. Parent caregivers are a prime example of individuals that must adapt to challenges as they navigate a number of personal and care-related systems in their child’s care. Existing literature indicates that many caregivers experience significant levels of stress26, high levels of negative affect27, elevated anxiety and depression28,29, and poor health-related quality of life30, which negatively impact physical health, such as through pain and sleep disturbances31,32. Models of resilience frame self-efficacy and social connectedness as protective factors, increasing an individual’s capacity for positive adaptation24,25. However, little research has examined positive experiences in parent caregivers. A scoping review of parent stress in rare genetic conditions noted that research rarely examines positive aspects of caregiving, such as self-efficacy, therefore limiting our conceptualization of caregiver well-being to the absence of negative outcomes rather than the presence of positive ones26. At a daily level, positive affect may also serve as a facilitator of positive adaptation33. Guided by the resilience cascade model, we conceptualize relationships among both adaptive (e.g., positive affect, self-efficacy) and maladaptive (e.g., stress, negative affect) indicators of well-being as dynamic, particularly in the context of daily caregiving challenges.

Here, measuring NGC caregivers’ experiences using EMA has the potential to assess multidimensional, temporal patterns of daily experiences, including how these resilience cascades temporally unfold in this unique population. For example, days characterized by greater caregiving demands may elicit future higher negative affect and lower self-efficacy days. Conversely, greater self-efficacy on a given day may buffer against maladaptive outcomes, reducing stress and negative affect. The goals of the present study were to (1) describe the daily experiences of NGC caregivers as measured by EMA, including typical daily activities, health behaviors, caregiving duties, and social support and (2) investigate how, within caregivers, temporal patterns of daily experiences interrelate with stress, affect, and self-efficacy.

Methods

This study was approved by the Purdue University Institutional Review Board on December 18th, 2021 (IRB ID#2021-843). All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Data were drawn from Project WellCAST (Supporting the WELL-being of CAregiverS via Telehealth), a series of interrelated virtual research studies designed to optimize telehealth supports for NGC caregivers34,35. Project WellCAST 3.0 refers to a NIH-funded clinical trial (NCT05999448) in which NGC caregivers were prospectively matched to support programs based on clinical characteristics and lifestyle EMA profiles. This matching was conducted via a precision health decision tree algorithm36; primary trial results are forthcoming and will be reported elsewhere. The present study focuses on the pre-intervention baseline EMA data from this trial, collected prior to caregivers consenting to any particular treatment program.

Participants

Participants were primary caregivers to individuals aged 2-35 years old with a NGC associated with intellectual or developmental disability. Of the 516 NGC caregivers who consented to the baseline phase of Project WellCAST 3.0, 495 caregivers completed at least one EMA and were included in subsequent analyses; 21 participants did not engage with EMA and are not reported further.

Procedures

Participants were recruited through a variety of sources, including social media advertisements; materials shared with genetics clinics, providers, and patient advocacy groups; condition-specific email distribution lists; and recruitment through registries. Broader trial procedures related to recruitment, screening, enrollment, and compensation are described in detail in the project’s protocol36 and clincialtrials.gov registration (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05999448). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to engaging in study activities. Although survey questions included reflections on their children, only adult caregivers directly participated in the study. No data were collected from children and thus their children were not considered study participants. Baseline data collection took place over a two-week period, prior to participants consenting to or receiving any support programs. During this time, participants completed a demographics survey, one set of clinical assessment forms, and 14 days of EMA surveys, which were deployed three times per day. Surveys were deployed via text message that arrived randomly between 7 AM and 8 AM, 11 AM and 2 PM, and 7 PM and 8 PM, anchored to the participant’s local time.

Measures

Demographic variables

Participants completed a demographic form that assessed caregiver age, sex, race/ethnicity, employment, relationship status, rurality, and household income. Participants also reported their child’s age, sex, and syndrome.

Momentary and daily variables

EMA surveys included both momentary and daily variables. Variables labeled as ‘momentary’ were assessed morning, afternoon, and evening. Variables labeled as ‘daily’ were asked once per day, with variables denoted as ‘AM’ included in the morning survey and ‘PM’ included in the evening survey. Specific variables were as follows:

Momentary Activities were measured by asking participants to select any items they were doing immediately before receiving the survey. Options included supervising children, paid work, household chores/cooking, coordinating family needs, errands or driving, exercise, hobbies or leisure, socializing, personal care, eating a meal or snack, watching TV or a movie, using social media, nothing, or I am not doing any of these things. We specifically focus on descriptive activities (e.g., errands or driving) rather than the function of those activities (e.g., transporting to appointments, picking up medications, school drop-offs) to streamline reporting.

Momentary Social Context was measured by asking participants who they were with at the time of the survey. Options included no one (alone), partner/spouse, co-parent (non-partner), my child(ren) with a rare disorder, my child(ren) without a rare disorder, friends, extended family, work colleagues, providers or teachers, or other people.

Momentary Stress was measured by asking, “How STRESSED are you feeling right now?”. Participants responded from 0 (Not at all) to 100 (Extremely).

Momentary Positive and Negative Affect were measured using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule - Short Form (PANAS-SF)37, which has been validated in previous EMA studies (α=.89)38. Participants were asked to indicate how much they felt each emotion in that moment, ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always).

Momentary Self Efficacy was measured by asking participants to indicate how certain they felt they would be able to handle everything they needed to do that day. Participants responded from 0 (not at all) to 100 (extremely).

Daily Caregiving Responsibility (PM) was measured by asking how much caregiving responsibility they had for their loved one with a disability that day. Participants responded from 0 (not at all) to 100 (extremely).

Daily Child-related Stressors (PM) was measured by asking if specific aspects of caring for their child(ren) with a disability caused stress that day. Responses included: my child’s personal care needs, my child’s behavior, my child’s medical needs, my child’s school needs, issues with my child’s providers/teachers, issues with logistics or scheduling, issues with discrimination, impact on my mental health, impact on my physical health, impact on my finances, worrying about the future, or none of these.

Daily Challenging Behavior Severity (PM) was measured by asking participants to rate the severity of problem behaviors for their child with a disability that day. Participants responded from 0 (very low) to 100 (extremely).

Daily Perceived Caregiving Support (PM) was measured by asking participants how supported they felt with respect to their caregiving duties that day. Participants responded from 0 (not at all) to 100 (extremely).

Daily Social Support Types (PM) was measured by asking participants to indicate how they received social support from other adults that day. Options included in-person conversations, phone calls, text messaging or direct messaging, teleconferencing, social media, or indicating they did not receive support from other adults that day.

Daily Sleep Duration and Quality (AM) was measured by asking participants how much they slept the previous night in hours and minutes, with a follow up question asking participants to rate their sleep quality from 0 (very poor) to 100 (very good).

Daily Exercise (AM) was measured by asking participants if they did any of the following the day before, including light exercise, moderate exercise, strenuous exercise, strength training, yoga, or meditation/mindfulness.

Daily Alcohol Intake (AM) was measured by asking participants to indicate how many standard drinks they had the day before. Participants responded from 0 (no drinks) to 6 (6 or more drinks).

Daily Smoking (AM) was measured by asking participants if they smoked/vaped tobacco the day before. Participants responded “yes” or “no”.

Analytic plan

Analyses included all available baseline data from Project WellCAST 3.0. A significance level of 5% (α = 0.05) was used. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.4.1 or greater). Code is available at https://osf.io/5j8xn/overview to ensure reproducibility.

We first examined daily objective behavioral data (daily activities, health behaviors, caregiving duties, social support) and daily subjective experiences (PA, NA, stress, self-efficacy) via descriptive statistics and intraclass correlations to examine sample averages and partition sources of variance in each variable.

We then used Autoregressive Cross-Lagged (ARCL) models39 to examine temporal relationships between pairs of variables across all time points during the baseline period. To reduce data for these analyses, we used exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to first create scale scores for momentary activities, social context, and child-related stressors. Scale scores for the activities and interaction factors were calculated as sum scores for each participant at each time point. Prior to calculating the sum score, the small amount of missing data (<1% on average) was imputed for each binary item as each person’s modal response.

ARCL models are particularly suited for studying reciprocal effects between longitudinal variables, accounting for both autoregressive (AR) effects (the effect a variable at the current time point on itself at the next time point) and cross-lagged (CL) effects (the effect a variable at the current time point has on another variable at the next time point). Rather than modeling the shape of the trajectory of the outcomes, the ARCL gains precision in its AR and CL estimates via multiple repeated measures. Specifically, coefficients represent a variable recorded today has on itself (AR effects) or another variable (CL effects) on the following day.

Models were fit using the lavaan version 0.6-19 R package with full information maximum likelihood (FIML) treatment for missing data40–42. P-values were adjusted for the false discovery rate to control Type I error rates using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate (FDR) correction43.

Daily activities

Most prominently endorsed daily activities were supervising children (Morning: 47%, Afternoon: 44%, Evening: 57%), household work (M:26%, A:24%, E:30%), family needs (M:24%, A:21%, E:22%), paid work (M:14%, A:28%, E:5%), eating (M:13%, A:16%, E:18%), errands/driving (M:12%, A:16%, E:6%), and watching TV/movie (M:5%, A:6%, E:21%). Other options were endorsed in less than 10% of the EMA responses on average. Most daily activities had a relatively static prevalence across days, though weekend effects (i.e., patterns specific to weekend days) were observed for some activities (less paid work, more time spent on TV/movies, socializing, hobbies/leisure, and doing nothing on weekends).

Social contexts

Most prominent social contexts were being alone (M:78%, A:77%, E:90%), with child(ren) with a rare disorder (M:59%, A:50%, E:71%), partner/spouse (M:31%, A:24%, E:52%), and child(ren) without a rare disorder (M:34%, A:30%, E:42%). Other options were endorsed in less than 10% of the EMA responses on average. Social contexts also varied by time of week, with less time spent with work colleagues, and more time spent with children, spouse, and friends.

Health behaviors

Exercise was infrequently endorsed as a daily activity across time of day and time of week (2-3%). The types of exercise endorsed each day varied, with light and moderate exercise endorsed most frequently (15-30%) and minimal endorsements of strenuous exercise, strength training, yoga or meditation (2-8%; Supplemental Figure 1). Average sleep quality during baseline was 56 (scored from 0 [very poor] to 100 [very good]), characterized by peaks and valleys, reaching just over 58 during the middle of the baseline period. Participants reported sleep duration, with an average of 6.9 hours of sleep across the baseline period, with weekend effects reaching 7.15 hours. Alcohol consumption was on average 1.3 standard drinks per day, with weekend highs of up to 1.5 drinks per day. Only 4% of participants reported smoking. Most participants either smoked daily or always abstained from smoking.

Caregiving

Average caregiving responsibility was 67 (scored from 0 [not at all] to 100 [extremely]; Supplemental Figure 2). Average child challenging behavior severity was 27.6 during the baseline period, with variation between 23 to 32 (Supplemental Figure 2). Average perceived caregiving support was of 56.5 (scored from 0 [not at all] to 100 [extremely]; Supplemental Figure 6).

Stress

Average stress was 40.94 with a minimum of 29 and maximum of 41 (scored from 0 [not at all] to 100 [extremely]), with lower stress on the weekends (Supplemental Figure 3). Most prominently endorsed child-related stressors were my child’s behavior (36%), my child’s personal care needs (34%), worrying about the future (27%), my child’s medical needs (22%), and logistics or scheduling (20%). Issues with child’s providers/teachers (6%) and discrimination (1%) had the lowest average prevalence. The ‘none of these’ response option ranged from 7% to 15%, with an average of 13% (Supplemental Figure 4).Fig. 4Example ARCL path diagram of stress and positive affect.

Support

Participants reported types of social support received, endorsing in-person support on an average 48% of days, texting (30% of days), phone calls (19% of days), social media (12% of days), and teleconferencing (3% of days). On 22% of days, participants endorsed receiving no support from any other adult (Supplemental Figures 5 and 6).

Stress

As reported in Table 5, stress was associated with increases in next-day negative affect, family management, number of child-related stressors, and behavior severity, as well as decreases in positive affect, self-efficacy, sleep quality, and perceived social support. Negative affect, positive affect, sleep quality and duration, exercise, and perceived social support were all associated with next-day decreases in stress, whereas number of child-related stressors and child behavioral severity were associated with increases in next-day stress.

Negative affect

Negative affect was associated with increases in next-day family management, number of child-related stressors and behavior severity, as well as decreases in stress and self-efficacy. Stress, number of child-related stressors, and child behavioral severity were all associated with increases in next-day negative affect, where self-efficacy, sleep duration and quality, and perceived social support were associated with next-day decreases in negative affect.

Positive affect

Positive affect was associated with increases in next-day caregiving responsibilities, as well as decreases in stress. Self-efficacy, Family Management Activities, sleep quality, and exercise were associated with next-day increases in positive affect, and stress was associated with next-day decreases in positive affect.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy was associated with increases in next-day positive affect, sleep quality, and perceived social support, as well as decreases in negative affect, number of child-related stressors, and behavioral severity. Sleep quality and duration, exercise, perceived social support and types of social support received were all associated with next-day increases in self-efficacy, whereas stress, negative affect, Family Management Activities, number of child-related stressors, and child behavioral severity were associated with next-day decreases in self-efficacy.

Summarizing disruptors caregiver well-being

Negative affect, stress, child-related stressors, and children’s challenging behaviors were independently associated with next-day reductions in aspects of caregiver well-being (e.g., increased stress and negative affect, decreased self-efficacy and positive affect).

Summarizing contributors to caregiver well-being

Sleep duration and quality, positive affect, self-efficacy, exercise, and social support were each independently associated with next-day improvements in aspects of caregiver well-being (e.g., increased self-efficacy and positive affect, decreased stress and negative affect) as well as some decreases in child behavioral severity.

Discussion

Parent caregivers of children with rare NGCs experience daily challenges as part of their child’s care, and framed by resilience models, their capacity for positive adaptation and resilience remains dynamic24,25. To this point, research has primarily examined maladaptive indicators of well-being26, disconnected from the daily experiences of caregiver life and caregiver resilience. Here, we leveraged over 18,000 datapoints to first describe aspects of NGC caregivers’ daily experiences, including daily activities, health behaviors, caregiving duties, and social support. Caregivers primarily reported activities related to the needs of their families, often with limited or no support for themselves. We then examined relationships among daily experiences with stress, affect, and self-efficacy, probing temporal dynamics and relationships among variables that characterize aspects of adaptation and resilience. Findings provided insights into the temporal associations between daily experiences, stress, affect, and self-efficacy, informing potential pathways for supporting family resilience and promoting overall healthy family dynamics.

Topography of daily experiences

The daily experiences of parent caregivers often reflect the needs of their children and family. Consistent with expectations from past literature44–46, descriptive data indicated that NGC caregivers spent a majority of their time meeting their family’s needs or doing paid work, with minimal endorsements of leisure and self-care activities; each potential leisure or self-care activity was endorsed in <12% of all EMA responses. In terms of health behaviors, caregivers reported low frequencies of alcohol consumption and smoking but also reported low levels of physical exercise and poor sleep. Caregivers averaged <7 hours of sleep and suboptimal sleep quality, in line with work suggesting that caring for children with medical needs impacts overall sleep and decreases sleep quality47.

Social support has been previously shown to buffer challenges in parent caregivers48,49, however reported support was generally low and variable across our sample. Caregivers most frequently reported receiving in-person support in just under half of the days, with fewer endorsements of other types of support (e.g., social media, phone calls). Caregivers also reported perceived support levels that were midrange (M=55.82, range=0-100), indicating ample room for more support. Importantly, on 22% of days, caregivers reported no social support from any other adult, underscoring the overall limited social support caregivers generally receive50. This finding aligns with a substantial corpus of work indicating that caregivers tend to have unmet needs for social support50,51, which is associated with greater caregiver burden52,53, high stress and mental health symptoms48,54, and poor quality of life49,55–57. Caregivers were often in physical proximity to their children or partners, reported being alone about 80% of the time, and infrequently reported any time with friends or other adults. Spending ample time with their child with a rare condition likely reflects ‘vigilance,’ which refers to the intensive supervision generally required for children with complex needs44. In our study, caregivers’ days were similarly characterized by meeting the needs of their families, often with limited support and at the cost of activities and experiences that would be supportive of their own mental and physical health needs. Taken together, our data suggests that NGC caregivers spend large amounts of time alone and receive minimal daily social support, likely contributing to the pervasive loneliness commonly experienced by caregivers across many caregiving populations58–60.

Subjective self-reported daily stress was lower than expected (M=40.94, range=0-100), given robust prior findings of significant stress levels in caregivers26. However, similarly low daily stress levels have been observed in our prior EMA studies with an independent sample of NGC caregivers, replicating current findings34. A possible explanation for the disconnect between daily ratings of stress and traditional questionnaires may involve shifts in how caregivers evaluate or habituate to their circumstances. Caregivers may not evaluate their lives against a fixed pre-caregiving or pre-diagnosis standard. Instead, over time they may undergo a response shift61 or process of normalization62,63, which can involve recalibrating their internal standards, finding a ’new normal,’ or redefining what counts as ’stress’ in light of ongoing caregiving demands. For example, a caregiver may be reporting low stress levels compared to their own ’usual’ amount of stress, though this may reflect a high baseline level of stress that would be distressing and unmanageable for someone who is not accustomed to these daily experiences. Future work may interrogate these possibilities by exploring explicit reference frames (e.g., ’compared to your own usual...’) to anchor and align interpretation, contextualizing how caregivers are reporting their own stress levels. This approach is in line with existing procedures that emphasize the role of reference points designed to enhance the comparability of subjective ratings64,65.

Interrelationships among daily experiences

We also examined how these variables interrelated across time, informing potential causal processes and points of intervention. ARCL model results suggest that many temporal relationships exist among study variables, some of which appear to support caregiver well-being, while others may exacerbate strain over time. Contributors to caregiver well-being included adequate sleep, exercise, and social support. Each activity was associated with next-day improvements in indicators of well-being, highlighting concrete, modifiable daily activities that support caregivers’ well-being. Increases in next-day positive affect, in particular, were linked to a variety of daily experiences, including Family Management Activities, exercise, and sleep quality, reinforcing its role as a potential resilience resource observed in other populations33. These data also align with a substantial corpus of mental health research demonstrating the benefits of behavioral activation on positive affect, where increasing engagement in meaningful activities (e.g., family management, exercise) can improve mood and mental health symptoms66. In parallel, self-efficacy, a known facilitator of positive adaptation24,25, was associated with increases in indicators of adaptive functioning (e.g., positive affect, sleep quality, social support) and decreases in maladaptive outcomes (e.g., negative affect, child behavior severity). This pattern aligns with prior work in caregivers of autistic children, where higher self-efficacy has been linked to less parenting stress, better parent mental health, and fewer child challenging behaviors67. These findings, in combination with evidence of improvements in self-efficacy via parent training programs for parents of children with disabilities68, underscore its potential as an intervention target despite being relatively understudied in NGC caregiver populations26.

In contrast, several daily experiences functioned as disruptors of next-day caregiver well-being. Daily stress, negative affect, and child behavior challenges were associated with worsening next-day outcomes (e.g., higher number of child-related stressors, worse sleep). Associations also emerged between child behavioral challenges with other variables, supporting the validity of the measurement itself and supporting strong past associations between challenging behaviors and stress, negative affect, positive affect, and self-efficacy67,69–71. However, the overall levels of challenging behaviors were lower than expected. Again, caregivers and families may adapt to ongoing behavioral challenges by reconstructing a “new normal” and recalibrating their expectations over time61,63,72, which can influence how daily challenges are perceived and reported. Of note, we measured challenging behaviors with a single, broad item in this study; thus, it is possible that caregivers considered an overall profile of daily behaviors that masked potential “spikes” in specific behavioral phenotypes. Lower reported levels of challenging behavior may also reflect the benefits of family-focused interventions and family resilience processes, both of which have been linked to better caregiver adjustment and, in some studies, fewer child behavior problems73–75. It is also possible that the “set point” for caregivers’ rating of challenging behaviors was lower than the center point of the scale to accommodate potential extreme behaviors (e.g., a caregiver may be hesitant to rate a very high score, even on a challenging day, because “it could be worse”). Established behavioral phenotypes in many of the NGCs included in this study reflect variable and non-linear change over time76,77, likely indicating that more nuanced approaches accounting for behavior type may be needed to appropriately contextualize behavior severity.

Finally, several additional findings help contextualize these patterns. Although health-promoting activities, such as exercise, were associated with improved well-being, they were reported minimally. Self-care and leisure activities were reported very infrequently (M=1.54, range=0-11), and sleep patterns suggested limited restorative rest, in line with work that highlights both the benefits and difficulties of engaging in self-care for caregivers78,79. Our findings further suggest that caregivers may not consistently access behaviors that confer benefit, thus limiting their cumulative impact. Additionally, while self-efficacy was more closely tied to adaptation-related processes, positive and negative affect showed distinct temporal patterns: positive and negative affect were weakly positively correlated at the same time point but not associated across days. This supports prior evidence that positive and negative affect can and do function independently80 and underscores the importance of examining both to fully capture caregivers’ experiences33.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

It is important to contextualize these findings within the present study and its limitations. The current study employs a micro-longitudinal design across a two-week period, with focus on relationships among daily activities and aspects of adaptation and resilience. Strengths include a high completion rate of EMA surveys (87%), yielding the largest compilation of daily data on NGC caregivers to date. However, this study is also unique, and findings may not generalize globally. Caregivers in this sample represent over 150 unique child diagnoses, with both rare and ultra-rare groups represented. The goals of this paper were broadly to examine shared experiences across NGC caregivers, though important future directions will include looking for syndrome-specific patterns and differences in caregivers’ well-being to inform specific care and needed supports. While diverse in child condition, our sample is comprised primarily of educated White mothers with relatively high household incomes, limiting generalizability. The affluence of our sample may also be another contributor to less severe daily stress levels and child behavioral challenges observed in our sample, as more resources would positively impact both stress and child behaviors. Given the limited empirical insights into caregivers’ lives, this work represents a first step in understanding their experiences, however additional work in diverse samples will be important to appropriately characterize the experiences and needs of diverse caregivers.

A number of important next steps remain. Given the incongruence observed in EMA stress reports here and elsewhere34 as compared to overall stress levels26, important future directions may include employing cognitive interviewing to improve survey questions. This method is designed to interrogate how questions are understood by participants and potentially improve generated information81, which may address issues raised in the interpretation of caregivers’ stress reports. Another possibility is including reference points64,65 (e.g., rating stress compared to their own usual or compared to someone who is not a caregiver), which may improve the comparability of subjective ratings and provide clearer insights into how ratings should be interpreted.

It will also be important to understand the degree to which EMA data collection itself impacts variables of interest, particularly those related to health. Beyond the context of EMA studies, self-monitoring is known to be a useful and often therapeutic technique that can lead to changes in behaviors and emotions82. The repeated daily assessments characteristic to EMA facilitate self-monitoring, which situates EMA to potentially influence responses over time83. Work in other fields has already begun to deliver EMA-style interventions (e.g., ecological momentary intervention [EMI]84), suggesting EMA represents a fruitful direction for research with NGC caregivers, expanding from current conceptualizations of EMA as a monitoring and assessment tool to potential future use of EMA as an intervention to address caregiver well-being needs.

Methodologically, building on our current findings, it will be important to determine the degree to which these relationships shift across longer time periods and, as relevant to the broader clinical trial, intervention. The ARCL modeling approach was intentionally chosen to focus on within-person temporal relationships and, as such, did not model demographic covariates to preserve power and parsimony. As such, interpretations of results are limited to within-person patterns, though ARCL models do control for between-person differences in each included variable. Potential next steps may include examining reciprocal, temporal influences by combining the current approach with growth models, investigating lagged effects and change over time simultaneously, or modeling feedback loops to test reciprocal temporal influences. The larger sample afforded by the full clinical trial sample will facilitate these directions.

Finally, our identified patterns of daily experiences facilitating well-being or predicting strain are intuitive but simultaneously highlight practical points for intervention. With initial evidence of temporal relationships, there may be many areas to intervene upon that could produce positive spillover effects into other domains (e.g., intervention to reduce negative affect may lead to less stress and subsequently fewer child behavioral challenges). Particularly for contributors to well-being, intervention may first need to address barriers to engaging in restorative activities common to caregivers (e.g., limited time, lack of respite support) in order to create space for engagement.

Conclusion

To this point, the daily lives of NGC caregivers have been mostly hidden from the public’s eye. Leveraging data from the pre-intervention baseline phase of Project WellCAST, the present study described caregivers’ daily experiences and the connections between daily activities and broader adaptation and resilience outcomes. Despite their potential benefits, activities intended to improve well-being (e.g., exercise, leisure and self-care) were infrequently reported by caregivers, likely indicating substantial barriers to engagement. Findings indicated that daily activities can yield powerful impacts on stress, affect, and self-efficacy, further highlighting concrete areas to intervene and improve caregivers’ day-to-day well-being.
