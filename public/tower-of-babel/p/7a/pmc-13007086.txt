Brief narrative interventions for adults with chronic illness or psychosocial distress: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Brianna Polenz, Taylor Rigby, Caroline Logue, Sarah L Krein, Victoria D Powell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNarrative interventions elicit personal stories from participants to impact outcome(s). The process of organising a troublesome experience into a cohesive story may benefit individuals dealing with a variety of complex medical conditions and psychosocial stressors. A better understanding of prior work is a critical step to guide further development, refinement and expansion of this under-recognised therapeutic modality for new populations.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will follow recommendations of the Joanna Briggs Institute and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews to map and summarise the content and context of brief narrative interventions intended for individuals with chronic illness and/or psychosocial distress. Inclusion criteria are records describing brief (≤4 discrete session) interventions eliciting personal stories with the intent of impacting patient-centred outcomes (eg, depression, quality of life) delivered in any context. Participants will be adults (≥18 years old) living with chronic medical or psychiatric conditions (eg, metastatic cancer, chronic pain) or psychosocial distress (eg, grief, loneliness). Studies whose focus is not direct participant benefit or studies conducted in populations without evident illness or psychosocial stressors will be excluded. An initial search of PubMed (MEDLINE) identified sentinel articles; a comprehensive strategy was developed with the assistance of a librarian for systematically searching PubMed (MEDLINE), PsycInfo (EBSCOhost), CINAHL (EBSCOhost) and Embase (Ovid). Bibliographies of relevant articles will also be examined. Two independent reviewers will screen articles; disagreements will be discussed at regular meetings. U-M GPT, an artificial intelligence-powered institutional large language model, will perform initial data abstraction with a human reviewer verifying accuracy. Findings will be qualitatively summarised according to the three research questions, presented in tabular format, and described narratively.Ethics and disseminationAs data will be obtained only from secondary sources, no ethics approval will be sought. Findings will be disseminated via the scoping review Open Science Framework (OSF) project site and submitted for peer review. Results will elucidate promising areas for future research, including narrative intervention development and refinement; these findings will guide expansion into new populations.Trial registration number https://osf.io/85hjd/

Introduction

Narrative interventions are those that elicit a personal story from participants with the intent of impacting relevant outcome(s). Fundamentally, they are based on the theoretical underpinning that the process of organising troublesome experiences or events into a cohesive story involves assigning structure and meaning, which improves the perception that it is more manageable.1 This process can result in a reduction in distress associated with the experience or event. Across a wide range of ill and healthy individuals, constructing written and spoken stories improves health and well-being.2 Because of this, narrative interventions show promise as a complement or alternative to more time-intensive treatments (eg, traditional psychotherapy) in multiple populations dealing with chronic medical or psychiatric illness and/or ongoing psychosocial stress.

Narrative interventions may consist of open-ended prompts that are structured to address participants’ needs or constraints. In patients with serious illness receiving palliative care, several brief narrative interventions (defined as four or fewer sessions lasting approximately 60 min each) have been developed and successfully implemented.35 Their brevity is well-suited for those with barriers to more time-intensive treatment, such as illness-related fatigue or brief life expectancy. For example, Dignity Therapy is a brief narrative intervention intended to enhance a sense of meaning, purpose and legacy in patients with serious or terminal illness such as metastatic cancer.4 Participants are asked a series of questions to elicit personal stories over one or two sessions lasting approximately 60 min. Interviews are then summarised, transcribed and returned to the participant in the form of a keepsake document. Dignity Therapy and related interventions have been studied for potential impact on depression, pain, anxiety and quality of life.35

Even in the absence of a terminal or progressive disease, brief narrative interventions have potential benefits.6 7 Patients living with a variety of chronic illnesses and psychosocial stressors frequently report distress that may be amenable to a narrative approach, such as feelings of grief, hopelessness, loss of former identities, loneliness and the strong need to have someone listen empathically and believe that they are suffering.8 9

Narrative interventions appear to be a promising yet underused therapeutic technique with the potential to benefit people experiencing many forms of chronic illness or stressors. However, before more widespread adoption can be recommended, a better understanding of the variability across populations, intervention content and format is needed. It is also critical to understand which patient-centred outcomes were targeted and which tools were used to measure them. A scoping literature review provides a suitable framework to address these factors and develop a better understanding of the content and context in which brief narrative interventions may be useful.

In this protocol, we describe the methodology this scoping review will use to derive a comprehensive understanding of the characteristics of brief narrative interventions and identify knowledge gaps for future research. If possible, we also aim to describe mechanisms or domains of reported impact to better understand whether certain outcomes may be particularly amenable to a brief narrative approach to inform future research. Our decision to limit the focus to interventions containing four or fewer sessions was based on studies of populations with serious medical illness (eg, patients with metastatic cancer receiving palliative care), where there is a consistent and rapid rise in attrition as the number of intervention sessions increases.31012 For patients with limited life expectancy, ethical concerns regarding patient burden at the end of life have resulted in prioritising intervention brevity,13 14 though this may be less relevant for other populations. We acknowledge that restricting the number of sessions could limit the ability to evaluate benefits that may become apparent only with longer interventions, such as cumulative dose-response effects. A preliminary search identified no current or in-progress systematic reviews or scoping reviews on this topic.

Methods and analysis

This scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews.15 We will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for Scoping Reviews checklist.16 The protocol is registered with Open Science Framework (OSF) (https://osf.io/8kwbr). The study activities described will take place between November 2025 and June 2026.

We will aim to address three research questions in complementary, preliminary domains, developed to balance specificity with the need to maintain flexibility. We anticipate findings will include these aspects but may undergo iteration as the review proceeds.

Research question 1: What are the characteristics (duration, content, structure, delivery and setting) of brief narrative interventions (defined as no more than four sessions of approximately 60 min each)?

To address this question, we will extract the following information from studies meeting inclusion criteria:

Duration/dose: Number and length of sessions; overall time required of participants.

Research question 3: Which outcomes (eg, quality of life, depression) were targeted by the intervention and what tools were used to measure them?

We will extract data regarding the targeted outcomes and assessment tools (eg, names of instruments, whether tools are validated, qualitative vs quantitative vs mixed methods). If possible, we will aim to describe the mechanism of reported impact on the outcomes targeted by the intervention.

Inclusion criteria

To find relevant studies, we will use the following inclusion criteria. If necessary, we will update criteria to ensure all relevant literature is captured. The criteria will be discussed among screeners to ensure uniformity.

Participants: Adults (≥18 years old) with medical or psychiatric illness and/or psychosocial distress. In the absence of a stated diagnosis, participants must have a condition or state that is either explicitly stated by the authors or could be reasonably assumed, based on the authors’ description, to be a source of chronic ongoing stress. Examples include, but are not limited to: terminal illness, end-of-life, existential distress, loneliness, experiencing homelessness, experiencing trauma, membership in a socially marginalised group. Studies conducted in populations with no evidence of illness or psychosocial distress (eg, college students undergoing self-exploration, athletes seeking to improve performance, older adults undergoing healthy ageing) will be excluded. Studies of children or adolescents will be excluded. There will be no limitations on sex, race, ethnicity, education or socio-economic status. Studies may be conducted in any location or country. Due to resource limitations, use of a professional translation service or site of suitable rigour and reliability is not feasible; we will only consider articles written or previously translated into English. We acknowledge that including only English-language studies limits the ability to assess cultural factors, especially non-Western cultures.

Concept: Studies that use brief narrative interventions will be included. Brief narrative interventions are defined as ≤4 sessions where a structured or semi-structured personal narrative or story is elicited from the participant with the intention of improving specific outcome(s) in the participant who tells or writes the narrative. Interventions will be considered regardless of structure (semi-structured or unstructured), mode of delivery (eg, in-person, telehealth or written) or interventionalist type (eg, psychotherapist, trained nurse). We will not include studies whose primary focus is impacting outcomes outside of direct participant benefit (eg, studies which elicit patient narratives to promote empathy in health profession trainees only, studies whose objective is to evaluate cost-effectiveness and scalability of brief narrative interventions but do not assess the impact of the narrative intervention on the participants themselves). We will only include studies in which a personal narrative is elicited from participants with the goal of impacting a patient-centred outcome. Studies that involve participants only watching or reading a pre-existing (relative to study start) narrative will be excluded (eg, participants watch a video recording of someone else telling a story, but only respond or react to another’s story rather than creating or telling their own). Studies must elicit a narrative from participants as the focus of the intervention to be included. Studies including a narrative activity only but not an intervention will be excluded. An activity, in contrast to an intervention, is an act with no defined outcome (eg, participants who undergo an exit interview to give feedback to investigators would be excluded), whereas an intervention is an intentional act(s) to improve a specific outcome. Studies that do not assess any patient-centred outcomes will be excluded.

Context: This review will consider studies from all geographical locations and countries. We will consider cultural contexts that may influence structure, delivery and outcome measurements of interventions. We will include those conducted in multiple settings, including healthcare settings such as inpatient hospital units or outpatient clinics, community settings such as participants’ homes or places of worship and virtual settings such as internet-based interventions. We will include any structure format (eg, semi-structured vs unstructured), any mode of delivery (eg, in person, telehealth, written) and any interventionalist type (eg, psychotherapist, community health worker).

Types of sources: This scoping review will consider experimental, quasi-experimental and observational study designs, case reports/series and other uncontrolled designs such as preliminary feasibility studies if otherwise meeting inclusion criteria. Grey literature such as protocol registries, dissertations and conference abstracts will be considered if the record is indexed by the databases to be searched. Protocols of future brief narrative interventions may be considered if containing sufficient information to address the research questions. Protocols of published studies will be excluded, with the exception of protocols providing detailed information regarding theoretical underpinning and intervention development not found in the subsequent results publication. We will not consider systematic or scoping reviews or meta-analyses. All other records not directly reporting results of an interventional study, such as letters to the editor or commentaries on other publications, will be excluded.

Search strategy

A three-step search strategy will be used. We identified several sentinel articles. With the assistance of a research librarian, the text words contained in the titles and abstracts of sentinel articles, and the articles’ index terms (eg, Medical Subject Headings terms) were used to develop a full search strategy for PubMed (MEDLINE) (online supplemental appendix 1). The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, will be adapted for the other databases (PsycInfo (EBSCOhost), CINAHL (EBSCOhost) and Embase (Ovid)). A third search will examine the reference list of identified sources included in the review. Studies published on any date will be included.

Study or source of evidence selection

A preliminary search was conducted and no current or in-progress systematic reviews or scoping reviews on the topic were identified. All identified citations will be imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia, available at www.covidence.org) for deduplication and screening. Titles and abstracts will be screened by two independent reviewers.

Records that are not excluded at this first level will undergo full text review by two independent reviewers.

Reasons for exclusion after full text review will be recorded. Any disagreements that arise between the reviewers at each stage of the selection process will be resolved through discussion among the study team during regular meetings. The results of the search and the study inclusion process will be reported in full in the final scoping review report and presented in a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.18

Data extraction

To address the research question of the first domain (characteristics of brief narrative interventions), we will extract information regarding study characteristics including: date(s) the study was conducted, the country/location of the study, format and setting, duration/dose (number and length of sessions) and content (eg, theoretical frameworks or conceptual models underlying intervention content; how content was adapted from prior interventions) and structure of the intervention (eg, written vs interview, preparation and/or credentials of interventionalist, in person vs telehealth). To address the research question of the second domain (populations and population-specific needs), we will extract information regarding the targeted population (eg, clinical diagnosis, presence of psychosocial stressors such as loneliness), the rationale for how the intervention addresses the population’s identified needs (eg, cultural adaptations) and the theoretical and practical approach used by the investigators to address these needs, including whether and how representative members of the study population were consulted. Key limitations will also be recorded, such as barriers to intervention delivery or participant recruitment and/or retention. To address the research question of the third domain (patient-centred outcomes), we will extract data regarding study design, describe control groups if present, study sample size, patient-centred outcome(s) assessed, tools and methodologies used in assessment and the rationale for intervention targets, including whether and how representative members of the targeted population were consulted.

Use of artificial intelligence

For each record meeting inclusion criteria, we will use U-M GPT (GPT-4.1, University of Michigan), an institutional artificial intelligence (AI)-powered large language model service for the initial extraction of data relevant to the research questions.19 Use of AI was chosen to streamline and standardise the initial data extraction process with the goal of improving efficiency while maintaining accuracy. We chose U-M GPT after piloting several candidate AI tools because test abstractions were accurate, complete and without ‘hallucinations’ (confabulated yet plausible-sounding AI output). A human reviewer will examine all AI-abstracted data, recording any errors and disagreements. Online supplemental appendix 2 contains the draft prompt used in pilot abstractions. This prompt will be refined as needed, with all modifications detailed in the final report of the review.

Data analysis and presentation

Data will be presented in tabular format with tables corresponding to each review question. Each table will be examined for emerging themes. We will qualitatively synthesise findings and outline recommendations for future research as it pertains to each review question. To address the first research question (characteristics of brief narrative interventions), we will summarise and report dates, settings, locations and relevant cultural factors. The number of sessions and length of each session(s) along with modes of intervention delivery will be summarised. A detailed assessment and synthesis of the content and structure of the intervention will be conducted. We will focus on details of topics covered in the intervention, structure (eg, semi-structured, unstructured), background and training of the interventionalist(s) and additional important elements such as whether a summary of the stories ascertained was provided to participants and in what form. To address the second review question (populations and population-specific needs), we will report details of the population for whom the intervention was developed and studied, including any important inclusion/exclusion criteria that may affect generalisability. We will summarise key themes regarding theoretical approaches and rationale for intervention development, such as identified needs addressed by the researchers (eg, addressing and alleviating existential distress leading to requests for hastened death in patients who are terminally ill with cancer). Studies’ approaches to addressing these needs, such as conceptual models, will be examined for emerging themes that populations may have in common. Key limitations will be summarised, such as highly specific population needs likely limiting broader adoption. Finally, patient-centred outcome domains will be qualitatively synthesised into key emerging themes to address the third review question. Gaps in the literature to be targeted by future research will be highlighted. Key findings relevant to patient-centred outcomes will be reported and summarised.

Ethics and dissemination

This scoping review does not require ethics approval as all data will be obtained from published records. Findings will be disseminated via the scoping review OSF project site and submitted for peer review in the form of a journal article and conference presentations. We anticipate providing the first comprehensive synthesis of brief narrative interventions across a variety of adult populations experiencing chronic illness or psychosocial distress. By mapping the characteristics, populations and outcomes of these interventions, we anticipate clarifying how narrative approaches are currently applied and where gaps remain for future research. Ultimately, results of this scoping review may assist with positioning brief narrative interventions as practical, patient-centred, integrative complements to standard treatment, such as traditional psychotherapy and medical management.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this protocol. Because of this scoping review’s broad inclusion criteria, we anticipate that the study populations will be too heterogeneous to involve representative patient input. Rather, the final results may be used to inform narrative intervention development by refining the information that is still needed from patients for whom future interventions are intended to benefit.
