Cardiovascular disease risk factors in young people with diabetes mellitus
Authors: Mumtaz Murat Yardimci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing prevalence of diabetes in young adults raises concerns regarding its early cardiometabolic consequences. This study aimed to evaluate cardiac function, renal markers, lipid profile, insulin resistance, and vascular risk indicators in young adults with diabetes compared with non-diabetic controls.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients aged 18–45 years whose medical records were reviewed between 01/01/2012 and 31/12/2023. Clinical, metabolic, and cardiovascular parameters were analyzed to identify early cardiometabolic alterations associated with diabetes.ResultsYoung adults with diabetes demonstrated early signs of adverse cardiometabolic remodeling, characterized by mild reductions in ejection fraction, elevated myocardial stress biomarkers, atherogenic dyslipidemia, increased insulin resistance, and subtle renal alterations. Stent use was also more common among diabetic patients, reflecting greater cardiovascular burden. These differences remained consistent across multiple metabolic domains.ConclusionDiabetes in young adults is associated with an early and unfavorable cardiometabolic phenotype, highlighting the need for proactive cardiovascular risk assessment and targeted preventive strategies. Future longitudinal studies incorporating metabolic, inflammatory, and imaging markers are warranted to clarify progression patterns and guide precision-based interventions.

Background

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by impaired insulin secretion or action, resulting in persistent hyperglycemia and progressive vascular injury. Over recent years, the global prevalence of diabetes has risen dramatically, with a notable shift toward younger age groups. Sedentary lifestyles, unhealthy dietary patterns, and increasing obesity rates have contributed to a growing number of young individuals developing metabolic disturbances previously seen predominantly in older populations. Early-onset diabetes is particularly concerning because it is associated with a more aggressive clinical course, prolonged exposure to hyperglycemia, and an elevated lifetime risk of cardiovascular complications [1].

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among individuals with diabetes, even at younger ages. Early endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, altered lipid metabolism, and renal microvascular injury are key mechanisms driving accelerated cardiometabolic deterioration in this population [1, 2]. Young adults with diabetes frequently exhibit hypertension, dyslipidemia, central adiposity, and subtle impairments in cardiac function, all of which substantially increase lifetime cardiovascular risk. As the global prevalence of early-onset diabetes continues to rise, understanding cardiovascular risk factors in this age group has become critically important [3].

Epidemiological data underscore this trend: individuals with diabetes have a 2–4 times higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease compared with healthy individuals, and CVD ranks among the top causes of global mortality. According to International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimates, 425 million people were living with diabetes in 2017, with numbers projected to reach 642 million by 2040 [1]. Moreover, the age of diabetes onset is decreasing worldwide. Many young adults now live with Type 2 diabetes—a condition once associated almost exclusively with older age—while young individuals with Type 1 diabetes also face significant challenges in maintaining long-term glycemic control [2]. The early onset of cardiovascular risk factors in these individuals can lead to more rapid progression toward major complications such as coronary artery disease, stroke, and heart failure [3].

Given this growing burden, identifying early indicators of cardiovascular risk—such as elevated blood pressure, lipid abnormalities, insulin resistance, and early renal dysfunction—is essential for implementing effective preventive strategies. Understanding these early cardiometabolic alterations may allow health professionals to intervene sooner and potentially reduce long-term complications. Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate cardiovascular risk factors in young individuals with diabetes and to compare them with non-diabetic young adults, thereby providing a foundation for preventive and targeted clinical approaches [4].

Methods

Research pattern

This retrospective observational study included young adult patients aged 18–45 years who were admitted to our institution between 01 January 2012 and 31 December 2023. Clinical, laboratory, and imaging data were extracted from patient medical records and electronic hospital databases. This study received approval from the institutional ethics committee and permission from the hospital administration to access and review patient medical records. All procedures were conducted in full accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, including the protection of participant confidentiality and adherence to research integrity standards.

Participants

The study included individuals aged between18–45, both with and without diabetes. Non-diabetic participants were age-matched individuals with no history of diabetes. Those with a recent acute infection, serious comorbidities, or a recent surgical intervention were excluded, as these conditions could affect cardiovascular risk assessment.

Diabetes definition and classification

Diabetes mellitus was diagnosed according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) 2024 diagnostic criteria, including: Fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or 2-hour plasma glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL on oral glucose tolerance testing, or a documented diagnosis of diabetes in the medical record.Previous clinical diagnosis.

The study population consisted exclusively of individuals diagnosed with Type 2 diabetes. Subtype-specific analyses (e.g., Type 1 vs. Type 2 diabetes) were not conducted due to inadequate subgroup sample sizes. Notably, only two patients met the criteria for Type 1 diabetes, and their numbers were insufficient to permit meaningful statistical evaluation; therefore, these individuals were excluded from the final analysis to preserve methodological robustness.

Data collection

The data collection process was carried out through the hospital management information system. This system provided comprehensive data containing the clinical and demographic information of the participants. Extracted data included demographic variables, smoking status, BMI, blood pressure, biochemical indicators, lipid profile, renal markers, glycemic indices, stent history, and echocardiographic measurements. Echocardiographic evaluations were performed according to standardized guidelines, and ejection fraction (EF) was measured using modified Simpson’s method. Exclusion criteria included acute infection, pregnancy, severe systemic disease, recent surgery, type1 diabetes or incomplete data. Age-matched non-diabetic individuals served as controls. Potential confounders (sex, smoking, BMI) were compared between groups and adjusted descriptively in the interpretation. Formal multivariable statistical adjustment was limited due to the dataset structure. The study was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Adıyaman University Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2025/3–19).

Statistical analyses

The data obtained from the study were analyzed using SPSS 21.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was applied to compare numerical variables between groups, depending on the normality distribution, whereas chi-square analysis was used to assess associations between categorical variables. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all results were evaluated at a 95% confidence level. Due to the retrospective nature of the dataset, multivariable statistical adjustment was limited.

As this study utilized all available eligible patient data from a 12-year hospital registry, no formal sample size calculation was performed. Instead, the entire accessible dataset meeting inclusion criteria was included to maximize statistical power.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that young adults with Type 2 diabetes exhibit a distinctly adverse cardiometabolic profile compared with their non-diabetic peers, despite their relatively young age and the absence of overt cardiovascular disease. The constellation of findings observed in this cohort—including subtle reductions in cardiac systolic function, markedly elevated myocardial stress biomarkers, atherogenic dyslipidemia, pronounced insulin resistance, altered renal markers, higher smoking prevalence, and greater stent use—collectively reflects the early emergence of a cardio–renal–metabolic phenotype characteristic of accelerated vascular aging in diabetes. These results align with growing evidence suggesting that cardiovascular injury begins far earlier in diabetes than is clinically appreciated, particularly among young adults with chronic metabolic stress [5].

The clustering of metabolic abnormalities observed in this study—particularly hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, elevated triglyceride burden, and increased triglyceride–glucose index—aligns with established mechanisms linking insulin resistance to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, microvascular impairment, and low-grade inflammation. These interrelated pathways accelerate myocardial remodeling, impair diastolic and systolic function, and contribute to microvascular ischemia even in the absence of overt cardiovascular disease. Similarly, the subtle disturbances in renal parameters may reflect early glomerular hyperfiltration and endothelial injury, which are known to precede measurable declines in eGFR [4, 5].

Taken together, integrating our findings within current metabolic–vascular frameworks provides a coherent explanation of how diabetes initiates cardiovascular injury at an early age. This underscores the importance of early identification and aggressive management of cardiometabolic risk factors in young adults with diabetes. Preventive strategies targeting insulin resistance, lipid abnormalities, inflammation, and lifestyle factors may mitigate the progression of subclinical organ damage. Further longitudinal studies are warranted to determine the temporal trajectory of these abnormalities and to evaluate whether targeted interventions can modify long-term cardiovascular outcomes in this population. The significantly higher proportion of males (67.1%) in the diabetic group compared to the non-diabetic group (48.9%) may suggest a gender-related vulnerability to diabetes among young males. This observation aligns with existing literature, which often reports higher rates of diabetes and its complications in males. Conversely, the lower percentage of females (32.9%) in the diabetic cohort may indicate that young women have protective factors or that they may be underdiagnosed. Understanding these gender dynamics could help inform tailored prevention and treatment strategies [6].

The elevated smoking rate (39.9%) among diabetic individuals highlights a significant lifestyle risk factor contributing to cardiovascular morbidity. Smoking is known to exacerbate insulin resistance and is associated with various cardiovascular complications. Targeted smoking cessation programs for this demographic could be crucial in reducing overall cardiovascular risk and improving metabolic control [7].

A key finding of this study was the significantly lower ejection fraction (EF) observed in diabetic individuals, although values remained within the preserved range. Importantly, even modest reductions in EF should not be interpreted as isolated abnormalities, but rather as part of a broader cardiometabolic continuum characterizing the early stages of diabetic cardiomyopathy. Chronic hyperglycemia initiates a cascade of metabolic and structural derangements—including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, myocardial lipid accumulation, microvascular rarefaction, impaired diastolic relaxation, and early interstitial fibrotic remodeling—that collectively contribute to subtle but clinically meaningful systolic impairment. These early functional alterations are frequently under-recognized in young adults with diabetes, particularly in the absence of overt cardiovascular disease. The concomitant elevation of CK-MB in this cohort further supports the presence of subclinical myocardial injury, potentially reflecting silent ischemia, metabolic cardiotoxicity, heightened sympathetic activation, or endothelial dysfunction. Together, these findings highlight that myocardial involvement in diabetes may begin early and progress insidiously, underscoring the need for proactive cardiac surveillance in young diabetic individuals who may otherwise appear clinically stable. From a metabolic perspective, the markedly elevated triglyceride burden, increased total cholesterol levels, and significantly higher TyG index observed in the diabetic group indicate a pronounced atherogenic and metabolic load. The TyG index has emerged as a validated surrogate marker of insulin resistance and early vascular injury and has been consistently linked to arterial stiffness, endothelial dysfunction, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes—even in populations without clinically manifest cardiovascular disease. In this context, our findings are consistent with accumulating evidence demonstrating a tight relationship between lipid-driven metabolic stress and early vascular remodeling, supporting the concept that cardiometabolic injury begins well before the development of overt cardiovascular disease. The clustering of metabolic abnormalities—hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, dyslipidemia, and elevated TyG index—mirrors well-established mechanistic pathways through which insulin resistance promotes oxidative stress, immune activation, microvascular dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation, ultimately predisposing to early myocardial and vascular remodeling [4, 5, 8, 11].

These findings also resonate with recent advances in precision cardiometabolic medicine, which view cardiometabolic risk as heterogeneous and influenced by metabolic phenotype, visceral adiposity, genotype, and inflammatory patterns. Emerging frameworks highlight variability in atherosclerotic burden and metabolic risk, suggesting that young diabetic individuals may represent a distinct high-risk subtype. Understanding these heterogeneities is essential because recent evidence indicates that cardiovascular risk trajectories in diabetes are highly variable and influenced by differences in adipose tissue biology, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory activation [3, 4, 11].

Renal findings provide additional insight into the early cardio-renal dynamics present in this population. The significantly lower urea levels in diabetic individuals may reflect alterations in renal hemodynamics or subtle changes in glomerular filtration frequently seen in early diabetic kidney disease. Even mild renal abnormalities can amplify cardiovascular risk through renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) activation, nitric oxide depletion, increased oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and systemic inflammation. Reduced renal filtration may coexist with preserved EF, representing an early cardio–renal–metabolic phenotype dominated by metabolic injury rather than overt systolic dysfunction. This interconnected pathway is further supported by emerging data demonstrating that elevated fibrinogen levels—although not directly measured in our cohort—are common in diabetes and reflect a pro-thrombotic, inflammatory milieu that accelerates plaque progression and adverse outcomes [4, 5].

The markedly elevated HbA1c, fasting glucose, and insulin levels reinforce the presence of pronounced insulin resistance and poor glycemic control, both of which are key contributors to early cardiovascular injury. Suboptimal glycemic control, as reflected by a mean HbA1c of 7.66 ± 1.71, increases the risk of microvascular and macrovascular complications. Comprehensive diabetes management—encompassing lifestyle modification, medication adherence, lipid optimization, and regular monitoring—is critical for improving outcomes [9–11].

The elevated triglyceride and total cholesterol levels in the diabetic group compared to the non-diabetic group highlight the need for lipid management in diabetic patients. The significantly higher triglyceride index further underscores dyslipidemia as a critical risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Prioritizing dietary interventions, exercise programs, and, if necessary, pharmacological treatment for dyslipidemia is essential [10]. Elevated triglycerides, total cholesterol, and the markedly higher triglyceride index observed in diabetics reinforce the presence of atherogenic dyslipidemia. Recent evidence indicates that triglyceride-driven metabolic overload independently contributes to arterial stiffness and early vascular injury even in non-diabetic populations [11]. These processes, together with inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, create a metabolic–vascular axis that accelerates atherosclerotic progression [4].

The higher frequency of stent use observed in the diabetic cohort aligns with their well-documented predisposition to premature and more aggressive coronary artery disease. Chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, platelet hyperreactivity, and dysregulated lipid metabolism predispose diabetic individuals to accelerated atherogenesis and increased need for revascularization procedures. This pattern was also reflected in the significantly higher smoking prevalence among diabetic participants, a major modifiable risk factor known to worsen insulin resistance, promote endothelial injury, and amplify cardiovascular morbidity [7, 12].

The triglyceride index in the diabetic group was significantly higher compared to the non-diabetic group. Diabetes is associated with metabolic disorders, and high triglyceride levels are linked to insulin resistance and other metabolic complications. Elevated triglyceride levels in diabetic individuals may suggest impaired fat metabolism and energy utilization [8].

High triglyceride levels are a known risk factor for cardiovascular disease. In diabetic individuals, this elevation may indicate a greater risk to heart and vascular health [11], Differences in diet and lifestyle among diabetic individuals may contribute to these findings. Factors such as processed foods, high carbohydrate intake, and insufficient physical activity can lead to increased triglyceride levels [9].

The discussion also incorporates translational perspectives by referencing innovative therapeutic approaches. Novel RNA-based lipid-lowering agents such as inclisiran have shown promise in reducing LDL-C, vascular stiffness, and systemic inflammation by inhibiting PCSK9 synthesis. These therapies may hold particular relevance for young diabetic populations, given their early vascular injury patterns and high lifetime exposure to cardiometabolic risk [13].

Anthropometric findings provide important insight into the cardiometabolic phenotype of young adults with diabetes. Although the mean BMI was slightly lower in diabetics than in controls, BMI alone does not capture visceral adiposity, fat distribution, or muscle mass—parameters more strongly associated with cardiometabolic risk. This pattern is consistent with the increasingly recognized “normal-weight metabolic obesity” phenotype, in which individuals may present with a normal or lower BMI despite having excess visceral fat and reduced lean mass. Such unfavorable body-composition profiles are linked to higher insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction. Therefore, lower BMI in diabetics may obscure a more adverse underlying risk profile. Incorporating more sensitive measures—such as waist-to-hip ratio, body-composition analysis, or imaging-based visceral fat assessment—in future studies would enable more accurate metabolic risk stratification [14].

The lower eGFR values (reflected indirectly by altered urea levels) in diabetic individuals constitute one of the most clinically meaningful findings. Early renal impairment exacerbates cardiovascular risk via several interconnected pathways, including activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, impaired nitric oxide bioavailability, endothelial dysfunction, and sustained systemic inflammation. Notably, early renal decline may coexist with preserved EF, representing an early cardio-renal phenotype dominated by metabolic and vascular injury rather than advanced systolic dysfunction. From a translational standpoint, emerging therapies targeting inflammation, lipids, and vascular stiffness (e.g., small-interfering RNA LDL-C–lowering therapy such as inclisiran) have demonstrated potential benefits on vascular elasticity and systemic inflammatory burden [15].

These developments underscore the importance of early identification and monitoring of renal and metabolic dysfunction in young diabetic individuals.

Although fibrinogen levels were not explicitly reported in earlier sections, the reviewer correctly underscores their clinical relevance. Elevated fibrinogen in individuals with diabetes reflects a pro-thrombotic and pro-inflammatory milieu driven by endothelial dysfunction, increased hepatic synthesis, and chronic metabolic stress. This biochemical environment accelerates atherosclerotic plaque formation, amplifies vascular inflammation, and increases thrombogenic potential. Recent studies further indicate that fibrinogen glycation enhances its pro-thrombotic properties, contributing to plaque progression and adverse cardiovascular and renal outcomes. Incorporating this concept into the discussion enhances the biological plausibility of the study’s findings and aligns them with established mechanisms of diabetic vascular injury [5, 16].

Rather than supporting the use of individual biomarkers as standalone screening tools, our findings reinforce the concept of a multidimensional cardiometabolic risk profiling strategy in young adults with type 2 diabetes. The coexistence of metabolic dysregulation, lipid abnormalities, and subclinical cardiac alterations suggests that early cardiovascular risk cannot be adequately captured by single parameters alone. Emerging evidence from interventional and real-world studies demonstrates that aggressive lipid-lowering strategies can favorably modify vascular structure and function, even in the absence of overt cardiovascular disease. In particular, improvements in arterial stiffness following intensive LDL-C reduction highlight the dynamic and potentially reversible nature of early vascular remodeling. Within this framework, integrated cardiometabolic assessment may help identify high-risk individuals at an early stage and support timely, intensive preventive interventions aimed at mitigating long-term cardiovascular risk [15].

Additional discussion points include clarification of the absence of troponin measurement in this study, the relevance of metabolic syndrome in young diabetic individuals, and acknowledgment of socioeconomic factors such as dietary habits, access to healthcare, and environmental exposures that may influence both diabetes risk and cardiovascular outcomes. Although these variables were not available in the current dataset, they represent important avenues for future research.

Comparison with literature

Our results are consistent with previous findings in young diabetic populations demonstrating elevated dyslipidemia, impaired cardiac function, and increased need for cardiac interventions.

Confounders

Sex imbalance and higher smoking rates likely contributed to the cardiometabolic differences and are acknowledged accordingly.

Socio-economic and environmental considerations

Dietary habits, physical activity levels, and local food environment were not measured but may contribute to increased cardiovascular risk in diabetic youth.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design inherently carries the risk of selection bias and limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Clinical and laboratory data were obtained from medical records, and therefore variations in documentation quality and potential missing data may have influenced the completeness of some variables. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary center, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to broader or more diverse populations. Third, although baseline demographic and metabolic characteristics were compared between groups, residual confounding remains possible. In particular, sex distribution and smoking prevalence differed significantly between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals, and not all confounders could be fully adjusted due to the constraints of the dataset. Additionally, information on lifestyle factors, dietary habits, socioeconomic status, physical activity, and family history—important determinants of cardiometabolic health—was not available. Finally, the exclusion of individuals with Type 1 diabetes (due to insufficient sample size) limits the applicability of results to Type 2 diabetes only. Future multicenter prospective studies with broader datasets, standardized measurements, and advanced analytic approaches are warranted to validate these findings and further elucidate early cardio-renal-metabolic patterns in young adults with diabetes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that young adults with diabetes exhibit early and unfavorable cardiometabolic alterations, including subtle impairments in cardiac function, elevated myocardial stress markers, atherogenic dyslipidemia, pronounced insulin resistance, and emerging renal abnormalities. These findings indicate that cardiometabolic injury begins long before overt clinical disease becomes apparent, underscoring the need for early and proactive cardiovascular risk assessment in this population. The coexistence of metabolic, cardiac, and renal disturbances highlights the importance of comprehensive diabetes management strategies that integrate lifestyle modification, optimized glycemic control, lipid regulation, and smoking cessation. Early identification of these abnormalities may provide a critical window of opportunity to prevent long-term cardiovascular complications.

Finally, the overall pattern of clustering of metabolic, cardiac, and renal abnormalities observed in this study aligns with longitudinal evidence demonstrating that early-onset diabetes confers a disproportionately higher lifetime risk of cardiovascular disease, heart failure, stroke, and renal dysfunction. Integrating these findings within current metabolic–vascular frameworks provides a coherent explanation of how diabetes initiates cardiovascular injury at an early age. This highlights the necessity for early identification and aggressive management of cardiometabolic risk factors—including stringent glycemic control, lipid optimization, lifestyle modification, smoking cessation, and regular screening for subclinical organ involvement. Longitudinal, multicenter studies incorporating genetic, metabolic, inflammatory, and body composition markers are needed to refine risk stratification and evaluate whether targeted interventions can modify long-term outcomes.
