Development of a Unified Framework for Evaluating the Quality of Physician Communication in Prostate Cancer Treatment Consultations
Authors: Alexander Hernandez-Tirado, Renning Zheng, Michael Luu, Nadine A Friedrich, Paul Kokorowski, Judy Tan, Timothy J Daskivich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Physicians often fail to communicate important details about key risks and benefits of treatment in prostate cancer consultations, an information gap that may inhibit effective patient participation in care decisions. We developed a framework to assess detail and personalization of physician communication of key concepts, derived from observations of treatment consultations.Methods:We recorded and transcribed treatment consultations of 50 men with newly diagnosed prostate cancer across 10 multidisciplinary providers. Using open coding, analysts identified statements related to guideline-endorsed SDM concepts and categorized the level of detail for each topic. Concept-specific hierarchies indicating increasing detail were integrated into a unified framework. We reported the highest observed level for each concept across consultations.Results:Across 19,388 statements, sentences addressing cancer severity (1,016; 5.2%), baseline function (186; 1.0%), oncologic endpoints (343; 1.8%), cancer prognosis (318; 1.6%), life expectancy (111; 0.57%), and treatment-related side effects (952; 4.9%) were extracted. Empirically derived frameworks for evaluating the level of detail and personalization for each concept consistently displayed parallel levels of detail. We created a unified framework, with each level providing more precise, patient-specific risk communication: (0) omission, (1) no quantification, (2) binary assessment, (3) imprecise quantification, (4) specific quantification, and (5) patient-specific estimate. The prevalence of the highest observed level of detail and personalization conveyed varied by concept, ranging from 4% to 84%, 0% to 47%, 0% to 42%, 2% to 8%, 0% to 48%, and 0% to 66% for levels 0 to 5, respectively.Conclusions:Our framework introduces an empirically derived, structured foundation for characterizing the level of detail and personalization of physician risk communication of key concepts in prostate cancer consultations.Keywords: prostate cancer, shared decision making, risk communication, physician-patient communication, communication framework

Introduction

Shared decision making (SDM), the process in which physicians partner with patients to make treatment decisions aligning with patients’ preferences and values, is the guidelines-endorsed standard of care in prostate cancer treatment consultations.1,2

SDM holds particular importance for prostate cancer patients, who require adequate detail and personalization of key concepts to make informed, preference-sensitive decisions. The American Urology Association (AUA) recommends that clinicians discuss 5 key concepts with patients, including life expectancy, treatment-related side effects (SEs), cancer severity, baseline function, and oncologic endpoints, such as recurrence and likelihood of cure. 2 These domains represent critical elements of prostate cancer treatment decisions. Life expectancy informs treatment eligibility, cancer severity guides treatment intensity, treatment-related SEs anticipate potential harms, baseline function sets expectations, and prognosis helps balance these elements between cancer control and quality of life.3,4 However, urologists often fail to incorporate key elements of SDM in their clinical encounters, and even when the concepts are discussed, there is substantial heterogeneity in the level of detail and personalization of the data that physicians communicate to patients.5,6 For example, in a qualitative analysis of 42 consultations of men with newly diagnosed prostate cancer, we recently reported that life expectancy, cancer prognosis, and treatment-related SEs were found to be omitted or not quantified in 17 %, 24%, and 12% to 68% of prostate cancer consultations, respectively.7,8 When risks were mentioned, the level of detail varied widely, ranging from a generalization (“low”/”high”) to patient-specific, quantified estimates. While not all clinical scenarios require precise risk estimates, some evidence suggests that patients desire tailored, numeric information to meaningfully participate in SDM.9,10

The heterogeneity of physician communication observed in practice has highlighted the absence of standardized tools to assess the level of detail provided and personalization of data in prostate cancer treatment consultations. In parallel, prior work has described a wide range of observer-based coding systems developed to assess patient–physician interactions, but these tools vary substantially in clinical contexts and coding.11 –13 Importantly, most existing systems are designed to assess or score general communication behaviors and are not tailored to evaluating the content-specific risk concepts in prostate cancer treatment decisions. Accordingly, we sought to develop a framework that captures the level of detail and personalization of the data in these consultations. To accomplish this, we used data from two previously published observational studies that reported on variation in physician communication of life expectancy, cancer prognosis, and treatment-related SEs and applied the same methodology to additional American Urological Association–endorsed concepts for SDM, including cancer severity, assessment of baseline sexual and urinary function, and oncologic endpoints (eg, recurrence, metastasis, and likelihood of cure). Drawing on insights from these studies, we combined observations into a unified hierarchical framework for assessing the level of detail in prostate cancer treatment decision-making discussions that would be applicable to all concepts. We hypothesized that the individual and unified hierarchies could allow for structured assessments of the level of detail and personalization of data that physicians communicate during clinical encounters, providing a foundation for future work that links these levels to patient comprehension, decisional outcomes, and SDM, and that may inform individual clinicians about the informational content of their own consultations.

Methods

Study Design

After obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board, we audio-recorded and transcribed the initial treatment consultation of 50 patients diagnosed with clinically localized prostate cancer between January 2019 and December 2020. Eligible patients were native English speakers older than 18 y who had a new diagnosis of clinically localized prostate cancer. To ensure a maximum variation sample, we first approached and consented 4 urologists, 4 medical oncologists, and 2 radiation oncologists at our institution, a tertiary care center. Then, treating clinicians and study investigators identified potentially eligible patients at the time of their initial pathologic diagnosis and consented them before their treatment counseling discussion. For each participating patient–physician pair, an audio recorder was placed in the consultation room and started by study staff or the treating physician when the physician entered the room and stopped when the physician left. Importantly, participation was voluntary, and patients or clinicians could decline without affecting care. The study population reflected a typical baseline demographic of the general prostate cancer population (Supplementary Table 1).

Development of Individual Hierarchies for Key Concepts

A portion of the data for the unified hierarchy was based on our previously published observational studies noting variation in communication for life expectancy, cancer mortality, and treatment-related SEs in 2 independent studies.1,2 In those studies, the data for these concepts were qualitatively analyzed, and concept-specific hierarchies were developed. To ensure consistency and comparability across concepts, we standardized all hierarchies to 6 ordered levels and applied conceptually equivalent, uniform terminology across the levels of detail, reflecting the depth and personalization of information conveyed by physicians in consultations (Supplementary Table 2). We then developed distinct, new hierarchies for additional key concepts in SDM for prostate cancer that have not been previously reported, including cancer severity (tumor risk and pathology results), oncologic endpoints beyond mortality (metastasis, progression and recurrence), and baseline function assessments (urinary, sexual, and bowel function) using the same methodological approach. We first manually extracted physician quotes related to each key concept from consultation transcripts. Multiple coders then used an inductive coding approach to empirically categorize levels of informational detail. Coders (R.Z., A.B.) then met to create a consensus codebook, then reapplied codes to the entire dataset. Coders then met to adjudicate any discrepancies in consensus coding of individual quotes. Disagreements were resolved through group discussion until consensus was achieved. The adjudicated dataset was used as our final analytic sample. We then calculated the highest level of informational detail conveyed across the entire consultation for each key concept using counts and proportions at the consultation level. Thus, each hierarchy was developed de novo from the raw transcripts for its specific concept, rather than by generalizing a single hierarchy across topics. Coding and data management were conducted using ATLAS.ti qualitative analysis software. In prior analyses of this same dataset focused on life expectancy, cancer prognosis, and treatment-related SEs, thematic saturation was reached after approximately 20 consultations, which informed the adequacy of the sample size for the present study. 7

Development of the Unified Hierarchy

The entire team (R.Z., A.B., T.J.D.) then met to establish a unified hierarchy reflecting increasing granularity in communication using a consensus approach. This step was undertaken after individual, concept-specific hierarchies had been developed. The process involved synthesizing the individual hierarchies for each key concept to create a unified hierarchical framework that would provide a generalized structure that would be applicable to all key concepts while preserving the ability to capture progressively more precise and patient-specific communication (Supplementary Figure 1).

Results

Our analytic sample included 19,388 sentences from 50 consultations, of which 1,016 were related to cancer severity (5.2%), 186 to baseline function (1.0%), 343 to oncologic endpoints (1.8%), 318 to cancer prognosis (1.6%), 111 to life expectancy (0.57%), and 952 to treatment-related SEs (4.9%). Consultations had an average transcript length of approximately 6,500 words, corresponding to an average consultation duration of approximately 45 min. Our cohort included 10% of men with low risk, 36% favorable intermediate risk, and 40% with unfavorable intermediate-risk disease, with a median age of 67 y and 18% identifying as Black.

Hierarchies for Cancer Severity

Cancer severity included separate categories for communication of tumor risk and pathology results. The individual communication hierarchies (along with frequencies of occurrence at a consult level) for tumor risk and pathology results ranged from (0) not mentioned (8% and 4%, for tumor risk and pathology results, respectively), (1) name only (2% and 0%), (2) generalization (0% and 8%), (3) risk group/Gleason score without qualification (12% and 6%), (4) risk group/Gleason score with qualification (12% and 22%), to (5) risk group/Gleason score with qualification and key associated information (66% and 60%) (Table 1).

The level of detail varied significantly across these categories. The “name-only” level provided background information without patient-specific results, while “generalization” used vague description (ie, “good,” “found cancer”) without further explanation (Table 1). While tumor risk group (eg, “low risk”) and Gleason score (eg, Gleason 3 + 4 = 7) without qualification directly described the tumor risk and pathology categories, more detailed levels required explanation of the qualifications for the classification (eg, “low risk because of PSA, Gleason grade, and clinical stage”) and key associated information (guidelines-concordant treatments for risk group and additional details on pathologic data such as multifocality or percentage of tumor core volume for pathology results).

The level of detail also varied significantly across these categories. The “name only” level introduced the concept without further details, whereas “generalization” used indistinct descriptive phrasing to characterize magnitude (ie, “It’s possible that things won’t progress, actually, and that it stays.”) (Table 2). More detailed levels included using a probability to quantify risk but differed in the how specific the reference time point was. Category (3) described probabilities without reference to a time point (ie, “The likelihood that this would spread to your lymph nodes or seminal vesicles is very low, 3 percent.”). Category (4) advanced this by providing probabilities tied to an arbitrary time point (ie, “Now the likelihood of recurrence after a good prostate surgery, good radiation is the same, and it’s about 30% over a ten-year period.”). Category (5) described a timeline tailored to patient-specific demographic and clinical characteristics, such as age, PSA, tumor characteristics, and life expectancy, ideally informed by risk calculators or nomograms (ie, “At ten years with your PSA, your age, reaching to the age of 71, there is 50% chance that the cancer comes back.”).

In the “name only” level, assessments were often minimal, lacking both open- and close-ended questions to explore baseline function (Table 3). In the “generalization” level, physicians asked broad, nonspecific questions (ie, “Do you have any issues with bowel movements?”). The “specific questions” level included more targeted questions but still lacked comprehensive evaluation. In the “review of nonvalidated scoring estimate” mode, physicians typically asked patients to rate their function (ie, 1 to 10). The highest level, “review of validated questionnaire,” involved the use of standardized instruments such as the International Prostate Symptom Score for urinary function and the International Index of Erectile Function for sexual function (ie, “Your AUA symptom score is only 4 and didn’t really impact your life.”).

Unified Hierarchy

Discussion

In patients with clinically localized prostate cancer, clear communication of treatment risks and benefits is critical for patients to make informed decisions. However, findings from our previous studies reveal substantial heterogeneity in how life expectancy, cancer mortality, and side effects are communicated to patients.7,8 The findings of the current study show that physician communication of baseline urinary, sexual, and bowel function; cancer severity; and oncologic endpoints beyond mortality is similarly heterogeneous. However, within this heterogeneity, we identified a parallel structure in the level of detail that was common to all risk communication domains, which provided the foundation for developing a unified, hierarchical framework to characterize the level of detail and personalization of physician communication across all topics. The unified framework consists of 6 levels of informational detail, ranging from (0) omission, (1) name only, (2) binary assessment, (3) imprecise quantification, (4) precise quantification, to (5) patient-specific communication. Within this generalized framework, there are some levels that include details that are unique to each topic; for example, for life expectancy, “imprecise communication” is defined as a “rough number of years,” while for cancer mortality, the same score is defined as “probability of cancer mortality with or without treatment only.” However, other levels are defined commonly across all topics; for example, “name only” was defined as mention of the risk communication domain by name only (ie, without any form of quantification) across all topics. Importantly, we calculated the frequency of use of each level of the hierarchy in clinical practice to justify our empiric categorization.

Each concept included in the framework represents a critical domain in decision making for prostate cancer treatment, as recommended by AUA guidelines.1,2 Life expectancy is fundamental in treatment eligibility, as patients with limited life expectancy may not benefit from aggressive treatment modalities. 3 Similarly, treatment choice is tied to cancer severity, as disease risk must be accounted for during counseling and decision making to align the intensity of treatment with severity of disease, thus avoiding under- or overtreatment. Prognosis, including mortality, metastases, progression, and recurrence, informs patients about the potential risk domains that are relevant for their decisions, allowing them to consider factors such as length versus quality of life.14,15 Baseline function (urinary, sexual, and bowel) is a strong predictor of posttreatment outcomes and plays a crucial role in setting expectations for recovery and quality of life. 4 Likewise, treatment-related side effects must be discussed in depth to help patients anticipate and weigh potential harms. These concepts collectively form the informational foundation needed for patients to engage meaningfully in SDM.

Creation of a hierarchy for characterizing the level of detail in physician communication is a critical step to improving communication of these key concepts. Failure to communicate these key concepts can leave patients misinformed and unprepared for SDM, as they may not fully understand the potential short- and long-term consequences of their treatment choices. 2 The fact that physicians had such broad heterogeneity in observed communication practices may owe to the lack of a clear standard for risk communication that would act as a reference for best practice. While we acknowledge that physicians might choose to communicate some aspects of risk with fewer details in some situations—for example, communication of secondary malignancy associated with radiation to a patient with low-risk disease preferring active surveillance—the persistence of heterogeneity across tumor risk subgroups that we observed in all of our studies would argue that physicians are not selectively tailoring the level of detail for certain situations.7,8 More broadly, we acknowledge that higher levels of our framework do not represent universally preferable targets. Whether and to what degree higher levels of informational detail translate into improved patient comprehension and meaningful participation in SDM remains to be determined and represents an important direction for future research.

Importantly, we used an empiric approach to develop our framework. Each level of the framework was derived from observations of patient–physician encounters and built upon common communication patterns seen across multiple concepts. Although the content of the discussion of each concept naturally differed, we noted parallel trends in how the information was conveyed, ranging from complete omission to patient-specific discussion. Moreover, each of the six levels was identified for each concept across the consultations, although it may not have been the highest level identified across the consultation. Our framework thus emerges organically from the qualitative analysis of real-world treatment consultations. The framework was also supplemented by patient interviews exploring communication preferences for life expectancy, where we obtained patient views on how information should be optimal. 9 Altogether, this analysis aligns physician behavior with patient preferences and codifies it into a practical tool.

To support patient-specific communication, we proposed several critical points for each topic. For tumor risk and pathology results, physicians should (1) present patient-specific results, (2) provide qualification for the results, and (3) explain important information associated with the results, such as guideline-concordant treatment for tumor risk and core sampling for pathology results. When communicating risk of progression, metastasis, and mortality, clinicians should (1) mention and briefly describe the risks; (2) quantify the risks by probabilities, ideally accounting for patient characteristics such as age and comorbidities; and (3) associate these probabilities with a specific time point. When available, physicians should use standardized risk calculators, nomograms, and validated prognostic tools as they provide reproducible estimates that incorporate patient-specific clinical and demographic characteristics. For baseline function assessment, validated questionnaires should be used to ensure a comprehensive and reliable estimate. While it may seem challenging to cover all critical points in communication, we have observed real-life examples in which these points are summarized succinctly in a few sentences (see quotes corresponding to the highest hierarchy levels in Tables 1–3). As such, structured training equipping physicians with the elements of detailed, patient-specific communication represents a practical and efficient strategy for improving informational depth in clinical practice. Understanding the current distribution of informational detail across these topics provides a necessary foundation for future work examining how the depth and personalization of risk communication relate to SDM outcomes. These findings could identify the most critical gaps in physician communication and guide the development of targeted feedback and training programs.

This framework has potential applications in both the clinical and research settings of prostate cancer care.

Our prior work provides a foundation for this effort. In studies of life expectancy, cancer prognosis, and treatment-related survival benefit, we developed concept-specific hierarchies and proposed practical strategies for achieving higher levels of informational detail when communicating these concepts. 7 These hierarchies, while derived empirically, reflect the AUA/ASTRO guideline recommendations that clinicians should inform patients about these key domains, quantify them, include timelines for side effects, include comparisons of the natural history of disease when discussing treatment outcomes, and provide patient-specific estimates as a core component of SDM. 2 However, it is an important limitation that these levels lack data regarding patient preference (with the exception of life expectancy) and relationship to SDM and comprehension outcomes. Future research examining how specific levels of our unified framework relate to patient comprehension and SDM outcomes will help to identify optimal levels of informational detail, a finding that could enhance the framework from a descriptive tool reflecting guideline recommendations to one that is rooted in patient preferences and outcomes. Given the resource and training requirements of manually applying the framework, future work should evaluate automated or artificial intelligence–assisted approaches in clinical trial settings to determine whether scalable and reliable assessment of physician communication is feasible in routine practice. Consequently, we have developed and validated practical methods to operationalize this hierarchical framework in a scalable manner using trained natural language processing models to automatically identify and categorize key elements of risk communication in prostate cancer treatment consultations. 16 We also extended this approach using large language models to directly apply this hierarchy and assign sentence-level scores on the level of detail of physician risk communication. 17 Finally, this hierarchical framework could be adapted and applied to study the level of detail in other topics in urology or other medical fields. Future studies are needed to evaluate how use of this framework affects patient understanding, visit length, and downstream SDM outcomes as well as to identify strategies for efficiently conveying complex information in a clear and detailed manner.

This study presents some limitations to consider. Physician characteristics, such as specialty training, experience, and communication style, may affect how they communicate; because our data were collected in an academic medical center, findings may not be generalizable to other clinical settings. However, given the breadth of informational detail represented in the hierarchies and their universality across topics, we believe it is unlikely that practice setting will affect the hierarchy levels but may affect the distribution of levels of information in physician communication. While informational detail and personalization are critical components of SDM, there are other important aspects of SDM, such as clarity and accuracy, that were not captured by this framework and warrant further study. In addition, patient factors such as primary language, health literacy, and cultural background may influence both the way information is communicated and how it is received and processed; further research is needed to determine the optimal approach of communication for these groups.

Conclusions

Our framework provides a structured, empirically derived approach to characterizing the depth and personalization of physician risk communication across key concepts in prostate cancer consultations. By describing the current distribution of informational detail in clinical practice, it establishes a foundation for future research examining how specific levels of communication relate to patient comprehension and SDM outcomes, findings that could ultimately inform targeted feedback and physician training. Its development highlights the absence of standardized descriptive tools for characterizing risk communication in prostate cancer care. With potential applications in both clinical and research settings, this framework may also be adapted to improve communication practices across other areas of urology and medicine.
