Seasonal dynamics of hedgehog-borne ticks and severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus in Beijing's urban parks
Authors: Chunzheng Li, Hongyue Li, Yuanchi Ye, Lianglong Zhu, Aihua Zheng, Xing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV), an emerging tick-borne pathogen, has caused a rising number of human cases in the urban-rural fringe of Beijing since 2021. This study explores the seasonal dynamics of hedgehog-associated ticks and SFTSV transmission in urban parks of Beijing. Surveys across six parks revealed distinct activity patterns: adult Haemaphysalis longicornis peaked in summer, while nymphs dominated spring and autumn. All collected H. longicornis belonged to parthenogenetic populations. A near-complete SFTSV genome (C4 strain) was identified in a tick collected from Taoranting Park, suggesting multiple viral introductions into Beijing. Serological analysis showed that > 50% of hedgehogs carried SFTSV-neutralizing antibodies in spring; yet seropositivity declined markedly in summer and autumn, indicating recurrent infections and implicating hedgehogs as potential reservoirs. These findings reveal an urban SFTSV transmission cycle maintained by hedgehogs and parthenogenetic H. longicornis, emphasizing the urgency of enhanced surveillance and public health interventions to curb urban zoonotic risks.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV), a member of the genus Bandavirus (family Phenuiviridae, order Bunyavirales), was first identified in China in 2009 as the causative agent of a febrile illness characterized by thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, gastrointestinal symptoms, and potential fatal outcomes due to multi-organ failure (Yu et al., 2011; Zhuang et al., 2018). Since its discovery, SFTSV has spread across East Asia, with reported cases in South Korea, Japan, Vietnam, and Pakistan (Tran et al., 2019; Zohaib et al., 2020; Denic et al., 2011; Takahashi et al., 2014). In China, the virus initially emerged in the Dabie Mountain region but has since expanded to 27 provinces, with Shandong, Anhui, Hubei, Henan, and Liaoning now recognized as major epidemic hotspots (Sun et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2020).

The Asian longhorned tick (Haemaphysalis longicornis), a key vector for SFTSV, exhibits a broad host range spanning wild mammals and livestock (Heath, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020). H. longicornis is mainly distributed in eastern China, Japan, the Russia Far East and South Korea (Hoogstraal et al., 1968; Zhang et al., 2012; Park et al., 2014). Over the past century, new populations have been established in Australia, New Zealand and some island countries in the Western Pacific Region (Hoogstraal et al., 1968; Tufts et al., 2019). It was first detected in New Jersey in the United States (U.S.) in 2017, and then spread rapidly across several eastern states of the United States (Beard et al., 2018; Rainey et al., 2018). By 2022, H. longicornis had been reported in 19 eastern and central U.S. states (Egizi et al., 2020; Hubbard, 2024). Studies have shown that H. longicornis is the primary vector and reservoir of SFTSV in East Asia (Park et al., 2014; Zhuang et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2015). Meanwhile, H. longicornis is one of the few tick species with both parthenogenetic and sexual reproductive populations (Oliver, 1971). The parthenogenetic population of H. longicornis originated in northern Japan (Hoogstraal et al., 1968). Karyotype analysis showed that the sexual reproductive population was diploid chromosome group, while the parthenogenetic population was triploid chromosome group, and the formation mechanism was not clear (Oliver et al., 1973). At present, both parthenogenetic and sexual populations can be found in East Asia, while only parthenogenetic populations have been found in Oceania and the United States (Egizi et al., 2020; Hubbard, 2024). Recent studies have indicated that the distribution of parthenogenetic populations of H. longicornis in China is severely underestimated. At the same time, it was found that the spread rate of parthenogenetic population was significantly higher than that of sexual population, and the distribution of parthenogenetic population was positively correlated with the occurrence of SFTS cases. These findings further demonstrated that the rapid spread of SFTSV is closely related to the expansion of parthenogenetic H. longicornis population (Zhang et al., 2022).

Our recent study showed that SFTSV is gradually spreading to urban areas, particularly in densely populated megacities. Parthenogenetic populations of H. longicornis and SFTSV viral nucleic acid fragments have been detected in the outer suburbs and urban area of Beijing (Zhang et al., 2023). On the other hand, although many domestic animals and companion animals exhibit high SFTSV seropositivity rates, we identified hedgehog as the primary host driving SFTSV expansion in China (Zhao et al., 2022). Hedgehogs are widely distributed across China, covering SFTSV-endemic areas. In recent years, hedgehogs have become increasingly common in cities including Beijing (Zhao et al., 2022). In Beijing, the first local SFTS case occurred in Mentougou District in 2021 (Sun et al., 2023); subsequent local cases were reported in Pinggu, Tongzhou, Fengtai, Shijingshan, and other districts in 2023 and 2024 (unpublished data, Beijing CDC). In 2023, we conducted a field survey of ticks and hedgehogs within the Second to Fifth Ring Roads of Beijing to assess whether a natural transmission cycle had formed in urban areas. Our survey revealed a diverse tick population in city parks, including H. longicornis—the primary SFTSV vector. Parthenogenetic H. longicornis, known for its role in the rapid spread of SFTSV, was detected in key locations near the city centre, such as Beihai Park and Taoranting Park. Notably, high SFTSV seroprevalence and viral RNA prevalence were observed in hedgehogs and their parasitic ticks in the suburbs of Beijing (Yuan et al., 2024). These findings underscore the establishment of an urban SFTSV transmission cycle, driven by hedgehogs and invasive parthenogenetic ticks. This necessitates urgent investigations into the seasonal vector-host-pathogen interactions.

In this study, we conducted year-round monitoring of H. longicornis population dynamics and SFTSV prevalence in urban parks of Beijing. By elucidating seasonal patterns of tick activity, viral persistence, and hedgehog serological profiles, we aim to provide evidence for targeted surveillance and mitigate zoonotic risks in densely populated megacities.

Results

Surveillance on hedgehog-borne ticks

During the hedgehog activity season (April to October 2024), a total of 1834 ticks were collected from the body surfaces of hedgehogs in six urban parks of Beijing (Fig. 1). Of these, 81 were Haemaphysalis longicornis (4.4%), 1277 were Haemaphysalis flava (69.6%), and 476 were Dermacentor sinicus (26.0%). H. flava are the most predominant species, accounting for more than two-thirds of the total collected ticks. Dermacentor sinicus: This tick species was the second most abundant in urban areas of Beijing. The seasonal distribution of this tick at different life stages was similar to that of H. flava tick. We found that almost all adult D. sinicus ticks were collected in spring and autumn. In terms of quantity, there was a distinct peak in the parasitism of adult D. sinicus ticks in spring (April, May, and June), with a secondary peak in autumn. Nymphs and larvae of D. sinicus ticks were only collected in July and August during summer, indicating that summer is the primary season for nymphal and larval parasitism of D. sinicus ticks. In terms of distribution, D. sinicus was more abundant in the southern part of the city, primarily in Temple of Heaven and Taoranting parks, whereas fewer individuals were found in the northern parts of Beijing (Olympic Forest and Haidian parks). No D. sinicus ticks were collected in Zizhuyuan Park (Fig. 2D–F).

Haemaphysalis longicornis: This tick species was the least abundant among those species collected in urban areas of Beijing, accounting for less than 5% of the total. Our survey found that H. longicornis were collected from all six parks, with 29 individuals in Temple of Heaven Park, 18 in Beihai Park, 14 in Olympic Forest Park, 8 in Taoranting Park, 7 in Zizhuyuan Park, and 5 in Haidian Park. In terms of seasonal distribution, adult H. longicornis ticks were most abundant in June, which is significantly different from the spring and autumn adult peaks of H. flava and D. sinicus. Nymphal ticks were most abundant in September during autumn, with peaks also occurring in April and May during spring. Since October, H. longicornis parasitizing hedgehogs became difficult to collect. Only one larval H. longicornis tick was collected from Beihai Park across the entire 2024 survey. Ploidy analysis indicated that all H. longicornis ticks collected from the six parks belonged to parthenogenetic populations (Fig. 2G–I).

SFTSV prevalence in ticks and hedgehogs

From April to October 2024, a total of 87 hedgehogs from six parks (spanning the Second to Fifth Ring Roads in Beijing urban area) and assayed for SFTSV seroprevalence against the recombinant SFTSV AH12 strain. Of these, 30 hedgehogs (from all six parks) tested positive for SFTSV-neutralizing antibodies, with the highest titer (of about 100) detected in hedgehog from Temple of Heaven Park within the Second Ring Road (Fig. 3). Discussion

SFTSV, an emerging tick-borne pathogen, has intensified public health risks in Beijing, with a marked increase in urban-rural fringe cases since 2021 (Yuan et al., 2024). While previous studies have identified SFTSV circulation in suburban regions, the dynamics of urban transmission remain poorly understood. Given the challenges of sampling free-living ticks due to urban park management practices, we only conducted this investigation of hedgehog-borne ticks and SFTSV seasonality characteristics in Beijing's core urban areas. Our findings provide critical insights into the urban ecology of SFTSV and underscore the urgent need for enhanced surveillance.

Contrary to expectations, H. longicornis—the primary SFTSV vector—was not dominant in urban parks. Instead, H. flava and D. sinicus predominated on hedgehogs, exhibiting distinct seasonal activity: adult H. flava peaked in spring and autumn, with nymphs following this pattern and larvae surging in autumn; in contrast, D. sinicus nymphs/larvae peaked in summer. For H. longicornis, adult populations peaked in summer (June–August), which is consistent with the seasonal dynamics of free-living populations in other regions (Zhang et al., 2022). Although the numbers of H. longicornis in urban areas remain low, their detection across all six surveyed parks in 2024—exclusively parthenogenetic populations—signals ongoing range expansion. This spread raises concerns, as evidenced by the amplification of a near-complete SFTSV genome (C4 strain) from a Taoranting Park tick, contrasting with the suburban C2 strains identified in 2023 (Yuan et al., 2024). Additionally, the phylogenetic divergence of this C4 strain from the C1 strain detected in a 2021 Beijing patient further confirms multiple introductions of SFTSV, which complicates regional control efforts.

Among major wild animals in Beijing's urban areas, rodent populations are well-controlled, and predatory mammals are largely limited to yellow weasels—both of which host few ticks. Consequently, hedgehogs emerge as the primary tick hosts in spring. Once infected with SFTSV during this season, hedgehogs create a critical time window for tick infection. Simultaneously, this period enables hedgehogs to be reinfected and potentially multiply infected, establishing them as key hosts for urban SFTSV circulation (Zhao et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2024). In this study, hedgehog serological profiles revealed striking seasonal dynamics of neutralizing antibodies: seropositivity exceeded 50% in spring (April–May) and declined sharply in summer and autumn, except for a minor peak in August (Fig. 3). This aligns with laboratory findings that hedgehog have transient immunity to SFTSV of approximately 40 days (Zhao et al., 2022), facilitating recurrent SFTSV infections and implicating hedgehogs as critical reservoirs. However, urban hedgehogs exhibited lower neutralizing antibody titers compared to suburban counterparts near SFTS case clusters, suggesting nascent urban viral circulation. The absence of reported urban SFTS cases likely reflects low H. longicornis densities, reduced viral titers, and limited human-tick contact in well-maintained parks.

This study also have some limitations. Small sample sizes and park-specific tick control measures (e.g., Beihai Park's post-June tick decline) may introduce bias into estimates of tick abundance. Another limitation is ecological complexity: birds and stray cats may contribute to urban SFTSV transmission cycles, yet ethical and logistical barriers have hindered investigations into their role in these cycles.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the seasonal dynamics of hedgehog-associated ticks and SFTSV in Beijing's urban parks. Although H. longicornis remains scarce in these areas, its expanding parthenogenetic populations and the detection of a near-full length genome of SFTSV in central Beijing highlight escalating urban transmission risks. Hedgehogs, which exhibit recurrent SFTSV infections in spring, likely sustain viral circulation. Proactive surveillance of vectors, reservoirs, and viral evolution, coupled with public education on tick avoidance, is essential to mitigate zoonotic threats in densely populated megacities.

Methods and materials

Field survey

From April to October 2024, excluding the hibernation period (November to March), hedgehogs (Erinaceus amurensis) were captured monthly during night-time (19:00–21:00) in six urban parks within Beijing's Fifth Ring Road: Olympic Forest Park, Zizhuyuan Park, Temple of Heaven Park, Taoranting Park, Beihai Park, and Haidian Park (Fig. 1).

Following anesthesia with isoflurane, approximately 0.5 mL of blood was collected from each hedgehog via cardiac puncture. The hedgehogs were released back into the wild after blood collection. Ticks were removed from the body surface of each hedgehog using forceps and morphologically identified under a stereomicroscope. Blood samples were centrifuged (3000×g, 10 min) to separate serum and cellular components, which were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Ploidy analysis of H. longicornis ticks

Parthenogenesis in H. longicornis was confirmed via mitochondrial DNA analysis. One leg per tick was excised, and genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Parthenogenetic populations were identified by a single thymine (T) deletion at position 8497, as previously described (Zhang et al., 2022).

RNA extraction and amplification of SFTSV RNA by nested PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tick homogenates and hedgehog blood samples using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Germany), respectively. Subsequently, the cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription using the One-Step SYBR PrimerScript Reverse Transcription (RT)-PCR technology kit (TaKaRa, Japan). The conditions for nested PCR were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, and 40 cycles of amplification at 95 °C, each cycle lasting for 5 s, with annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The primer sequences used are detailed in our previous study (Yuan et al., 2024). The PCR products were sequenced using the Sanger method and deposited in GenBase (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/) and Science Data Bank (https://www.scidb.cn/en).

SFTSV sequences from previous studies were retrieved from GenBank (Shi et al., 2017; Yoshikawa et al., 2015). The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method in MEGA program. The robustness of the resulting tree was evaluated by conducting 1000 bootstrap replicates to determine the level of consensus confidence.

Neutralizing assay

Serum neutralizing titers were assessed via focus reduction neutralization test (FRNT) against the rVSV-GFP-SFTSV AH12 strain. Heat-inactivated sera were serially diluted (1:20–1:2500) and incubated with ∼1000 FFU of virus (30 min, 25 °C). Mixtures were inoculated onto Vero cells (ATCC CCL-81) in 96-well plates (2 h, 37 °C), followed by overlay with DMEM containing 2% FBS and 20 mM NH4Cl (24 h, 28 °C). GFP-positive foci were quantified using a fluorescent microscope or Opera Phenix High Content Screening System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). The neutralizing activity of sera from eight naïve hedgehogs against the rVSV-GFP-SFTSV AH12 and rVSV129 GFP-WH01 strains (Li et al., 2020) was determined by neutralization assay, using the cutoff value to define positive/negative neutralization. The neutralization titers (FRNT50) were calculated via the Reed-Muench method, with a cutoff value (FRNT50 ≥ 30) established based on sera from naïve hedgehogs.

Statistical analysis

All graphs and statistical analysis were generated with GraphPad Prism v.8.3.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Error bars of all figures represent as mean ± SD. The unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test was applied to calculate the significance as indicated in each figure legend. A P value of ≤ 0.05 is considered statistically significant.
