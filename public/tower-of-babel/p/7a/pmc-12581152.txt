Central Nervous System Vasculitis Due to Ehrlichia canis in a Dog
Authors: Miguel Benito Benito, Elisabet Dominguez, Nora Romero‐Fernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 4‐year‐old female neutered crossbreed dog was presented for a 2‐month history of intermittent vestibulo‐cerebellar ataxia and right head tilt. On admission, she presented clinical signs compatible with right paradoxical vestibular syndrome. Haematology showed moderate lymphocytosis, thrombocytopenia and hyperproteinaemia with hyperglobulinemia. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed multiple, bilateral, ill‐defined lesions involving the white matter of the brainstem, cerebellum and forebrain with strong diffuse meningeal contrast enhancement. Cerebrospinal fluid cytology showed lymphocytic pleocytosis, and Ehrlichia spp. serology was positive. Treatment with doxycycline showed clinical improvement within 24 h, treatment was continued for 6 weeks. A follow‐up magnetic resonance imaging study was performed after treatment and showed complete resolution of the previous lesions. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed only albumin‐cytological dissociation. Since treatment initiation, the patient remained asymptomatic. Three months after hospital discharge, no further episodes were reported.

Introduction

Canine Ehrlichiosis is a tick‐borne disease caused by the infection of bacteria of the family Ehrlichiaceae, such as Ehrlichia canis, Ehrlichia ewingii, among others (Sainz et al. 2015). Ehrlichia canis can have an acute (2–4 weeks), subclinical or chronic (between months to years) phase, but there is no clear cut‐off between phases. Common clinical signs include anaemia, epistaxis, petechiae, ecchymoses, haematuria or melena associated with thrombocytopenia, thrombocytopathy or vasculitis. In chronic cases, neurological signs such as seizures and vestibular signs—possibly related to spontaneous bleeding or meningitis—can be observed. Rarely, in chronic cases, the presence of a leukemoid reaction associated with gamma hyperglobulinemia and granular lymphocytosis can be observed.

The diagnosis is based upon visualization of intracytoplasmic morulae in blood smears, serology or DNA detection in blood or other biological fluid via polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

To the author's knowledge, there are few reports (Dona et al. 2021; Lang et al. 2011; Baba et al. 2012) of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings in patients with neurological signs seropositive to Ehrlichia spp., including different degrees of meningeal enhancement, lacunar infarcts and ill‐defined multifocal lesions involving the grey matter.

The present case reports the unusual clinical presentation and MRI findings in a patient with neurological signs seropositive to Ehrlichia spp. and resolution after treatment.

Case History

A 4‐year‐old, shepherd crossbreed, female neutered was assessed for a 2‐month history of intermittent vestibular episodes, lasting from seconds to over 2 min with spontaneous resolution. No previous relevant medical history was reported. The patient was imported from South Africa 10 months before and was up to date with vaccination and prophylactic parasiticides.

Physical and neurological examinations were unremarkable. Serum biochemistry and haematology showed hyperproteinaemia (90 g/L RI: 54–77) with hyperglobulinemia (62 g/L RI: 20–47), mild‐to‐moderate thrombocytopenia (122 × 109/L RI: 140–700) and leucocytosis with moderate lymphocytosis (9.9 × 109/L RI: 1.0–4.8). On blood smear assessment, small lymphocytes with round to indented nuclei (1.2–1.5 RBC diametre), clumped chromatin, indiscernible nucleoli and slightly expanded amount of pale to mid blue cytoplasm containing few fine magenta granules. Based on these findings, the main differential diagnoses included chronic lymphoid leukaemia (CLL) and canine monocytic ehrlichiosis (CME).

Three days after the initial consultation, pending test results, the patient was readmitted as an emergency due to a new vestibular episode that had not resolved spontaneously.

Neurological examination revealed obtundation, ambulatory tetraparesis with vestibular ataxia, left‐sided head tilt, reduced postural reactions on the right, reduced oculocephalic reflex to the right, left ventral positional strabismus and positional non‐consensual nystagmus. Neurolocalisation was compatible with right paradoxical vestibular syndrome, suggesting a lesion involving the right caudal cerebellar peduncle, right fastigial nucleus or right flocculonodular cerebellar lobe.

Following admission, an MRI (1.5 Petvet, Hallmarq, Guilford, UK) scan of the brain was performed, showing multifocal, intra‐axial, bilateral and slightly symmetrical, ill‐defined lesions. These lesions involved grey and white matter with white matter predilection and were distributed throughout the corona radiata, thalamus, brainstem and cerebellum. The lesions were hyperintense to normal grey matter in T2 and FLAIR, and isointense in T1 and T2 × GRE with no obvious contrast enhancement (Figure 1). The cerebral and meningeal vessels appeared tortuous in T2 × GRE with strong peripheral contrast enhancement around them. Furthermore, a strong leptomeningeal contrast enhancement was detected. The MRI features were suggestive of meningoencephalitis. After MRI, a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sample was collected from the cisterna magna showing lymphocytic pleocytosis (102 NC/µL RI: 0–5) with severe proteinorachia (756 mg/dL RI: 25–30). On CSF cytology, lymphocytes presented similar morphological characteristics to the ones described in the blood smear. Serum protein electrophoresis results consisted of a polyclonal gammopathy. PCR against Toxoplasma gondii, Neospora caninum , tick‐borne encephalitis and Brucella canis in CSF were negative, and ELISA serology (SNAP 4Dx Plus Idexx laboratories, USA) for Anaplasma spp., Borrelia Burgdorferi, Dirofilaria emmitis and Ehrlichia spp. was positive for the latter. After the positive serology, PCR against E. canis in CSF was performed with a negative result.

Treatment with doxycycline 5 mg/kg/12 h (Doxycare 200 mg, Animalcare Ltd., York, UK) for 6 weeks and paracetamol 10 mg/kg/8 h (Paracetamol 500 mg, Ipca Laboratories UK Ltd., Sunderland, UK) for 5 days was started. One day after the beginning of the treatment, most of the abnormalities in the neurological examination resolved; however, the ventral positional strabismus in the left eye with mild vestibular ataxia persisted.

Two weeks after treatment initiation, the owner reported a single short vestibular episode related to exercise that completely resolved within 1 min. The neurological examination showed left eye ventral positional strabismus and was otherwise unremarkable. Follow‐up haematology showed a reduction in the lymphocyte count (6.72 × 109/L) and total proteins (81 g/L RI: 54–77), although these remained increased. Due to the satisfactory response, the treatment was continued for a total of 6 weeks.

Six weeks after treatment initiation, the patient remained clinically normal with an unremarkable neurological examination. A follow‐up MRI study was performed showing complete resolution of the previous lesions. Repeat CSF analysis revealed cytological‐albumin dissociation (70 mg/dL) with no abnormalities on cytological examination, serology against E. canis (IFA) was also performed showing a positive title of 1:320 (RI < 1:40) (Figures 2, 3, 4).

To the author's knowledge, the present case reports a novel clinical presentation and MRI findings in a patient with meningoencephalitis due to E. canis and its resolution after the treatment.

Discussion

The present case report describes novel MRI findings in a dog with CME, not previously reported in the literature.

CME is a disease commonly associated with thrombocytopenia, hyperglobulinemia or anaemia, but it can be associated with other less common findings such as polyclonal or monoclonal lymphocyte proliferation. To the authors’ knowledge, it is the only non‐neoplastic infectious disease that has been described with an accompanying circulating large granular lymphocytosis in the dog, reported together (Heeb et al. 2014; Weiser et al. 1991; Diniz and de Aguiar 2022) or separately with dysproteinaemias. In the present case, in addition to the mentioned changes, a polyclonal gammopathy and small granular lymphocytosis were present not only in the blood smear but also in the CSF examination, which has not been reported before.

Different species of Ehrlichia have been described to cause clinical signs in dogs, where fever, anaemia and bleeding diatheses are common clinical signs. Only E. canis and E. ewingii have been associated with neurological signs (Dona et al. 2021; Diniz and de Aguiar 2022; Kaewmongkol et al. 2016; Lang et al. 2011; Aroch et al. 2018; Goodman et al. 2003).

Despite the positive serology results, E. canis was not detected in CSF PCR. This could be explained by the lack of the pathogen in the collected sample or poor test performance. PCR sensitivity is inferior to serology, reported to be 66.7% and 93.4% (Beall et al. 2022; Azhahianambi et al. 2018), respectively.

The most recent guidelines state that, ideally, a combination of clinical signs, serology (ELISA/IFA) and positive PCR are necessary to achieve a confident diagnosis of ehrlichiosis (Sainz et al. 2015; Mylonakis et al. 2019). However, obtaining a positive PCR in infected animals can be challenging, given the previously mentioned PCR low sensitivity, probably because bacteria may not always be present in the sampled tissue or fluid. Using splenic tissue may improve PCR sensitivity, as Ehrlichia can sequestrate within the spleen (Sainz et al. 2015).

The possible association between Ehrlichia spp. and meningoencephalitis of unknown aetiology was not supported when investigated in 109 dogs in endemic areas via PCR in CSF and frozen brain tissue (Barber et al. 2010). However, two cases of dogs with neurological signs and positive E. canis PCR in CSF have been reported (Kaewmongkol et al. 2016; Lang et al. 2011). In all the reported cases, the treatment was based on different doses of steroids and antibiotics, mainly doxycycline, with only two cases reporting long‐term outcome (Dona et al. 2021; Kaewmongkol et al. 2016; Lang et al. 2011; Aroch et al. 2018). In the present case, only doxycycline was used with a complete clinical response in the following 24 h. This suggests that, in the cases where E. canis is responsible for the clinical signs, doxycycline might be sufficient to achieve recovery, not clinical but also of the MRI and CSF changes.

Ehrlichia spp. is a well‐known aetiological agent causing vasculitis in dogs, leading to bleeding such as epistaxis or retinal haemorrhages, and thrombosis (Sainz et al. 2015; Mylonakis and Theodorou 2017; Rodríguez‐Alarcón et al. 2020). In human medicine, vasculitis has been described in MRI as thickening of vessel walls with intramural and leptomeningeal contrast enhancement (Salvarani et al. 2012; Guggenberger and Bley 2020). In primary central nervous system vasculitis in humans, apart from these changes, it is common to find areas of increased signal intensity on fluid attenuation inversion recovery (FLAIR) or T2‐weighted images, often bilateral, involving the cortical white matter (Salvarani et al. 2012; Guggenberger and Bley 2020). Although this condition is often autoimmune in humans, similar findings consistent with cerebral vasculitis have been reported with infectious agents such as Rickettsia, Borrelia and Treponema pallidum (syphilis) (Akgoz et al. 2012) among others. As the MRI findings in the present case are similar to those described in human patients with vasculitis, the authors consider that vasculitis could be a potential explanation for the lesions. However, the lack of histopathology of the lesions makes it impossible to confirm this suspicion. In previous reports of canine CNS disease associated with Ehrlichia spp. infection, the authors hypothesised that the lesions could be due to arterial infarction, perivascular inflammation (Lang et al. 2011), plasma cell infiltration or vasculopathy (Dona et al. 2021).

Although the clinical response to doxycycline and the resolution of neurological signs, MRI lesions and CSF abnormalities strongly suggest an association with E. canis, a direct causal relationship between the pathogen and the observed neuropathological changes cannot be definitively established. The diagnosis in this case relied on clinical presentation, serology and treatment response, in the absence of a positive PCR result in CSF or histopathological confirmation. Therefore, while E. canis is considered a likely contributor to the observed pathology, alternative or concurrent causes cannot be fully excluded, although they are considered unlikely.

In summary, this case report presents previously unreported MRI findings in a dog with CME, indicating that vasculitis may be a potential cause of the observed lesions. In addition, it documents the novel presence of large granular lymphocytes in the CSF and demonstrates that these changes are reversible with treatment, which may provide a useful marker for monitoring treatment response.
