Effect of human adipose stem cell-derived mitochondrial transplantation on the activity of chronically injured anterior cruciate ligament cells
Authors: Hon Lok Lo, Yao-Hui Huang, Yu-Chuan Lin, Wen-Wei Li, Shun Cheng Wu, Pei-Hsi Chou, Cheng-Chang Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsMitochondrial transplantation has been proposed as a potential treatment for injured ligament tissue. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between the time from injury to surgery and the activity of injured anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) cells. Additionally, we evaluated the effectiveness of mitochondrial transplantation in chronically injured ACL cells and in an in vivo ACL partial tear animal model.MethodsFirst, ACL injured tissue from rabbits (n = 6 for each timepoint) was harvested at two (acute), four (subacute), and eight (chronic) weeks following ACL transection. We investigated cell proliferation, migration capability, and the expression of collagen synthesis, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) genes in injured ACL cells at different injury timepoints. Second, we isolated mitochondria from human adipose-derived stem cells (hADSCs) and transplanted them into the ACL cells. Lastly, we evaluated the in vivo effects of hADSCs-mitochondrial transplantation in a rabbit knee model with partial ACL tear.ResultsAfter injury, the highest cell activity was observed at four weeks. Immunohistochemical staining showed that hADSCs-mitochondria were taken up by the eight-week injured ACL cells, leading to subsequent improvements in cell viability, migration capability, and collagen synthesis, and VEGF gene expression which were superior to those of untreated cells. At eight weeks, treated injured ACL cells achieved cell proliferation and collagen type I, type III, and VEGF expression levels comparable to those of four-week injured cells. In the in vivo study, enhanced collagen synthesis was observed in the histological analysis following hADSCs-mitochondrial transplantation.ConclusionThe optimal timing of ACL reconstruction is four to six weeks after injury due to peak cell activity. Mitochondrial transplantation improves the activity of chronically injured ACL cells and enhances collagen synthesis in ACL partial tears, highlighting its potential as a treatment for improving their regenerative capability.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2025;14(9):805–819.Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament injury, Stem cell transplantation, Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, Mitochondria, adipose-derived stem cells, transplantation, anterior cruciate ligament cells, Collagen, vascular endothelial growth factor, gene expression, anterior cruciate ligament remnant, rabbit model, transforming growth factor-β

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury, the most common sports injury of the knee joint, usually occurs during non-contact landing and twist movements.1-3 ACL reconstruction is indicated in patients who partake in high activity levels and when combined with associated ligament and meniscus injuries. After ACL reconstruction, there are two major concerns: maturation of the implanted tendon graft (ligamentization), and healing of the graft tendon-bone tunnel interface.4-6 In the early stage of ACL reconstruction, the implanted tendon graft undergoes cell apoptosis and death, which destroys the graft structure, weakens the mechanical strength of graft, and delays graft maturation.6-8 Therefore, it is important to enhance early healing of the implanted graft to prevent graft re-tear and allow early return to sports after ACL reconstruction.9

Reports suggest that remnant preservation ACL reconstruction enhances graft maturation and improves clinical outcomes.10-13 It preserves the native remaining fibres and proprioceptive nerve endings, contains mesenchymal stem cells, retains paracrine capabilities to regulate the surrounding tissue, and attenuates the apoptosis of hamstring tenocytes, all of which contribute to enhanced graft maturation.10-12,14-18 Traditionally, the ideal time for ACL reconstruction is around four to six weeks after injury. However, the effect of surgery timing on ACL cell activity and healing capability needs further clarification.

Mitochondria play an important role in cell growth, signal transmission, cell differentiation, cell apoptosis, and osteoarthritis pathogenesis.19,20 Mitochondria dysfunction not only induces the tendon pathology, it also impairs the tendon/ligament healing capability.21-24 In general, tendon/ligament injury happens in the critical hypoxic zone, which limits oxygen availability, leading to oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage, and further aggravating the pathology. In recent years, mitochondrial transplantation has been applied for recovery of the stressed cells and damaged tissue in respiratory lung disease, cardiomyopathy, neurological disease, and renal systems, as well as orthopaedic disease.25-28 Mitochondrial transplantation has been proven to be an effective and safe biomaterial for cell and tissue repair for the following reasons: mitochondria are intrinsic cellular organelles, allogenous mitochondrial transplantation is possible without nuclear material transfer, and mitochondrial transplantation can modulate injured cells without changing the extracellular microenvironment.28

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between the time from injury to surgery and the activity of the injured ACL cells. Additionally, we evaluated the effectiveness of mitochondrial transplantation on the cell activity and regeneration in chronically injured ACL cells and an in vivo ACL partial tear animal model. We had two hypotheses: first, that the activity and mitochondrial function of injured ACL cells would decrease with longer injury time, and second, that mitochondrial transplantation would improve the cell activity and regeneration capability of chronically injured ACL cells and in an ACL partial tear model.

Methods

To confirm these hypotheses, we conducted three experiments (Figure 1). In the first experiment, we investigated changes in ACL cell activity following acute (2 weeks), subacute (4 weeks), and chronic (8 weeks) injury periods by analyzing cell proliferation, migration capability, and the expression of collagen synthesis, VEGF, and TGF-β gene. In the second experiment, we examined the improvement in cell viability, proliferation, migration capability, and gene expression in chronically injured (8 weeks) ACL cells after hADSCs-mitochondrial transplantation, comparing the results to those of four-week (control 1) and eight-week (control 2) ACL cells without mitochondrial treatment. In the final experiment, we transplanted the human adipose-derived stem cells (hADSCs)-mitochondria into partially torn ACLs in three rabbit knees and observed its capability of collagen synthesis.

Preparation of normal and injured ACL cell samples from rabbits

In this study, we harvested normal and injured ACL tissues from rabbit knees at two, four, and eight weeks after complete ACL transection (n = 6 for each timepoint), with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Figure 2). In brief, skeletally mature New Zealand male rabbits weighing 2.5 to 3.0 kg were used in this study. After anaesthesia with an intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital (30 mg/kg), lateral patella subluxation was performed with a medial parapatellar approach. The normal ACL was detached from the femoral insertion as described in a previous study.29 To simulate clinical conditions,30 we harvested the injured ACL tissue at two, four, and eight weeks following the initial surgery. At the same time, normal ACL tissue from the contralateral knee was harvested for further study and cell culture. After surgery, these rabbits were kept in individual cages without activity restriction. To prevent infection and alleviate pain after surgery, these rabbits received intramuscular administration of Cefa (cefazoline, 15 mg/kg; Taiwan Biotech, Taiwan) twice, Keto (ketorolac, 0.7 to 1 mg/kg; Yungshin Pharm, Taiwan) once, and Temgesic (Buprenorphine, 0.01 to 0.05 mg/kg; Reckitt Benckiser, UK) twice per day for three days. Based on preliminary research results in experimental rabbits, which included four groups (Normal ACL, Injured ACL at 2 weeks, Injured ACL at 4 weeks, and Injured ACL at 8 weeks), the mean and SD (with a SD of 0.14, derived from the highest deviation among the four groups) were used for power analysis. Using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 statistical software (developed by Franz Faul, Edgar Erdfelder, Axel Buchner, and Albert-Georg Lang, Germany)31,32 with analysis of variance (ANOVA) (fixed effects, omnibus, one-way), the effect size was calculated as f = 1.023435, and the required sample size was determined (Supplementary Figure a). For α = 0.05 and power (1-β) = 0.90, the total required sample size was n = 20 (n = 5 per group). To enhance the accuracy and robustness of the experiment, the total sample size was increased to n = 24 (n = 6 per group). The authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines during the entire experiment.

Experimental timeline for sample preparation. Normal and injured anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tissues were harvested at two, four, and eight weeks post-transection.

After tissue harvest, the normal and injured ACL tissues were digested using 5 mg/ml type I collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then sub-cultured to obtain normal and injured ACL cells.18,33 Cell samples were cultured in a 100 mm tissue-cultured dish with a medium comprising low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and 1% antibiotics (penicillin/streptomycin). Cultures were incubated at 37°C and in a 5% CO2-humidified atmosphere, with the cell culture medium replaced twice a week.

First experimental stage

To investigate changes in the activity of injured ACL cells at different timepoints, normal ACL cells (n = 6) and injured ACL cells (2, 4, and 8 weeks post-injury, n = 6 for each timepoint) at the third passage were used in the following experiments.

Cells were implanted in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well. After 24 hours, 3-(4,5-dimenthylthiazol-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyl-2H-teterzolium bromide (MTT) (Cat. No. M2003; MilliporeSigma, USA) was dissolved in PBS (2 mg/ml), and then 50 μl was added into each well for four hours. The plate was then centrifuged at 700 g for five minutes and the supernatant was discarded. Subsequently, 150 μl of DMSO was added to each well to detect the absorbance of each well at 570 nm using the Bio-Rad Microplate Manager Benchmark Plus Reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA).

The normal (n = 6) and injured ACL cells were planted into 96-well plates with a cell density of 103 cells/well at the different times (n = 6 at each timepoint). After 24 hours, the mixture solution of 100 μl cultured medium with 10% Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Cat. No: KTA1020; Abbkine, USA) was added to the well and incubated for a further one hour at 37°C and 5% CO2. The reactive amount generated by cellular dehydrogenase activity was measured for absorbance at 450 nm by the microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories) at days 1, 3, and 5. The cell proliferation rate of normal and injured ACL cells was calculated at each timepoint with optical values from day 1 normalized as 100%.

Next, 750 μl of culture medium (DMEM low-glucose medium) was added below the Transwell insert (6.5 mm Transwell chamber with a pore size of 8 μm (MilliporeSigma)), and 250 μl of culture medium solution without FBS containing 5 × 104 cells were added to the Transwell insert and followed by careful pipetting. The plate was incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 20 hours to investigate the cell migration rate. The migrated cells were fixed with 10% paraformaldehyde and methanol for ten and 20 minutes, respectively, by sequence, and then 600 to 1,000 μl of 0.5% crystal violet was added for 30 minutes. We discarded the non-migrated cells from inside of the Transwell membrane by gently swabbing twice with water-soaked cotton swabs. Photos were taken underneath an inverted microscope and then dissolved the crystal violet out of stained cells with 200 μl of extraction buffer (50% ethanol, 40% deionized and distilled water (DDW), and 10% acetic acid), and 100 μl was aspirated to another 96-well plate to monitor the absorbance at 595 nm with a microplate analyzer (Bio-Rad Benchmark Plus Microplate; Bio-Rad Laboratories, UK) for quantification. The absorbance values of injured ACL cells were compared with those of normal ACL cells (set as 100%) at different timepoints to represent the migration capability of ACL cells.

Total RNA was extracted from normal ACL cells (n = 6) and injured ACL cells from different injured timepoints (n = 6 for each timepoint) using RNAzol RT (Cat. No. RN-190; Molecular Research Centre, USA). Reverse transcription from total RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) was conducted with 2 μg total RNA collected from samples and a Thermo Scientific Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat. No. K1642; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was carried out using a LightCycler PCR 7500 system (Bio-Rad) following the manufacturer’s specifications with specific primers, which are listed in Table I. In this study, reference primers were identified using the online tools NormFinder (MOMA, Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark) and GeNorm. By integrating the results from both tools, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was identified as the most stable gene. To ensure accurate and reproducible quantitative PCR (qPCR) measurements, GAPDH was selected as the housekeeping gene and designated as an internal control to normalize the data. The transcription level of target genes normalized to GAPDH was then calculated using 2Δ Ct formula with reference to all samples (Supplementary Figures b and c).34

Four proteins – collagen types I and III (COLI and COLIII), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) – were selected to be assessed in this study. A total of 1 × 104 cells were implanted on a coverslip and washed three times using PBS, then fixed with 10% paraformaldehyde for ten minutes. Following PBS washing, samples were permeabilized using 1% Triton for five minutes at room temperature. After washing three times using PBS, sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for one hour at room temperature. Following washing three times with PBS, primary antibodies (Arigo Biolaboratories, Taiwan) against selected proteins were diluted into 0.4% and used to stain the cells overnight at 4°C under a shading ambient atmosphere. The coverslip was washed with PBS three times and incubated with corresponded 0.4% fluorescent secondary antibodies: donkey anti-goat immunoglobin G (IgG) (H + L)-FAM (Leadgene Biomedical, Taiwan) for COL-1 and COL-3, and goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L)-TAMRA (Leadgene Biomedical) for VEGF and TGF-β at room temperature for one hour. Then, coverslips were mounted and counter-stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Observation was performed under a confocal microscope (Olympus IX-81-FV100; Olympus, Japan).

We used MitoTracker, MitoSOX, and JC-10 to assess changes in mitochondrial function over time in injured ACL cells. The MitoTracker survey was conducted to evaluate the fluorescence viewing of mitochondria within the injured ACL cells. Mitochondria were labelled with Invitrogen MitoTracker Green FM (100 nM) (Cat No. M7514; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C for 15 minutes followed by two washes with PBS at 900 g × 15 minutes. Additionally, MitoTracker Red was applied to stain and label the mitochondria. The experiment was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Slides were examined under a confocal microscope, and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured and expressed as a fold change (ACL remnant cells/normal ACL cells).

MitoSOX was used to detect superoxide levels in the mitochondria of injured ACL cells. In this study, we used the Invitrogen MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (Cat No. M36008; Thermo Fisher Scientific) to assess changes in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress in ACL remnant cells at different timepoints, as well as in normal ACL cells. The MFI was measured and expressed as fold change (ACL remnant cells/normal ACL cells).

The integrity of mitochondrial membrane potential was measured using JC-10 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (cat no. ab112134; Abcam, UK), following the manufacturer’s protocol to investigate changes in mitochondrial membrane potential. In brief, cells were rinsed once with PBS and incubated with 50 μl of JC-10 loading solution for 30 minutes at 37°C in the dark. Subsequently, 50 μl of assay buffer B was added to the plate, and fluorescence was read at excitation/emission wavelengths of 490/525 and 540/590 for ratio analysis using the Biotek FLx800 Fluorescence Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). The mitochondrial membrane potential was determined using the following formula: ((ratio of 520 nm/590 nm in treated cells)/(ratio of 520 nm/590 nm in control cells)) × 100%.

Second experimental stage

hADSCs were isolated from subcutaneous adipose tissue as previously described.35 Human adipose tissue was obtained from six healthy patients (three male and three female) under approval from the ethical committee of our hospital (KMUHIRB-E(II)-20220292). After obtaining informed consent, subcutaneous adipose tissue from the gluteal area was taken from patients during plastic surgery. A total of 3 g of subcutaneous adipose tissue was isolated and minced with scissors. After digestion with 1 mg/ml of type IA collagenase (125 units/mg) at 37°C under 5% CO2 for 24 hours, these tissues were centrifuged at 200 g for five minutes, and the pellet was washed twice with PBS. The pellet was then resuspended in K-NAC medium and plated in a 100 mm culture dish. The K-NAC medium used in this study helped to isolate and expand the hADSCs, according to previous reports.36 The mitochondria were extracted from hADSCs (passages 6–8) in culture medium using Mitochondria Isolation Kit for Cultured Cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.28 Then, the mitochondria solution was stained with MitoTracker Green dye (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for mitochondria tracking.

The injured ACL cells at eight weeks (third passage) were cultured into a six-well plate at 2 × 105 cells/per well for 24 hours. Meanwhile, the native mitochondria of injured ACL cells were labelled with MitoTracker Red dye (Invitrogen). Then, 1 ml of mitochondria solution (stained with MitoTracker Green dye (Invitrogen)) isolated from 4 × 106 cells/ml-density hADSCs was added slowly into the eight-week injured ACL cell culture well. The chosen cell density (4 × 106 cells/ml) was adapted from the study results of Lin et al.37

After 30 minutes, the injured ACL cells were observed under the confocal scope (Olympus IX-81-FV100; Olympus) to confirm that the transferred hADSCs-mitochondria had been taken up by the injured ACL cells. These cells were further cultured in a 37°C incubator for a further 24 hours.

In the second experiment, we investigated the cell viability (MTT), proliferation (Edu, Ki67 gene), migration capability (Transwell assay), and gene expression (collagen type I and III, VEGF, TGF-β) of hADSCs-mitochondria transplanted into chronically injured ACL cells at eight weeks and compared the results to those of four-week (control 1) and eight-week (control 2) injured ACL cells without mitochondria treatment. These experimental procedures are described above.

Third experimental stage

To investigate the effect of hADSCs-mitochondrial transplantation in vivo, we created an ACL partial tear model in three rabbit knees (Figure 1).

New Zealand male rabbits weighing 2.5 to 3.0 kg were used in this study under Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval (KMU 110232). The ACL was partially transected with a scalpel to a depth of 1 mm. The joint capsule, fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin were closed in layer with a 4-0 nylon suture. The knee joint was injected with either the mitochondrial solution (derived from 5 × 106 human ADSCs, with a total volume of 250 μl) or PBS, as assigned randomly.38,39 One dose was injected intra-articularly once per week for three weeks. Two weeks after the third injection, the knee joints were obtained from the rabbits. The knee joint samples were prepared, and histological analysis and immunohistochemistry of collagen I was performed for each group.

To observe collagen formation, tissue samples were prepared as frozen sections (4 μm thick) for immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. IHC was performed using antibodies against collagen I (1:50; Arigo Biolaboratories, cat# ARG21965). Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.5% Triton for 30 minutes, slides were washed twice with PBS for five minutes. IHC staining was conducted with a commercial IHC kit (cat. no. ab64264; Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Slides were blocked with hydrogen peroxide for ten minutes at room temperature, then washed twice with PBS. A protein blocking reagent was applied for ten minutes, followed by two PBS washes. Primary antibodies, diluted in antibody diluent solution (ab64211; Abcam), were incubated overnight at 4°C. The following day, slides were incubated with secondary antibodies, stained with 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB), and counterstained with haematoxylin. Finally, the slides were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Images were captured with the TissueFAXS cytometer microscope (TissueFAXS; TissueGnostics, Austria).

In this study, the histochemistry score (H-score) was used to semiquantitatively assess collagen type I expressions in tissue samples. The H-score combines staining intensity (0 = none, 1 = weak, 2 = moderate, 3 = strong) and the percentage of positive cells using the formula: H-score = ∑(Intensity × Percentage). Scores range from 0 to 300, reflecting the extent and intensity of expression.

Statistical analysis

The sample size calculation was performed using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. All data are presented as mean (SD) with triple measurements. A one-way ANOVA and a Tukey’s post-hoc test were used to determine the significant differences in multiple comparisons. In the third experiment, a paired t-test was used to determine the significant difference in H-scores between groups. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 20 (IBM, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Injured ACL cell activity

Cell viability, proliferation, and migration capability in injured ACL cells were reduced at two weeks compared to normal ACL cells. The four-week injured ACL cells demonstrated the highest cell viability and migration capability, with statistically significant increases from two to four weeks, followed by a significant decrease at eight weeks. As shown in Figure 3b, the cell proliferation rate and Ki67 gene expression were higher in the eight-week injured ACL cells compared to the two- and four-week injured cells. Similarly, in the histological study (Figure 3a), the two-, four-, and eight-week injured ACL remnant tissue showed disoriented collagen fibres with embedded cells, consistent with findings from the proliferation study.

The injured ACL cells at four weeks demonstrated the highest gene expression level for collagen synthesis (collagen types I and III), VEGF, and TGF-β, with significant increases from two to four weeks and a decline from four to eight weeks. The four-week injured ACL cells demonstrated a significantly increased collagen type I and VEGF gene expression compared to that of normal ACL cells. There was no significant difference in TGF-β gene expression between the four-week and eight-week injured ACL cells. The four-week injured ACL cells exhibited the significantly highest level of MitoSOX and JC-10, which declined in the eight-week injured cells (Figures 5b and 5c).

hADSCs-mitochondrial transplantation

Based on the above findings, the eight-week injured ACL cells demonstrated inferior cell and mitochondrial activity. Therefore, we treated the eight-week injured cells with hADSCs-mitochondria to improve their cell activity. After transplantation, the transferred hADSCs-mitochondria (stained in green) were taken up by the eight-week injured ACL cells (Figure 6).

After transplantation, the human adipose stem cell (hADSC)-mitochondria (Mito) were taken up by the eight-week injured anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) cells. b) The transplanted mitochondria did not infiltrate into the cell nucleus (blue: 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI); red: ACL native mitochondria; green: transplanted mitochondria).

After uptake of the hADSC mitochondria, the eight-week injured ACL cells showed significantly improved cell viability compared to the eight-week injured cells without mitochondria treatment (Figure 7a). The cell proliferation rate of eight-week injured ACL cells treated with hADSC-mitochondria decreased significantly compared with that of the untreated eight-week injured ACL cells, and there was no significant difference compared with that of the untreated four-week injured ACL cells. The eight-week injured ACL cells, both with and without mitochondrial treatment, showed significantly higher Ki67 gene expression than the four-week injured ACL cells (Figure 7b). In terms of cell migration, the eight-week injured ACL cells treated with hADSC mitochondria showed significantly improved migration capability compared to the untreated eight-week injured ACL cells. The migration capability of eight-week injured ACL cells, both with and without mitochondrial treatment, was significantly lower than that of four-week injured ACL cells (Figure 7c).

At eight weeks, hADSC-mitochondria-treated ACL cells showed significantly improved a) cell viability and c) Transwell migration (without significant difference). b) At eight weeks, cell proliferation in injured ACL cells declined significantly after mitochondria treatment and returned to the same level observed in four-week injured cells. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01, one-way analysis of variance with a Tukey’s post-hoc test. Following hADSC mitochondrial transplantation, the eight-week injured ACL cells showed significantly improved collagen synthesis and VEGF gene expression compared to untreated eight-week injured cells. Furthermore, collagen types I and III and VEGF gene expression in mitochondria-treated eight-week injured ACL cells achieved the same level as that of the four-week injured cells. Discussion

The effect of injured time (from initial injury to reconstruction) on the quality of injured ACL tissue is an important issue for the clinical application of remnant preservation in ACL reconstruction. In this study, we investigated changes in ACL cell activity at two weeks (acute injury phase), four weeks (subacute injury phase, the most common ACL reconstruction timepoint), and eight weeks (chronic injury phase). We found that the cell activity of ACL cells varied at different injury time points. At four weeks, the ACL cells exhibited significantly higher cell viability, migration, and collagen synthesis and VEGF gene expression compared to two-week and eight-week injured cells. By eight weeks, ACL cells showed significantly reduced cell viability, migration, and collagen synthesis and VEGF gene expression compared to four-week injured cells. Following the observed changes in cell activity at different injury timepoints, mitochondrial function was found to be highest in four-week injured ACL cells and declined by eight weeks post-injury. After mitochondria (isolated from hADSCs) transplantation, the delivered mitochondria were taken up by eight-week injured ACL cells, leading to improved cell viability, migration, and collagen synthesis and VEGF gene expression compared to untreated eight-week injured cells, partially restoring these functions to the levels observed in four-week injured cells. In the rabbit ACL partial tear model, the transplanted mitochondria remained within the injured ACL tissue and enhanced collagen synthesis.

The optimal timing for ACL reconstruction after the initial injury remains controversial.40 In the early stages following ACL injury, the accumulation of inflamed fluid within the knee joint inhibits ACL cell migration and proliferation.41,42 It is generally recommended to perform ACL reconstruction around four to six weeks after initial injury, once inflammation subsides and range of motion is regained.30,43 However, there are few studies examining the effects of surgical timing on the activity and healing capability of injured ACL cells.44,45 Zhang et al44 found that within 90 days post-injury, human ACL cells exhibited a higher stem-like cell fraction and greater expansion potential compared to ACL cells injured for more than 90 days.

In this study, we investigated and compared the cell activity and gene expression among two-, four-, and eight-week injured ACL cells. We found that after acute ACL injury (2 weeks post-injury), cell proliferation and the expression of collagen type III and TGF-β were decreased compared to normal ACL cells. After four weeks post-injury, ACL cells exhibited the highest levels of cell viability, migration, collagen synthesis gene expression, growth factor secretion, and mitochondrial function compared to two- and eight-week injured cells. In other words, at four weeks, injured ACL cells were actively synthesizing collagen, enhancing mitochondrial function, stimulating cell migration to the injury site, and secreting growth factors for revascularization and graft maturation, indicating the optimal potential for enhanced graft healing. These findings provide strong evidence supporting ACL reconstruction at four to six weeks.

However, ACL reconstruction is often delayed and performed more than four to six weeks after injury. Our results clearly show that eight-week injured ACL cells exhibit lower cell viability, migration capability, collagen synthesis, VEGF gene expression, and mitochondrial function compared to that of four-week injured cells. Interestingly, the cell proliferation rate at eight weeks was significantly higher than that of the four-week injured cells and normal ACL cells. Similarly, Lu et al33 studied the cell activity of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendon injury stump after failed repair at six weeks, and found that FDP stump tendon cells showed a higher proliferation rate with stem preservation compared to normal FDP tendon cells. Together, these findings suggest that chronically injured ACL cells in our study maintained a higher proliferation capability, but had reduced cell viability, migration, gene expression, and mitochondrial function. This indicates the need for biological augmentation to improve cell activity and enhance graft maturation in chronically injured ACLs.

Mitochondrial transplantation has recently been explored in the orthopaedic field.27,28 There are two primary mechanisms for administering mitochondrial transplantation to support tissue or cell repair: 1) using stem cell therapy as the donor, which facilitates intercellular mitochondria transfer through tunnelling nanotubes (TNT), dendrites, or microvesicles; and 2) directly using isolated mitochondria for transplantation.26 Lee et al27 demonstrated that the transplantation of exogenous intact mitochondria into damaged tenocytes restored their tenogenic capability, reduced inflammation, and attenuated apoptosis. Similarly, Guo et al28 transplanted mitochondria from donor bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) to recipient BMSCs and found that the recipient BMSCs exhibited significantly enhanced proliferation, migration, and osteogenic capability in vitro, as well as increased new bone formation in a rat cranial defect model.

In our study, we harvested mitochondria from hADSCs and found that hADSC mitochondria could be successfully taken up by eight-week injured ACL cells. Following hADSC mitochondrial transplantation, the chronically injured eight-week ACL cells exhibited improved cell viability, migration, collagen synthesis, and VEGF gene expression, partially restoring these functions to the levels observed in four-week injured cells. These findings suggest that mitochondrial transplantation could serve as a novel biological approach to enhance the activity of chronically injured ACL cells, thereby promoting graft maturation during ACL reconstruction at the chronic injury stage.

ACL partial tears account for an estimated 10% to 27% of isolated ACL injuries.46 Treating partial ACL tears remains challenging, with a key focus on preventing their progression to complete tears to avoid surgical reconstruction. Kanaya et al38 investigated the healing effects of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells in a rat model of ACL partial tear, and found that MSC-treated ACLs showed gap coverage with newly formed tissue, higher histological scores, and greater ultimate failure loads than controls. Using the same rat ACL partial tear model, Li et al47 demonstrated that a thermosensitive hydrogel platelet-rich plasma complex enhanced biomechanical properties, histological maturity, and the expression of pro-collagen I and VEGF.

In our study, we innovatively transplanted hADSC mitochondria into the ACL partial tear site of a rabbit model. The transplanted mitochondria remained within the ACL tissue and enhanced its collagen synthesis capability. However, more comprehensive histological and mechanical evaluations in animal studies are needed to further investigate the effects of mitochondrial transplantation in partial ACL tear and ACL reconstruction models. This study had several limitations. Only two-, four-, and eight-week injured cells were studied, leaving the changes in cell activity in injured ACL cells beyond eight weeks unknown. Additionally, the optimal dose and the precise mechanisms of mitochondrial transplantation for enhancing injured ACL cell activity require further investigation in future animal studies. We found inferior cell viability, migration rate, collagen synthesis and VEGF gene expression, and mitochondrial function in chronically injured eight-week ACL cells compared to those of subacute-term four-week injured cells. Given that four-week injured ACL cells are still actively synthesizing collagen, elevating mitochondrial function, stimulating cell migration to the injury site, and secreting growth factor for revascularization and graft maturation, ACL reconstruction was recommended to be performed at four to six weeks to facilitate enhanced graft healing. hADSCs-mitochondrial transplantation improved cell activity in eight-week injured cells and partially restored cell activity to the level of four-week injured cells. Mitochondrial transplantation shows much promise as an effective biological approach to improve ACL cell activity in the chronic stage (eight weeks post-injury), thereby enhancing graft maturation in ACL reconstruction performed at this late stage.
