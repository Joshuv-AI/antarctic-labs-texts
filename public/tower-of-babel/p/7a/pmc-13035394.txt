Orthostatic hypotension in Parkinson disease
Authors: Xue Zhu, Jun Liu, Andrei Krassioukov
Published: 2026

Orthostatic hypotension is commonly associated with Parkinson disease, which poses serious risks to patients

Parkinson disease affects 3.2 to 3.6 per 1000 people living in Canada. Orthostatic hypotension occurs in an estimated 36% of people with Parkinson disease;1 it increases fall risk 10-fold2 and nearly triples patients’ risk of cognitive impairment.3 It can be symptomatic (e.g., dizziness, syncope) or asymptomatic.

Routine standardized screening for orthostatic hypotension is required for patients with Parkinson disease

Clinicians should use a standardized lying-to-standing test, measuring the patient’s blood pressure (BP) after at least 5 minutes in the supine position, with readings taken while supine and after 1, 2, and 3 minutes. A sustained drop in systolic BP of more than 20 mm Hg (or diastolic BP ≥ 10 mm Hg) confirms orthostatic hypotension.4

Parkinsonian symptoms with orthostatic hypotension raises suspicion for atypical parkinsonism

Orthostatic hypotension with poor levodopa response, lack of tremor, urinary incontinence, or ataxia is suggestive of multiple system atrophy. When orthostatic hypotension presents alongside fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, or sensitivity to antipsychotics, Lewy body dementia is likely.5

Orthostatic hypotension should be managed with a stepwise approach, starting with nonpharmacologic strategies

Non-Parkinsonian causes (e.g., dehydration, anemia, cardiac dysfunction, diabetic neuropathy) should be excluded, and aggravating factors (e.g., large meals, alcohol) should be avoided. All medications — particularly dopamine agonists, α-blockers, diuretics, antidepressants, and antihypertensives — should be reviewed and adjusted if necessary.6 Nonpharmacologic measures should be instituted first, such as increased salt or fluid intake, pressurized elastic stockings, and postural manoeuvres (e.g., leg crossing, squatting).4

Pharmacologic therapy for orthostatic hypotension should be added when nonpharmacologic measures are insufficient

Domperidone (10 mg, 3 times daily) should be administered 30 minutes before meals or dopaminergic agents.6,7 Fludrocortisone (0.05 to 0.2 mg, daily) and midodrine (2.5 to 15 mg 2 or 3 times daily, 1 hour before mobilization) may also be added. Fludrocortisone carries risks of pedal edema, particularly in immobile patients. Both fludrocortisone and midodrine present a substantial risk of supine hypertension.4,6 Effectiveness can be assessed using 24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring.4
