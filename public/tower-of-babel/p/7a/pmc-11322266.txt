Comparative analysis of short- and long-term outcomes in laparoscopic versus open surgery for colorectal cancer patients undergoing hemodialysis
Authors: Hsin-Yuan Hung, Shu-Huan Huang, Tzong-yun Tsai, Jeng-Fu You, Pao-Shiu Hsieh, Cheng-Chou Lai, Wen-Sy Tsai, Kun-Yu Tsai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeAlthough minimally invasive colorectal surgery has been proven to have a shorter hospital stay and fewer short-term complications than open surgery, the advantages of laparoscopic surgery for colorectal cancer patients undergoing hemodialysis have not been validated. This study compared the outcomes of open and laparoscopic approaches in these patients.Materials and methodsBetween January 2007 and December 2020, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 78 hemodialysis patients who underwent curative-intent, elective colorectal surgery. Patients were divided into two groups according to the surgical method: open and laparoscopic.ResultsPostoperative morbidity (p = 0.480) and mortality (p = 0.598) rates and length of hospital stay (28.8 vs. 27.5 days, p = 0.830) were similar between the groups. However, laparoscopic surgery patients had a shorter return to clear liquid, full liquid, or soft food time than open surgery patients (p < 0.001, p = 0.007, and p = 0.002, respectively). Disease-free survival and long-term cancer-specific survival rates were also similar between the two groups (p = 0.353 and p = 0.201, respectively). Multivariate analysis revealed that intraoperative blood transfusion was a risk factor for severe complications and mortality (OR 6.055; p = 0.046), and the odds ratio (OR) of laparoscopic surgery was not significantly greater than that of open surgery (OR = 0.537, p = 0.337).ConclusionAlthough laparoscopic surgery did not result in hemodialysis patients having a shorter postoperative hospital stay, our results suggest that the laparoscopic approach is as safe as open surgery for hemodialysis patients and may be beneficial for shortening the return time to food intake.

Introduction

Advancements in minimally invasive techniques have led to minimally invasive surgeries becoming increasingly popular for managing colon and rectal cancers [1]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that patients can benefit from laparoscopic colorectal surgery. Compared to those of open surgery, the advantages of laparoscopic surgery include lower postoperative opioid consumption [2], lower surgical site infection rates [3], reduced intraoperative blood loss, faster return of bowel function, shorter hospital stays [4], lower readmission rates, and, consequently, lower hospital costs [5]. Most importantly, according to the European multicenter COLOR II trial, short-term complication and mortality rates were similar among rectal patients who underwent either laparoscopic or open surgery [4]. Subsequent research confirmed that oncological outcomes, such as local regional recurrence, disease-free survival, and overall survival rates, were comparable between these two groups. These findings indicate that the laparoscopic approach is a safe and feasible approach for treating colorectal cancer. Moreover, for elderly patients who have higher American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) scores, more cardiac and pulmonary comorbidities, and poorer preoperative nutritional conditions than younger patients [6], laparoscopic colorectal surgery still offers more advantages, as it yields similar oncological clearance outcomes compared to open operations in patients aged older than 65 years [7].

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) represents a rapidly increasing global health and healthcare burden. According to the International Society of Nephrology’s survey, the average number of new ESKD diagnoses worldwide was 144 individuals per million individuals in the general population (pmp). Countries such as Taiwan, the USA, the Jalisco region of Mexico, and Thailand have the highest incidences of treated ESKD (493, 378, 355, and 346 pmp/year, respectively) [8]. Compared with individuals without ESKD, patients with ESKD have an increased risk of postoperative complications such as myocardial infarction, stroke, sepsis, surgical site infections, and pneumonia after elective surgery, leading to prolonged hospital stays and higher postoperative mortality rates [9, 10].

Studies have also revealed that chronic dialysis patients who underwent colorectal surgery had higher mortality rates [11]. Even in elective procedures, the reported mortality rate was up to 10% in this population [12]. Not surprisingly, compared to nondialysis patients, these patients were less likely to undergo a laparoscopic approach [11, 13]. Recently, Shiraishi et al. published a retrospective study of the clinical data of 19 hemodialysis patients who underwent elective curative resection of colorectal cancer [14]. Although approximately 80% of these patients underwent minimally invasive surgery, short-term complications still occurred more frequently in these patients than in nonhemodialysis patients (36.8% vs. 15.5%, p = 0.009). On the other hand, two propensity score matching studies from Japan (with sample sizes of 14 and 24) indicated no significant differences in postoperative short-term outcomes or survival rates between dialysis and nondialysis patients who underwent laparoscopic colorectal surgery [15, 16]. However, these studies were limited by small sample sizes and lacked direct comparisons between different surgical methods; moreover, they compared the data of ESKD patients to healthy individuals. However, the benefits of laparoscopic surgery for hemodialysis patients remain unclear. Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate the outcomes of laparoscopic versus open surgery in colorectal cancer patients who underwent hemodialysis.

Materials and methods

A total of 7,516 consecutive patients diagnosed with stage I-III colorectal adenocarcinoma between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2020, underwent elective surgical resection at the Division of Colorectal Surgery, Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. IRB approval was obtained from the Chang Gung Medical Foundation Institutional Review Board (IRB No.202301846B0). Among them, 94 (1.2%) patients were undergoing hemodialysis. The inclusion criteria also enrolled patient with locally advanced T4 tumor stage. The exclusion criteria included patients who underwent only local excision (N = 5) and those who underwent two or more simultaneous operations, such as cholecystectomy, gynecological surgery, or synchronous cancer operations (N = 11).

We divided the patients into laparoscopic and open groups. We compared the demographic and clinical characteristics between these groups, including comorbidities, duration of hemodialysis, essential presurgical laboratory examination results, surgical details, short-term postoperative outcomes, stoma status, length of hospital stay, long-term survival rates, and stoma closure rates. The number of postoperative days to return to food intake and intravenous medications administered for pain control were also reviewed for analysis.

Ideally, hemodialysis is performed the day before surgery in our hospital [17]. If surgery was planned for Monday, hemodialysis was arranged for Saturday night. Surgical complications were classified according to the Clavien‒Dindo classification [18], with Grade III to IV complications defined as severe complications. In our institution, water intake was reintroduced first after surgery, followed by a clear liquid diet, a full liquid diet, and, finally, a soft diet. The timing of the return to food intake was decided by the chief surgeon. The protocol for pain control included intravenous medications such as morphine, meperidine, and parecoxib and patient-controlled analgesia (PCA). Acetaminophen and NSAIDs were routinely prescribed if water intake was allowed.

Disease-free survival (DFS) and cancer-specific survival (CFS) were defined as the time from the index surgery to the first recurrence related to colorectal cancer and cancer-related death, respectively. The last follow-up date was October 31, 2023.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS statistics software (version 24.0; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A comparative analysis between the groups was performed using an independent sample t test for continuous variables and a chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Survival curves were generated using Kaplan‒Meier analysis and compared using the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis was used to evaluate the associations between severe complications and mortality. A two-sided p value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

A total of 78 patients were enrolled and then divided into two groups according to their surgical methods. The laparoscopic group comprised 34 patients, while the open group included 44 patients. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, sex, tumor location, comorbidities, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, duration of hemodialysis, preoperative laboratory examinations, tumor staging, or administration of adjuvant chemotherapy (Table 1). In this study, only a few patients received adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery. Among the 22 rectal cancer patients, only 2 received neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

Demographics and clinicopathologic characteristics of the groups

No significant differences were detected in terms of surgical type, operation timing, amount of blood loss, percentage of intraoperative blood transfusions, stoma creation and closure rates, or stoma type (Table 2). In the laparoscopic group, 50% of patients underwent right hemicolectomy, while more than 47.8% of patients in the open group underwent anterior resection surgery. The operation time was approximately 30 min shorter in the open group than in the laparoscopic group, but the difference was not significant. No significant differences were noted in terms of the postoperative details, including the need for intensive care unit care, length of hospital stay, mortality, or complication rates (Table 3). Grade III or IV complications primarily resulted from anastomotic site leakage (N = 7), intra-abdominal infection (N = 3), prolonged postoperative ileus (N = 3) requiring surgical intervention, and pneumonia or cardiovascular disease (N = 5) leading to respiratory failure necessitating intensive care and intubation.

Perioperative outcome of surgery

The mean hospital stay was 28.8 days (range 6-128 days) in the laparoscopic group and 27.5 days (10–133 days) in the open group (p = 0.830). The median hospital stay was 20 days in the laparoscopic group and 18 days in the open group, with similar percentages of hospital stays longer than 30 days (p = 0.883). There was no significant difference in the proportion of patients who required postoperative intravenous pain medication between the two groups (p = 0.266). The number of postoperative days to return to water intake was approximately one day shorter in the laparoscopic group than in the open surgery group, but the difference was not significant (p = 0.101). However, patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery had shorter time to return to food and water intake, including the timing of clear liquid, full liquid, and soft diet intake (p < 0.001, p = 0.007, and p = 0.002, respectively), than the patients who underwent open surgery (Table 4). According to the multivariate analysis, intraoperative blood transfusions were associated with severe postoperative complications and mortality (OR 6.055; p = 0.046) (Table 5).

Discussion

Hemodialysis patients who underwent colorectal surgery accounted for only a small portion (0.7–2.4%) [11, 12, 14, 15] of the general population in previous research. This study represents the largest series of patient data collected from a single medical center database with the aim of assessing the outcomes of open and laparoscopic colorectal surgery in patients undergoing hemodialysis. Previous randomized controlled trials revealed that the short-term complication and mortality rates were similar in rectal patients who underwent laparoscopic or open surgery. Additionally, patients who underwent laparoscopic colorectal surgery had a faster return to food and water intake, a finding confirmed by several studies involving general populations [4, 19]. Fasting after surgery contributes to the exacerbation of catabolism, accompanied by weight loss [20]. Therefore, an early postoperative return to regular diet may improve patient recovery. Additionally, the length of hospital stay and risk of complications decreased [21]. Early oral feeding is also associated with improved clinical outcomes, decreased total hospital costs and low rates of serious adverse events [22]. Our results revealed that early dietary intake associated with the laparoscopic approach is beneficial, even for patients receiving hemodialysis. However, the length of hospital stay in this study was similar, indicating that early oral intake had a limited impact on the recovery of hemodialysis patients. We believe that the critical factor affecting patient recovery is underlying comorbidity. Studies have shown that hemodialysis patients are at risk of infectious complications [12, 14], and once infectious complications occur, the patient’s clinical condition is severely affected [15]. Therefore, caution should be taken to avoid the risk of aspiration by eliminating gastric residue [23], especially for hemodialysis patients who have weakened immune systems and poor general conditions. Our research also evaluated both superficial and deep surgical site infection rates between groups. A meta-analysis showed that laparoscopic surgery patients had significantly lower wound infection rates (p = 0.001) and abdominal abscess rates (p = 0.51) [3]. However, the low surgical site infection rate associated with laparoscopic surgery did not show a positive impact on the general conditions of hemodialysis patients. Therefore, using postoperative antibiotics, placing drainage tubes, paying special attention to chest care, and closely monitoring infection symptoms and signs postoperatively are critical for hemodialysis patients.

This study is also the first to analyze stoma creation and closure rates for different approaches in colorectal cancer patients receiving hemodialysis. A total of 28 (35.8%) patients in this cohort underwent creation and closure of a diverting or end stoma. However, approximately 68% of patients could not undergo closure of their stomas during the follow-up period, which is much greater than that of the general population, in which the permanent stoma rate was 18–25% [24]. Many risk factors for permanent stoma have been well analyzed and include anastomosis-related complications, especially anastomotic leakage or more advanced disease [25, 26]. Our group also published a study evaluating the risk factors for permanent stoma in rectal cancer patients undergoing low anterior resection [27]. However, none of the studies have confirmed that hemodialysis is an independent risk factor for nonclosure of the stoma. The etiology of the low stoma reversal rate is the patient’s poor condition making them unsuitable for another closure surgery and this might be reflected in a higher ASA score and comorbidity, which have already been proven to be critical predictors for permanent stoma [24, 27]. Well-designed studies are required to answer this question in the future.

According to our multivariate analysis, intraoperative blood transfusions were associated with severe postoperative complications or death in this study. Ito et al. analyzed the data of 36 dialysis patients who underwent abdominal surgery for gastrointestinal disease [28]. They found that physical status, a higher ASA grade, and intraoperative blood loss were associated with postoperative complications. Abe et al. revealed the risk factors for morbidity and mortality in 63 hemodialysis patients who underwent elective or emergent surgery [29]. The authors concluded that the risk factors for poor surgical outcome included high BUN levels in elective surgery patients and hypoproteinemia, hypoalbuminemia, a more extended operation, and older age in patients undergoing emergency surgery. In addition, perioperative blood transfusion was also associated with a high complication rate in the emergency surgery group. Therefore, caution should be taken when treating patients with comorbidities, such as heart disease; these patients should be treated appropriately before surgery. In addition, it is essential to correct the bleeding tendency before surgery and to perform operations cautiously to avoid excessive blood loss. Toh et al. published a study evaluating the data of 30 hemodialysis patients who underwent abdominal surgery [30]. The study revealed that patients with more than a 2-year history of hemodialysis had a significantly higher mortality rate following abdominal surgery than those with less than a 2-year history (p < 0.01). However, the authors did not explain the possible etiology of these positive findings. The average duration of hemodialysis was 5 ± 4.8 years in the laparoscopic group and 5.8 ± 5.3 years in the open group. We used a 2-year history as a cutoff point to evaluate the risk of severe complications, but the difference was not significant. Moreover, the OR of laparoscopy surgery was not significantly different from that of open surgery (OR = 0.537, p = 0.337). Overall, no significant differences in disease-free survival or cancer-specific survival rates were observed between the groups.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective data collection study in which a limited number of patients and some selection bias existed. Although experienced surgeons performed all the operations, every operator had their preferred surgical methods. Younger surgeons may favor laparoscopic approaches more frequently, while senior operators prefer the traditional approach. Not surprisingly, 80% of laparoscopic approaches were performed later in this cohort. As technology advanced, surgical techniques and medical aids have been further developed over time, including newer medications, better anesthesia induction and maintenance strategies, highly skilled patient care teams, and monitoring equipment and all these advancements may encourage surgeons to take on more challenging cases. Second, although the quicker return to a liquid and soft food diet is a notable advantage observed in the laparoscopic group in the cohort, this outcome could be influenced by individual surgeons’ decisions regarding different post-operative care in clinical practice. Third, the percentage of rectal cancer patients treated with neoadjuvant radiochemotherapy and adjuvant chemotherapy was low in this study because of the poor condition of the patients in this immunosuppressed group. Last, the National Health Insurance in Taiwan is unique in that it covers many medical fees, from surgery to hospital expenses, which might be related to the long-term hospital stays in our study. Further well-designed studies under different health insurance systems are needed to answer this question. However, this is the first large study to analyze the benefits of performing laparoscopic surgery on hemodialysis patients. In conclusion, laparoscopic surgery for colorectal cancer resection is advantageous in that it allows faster return to food and water intake, and has similar complications, short-term outcomes and cancer-specific survival rates as open surgery for hemodialysis patients.
