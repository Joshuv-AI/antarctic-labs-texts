Agentic neurotechnology: A multi‐agent AI framework for next‐generation dementia care
Authors: Kevin Mekulu, Faisal Aqlan, Hui Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDementia care is reaching a technological turning point as rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI), digital biomarkers, and sensor‐based monitoring continue to outpace current clinical practice. Yet most tools remain episodic and disconnected from the everyday environments where cognitive change unfolds. Early cognitive drift often goes unnoticed, the mild cognitive impairment (MCI) window remains a major diagnostic gap, and care teams lack continuous, actionable insights. In this perspective, we introduce Agentic Neurotechnology, an integrated framework that combines adaptive multi‐agent AI systems with longitudinal cognitive modeling to support proactive and individualized dementia care. We define a five‐layer agentic stack consisting of (1) sensing and digital biomarker agents, (2) interpretation and reasoning agents, (3) longitudinal cognitive trajectory models, (4) care navigation agents, and (5) population‐level forecasting agents. We further outline the Cognitive Continuum Loop, a closed‐cycle process in which detection, interpretation, reasoning, monitoring, intervention, and adaptation occur continuously across the care environment. This framework highlights three actionable windows for cognitive intervention: the Preclinical Risk Detection window, the MCI Detection window, and the Functional Instability Response window. Each stage presents unique opportunities for agentic AI systems that operate continuously rather than through isolated assessments. We conclude by presenting a translational pathway for deploying agentic neurotechnologies across assisted living, primary care, telehealth, and home‐based settings. The goal is to enable earlier detection, greater clinical efficiency, and more personalized cognitive support. Agentic Neurotechnology reframes AI not as a passive screening tool but as an active partner in continuous cognitive care.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment develops gradually and often emerges long before standard assessments detect meaningful change. Subtle alterations in speech, attention, memory, and behavior may unfold over several years, yet current clinical practice remains anchored to infrequent, clinic‐based evaluations that provide only momentary snapshots of cognitive health. This episodic model leaves most individuals with early impairment unidentified, which contributes to significant diagnostic delays and missed opportunities for intervention. Recent estimates suggest that mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is substantially undetected, with the majority of individuals who will later develop dementia not identified during the period when interventions may have the greatest effect. 1 , 2 Recent global syntheses further reinforce this urgency, with the 2024 Lancet Commission on dementia identifying 14 modifiable risk factors that collectively account for ≈45% of dementia cases worldwide. 3

Advances in digital biomarkers, remote monitoring, and artificial intelligence (AI) promise to fill this gap, but most existing systems operate as passive analytic tools. They classify, summarize, or predict without adapting, reasoning, or guiding action over time. As a result, the cognitive signals detectable through everyday behavior, such as speech patterns, gaze behavior, mobility, and task performance, remain largely unused in real‐world care. The field lacks a systems‐level framework that connects these diverse data streams to continuous processes of interpretation, monitoring, and support.

At the same time, AI is shifting toward agentic systems that can decompose complex tasks, evaluate uncertainty, coordinate multiple specialized models, and interact dynamically with their environment. These developments carry important implications for dementia care. Instead of functioning as isolated diagnostic add‐ons, AI systems can act as adaptive cognitive partners that detect early drift, reason over multimodal input, update individualized cognitive trajectories, and recommend micro‐level interventions aligned with real‐world care contexts.

In this perspective, we introduce Agentic Neurotechnology, a unified systems‐level framework that organizes these capabilities into a multi‐layer architecture of agentic AI systems. Agentic neurotechnologies are not isolated algorithms. They are coordinated networks of sensing agents, reasoning agents, and navigation agents that operate continuously across the care ecosystem. By integrating multi‐agent reasoning with longitudinal models of cognitive health, these systems offer a pathway toward earlier detection, more precise monitoring, and more responsive intervention.

Although prior work has examined individual components of this vision in isolation, including digital biomarker extraction, passive monitoring, and AI‐assisted screening, to our knowledge no existing framework has proposed a coordinated, closed‐loop architecture intended to operate continuously across the preclinical risk detection, MCI detection, and functional instability response windows. We present this as a conceptual systems‐level framework rather than a clinically validated architecture. The novelty of Agentic Neurotechnology lies not in any single component but in this systems‐level integration and its implications for translational deployment.

Here we outline this emerging paradigm, describe how agentic systems can support cognition across the preclinical risk detection, MCI detection, and functional instability response windows, and propose a translational roadmap for deployment across assisted living, primary care, and home environments. As multimodal data collection becomes increasingly frictionless, agentic neurotechnology provides a foundation for reimagining dementia care as a continuous, adaptive, and personalized process.

THE AGENTIC NEUROTECHNOLOGY STACK

Current approaches to digital dementia care typically focus on isolated components, such as a single biomarker, model, or task, evaluated at discrete points in time. Although these efforts have produced important insights, they do not reflect how cognitive decline unfolds in real‐world settings. Standard clinic‐based screening instruments perform variably across populations and capture only narrow slices of cognitive function at isolated moments. 4 , 5 Yet dementia is a longitudinal, context‐dependent process shaped by behavior, environment, health status, and care interactions, with day‐to‐day variability in language, attention, mobility, sleep, and social engagement that single‐visit assessments cannot capture. Detecting cognitive change as it actually occurs requires monitoring approaches that operate within the everyday contexts where individuals live, work, and receive care. This calls upon AI systems that can integrate diverse signals, reason over uncertainty, and adapt continuously as new information becomes available.

Throughout this framework, we use the term agentic to describe components that perceive inputs from their environment, reason under uncertainty, and take or recommend actions that adapt over time as new information becomes available. Agentic Neurotechnology reframes cognitive assessment and care as a coordinated, multi‐layer system rather than a collection of standalone tools, aligning with recent advances in agentic artificial intelligence that emphasize task decomposition, coordination among specialized agents, and adaptive reasoning in complex domains. 6 , 7 Within cognitive health, early formulations of agentic AI have begun to explore how reasoning‐based systems can support screening and interpretation tasks, motivating the need for a broader systems framework that extends beyond isolated assessments. 8

At its core is an agentic stack, a structured architecture in which specialized agents operate at different functional layers while exchanging information across time and context. This stack is not intended as a fixed implementation but as a conceptual framework for organizing future research, development, and deployment.

As illustrated in Figure 1, agentic neurotechnology operates as a coordinated system rather than a single analytic model. The foundational layer consists of sensing and digital biomarker agents. These agents operate at the interface between individuals and their environments, capturing signals such as speech and language patterns from smartphone‐based voice interactions, gaze behavior from tablet or webcam‐based tasks, motor activity and gait from wearables or ambient motion sensors, sleep from bed sensors or smartwatches, driving patterns from in‐vehicle telematics, and household routine patterns from smart‐home devices such as connected appliances or activity sensors. Of note, these agents emphasize unobtrusive and repeatable measurements that can be collected in everyday settings rather than controlled clinical environments, building on foundational work establishing that passively collected smartphone and sensor data can serve as ecologically valid behavioral markers suitable for longitudinal clinical reasoning. 9 Reviews of digital biomarkers in Alzheimer's disease and home‐based monitoring for MCI document a rapidly maturing evidence base for such approaches across cognitive, behavioral, motor, and sleep domains. 10 , 11

The left panel illustrates a multi‐layer agentic architecture spanning sensing, reasoning, longitudinal modeling, care navigation, and population‐level insight. The right panel depicts the Cognitive Continuum Loop, a closed‐cycle process enabling continuous detection, interpretation, reasoning, monitoring, intervention, and adaptation across time.

Above this layer are interpretation and reasoning agents, which contextualize raw signals and evaluate their relevance to cognitive health. Rather than producing static scores, these agents reason over multimodal inputs, estimate uncertainty, and adapt their interpretations as new data arrive. This layer enables the system to distinguish transient fluctuations from meaningful cognitive change.

The third layer comprises longitudinal cognitive trajectory models. These models integrate observations across weeks, months, or years to estimate individualized cognitive baselines and detect deviations over time. In this sense, the trajectory model functions agentically: it continuously updates its representation of an individual's cognitive state, monitors for meaningful deviations, and initiates signals that drive downstream care navigation. By emphasizing trajectories rather than thresholds, this layer aligns more closely with the progressive nature of cognitive decline and the clinical need to identify early drift.

The fourth layer includes care navigation agents, which translate model outputs into actionable guidance for clinicians, caregivers, and care staff. These agents do not replace clinical judgment but support it by highlighting emerging risks, suggesting follow‐up actions, and facilitating communication across care teams.

At the highest level are population‐level forecasting agents, which aggregate data across individuals to identify patterns relevant to facility operations, population health, and resource allocation. This layer enables insights that extend beyond individual assessment to inform system‐level decision‐making, particularly in assisted living and long‐term care environments.

Together, these layers form an adaptive system in which sensing, reasoning, modeling, and action are tightly coupled. The agentic stack provides the structural foundation for continuous cognitive care, enabling systems that evolve with individuals rather than evaluating them in isolation.

Although this framework is presented at the level of cognitive trajectories rather than specific neuropathologies, agentic neurotechnology is designed to accommodate disease‐specific heterogeneity. The conditions that cause MCI and dementia, including Alzheimer's disease, vascular cognitive impairment, Lewy body dementia, and frontotemporal dementia, differ in their early behavioral signatures, progression patterns, and care needs. Alzheimer's disease often manifests early through subtle changes in episodic memory and semantic fluency, vascular cognitive impairment through stepwise changes in processing speed and executive function, Lewy body dementia through fluctuations in attention and visuospatial performance, and frontotemporal dementia through changes in language and social behavior. Within the agentic stack, these distinctions can be encoded as disease‐specific priors and feature weightings in the reasoning and longitudinal modeling layers, complemented by emerging fluid and imaging biomarkers (such as plasma phosphorylated tau [p‐tau], neurofilament light, and amyloid positron emission tomography [PET]) as they become accessible in routine care. This allows the framework to remain general at the systems level while supporting differential reasoning across underlying etiologies.

THE COGNITIVE CONTINUUM LOOP

Most dementia screening technologies remain anchored to static decision points. A test is administered, a score is generated, and the result is interpreted in isolation from prior context or future change. This approach contrasts sharply with the nature of cognitive decline, which unfolds continuously and nonlinearly over time. Even when digital biomarkers are available, they are often treated as snapshots rather than as components of an evolving cognitive process.

The Cognitive Continuum Loop reframes cognitive assessment and care as a closed, adaptive cycle rather than a linear pipeline, extending our prior work introducing agentic AI to cognitive screening by conceptualizing it as a continuous process designed to operate over real‐world data streams rather than isolated analytic tasks. 8

Within this loop, sensing, interpretation, reasoning, monitoring, intervention, and adaptation occur continuously and inform one another. Each pass through the loop updates the system's understanding of an individual's cognitive state, enabling decisions that evolve with accumulating evidence rather than relying on fixed thresholds.

At the entry point of the loop is detection, where sensing agents capture multimodal signals embedded in everyday behavior. These signals include speech and language patterns, task performance, gaze behavior, movement, and other digital biomarkers collected passively or through brief interactions. Detection is not a one‐time event but a recurring process that establishes a dynamic stream of observations.

Detected signals are then subjected to interpretation, in which reasoning agents contextualize observations relative to prior data, environmental factors, and individual baselines. Interpretation emphasizes uncertainty estimation and pattern recognition rather than binary classification, allowing transient fluctuations to be distinguished from sustained change.

The reasoning phase integrates interpreted signals across modalities and time. Here, agentic systems evaluate competing explanations, reconcile conflicting evidence, and update probabilistic representations of cognitive status. This phase enables the system to move beyond pattern detection toward structured cognitive inference.

Outputs from reasoning feed into longitudinal monitoring, where cognitive trajectories are modeled explicitly. Rather than tracking absolute scores, the system focuses on deviations from personalized baselines and rates of change. This trajectory‐centered view aligns with clinical decision‐making, which often depends on progression patterns rather than single measurements.

When a meaningful changepoint is detected, the loop supports intervention through care navigation agents. Interventions may include recommendations for follow‐up assessment, caregiver guidance, environmental adjustments, or clinical referral. These actions are designed to be proportionate, context‐aware, and reversible rather than prescriptive.

Finally, adaptation closes the loop. The underlying longitudinal trajectory models, uncertainty‐aware reasoning models, and intervention policy models are iteratively updated using new behavioral signals and clinical outcomes, refining future detection, interpretation, and care recommendations. Over time, this adaptive process allows agentic neurotechnologies to become increasingly individualized and sensitive to early cognitive drift.

As shown in Figure 2, opportunities for cognitive intervention are distributed across three temporally distinct windows.

Three windows of cognitive intervention across the dementia continuum. The preclinical risk‐detection window enables identification of subtle cognitive drift prior to symptom onset. The mild cognitive impairment (MCI) detection window represents the most actionable opportunity for frequent, low‐friction monitoring and individualized baseline modeling. The functional instability response window emphasizes adaptive support and caregiver‐informed intervention following functional decline.

Cognitive decline does not emerge as a discrete clinical event. It unfolds across multiple temporal phases, each characterized by distinct signals, risks, and opportunities for intervention. Traditional screening paradigms treat these phases as largely indistinguishable, relying on static thresholds that collapse heterogeneous trajectories into binary outcomes. As a result, many individuals are first identified only after functional impairment has become apparent.

Agentic neurotechnology enables a more nuanced view by framing cognitive change across three actionable windows of intervention. These windows are defined not by diagnostic labels alone but by the type of cognitive information available and the nature of decisions that can be supported at each stage.

The first window is preclinical risk detection window. During this phase, individuals function independently and do not meet criteria for MCI or dementia. However, subtle changes in language use, attentional patterns, task strategies, or behavioral regularity may already be present. These changes are often invisible to episodic assessments but can be detected through continuous, low‐burden sensing embedded in everyday activities. Agentic systems are particularly well suited to this window because they emphasize longitudinal baselines, uncertainty‐aware reasoning, and pattern accumulation rather than categorical diagnosis. The goal at this stage is not labeling but early signal detection and monitoring.

The second window is the MCI detection window, which represents the largest diagnostic gap in current care pathways and a critical, yet frequently missed, opportunity for timely intervention. 12 Individuals in this phase may experience measurable cognitive changes, yet symptoms are frequently missed, misattributed, or inconsistently documented. Agentic neurotechnologies can support this window by integrating repeated observations across time and context, enabling systems to detect deviations from individualized baselines and flag emerging risk earlier than threshold‐based tools. Of note, this window is also where timely identification has the greatest potential impact on care planning, lifestyle intervention, and clinical follow‐up.

The third window is the functional instability response window, where cognitive impairment is clinically evident, but trajectories remain heterogeneous. In this phase, agentic systems shift from detection toward ongoing monitoring and support. Longitudinal modeling enables differentiation between stable and rapidly progressing courses, whereas care navigation agents can assist with coordination across caregivers, clinicians, and support services. Rather than replacing clinical judgment, agentic neurotechnology augments it by providing continuous context that extends beyond episodic visits.

Across all three windows, the value of agentic systems lies in their ability to adapt as individuals transition between phases. Cognitive intervention is no longer tied to a single test or moment but becomes a continuous process aligned with real‐world change. Framing dementia care through these windows highlights how agentic neurotechnology can support earlier detection, more precise monitoring, and more responsive intervention across the full cognitive continuum.

TRANSLATIONAL ROADMAP FOR REAL‐WORLD DEPLOYMENT

Translating agentic neurotechnology from a conceptual framework to real‐world dementia care requires careful alignment with clinical workflows, care environments, and operational constraints, consistent with prior stakeholder‐informed analyses of barriers to dementia screening and care delivery. 13 Unlike conventional screening tools, agentic systems are designed to operate continuously across time and settings. Their successful deployment, therefore, depends not only on technical performance but also on integration into existing care ecosystems.

A pragmatic pathway for deployment begins in environments where continuous observation already occurs and where cognitive status spans the full range from healthy aging through early decline. Assisted living and Continuing Care Retirement Communities are particularly well suited as initial deployment settings because their residents are largely independent and cognitively heterogeneous, including individuals who are cognitively healthy, those in the preclinical risk‐detection window, and those with early MCI or mild dementia. This population mix offers the signal density needed for longitudinal model development while preserving individual autonomy and self‐directed consent, in contrast to memory care settings where dementia is already established and oversight structures are more constrained. The same agentic infrastructure can extend naturally to independent community‐dwelling adults through smartphone, wearable, and smart‐home modalities, which we view as a parallel and equally important deployment track for the preclinical risk‐detection window. In these settings, agentic neurotechnologies can augment routine interactions without introducing additional burden on staff or residents. Low‐friction sensing modalities, brief structured tasks, and passive monitoring allow systems to establish individualized cognitive baselines while preserving everyday routines. Outputs at this stage should emphasize monitoring and risk flagging rather than diagnosis.

To illustrate, consider how such a system might operate in an assisted living community. Sensing modalities could include brief voice‐based interactions captured during routine care touchpoints (such as morning check‐ins or medication reminders), passive measures of mobility and gait derived from ambient sensors or wearables, sleep and activity patterns collected from bed sensors or smartwatches, and engagement metrics drawn from tablet‐based interactions. Voice and behavioral signals could be sampled daily, while higher‐level summaries (such as deviations from individualized cognitive or behavioral baselines) could be reviewed weekly or monthly by care staff. When the longitudinal trajectory model detects a meaningful deviation, such as a sustained shift in semantic fluency over several weeks, the system could trigger context‐appropriate actions: prompting a brief follow‐up assessment, alerting a care navigator to schedule a clinician review, suggesting environmental adjustments, or initiating caregiver outreach. Specific cadence, signal selection, and action thresholds would vary by setting, individual baseline, and care context, and represent open questions for future empirical work.

Analogous instantiations can be envisioned in primary care (where periodic visit‐based screening could be augmented by between‐visit voice and behavioral signals collected via smartphone), in telehealth (where video‐based gaze, speech, and engagement metrics could be continuously captured during routine virtual encounters), and in home‐based care (where smart‐home sensors, wearables, and connected appliances could provide passive monitoring within the individual's everyday environment).

From these environments, agentic systems can interface with primary care and telehealth workflows by summarizing longitudinal cognitive trends rather than presenting raw data. This summary‐based integration is critical for clinical adoption, as it respects time constraints while providing context that episodic assessments lack. Of note, agentic outputs should be interpretable, uncertainty‐aware, and explicitly framed as decision support rather than clinical determinations.

As deployment expands, population‐level forecasting agents can support facility operations and care planning by identifying aggregate trends related to cognitive decline, depression risk, or functional instability. These insights may inform staffing strategies, program design, and resource allocation without exposing individual‐level predictions beyond appropriate governance boundaries.

Across all stages, translational success depends on iterative refinement informed by real‐world feedback. Agentic neurotechnology is capable of functioning as an evolving system that adapts alongside care practices rather than a static product delivered into fixed workflows.

ETHICAL AND GOVERNANCE CONSIDERATIONS

The continuous and adaptive nature of agentic neurotechnology introduces ethical considerations that extend beyond those associated with episodic screening tools. Because these systems infer cognitive change over time, preserving individual autonomy is paramount, particularly during preclinical and MCI stages when individuals may not perceive themselves as cognitively impaired.

Consent in this context must be treated as an ongoing process rather than a one‐time event. Individuals and caregivers should retain the ability to review, adjust, or withdraw participation as system behavior evolves, with consent processes that distinguish between preclinical monitoring, MCI‐stage longitudinal tracking, and early dementia care coordination, since the stakes and individual capacity to consent differ meaningfully across these phases. Transparent communication about what is monitored, how inferences are generated, and how outputs are used is essential to maintaining trust.

Agentic systems must also be designed to communicate uncertainty explicitly. Longitudinal inference should not be presented deterministically, and safeguards are required to prevent premature labeling or overinterpretation of early signals. Human‐in‐the‐loop governance remains critical, with clear boundaries separating agent recommendations from clinical decision making, and with explicit designation of which human actor (the individual being monitored, a designated caregiver, a care navigator, or a clinician) is responsible for reviewing and acting on outputs at each phase.

Finally, population‐level forecasting raises concerns related to equity, bias accumulation, and data stewardship. Continuous systems can amplify biases over time if not regularly audited and recalibrated. Ethical deployment, therefore, requires mechanisms for monitoring system behavior, ensuring accountability, and aligning agentic outputs with patient‐centered care values. Emerging models of clinical AI governance suggest that such mechanisms are most effective when implemented as multi‐stakeholder oversight structures spanning the full deployment lifecycle, from initial validity assessment through active post‐deployment monitoring for effectiveness and equity. 14

Translating this framework responsibly requires explicit attention to questions of process and practice. Consent should ordinarily be obtained by a clinician or trained care navigator with established standing in the individual's care, supported by surrogate decision‐making frameworks when capacity is in question. System ownership, data ownership, and ownership of derived inferences are distinct and need not align: the platform may be operated by a vendor, the raw data held by the care facility or health system under appropriate stewardship agreements, and the resulting clinical conclusions remain the responsibility of the treating clinician. The individual being monitored can and should serve as the primary human in the loop wherever capacity permits, with caregivers, care navigators, and clinicians engaged progressively as cognitive change advances or as system outputs cross predefined thresholds for clinical relevance. Participation in individual‐level monitoring should be separable from contribution to population‐level analyses, allowing individuals to opt out of aggregate analyses without losing access to personal monitoring and care. Population‐level oversight should rest with multi‐stakeholder governance structures that include clinicians, ethicists, patient and family representatives, and operational leadership, with explicit procedures for periodic audit, bias monitoring, and recalibration. These design choices are not incidental to the framework but are part of what makes continuous, agentic cognitive monitoring ethically deployable at scale.

CONCLUSIONS

Dementia care is at an inflection point. Although rapid advances in artificial intelligence and digital biomarkers have expanded what can be measured, they have not fundamentally changed how cognitive change is understood or acted upon in real‐world settings. Episodic assessments remain dominant, and early cognitive drift continues to go largely undetected. Filling this gap requires a shift from isolated tools toward systems designed for continuity, adaptation, and contextual reasoning.

In this perspective, we introduced Agentic Neurotechnology as a systems‐level framework for reimagining cognitive screening and care. By organizing sensing, reasoning, longitudinal modeling, care navigation, and population‐level insight into a coherent agentic stack, and embedding these capabilities within the Cognitive Continuum Loop, the framework aligns technological innovation with the temporal realities of cognitive decline. Framing dementia care across three windows of intervention further highlights how continuous, adaptive systems can support earlier detection, more precise monitoring, and more responsive care without reliance on static thresholds or single‐point diagnoses.

Agentic neurotechnology is not proposed as a replacement for clinical judgment, but as an active partner that augments decision‐making through longitudinal context, uncertainty‐aware reasoning, and adaptive support. As cognitive care extends increasingly beyond the clinic and into everyday environments, this framework provides a foundation for future empirical validation and responsible deployment of systems that are proactive, personalized, and aligned with the lived experience of cognitive aging.
