A rare case of angiosarcoma of the ureter

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary angiosarcoma of the urinary system is a rare, aggressive endothelial malignancy, with only 113 cases of renal angiosarcoma documented as of 2023. Angiosarcoma of the ureter is a much less common condition. Clinical manifestations commonly include flank pain, hematuria, an abdominal mass, weight loss, fatigue, and dizziness. In rare instances, spontaneous tumour rupture may result in retroperitoneal haemorrhage. Management primarily involves surgical resection, often complemented by adjuvant radiation therapy and chemotherapy.Case summaryAn 88-year-old patient was admitted to the hospital due to weight loss, urine retention and macroscopic hematuria. Initial evaluation with US and CT urography found a tumorous lesion in the pelvic part of the right ureter. PET/CT confirms a hypermetabolic enhancing lesion of the right ureter, with no metastatic lymph nodes or evidence of distant metastases. The patient was indicated for a laparoscopic right-sided nephroureterectomy, and the surgery was completed without complications. Histopathological evaluation confirmed the diagnosis of angiosarcoma, which was classified as grade II according to the FNCLCC classification and staged as pT3N0M0. A month after the surgery, the patient was bleeding from the right internal iliac artery. Embolisation was performed, and stent grafts were inserted. A few days later, the patient died from sepsis.ConclusionAngiosarcoma within the urinary system is rare. To date, only three cases of angiosarcoma of the ureter have been reported. Cases of angiosarcoma originating in the urinary bladder, kidney or other retroperitoneal structures were reported more frequently. Surgical intervention is the principal therapeutic option, followed by adjuvant therapy. Establishing standardised diagnostic and treatment protocols is crucial to improving patient outcomes, but at this moment, case reports are the only source of information on clinical management.

Introduction

Angiosarcoma is a rare malignancy characterised by the proliferation of endothelial cells. Tumourigenesis is initiated by the upregulation of transcription factors c-Myc, Forkhead Box F (FoxF1/FoxF2), and T-box transcription factor 3 (TBX3). Mutations in PDGF, EGF, c-KIT and c-MET are associated with activation of signalling pathways, such as Ras/Raf/MAPK and PI3K/PTEN/AKT/mTOR pathways, which contribute to cell cycle progression, cellular growth and survival. Stimulation of other pathways, such as WNT, Notch, and Hedgehog, contributes to metastatic spread via Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT). Targeting mTOR thus became an attractive approach for treating sarcoma [1]. In radiation-associated and Stewart-Treves angiosarcoma, MYC amplification correlates with shorter overall survival (OS) and is considered a potential prognostic biomarker [2]. While angiosarcoma typically arises in soft tissues such as the skin, bone, liver, and breasts, primary renal and ureteral angiosarcomas are exceedingly rare. As of 2023, only 113 cases of primarily renal angiosarcomas have been reported [3], and even fewer cases of angiosarcomas of the ureter, urinary bladder and retroperitoneum have been described. The neoplasm most commonly presents during the sixth and seventh decades of life and exhibits a marked male predominance, with a male-to-female ratio of 7:1 [4]. Clinical manifestations frequently include flank pain, hematuria, an abdominal mass, weight loss, fatigue, and dizziness. In rare instances, patients may present with spontaneous tumour rupture, resulting in a retroperitoneal hematoma [4]. While computed tomography (CT) is valuable for disease staging, distinguishing angiosarcoma from other urinary tract malignancies can be challenging because specific imaging findings are lacking [4]. Early diagnosis is a critical factor in sarcoma management; the backbone of treatment involves surgical resection, followed by radiation therapy and chemotherapy [4]. The article will describe a case of angiosarcoma of the ureter and the management that was provided. The number of reported cases of angiosarcoma is limited; such case reports may help to develop a standard treatment for future cases.

Case description

An 88-year-old Caucasian female, non-smoker, with a medical history of type II diabetes mellitus, grade III hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic vertigo, depression, hysterectomy and adnexectomy due to unknown causes, transient ischemic attack (TIA), fracture of the right ankle and no family history of cancer, was admitted to the hospital because of a weight loss of 6 kg, urine retention, grade II anaemia and macroscopic hematuria. The patient was using antihypertensive drugs, atorvastatin, melperone, and tranexamic acid for the hematuria. The patient denied allergies and did not consume alcohol. Laboratory testing revealed haemoglobin of 81 g/L, leukocyte count of 8.3 × 10⁹/L, hyperkalaemia (5.59 mmol/L), hyperuricaemia (11 mmol/L), CRP of 68.5 mg/l and creatinine of 103 µmol/L (baseline of 50 µmol/L). Urinary cytology was not obtained. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed dilatation of the pelvis of the right kidney. On CT urography, a tumour of the right ureter was found at the distal 2/3 of the ureter in the region of its intersection with the iliac vessels, with a suspicion of infiltration into the common iliac vein. A positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET/CT) scan was performed to exclude metastatic disease. The PET/CT showed hypermetabolism of 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) in an enhancing mass obstructing the pelvic part of the right ureter (Fig. 1). The initial differential diagnosis included urothelial carcinoma, sarcoma and metastasis from another site. The treatment options were subsequently discussed with the patient and her family, who insisted on surgery.

The case was discussed by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) comprising urologists, medical and radiation oncologists, radiologists, and pathologists. Preoperative systemic therapy was not considered. Targeted therapy was not available at the time for urinary tract tumours, and the only option would be chemotherapy, which would be extremely risky given the patient’s age. Moreover, a biopsy that would be needed to start systemic treatment was considered risky because of the tumour location, and the decision was made to proceed with surgery. The patient was indicated for a right-sided nephroureterectomy (RNU), which was performed laparoscopically. After initial mobilisation of the ascending colon, the Gerota fascia was opened, and the anatomical structures were identified. Subsequently, the vasculature was clipped, the ureter was resected, and the insertion point of the ureter to the urinary bladder was stitched. The whole specimen was sent to the Department of Clinical and Molecular Pathology for histopathological examination. The surgery was done without complications and with a total blood loss of 300 ml.

Two weeks after the laparoscopic surgery, a postoperative hematuria was examined with a cystoscope, without signs of bladder tumour, and a control CT urography confirmed it. During the cystoscopy procedure, a small biopsy measuring 10 × 10 × 4 mm and 3 mm was obtained for histopathological examination. Abdominal CT did not detect a hematoma or active bleeding. Later, the patient was transferred to the general ward for recovery, pain management and blood transfusions.

The first biopsy specimen received contained the right kidney with perirenal fat tissue, measuring 140 × 80 × 40 mm, while the kidney itself measured 90 × 60 × 30 mm. The cut surface showed light brown renal parenchymal tissue, with a dilated renal pelvis extending 50 × 45 mm and a parenchymal thickness of 10–25 mm, without macroscopic signs of a tumorous lesion. A separated right ureter, measuring 220 mm in length, was present, with a diameter of 5–20 mm. The length of its narrow portion measured 190 mm, while the wider and thicker portion at the distal 2/3 part extended up to 30 mm. The tumour was absent at the resection margins and was suspected at the circular margin. During the nephroureterectomy, a soft-tissue fragment measuring 20 × 15 × 3 mm, containing a small pelvic lymph node, was also obtained and processed for histopathological examination.

Microscopically (Fig. 2), the tumour was composed of vascular formations growing into the ureter, forming compact solid nests or slit-like and papillary structures, with atypical mitotic figures and pleomorphic, large cells with vesicular nuclei and prominent nucleoli. The lumen of these structures contained entrapped erythrocytes. Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for ERG, CD31, and some positivity for CD34. Tumour cells were negative for CK7, CK20, CDX2, ER, PR, PAX8 and WT-1. Ki-67 proliferation index of about 15%. The urothelium was intact upon examination, and no dysplasia was shown. The pelvic lymph node showed mild sinus histiocytosis, without evidence of metastasis. Microscopic angiosarcoma structures were also found in a soft-tissue fragment and in two small biopsy samples obtained from the right lateral wall of the urinary bladder during transurethral examination, 12 days after RNU.

The case was concluded as angiosarcoma based on its morphological appearance and immunohistochemical profile and was classified as grade 2 according to the FNCLCC classification (Table 1) and staged as pT3N0M0 with positive microscopic surgical margins (R1) according to the AJCC criteria. Adjuvant systemic therapy was not considered due to the patient’s frailty.

A month after the RNU, the patient was diagnosed with deep vein thrombosis (DVT) of the right posterior tibial vein and urinary tract infection and was treated with antibiotics and enoxaparin 0.6 ml (6000 IU) subcutaneously. The next day, she was presented with pyrosis, meteorism and abdominal pain. Laboratory testing revealed haemoglobin of 74 g/L, leukocyte count of 15.19 × 10⁹/L, hyperuricaemia (11.2 mmol/L), CRP of 113.4 mg/l and creatinine of 144 µmol/L. A hematoma with dimensions of 124 × 39 mm was revealed on a CT scan, which originated from active bleeding from a dissection of the right internal iliac artery at the level of the original tumour. Embolisation was performed distally to the bleeding locus, and two stent grafts were inserted. A sonographic examination showed dilation of the left renal pelvis, which was caused by the chronic, bulky clot in the bladder that was present at the time of operation, and a nephrostomy was performed. During the hospitalisation, the patient deteriorated and acquired sepsis, with positive cultures for Pseudomonas Aeruginosa. Antibiotic therapy with amikacin 500 mg i.v. was initiated, and Noradrenalin was given for hypotension, but the condition of the patient did not improve. After the decision to proceed merely with palliative care, the patient died. An autopsy was not performed.

Discussion

Angiosarcomas are aggressive soft-tissue tumours of endothelial origin [5]. The tumours may be found mainly in the head and neck or extremities [6]. In some cases, angiosarcomas were reported to be secondary to radiation exposure, e.g. after radiotherapy [5]. Another aetiology may be longstanding lymphedema, which can occur in the regions of implanted foreign material, arteriovenous fistulas, vascular malformations, areas of trauma or post-operative wounds. Certain syndromes, such as neurofibromatosis, xeroderma pigmentosa, Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome, Ollier disease and others, are associated with angiosarcomas [7]. Rarely, the tumour may arise from other neoplasms (benign and malignant nerve sheath tumours) [8,9]. Pediatric age angiosarcoma can be a part of Maffucci syndrome [10].

The tumour may metastasise to other loci, especially the lungs, bones, lymph nodes or other soft tissues [11]. Rare metastatic sites include the adrenal [12], bone marrow [13], pancreas [14] and thyroid [15]. Nevertheless, most of the patients with angiosarcoma present with a localised disease. Following diagnosis, the prognosis is poor, with an overall 1-year survival rate of 50% and a 5-year survival rate of 35% [5,6].

Data about primary ureteral angiosarcoma cases are limited. Only three other cases have been reported in humans. Two of the cases were localised tumours, and the third one developed metastases 8 months after the surgery. One patient lived for 1 year and 8 months without recurrence, but no further information is available on this case. No data on the OS rate is available. The case reported by Padaki et al. [16] was initially treated conservatively, but upon symptomatic deterioration, was indicated for a left nephroureterectomy. In this case, the MDT did not consider a ureteroscopic biopsy. In the present case, the tumour caused near-complete ureteral obstruction and was located adjacent to major iliac vessels, increasing the risk of bleeding. In addition, the imaging suggested a malignant ureteral tumour, for which radical nephroureterectomy represents the standard treatment modality. Therefore, and according to the patient’s wish, the MDT recommended proceeding directly to surgery, even though the patient was 88 years old and had a broad history of underlying diseases.

Nomura et al. [18] reported a similar case of a younger patient who underwent right nephroureterectomy. Ki-67 proliferation index was 20–30%, which is higher than in our case. On PET/CT, infiltration of the surrounding fat was observed, and an adjuvant radiation treatment was initiated. In the present case, the patient did not fully recover, had a low performance status and died before adjuvant treatment started.

The cases provided demonstrate that combining surgery with adjuvant treatment may prolong OS; however, it is impossible to infer statistical significance from so few cases.

Renal and ureteral angiosarcomas are typically managed with surgical resection, although surgery is rarely curative [16,19,20]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy may be considered following histological confirmation, as angiosarcomas demonstrate limited sensitivity to anthracyclines and taxanes [20] in the metastatic disease setting. Nevertheless, in the cases of ureteral angiosarcoma described in the literature, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was never indicated [16]. In the present case, neoadjuvant chemotherapy was not indicated due to the age of the patient, lack of histological confirmation, high toxicity profile and low efficacy. Radiotherapy is also considered a conventional treatment option, especially for elderly patients and is administered in higher doses (> 70 Gy) to improve OS [7]. Other studies emphasise that adjuvant radiotherapy is considered a better therapeutic solution than surgery alone [21]. Other possibilities, such as targeted therapies and immunotherapy, are currently under investigation [7]. The efficacy of targeted therapy, including immunotherapy, in angiosarcoma is limited to rare cases with positive molecular predictors. Histopathological analysis classified the tumour as FNCLCC grade 2 with a Ki-67 index of 15%. The discrepancy between histological grading and a fatal clinical course has been documented in other cases, where grading has limited prognostic value given the inherently aggressive nature of the disease [22,23].

Among reported genitourinary sites of angiosarcoma, the urinary bladder is the most frequently involved, followed by the ureter and periureteral soft tissues. In the present case, the largest lesion was present in the ureter, which was considered the primary site with secondary extension to nearby structures. Periureteral soft-tissue involvement was considered to represent local invasion rather than indicating the primary tumour site. The bladder lesion may result from direct extension from the ureter, metastatic spread, or seeding, although the latter is less common in angiosarcoma.

A diagnostic issue lies within the morphological characteristics of the tumour. In many cases, histological distinction between angiosarcomas and epithelioid haemangioendothelioma is challenging. Additionally, it should be carefully distinguished from other benign entities, such as haemangiomas [24]. The irregularity of the vasculature, the high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio and pleomorphism are important landmarks for definitive diagnosis [11].

The postoperative course was complicated by haemorrhage from the right internal iliac artery, which may have been related to the pre-existing vascular fragility associated with comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus and hyperlipidemia [25]. The combination of a high enoxaparin dose the day before may explain the bleeding. Another possible cause for bleeding is tumour infiltration, which could weaken the vascular wall, although this was not confirmed histologically. Preoperative CT urography suspected vascular involvement, and angiosarcomas are known for their aggressive vascular invasion [26].

Early diagnosis is crucial and can significantly impact the clinical outcome. Nevertheless, in the cases described to date, a high-grade tumour was revealed at the time of the diagnosis. Unfortunately, there are no screening possibilities or an option to obtain a biopsy before the surgery due to the low number of cases worldwide and due to the challenging location, which requires a radical therapeutic measure.

This case report has inherent limitations related to the retrospective nature and potential selection bias. Direct histological assessment of the iliac vessels was not performed, limiting a definitive determination of tumour origin. Urine cytology was not obtained as the initial clinical and radiological suspicion was not directed toward a primary urothelial malignancy. Despite the limitations, this case presents clinical considerations, including diagnostic challenges, and an aggressive clinical course even in tumours with intermediate histological grade. Case reports or small retrospective case series are the only sources of information on the management of patients with this rare condition.

Conclusion

Angiosarcoma of the ureter is a rare and aggressive tumour. As a result, very few cases have been reported. The treatment is usually surgical, although neoadjuvant chemotherapy with taxans or anthracyclines may be indicated in selected cases due to their relative sensitivity. The application of systemic or radiation treatment is determined by Medical and Radiation Oncologists and depends on the performance status of the patient and comorbidities. In hospitalised patients with deep vein thrombosis, precautions should be taken to avoid possible bleeding according to the HAS-BLED score. More cases should be described alongside the proposed treatment regimens to establish unified diagnostic and treatment guidelines that yield better outcomes in terms of OS rate and quality of life.
