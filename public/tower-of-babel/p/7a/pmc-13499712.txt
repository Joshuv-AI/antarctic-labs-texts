Exploring the Impact of Delivering Allergy Medication as Self-Care on Healthcare Greenhouse Gas Emissions in the United Kingdom
Authors: Patricia Pascual, Amanda Caudwell, Stephanie De Man, Michael Lally, Edouard Desrumaux, Thierry Rigoine de Fougerolles, Fiona Adshead
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe National Health Service is responsible for 4% of total greenhouse gas emissions in England and is committed to net zero carbon emissions and reduced carbon-intensive care practices. Self-treatable diseases represent significant avoidable healthcare resource utilisation, and the socio-economic value of self-care has been documented—self-care practices globally deliver savings of approximately $120 billion for healthcare systems and almost 41 billion productive days, gaining 22 million quality-adjusted life years each year. However, the impact on greenhouse gas emissions is poorly defined. This analysis investigated the effect of self-care on greenhouse gas emissions of the healthcare system in the UK, with a focus on self-care for allergies.MethodsThree scenarios were used to evaluate the potential impact on greenhouse gas emissions of the substitution of all self-treatable healthcare visits by over-the-counter and self-care medication, and the potential and current impact of self-care medication was explored for allergic rhinitis. Scenario 1 evaluated the impact of self-care, if used for all self-treatable diseases. Scenario 2 and scenario 3 focused on, respectively, the use of self-care for the treatment of allergic rhinitis today and the potential future value if all eligible medication for allergic rhinitis was obtained over-the-counter. Avoidable greenhouse gas emissions were based on avoided primary care consultations and accident and emergency visits.ResultsEach scenario resulted in reductions in greenhouse gas emissions from 2030 to 3930 kilo tons (kt) carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2eq) (scenario 1), 330–1000 kt CO2eq (scenario 2), and 100–200 kt CO2eq (scenario 3). These reductions represent approximately 8–16% (scenario 1), 1–4% (scenario 2), and 0.5–1% (scenario 3) of total National Health Service carbon emissions.ConclusionThis study confirmed the environmental aspect of the triple value of self-care (social, economic, and environmental), by showcasing how self-care can help to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by the healthcare system in the UK.Keywords: Allergies, Allergic rhinitis, Carbon emissions, Climate change, Fexofenadine, Greenhouse gas, Self-care, Sustainability

Introduction

Globally, the healthcare sector has a significant carbon footprint of 2.4 gigatons (gt) of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2eq) annually, which represents close to 5% of total global emissions [1, 2]. As such, and in line with its mission to protect and promote health and well-being, it is important for the healthcare sector to decrease its adverse environmental impact [1]. In the UK the National Health Service (NHS) is responsible for 4% of England’s greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and is calling for reduced carbon-intensive practices in response to climate change [2].

A major driver of avoidable healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU) is self-treatable diseases. The socio-economic role of self-care in terms of avoiding HCRU, budget savings, and productivity gains has been documented [3]. Globally, self-care practices deliver savings of approximately $120 billion for healthcare systems and almost 41 billion productive days, gaining 22 million quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) each year. However, barriers to widespread adoption of these practices remain, including feelings of loneliness and social isolation, poor mobility, and access issues [4]. The value of self-care comes from three main drivers: cost containment (i.e. avoiding unnecessary healthcare visits), welfare gains from increased productivity, and quality-of-life improvements from broader, more convenient, and faster access to healthcare. In Europe, current self-care and self-medication practices through long-term repeat prescriptions enable savings of over €23 billion annually for medical services and products [5]. Without self-care, in Europe, it has been estimated that each physician would have to work an extra 2.4 h per day or an extra 120,000 physicians would be required, placing further strain on healthcare systems in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic [5]. In the UK, self-care practices avoid the need for substantially increased healthcare resources and provide economic benefits to the NHS through avoided extra wages, workplace absences, and prescription and appointment costs, saving £6.4 billion each year in medical service and product costs and preventing £18 billion of costs related to work absences. Every £1 spent on self-care medication creates £8.40 in economic and social benefit [6]. However, little evidence is available regarding the impact of self-care on GHG emissions. Beyond its socio-economic value, self-care may also provide environmental benefits, representing this component of the triple value of self-care (social, economic, and environmental) [7].

In the UK, approximately 13 million individuals (representing around 20% of the population) suffer from allergic rhinitis [7, 8]. Environmental factors play a significant role in both its prevalence and severity, and both climate change and air pollution are known to worsen symptoms and are associated with elevated histamine levels [9–12]. Changes in climate—such as longer summers and shorter winters—extend the duration of pollen circulation, leading to prolonged exposure and more severe symptoms [13–15]. In addition, biodiversity loss can increase the prevalence of certain high-allergen plant species, such as ragweed, birch, Timothy grass, and nettle, further exacerbating allergic disease [13]. These environmental changes, partly driven by GHG emissions from healthcare and other sectors, have wide-ranging consequences for public health [16]. A feedback loop emerges: rising temperatures and pollution increase the burden of allergic and respiratory conditions, which drives greater HCRU, in turn contributing to further emissions and environmental impact [9–12].

This study was conducted to investigate the impact of delivering self-care medications, with a focus on self-care for allergies, on the GHG emissions of the healthcare system in the UK.

Methods

Scope

This study focused on the potential environmental value of patient self-care in the UK by evaluating the potential impact on GHG emissions of the substitution of all self-treatable healthcare visits by over-the-counter (OTC) and self-care medication. Ethics approval was not required for this study, which did not involve any animals or human participants. The potential and current impact on GHG emissions of self-care medication was also explored for allergic rhinitis, with the example of fexofenadine, a second-generation antihistamine medication which is available without a prescription in the UK and has demonstrated efficacy, with associated improvements in quality of life [17]. Equivalent doses of prescription and OTC preparations of fexofenadine have equivalent potency, although prescribed preparations can include a higher dose level than OTC products. The impact was measured in terms of patient care pathway GHG emissions only, based on a modelled reduction in HCRU (primary care [PC] and accident and emergency [A&E] use) [6]. The study compared a prescription-based journey with a self-care journey and therefore includes only the use phase, with no analysis of the carbon footprint associated with the product material acquisition and pre-processing production, distribution and storage, and end of life as per the GHG protocol [18, 19].

Patient Care Pathway (PCP)

In line with the technical guidance from the Sustainable Healthcare Coalition on sustainable patient care pathway analysis, the impact on HCRU of self-managed cases was measured through three outcomes: PC consultations, A&E attendance, and patient travel [20]. Among other possible outcomes, inpatient admission and surgery were excluded as these are not applicable to self-treatable conditions. An approach was applied which mapped HCRU associated with self-treatable illnesses for pathways with and without a visit to the doctor for a prescription (the only difference between the prescription and self-care pathways is the presence of this visit for the former) (Fig. 1) and estimated carbon emissions from the resulting PCP.

General Assumptions

The study used emission factors from the NHS estimated in Tennison et al. following a top-down approach which assumes an extended definition of the GHG protocol including patient and visitor travel [21]. The carbon footprint was estimated for HCRU for self-treatable healthcare utilisation and the avoidable emissions if the amount of OTC sold today were instead obtained via prescription for all self-treatable diseases and for allergic rhinitis alone. NHS GHG unitary emission factors were applied to HCRU to measure the associated GHG emissions of different scenarios of self-care adoption for all self-treatable conditions, and more specifically in the field of allergies.

Emissions Factors

Two sources, Tennison et al. [21] and the Sustainable Healthcare Coalition [22], were identified from the literature as the most representative and up-to-date sources for NHS England emission factors. The dataset reported by Tennison et al. was selected as it provides the most recent NHS England unit emission estimates derived from the 2019 NHS carbon footprint. These estimates encompass direct emissions from sources owned or controlled by healthcare organisations, indirect emissions associated with purchased energy consumption, broader value-chain emissions linked to organisational activities, as well as emissions generated through the transportation of patients and visitors. Accordingly, this source was selected for use here [21]. The analysis incorporated the following healthcare event-specific estimates reported by Tennison et al.: 66 kg CO2eq per primary care visit and 75 kg CO2eq per A&E visit [21]. In the absence of equivalent data for Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland, these figures were used universally [23–25].

Anaesthetic gases and metered dose inhalers as well as visitor travel were removed from the emission factor as these were not applicable to our study. Where relevant, patient travel was recalculated to be specific for travel to pharmacies in the UK.

Scenarios and Modelling Approach

Three investigated scenarios were included in the analysis and are described below. For each scenario we compare to the base case (i.e. the situation today) and only look at incremental GHG emission savings that can be achieved. The base case and the three investigated scenarios are presented in Fig. 1, and the scenarios are described below.

Scenario 1 (S1) was used to indicate the environmental value of self-care. This scenario assumed that self-care medications were used for all self-treatable diseases, including not only allergies but also headache, colds, indigestion, and other commonly self-treated conditions, and provided the total GHG emissions that would be saved if all self-treatable diseases were treated with OTC medication and all HCRU (GP and A&E visits) were thereby avoided. The number of GP and A&E visits that would be avoided (from published data [6]) was multiplied by the emissions factors as described above to obtain the total emissions avoided per person.

Scenario 2 (S2) focused on allergic rhinitis (i.e. the priority indication for this work) and evaluated the share of allergy medications that are currently sold with a prescription, and the environmental impact in terms of reduced GP visits if all prescription were replaced by OTC medication. The number of prescriptions for allergic rhinitis was multiplied by the emissions factors as described above to obtain the total emissions avoided per person.

Scenario 3 (S3) measured the impact of switching all patients with allergic rhinitis on prescribed medication to second-generation OTC antihistamine drugs (fexofenadine) to evaluate the reduction on HCRU (GP visits) in the event of severe allergy episodes. The avoided HCRU (from published data [6]) was multiplied by the emissions factors as described above to obtain the total emissions avoided per person.

For each scenario, the total emissions avoided per person was multiplied by the total patient pool to derive the total GHG emissions that would be avoided. A schematic overview of the modelling approach is presented in Fig. 2.

Data Inputs

For S1, HCRU attributable to self-treatable illnesses was directly available in the literature based on a study that estimated the socio-economic impact of self-care in the UK [6]. For S2, 2023 market data on OTC sales of allergy medication were used to derive the number of PC visits avoided. An estimated 1.4 packs of allergy medication per OTC trip was found, with one or two trips per buyer. The range 1.4 to 2.8 was therefore taken as an upper and a lower boundary. The total number of OTC boxes was divided by 1.4 and 2.8 to estimate the number of PC visits required to prescribe an equivalent amount of allergy medication. The same logic was applied to S3, with market data on allergy drugs delivered upon prescription being used. Yet, as only a proportion of patients would feel comfortable to self-manage their allergy symptoms, an adjustment factor was used, based on a behavioural study conducted in the UK in which it was found that 58% of people would be comfortable self-treating allergies [26].

Expert Review

Meetings were held between February 2024 and May 2024 with two subject matter experts to review the methodology, audit the GHG emission impact model, assess the data sources used, and discuss the findings of the study. Any disagreements were fully discussed and resolved at online meetings during this timeframe.

Results

For S1, which considers potentially avoided healthcare visits for all treatable diseases, the substitution of 25–48 million PC visits and 5–10 million A&E visits with OTC self-care would avoid emissions of 1650–3170 kt CO2eq and 380–760 kt CO2eq, respectively, i.e. a total of 2030–3930 kt CO2eq (Table 2). In the UK, this is equivalent to approximately 8–16% of total NHS carbon emissions [21].

Discussion

In this novel study based on UK data, we show self-care medications for self-treatable diseases, with a particular focus on allergic rhinitis, can significantly lower GHG emissions by reducing HCRU. The selection of allergic rhinitis, a common low morbidity condition increasingly influenced by climate change and pollution, is appropriate and plausibly impactful for this type of evaluation. Replacing all prescription medication for self-treatable disease with OTC medications in the UK (avoiding 2030–3930 kt CO2eq) equates to a reduction of up to 16% of the total NHS carbon footprint. This includes emissions from GP and A&E visits, patient travel, buildings, medical equipment, and business travel, and amounts to a significant carbon impact. While a full shift towards OTC for self-treatable diseases is highly unlikely, the result show potential role of self-care in reducing emissions. A more realistic scenario of delivering as self-care all eligible OTC allergy medication currently prescribed (i.e. S3 in our analysis) could avoid 100–200 kt CO2eq (approximately 0.5–1% of the NHS carbon footprint), equivalent to approximately 150,000–300,000 return flights from London to New York [21, 29]. Current self-care OTC use of medication for allergic rhinitis (i.e. S2 in our analysis) results in the avoidance of approximately 330–1000 kt CO2eq annually in the UK (approximately 1–4% of the NHS carbon footprint), equivalent to approximately 0.5–1.5 million such flights [21, 29]. The carbon impact of switching to OTC second-generation antihistamines (S3) therefore appears lower than that of complete avoidance of prescription medication (S2). A transition towards self-care for allergy treatment appears reasonable and safe, given the widespread existing OTC use, though improved patient education at point of purchase could help ensure patients understand when medical consultation is required.

The impact of the environment on human health is increasingly well documented, with allergies being one of the most striking examples. Our study focused on allergic rhinitis medication and used as an example the use of fexofenadine which is available both OTC and on prescription. Approximately 40–50% of individuals with allergic rhinitis have coexisting asthma which can become exacerbated if the allergic rhinitis is not treated adequately [31]. The impact of fexofenadine to reduce carbon emissions therefore is twofold. First, its availability as a self-care medication for individuals with allergic rhinitis means that PC visits can be avoided, translating into reduced carbon emissions that would otherwise be generated by HCRU. Second, the treatment of allergic rhinitis, using second-generation antihistamine drugs such as fexofenadine, is crucial in reducing the burden of allergic rhinitis and associated complications such as asthma exacerbation, thereby avoided the generation of GHG emissions from the use of metered dose inhalers which themselves contain hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) and generate significant GHG emissions that represent approximately 3% of the total NHS carbon footprint [17, 21, 32, 33].

In October 2020 the NHS became the world’s first health system to commit to reaching net zero and in 2022 became the first health system to embed net zero into legislation. An important aspect in achieving this goal is to use less carbon-intensive and resource-intensive healthcare practices, including the promotion of self-treatment rather than HCRU where clinically appropriate. As such, NHS England has published a commitment to ensure that sustainability is considered in all procurement decisions, including a call to the NHS suppliers to align with the net zero ambition [34] and it is important for manufacturers to make similar efforts. The example of fexofenadine illustrates the positive and quantifiable impact of increased self-care to reduce carbon emissions.

There are several assumptions and limitations to our study. First, regarding the patient care pathway to self-care, we estimated the benefits in terms of avoided HCRU and assumed that patients would seek healthcare advice for a single reason (i.e. allergic rhinitis in our study). No top-down emission factor was available for a pharmacy visit and it was assumed patients only purchased allergy drugs, with one or two visits and an average of 1.4 packs per visit; no assessment of e-commerce carbon footprint of was included. Second, emissions factors were specific for England but were extrapolated to the UK, and were not specific to self-treatable disease. Third, it is unclear whether prescription or OTC medication would result in higher patient costs, since prescription charges are not always reimbursed and OTC medication is not always out-of-pocket, and the analysis did not account for the reimbursement probability or pharmacy margins, both of which could increase prices. Fourth, in S3 we assumed asthma exacerbations due to pollution/pollen-induced rhinitis occur to a similar extent for prescribed allergic rhinitis medication and fexofenadine and would have the same HCRU impact per patient. Fifth, we did not include the costs of implementing self-care medications for allergies or the impact on emissions from other changes to health systems, which were out of scope but could be explored in future analyses. Lastly, in terms of the value of the switch to OTC medication, in the absence of data on prescribing causes, it was not possible to estimate the proportion of currently prescribed drugs could realistically be switched to OTC, as some would require medical attention.

However, despite these limitations, we have shown that a simplified patient pathway and reduced HCRU through increased self-care can reduce a health system’s carbon footprint in addition to delivering already known benefits to public health and alleviation of system capacity. Additionally, self-care avoids HCRU and unlocks economic benefits, and in the UK saves the NHS £6.4 billion in medication and appointment costs. This can be seen as another piece of evidence in support of the switch to OTC-eligible drugs from prescription and evidences the environmental aspect of the triple value of self-care.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated how rethinking the provision of treatment for allergies, which are exacerbated through air pollution, through increased self-care can be a key tool in breaking the vicious cycle of environmental factors and public health, where healthcare delivery, medication, and patient travel can further worsen environmental drivers of poor health, such as pollution and rising temperatures. This study therefore confirms the environmental aspect of the triple value of self-care, which also includes social and economic benefits, by closing the information gap on the environmental value of self-care.
