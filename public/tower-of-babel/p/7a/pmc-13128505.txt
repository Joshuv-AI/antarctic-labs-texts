Oral Administration of Withaferin A Increases Infiltration of CD4+ Helper T Cells in a Mouse Model of Mammary Tumor
Authors: Eun-Ryeong Hahm, Krishna B. Singh, Shivendra V. Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Withaferin A (WA) is a small molecule present in Ashwagandha plant that prevents breast cancer progression in mice and rats. The mechanisms underlying breast cancer prevention by WA are not fully understood. Herein, we report effects of WA treatment on an immune landscape in C3(1)-TAg transgenic mice, which develop basal-like breast cancer. Oral administration of 12 mg/kg body weight of WA decreased the wet tumor weight by about 43%. Weight loss or any other adverse effects (e.g., impaired movement, hunched back, ruffled fur, etc.) were not observed. Tumors and splenocytes from WA-treated mice exhibited the increased proportion of CD4+ helper T cells when compared to control mice. The proportion of CD8α+ T cells and natural killer cells was not affected by WA treatment in either mammary tumor or splenocytes. Similarly, the proportions of lymphoid dendritic cells, myeloid dendritic cells, M2 macrophages, regulatory T cells, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells were comparable in mammary tumors and splenocytes of both control and WA-treated mice. Unlike the C3(1)-TAg mouse model, WA administration failed to increase the proportion of CD4+ T cells in mammary tumors of rats. The present study indicates that even though WA treatment increases the proportion of helper T cells in mammary tumors and spleen, attenuation of immune evasion may have a minimal role in its breast cancer prevention at least in rodent models of basal-like [C3(1)-TAg model] and luminal-type (rat model) breast cancer.

INTRODUCTION

Withania somnifera is a medicinal plant native to Southcentral and Southeast Asia. Ashwagandha root has been used in Indian medicine practice (Ayurveda) for thousands of years [1]. Formulations containing the Withania somnifera root extract (WRE) have been tested clinically in randomized and controlled trials for health benefits and alleviation of symptoms of various ailments [2]. For example, meta-analysis of 12 articles involving a total of 1,002 study participants whose age ranged from 25 to 48 years concluded that Ashwagandha significantly reduced anxiety (standardized mean difference [SMD]: –1.55; 95% CI: –2.37 to –0.74; P = 0.005) and the stress level (SMD: –1.75; 95% CI: –2.29 to –1.22; P = 0.005) when compared to the placebo group [3]. A total of 5 randomized and controlled clinical trials revealed that the Ashwagandha extract exhibited a small but significant effect on overall sleep (SMD: –0.59; 95% CI: –0.75 to –0.42), and this effect was more pronounced in a subgroup of adults with insomnia with dosage of ≥ 600 mg/day and treatment duration of ≥ 8 weeks [4].

Ashwagandha supplementation was more effective in improving physical performance in healthy men and women [5]. Early evidence also exists from randomized, placebo-controlled, and double-blind clinical trials supporting cognitive benefits of Ashwagandha [6]. Preclinical studies in rodents have revealed an appreciable effect of WRE on neurological abnormalities from oxidative stress in brain caused by physical and chemical insults [7]. Even though Ashwagandha is generally considered safe, a recent review highlights some potential adverse effects including gastrointestinal side effects, immune hypersensitivity, liver changes, and endocrine disruption especially with prolonged use and at a higher dose [8]. However, the evidence for such side effects is not consistent or corroborated by preclinical studies [8].

Clinical trials exploring anticancer outcomes (e.g., response rate, overall survival, etc.) of Ashwagandha are yet to be conducted, but the anticancer effect of WRE is mainly attributed to WA [9]. WA treatment inhibited growth of MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 human breast cancer cells in association with apoptosis induction [10]. Notably, a 4-fold higher concentration of WA was needed to induce modest apoptosis in non-oncogenic immortalized human breast epithelial MCF-10A cells [10]. Cancer cell selectivity for apoptosis induction is a desirable characteristic of preventive interventions. Treatment of MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells with WA also caused G2/M phase cell cycle arrest [11]. Interestingly, the G2/M phase cell cycle arrest in WA-treated cells was maintained even after removal of the drug, indicating that the cell cycle arrest was irreversible [11].

Inhibition of oncogenic transcription factors and kinases in human breast cancer cells upon treatment with WA has been documented to explain its anticancer mechanisms [12-15]. Other notable anticancer effects of WA in breast cancer include: (a) inhibition of cell migration, (b) inhibition of breast cancer stem like cells in vitro and in vivo, (c) inhibition of epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and (d) suppression of breast cancer cell-induced osteoclast differentiation [12,16-18].

In vivo anticancer effect of WA has been demonstrated in a therapeutic as well as preventive setting [10,19,20]. WA treatment inhibited breast cancer development in a transgenic mouse model of Her2+ subtype and luminal-type mammary cancer in rats without causing any side effects [19,20]. The present study extends these observations and investigates the effect of WA treatment on the immune landscape using C3(1)-TAg mice, which develop basal-like breast cancer [21].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

Use of mice and rats for the present study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care Use Committee of the University of Pittsburgh (protocol # 20128342).

Reagents

WA (purity > 95%) was purchased from ChromaDex. Stock solution of WA was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; 20 mM stock) and stored at –80°C. An antibody against CD4 was from BD Horizon (cat. # 565974; RRID: AB_2738426). Antibodies against CD45 (cat. # 103106; RRID: AB_ 312971), T cell receptor β chain (TCRβ; cat. # 109251; RRID: AB_2810348), CD8α (cat. # 100721; RRID: AB_312760), CD19 (cat. # 115543; RRID: AB_11218994), CD49b (cat. # 103503; RRID: AB_313026), NKp46 (cat. # 137612; RRID: AB_2563104), CD11b (cat. # 101245; RRID: AB_2561390), CD11c (cat. # 117327; RRID: AB_2129642), Lys6C (cat. # 128025; RRID: AB_10643867), Lys6G (cat. # 127636; RRID: AB_2563207), and CD206 (cat. # 141727; RRID: AB_2565822) were from BioLegend. Antibodies against major histocompatibility complex class II (MHCII; cat. # 50-163-69) and FoxP3 (cat. # IC8214A-100) were from Thermo Fisher Scientific and R&D systems, respectively. Antibodies used for immunohistochemistry of CD4 (cat. # 203305; RRID: AB_1501180) and FoxP3 (cat. # 320008; RRID: AB_492980) were purchased from BioLegend. An antibody against CD8α (cat. # sc-7970; RRID: AB_627208) for immunohistochemistry was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology.

C3(1)-TAg mice study

Noncarrier female and hemizygous male FVB-Tg(C3-1-TAg)cJeg/JegJ mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (cat. # 013591). After transgene verification, 6-week old female transgenic mice were divided into two groups. Control mice (n = 5) were orally treated with vehicle (10% DMSO, 10% ethanol, 30% Cremophor EL, and 50% PBS) while mice in the WA treatment group were orally treated with 12 mg/kg body weight. Treatments were done five times/week for 19 weeks. Body weights, tumor presence, and tumor burden were monitored weekly. At 25 weeks of age, mice were sacrificed, and blood, spleen, and mammary tumors were collected and processed for the experimental procedures.

Flow cytometric immunophenotyping

Single cells were prepared from spleen and mammary tumor tissues. For preparation of splenocytes, spleen was placed in 70-μm cell strainer, mashed with 3-5 mL of syringe plunger, and then flushed with ice-cold PBS. After centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 3 minutes, pellets were resuspended in 5 mL of ACK Lysing Buffer (Gibco; cat. # A1049201) and then incubated for 8 minutes at room temperature to remove red blood cells. The reaction was stopped by adding 10 mL of cold PBS. Mouse splenocytes were obtained after centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 3 minutes. Splenocytes were resuspended in cell staining buffer from BioLegend (cat. # 420201). For isolation of single cells from mammary tumors, tissues were homogenized by being minced with scalpel and incubated in digesting medium containing collagenase/hyaluronidase/DNase at 37°C for 30 minutes with gentle stirring. Resultant homogenates were sieved through a 70-µm cell strainer to obtain single cell suspension. ACK Lysing Buffer was added to remove red blood cells. Single cells were incubated with 14 different fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies and then processed for multicolor flowcytometry using a Cytek® Aurora flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences).

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor sections (4-5 mm thickness) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and washed with PBS. Antigen retrieval was done in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 minutes at 100°C. After blocking for 1 hour, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber with the following primary antibodies: PE-conjugated anti-FoxP3 (1 : 50 dilution), FITC-conjugated anti-CD4 (1 : 50 dilution), and unconjugated anti-CD8α (1 : 50 dilution). After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 633-conjugated secondary antibody (1 : 150 dilution) for 1 hour at room temperature. Sections were washed again with PBS and mounted in media containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Images were acquired using a Nikon A1 confocal microscope. Quantification of positively stained cells was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical tests

GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.0) was used for statistical analyses. Statistical analysis for tumor incidence data was performed using Chi square test. Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons. A P-value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data availability

All raw data may be shared upon request to the corresponding author.

RESULTS

Effect of oral WA administration on mammary tumorigenesis in C3(1)-TAg mice

WA treatment did not affect body weight of mice (Fig. 1A). Mice of the control and WA treatment groups did not exhibit any other sign of side effects such as impaired movement, hunched back, ruffled fur, etc. At 19 weeks of age, 20% of the control mice had mammary tumors (Fig. 1B). Mammary tumors in WA-treated mice were detectable at 22 weeks of age with about 20% incidence (Fig. 1B).

Hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections from all mammary glands of each mouse of control and WA-treatment groups are shown in Figure 2. Except for mouse #943, each mouse of the control group developed multiple mammary tumors (Fig. 2). There was little evidence of multiple mammary tumors in mice of the WA treatment group (Fig. 2). Accordingly, the overall mammary tumor incidence at 25 weeks of age in the WA treatment group was 50% lower when compared to control mice (Fig. 1B).

As previously reported in the literature, the C3(1)-TAg model exhibits a stepwise progression of mammary tumorigenesis [21]. Mice develop mammary epithelial atypia at approximately 8 weeks of age, which advances to mammary intraepithelial neoplasia, analogous to human ductal carcinoma in situ at about 12 weeks of age [21]. These lesions progress to invasive carcinoma with palpable tumors [21]. At 25 weeks of age, majority of the control mice (4 out of 5) exhibited advanced tumors along. In contrast, only 2 out of 5 mice in the WA-treated group displayed such advanced pathological features. Notably, one control mouse and three WA-treated mice exhibited only ductal hyperplasia without progression to more advanced lesions. Cumulative tumor weight (Fig. 1C) and tumor volume (Fig. 1D) were also measured in WA-treated mice in comparison with control mice. This study was not powered for statistical comparisons because the primary focus was on determination of the effect of WA treatment on the immune landscape. However, these results suggest that WA treatment might be effective in preventing development of basal-like breast tumors in C3(1)-TAg mice, but a sufficiently powered study in future is needed to validate this contention.

Oral WA administration increases the proportion of CD4+ helper T cells

The proportion of mammary tumor infiltrating CD4+ helper T cells was higher by about 2.1-fold in WA-treated mice when compared to the control group (Fig. 3). In addition to its role in hematopoiesis and red blood cell clearance, spleen is also responsible for a broad spectrum of immunological functions [22]. Similar to mammary tumor tissues, the proportion of CD4+ T cells in the spleen was increased by about 1.9-fold in mice treated with WA in comparison with control mice (Fig. 3). The proportion of other cells of the immune system, including CD8α+ cytotoxic T cells and CD49b+NKp46+ natural killer (NK) cells, was not affected by WA administration (Fig. 3).

The effect of WA administration on the proportion of dendritic cells (DC) and macrophages

The proportion of CD45+CD11b-CD11c+MHCII+ lymphoid DC and CD45+CD11b+CD11c+MHCII+ myeloid DC was comparable in the mammary tumors and spleen of control and WA-treated mice (Fig. 4). WA treatment did not affect the proportion of CD45+CD11b+CD11c-Lys6C-Lys6G- macrophages and M2 macrophages either in mammary tumors or in the spleen (Fig. 4).

The effect of WA administration on the proportion of regulatory T cells (Treg) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC)

Immunosuppressive cells in the tumor microenvironment include Treg and MDSC [23,24]. Figure 5 shows the effect of WA administration on proportion of CD4+FoxP3+ Treg and MDSC in the mammary tumor and splenocytes. The proportion of CD4+FoxP3+ Treg did not differ significantly between control and WA-treated mice either in the mammary tumor or in the splenocytes (Fig. 5). Likewise, the proportion of granulocytic and monocytic MDSC remained unaffected by WA treatment (Fig. 5).

The effect of WA administration on the proportion of T cells and Treg in mammary tumors of rats

Figure 6 shows immunohistochemistry for CD4, CD8α, and FoxP3 in mammary tumors of control and WA-treated rats. The proportions of CD4+ T cells, CD8α+ T cells, and Treg were apparently lower in the mammary tumors of WA-treated rats when compared to that in control mice, but the difference was not statistically significant. Because fresh mammary tumor tissues or spleen from rats were not available, flow cytometric analysis of other immune cells was not possible.

DISCUSSION

In our earlier published study, WA treatment (4 mg/kg body weight) inhibited breast tumor development in mouse mammary tumor virus (MMTV)-neu mice [19]. WA treatment resulted in a decrease in the macroscopic mammary tumor size, the microscopic mammary tumor area, and the pulmonary metastasis incidence in MMTV-neu mice [19]. For example, the area of invasive breast cancer was decreased by about 95% in the WA-treated mice compared with the control group [19]. WA treatment resulted in a 50% reduction in palpable (macroscopic) tumor weight [19].

Prevention of N-methyl-N-nitrosourea-induced luminal-type breast cancer upon intraperitoneal injection of 4 mg/kg and 8 mg/kg WA has also been demonstrated by us previously [20]. In the rat model, WA administration at 4 mg/kg and 8 mg/kg body weight resulted in a statistically significant decrease in mammary tumor incidence when compared to control rats [20]. Moreover, the average number of mammary tumors/rat (tumor multiplicity) was decreased by 40%-50% in WA treatment groups in comparison with control rats [20]. Finally, mammary tumor weight in the 8 mg WA/kg group was reduced by about 68% when compared with control rats [20]. Collectively, these studies established preventive efficacy of WA for two major subtypes (Her2-enriched and luminal-type) of breast cancer. In the present study, we extended these findings to determining the effect of oral WA treatment on mammary tumorigenesis in a mouse model of basal-like breast cancer. The incidence of breast cancer in C3(1)-TAg mice was decreased by about 50% suggesting that WA may have preventive activity against basal-like breast cancer as well. However, a larger and sufficiently powered study in future is needed to validate this contention.

In the present study, WA treatment increased mammary tumor infiltration of CD4+ T cells in mice but not in rats. The reasons for this discrepancy are not yet clear, but may be due to differences in species-specific immune response, tumor models (transgenic model of breast cancer in mice versus chemically induced breast cancer in rats), and possibly pharmacokinetic behavior of WA in mice and rats. Pharmacokinetics of WA has been studied in mice and rats, but the results are variable [25-28]. A single intraperitoneal injection of 4 mg WA/kg body weight to female Balb/c mice resulted in plasma Cmax of about 1.8 μM [25]. A Cmax of only 14 nM was reported following a single intraperitoneal injection of 5 mg WA/kg in male C57BL/6N mice [26]. Strain and sex of mice could be one reason for variability in results. The Cmax of WA in male Sprague-Dawley rats was 1.32 μM and 6.5 µM after oral (10 mg WA/kg) and i.v. administration of WA (5 mg/kg), respectively [27]. Another study using Sprague-Dawley rats (sex not disclosed) reported a Cmax of about 62 nM following i.v. injection of 4.5 mg/kg [28]. Differences in the immune landscape between mice and rats have also been noted [29].

Solid tumors including breast cancer are characterized by different hallmarks, including genome instability, sustained proliferation, evasion of apoptotic cell death, circumvention of growth suppressors, deregulation of bioenergetics, enabling of replicative immortality, infiltration of tumor promoting factors, promotion of angiogenesis, evasion of immunity, and invasion and metastasis [30,31]. WA treatment was previously shown to attenuate many of these cancer hallmarks including inhibition of cell proliferation and apoptosis induction [10,11,19,20], normalization of genome instability [14], inhibition of aberrant bioenergetics [19,20,32,33], and suppression of cell migration/invasion and metastasis [12,15,19,25].

The primary focus of the present study was to determine the effect of WA treatment on immune evasion using a C3(1)-TAg mouse model of breast cancer. Except for an increase in proportion of CD4+ helper T cells in the mammary tumors and splenocytes of C3(1)-TAg mice upon oral WA treatment, other types of immune cells were not affected by this phytochemical. Based on these results, we conclude that restoration of immune surveillance may have a minimal role in breast cancer prevention by WA treatment at least in rodent models of basal-like and luminal-type subtypes.

It is interesting to note that increased tumor infiltration of CD4+ T cells was not accompanied by an increase in the proportion of CD8+ T cells or NK cells. Studies have shown that CD4+ T cells can reject tumor cells without involvement of CD8+ T cells or NK cells [34]. Mechanisms underlying tumor cell rejection by CD4+ T cells include direct cytotoxicity through expression of perforin and granzymes or recognition of major histocompatibility complex II expressing tumor cells, indirect tumor cell killing by IFNγ, induction of senescence, and targeting vasculature [34].

While further work is necessary to determine which mechanism contributes to tumor cell killing by WA in the C3(1)-TAg model, a few reports have documented the effect of WA on senescence in a lung cancer cell line, a neuroblastoma cell line, and breast cancer cells [35-37]. Treatment with WA resulted in induction of senescence in lung cancer and neuroblastoma cells [35,36]. In contrast, we observed inhibition of senescence upon treatment of breast cancer cells with WA [37]. Induction of the senescence marker p21 protein by WA treatment was also observed in mammary tumors of rats [37]. Such discrepancy in results between lung cancer/neuroblastoma cells and breast cancer cells could be due to the dose of WA used. However, further work is necessary to determine the functional significance of senescence inhibition in the antitumor effect WA exerts in breast cancer.

Several cytokines play an important role in survival and function of CD4+ T cells: these include IFNγ, IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-12, and IL-15 [38]. We compared cytokine levels in plasma and mammary tumors from control and WA-treated rats [20]. WA treatment did not affect levels of any of these cytokines in either plasma or tumor [20].

Published work by others paints a different rosy picture on attenuation of immune evasion by WA treatment. WA treatment decreased MDSC production of reactive oxygen species, a plausible mechanism by which MDSC impairs T cell activation [39,40]. Suppression of granulocytic MDSC upon oral administration of WA has been demonstrated in the 4T1 mouse mammary tumor model [39]. In the present study, we did not observe a change in monocytic or granulocytic MDSC upon WA treatment in the C3(1)-TAg model. WA treatment was shown to decrease in vitro MDSC production of IL-10, which intensifies upon crosstalk with the macrophages to promote a pro-tumorigenic phenotype [39,41]. Even though it is not clear if WA treatment affects MDSC IL-10 production, we did not see any effect of WA treatment on the proportion of macrophages or CD206+ M2 macrophages. Inhibition of mitogen-stimulated secretion of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IFNγ in CD8+ and CD4+ T cells was also reported [42]. Immune modulation by WRE has also been reported and this may be attributable to WA [43,44]. Discrepancy in results between our data and those reported by others in the literature may be attributed to the different animal models used.

There are other known effects of WA that deserve attention to its antitumor properties, including antiestrogenic and epigenetic activities [13,45,46]. For example, the protein level of estrogen receptor (ER)-α, but not ER-β, was decreased upon treatment of MCF-7 cells with WA [13]. Apoptosis induction by WA treatment was modestly attenuated by overexpression of ER-α in MDA-MB-231 cells [13]. WA-induced apoptosis was also attenuated by supplementation of 17β-estradiol [13]. WA treatment caused a decrease in DNA methyltransferase activity in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells [46]. Expression of histone deacetylase 1 mRNA and protein was suppressed following treatment of MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells with 1 mM WA [46].

In conclusion, WA treatment increases the proportion of helper T cells in mammary tumors and spleen, but other immune cells are not affected by this phytochemical.
