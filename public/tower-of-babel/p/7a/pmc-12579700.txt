Epidemiology, treatment patterns and effectiveness of adherence to Bacillus Calmette-Guérin therapy in intermediate and high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer patients: a retrospective cohort analysis using German health claims data
Authors: Jaime Luna, Eveline Otte im Kampe, Heja Aga-Barfknecht, Moritz Lehne, Leonie Kunk, Ivonne Hänsel, Saskia Trescher, Antje Mevius, Agnes Kisser, Nils Kroeger, Maria De Santis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo assess the epidemiology, treatment patterns, and Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) instillations in Non-Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer (NMIBC) patients to evaluate guidelines compliance and effectiveness of adherence on real-world outcomes.MethodsNMIBC patients with BCG-treatment were identified in a retrospective cohort study using German health claims data. The first BCG prescription was defined as index date. Crude and standardized annual incidence were calculated. Subsequent treatment regimens were described based on prescriptions to assess adherence. BCG-maintenance adherents were compared with non-adherents using a landmark time approach and Cox proportional hazard model.ResultsThe annual standardized incidence per 100,000 for intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC patients ranged from 7.6 to 10.4 in males and 1.2 to 1.9 in females from 2017 to 2021. The main cohort consisted of 817 patients (mean age: 72.5; male: 78%). Most patients (98%) received transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) as initial treatment followed by BCG, with a median number of 13 BCG instillations per patient (IQR: 8–21). 28% continued BCG in the maintenance phase at 24 months post-induction and 62% received at least one Re-TURBT post-index. BCG maintenance-adherent patients showed lower risk compared with non-adherent patients: adjusted hazard ratio (aHR): 0.92 [0.73–1.18] for all-cause death; aHR: 0.49 [0.36–0.67] for advanced treatment; aHR: 0.65 [0.53–0.80] for disease progression.ConclusionReal-world compliance with clinical guidelines may require improvement based on BCG maintenance duration and adherence to treatment schedules. The benefits of BCG adherence in improving oncology-relevant outcomes support guidelines recommendations for sufficient BCG maintenance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00345-025-06025-1.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BC) is among the 10 most common cancers worldwide, accounting for 3% of all cancer diagnoses [1], and its incidence increases with age [2, 3]. Non-Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer (NMIBC) accounts for approximately 75% of all incident BC diagnoses [4]. In Germany, approximately 30,000 patients were newly diagnosed with BC in 2022 [5].

Medical guidelines from the European Association of Urology (EAU), and the German S3 Guidelines recommend TURBT followed by intravesical BCG immunotherapy for intermediate and high-risk NMIBC patients [6, 7]. For intermediate-risk patients, one year of BCG instillation therapy is recommended [6–9]. For maintenance therapy in high-risk NMIBC patients, intravesical BCG treatment is recommended for 1 to 3 years [6–9]. A reduction in the risk of progression has been shown for both intermediate and high-risk patients when the BCG maintenance schedule is followed [10, 11]. Furthermore, a three-year maintenance regimen has been shown to be more effective than one year in preventing recurrence in high-risk patients [12]. Despite clinical guideline recommendations, adherence to long-term BCG treatment for NMIBC remains challenging [13, 14].

Other than BCG, the therapeutic landscape regarding instillation treatments for NMIBC is very limited, with few treatment options and low level of evidence in high-risk NMIBC available, such as intravesical chemotherapy instillations including mitomycin C, gemcitabine, epirubicin, or doxorubicin.

Scarce evidence on routine clinical practice in Germany limits our understanding of treatment patterns, adherence, and patient outcomes, highlighting a gap in knowledge regarding the effectiveness and challenges of managing NMIBC. This study aimed to generate real-world evidence from NMIBC patients in Germany, focused on BCG-treated patients, to assess compliance with clinical guidelines and its effectiveness on real-world outcomes.

Methodology

Study design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using German Statutory Health Insurance (SHI) data from January 1, 2010, to December 31, 2023, provided by the AOK PLUS sickness fund.

Study population

NMIBC patients with BCG-treatment were identified using the following selection criteria. Eligible patients had at least one inpatient and/or two confirmed outpatient diagnoses of BC within two quarters over 12 months (International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision German Modification (ICD-10-GM): C67, D09.0). To ensure the inclusion of incident BC patients, those without a prior BC diagnosis within two years before the first recorded diagnosis were included. The index date was defined as the first BCG prescription, after the incident BC diagnosis and before the end of the inclusion period. Patients with any prior BCG prescription within two years before the index date were excluded. Only adult patients (≥ 18 years) who were continuously insured for at least two years before their first BC diagnosis were included. Patients were excluded if they had any record indicative of muscle-invasive BC (MIBC) or late-stage BC within two years before the index date and/or within three months after the index date (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To estimate the incidence, NMIBC patients with BCG-treatment between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2021, were identified. A main cohort to determine treatment patterns and adherence effectiveness was established from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2019, ensuring a potential minimum follow-up period of approximately 36 months of maintenance post-induction and a potential follow-up extending beyond the maintenance phase. This approach enabled us to record time-to-event outcomes within a reliable timeframe. An overview of the study period for the epidemiological analysis and main cohort is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

BCG prescription was used as a treatment proxy to identify intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC patients, based on clinical guideline recommendations and the absence of detailed clinical information in health claims data. High-risk NMIBC patients were further classified using a proxy for BCG prescription duration: those who received BCG induction (within 70 days of initiation) followed by maintenance therapy (BCG prescription ≥ 18 months post-induction).

Adherence definitions

BCG-induction adherence was defined as receiving ≥ 5 instillations of BCG within 70 days of BCG initiation, including the first instillation. BCG-maintenance adherence was defined as receiving ≥ 7 instillations of BCG within 274 days of BCG initiation, including the first instillation [15, 16].

Data source

The AOK PLUS database contains anonymized patient-level data covering approximately 3.4 million insured individuals in the federal states of Saxony and Thuringia in central-eastern Germany (over a half of the local population). Health insurance is mandatory in Germany with around 90% of the population (almost 75 million persons) being covered by German SHI. German health insurance bodies provide nearly full coverage of all healthcare services.

Statistical analysis

All findings were reported using the guidelines of the Standardized Reporting of Secondary data Analyses (STROSA) [17]. Descriptive and analytical statistical analyses were conducted using STATA software version 17 (STATA Corp., College Station, Texas).

Categorical variables were described as frequencies with percentages, and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and means with standard deviations (SDs).

Epidemiology

NMIBC patients with an index BCG prescription in a given calendar year were counted as incident cases for that year. The cumulative incidence was estimated as the proportion of newly diagnosed patients divided by the population. The population was defined as the number of insured people enrolled in AOK PLUS susceptible to developing NMIBC (adults alive and continuously insured for at least 12 months at the beginning of every calendar year).

Crude and sex- and age-standardized incidence were calculated annually based on the German statutory health insurance population (KM6 statistic, German Federal Ministry of Health) and the national population (census data, German Federal Statistical Office). The number of expected cases in the reference populations was extrapolated based on sex and age distribution per calendar year.

Treatment patterns & time-to-event outcomes

A Sankey diagram was used to visualize treatment patterns. BCG instillations were analyzed, including the number of instillations per patient and total duration of therapy (from first to last prescription). Since health claims data capture BCG prescriptions rather than instillation records, each prescribed BCG vial was assumed to correspond to one instillation. The number of vials was determined using the German pharmaceutical registration (PZN) number for each prescription.

Relevant outcomes including advanced therapy, real-world disease progression (RWDP), and overall survival (OS), were analyzed among BCG-maintenance adherent and non-adherent patients.

A landmark analysis was conducted to estimate OS, as adherence status was determined at a specific time point during follow-up (landmark time) [18, 19]. The landmark time was set at 274 days after BCG initiation. Patients who were lost to follow-up or died prior to the landmark time were excluded. OS analysis was conducted from the set landmark until the date of death (all-cause) or censoring (end of continuous insurance or end of the study), whichever occurs first.

Advanced treatment was defined as the time to the first occurrence of advanced therapy (Supplementary Table 1 shows the treatment list) or radical cystectomy. RWDP was assessed as a composite outcome, defined by the first occurrence of radical cystectomy, metastasis diagnosis, administration of advanced therapies, or death. Advanced treatment and RWDP analyses were conducted from the index date until the occurrence of an event or censoring, whichever occurs first. The analyses were restricted to patients who reached the landmark time.

Time-to-event outcomes and probabilities over time were reported using the Kaplan-Meier method. Cox proportional hazard modelling was performed and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% CIs were estimated adjusting for potential confounding (including baseline variables age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index score and number of hospitalizations).

Results

Incidence

From 2017 to 2021, the incidence of intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC patients remained relatively stable, ranging from 7.6 to 10.4 per 100,000 in males and 1.2 to 1.9 per 100,000 in females. Supplementary Table 2 presents crude and standardized incidence, along with crude numbers and patient extrapolation for intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC and high-risk NMIBC.

Main cohort

The main cohort consisted of 817 incident intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC patients identified between 2012 and 2019. The stepwise patient selection process is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Most patients (98%) received TURBT as initial treatment, followed by BCG. The median number of BCG instillations per patient was 13 (IQR: 8–21), with a median treatment duration of 352 days (IQR: 56–861).

Adherence status

A total of 703 patients (86.0%) were adherent in the induction phase, while 587 patients (71.8%) were adherent in the maintenance phase. Adherent patients were slightly older, with a higher comorbidity index and more hospitalizations compared to non-adherent (Table 1).

Treatment patterns

The treatment pattern of patients who received BCG as first-line therapy showed that most patients did not receive sufficient BCG maintenance as per schedule. Only 28% continued BCG in the maintenance phase at 24 months post-induction. A low rate of switching to other NMIBC therapies was observed during the follow-up (Fig. 1), with Mitomycin C being the most common switch. More than half of the patients (61.6%) underwent at least one Re-TURBT during the follow-up period. Among them, the median number of Re-TURBT procedures post-index was 2 (IQR 1–3). The Sankey diagram in Fig. 1 illustrates the treatment pathway, describing the pattern of NMIBC therapies following the first BCG instillation, as well as the initiation of advanced treatments and occurrences of death during follow-up.

Overall survival

From the main cohort, 788 patients reached the landmark time and were included in the survival analysis. At the data cut-off, 41.1% of these patients had died. The median overall survival was 92.1 months (95% CI: 84.5–103.6). The estimated five-year survival probability was higher for BCG-maintenance adherent patients (70.8% [95% CI: 66.7%–74.5%]) compared to non-adherent patients (63.8% [95% CI: 56.5%–70.2%]) (Fig. 2a). The adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) suggested a lower risk of death for BCG-maintenance adherent patients compared to non-adherent patients (aHR = 0.92 [95% CI: 0.73–1.18], p = 0.526).

Advanced treatment & real-world disease progression

Among patients who reached the landmark time, 171 (21.7%) received advanced treatment. The probability of remaining free from advanced treatment one year after the first BCG instillation was higher in BCG-maintenance adherent patients (94.7% [95% CI: 92.5%–96.2%]) compared to non-adherent patients (82.0% [95% CI: 76.0%–86.6%]) (Fig. 2b). BCG-maintenance adherent patients had a 51% lower risk of progressing to advanced treatment than non-adherent patients (BCG-maintenance-adherent aHR = 0.49 [95% CI: 0.36–0.67], p < 0.001). More than half of the cohort that reached the landmark time (52.4%) experienced at least one RWDP event. The probability of remaining free from disease progression after one year was 93.1% [95% CI: 90.7%–94.9%] for BCG-maintenance adherent patients and 77.2% [95% CI: 70.8%–82.3%] for non-adherent patients (Fig. 2c). BCG-maintenance adherent patients had a 35% lower risk of disease progression than non-adherent patients (aHR = 0.65 [95% CI: 0.53–0.80], p < 0.001).

Discussion

Worldwide, the age-standardized incidence rate of BC is 6.35 per 100,000 persons (95% CI: 5.80–6.85) [20]. The incidence is considerably higher in males (10.92 [95% CI: 10.04–11.91]) than in females (2.64 [95% CI: 2.34–2.89]) [20]. Rates are higher in regions with a higher socio-demographic index, with the highest occurrences reported in Europe and Northern America [1, 3, 20]. These differences may be partially explained by stronger healthcare systems in these regions, which facilitate cancer screening and detection. Other contributing factors include tobacco smoking [21], occupational exposures (e.g., aromatic amines and ionizing radiation), and other environmental risks common in industrialized countries [22]. Additionally, variations in coding and registration practices, particularly regarding the inclusion of noninvasive carcinomas, may also influence reported incidence rates [23]. In Germany, the age-standardized incidence rate per 100,000 persons for BC in 2022 was 17.0 in males and 5.1 per 100,000 in females [24]. As our study estimates the incidence in intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC patients, these estimates are not directly comparable. It should also be considered that, due to the treatment proxy applied to identify patients, untreated cases without BCG were not included in our analysis.

Clinical guidelines consistently recommend BCG for high-risk patients, with an induction phase followed by maintenance therapy [6, 25, 26]. The German guidelines recommend that if early cystectomy is not indicated for patients with high-risk BC, BCG instillation therapy should be administered. In cases of complete remission after the induction phase, maintenance therapy should be given for a minimum of 1 year and a maximum of 3 years [7, 9]. Our findings show that intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC patients are not receiving optimal BCG maintenance therapy, with approximately 45% of patients continuing BCG at 12 months post-induction, and 28% continuing at 24 months post-induction. Upon analyzing the number of patients receiving BCG, we also observed a decline in instillations over time (Fig. 1) and noted that the recommended schedule was not followed. In this context, our results underline previous findings from international studies in clinical practice [27, 28]. BCG was the main therapy for disease management after receiving first-line BCG treatment, with minimal switching between NMIBC treatments. This aligns with a recent study, which indicates that only 2.5% of patients switch to a different intravesical therapy, either alone or in combination with BCG [27]. A global real-world study also demonstrated that BCG remains the most frequently prescribed treatment for high-risk NMIBC [29]. The median number of BCG instillations and treatment duration in Germany were higher than in previous studies [27, 28]. A recent real-world study in the U.S. (2015–2022) using claims data and electronic health records reported a median of 7 BCG instillations (IQR: 5–14) and a median treatment duration of 156 days (IQR: 36–489) [28]. Another study showed that more than three-quarters of patients treated with BCG in first line therapy discontinued treatment without a subsequent line of therapy [27]. The longer median treatment duration in Germany (352 days), compared to other reports in the literature from the U.S. (156 days), may partially explain the high proportion of adherent patients in our study. Most patients in our main cohort were BCG-adherent during both the induction and maintenance phases, based on the definition proposed by Williams et al. [15]. In contrast, studies from the U.S. have reported lower BCG adherence, particularly during the maintenance phase, with adherence ranging from approximately 42% to 48% using the same definition [27, 28, 30]. Although the definition of BCG adherence is a valid and reliable approach, it does not fully reflect the duration of the maintenance phase recommended in clinical guidelines. The definition of BCG adherence assessment includes a period of nine months and at least seven BCG instillations, including induction, while the recommended maintenance phase for high-risk patients with NMIBC is one to three years. A detailed analysis of treatment patterns over time revealed that even patients classified as BCG maintenance-adherent often exhibited partial compliance with the recommended treatment schedule and duration of maintenance therapy. This is evident from the fact that only 28% of the main cohort received BCG instillations at two-years post-induction. Reports from the U.S. indicate even lower adherence, with only 0.3% of patients completing two years of maintenance therapy [27]. A systematic literature review also found that approximately 60% did not receive adequate BCG treatment and only 28% of high-risk patients completed at least one year of maintenance therapy [31].

Multiple factors may contribute to inadequate BCG adherence, including poor patient compliance, health status, and urologist decisions [32]. Furthermore, BCG intolerance and resistance are important factors to consider in BCG adherence. A systematic literature review identified urinary adverse events, such as frequent and painful urination and hematuria, as frequent reasons for BCG discontinuation [33]. Another study reported that approximately 60% of patients declined BCG due to mild discomfort from persistent grade 1 toxicity that was not adequately managed [34]. Note that our study was not designed to assess reasons for treatment discontinuation, as these are not captured in German claims data. Understanding the factors leading to BCG discontinuation is crucial for improving adherence, and we encourage further research to explore this important issue.

There is a significant risk of residual disease after initial TURBT in patients with high-risk NMIBC [35]. Studies have demonstrated that a second TURBT improves the detection of residual tumors and has the potential to prevent disease recurrence [36, 37]. A second resection is recommended to be conducted 4 to 6 weeks after the initial TURBT under specific circumstances [8, 9, 38]. We reported 62% of NMIBC patients in our main cohort receiving Re-TURBT during the follow-up.

One of the most important findings in our study was the positive effectiveness of BCG adherence on disease progression. This effect has also been reported in the literature, demonstrating a longer time to radical cystectomy, delayed need for systemic chemotherapy, and extended time to metastasis in NMIBC patients receiving adequate BCG therapy [30, 39]. Adherence to BCG during the maintenance phase has particularly shown strong evidence of benefits [40]. Given that a significant proportion of NMIBC patients receive partial BCG treatment in terms of treatment schedule and duration, our results highlight the need for interventions to improve adherence and promote compliance with clinical guidelines.

Further research should investigate additional questions, such as the impact of varying maintenance durations, by collecting detailed data on treatment discontinuation and clinical indicators of disease progression. Moreover, propensity score methods could be considered in future clinical studies to validate the comparison between adherent and non-adherent patients.

Strengths & limitations

One of the main strengths of our study is the long observational period, which allowed for patient identification and provided adequate follow-up to record events over time. This was achieved using a well-structured real-world database that captures inpatient and outpatient diagnoses, as well as a complete record of prescriptions. Additionally, several research studies have shown that the demographic and comorbidity profiles of patients insured by AOK PLUS are comparable to those of the national population covered by other German sickness funds [41–43].

Our study has limitations common to claims data. First, clinical characteristics are not available. This limited our ability to differentiate tumor stage, grade, size and disease states. However, proxies were used to identify incident NMIBC patients by excluding those with diagnoses or treatments indicative of advanced disease, such as metastasis, radiotherapy, cystectomy, or systemic therapy, thereby distinguishing them from patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) or metastatic bladder cancer (mBC). The classification of intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC was based on observed prescription patterns. Disease progression during follow-up was also assessed using proxies, including death, radical cystectomy, metastasis, or the administration of advanced therapies. The use of proxy variables such as advanced treatment, metastasis, and death to define disease progression in a real-world study is widely accepted as established in numerous publications [41, 44–49]. Second, the main cohort in our study is likely to consist predominantly of intermediate- and high-risk patients, as these patients receive BCG treatment according to clinical guidelines [7], however, the uncertainty on the risk categorization cannot be completely ruled out. Third, there is difficulty in differentiating between BCG-unresponsive patients and those who are non-adherent to the treatment. Fourth, since we identified patients using the ICD-10 code for BC in conjunction with BCG treatment, a BCG shortage from November 2019 to October 2022, may have impacted both the epidemiological estimates and the formation of cohorts during those periods. However, we did not observe any relevant changes in epidemiology or BCG adherence during this time period. Fifth, due to the treatment proxy used to identify patients, those who undergo primary cystectomy without receiving BCG will not be identified. However, we expect this to involve only a limited number of patients, as primary cystectomy is typically recommended only for very high-risk NMIBC. Sixth, claims databases, which are based on routine practice data, may contain missing information and coding imperfections regarding diagnoses. Despite potential limitations, claims data remain a reliable source, particularly for diseases like cancer, as all reimbursed treatments are recorded. Seventh, it should be noted that claims data capture prescriptions rather than actual instillations. Therefore, our study used BCG prescriptions as a proxy for instillations. However, we cannot confirm whether patients received the instillations or whether the prescription date accurately reflects the instillation date. This approach described a conservative estimate of non-adherence relative to actual practice. The use of prescription data to assess treatment utilization, despite its limitations, has been evaluated and supported in the literature [50–52]. Several real-world studies have applied a similar approach to assess treatment adherence and persistence [53–55]. Lastly, regional bias cannot be completely ruled out. However, due to uniform healthcare regulations in Germany, treatment of AOK PLUS patients is not expected to differ significantly from other regions.

Despite their limitations, the FDA [56] and EMA [57] increasingly recognize real-world evidence as an important source of evidence to complement other types of observational studies and clinical trials.

Conclusion

Our real-world study provides valuable insights into epidemiology, treatment management, and disease progression of intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC patients. Compliance with clinical guidelines may require improvement, as demonstrated by BCG maintenance duration and treatment schedules. Our findings indicate a potential benefit of BCG adherence, especially in relation to oncology-relevant outcomes. This highlights the critical importance of sufficient BCG maintenance and compliance with clinical guidelines to ensure optimal patient outcomes.
