Net zero emission MR imaging using a permanent 0.4 T magnet
Authors: Hans-Martin Klein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesRadiology, particularly superconducting MRI, accounts for a significant share of healthcare energy consumption. We aimed to achieve zero-emission MR imaging by combining a permanent-magnet MRI with a solar energy system.Material/methodsWe installed a 30-kW peak rooftop solar array on an energy-efficient building. We used a permanent-magnet MRI system with a 0.4 T field strength, 25 mT gradients, and a 55 T/m/s slew rate. We installed a 22 kWh LiFePO4 battery for short-term energy storage. A smart consumer controller routed solar energy as the primary source. Surplus solar energy was fed into the power grid. If solar production was too low, energy was purchased from the grid. In the event of a grid failure with no available solar energy, a high-voltage DC generator would supply grid energy. Energy consumption and solar energy production were analyzed from January 2024 to December 2025.ResultsTotal energy consumption in 2025 was 22,992 kWh. Total energy production in 2025 was 30,823 kWh. Total grid feed in 2025 was 16,364 kWh. Total energy retrieved from the grid in 2025 was 8948 kWh. From May to August 2025, the practice operated grid independent for more than 94% of the time. The practice’s energy balance was positive (negative CO2 emissions) at 7416 kWh (− 2692 kg CO2).ConclusionA permanent-magnet MRI, combined with a solar power system, enabled grid-independent operation with net negative CO2 emissions.

Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging is a major driver of energy consumption in healthcare. Heye and coworkers [1] found that CT and MRI accounted for about 4% of hospital energy consumption. The total annual energy uptake of a 1.5 T MRI was determined to be 241,508 kWh. A main reason for this high energy demand is the need for permanent cooling of the cryomagnet [2]. There have been attempts to improve the sustainability of superconducting magnets by recovering waste heat [3, 4].

With advances in signal and image processing and the growing use of deep learning-based image enhancement [5], low-field MRI systems experienced renewed interest [6]. High-performance low-field MRI has been developed, offering new opportunities in clinical whole-body MRI [7]. This justifies the use of a low-field permanent-magnet MRI system as an alternative, sustainable MRI technology [8].

There are three sources of energy: fossil (oil and gas), nuclear (fission), and renewable (wind, solar, and waste-to-energy). Fossil energy sources are limited; their use produces carbon dioxide. Nuclear fission produces radioactive waste. To improve sustainability, renewable energy sources are preferable. Wind energy is highly variable, and installing wind turbines is difficult in urban areas. Solar energy arrays are easy to install and offer a reliable energy source during daylight hours.

In this study, we sought to integrate permanent magnet technology with an on-site solar system to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, minimize energy consumption, and enhance sustainability in MRI.

Materials and methods

All procedures and materials were commercially available, and installation was carried out in close cooperation between the radiologist and local craftsmen and technicians. No custom engineering or production was involved.

Building

The standalone building is a standard energy-saving construction, adapted to the room requirements of the imaging site (Fingerhut-Haus GmbH, Germany). Heat transmission loss for building parts with an opaque structure was 0.182 W/m2K, and for a transparent structure it was 0.85 W/m2K. Net surface area was 240 m2. For heating, an air–water inverter with an energy uptake of 6.7 kW (Vitocal 200, Viessmann, Germany) was used. For air-conditioning of the MR technique room, a 10-kW inverter was installed. For air-conditioning of the remaining rooms, a 15-kW cooling unit (Toshiba, Germany) was installed.

RIS/PACS-components

We used six client computers (Pavilion 27-d1609nz, Hewlett-Packard, Germany), one RIS server, and one PACS server (Dell PowerEdge R440, Dell Inc., Germany). For CD production, we used an Epson Disc producer PP-100 III (Epson Europe Electronics GmbH, Germany). All monitors and computers were set to energy-saving mode: Monitors switched to standby after 10 min of inactivity, and computers after 30 min.

MRI

We used an air-cooled 0.4 T permanent magnet (Aperto Lucent Plus, Fujifilm Europe GmbH, Germany). System weight was 14.8 t. Gradient strength was 25 mT/m per axis, and slew rate was 55 T/m/s. The connection power was 9.5 kVA. Image processing included sparse k-space filling and iterative reconstruction (IP-rapid). Simultaneous dual-slice acquisition was possible. Additional AI-based image enhancement was applied (SwiftMR, AIRS Medical (Schaumburg, IL)).

We examined a total of 3.521 patients in 2025. The average workload was 16 patients/day. The majority of indications were musculoskeletal, followed by craniospinal examinations. Abdominal and pelvic examinations were rare. Breast or prostate examinations were only performed in cases with contraindications to strong magnetic fields.

Solar system

The rooftop area allowed the installation of a 180 m2-solar array with 106 modules each with an individual capacity of 280 W peak (PolySol 280 W peak, SMA Solar Technology AG, Germany). Modules were oriented southward and tilted 5° to improve solar efficiency. The array’s total peak performance was 29.8 kW peak. The array was divided into two partitions: 21.2 kW peak of solar array capacity was connected to a 25-kW inverter (Sunny Tripower STP 25.000, SMA Solar Technology AG, Germany). 8.6 kW peak of solar array capacity was connected via a manual switch to a 10-kW inverter (Fenecon Energy Management Systems, Fenecon GmbH, Germany). We installed a lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) battery with a net capacity of 22 kWh (Fenecon Energy Systems, Germany). The system provides 10 kW of three-phase power.

Grid-independent operation (‘island solution’) was managed by a smart consumer controller (ACC, Fenecon Germany). The primary energy source was solar. If there was surplus solar energy, it was fed into the power grid. If solar energy was insufficient, power was drawn from the grid. If there was insufficient solar energy and the grid was down, the battery served as an intermittent power source for up to 4 h.

To provide electrical power during extended periods of insufficient solar energy and during a power grid failure, we used a 1-cylinder direct-current (DC) diesel generator simulating the energy profile of a solar array. The voltage was 400–600 V, and the maximum power output was 5 kW (DM EPC 2165, DM-Elektrotechnik, Austria). The solar array and the generator can be selected with a manual switch (Fig. 1). This configuration allows the use of a small, fuel-efficient generator together with the battery/inverter system to provide a stable AC frequency at sufficient power output (max. 10 kW). Measurement concept

We considered data from 2024 and 2025. The energy measurement system (Fenecon GmbH, Germany) recorded total solar energy production, battery feed, power grid feed, generator current, and total consumption from the solar array, battery, and power grid feed (in kWh). For the separation of the components, power meters (KDK Dornscheidt 1-45AM-13, Germany) were installed. We measured the energy consumption of MRI, room heating and cooling, IT, and other components (lighting, kitchen, waiting room TV, and telephone).

The specific CO2 emission per kWh of electricity in Germany was 363 g CO2eq/kWh [9].

Results

Consumption

Total annual consumption was 23,373 kWh in 2024 and 22,992 kWh in 2025. The highest consumption occurred during the heating period from November to February, and the lowest in May. In July and August, air conditioning increased energy consumption. Between May and August, solar energy production exceeded consumption, resulting in a 58–64% surplus. Between November and January, the rate of solar energy supply (self-sufficiency) ranged from 0 to 27%. Correspondingly, energy retrieval from the power grid peaks in winter, with nearly grid-independent operation in summer (Fig. 2). Building

The average annual energy use for heating was 6320 kWh/year, and for cooling (excluding MRI room air cooling), it was 2371 kWh/year. Other electrical components used 2320 kWh/year. The building’s total average energy use was 11,011 kWh/year, leading to CO2 emissions of 3997 kg/year.

MRI

Average power uptake in the standby state was 0.4 kW. In the operational state, average power uptake was 1.3 kW. During scan procedures, average power uptake was 1.9 kW, with a peak of 6 kW. System cooling consumed an average of 1.2 kW during operation. Since the MRI is air-cooled and has no direct connection to the cooling inverter, there were no measurable differences in cooling energy uptake between operational and scanning status in clinical routine. In 2025, 2864 kWh/year were consumed in standby status, 1388 kWh/year in the operational state without scanning, and 1010 kWh/year during scanning. Total MRI consumption was 5262 kWh. Total energy consumption for MRI room cooling was 1760 kWh. Total system annual consumption in 2025 was 7022 kWh, resulting in an annual CO2 production of 2549 kg.

IT

The total annual energy consumption of RIS- and PACS-components was 4959 kWh in 2025, corresponding to 1800 kg of CO2.

Solar system

Total energy production in 2024 was 27,372 kWh and in 2025 was 30,823 kWh (Fig. 3). In 2025, direct consumption totaled 9586 kWh, accounting for 31% of the energy generated. Battery load was 4873 kWh. The overall self-consumption rate was 47% in 2025. The system achieved 56% self-sufficiency in 2024 and 61% in 2025. From May to September, the self-sufficiency rate exceeded 91%, peaking at 98% in June 2025. Between November and January, it was below 27%, with a minimum of 0% in January 2024 (Fig. 4). Battery loading in 2024 and 2025 was 4663 and 4873 kWh, respectively. Battery unloading was 4399 and 4469 kWh. The difference is due to conversion losses. In 2024 and 2025, 13,734 and 16,364 kWh were fed into the power grid (Fig. 5), respectively, and 10,382 and 8948 kWh were drawn from the grid. Emergency power system

In emergency situations, the operating time is limited to 8 h/day. Power uptake of the practice, including standby energy, was 57.6 kWh/day. The generator has a mean power output of 4.3 kW at a gasoil consumption of 1.9 L/h. During 14 h of generator operation, it produces 60.2 kWh at a gasoil consumption rate of 28 L/day. This situation occurred once during our study on January 1st and 2nd 2025, when a severe cable icing event caused a regional power outage, and no solar energy was available.

Ecological and economical aspects

The energy balance between solar production and total energy consumption in 2024 and 2025 was 3955 kWh and 7831 kWh, respectively. The energy balance between grid feed and grid retrieval was 3352 and 7416 kWh in 2024 and 2025, respectively.

The difference results from conversion and transfer losses during battery loading and unloading (see above). CO2-reduction by the system was 1216 kg in 2024 and 2692 kg in 2025 (Table 1, Fig. 6). At an average energy price of 0.315 €/kWh, the total MRI system energy consumption of 7022 kWh caused costs of 2212 € in 2025. Discussion

We demonstrated that sustainable MR imaging with a net-neutral energy balance is possible. The low-field MRI with a permanent magnet consumed 7022 kWh/year. Room heating and cooling were optimized using an energy-saving building. The 180 m2 rooftop solar array had a maximum output of 29.8 kW peak, generating about 30,000 kWh/year. Grid-independent operation was achieved with a battery energy storage system and smart consumer control. An emergency power supply for the event of a power grid failure could be provided by a specially designed 5 kW DC diesel generator.

Permanent magnets were used in the early phase of MR imaging development. They are alloys of 60–70% iron and 30–32% rare earths, with up to 30% neodymium [10]. Because the neodymium component represents about 30% of the magnet material, a permanent-magnet MRI contains more than 2000 kg of neodymium. 90% of all NdFeB magnets are currently produced in China. The mining and refining of neodymium cause considerable environmental pollution. A more widespread use of permanent magnets, therefore, requires new rare-earth-free magnet materials, which are currently under development [11]. The field strength of permanent-magnet MRI systems is limited to about 0.5 T because the magnet weight increases with field strength.

A new concept for MR imaging has been realized with mobile ultra-low-field systems using permanent magnets combined with high-end signal and image processing. They provide point-of-care MRI, further advancing the sustainability and accessibility of MRI [12].

Superconducting cryomagnets, available since the 1980s, provide a stable, uniform magnetic field. The field strength is not limited by weight and ranges usually from 1 T to over 7 T. Since the 1990s, development has focused on superconducting high-field MRI. Alternative low-field magnet technologies, such as resistive electromagnets and permanent magnets, have fallen out of the market [13]. Conventional superconducting magnets require constant cooling of a reservoir containing 1000 to 2000 L of helium to near 0 K, which is the main reason for high energy consumption in MR imaging [14].

Regarding sustainability, permanent magnets offer advantages. The power consumption of our MRI was only about 3% of that of a superconducting system [1]. It does not require helium for cooling and has a lifespan of up to 100 years.

In 2019, Campbell-Washburn et al. reported on an experimental superconducting low-field MRI at 0.55 T [7]. In 2021, a 0.55 T MRI was commercially launched, featuring advanced signal and image processing, including deep-learning reconstruction. This new generation of high-performance low-field MRI has sparked a series of studies re-evaluating the impact of field strength on image quality [15–18]. Regarding sustainability, these systems use only 0.7 L of liquid helium, cooled by a compressor-driven cryocooler (‘cold head’). The compressor requires about 6–8 kW of continuous power, comparable to that of a high-field system. The compressor is cooled using either a chiller system or a local water supply. Compared to conventional MRI, this concept improves sustainability by avoiding helium loss and uncontrolled emergency shutdowns [19]. A water-cooling system, integrated with the building's heating, enables the use of MRI waste energy as an alternative thermal energy source. We demonstrated this solution in an earlier study, reducing the heating energy consumption of a medical center by 85% [4].

If 0.55 T is considered suitable for clinical imaging, then a 0.4 T permanent magnet system should also be adequate, provided it offers comparable signal and image processing, coil systems, and magnetic field uniformity [15]. However, there are currently no permanent magnet MRI systems available that include a full set of tools for image optimization. Until these systems become available, certain procedures, such as thoraco-abdominal imaging and breast and prostate MRI, should be performed using state-of-the-art tunnel systems.

Wind and solar energy have seen increasing deployment during recent years. Solar energy is produced during daylight hours, when most MRI operations take place. This increases the rate of direct local energy use. With battery storage, we could achieve an average direct energy use rate of 47%. Between May and September of 2025, the practice operated independently of the grid for over 94% of the time.

Grid independence is particularly important in regions with an inadequate power infrastructure [20, 21]. The annual solar power production was determined to be 976 kWh/kWpeak at our location in the middle of Europe at an elevation of 600 m. In places with higher solar activity, this concept should work even better.

The MRI system implemented in our practice has demonstrated a net positive energy balance. The surplus energy generated and fed into the grid during the summer months exceeds the energy drawn from the grid throughout the winter. As a result, the overall greenhouse gas emissions associated with our practice were negative, with a net reduction of 2.69 t of carbon dioxide in 2025. To truly operate without emissions, long-term energy storage is essential. A promising option is to store excess summer solar power as hydrogen or synthetic fuels [22], which can be used when solar production decreases.

Our study shows that permanent magnets could provide a resource-saving alternative to superconducting systems. They could improve access to care in regions with developing infrastructure and power grid instability. A precondition for wider distribution is the availability of rare-earth-free magnet materials, and the implementation of high-end signal and image processing.

Superconducting systems can profit from regenerative technology as well, using solar energy systems and waste heat recovery.
