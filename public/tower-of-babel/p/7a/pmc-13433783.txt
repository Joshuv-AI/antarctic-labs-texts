CBC-derived inflammatory indices predict adverse outcomes in geriatric emergency patients with acute infectious diarrhea: a MIMIC-IV analysis
Authors: Mete Ucdal, Karya Yurtsever, Evren Ekingen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute infectious diarrhea remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Similar to pediatric populations, geriatric patients represent a highly vulnerable group with increased hospitalization rates, prolonged length of stay, heightened dehydration susceptibility, diminished physiological reserve, and elevated mortality risk. While pediatric populations benefit from well-established clinical severity scores such as Vesikari and Clark scales, analogous prognostic tools specifically validated for geriatric patients with acute infectious diarrhea remain lacking. Specific inflammatory markers such as procalcitonin and C-reactive protein require additional testing and increase costs. Complete blood count (CBC)-derived inflammatory indices offer a practical alternative as they are inexpensive, universally available, and calculated from routine tests. This retrospective study utilized the MIMIC-IV database (2008–2019) to assess diagnostic accuracy of nine CBC-derived inflammatory indices (AISI, NPAR, HALP, NLR, SII, PLR, MLR, SIRI, PNI) for predicting adverse outcomes in geriatric patients (≥ 65 years) with acute infectious diarrhea. Patients were identified through two independent database queries using ICD-9 codes (008–009 infectious diarrhea, 003–005 bacterial/foodborne; n = 2,208 raw records) and ICD-10 codes (A02–A05 bacterial/foodborne, A08 viral enteritis, A09 infectious gastroenteritis unspecified; n = 1,868 raw records), which yielded overlapping results due to MIMIC-IV spanning both coding eras (2008–2012 ICD-9; 2012–2019 ICD-10); after cross-encounter deduplication retaining one index admission per patient, the non-redundant cohort comprised 3,934 patients. Exclusions (n = 1,087) comprised non-infectious gastroenteritis, inflammatory bowel disease, radiation enteritis, ischemic colitis, drug-induced diarrhea, immunosuppressed patients, missing laboratory data, and C. difficile recurrence. Among 2,847 immunocompetent geriatric patients, comorbidities included hypertension (32.8%), diabetes mellitus (18.6%), and chronic kidney disease (10.4%). Bacterial pathogens were identified in 276 (9.7%) and viral in 151 (5.3%; norovirus 64.1%, rotavirus 23.4%, adenovirus 12.5%). Hospital admission occurred in 668 (23.5%), ICU admission in 34 (1.2%), in-hospital mortality in 14 (0.5%), and AKI in 421 (14.8%). Thirty-day mortality, ascertained via Social Security Death Index linkage, occurred in 31 patients (1.1%). Bacterial infections demonstrated significantly elevated indices versus viral: AISI (9,845 vs. 920, more than tenfold higher), NLR (14.8 vs. 1.82, more than eightfold higher; all p < 0.001). For bacterial-viral differentiation, NLR achieved AUC 0.892. In bacterial infections (n = 276), AISI demonstrated highest accuracy for AKI (AUC 0.896), ICU (0.882), and mortality (0.868); NPAR showed comparable results (AKI 0.854, ICU 0.872, mortality 0.848). In viral infections (n = 151), indices showed moderate performance: AISI for AKI (AUC 0.782), ICU (0.768), mortality (0.754); NPAR for AKI (0.764), ICU (0.756), mortality (0.742). HALP achieved strong mortality prediction in both groups (bacterial 0.874, viral 0.768). In diabetic patients (n = 530), AISI achieved strong discriminatory performance (AUC 0.908 for AKI) with higher complications (AKI 26.8% vs. 12.1%; p < 0.001). CKD patients (n = 296) showed highest complications (AKI 38.6%, mortality 2.1%). Multivariable analysis confirmed AISI (aOR 1.052), NPAR (aOR 1.046), and HALP (aOR 0.91) as independent predictors (all p < 0.001). Among nine CBC-derived indices evaluated in geriatric patients with acute infectious diarrhea, AISI, NPAR, and HALP demonstrate superior diagnostic value for predicting AKI, ICU admission, and mortality, with higher accuracy in bacterial compared to viral infections. These cost-effective biomarkers may address the current gap in geriatric-specific prognostic tools; however, pathogen-stratified AUC values pertain exclusively to microbiologically confirmed cases and represent upper-bound estimates requiring prospective validation with multiplex PCR-based pathogen ascertainment. Introduction

Acute infectious diarrhea continues to pose a considerable global health challenge, constituting the second most prevalent cause of illness worldwide, with 467 million new cases documented in 20211. Although global mortality has declined over recent decades—with diarrhoeal diseases estimated to have caused 1.17 million deaths worldwide in 2021—the absolute burden remains substantial, and age-specific data indicate that adults aged 70 years and above experienced mortality rates that were higher than those of young children, underscoring the continuing impact on older populations2. This persistent high mortality in geriatric cohorts reflects age-related immune senescence, a higher prevalence of chronic comorbidities, and reduced physiological reserve, which collectively increase vulnerability to severe dehydration and complications from diarrheal illness relative to younger age groups2. The global health community has extensively focused on diarrheal disease in children, leading to numerous studies and the development of effective clinical severity scores such as the Vesikari and Clark scales3. In contrast, geriatric patients represent an equally vulnerable yet significantly underappreciated population with distinct pathophysiological vulnerabilities.

Older adults face substantially higher morbidity and mortality from acute infectious diarrhea compared to younger populations1. Age-related physiological changes including diminished renal concentrating ability, reduced thirst perception, decreased total body water, and blunted cardiovascular responses to hypovolemia predispose elderly patients to rapid dehydration and hemodynamic compromise4. Furthermore, immunosenescence impairs pathogen clearance, while comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease amplify the risk of adverse outcomes5–7. Epidemiological data demonstrate that diarrhea-related mortality disproportionately affects adults over 65 years, with case-fatality rates substantially exceeding those in younger cohorts1,8. Despite this vulnerability, validated prognostic tools specifically designed for geriatric patients with acute infectious diarrhea remain notably absent from clinical practice.

Early identification of high-risk geriatric patients presenting to emergency departments with acute infectious diarrhea is crucial for optimizing resource allocation, guiding disposition decisions, and initiating timely interventions. Traditional inflammatory biomarkers such as procalcitonin and C-reactive protein, while valuable, require additional laboratory testing, increase healthcare costs, and may not be readily available in resource-limited settings or during high patient volumes9. Complete blood count (CBC)-derived inflammatory indices have emerged as practical alternatives, offering several advantages: they are inexpensive, universally available, rapidly obtainable, and calculated from routine laboratory tests without additional cost10,11.

Recent advances have identified multiple CBC-derived indices with prognostic utility across various disease states. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) reflects the balance between innate and adaptive immunity and has demonstrated predictive value for mortality in infectious diseases, cardiovascular events, and critical illness12,13. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), incorporating platelet counts alongside neutrophil and lymphocyte values, provides a more comprehensive assessment of systemic inflammation14,15. More recently, composite indices including the aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI), neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR), and systemic inflammation response index16 have shown superior prognostic performance by integrating multiple inflammatory and nutritional parameters17–19. The AISI, calculated as neutrophil × monocyte × platelet/lymphocyte, captures broader inflammatory dynamics, while NPAR incorporates albumin levels to reflect both inflammation and nutritional status—factors particularly relevant in elderly patients19.

The hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet score represents another novel biomarker that simultaneously evaluates nutritional status, immune function, and inflammatory response20. Originally developed for oncological prognostication, HALP has demonstrated predictive value for mortality in various conditions affecting elderly populations, including hip fractures, cardiovascular disease, and critical illness20–23. Lower HALP scores reflect malnutrition, immunosuppression, and increased systemic inflammatory burden, all of which are frequently observed in geriatric patients presenting with acute infections. The prognostic value of HALP and other complete blood count–derived inflammatory indices for predicting adverse outcomes in elderly patients with acute infectious diarrhea has not yet been systematically evaluated.

Given the absence of validated geriatric-specific prognostic tools for acute infectious diarrhea and the practical advantages of CBC-derived indices, we conducted this study to assess the diagnostic accuracy of CBC-derived inflammatory indices (AISI, NPAR, HALP, NLR, SII, PLR, MLR, SIRI, and PNI) for predicting hospital admission, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, acute kidney injury (AKI), and mortality in geriatric patients presenting to the emergency department with acute infectious diarrhea.We hypothesized that these readily available biomarkers could serve as practical prognostic tools analogous to pediatric severity scores, addressing the current gap in age-specific risk stratification systems for this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, version 2.2. MIMIC-IV is a large, publicly available, de-identified database comprising comprehensive clinical data from patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, a tertiary academic medical center in Boston, Massachusetts, United States. The database contains detailed information on patient demographics, diagnoses, laboratory results, vital signs, medications, procedures, and clinical outcomes for admissions between 2008 and 201924. Data were extracted using Structured Query Language from the Google BigQuery platform. The study protocol adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies. The overall study design, patient selection process, subgroup classifications, and outcome assessments are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Ethical considerations

De-identified patient datasets were acquired from the publicly accessible MIMIC-IV database via PhysioNet under the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) “Data or Specimens Only Research” credential (Record ID: 64600981) and the corresponding PhysioNet Data Use Agreement, with no direct patient contact at any stage. The study was reviewed in two stages by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University Yenimahalle Training and Research Hospital: the descriptive phase received a formal exemption letter (Decision No. E-2025-04, January 2025) confirming that ethics-committee approval was not required for purely descriptive analysis of the de-identified dataset, and the post-hoc analytical expansion comprising the nine CBC-derived inflammatory indices, multivariable logistic regression with Firth-penalised estimation, LASSO variable selection, and the composite severe-adverse-outcome analysis was subsequently approved by the same committee under Decision No. E-2025-36 (27 November 2025), in compliance with Turkish national research ethics regulations (Regulation on Clinical Research, revised 2014 and subsequent amendments) which require ethics-committee approval for any expansion of a secondary-analysis plan prior to publication. All analyses reported in this manuscript were conducted on the retrospective de-identified dataset under the authority of this second approval and prior to manuscript submission.

Study population

Geriatric patients aged 65 years or older presenting to the emergency department with a primary or secondary diagnosis of acute infectious diarrhea or gastroenteritis were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) and Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes. From the MIMIC-IV database, we initially identified 5,021 geriatric patients with diarrhea-related diagnostic codes between 2008 and 2019. Inclusion criteria comprised infectious diarrhea codes: ICD-9 codes 008–009 for infectious diarrhea and 003–005 for bacterial and foodborne infections, as well as ICD-10 codes A02-A05 for bacterial intestinal infections, A08 for viral enteritis including rotavirus (A08.0), norovirus (A08.1), and adenovirus (A08.2), and A09 for infectious gastroenteritis unspecified. After applying these inclusion criteria, 3,934 patients representing 5,075 hospital admissions met the initial eligibility requirements.

A total of 1,087 patients were subsequently excluded based on predefined criteria. Non-infectious diarrhea diagnoses accounted for 647 exclusions, including non-infectious gastroenteritis (ICD-10: K52.9, K52.89) and irritable bowel syndrome (ICD-10: K58.0-K58.9). Inflammatory bowel disease, comprising Crohn’s disease (ICD-10: K50.0-K50.9) and ulcerative colitis (ICD-10: K51.0-K51.9), resulted in 205 exclusions. Other gastrointestinal pathology including radiation enteritis (ICD-10: K52.0), ischemic colitis (ICD-10: K55.0-K55.9), and drug-induced diarrhea (ICD-10: K52.1) led to 304 exclusions. Immunosuppressed patients were excluded due to the known attenuation of inflammatory responses in this population, which would confound the assessment of complete blood count-derived indices. This category included patients with active malignancy (ICD-10: C00-C96), solid organ transplant recipients (ICD-10: Z94.0-Z94.9), and hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients (ICD-10: Z94.84), totaling 168 exclusions. Missing laboratory data essential for index calculation within 24 h of presentation resulted in 141 exclusions, including 89 patients with missing complete blood count data and 52 patients with missing serum albumin measurements. Additional clinical exclusions totaling 88 patients included Clostridioides difficile infection recurrence within 8 weeks, post-surgical diarrhea within 30 days, and tube feeding-related diarrhea to ensure a homogeneous study population with primary infectious etiology. The final study cohort comprised 2,847 immunocompetent geriatric patients with acute infectious diarrhea.All pathogen-stratified diagnostic performance metrics pertain exclusively to the 427 microbiologically confirmed cases and should be interpreted as upper-bound estimates of performance in unselected emergency department populations; CBC-derived inflammatory indices in this study are best understood as biomarkers of host inflammatory response severity rather than etiological classifiers.

Data extraction and variable definitions

Demographic and clinical data extracted from the database included age at admission, sex, race/ethnicity, and marital status. Comorbid conditions were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes and were used to calculate the Charlson Comorbidity Index. Individual comorbidities extracted included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, liver disease, and dementia. Vital signs at presentation including heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, and oxygen saturation were recorded. Clinical presentation variables included duration of symptoms prior to presentation, presence of fever defined as temperature 38.0 °C or higher, presence of bloody diarrhea, and reported frequency of bowel movements.

Laboratory variables and microbiological data

Laboratory values obtained within 24 h of emergency department presentation were extracted, with the first available value used for patients with multiple measurements. Complete blood count parameters included white blood cell count, neutrophil count, neutrophil percentage, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, platelet count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, red cell distribution width, and mean corpuscular volume. Chemistry panel values included serum albumin, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, electrolytes, glucose, and anion gap. Additional inflammatory markers including C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and lactate were extracted when available.

Baseline serum creatinine, used to compute the KDIGO change-from-baseline criterion, was defined as the median outpatient value within the 7 to 365 days preceding the index emergency presentation. Pre-admission outpatient creatinine in MIMIC-IV is captured only when patients have a longitudinal primary care relationship within the same health system; missingness is therefore structural rather than random. For patients without an outpatient baseline, the lowest measured creatinine during admission was used as the surrogate, consistent with the KDIGO 2012 working-group recommendation when MDRD back-calculation cannot be reliably anchored. To assess the potential influence of surrogate baseline use on cohort ascertainment, baseline characteristics of patients with versus without a confirmed outpatient baseline were compared. Patients with a confirmed baseline were more likely to have documented chronic kidney disease and were on average older, a pattern expected because patients with established CKD undergo more frequent outpatient laboratory monitoring; surrogate baseline use may therefore modestly underestimate AKI incidence among patients with previously undocumented CKD, in whom a falsely low admission nadir would attenuate the apparent creatinine excursion. The direction of any resulting misclassification is conservative for the discrimination metrics reported.

Stool culture results were extracted from the microbiology module. Patients were classified based on culture results as bacterial-positive (Clostridioides difficile, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Shigella, pathogenic Escherichia coli, Yersinia enterocolitica, or Vibrio species), viral-positive (norovirus, rotavirus, enteric adenovirus, astrovirus, or sapovirus), mixed infection, or culture-negative/not performed.

Because microbiological confirmation was available for only 427 of 2,847 patients (15.0%), and culture testing in MIMIC-IV is initiated on clinical grounds (severe or persistent symptoms, recent healthcare exposure, immunosuppression), the tested subgroup is enriched for higher-acuity presentations. To characterise this enrichment, baseline characteristics and admission inflammatory index distributions were compared between culture-tested and non-tested patients using Mann-Whitney U or Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables, with results reported in the Sensitivity Analyses section of the Results. Among the 226 culture-positive bacterial cases, 51 (22.4%) had documented systemic antibiotic exposure within the preceding 30 days; we tested whether this exposure modified index-outcome associations by introducing index-by-prior-antibiotic interaction terms in the AKI and composite-outcome multivariable models.

CBC-derived inflammatory indices

Nine inflammatory indices were calculated from the initial laboratory values obtained within 24 h of emergency department presentation. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count, reflecting the balance between innate and adaptive immunity. The platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) was calculated as platelet count divided by lymphocyte count. The monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) was calculated as monocyte count divided by lymphocyte count. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was calculated as platelet count multiplied by neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count, integrating three key cellular components of systemic inflammatory response. The systemic inflammation response index was calculated as neutrophil count multiplied by monocyte count divided by lymphocyte count.

The aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI) was calculated as neutrophil count multiplied by monocyte count multiplied by platelet count divided by lymphocyte count, providing a comprehensive assessment of multiple inflammatory cell types. The neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio (NPAR) was calculated as neutrophil percentage divided by serum albumin, integrating inflammatory status with nutritional status, which is particularly relevant in elderly patients with potential malnutrition. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was calculated as serum albumin multiplied by 10 plus lymphocyte count multiplied by 0.005, with lower values indicating malnutrition and immunosuppression. The hemoglobin-albumin-lymphocyte-platelet score was calculated as hemoglobin multiplied by serum albumin multiplied by lymphocyte count divided by platelet count multiplied by 100, with lower scores indicating anemia, hypoalbuminemia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytosis, all of which are markers of poor nutritional status, impaired immunity, and systemic inflammation.

Primary and secondary outcomes

Primary outcomes included hospital admission defined as admission to an inpatient ward or intensive care unit following emergency department evaluation, intensive care unit (ICU) admission defined as transfer to any intensive care unit during the index hospitalization, in-hospital mortality defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization, and acute kidney injury (AKI) defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria as an increase in serum creatinine by 0.3 mg/dL or more within 48 h, an increase in serum creatinine to 1.5 times baseline or more within 7 days, or urine volume less than 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h. Baseline creatinine was defined as the lowest serum creatinine recorded in the 365 days preceding the index emergency department visit, available for 2,412 patients (84.7% of the cohort). For the remaining 435 patients (15.3%) without prior creatinine data in the MIMIC-IV record, the admission creatinine value was used as the surrogate baseline, consistent with KDIGO guidance for patients with unknown baseline and previously validated in MIMIC-IV-based AKI identification algorithms25. A sensitivity analysis restricting AKI ascertainment to patients with confirmed baseline creatinine (n = 2,412) yielded identical AISI AUC (0.844; 95% CI: 0.824–0.864) and comparable event rate (14.2% vs. 14.8% in full cohort), confirming that surrogate baseline use in the 15.3% subset does not materially affect findings.Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay, length of ICU stay, mechanical ventilation requirement, vasopressor requirement, and 30-day mortality ascertained from Social Security Death Index linkage in MIMIC-IV.

Subgroup definitions

Subgroup analyses were performed based on predefined categories. Pathogen type subgroups included bacterial infection, viral infection, and culture-negative cases. Comorbidity subgroups included diabetic patients identified by ICD codes E10-E14 or 250, hypertensive patients identified by ICD codes I10-I16 or 401–405, and chronic kidney disease patients identified by ICD codes N18 or 585. Age subgroups included young-old (65–74 years), middle-old (75–84 years), and oldest-old (85 years or older). Frailty status was assessed using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score calculated from ICD codes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2 and Python version 3.10. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile range and compared using Mann-Whitney U tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate.

Receiver operating characteristic curves were constructed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of each inflammatory index for predicting primary outcomes. The area under the curve with 95% confidence intervals was calculated using the DeLong method. Pairwise comparisons of area under the curve values between different indices were performed using the DeLong test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Optimal cutoff values for each inflammatory index were determined using the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and likelihood ratios were calculated at optimal cutoff points.

Univariable logistic regression was performed to identify potential predictors of each primary outcome. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis were included in multivariable logistic regression models. Multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, individual comorbidities, baseline serum creatinine, and presenting vital signs. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and model discrimination was evaluated using the C-statistic. For mortality outcomes with longer follow-up, Cox proportional hazards regression was performed, and hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported.

Subgroup analyses were performed for each predefined subgroup, and interaction terms between inflammatory indices and subgroup variables were included in regression models to test for effect modification. Sensitivity analyses included complete case analysis, analysis restricted to culture-confirmed cases only, analysis excluding patients with Clostridioides difficile infection, and multiple imputation for missing data. A two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all multivariable logistic regression models and pre-specified interaction tests. Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied specifically and exclusively to pairwise DeLong test comparisons of AUC values between different inflammatory indices within each primary outcome. For nine inflammatory indices, the total number of possible pairwise AUC comparisons within a single outcome is C2,9= 36, yielding a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold of p < 0.05/36 = 0.00139, herein reported as p < 0.001. All pairwise AUC comparisons described as statistically significant in the Results section meet this corrected threshold. Bonferroni correction was not applied to multivariable logistic regression models because each model incorporated a single inflammatory index by design, with one index entered per model across the same clinical covariate set, thereby precluding cross-index multiple comparison issues within the regression framework. Pre-specified interaction terms testing effect modification by pathogen type, comorbidity status, and age group were evaluated at the uncorrected p < 0.05 threshold in accordance with the pre-specified nature of these hypothesis tests. The LASSO logistic regression was applied as a single simultaneous model entering all nine inflammatory indices alongside the full clinical covariate set, with the AKI prediction outcome pre-specified as the LASSO target outcome on the basis of its superior statistical power (n = 421 events; EPV = 52.6). Separate LASSO models were not fitted for ICU admission or in-hospital mortality given insufficient event counts for reliable cross-validated LASSO estimation in these outcomes (EPV 4.25 and 1.75, respectively).

For AKI and hospital admission outcomes, where the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio exceeded 50 (EPV = 52.6 and 83.5, respectively), standard maximum likelihood logistic regression was employed. For ICU admission (n = 34 events; EPV = 4.25) and in-hospital mortality (n = 14 events; EPV = 1.75), Firth’s penalized logistic regression was applied using the ‘logistf’ package (v1.26, R), which incorporates a first-order Jeffreys prior penalty term into the log-likelihood to reduce small-sample bias and prevent quasi-complete separation. Penalized estimates with profile likelihood confidence intervals are reported for these two outcomes. Post hoc power analysis (R ‘pwr’ package) confirmed greater than 95% statistical power for AKI and hospital admission models at the observed effect sizes and α = 0.05. As a pre-specified complementary analysis to increase power for rare outcomes, a composite ‘severe adverse outcome’ endpoint was defined as any of: ICU admission, in-hospital mortality, or AKI requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT); this composite event occurred in 62 patients (2.2%), yielding EPV = 7.75.

To address the inherent mathematical correlation among several CBC-derived indices, particularly those sharing neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet count components (AISI, SII, SIRI, NLR), each inflammatory index was incorporated into a separate, independent multivariable logistic regression model (one index per model), rather than entering all nine indices simultaneously into a single model. This by-design strategy prevents cross-index multicollinearity while allowing evaluation of each index’s independent predictive value after adjustment for the same set of clinical covariates (age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, diabetes mellitus, CKD, congestive heart failure, bacterial infection status, fever at presentation, and baseline serum creatinine). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were additionally calculated for each inflammatory index within the context of the full covariate matrix to quantify collinearity with the clinical predictors; all VIF values were below 5.0, confirming acceptable collinearity levels (Supplementary Table S2), consistent with established thresholds for multivariable regression stability. As a complementary data-driven variable selection approach, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation (lambda = lambda.min) was applied to the AKI prediction model, the outcome with the highest event rate and statistical power, to identify the most parsimonious set of non-redundant inflammatory predictors. The LASSO penalty simultaneously performs variable selection and coefficient shrinkage, providing an unbiased empirical test of which indices contribute independent predictive information.

The primary analysis was conducted on complete cases (n = 2,847) after excluding 141 patients (4.7% of the 2,988 potentially eligible patients) with missing laboratory values required for inflammatory index calculation. This low missingness rate (below the 10–15% threshold commonly recommended as a trigger for primary imputation-based analysis) and the biological plausibility of a non-random missingness mechanism — albumin measurement more likely to be omitted in less severely ill patients undergoing rapid discharge assessment — justified complete case analysis as the primary approach. As a pre-specified sensitivity analysis, multiple imputation was performed using predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic imputation for binary variables, generating 5 imputed datasets with 20 iterations each. The pooled estimates from the imputed datasets were virtually identical to the complete case results (pooled AISI aOR for AKI: 1.054 [95% CI: 1.044–1.064] vs. complete case: 1.052 [1.042–1.062]; difference < 0.2%), confirming that neither the primary estimates nor their confidence intervals are materially influenced by the chosen missing data strategy.

The median age of the study population was 74.2 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 69.1–81.4 years), with 1,538 patients (54.0%) being female. The age distribution revealed 1,182 patients (41.5%) in the young-old category (65–74 years), 1,024 patients (36.0%) in the middle-old category (75–84 years), and 641 patients (22.5%) in the oldest-old category (≥ 85 years). Regarding racial composition, 2,106 patients (74.0%) were White, 412 patients (14.5%) were Black, 186 patients (6.5%) were Hispanic, and 143 patients (5.0%) were of other racial backgrounds.

Comorbid conditions were prevalent in this geriatric cohort. Hypertension was the most common comorbidity, present in 934 patients (32.8%), followed by diabetes mellitus in 530 patients (18.6%), chronic kidney disease in 296 patients (10.4%), congestive heart failure in 267 patients (9.4%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in 228 patients (8.0%), coronary artery disease in 199 patients (7.0%), cerebrovascular disease in 171 patients (6.0%), peripheral vascular disease in 142 patients (5.0%), liver disease in 114 patients (4.0%), and dementia in 85 patients (3.0%). The median Charlson Comorbidity Index was 3 (IQR: 2–5).

Clinical presentation at emergency department arrival revealed a median symptom duration of 2.4 days (IQR: 1.2–4.1 days). Fever, defined as temperature ≥ 38.0 °C, was present in 456 patients (16.0%). Bloody diarrhea was reported in 228 patients (8.0%). The median reported bowel movement frequency was 6 episodes per day (IQR: 4–9). Vital signs at presentation demonstrated a median heart rate of 92 beats per minute (IQR: 78–108), median systolic blood pressure of 124 mmHg (IQR: 108–142), median respiratory rate of 18 breaths per minute (IQR: 16–22), and median oxygen saturation of 97% (IQR: 95–99%).

Microbiological findings

Stool culture and pathogen identification results stratified the cohort by infectious etiology. Bacterial pathogens were identified in 276 patients (9.7%), viral pathogens in 151 patients (5.3%), and the remaining 2,420 patients (85.0%) were culture-negative or had testing not performed. Among bacterial infections, Clostridioides difficile was the most frequently isolated pathogen (n = 142, 51.4%), followed by Salmonella species (n = 56, 20.3%), Campylobacter species (n = 42, 15.2%), Shigella species (n = 19, 6.9%), pathogenic Escherichia coli (n = 11, 4.0%), and other bacterial pathogens including Yersinia and Vibrio species (n = 6, 2.2%).

Among viral infections (n = 151), norovirus was predominant, identified in 97 patients (64.2%), followed by rotavirus in 35 patients (23.2%), and enteric adenovirus in 19 patients (12.6%). Mixed bacterial-viral coinfection was rare, observed in only 8 patients (0.3%), who were analyzed within the bacterial infection subgroup due to the presumed clinical dominance of bacterial pathogens.

Primary outcomes

Among the 2,847 geriatric patients with acute infectious diarrhea, hospital admission occurred in 668 patients (23.5%), intensive care unit admission in 34 patients (1.2%), in-hospital mortality in 14 patients (0.5%), and acute kidney injury in 421 patients (14.8%). Secondary outcomes revealed a median hospital length of stay of 4.2 days (IQR: 2.1–7.8 days) among admitted patients, median ICU length of stay of 3.1 days (IQR: 1.8–6.4 days) among ICU-admitted patients, mechanical ventilation requirement in 12 patients (0.4%), and vasopressor requirement in 18 patients (0.6%). Thirty-day mortality, ascertained through Social Security Death Index linkage, occurred in 31 patients (1.1%).

Patients who experienced adverse outcomes were significantly older, had higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores, and presented with more severe vital sign abnormalities. Specifically, patients with in-hospital mortality had a median age of 82.6 years compared to 73.9 years in survivors (p < 0.001), and those requiring ICU admission had a median Charlson Comorbidity Index of 5 compared to 3 in non-ICU patients (p < 0.001).

CBC-derived inflammatory indices by pathogen type

Marked differences in inflammatory index values were observed between bacterial and viral infections. In bacterial infections (n = 276), the median AISI was 9,845 (IQR: 4,218–18,642), representing a greater than tenfold elevation compared to the median AISI of 920 (IQR: 412–1,864) in viral infections (n = 151; p < 0.001). Similarly, the median NLR was 14.8 (IQR: 8.2–24.6) in bacterial infections compared to 1.82 (IQR: 1.12–3.24) in viral infections, representing an approximately eightfold difference (p < 0.001).

Other indices demonstrated comparable patterns: SII (bacterial: 2,845 × 10³ vs. viral: 324 × 10³; 8.8-fold; p < 0.001), SIRI (bacterial: 8.42 vs. viral: 1.24; 6.8-fold; p < 0.001), PLR (bacterial: 286 vs. viral: 142; 2.0-fold; p < 0.001), and MLR (bacterial: 0.68 vs. viral: 0.32; 2.1-fold; p < 0.001). Nutritional indices also differed significantly: NPAR (bacterial: 24.8 vs. viral: 16.2; p < 0.001), PNI (bacterial: 38.4 vs. viral: 46.2; p < 0.001), and HALP (bacterial: 18.6 vs. viral: 42.8; p < 0.001). These findings indicate that bacterial infections elicit substantially greater systemic inflammatory responses compared to viral gastroenteritis in geriatric patients.

Diagnostic accuracy for bacterial-viral differentiation

Receiver operating characteristic analysis for differentiating bacterial from viral infections demonstrated that NLR achieved the highest discriminatory performance with an area under the curve of 0.892 (95% CI: 0.864–0.920). The optimal NLR cutoff of 5.8 yielded sensitivity of 84.1%, specificity of 82.8%, positive predictive value of 89.9%, and negative predictive value of 74.0%. AISI demonstrated comparable performance (AUC 0.886; 95% CI: 0.856–0.916), followed by SIRI (AUC 0.874; 95% CI: 0.842–0.906) and SII (AUC 0.868; 95% CI: 0.834–0.902). HALP demonstrated inverse discriminatory value (AUC 0.824; 95% CI: 0.786–0.862), with lower scores indicating bacterial etiology.

DeLong test comparisons revealed no statistically significant difference between NLR and AISI (p = 0.482), but NLR significantly outperformed PLR (AUC 0.724; p < 0.001) and MLR (AUC 0.698; p < 0.001). These results support NLR and AISI as the most reliable indices for distinguishing bacterial from viral gastroenteritis at initial emergency department presentation.

Acute kidney injury prediction

For AKI prediction in the overall cohort (n = 2,847), AISI demonstrated the highest discriminatory performance with an AUC of 0.842 (95% CI: 0.824–0.860), followed by NPAR (AUC 0.826; 95% CI: 0.806–0.846), SII (AUC 0.814; 95% CI: 0.794–0.834), NLR (AUC 0.808; 95% CI: 0.788–0.828), and SIRI (AUC 0.796; 95% CI: 0.774–0.818). HALP showed inverse prediction with an AUC of 0.812 (95% CI: 0.792–0.832), where lower scores indicated higher AKI risk. PNI demonstrated moderate performance (AUC 0.764; 95% CI: 0.742–0.786), while PLR (AUC 0.712; 95% CI: 0.688–0.736) and MLR (AUC 0.698; 95% CI: 0.674–0.722) showed the lowest discriminatory ability.

The optimal AISI cutoff of 5,842 for AKI prediction yielded sensitivity of 78.4%, specificity of 76.2%, positive predictive value of 42.8%, negative predictive value of 93.6%, positive likelihood ratio of 3.29, and negative likelihood ratio of 0.28. DeLong test comparisons confirmed that AISI significantly outperformed PLR (p < 0.001), MLR (p < 0.001), and PNI (p = 0.002), but was not significantly different from NPAR (p = 0.124) or SII (p = 0.086).

ICU admission prediction

For ICU admission prediction (n = 34 events), indices demonstrated strong discriminatory performance. AISI achieved an AUC of 0.868 (95% CI: 0.812–0.924), NPAR 0.854 (95% CI: 0.796–0.912), HALP 0.846 (95% CI: 0.784–0.908), SII 0.838 (95% CI: 0.774–0.902), and NLR 0.824 (95% CI: 0.758–0.890). At the optimal AISI cutoff of 9,624, sensitivity was 82.4%, specificity was 80.6%, and the negative predictive value was 99.4%, indicating excellent ability to rule out ICU admission when AISI was below this threshold.

Mortality prediction

For in-hospital mortality prediction (n = 14 events), despite the limited number of events, indices demonstrated notable discrimination. HALP achieved the highest AUC of 0.862 (95% CI: 0.786–0.938), followed by AISI (AUC 0.848; 95% CI: 0.768–0.928), NPAR (AUC 0.836; 95% CI: 0.754–0.918), and PNI (AUC 0.824; 95% CI: 0.738–0.910). The superior performance of HALP and PNI for mortality prediction reflects the critical role of nutritional status in determining survival outcomes in this geriatric population.

Chronic kidney disease patients (n = 296)

Patients with pre-existing CKD exhibited the highest complication rates: AKI (38.6% vs. 12.1% in non-CKD; p < 0.001), hospital admission (42.9% vs. 21.2%; p < 0.001), ICU admission (3.4% vs. 0.9%; p < 0.001), and mortality (2.1% vs. 0.3%; p < 0.001). Baseline inflammatory indices were significantly elevated in CKD patients: AISI (6,248 vs. 3,842; p < 0.001), NLR (8.4 vs. 5.8; p < 0.001), and NPAR (26.4 vs. 20.2; p < 0.001).

Despite elevated baseline values, inflammatory indices maintained discriminatory ability in CKD patients: AISI for AKI (AUC 0.834; 95% CI: 0.794–0.874), NPAR (AUC 0.818; 95% CI: 0.776–0.860), and HALP (AUC 0.806; 95% CI: 0.762–0.850). Notably, HALP demonstrated particularly strong mortality prediction in CKD patients (AUC 0.856; 95% CI: 0.778–0.934), likely reflecting the heightened importance of nutritional status in this vulnerable subgroup.

Hypertensive patients (n = 934)

Hypertensive patients showed moderately elevated complication rates: AKI (18.4% vs. 13.1%; p < 0.001), hospital admission (27.0% vs. 21.8%; p = 0.002), ICU admission (1.6% vs. 1.0%; p = 0.142), and mortality (0.7% vs. 0.4%; p = 0.248). The discriminatory performance of indices in hypertensive patients was comparable to the overall cohort, with no significant interaction effects observed (p > 0.10 for all indices).

Age-stratified analysis revealed progressively increasing complication rates with advancing age. In the young-old group (65–74 years; n = 1,182), AKI occurred in 10.2%, hospital admission in 18.4%, ICU admission in 0.6%, and mortality in 0.2%. In the middle-old group (75–84 years; n = 1,024), corresponding rates were 15.8%, 25.2%, 1.4%, and 0.5%. In the oldest-old group (≥ 85 years; n = 641), rates were highest: AKI 22.3%, hospital admission 31.4%, ICU admission 2.3%, and mortality 1.1% (p < 0.001 for trend across all outcomes).

Inflammatory indices demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance: AISI for AKI (AUC 0.782; 95% CI: 0.696–0.868; Fig. 3A), ICU (AUC 0.768; 95% CI: 0.624–0.912; Fig. 3B), and mortality (AUC 0.754; 95% CI: 0.586–0.922; Fig. 3C); NPAR for AKI (AUC 0.764; 95% CI: 0.676–0.852; Fig. 3A), ICU (AUC 0.756; 95% CI: 0.608–0.904; Fig. 3B), and mortality (AUC 0.742; 95% CI: 0.572–0.912; Fig. 3C). HALP maintained reasonable mortality prediction (AUC 0.768; 95% CI: 0.602–0.934; Fig. 3C).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression confirmed the independent prognostic value of CBC-derived inflammatory indices after adjustment for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, individual comorbidities, baseline serum creatinine, and presenting vital signs (Table 3).

AISI emerged as the strongest independent predictor among inflammatory indices. Each 1,000-unit increase in AISI was associated with 5.2% increased odds of AKI (adjusted OR [aOR] 1.052; 95% CI: 1.042–1.062; p < 0.001), 6.8% increased odds of ICU admission (aOR 1.068; 95% CI: 1.048–1.088; p < 0.001), 7.4% increased odds of mortality (aOR 1.074; 95% CI: 1.046–1.102; p < 0.001), and 4.6% increased odds of hospital admission (aOR 1.046; 95% CI: 1.038–1.054; p < 0.001).

NPAR demonstrated comparable independent predictive value: each unit increase was associated with 4.6% increased odds of AKI (aOR 1.046; 95% CI: 1.034–1.058; p < 0.001), 5.8% increased odds of ICU admission (aOR 1.058; 95% CI: 1.036–1.080; p < 0.001), and 6.2% increased odds of mortality (aOR 1.062; 95% CI: 1.032–1.092; p < 0.001).

HALP showed inverse association with adverse outcomes, reflecting its protective nutritional component. Each unit increase in HALP was associated with 3.2% decreased odds of AKI (aOR 0.968; 95% CI: 0.958–0.978; p < 0.001), 4.6% decreased odds of ICU admission (aOR 0.954; 95% CI: 0.934–0.974; p < 0.001), and 5.4% decreased odds of mortality (aOR 0.946; 95% CI: 0.918–0.974; p < 0.001).

Among clinical variables, chronic kidney disease conferred the highest risk: aOR 2.42 (95% CI: 1.94–3.02) for AKI, aOR 2.86 (95% CI: 1.62–5.04) for ICU admission, and aOR 3.24 (95% CI: 1.38–7.60) for mortality. Diabetes mellitus also significantly increased risk across all outcomes. Bacterial infection independently predicted adverse outcomes (aOR 2.24 for AKI; 3.42 for ICU; 4.12 for mortality; all p < 0.001).

Model performance was excellent across outcomes. The C-statistic was 0.846 (95% CI: 0.828–0.864) for AKI prediction, 0.872 (95% CI: 0.816–0.928) for ICU admission, 0.886 (95% CI: 0.812–0.960) for mortality, and 0.784 (95% CI: 0.764–0.804) for hospital admission. Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests indicated adequate calibration for all models (p > 0.05).

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of findings. Complete case analysis restricted to patients with all laboratory values available (n = 2,706) yielded similar results: AISI AUC for AKI was 0.844 (95% CI: 0.824–0.864), and multivariable associations remained significant.

Analysis restricted to culture-confirmed cases (n = 427) demonstrated enhanced discriminatory performance: AISI AUC 0.872 for AKI (95% CI: 0.838–0.906), reflecting the greater inflammatory burden in patients with identified pathogens. Analysis excluding Clostridioides difficile infections (n = 2,705) showed comparable results to the primary analysis, with AISI AUC 0.838 (95% CI: 0.818–0.858) for AKI prediction.

A pre-specified sensitivity comparison of the 427 culture-tested patients with the 2,420 non-tested patients confirmed the expected selection pattern: culture-tested patients were modestly older (median age 76.8 vs. 73.5 years; P < 0.001), had higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores (median 4 vs. 3; P < 0.001), presented with greater systemic inflammation (median admission AISI 3,840 vs. 1,420; P < 0.001), and had higher rates of fever and tachycardia at presentation (P < 0.001 for both). Inflammatory index distributions were correspondingly right-shifted in the culture-tested subgroup, consistent with the interpretation that discrimination metrics derived from this subgroup represent upper-bound estimates applicable to culture-indicated presentations. Introducing an index-by-prior-antibiotic exposure interaction term into the multivariable AKI and composite-outcome models did not materially alter the index-outcome associations (P interaction = 0.34, 0.41, and 0.28 for AISI, NPAR, and HALP respectively in the AKI model; corresponding values 0.39, 0.44, and 0.31 in the composite-outcome model), indicating that pretreatment effects on the leukogram and serum albumin within the culture-positive bacterial subgroup were modest at the index level and did not confound the primary multivariable estimates.

Multiple imputation analysis for missing data (5 imputed datasets, 20 iterations) produced consistent estimates: pooled AISI aOR for AKI was 1.054 (95% CI: 1.044–1.064), virtually identical to the complete case estimate. These sensitivity analyses confirm the robustness and generalizability of the primary findings.

Composite outcome analysis

To increase statistical power for the evaluation of rare adverse outcomes, a pre-specified composite endpoint was analysed, defined as the occurrence of any of the following: ICU admission (n = 34), in-hospital mortality (n = 14), or AKI requiring renal replacement therapy (RRT; n = 18). The composite severe adverse outcome occurred in 62 patients (2.2% of the total cohort), with no overlap counted more than once. Among the nine CBC-derived inflammatory indices, AISI demonstrated the highest discriminatory performance for the composite endpoint (AUC 0.876; 95% CI: 0.826–0.926), followed by NPAR (AUC 0.862; 95% CI: 0.810–0.914), HALP (AUC 0.848; 95% CI: 0.792–0.904), NLR (AUC 0.834; 95% CI: 0.778–0.890), and SII (AUC 0.826; 95% CI: 0.768–0.884). In Firth’s penalized logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, individual comorbidities, baseline serum creatinine, and presenting vital signs, AISI independently predicted the composite severe adverse outcome (penalized aOR 1.068; 95% CI: 1.048–1.090; p < 0.001 per 1,000-unit increase), as did NPAR (penalized aOR 1.058; 95% CI: 1.036–1.082; p < 0.001) and HALP (penalized aOR 0.952; 95% CI: 0.930–0.974; p < 0.001; inverse association). These composite outcome findings are consistent with the individual-endpoint results and provide additional statistical support for the prognostic value of AISI, NPAR, and HALP in geriatric patients with acute infectious diarrhea. Full diagnostic performance metrics for the composite endpoint are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

These four outcomes differ materially in statistical power and warrant distinct interpretive conclusions. The AKI model (events per variable greater than 50) and the hospital-admission model are well powered, and we regard the AISI threshold for AKI and the NPAR threshold for hospital admission as candidates for prospective external validation in their current form. The composite severe adverse outcome (62 events, events per variable 7.75) provides intermediate evidence and is the strongest candidate for the operational take-home message, since it pools rare endpoints into a single statistically tractable target and reduces the multiplicity penalty of testing each separately. The in-hospital mortality (14 events) and ICU admission (34 events) models generated promising but underpowered signals; the HALP threshold of 18.6 for mortality and the AISI threshold of 9,624 for ICU admission are reported as benchmarks for prospective validation rather than as clinically actionable cut-offs.

LASSO variable selection

LASSO logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation applied to the AKI prediction model (n= 2,847; 421 events) retained non-zero coefficients for three inflammatory indices: AISI (β = 0.0000421; penalised), NPAR (β = 0.0182), and HALP (β=−0.0128 [inverse association]). The remaining six indices — SII, SIRI, NLR, PLR, MLR, and PNI — were regularised to zero by the LASSO penalty, indicating that their predictive information for AKI is subsumed by the AISI/NPAR/HALP combination within the full covariate matrix. This data-driven result independently validates our primary emphasis on AISI, NPAR, and HALP as the most parsimonious and non-redundant CBC-derived predictors of adverse outcomes in this cohort, and provides formal statistical justification for the biological framework of three complementary pathophysiological axes1: multi-lineage innate immune and platelet activation (AISI)2, inflammation-nutritional interface (NPAR), and3 haemoglobin-lymphocyte-platelet nutritional-immune reserve26. The cross-validated AUC for the LASSO-selected model was 0.838 (95% CI: 0.818–0.858), indistinguishable from the AUC of the individually optimal index (AISI: 0.842), confirming that no additional discriminatory information is gained by the full nine-index panel beyond the three-index parsimonious model.

Given the very limited number of in-hospital mortality events (n = 14; EPV = 1.75 for the full multivariable model), all mortality AUC estimates and adjusted odds ratios derived from Firth’s penalized logistic regression should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The wide confidence intervals, for example an HALP AUC 95% CI of 0.786 to 0.938, accurately reflect the substantial statistical uncertainty inherent in small-event analyses and should be understood as representing the plausible range of the true effect rather than evidence of poor model performance. These mortality-related findings are best considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating, warranting prospective validation in cohorts with higher event rates before clinical implementation of mortality-specific threshold values.

Discussion

This large-scale retrospective analysis of 2,847 immunocompetent geriatric patients from the MIMIC-IV database demonstrates the significant prognostic utility of CBC-derived inflammatory indices for predicting adverse outcomes in acute infectious diarrhea. Among nine indices evaluated, AISI, NPAR, and HALP demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy for predicting AKI, ICU admission, and mortality. Their performance was markedly enhanced in bacterial versus viral etiologies and further potentiated in high-risk subgroups such as patients with diabetes and chronic kidney disease. These findings address a critical gap in geriatric-specific prognostic tools for acute infectious diarrhea and propose readily available, cost-effective biomarkers for risk stratification in emergency department settings.

Although no validated severity score exists specifically for geriatric acute infectious gastroenteritis, several general geriatric risk stratification instruments have been developed for emergency settings. The Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS), derived from ICD codes and validated in large UK hospital cohorts, provides a global frailty-based risk estimate but does not incorporate acute infectious or inflammatory parameters27. The Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) offers rapid clinical frailty assessment at the point of care but is assessor-dependent and requires face-to-face evaluation, limiting its use in high-volume emergency settings. The Identification of Seniors At Risk (ISAR) tool identifies functional decline risk but was not designed for infectious illness severity stratification. The CURB-65 and PSI scores share some conceptual overlap with our approach in using routinely available parameters to guide disposition, but are disease-specific and do not incorporate CBC-derived inflammatory indices. None of these instruments was developed or validated for the specific context of acute infectious gastroenteritis in older adults. Our findings suggest that CBC-derived indices, particularly AISI, NPAR, and HALP, could serve as the biological complement to these functional assessment tools, with combined functional-inflammatory risk stratification potentially offering superior discriminatory performance compared to either domain alone, a hypothesis worthy of prospective evaluation.

Our findings align with the growing recognition of the utility of CBC-derived indices across diverse clinical contexts. Recent discussions have increasingly emphasized the clinical relevance of hematological inflammatory markers, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, systemic immune-inflammation index, systemic inflammation response index, and aggregate index of systemic inflammation, which have gained prominence as readily accessible and cost-effective indicators of systemic inflammatory burden across a wide range of acute and chronic diseases28. Similarly, Zhang et al. demonstrated using NHANES data that these inflammatory indicators, particularly SIRI and AISI, were independently associated with all-cause mortality in arthritis patients with hypertension, highlighting their broad prognostic applicability29. The recent Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 analysis by Zhao et al. (2025) specifically emphasized that diarrheal disease in older adults remains an underappreciated global health issue, with mortality rates showing a concerning upward trend in individuals over 65 years, underscoring the urgent need for validated prognostic tools in this population1.

The distinctive strength of our study lies in its exclusive geriatric focus, which addresses a significant demographic gap. While pediatric acute infectious diarrhea benefits from established severity scores and research on inflammatory markers, analogous tools for older adults have been notably lacking despite their heightened vulnerability. Tang et al. demonstrated that CBC-derived inflammatory markers including NLR, MLR, PLR, SII, SIRI, and pan-immune inflammation value (PIV) were positively associated with both frailty risk and mortality in middle-aged and older adults, with the highest quartiles showing significantly increased mortality risk (HR 1.73 for NLR, 1.88 for SIRI)30. Our findings extend this body of evidence to the specific context of acute infectious diarrhea. In this setting, a distinct differential inflammatory response was observed, with bacterial infections demonstrating approximately eight to ten fold higher AISI and NLR values compared with viral infections. This marked disparity provides a plausible physiological explanation for the increased risk of complications and highlights the potential utility of these indices for early risk stratification and identification of high-risk patients.

The exceptional discriminatory power of NLR (AUC 0.892) and AISI (AUC 0.886) for distinguishing bacterial from viral pathogens has immediate clinical implications. In emergency settings where rapid decisions on antibiotic therapy are required, these indices offer an objective, readily available guide superior to clinical assessment alone. This is particularly valuable given recent evidence on the limitations of traditional biomarkers. A large-scale study by Gallo et al. evaluating 1,910 hospitalized adults with acute diarrhea demonstrated that serum procalcitonin was not predictive of bacterial etiology; however, it proved to be valuable for the identification of concurrent bloodstream infection31. Similarly, Cho et al. confirmed that while procalcitonin and CRP showed moderate accuracy for bacterial gastroenteritis differentiation (AUC 0.708 and 0.723, respectively), their performance was inferior to comprehensive CBC-derived indices. Our findings suggest that composite indices like AISI, which integrate neutrophil, monocyte, and platelet counts with lymphocyte counts, provide a more nuanced assessment of the host inflammatory response to enteric pathogens9.

The robust association between inflammatory indices and AKI development deserves particular attention. AKI occurred in 14.8% of our cohort, substantially higher than the 8–10% reported in general hospitalized populations, reflecting the vulnerability of elderly patients with infectious diarrhea to renal complications. Recent evidence supports the mechanistic link between systemic inflammation and AKI. Gao et al. analyzed 17,491 ICU admissions from the MIMIC-IV database and demonstrated that dynamic changes in SII within the first 24 h independently predicted AKI development, with steeper positive SII slopes associated with significantly worse outcomes32. Furthermore, Yilmaz et al. (2025) showed that both SII and SIRI independently predicted contrast-induced AKI in elderly STEMI patients, with these indices offering a more comprehensive reflection of immune status compared to NLR and PLR alone33. These findings support our observation that AISI, which incorporates multiple cell lineages, achieved the highest AUC (0.846) for AKI prediction among all evaluated indices.

The superior discrimination of AISI relative to single-lineage ratios in bacterial enteritides has a coherent mechanistic basis. Bacterial colitis, and Clostridioides difficile colitis in particular, is characterised by neutrophil-rich pseudomembrane formation, reactive thrombocytosis driven by mucosal injury and IL-6 release, and a relative monocytosis reflecting expanded innate effector demand, all set against age-related lymphopenia. AISI integrates each of these lineages into a single value and therefore captures the breadth of the innate response, whereas NLR or PLR sample only one axis at a time. In older adults this advantage is amplified by immunosenescence: thymic involution and contraction of the naive T-cell repertoire produce a baseline lymphopenia that is largely uninformative as a stress signal, while persistent low-grade myelopoiesis (inflammaging) generates neutrophilia even in the absence of new infection. Neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytosis are concurrently impaired, so absolute neutrophil counts can paradoxically rise while functional clearance falls, a dissociation that the lymphocyte denominator alone captures poorly. AISI’s incorporation of platelets and monocytes, which are regulated more dynamically than the lymphocyte denominator in the elderly, plausibly explains its preserved discrimination across age strata in this cohort and is consistent with recent evidence that AISI independently predicts mortality in sepsis-associated AKI (Liu et al., Sci Rep 2025; PMID: pending).

The apparently high AKI rate among hospitalised patients (65.9%) alongside low in-hospital mortality (0.5%) merits explicit mechanistic explanation, as this pattern might otherwise appear paradoxical. Several converging factors account for this distribution. First, and most importantly, AKI severity staging indicates that the majority of AKI episodes in this cohort represent KDIGO Stage 1 injury — defined by a serum creatinine increase of ≥ 0.3 mg/dL but below the 1.5-fold threshold — which characteristically reflects dehydration-driven prerenal haemodynamic impairment rather than structural tubular necrosis. Prerenal AKI in the context ofacute infectious diarrhea, particularly when volume deficit is promptly corrected with intravenous isotonic crystalloid, typically resolves within 24–72 h and carries minimal mortality risk independent of the underlying infection severity34. Second, hospitalised patients in our cohort represented a severity-enriched subset rather than a random sample of the overall population: admitted patients had substantially higher CKD prevalence (18.2% vs. 10.4% in the full cohort; p < 0.001), higher median Charlson Comorbidity Index (4 vs. 3; p < 0.001), and higher rates of diabetes mellitus (24.6% vs. 18.6%; p = 0.002), all of which independently increase AKI susceptibility even at low infection severity. Third, the clinical context of acute infectious diarrhoea — which, unlike sepsis-associated AKI or contrast nephropathy, has a clearly reversible precipitating cause that can be addressed with targeted volume resuscitation — contributes to the dissociation between high AKI incidence and low mortality. The combination of these factors — predominantly mild, reversible AKI in a selected high-vulnerability.

The subgroup analyses revealing enhanced predictive accuracy in patients with diabetes are clinically insightful. In diabetic patients, AISI achieved an exceptional AUC of 0.908 for AKI prediction, significantly higher than in non-diabetic patients (AUC 0.824, p < 0.001 for interaction). This likely reflects the synergistic effect of infection-induced inflammation and underlying diabetic nephropathy, rendering the kidney particularly vulnerable. Gaydarski et al. comprehensively reviewed the molecular mechanisms explaining increased AKI susceptibility in diabetes, highlighting hyperglycemia-induced endothelial dysfunction, upregulation of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6), and impaired tubular repair mechanisms35. The 2024 Diabetes Care Standards emphasized that older adults with diabetes face heightened infection susceptibility due to immune dysfunction, supporting our findings of differential inflammatory responses36. Furthermore, a recent 10-year cohort study confirmed that diabetes independently increases AKI recurrence risk (80.5% vs. 66.7%) and mortality in older patients37.

The strong performance of HALP score for mortality prediction (AUC 0.812) highlights the critical intersection of inflammation and nutritional status in determining geriatric outcomes. HALP integrates hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, providing a composite assessment of both inflammatory burden and nutritional reserve. Vural et al. (2025) demonstrated that HALP score effectively predicted 30-day and 1-year mortality in elderly patients with proximal femoral fractures, with lower scores indicating poorer nutritional-immune status38. Similarly, Muge et al. (2025) found that HALP score predicted in-hospital mortality in acute heart failure patients, with a cut-off of 21.5 showing reasonable discriminatory power39. Findings from a large Turkish geriatric cohort indicate that reduced HALP scores are closely linked to adverse clinical vulnerability profiles in older adults, consistent with frailty-related phenotypes characterized by malnutrition, anemia, and chronic low-grade inflammation40. These converging findings support HALP as a valuable prognostic tool that captures the malnutrition-inflammation complex increasingly recognized as a driver of adverse outcomes in elderly patients.

The inverse association of HALP with adverse outcomes is best interpreted as a coupled signal of chronic inflammation and nutritional depletion rather than as either component in isolation. Albumin and lymphocyte counts share a common substrate in the inflammation-nutrition axis: both fall under sustained IL-6 and TNF-alpha exposure, and both are co-depressed by sarcopenic muscle protein loss and reduced dietary intake. The reciprocal lymphocyte-platelet product in the HALP denominator captures the immunological half of this axis, while the haemoglobin-albumin numerator captures anabolic and visceral protein reserves, and the resulting composite tracks the inflammation-frailty-sarcopenia triad more faithfully than any single component. The HALP threshold for adverse outcomes derived in this cohort aligns closely with the value of 17.97 reported for one-year mortality after proximal femur fracture in older adults and with values associated with low muscle mass in NHANES, supporting the interpretation that HALP indexes a frailty-inflammation phenotype rather than diarrhea severity per se.

The practical advantages of CBC-derived indices cannot be overstated. They are calculated from routine tests available in virtually all clinical settings globally, incur no additional cost, and provide results within hours of presentation. This makes them particularly suitable for resource-constrained environments and aligns with the search for affordable prognostic tools highlighted in recent critical care research. AlHammouri et al. meta-analysis of 25,505 patients confirmed that preprocedural NLR and PLR are simple, cost-effective biomarkers for predicting adverse renal outcomes, though the authors noted their moderate diagnostic performance highlights the potential value of more comprehensive composite indices41. Our proposed cut-off values (e.g., AISI ≥ 5,842 for AKI risk, HALP ≤ 28.4 for mortality risk) offer actionable thresholds that can be easily implemented in electronic health record systems for automated risk stratification.

In the majority of patients presenting to the emergency department with infectious diarrhea, microbiological confirmation of the causative agent is not feasible within the first few hours of clinical decision-making; indeed, in our cohort, an etiological pathogen could not be confirmed in approximately 85% of patients. It is therefore more appropriate to interpret CBC-based inflammatory indices in this clinical context not as etiological classifiers, but as practical biomarkers reflecting the severity of the host inflammatory response. All pathogen-stratified diagnostic performance metrics, including AUC values for bacterial-viral differentiation (NLR 0.892; AISI 0.886) and outcome prediction within pathogen subgroups, pertain exclusively to the 427 microbiologically confirmed cases and must be interpreted as upper-bound estimates of performance in unselected emergency department populations, where the 85% culture-negative majority substantially dilutes high-inflammatory-burden presentations. CBC-derived inflammatory indices in this study represent biomarkers of host inflammatory response severity rather than etiological classifiers of pathogen type. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and similar CBC-derived indices have been shown to reflect systemic inflammation and to correlate with prognosis, mortality, and clinical severity across a range of disease states42. Accordingly, regardless of pathogen type, AISI values of 5.842 or above, or NPAR values of 20.4 or above, should prompt heightened clinical vigilance, including early intravenous fluid resuscitation, serial monitoring of renal function, and a lower threshold for hospital admission, particularly in geriatric patients. Similarly, NLR at a threshold of 5.8 or above may assist antibiotic stewardship in settings where a bacterial etiology is clinically suspected; however, the AUC of 0.892 obtained for bacterial-viral differentiation was derived exclusively from microbiologically confirmed cases and therefore represents the likely upper bound of performance achievable in unselected patient populations. It bears explicit emphasis that all pathogen-stratified AUC values reported in this study, most notably NLR 0.892 and AISI 0.886 for bacterial-viral differentiation, were derived exclusively from the 427 microbiologically confirmed cases, a population systematically enriched for higher-severity inflammatory presentations relative to the full unselected cohort of 2,847 patients. These figures represent the upper bound of discriminatory performance achievable in a biased confirmed-case population and must not be extrapolated to general emergency department populations in which the 85% culture-negative majority, disproportionately comprising lower-severity and antibiotic-suppressed bacterial cases, would substantially attenuate observed AUC values. The quantitative threshold values and AUC estimates derived from this analysis must therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating benchmarks rather than definitive performance metrics until prospective studies employing point-of-care multiplex PCR syndromic gastrointestinal panels achieve near-comprehensive pathogen ascertainment in representative unselected cohorts. Multiplex PCR-based evaluation of emergency department diarrhea cohorts achieves pathogen identification rates of 50 to 70% compared with 15 to 20% for conventional stool culture alone, confirming the magnitude of the diagnostic gap that culture-based classification introduces into subgroup analyses of the type reported here43. CBC-derived inflammatory indices are simple, inexpensive, and readily accessible biomarkers that are increasingly employed in risk stratification owing to their capacity to reflect systemic immunoinflammatory status44. Nevertheless, future studies incorporating point-of-care multiplex real-time PCR panels would contribute to further refinement of the diagnostic utility of these indices by enabling more rapid and accurate etiological characterization in the emergency department setting.

A methodological consideration of importance is the potential for systematic misclassification bias in pathogen subgroup analyses arising from the 85% culture-negative or untested rate in our cohort. While all pathogen-stratified analyses were restricted to microbiologically confirmed cases (n = 427), the confirmed-case population is not a random subset of the full cohort. It represents patients who had clinical features compelling enough to prompt stool culture, laboratory resources available, and positive culture results, or who underwent viral panel testing. This introduces systematic selection bias: confirmed bacterial patients likely represent higher-severity inflammatory presentations where culture sampling is more likely to be ordered and more likely to yield positive results, while lower-severity bacterial cases are disproportionately concentrated in the unclassified group. Given published stool culture sensitivity of approximately 40–60% for common bacterial enteropathogens and prior antibiotic exposure in 22.4% of culture-positive bacterial patients, it is plausible that between 30% and 50% of patients in the culture-negative or unclassified group harbour subclinical bacterial infections or infections in which bacterial growth has been suppressed by prior antibiotic exposure. The confirmed bacterial group is accordingly enriched for high-inflammatory-burden presentations, and the resulting AISI differential and pathogen-differentiation AUC values likely represent upper-bound estimates of performance in an unselected emergency department population. The broad clinical conclusion that these indices provide substantially better predictive performance in the inflammatory, neutrophil-dominant host response characteristic of bacterial enteropathogens compared with the lymphocyte-sparing, neutrophil-modest response seen in viral gastroenteritis is biologically coherent and directionally robust regardless of the precise magnitude of misclassification. Future prospective studies should employ multiplex real-time PCR syndromic gastrointestinal panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray GI Panel, which detects 22 pathogens including bacteria, viruses, and parasites within 60 min) to achieve near-comprehensive pathogen identification at the point of care, enabling more precise assessment of these indices’ etiological differentiation capacity without the inherent limitations of culture-based classification.

Methodological advances in aetiological diagnostics represent a priority for future research in this area. The deployment of multiplex real-time PCR syndromic panels for gastrointestinal pathogens, which achieve comprehensive pathogen identification within 60 min at the point of care with substantially higher sensitivity than conventional stool culture, would enable future prospective studies to precisely characterise the etiological context in which CBC-derived inflammatory indices perform optimally. In particular, differentiating non-typhoidal Salmonella (which drives strong neutrophil-dominant responses) from toxin-mediated staphylococcal or preformed-toxin bacterial diarrhoeas (which may generate weaker cellular inflammatory signatures) and from norovirus (which activates NK cells but generates minimal neutrophilia) would allow refinement of index-guided antibiotic decision pathways with a level of biological precision not achievable using conventional microbiological methods.

The translation of the proposed CBC-derived index thresholds into routine emergency department practice is practically feasible through multiple implementation pathways. At the most basic level, all indices are manually calculable from parameters already reported on standard complete blood count with differential and basic metabolic panel results, which are the two most commonly ordered laboratory panels in geriatric emergency evaluations, using simple arithmetic operations performable on any smartphone calculator within 30 s of result availability. No additional blood sampling, dedicated reagents, or specialised personnel are required, and the marginal cost of index calculation is zero. For AISI (neutrophil count × monocyte count × platelet count ÷ lymphocyte count) and NPAR (neutrophil percentage ÷ serum albumin), the formulae are simple enough to be incorporated into laminated bedside reference cards or emergency department protocol documents. For HALP (haemoglobin × albumin × lymphocyte count ÷ platelet count × 100), the formula is likewise straightforward once the four component values are available on the laboratory result screen.

At the semi-automated level, all parameters required for index calculation are stored as discrete structured numeric fields in modern electronic health record (EHR) systems, enabling direct integration as clinical decision support (CDS) rules. Although manual calculation is feasible in lower-acuity or resource-limited settings, high-volume emergency department environments may present practical barriers to consistent bedside computation, including time pressure, competing clinical tasks, and cognitive load; in these circumstances, automated CDS integration represents the more reliable and sustainable implementation pathway. A CDS hook configured in Epic, Cerner, or equivalent EHR platforms could automatically calculate AISI, NPAR, and HALP upon completion of the first CBC with differential and albumin result for any patient aged ≥ 65 years with an infectious gastrointestinal diagnostic code in an emergency department encounter, with zero additional clinician input. Risk stratification outputs such as ‘AISI ≥ 5,842: Elevated AKI risk, consider early IV hydration and serial creatinine monitoring’ could be displayed as non-interruptive advisory alerts within the existing laboratory results view, minimising alert fatigue while providing actionable guidance. The feasibility of this approach is supported by recent work demonstrating that real-time AKI prediction models built from routinely collected EHR variables, including CBC and metabolic panel components, can be deployed across geographically distinct hospital tiers with consistently high discriminative performance (AUC 0.81–0.90), without requiring any data collection beyond standard clinical workflows45. Evidence further indicates that clinicians working under time constraints consistently favour tools that eliminate additional calculation steps and deliver risk estimates directly within the existing results workflow, and that non-interruptive passive alerts are associated with substantially lower override rates and greater clinical uptake compared with interruptive pop-up notifications46.

We propose a pragmatic two-tier triage framework based on our derived thresholds. Tier 1 (AISI ≥ 5,842; NPAR ≥ 20.4) defines a high-risk presentation, prompting initiation of early intravenous crystalloid resuscitation, 6-hourly creatinine monitoring, reassessment within 2 h, and a lower threshold for hospital admission. Tier 2 (AISI ≥ 9,624; HALP ≤ 18.6) defines a very high-risk presentation, warranting immediate senior physician review, nephrology notification, ICU liaison, and consideration of vasopressor readiness. Patients below both thresholds (AISI < 5,842; NPV 93.6% for AKI; NPV 99.4% for ICU admission) may be considered for a safely accelerated discharge pathway with 24-hour telephone follow-up, provided other clinical parameters are reassuring. The clinical relevance of serial inflammatory index monitoring in this context is further supported by a 2025 study demonstrating that early changes in the systemic immune-inflammation index within the first 24 h independently predict both AKI occurrence and mortality in ICU patients, reinforcing the time-sensitive utility of CBC-derived inflammatory indices as dynamic risk stratification tools32. This framework is analogous to established biomarker-guided discharge protocols in chest pain evaluation (troponin), pulmonary embolism exclusion (D-dimer), and bacterial meningitis screening (CSF lactate), and represents a natural extension of evidence-based emergency department risk stratification togeriatric acute infectious diarrhea.

Our findings should be interpreted in the broader context of sepsis-associated complications in elderly patients. Infectious diarrhea can progress to systemic infection, and early identification of at-risk patients is crucial. Xu et al. demonstrated that sepsis-associated AKI accounts for over half of all sepsis-related complications in ICU settings, with emerging biomarkers showing promise for early detection47. Kubo et al. demonstrated that procalcitonin-guided antibiotic discontinuation strategies are beneficial in sepsis management, particularly in clinical settings where antimicrobial therapy is prolonged beyond seven days48. However, the utility of procalcitonin for initial bacterial versus viral differentiation in gastroenteritis remains limited, as demonstrated by recent studies, reinforcing the complementary role of CBC-derived indices in the initial emergency department evaluation.

Several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and reliance on ICD codes for patient identification carry inherent risks of selection bias and misclassification. While we employed stringent exclusion criteria and validated outcomes through laboratory data, prospective validation is essential. Second, external validity represents a fundamental limitation that must be explicitly acknowledged. MIMIC-IV derives exclusively from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, a large quaternary academic centre with dedicated geriatric emergency medicine services, advanced microbiological diagnostics, and a specific regional pathogen epidemiology. Geriatric emergency care practices, including admission thresholds, empiric antibiotic policies, and hydration protocols, differ substantially between tertiary academic and community settings, and between high-income and low-to-middle-income healthcare systems. Furthermore, the microbial epidemiology of our cohort, with Clostridioides difficile predominating at 51.4% of bacterial cases, may not reflect pathogen distributions in other regions where non-toxigenic bacterial diarrhoea predominates and where the magnitude of the neutrophil-dominant inflammatory response may differ correspondingly. The proposed cutoff values, including AISI ≥ 5,842 for AKI risk, NPAR ≥ 20.4 for elevated inflammatory burden, AISI ≥ 9,624 for ICU risk stratification, and HALP ≤ 18.6 for very high-risk classification, were derived from a single-institution cohort and must not be applied to clinical practice in any other setting without prior institutional validation and threshold recomputation. Optimal cut-off values will require recalibration based on at least four institution-specific variables. Local patient demographics and baseline comorbidity burden determine resting inflammatory index values and must therefore be characterised in the target population before any threshold is adopted. Albumin assay methodology constitutes a second independent recalibration driver, as nephelometric and colorimetric platforms differ systematically by up to 10–15% in absolute serum albumin measurement, a discrepancy sufficient to materially displace NPAR-derived thresholds in institutions using a different analytical method from that employed at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Prevailing antibiotic prescribing practices represent a third source of institutional variability, as background antimicrobial exposure influences baseline neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios in presenting populations and thereby shifts the inflammatory index distribution against which thresholds are calibrated. Pathogen ecology constitutes a fourth and particularly salient source of non-transferability in the context of this study. Clostridioides difficile accounted for 51.4% of microbiologically confirmed bacterial cases in our cohort, and the multi-lineage, toxin-mediated neutrophilic response characteristic of CDI is the primary biological mechanism underpinning the magnitude of the AISI values from which the AISI ≥ 5,842 threshold was derived. In settings where non-toxigenic Salmonella, Campylobacter, or enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli predominate, the innate immune response is qualitatively similar but quantitatively attenuated, and the Youden-optimal AISI threshold would be expected to fall substantially below the value derived in this study. Clinicians and researchers should therefore treat each of the reported cut-offs as a hypothesis-generating estimate specific to a quaternary North American academic institution rather than an immediately actionable decision rule, and should undertake prospective local validation including Youden-optimal threshold recomputation in the institutional cohort prior to any electronic health record integration or clinical protocol development. Prospective multicentre validation studies encompassing community hospitals, rural emergency settings, and low-to-middle-income country institutions where pathogen ecology, albumin assay platforms, and patient demographics differ substantially from the MIMIC-IV population are strongly warranted before these thresholds can be considered for generalised clinical implementation.Third, the relatively low absolute mortality rate (0.5%) and ICU admission rate (1.2%) limit the precision of analyses for these outcomes, though these rates are consistent with contemporary hospitalized gastroenteritis populations. The distinction between the well-powered and hypothesis-generating components of this analysis warrants explicit articulation in the context of clinical translation. AKI prediction and hospital admission models were supported by event counts and events-per-variable ratios substantially exceeding accepted thresholds for stable logistic regression, and conclusions drawn from these analyses may be regarded as statistically robust within the constraints of the retrospective single-center design. By contrast, in-hospital mortality and ICU admission models were analysed in the context of very small event counts, and the correspondingly wide confidence intervals observed for these outcomes, exemplified by the HALP AUC 95% confidence interval of 0.786 to 0.938 for mortality prediction, accurately reflect the substantial irreducible uncertainty inherent in small-event analyses rather than indicating poor model calibration. Firth’s penalized logistic regression and the pre-specified composite severe adverse outcome endpoint were employed to mitigate but not eliminate this uncertainty. Mortality-specific and ICU-specific threshold values derived from this study must therefore be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating estimates warranting prospective validation in cohorts with substantially higher event rates before any clinical implementation. Fourth, we excluded immunocompromised patients to create a homogeneous cohort, but this limits applicability to the growing population of elderly patients on immunosuppressive therapy. Finally, the absence of serial measurements precludes assessment of dynamic changes in inflammatory indices during hospitalization, which may provide additional prognostic information as suggested by recent MIMIC-IV studies. A further and distinct limitation of temporal validity warrants explicit acknowledgement. The MIMIC-IV observation window spans 2008 to 2019 and therefore predates the COVID-19 pandemic, during which emergency department triage protocols, infection control frameworks, personal protective equipment standards, antibiotic stewardship programmes, and supportive care pathways underwent substantial and likely durable transformation. The epidemiology of infectious diarrhea has itself shifted in the post-pandemic period through at least two well-documented mechanisms. Non-pharmaceutical interventions implemented during 2020 to 2022 transiently suppressed the circulation of key enteric pathogens, most notably norovirus and rotavirus, subsequently generating a population-level susceptibility gap that contributed to intensified post-restriction resurgences of these agents, particularly in older adults whose pathogen-specific immunity wanes more rapidly than in younger populations2. Additionally, pandemic-era changes in broad-spectrum antibiotic prescribing, initially driven by high rates of empiric antibiotic use in COVID-19 patients and subsequently moderated by intensified stewardship initiatives, have altered the background antimicrobial exposure profile of emergency department populations, which influences baseline neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios and culture yield in ways that would shift the inflammatory index distributions from which our thresholds were derived49. The relative prevalence of Clostridioides difficile infection, which constituted 51.4% of microbiologically confirmed bacterial cases in our cohort and is the primary biological driver of the elevated AISI values underpinning our AISI ≥ 5,842 threshold, may similarly have shifted in the post-pandemic era given documented changes in CDI incidence, treatment strategies, and infection prevention bundle adherence since 201950. Furthermore, the increasing deployment of multiplex PCR syndromic gastrointestinal panels in contemporary emergency settings substantially reduces the high-severity enrichment bias inherent in culture-dependent pathogen classification, which would produce different pathogen-stratified inflammatory index distributions than those observed in MIMIC-IV. Taken together, these considerations indicate that the proposed index thresholds should be externally validated not only in geographically and demographically diverse cohorts but also in contemporary post-2020 emergency department populations, where the structural, epidemiological, and microbiological context differs meaningfully from the pre-pandemic institutional environment captured in this study. We consider such contemporary cohort validation to be a necessary prerequisite before these thresholds can be considered for integration into clinical decision support frameworks.

An additional residual-uncertainty consideration relates to the interaction between surrogate baseline creatinine use and chronic kidney disease ascertainment. Patients without an outpatient baseline are over-represented among those with limited longitudinal contact with the healthcare system, and undocumented CKD in this subgroup may compress the apparent creatinine excursion at presentation. Although the sensitivity analysis confined to confirmed-baseline patients was directionally consistent with the primary analysis, replication in cohorts with universal pre-admission renal function capture remains an important next step.

Systematic misclassification bias arising from the 85% culture-negative or untested rate represents an important methodological consideration that warrants explicit acknowledgement. While all pathogen-stratified analyses were restricted to microbiologically confirmed cases (n = 427), the confirmed-case population is not a random subset — it represents patients with clinical features compelling enough to prompt culture and yield positive results, introducing selection bias toward higher-severity inflammatory presentations. The directional consequence is important: the confirmed bacterial group is enriched for high-inflammatory-burden presentations, and the resulting AUC values (NLR 0.892; AISI 0.886 for bacterial-viral differentiation) likely represent upper-bound estimates of performance in unselected emergency department populations. However, the broad clinical conclusion — that these indices provide substantially better predictive performance in inflammatory, neutrophil-dominant bacterial gastroenteritis compared with viral gastroenteritis — is directionally robust. Future prospective studies should employ multiplex real-time PCR syndromic gastrointestinal panels (e.g., BioFire FilmArray GI Panel, detecting 22 pathogens within 60 min) to achieve near-comprehensive pathogen identification at the point of care.

The cut-off values reported in this analysis (AISI ≥ 5,842 for AKI, NPAR ≥ 20.4 for hospital admission, HALP ≤ 18.6 for the composite severe adverse outcome, and the corresponding mortality and ICU thresholds) were derived from a single quaternary academic centre with a distinctive pathogen ecology in which Clostridioides difficile accounted for 51.4% of bacterial cases. Direct adoption of these numbers in community hospitals, in low- and middle-income settings, or in systems with a different antibiotic prescribing profile risks biased calibration. We therefore recommend that institutions seeking to deploy these indices undertake local recalibration before clinical use, following a four-step framework: first, recomputation of the Youden-optimal threshold against the institution’s own age and comorbidity distribution; second, confirmation of inter-laboratory comparability of the albumin assay, since bromocresol green and bromocresol purple platforms can differ by 3 to 5 g per litre and will materially shift NPAR and HALP cut-offs; third, characterisation of local antibiotic prescribing patterns and resistance epidemiology, both of which influence the bacterial-to-viral case mix and the baseline leukogram; and fourth, review of the institutional pathogen distribution, particularly the proportion of C. difficile-associated diarrhea, which carries a different leukogram and albumin signature than non-difficile bacterial enteritides. A threshold-sensitivity analysis across age bands and Charlson Comorbidity Index tertiles is feasible in the present cohort and would provide an internal benchmark for the magnitude of threshold variation that external validators should anticipate; we have deferred this analysis from the current revision and identify it as the priority next step alongside prospective external validation in geographically and economically diverse cohorts.

Despite these external validity limitations, several features of our study design enhance confidence in the internal validity and biological plausibility of our findings. The MIMIC-IV database is extensively validated, with rigorous quality control, de-identification protocols, and standardised laboratory reporting. The large final cohort (n = 2,847), comprehensive pre-specified exclusion criteria ensuring diagnostic homogeneity, and the consistent performance of AISI, NPAR, and HALP across multiple pre-specified subgroups including bacterial infection, diabetes, CKD, and three age strata argue against chance findings or outcome-specific overfitting. The biological coherence of the findings, with indices that capture neutrophil activation, monocyte recruitment, platelet-neutrophil interactions, and nutritional reserve simultaneously showing superior performance over single-component ratios, provides mechanistic plausibility that supports their potential for extrapolation to other populations with appropriate local validation.

Future research should focus on several key areas. First, external validation in geographically and ethnically diverse cohorts is essential to confirm the generalizability of our findings and proposed cut-off values. Second, prospective studies integrating top-performing indices (AISI, NPAR, HALP) with clinical variables (vital signs, comorbidity indices, hydration status) could generate a comprehensive geriatric acute diarrhea severity score analogous to pediatric tools. Third, investigation of serial measurements and trajectory analysis may identify patients with deteriorating inflammatory profiles who require escalation of care. Fourth, comparative effectiveness studies evaluating whether index-guided management improves clinical outcomes and resource utilization are needed to justify implementation. Finally, extension of this research to immunocompromised elderly patients, a growing and exceptionally high-risk population, represents an important frontier.

Conclusion

This study identifies AISI, NPAR, and HALP as highly accurate, readily available prognostic biomarkers for adverse outcomes in geriatric patients with acute infectious diarrhea. These indices appear to differentiate bacterial from viral etiology, with NLR and AISI demonstrating AUCs exceeding 0.88 for pathogen differentiation in this cohort. They demonstrated enhanced performance in high-risk subgroups, particularly diabetic patients, where AISI achieved an AUC of 0.908 for AKI prediction in the current dataset. By leveraging universally available complete blood count parameters at no additional cost, these indices may offer a practical solution to the current lack of geriatric-specific risk stratification tools for acute infectious diarrhea, pending external validation in diverse clinical settings. Conclusions regarding AKI prediction and hospital admission are supported by well-powered analyses and may inform prospective clinical validation frameworks. Findings pertaining to in-hospital mortality and ICU admission are preliminary and hypothesis-generating in nature and must be regarded as benchmarks for future prospective investigation rather than thresholds ready for clinical implementation. External validation across diverse healthcare systems and contemporary patient cohorts is required before these indices can be recommended for routine clinical use.
