Integration of circulating tumor DNA in biliary tract cancer: the emerging landscape
Authors: Joy A Awosika, Cecilia Monge, Tim F Greten
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecision medicine has emerged as a cornerstone in cancer treatment revolutionizing our approach across malignancies. Molecular profiling of biliary tract cancers (BTCs) has changed the treatment landscape positively by prolonging survival in an aggressively fatal malignancy in its advanced stages. The acquisition of tissue tumor DNA for genomic analysis in BTC is often anatomically challenging, limited by quantity and quality. In response, ctDNA has emerged as a noninvasive means of molecular profiling. The utility of both plasma and bile ctDNA has been explored in several studies demonstrating the high mutation detection rates and the ability to isolate targetable mutations when present. In addition, the concordance between plasma and tissue DNA provides validity in utilizing ctDNA results to infer treatment decisions. Analysis of ctDNA in BTC has also provided prognostic information and facilitated evaluation of clonal evolution with ease of serial measurements. Insight into novel mechanisms of resistance to targeted therapies are being uncovered in ctDNA. As research endeavors continue to deepen our understanding in the field particularly in the space of ctDNA surveillance after curative intent, the tremendous progress made so far has enabled integration of ctDNA into the clinical practice of BTCs.

Introduction

The advancements made in the genomic landscape of various cancers has revolutionized our understanding of tumor biology, provided targeted treatment options, aided in diagnosis and prognosis and introduced an additional tool in the surveillance of cancer patients. The demonstration of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) was an observation first made as far back as 1977 recognizing that cancer patients carried cell free DNA in their peripheral blood [1]. The mechanism of ctDNA entry into the bloodstream is still an area of research but has been linked to release from apoptotic or necrotic tumor cells, living and circulating tumor cells [2–5]. Significant strides in refining sequencing methodologies have propelled advancements in the assays employed in ctDNA testing. While considerable progress has been made in bolstering the sensitivity and specificity of these assays, certain limitations persist. Challenges include false-negative results, as well as reduced sensitivity in detecting fusion events and copy number variations [6]. There is a strong correlation with the levels of ctDNA in plasma in reference to advanced tumor stage and volume however, patients with early cancer stages may have positive detection of ctDNA and ongoing studies are evaluating its utility in guiding adjuvant therapy and monitoring for recurrent disease [7–11] (Figure 1).

Biliary tract cancers fall under the umbrella assemble that incorporates gallbladder cancer (GBC), intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma and are a group of rare and aggressive malignancies with a dismal median 5-year overall survival of 2% for metastatic disease [12]. While surgery remains the only curative option, the vast majority of new cases are diagnosed in the advanced and metastatic setting necessitating palliative systemic therapy as the sole treatment option. Despite its relative rarity, the heterogeneity and complexity of BTC demand an intricate understanding of its molecular landscape to develop effective therapeutic strategies. With the advent of precision medicine, the molecular characterizations of BTCs has paved the way to targeted treatment options which has altered the treatment landscape and improved survival in these patients. The accessibility of next generation sequencing (NGS) methods previously confined to tissue genomic profiling, has now extended to encompass blood, bile and peritoneal fluid, thereby broadening the scope of tumor DNA detection. This has become increasingly attractive in BTC owing to the often-suboptimal yield attributable to either the anatomical limitations or the desmoplastic stroma hindering sufficient tissue sampling. As research continues to unravel the complexities of BTC at the molecular level, the use of ctDNA for noninvasive, real-time genomic profiling offers promising prospects for early detection, monitoring treatment response and detecting minimal residual disease. The objective of this review is to summarize the current evidence in the use of ctDNA in BTC with emphasis on mutation detection rates, concordance of tissue and blood DNA and the ability to infer treatment decisions.

Characterization of the molecular landscape in BTCs

Recent advances in genomic, transcriptomic and proteomic technologies have illuminated the diverse molecular alterations underlying BTC [13]. Key genetic mutations frequently observed in BTC include alterations in KRAS, TP53, IDH1/2, ARID1A and CDKN2A/B [14]. These mutations contribute to dysregulated signaling pathways that drive oncogenesis, tumor progression and resistance to conventional therapies. Additionally, epigenetic modifications and aberrant expression of non-coding RNAs further complicate the molecular profile of BTC [13]. A comprehensive genomic analysis evaluating ∼1200 CCA patients revealed on average 4.6 genomic alterations per patient comprising of short-variants followed by copy-number alterations and rearrangements [14]. The most frequently altered genes were TP53 (40.0%), CDKN2A (29.0%), KRAS (22.6%), CDKN2B (19.7%), ARID1A (16.0%), SMAD4 (11.7%), IDH1 (10.2%) and BAP1 (10.2%) [14]. Despite being grouped under the same umbrella of BTCs, gallbladder cancer, intrahepatic and extrahepatic CCA differ not only in their anatomical locations but also in their molecular characteristics and etiological backgrounds [15–18] Figure 2. This particularly holds true for iCCA where liver fluke related CCA is linked to HER2 amplification, TP53, KRAS and SMAD4 mutations and hepatitis-negative iCCA is associated with more KRAS and IDH mutations [19–23]. Whole exome sequencing performed in a large Asian population noted a significantly higher prevalence of P53, SMAD4, MLL3 and GNAS in liver fluke associated CCA while non-liver fluke associated CCAs had a higher rate of IDH1/2 and BAP1 mutations [21]. The molecular signature of liver fluke-associated CCA reflect the chronic inflammation and direct oncogenic effects of the parasites. In non-liver fluke related CCA, chromatin modifying genes often serve as the primary epigenetic driver mutation leading to DNA hypermethylation and tumor growth [16,21,24]. Several studies have identified clusters of BTC-associated molecular aberrations with unique genomic, epigenetic and clinical features [16,19]. The main targetable alterations, IDH1 and FGFR were notable in iCCA patients and while FGFR mutant tumors was associated with a better prognosis and a relatively indolent clinical disease course, IDH mutations did not appear to impact prognosis [14,25]. It is important to note that the presence of IDH and FGFR mutations are mutually exclusive. IDH associated tumors were found to have distinct mRNA, copy number and DNA methylation features with increased methylation of the ARID1A promoter and subsequent lower expression of ARID1A [24,25]. Of the IDH isoforms, IDH1 mutations are more prevalent than IDH2 mutations and tend to appear in non- liver fluke related and hepatitis-negative iCCA [22–24]. This became of therapeutic clinical relevance with the development of multiple IDH selective inhibitors of which the oral IDH1 targeted inhibitor, ivosidenib, is now approved in CCAs that have progressed on at least one prior line of therapy and harbor the IDH1 mutation [26]. FGFR genomic alterations are associated with non-liver fluke etiology and seen almost exclusively in iCCAs in 10–16% of cases [21,27]. FGFR2 which is part of the larger FGFR tyrosine kinase family is commonly implicated in CCA with fusion genes being the notable aberration involved in oncogenesis [28,29]. Dysregulation leads to constitutive activation of the FGFR signaling pathway, promoting cell proliferation and survival.The development of multiple FGFR selective inhibitors has led to approval of futibatinib and pemigatinib in previously treated CCAs harboring FGFR2 fusions or rearrangements [30,31]. In eCCA and GBC the predominant actionable mutation observed is in the ERBB/HER family of proteins and PIK3CA activating mutations [32,33]. The data so far has been disappointing with minimal efficacy demonstrated targeting HER amplifications and mutations in BTCs [34–37]. Although less frequently observed, NTRK, BRAF V600E mutations and RET fusions are still identifiable in a minority of BTC patients. These findings offer a gateway to the tumor agnostic use of their respective targeted therapies [38,39].

Utility of ctDNA in BTC

The emergence of targeted treatment options in the management of BTC underscores the importance of acquiring molecular data, thereby enabling potential access to these therapies. A frequent challenge encountered in clinical practice is the limited accessibility of tissue-based profiling resulting from difficult to access tumor locations, insufficient tissue sampling for sequencing and heterogeneity among metastatic sites [40]. Anatomical constraints, particularly encountered in hilar CCA, increases the risk of adverse procedural complications. Limited tissue availability from biopsies often restricts the amount of DNA that can be extracted leading to incomplete genetic profiling. Small samples may not reflect the tumor's heterogeneity, affecting the comprehensiveness of genetic analyses. Hematological malignancies and certain solid tumors, notably colorectal and lung cancer, have incorporated ctDNA in routine clinical practice with varying degrees of success. This certainly became an attractive tool to obtain crucial tumor molecular data in BTCs and several studies have since analyzed its utility in this space.

ctDNA analysis relies heavily on the technique and accuracy of the assays used. Despite the tremendous work that has gone into improving the validity and sensitivity, limitations are often encountered attributable to the negative predictable value particularly in the context of minimal residual disease [41]. The designation of the different ctDNA assay types fall into the detection of specific preselected mutations using digital PCR (dPCR)-based techniques, targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels and broader approaches involving whole-exome sequencing or the analysis of global methylation status [42,43] Figure 1. Digital PCR based techniques analyzes single-molecule amplification allowing for assessment of rare mutant alleles in a background of many wild-type alleles. A major limitation of this method is the finite number of mutations that are analyzed and lower throughput sequencing compared with NGS [41,44]. NGS based testing overcame this offering a deeper comprehensive analysis of genomic alterations involving large-scale parallel sequencing or high-throughput sequencing of either DNA or RNA which possesses the advantages of high sensitivity, specificity and speed [45]. The ability of NGS to allow for sequencing of entire genomes or specific gene panels provides detection of a wide range of mutations which is useful in detecting new mutations and understanding tumor heterogeneity. However, this also introduces limitations related to false positivity and lower sensitivity compared with dPCR, especially for low frequency mutations [45].

Genomic profiling & diagnostic potential

The current diagnostic modalities, including imaging and tissue biopsy, have limitations in sensitivity and specificity, particularly for early-stage disease. ctDNA can be used to support a diagnosis of cancer in patients with radiographically suspected BTC. This was supported by the PREVAIL ctDNA pilot trial demonstrating 100% sensitivity and 75% specificity as a diagnostic tool [46]. Several studies have highlighted ctDNA's capability to reflect the genetic landscape of BTC, enabling the detection of specific mutations and alterations associated with the disease.

The clinical application in BTCs has seen the most success in utilizing fixed panel NGS based testing as the preferred method with mutation detection rates as high as 80% comparable to various solid tumors [47,48]. TP53 and KRAS mutations were the most frequent detected genomic alterations in advanced BTC ctDNA consistent with prior analysis of tissue specific NGS [47,49]. Detection of clinically actionable mutations in plasma ctDNA of 1600 advanced BTC patients revealed the presence of IDH1 mutations at 9.1% and ERBB2 amplifications at 4.9%. Notably FGFR2 fusions were demonstrated in only 1.4% of patients which is consistent with known challenges in detecting fusions through ctDNA analysis. Attempts to overcome this limitation entails larger sample sizes to increase DNA yield, increased sequencing depth and improved bioinformatics approaches such as expanded probe coverage for fusion calling [50,51]. Targeted sequencing methods provides a sensitive approach in detecting fusions by providing a more focused manner in the isolation and sequencing of subsets of genes or regions of the entire genome [52]. Techniques for sequence enrichment include hybrid capture or amplicon-based techniques [53] Figure 3. Hybrid capture assays achieve target enrichment via gene-specific hybridization probes to select the desired target sequences from shotgun genomic DNA libraries (DNA-based method) or from libraries of expressed transcripts (RNA-based method) [45,53,54]. This offers comprehensive profiling of large gene panels generating more uniform coverage and yield for higher sensitivity in variant calling [45,53,54] In contrast, amplicon-based approaches are rapid and require relatively little DNA. This approach utilizes targeted genome or transcriptome sequences via target-specific primers for PCR amplification of target sequences during library construction [45,53,54] Figure 3.

Overview of target enrichment for amplicon and hybrid capture NGS assays. For amplification-based assays (left in blue): primer-based amplification selects regions of interest, followed by PCR with barcode and adapter ligation. Tumor agnostic therapies become an option with the rare chance ctDNA can detect the relevant genomic alterations. In advanced BTC this was seen in 1.3% of patients for BRAFV600E mutations and not surprisingly in only 0.1% for NTRK and RET fusions [47]. With increasing number of genes and regions of the genome being sequenced with ctDNA, addition of MSI and TMB are now incorporated in most commercial tests. Improvement in sequencing large number of base pairs allows for accurate TMB analysis and evaluation of designated microsatellite regions enabling analysis of the changes in individual DNA strands to be classified as microsatellite stable, low, or high [41,55]. While some fusions can be detected through DNA, more comprehensive assessment for the presence of fusions requires RNA analysis. RNA-based methods can distinguish in-frame, transcribed gene fusions versus out-of-frame fusions and avoid difficulties of sequencing large intronic regions which is a limitation of DNA based analysis [52]. RNA-based anchored multiplex PCR is particularly useful for the profiling of gene fusions by combining the use of universal and gene-specific primers in multiplexed assays allowing the simultaneous detection of any known or unknown 5′ or 3′ prime fusion partner of multiple targets [54]. RNA-based assays are being increasingly used for detection of gene fusions but there's limited data regarding its use in circulating tumor RNA analysis in BTCs [56].

Tissue & peripheral blood ctDNA concordance

Several studies have confirmed concordance between conventional tissue genomic analysis and blood ctDNA in BTCs. These studies indicate varying levels of concordance, which are crucial for understanding the reliability of ctDNA as a noninvasive diagnostic and monitoring tool in BTC Table 1. Ettrich et al. demonstrated a 74% tissue-blood concordance in therapy naive CCA patients of which 92% concordance was seen in iCCAs [57]. Okamura et al. found a higher blood-tissue concordance from the metastatic site compared with the primary tumor site [58]. Percentage mutational detection defined as the presence of at least one genomic alteration per patient averaged 70–80% in blood ctDNA which translated to43% of patients receiving a ‘matched’ therapy to an identified mutation [58]. Berchuck et al. demonstrated the presence of targetable mutations in blood ctDNA in 44% of patients of which concordance between ctDNA and tissue for mutation detection was high for IDH1 mutations (87%) and BRAF V600E (100%) and low for FGFR2 fusions (18%) [47]. The low detection rate of FGFR2 fusions was attributed to the identity of the companion fusion partner with FGFR2-BICC1 fusion being the most common partner [47]. FGFR2–AHCYL, FGFR2–BICC1, FGFR2–PPHLN1 and FGFR2–TACC3 fusions have been frequently described in patients with iCCA, although over 100 different FGFR2 partners have been reported in this disease [52,59]. The timing of ctDNA collection influenced the detection of IDH1 mutations with the highest concordance (100%) observed when ctDNA was collected before initiation of systemic therapy and the lowest concordance (56%) observed when ctDNA was collected while on systemic therapy or during a period of stable disease [47]. The frequencies of IDH1 mutations and FGFR2 fusions detected by ctDNA were 7.4 and 4.8%, respectively in a study by Chen et al. [60]. These were very close to the mutational frequencies in tissue-based testing, which were 6 and 2.7%, respectively. Overall, the research supports the promising role of ctDNA in blood as a reliable surrogate for tumor tissue DNA, with high concordance rates and the ability to isolate targetable mutations when present.

Bile versus blood ctDNA

Given the direct proximity of bile to tumor tissue, bile-derived ctDNA analysis has been shown to more accurately reflect the tumor tissue's genomic alterations compared with plasma ctDNA [61,62,64–68] Table 2. The isolation of ctDNA from bile is influenced by the mutation frequency in tumor tissue that needs to be relatively high [65]. This has been described as the mutation frequency in ctDNA being approximately half as much as those found in tumor tissue DNA [65]. A few retrospective series have demonstrated improved mutation detection in bile compared with blood, alongside a notable correlation between bile and tumor tissue mutations. Li et al. demonstrated a consistently higher percentage of genomic alterations in the bile supernatant and pellet compared with plasma supported by a higher cfDNA concentration, more genomic alterations than plasma and a higher mutational allele frequency [64] [Table 2]. Bile-tumor tissue concordance was also demonstrated ranging between 85–90% [64]. This highlights the role of bile ctDNA in diagnosing BTC, presenting bile as a superior source of ctDNA due to its higher concentration of tumor-derived DNA. It has been noted that bile mutation detection rates did not change with disease stage and had no influence on survival outcomes although per Hans et al., KRAS mutation in bile ctDNA showed significantly worse survival than those with wild-type KRAS and could potentially be used as a prognostic marker in BTCs [58,65,66].

Effect of treatment on ctDNA

There is paucity of data surrounding the changes in ctDNA in response to treatment in early or advanced BTCs. The utility of detecting acquired mechanisms of treatment resistance in ctDNA was evaluated by Berchuck et al. [47]. Specifically, in FGFR2 fusion positive CCAs the presence of FGFR KD mutations is a known acquired mechanism of resistance to FGFR inhibitors [69]. In this study, 31 unique FGFR KD mutations, which were not present in the tumor samples were present in plasma ctDNA and included unique variants of which the clinical relevance was not yet established. Follow-up data reported that TAS-120, an irreversible pan-FGFR inhibitor, was effective in 4 patients with FGFR2 fusion-positive CCA who developed resistance to previous FGFR inhibition and were selected through examination of serial biopsies, ctDNA and patient-derived tumor cells [70]. These mutations require further evaluation to explore their role in conferring resistance to FGFR inhibitors [47]. Assessment of clonal evolution additionally provides insight into tumor heterogeneity which has been a recognized mechanism of treatment resistance. The feasibility of obtaining serial ctDNA samples allows for the evaluation of emerging subclones. A greater number of alterations seen as disease progresses reflects the emergence of subclonal resistance alterations and indicates an increase in tumor complexity. This was supported by the significantly higher proportion of mutations in the final samples compared with the initial samples. Notably the higher frequency of mutated genes were TP53, KRAS and FGFR2 which is associated with cancer aggressiveness and resistance to anticancer therapy [47].

Methylated homeobox A9 (meth-HOXA9), a tumor specific prognostic and predictive biomarker, was evaluated in a study by Anderson et al. to determine the clinical impact of plasma meth-HOXA9 ctDNA in advanced BTC patients on their last line of therapy [71]. This study found increasing meth-HOXA9 levels to be negatively associated with survival in patients with late-stage BTC concluding that meth-HOXA9 may guide early discontinuation of ineffective treatment [71]. The impact of survival and high pretreatment variant allele frequency (VAF) in ctDNA has provided predictive value in advanced BTCs [47,71,72]. Among 86 patients with systemic therapy-naive advanced BTC, treated with first-line gemcitabine/platinum chemotherapy, patients with a high VAF (>9%) had a significantly shorter median PFS of 2.6 months compared with 7.6 months among those with a low VAF and a shorter overall survival of 7.7 months compared with a low VAF (19.9 months) (HR 2.1, 95% CI 1.1–4.0; P = 0.030) [47]. Similar results related to shortened survival correlating to higher ctDNA allele frequencies (>10%) in patients treated with upfront platinum-based chemotherapy were demonstrated in a smaller cohort [72]. The role of ctDNA in monitoring treatment response was further collaborated in a retrospective analysis of the ClarIDHy trial which evaluated the efficacy of ivosidenib in mutant IDH1 CCA. In addition to confirming the feasibility of IDH1 mutation detection in plasma, with a blood-tissue concordance of 92%, this study showed that IDH1 mutation clearance as evaluated on plasma ctDNA in patients followed longitudinally correlated with disease control [73].

ctDNA in resected BTC

The utility of ctDNA surveillance post-surgical resection to aid in monitoring for disease recurrence and/or deciding on the role of adjuvant therapies has gained momentum in colorectal cancer. The potential clinical application in BTC patients was evaluated in a small prospective study by King et al. that included 12 patients who had pre-operative detectable ctDNA, completed surgical resection and adjuvant therapy. During longitudinal follow-up, of the patients who had confirmed recurrence (4 out of 12), one had persistent ctDNA levels and the others converted from negative to detectable levels preceding their radiographical confirmation [74]. In another pilot study demonstrating the feasibility of testing for ctDNA following potentially curative resection, results showed the presence of ctDNA may be associated with increased relapse risk. Although the study did not reach statistical significance, there was a trend toward increased relapse risk with a relapse rate 37.5% (ctDNA absent) vs 66.67% (ctDNA present) [75]. Despite the limitations in sample size, the demonstration of negative ctDNA results correlating with improved recurrence free survival certainly warrants further investigation to assess clinical implications [74].

Conclusion

Understanding the molecular profile of BTC is crucial not only for the development of targeted therapies but also for improving diagnostic accuracy and prognostication. The use of ctDNA has received increasing attention as an alternate noninvasive means to obtain molecular profile data in BTC of which the implications for targeted treatment options has been well established. The existing body of evidence is encouraging to note high mutational detection rates in both plasma and bile ctDNA with high concordance when matched to tissue molecular DNA. The frequency of isolating targetable mutations in ctDNA translating to treatment decisions has been demonstrated in a few studies albeit limited by small sample size Moreover, the potential for ctDNA to serve as a tool for monitoring disease progression and treatment response in real-time further highlights its utility in the clinical management of BTC. The promise of ctDNA lies in its ability to enhance personalized medicine approaches in BTC, offering a more dynamic and less invasive method for guiding treatment decisions and improving patient outcomes.

Future perspective

As research continues to advance, larger well designed prospective studies are necessary to validate these findings and fully integrate ctDNA analysis into standard practice. In the setting of post-surgical surveillance of resected BTC, ctDNA monitoring may provide a sensitive method to monitor for minimal residual disease and early recurrence. Drawing inspiration from emerging data in colorectal cancer, adopting a ctDNA guided approach to inform treatment decisions during surveillance in resected BTC may catch disease recurrences early allowing for timely interventions. Ongoing clinical trials exploring these avenues are currently active: NCT06171321; NCT05743959; NCT04931420; NCT04183712 and NCT02809898. Additionally, future directions may employ exploring resistance mechanisms to targeted therapies, assessing the prognostic significance of novel resistance mechanisms and investigating RNA based circulating tumor analysis to enhance the detection of fusion alterations, thereby informing treatment strategies for BTCs. Overall, the current evidence highlights the transformative impact of ctDNA in the landscape of BTC, emphasizing its role in precision medicine. While challenges remain in standardizing ctDNA analysis and integrating it into clinical practice, the potential for ctDNA to offer non-invasive, accurate molecular profiling in BTC is significant, with ongoing research poised to address current limitations and expand its clinical utility.
