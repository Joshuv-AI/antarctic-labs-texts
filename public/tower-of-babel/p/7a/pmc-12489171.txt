Natural Setting Interventions to Increase Physical Activity Level in Older Adults With Intellectual Disabilities: A Systematic Review
Authors: Stéphanie Turgeon, Jason D'Amours, Christophe Maïano, Jade Paré, Diane Morin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe present study aimed to understand the effectiveness of natural setting interventions to increase the level of PA in older adults (≥ 40 years old) with intellectual disabilities.MethodFollowing PRISMA's most recent guidelines, a systematic review was used to synthesise research on PA interventions implemented in a natural setting to increase PA in older adults with intellectual disabilities.ResultsEight articles were retained based on inclusion and exclusion criteria: four group and four single‐case designs. Most studies showed positive but small changes in PA levels. Due to the heterogeneity of the interventions tested, the samples and PA outcomes measured as well as the quality and risk of bias of the studies, results could not be generalised.ConclusionThe systematic review highlights the pressing need for better quality research to understand if and how natural setting interventions can effectively increase PA levels in older adults with intellectual disabilities.

Summary Results from this systematic review suggest that very little research (K = 8) has examined natural setting interventions that aim to increase physical activity levels of older adults with intellectual disabilities.The studies included in the systematic review generally show positive but small changes in participants' physical activity but are mostly of low‐to‐moderate quality or present moderate‐to‐high risk bias.There is a pressing need to further expand knowledge on the effectiveness of intervention components and programmes that can benefit older adults with intellectual disabilities.

Intellectual Disabilities and Ageing

Individuals with intellectual disabilities are characterised by difficulties pertaining to intellectual and adaptive functioning occurring during the developmental period (American Psychiatric Association 2022). Before the 1990s, their life expectancy was less than 36 years (for a review see Dolan et al. 2019) and has recently increased to 59.5–69.9 years. This may be in part explained by advances in knowledge and improvements in healthcare, such as access to quality treatment, better management of chronic conditions through earlier diagnosis and increased awareness of specific health needs (Acharya et al. 2016; Dolan et al. 2019; Tyrer et al. 2021). This increased longevity raises new challenges related to the specific realities of ageing for people with ID and for providing services and interventions adapted to their reality (Dieckmann et al. 2015; Glover et al. 2016).

Physical Activity (PA) Issues Among Older Adults With Intellectual Disabilities

PA is defined as ‘any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure. The energy expenditure can be measured in kilocalories. PA in daily life can be categorised into occupational, sports, conditioning, household or other activities’ (Caspersen et al. 1985, Abstract). The importance of PA on physical and mental health, as well as on well‐being is well recognised worldwide (World Health Organization 2020; for a review see Mahindru et al. 2023). Indeed, practising PA on a regular basis has multiple benefits for physical (e.g., hypertension, Type‐2 diabetes), cognitive (e.g., executive functioning, school/work performance) and mental health (e.g., symptoms of anxiety and depression) outcomes (World Health Organization 2020). Similarly, positive outcomes of PA have been identified for individuals with intellectual disabilities of varying ages (see Diz et al. 2024 and Jacob et al. 2023 for recent systematic reviews). To the best of our knowledge, no systematic review or meta‐analysis has specifically studied the benefits of PA in older adults with intellectual disabilities. However, there is general agreement that engaging in at least 150 min/week of moderate‐to‐vigorous PA leads to physical and psychological health benefits for people with intellectual disabilities (Kreinbucher‐Bekerle et al. 2023) and without intellectual disabilities (Bull et al. 2020).

Yet, during adulthood, a large proportion of people with intellectual disabilities do not meet these guidelines. The systematic review conducted by Dairo et al. (2016) reported that only 9% of the adults with intellectual disabilities met the aforementioned PA guidelines. As is the case in the general population, the level of PA of people with intellectual disabilities tends to decrease with age. In the general population, it is estimated that 67% of older adults 1 are inactive or adopt a sedentary lifestyle (Statistics Canada 2018), whereas the prevalence reached 80% for older adults with intellectual disabilities (Hsieh et al. 2017; Oviedo et al. 2020). These results are, particularly, concerning given the various negative repercussions of physical inactivity or of a sedentary lifestyle on physical (e.g., decreased mobility, weight gain) and psychosocial outcomes (e.g., reduced engagement in communal activities, higher levels of depressive symptoms) among older adults with intellectual disabilities (Groessl et al. 2019; Park et al. 2020).

Barriers to PA Among People With Intellectual Disabilities

Individuals with intellectual disabilities face unique challenges in accessing and participating in PA. This may be explained by the fact that impairments in intellectual functioning and adaptive skills, which affect cognitive, functional and social skills (American Psychiatric Association 2022), can interfere with their ability to participate in PA (Bossink et al. 2017). Indeed, such impairments may reduce their ability to understand and apply the information necessary to be involved in PA or to adapt their involvement according to the context of practice.

Additionally, among the various barriers to PA identified among individuals with intellectual disabilities, environmental factors are, particularly, salient and may explain why the practice of PA in non‐natural settings (e.g., club, fitness centres, community centres) represents an important issue. Indeed, such practices require, for example, support or incentive from staff or family members, a social network, transportation, accessibility, finances, and autonomy. Yet, these factors are frequently reported as negative correlates of involvement in PA (Bowers et al. 2014; Burns et al. 2022; Mahy et al. 2010; McGarty and Melville 2018; van Schijndel‐Speet et al. 2014).

Moreover, in people with intellectual disabilities, the impact of these barriers may be even more pronounced around 40–50 years as the ageing process starts (Lin et al. 2011; World Health Organization 2000). Indeed, they are faced with additional physical (e.g., balance, mobility, strength), cognitive (e.g., cognitive ability to understand rules), and social (e.g., smaller social networks with fewer relatives) challenges that restrict their opportunities to be physically active (Bossink et al. 2017; Frey et al. 2005; Hilgenkamp et al. 2012; van Schijndel‐Speet et al. 2014). Finally, older adults with intellectual disabilities often live in group homes, where maintaining an active lifestyle, particularly, by taking part in leisure activities and activities of daily living that stimulate PA (e.g., cooking, gardening, shopping, housekeeping tasks) requires the initiation and support of carers (Bowers et al. 2014; van Schijndel‐Speet et al. 2014). Studies point out that the lack of support, staff and training adapted to stimulate engagement in PA are elements that act as catalysts to a sedentary lifestyle within this population (Cooper et al. 2018; Cartwright et al. 2017; Dairo et al. 2016). In such circumstances, it can be especially difficult for older adults with intellectual disabilities to find adequate environments or places to be physically active. Nevertheless, PA remains an important component for maintaining a healthy lifestyle for people with intellectual disabilities.

Possible Avenue for Increasing PA Levels in Adults With Intellectual Disabilities

A higher level of PA can be achieved by increasing the frequency (e.g., number of physical activities/week), duration (e.g., the number of minutes of PA/week), intensity (e.g., light, moderate, vigorous PA) or specific PA behaviours (e.g., step count, distance walked). Throughout the literature, various interventions have been examined as feasible or effective to increase PA among individuals with intellectual disabilities. These interventions have included self‐monitoring devices (e.g., Fitbit watches; Savage et al. 2022), incorporated exergaming (Martinez‐Millana et al. 2022; Torrado et al. 2020) or relied on educational interventions (Bodde et al. 2012; Brooker et al. 2015). While these interventions are promising in promoting PA participation, they are often temporary, operating in non‐natural and specifically designed settings, which may not address the environmental PA barriers (e.g., transportation, scheduling, lack of opportunities) faced by older adults with intellectual disabilities.

An especially promising approach for overcoming environmental PA barriers is to design PA interventions in a natural setting (e.g., home, residential, day activity centre, workplace), where older adults with intellectual disabilities can engage in activities within their familiar everyday environment. This approach aims to reduce barriers such as scheduling and transportation and can enhance accessibility and sustainability by allowing carers to more easily support and initiate these activities. Thus, by removing these barriers and promoting natural setting interventions, older adults with intellectual disabilities will have greater access and opportunities to be physically active. However, to our knowledge, no scholars have synthesised the results of studies examining the effectiveness of natural setting interventions to increase PA in older adults with intellectual disabilities.

Aims

Based on the aforementioned limitations (e.g., large proportion of older adults with intellectual disabilities physically inactive; the additional limitations increasing restriction to PA participation; the influence of the environmental barriers to physical inactivity), the main objective of the present systematic review is to synthesise the results of studies examining the effectiveness of natural setting interventions to increase PA level (e.g., frequency, duration, step count, intensity, distance travelled) in older adults (≥ 40 years old) with intellectual disabilities.

Method

The present systematic review has been declared to the first author's Research Ethics Committee (project # 2023–2296) and has been registered through the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/J6VK8). This study did not require animal or human ethics approval (Government of Canada 2022). The most recent PRISMAguidelines (Page et al. 2021) have been followed for this systematic review.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility of studies was based on several criteria related to the population, the intervention, outcomes, manuscript type, language and publication date. Specifically, articles were included if the mean age of the participants was 40 or older or at least half of the sample was aged 40 or older. 2 Studies were excluded if individuals were diagnosed with an autism spectrum disorder without intellectual disabilities but included if they were conducted with a sample of individuals with a genetic syndrome associated with intellectual disabilities (e.g., Down syndrome). Three inclusion criteria relating to the intervention were included. First, the primary or secondary aim of the study had to test an intervention that aimed to increase participants' PA level. The intervention could include single‐component or multi‐component interventions or programmes. Second, to be retained, interventions were required to be implemented in person's ‘natural setting’ (i.e., an environment or a place that the person regularly attended before the study), such as home, residential, day activity centre and workplace. Therefore, if an intervention was implemented in a fitness centre or a research laboratory and that the participants only travelled to that setting to take part in the study, the study would have been excluded. Regarding outcomes, the researchers must have included quantitative PA outcomes (e.g., frequency, duration, number of steps, intensity, distance walked) in their study. PA activity measures are defined as measures that serve to quantify Person's level of PA, such as the number of steps, distance walked, duration or frequency of PA and intensity of PA.

Finally, studies were included if they were published or accepted for publication between 2000 and 2024, in English, included primary data and used an experimental, quasi‐experimental or pre‐experimental design (e.g., randomised controlled trials, single‐case study designs, single‐group design). Studies using secondary data analyses (e.g., systematic reviews, book chapters, scoping reviews), master's theses and doctoral dissertations were excluded.

Search Strategy

The search strategy was implemented in the following databases: Academic Search Complete, SPORTDiscus, PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and PsycINFO. Each database was searched using keywords established by the research team. Additionally, as suggested by Peters et al. (2020), the search strategy was reviewed and refined by a trained librarian from the first author's university. Keywords were split into four groups: intervention (Group 1), PA (Group 2), intellectual disabilities (Group 3) and adults (Group 4). They were searched in ‘Any field’ of the manuscript (see Table 1 for the detailed search strategy). Additionally, two strategies were employed to obtain other pertinent studies not found through the database search. First, as recommended by Haddaway et al. (2015), the selected keywords for database searches were used in the Google Scholar search engine, with a focus on evaluating the first 200 results organised by relevance. Second, a hand search strategy was implemented to identify other relevant articles. Specifically, reference lists of retained articles were consulted and articles citing these studies were also inspected. The search strategy was first implemented on 30 April 2022, and updated on 20 March 2024, using the same databases and keywords.

Study Selection

Study screening and selection based on eligibility criteria were conducted in two steps. First, titles and abstracts were screened based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. The second step consisted of screening full texts of articles selected in the first step. For the first data extraction process completed in 2022, an undergraduate research assistant screened all the articles in Steps 1 and 2. Using a random sample generator formula in excel (i.e., = RAND[]), a subsample that included 20% of the articles selected in Steps 1 and 2 was created by ordering the numbers from smallest to largest and keeping the 20% lower bound. Completing an interrater process using subsamples as small as 10% has been shown to result in low bias and percentage of errors, especially with large samples (Armstrong et al. 2020). These articles were screened by a second independent reviewer (J.P.) to assess interrater agreement. For the second data extraction process, given the small sample size, all articles retained at Steps 1 and 2 were screened by two independent reviewers (S.T. and J.P.). Disagreements during the first and second extraction and selection processes were discussed verbally by both reviewers. In cases of a persisting disagreement, a third reviewer (D.M.) was consulted to make the final decision.

Risk of Bias and Quality Assessment

As recommended by Cochrane Collaboration (2024) and given that all quantitative designs were accepted in our systematic review, relevant risk of bias and quality assessment tools were selected based on individual study designs. For group design studies, risk of bias was assessed with the Risk of Bias 2 tool for cluster‐randomised trials (RoB 2 CRT; Eldridge et al. 2021) and with the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomised Studies—of Interventions tool for non‐randomly assigned designs (ROBINS‐I; Sterne et al. 2016). The RoB 2 CRT, a revised version of the Cochrane risk‐of‐bias assessment tools, is designed to assess the risk of bias within randomised trials conducted in cluster settings. Each domain is scored as low risk, some concerns or high risk. The ROBINS‐I assesses the risk of bias for intervention in non‐randomised studies and each domain is scored as low, moderate, serious or critical. Finally, for both bias assessment tools, an overall summary risk of bias (ROBINS‐I: low, moderate, serious or critical; RoB 2 cluster: low, some concerns or high risk) composite score was calculated for each outcome, where the study's overall bias level was established by considering the highest level of bias detected across various domains.

For single‐case design studies, study quality was assessed using Horner et al.'s (2005) 21 quality indicators. Each indicator was scored as yes or no. We used Cure et al.'s (2021) thresholds for qualifying the level of quality of each study. Specifically, a study was considered as high quality if all the quality indicators were scored as yes (i.e., score of 21), as acceptable quality if 17–20 of the items were scored as yes or as poor quality if < 17 items were scored as yes. If an item was not applicable due to the nature of the single‐case design, the item was automatically scored as no as this quality indicator was not present in the study (e.g., if no baseline was included in the design, questions pertaining to the baseline were scored as no).

Two of the authors (S.T. and J.D.) independently assessed the risk of bias or quality of the included studies based on the criteria relevant to their respective designs. Given the main focus of the present systematic review, the bias and quality assessment tools were used for PA level outcomes only. Any disparities in their evaluations regarding the risk of bias or the reasoning behind them were resolved through discussion.

Data Extraction and Analyses

Data Extraction

For all selected articles, the first two authors (S.T. and J.A.) extracted the data from the articles retained based on their full texts. All pertinent information was retrieved using a grid that included the following variables: Study reference, setting (i.e., country and location where the intervention was implemented), study design, study population (e.g., sample size, diagnostic, sex, gender, age), study concepts (i.e., inclusion criteria), study context (i.e., inclusion criteria met), study methodology (group assignment, tools, analyses), study goals and research questions, intervention description, results (i.e., PA level, effectiveness of intervention on PA participation), author's main conclusions, study limitations and strengths and reviewer's comments.

Following extraction and analysis, the main themes of the studies were identified and compared in terms of results. The main themes, subsequently organised into groups, are presented in the results section of this systematic review.

Data Analysis

Data extracted were analysed quantitatively with descriptive statistics to provide an overview of the characteristics of the studies. Interrater agreements for the screening of abstracts and titles, full texts and for the bias and quality assessment of the final sample of articles were quantified using the percentage of agreement and Cohen's κ, a statistic that accounts for chance (McHugh 2012).

The second extraction process completed on 20 March 2024, yielded a total of 653 articles (with 216 duplicates). Of the remaining 440 articles, 437 articles were rejected based on the screening of the title and abstract. The interrater agreement was perfect (Cohen κ = 100%).

Finally, the hand search strategy conducted with Google Scholar resulted in 12 articles (out of 212 results) being examined based on title and abstract. Of those, 11 were excluded based on inclusion and exclusion criteria and one was retained. Therefore, eight studies were retained for the present systematic review.

Study Characteristics

Table 2 presents a summary of the characteristics of the eight studies included in the present systematic review. Studies were conducted in North America (United States of America, k = 4; Canada, k = 1) and in Europe (Scotland, k = 2; Netherlands, k = 1) and were published between 2001 and 2024. The oldest participants in the samples were in their 80s at the time of the studies. Within these samples, the level of intellectual disabilities (i.e., mild to profound) and the presence and severity of comorbidities (i.e., physique or mental health related) were heterogeneous. Research designs also varied, with half of the studies using group designs (k = 4) and the other half single‐case designs (k = 4). Two of the group designs used a pre‐experimental design (pre‐post single group) and the remaining two studies implemented a cluster‐randomised controlled design.

Main Results

Interventions and Intervention Components

None of the interventions was used across multiple studies and all interventions were multi‐component interventions. The majority of the interventions included one or more recognised behaviour change techniques such as goal setting (Melville et al. 2011, 2015), self‐monitoring (Melville et al. 2011, 2015; Valbuena et al. 2019), mobilising social support (i.e., carers; Bazzano et al. 2009; Melville et al. 2011, 2015) or positive reinforcement (verbal or tangible; Krentz et al. 2016; Valbuena et al. 2019).

Six of the eight interventions tested included planned PA sessions (Bazzano et al. 2009; Krentz et al. 2016; Stanish et al. 2001; Turgeon et al. 2024; Valbuena et al. 2019; van Schijndel‐Speet et al. 2017); these consisted of walking sessions (n = 2), aerobic dance sessions (n = 1), cycling sessions (n = 1) and supervised PA sessions without giving further details on what type of PA (n = 2). The PA sessions generally varied between 15 and 60 min in duration. Brief descriptions of the interventions are presented in Table 2.

PA Level Outcomes

PA level outcomes included distance travelled (walking distance [Krentz et al. 2016], cycling distance [Turgeon et al. 2024]), step count (Melville et al. 2015; Valbuena et al. 2019; van Schijndel‐Speet et al. 2017), PA duration (Bazzano et al. 2009; Turgeon et al. 2024), PA frequency (Bazzano et al. 2009), and PA intensity (i.e., Melville et al. 2011, 2015; Stanish et al. 2001).

Distance Travelled

Krentz et al. (2016) measured PA by assessing the number of 50 m laps completed during the 1‐h walking sessions. Participation in the walking sessions was voluntary. The number of laps completed was counted by staff members. Visual analysis of the ABAB graphs suggested a significant increase from Baseline 1 to the Intervention Phase 1 for four of the five participants, followed by a return to the baseline 1 values during baseline 2 and finally a significant increase in laps completed from baseline 2 to the intervention phase 2. These results suggest that the implementation of daily 1‐h walking sessions combining token reinforcement, verbal reinforcement and encouragement was effective in increasing participants' PA level. Of interest, the mean number of laps 3 completed for the baseline phases was 23.85 and 22.17, representing a walking distance of 1 102 and 1 109 m. During the intervention phases, the mean number of laps more than doubled, with 53.80 laps for the first intervention phase and 50.66 laps for the second intervention phase. As such, the average distance walked during the intervention phases was around 2 690 and 2 532 m. The highest number of laps completed by a participant during a 1‐h session was approximately 93 laps walked (~4 650 m). Walking speed was not measured; thus, the intensity of the PA level is unknown.

Turgeon et al. (2024) implemented planned 1‐h indoor cycling sessions within their 10‐week gamification intervention. Participation in the cycling sessions was voluntary. Changes in distance cycled were assessed using an AB single‐case study with a sample of 18 adults with intellectual disabilities. Distance cycled served as the PA outcome and was extracted directly by the cycling equipment and transcribed manually by a staff member for each participant on every occasion they used the cycling equipment (i.e., during or outside of the planned PA sessions). Following the implementation of their gamification intervention combined with teamwork, cycling videos and music, a small increase in distance cycled/day was observed for seven of the participants. One participant had insufficient data to assess changes in distance cycled. The remaining participants did not increase nor or decrease their distance cycled. The mean distance cycled by the adults was 4.26 km during the baseline phase and 6.74 km during the intervention phase. Together, these mixed results suggest that the gamification intervention may lead to a slight increase in distance travelled cycling in some adults with intellectual disabilities.

Step Count

Three studies measured the level of PA using steps/day counts. First, in their cluster‐randomised waitlist‐controlled trial, van Schijndel‐Speet et al. (2017) tested the effect of their two‐component PA intervention that included an educational component and three planned PA sessions/week (15–45 min duration). Participants (N = 131) wore pedometers (The NL‐1000) to monitor their PA levels. Data obtained at baseline using the NL‐1000 was unreliable for 75% of the participants given that their walking speed was < 3.2 km/h. Data from the 38 participants with reliable data suggest that the intervention was effective in increasing adults' level of PA (i.e., step/day count) from baseline to the 8‐month post‐test. Results also showed a significant difference between groups from baseline to the 6‐month follow‐up. Specifically, the intervention group maintained a steps/day count similar to their baseline values while a large decrease was observed for the control group. For the remaining participants, a second pedometer, the StepWatch, was introduced halfway through the intervention phase (i.e., at four months). No significant differences were found between the data collected for these participants four months into the intervention and at the follow‐up. In parallel, the authors reported that during the PA sessions, participants trained an average of 6 min and 50 s as a moderate intensity based on a heart rate ≥ 108 beats/min. These results suggest that the two‐component PA intervention may be effective in increasing and maintaining participants' PA level (i.e., step/day count).

Melville et al. (2015) tested the effects of the Walk Well Program with 102 adults with intellectual disabilities. Specifically, they implemented a two‐group cluster‐randomised waitlist‐controlled trial with a pre‐test, post‐test (Week 12) and follow‐up (Week 24). The intervention did not include planned PA sessions but included face‐to‐face meetings with a walking advisor, personalised PA goal setting, self‐monitoring of PA and social support. Results from this study reveal the absence of a significant difference between the intervention group and the waitlist group from baseline to the 12‐week post‐test. No within‐group effects were observed for the intervention group from baseline to post‐test or from post‐test to the 24‐week follow‐up for the intervention group. Similarly, no within‐group effect was observed for the waitlist group after completing the 12‐week intervention. These results suggest that the Walk Well Program was ineffective in increasing participants' PA level (i.e., step/day count).

Valbuena et al. (2019) used an ABABC single‐case design to assess participants (N = 5) PA level during walking sessions. Specifically, participants wore the Yamax Digiwalker SW‐200 pedometer on their hip to calculate the number of steps during each 1‐h walking session. The number of steps was then divided by the duration of the session, resulting in an average steps/min outcome. On average, results show that participants' step/min count increased by over 300% during the first intervention phase and by over 400% during the second intervention phase. During the last phase of the study (i.e., phase C), staff implemented the intervention rather than the researchers. No significant changes were observed for the three participants with three or more intervention sessions implemented by facility staff members compared to the intervention sessions implemented by researchers. These results suggest that the implementation of the daily 1‐h walking session with a combination of self‐monitoring and contingent monetary reinforcement was effective in increasing participants' PA level.

PA Duration

Turgeon et al. (2024) measured changes in time cycling during the implementation of their gamification intervention. They reported a small increase in time cycling/day was observed from the baseline to the intervention phase for nine of the participants, 18 adults in the study. One participant had insufficient data to be analysed. The remaining participants did not increase nor decrease their time cycling. On average, the time cycled increased between the baseline and the intervention phase. Together, these mixed results suggest that the gamification intervention led to a slight increase in PA level (i.e., duration) in some adults with intellectual disabilities.

Bazzano et al. (2009) measured changes in participants (N = 44) self‐reported duration of PA using a pre‐experimental pre‐post single group design following the implementation of The Healthy Lifestyle Change Program. This programme includes educational sessions and bi‐weekly 1‐h supervised PA sessions. Specifically, participants self‐reported the number of min/week they did PA before and after the 7‐month intervention. The researchers found that participants' duration of PA (in min/week) increased significantly from baseline to post‐test. The results show that The Healthy Lifestyle Change Program led to significant but small‐to‐moderate increases in participants' duration of PA/week.

PA Frequency

In addition to assessing the duration of PA/week, Bazzano et al. (2009) studied participants' self‐reported frequency of PA of participants included in The Healthy Lifestyle Change Program. They found that the number of days/week the adults reported completing PA increased significantly from baseline to post‐test. The results of this study suggest that The Healthy Lifestyle Change Program may lead to significant but small/moderate increases in participants' participants PA level frequency of PA/week.

PA Intensity

Three studies used PA intensity as their PA level outcome. First, Stanish et al. (2001) measured participants' (N = 17) moderate‐vigorous PA using an observation measure (i.e., the System for Observing Fitness Instruction Time; McKenzie et al. 1992). Using a BCBCD single‐case design, the authors compared the effects of an aerobic dance video with and without a live exercise leader. Moderate‐vigorous PA was generally stable across conditions, suggesting that the addition of a live exercise leader did not lead to significantly better PA outcomes. However, these results do not allow us to conclude that the intervention was effective in increasing participants' moderate‐to‐vigorous PA, as baseline data were not collected.

Melville et al. (2011), using a pre‐experimental pre–post single‐group design, tested the Take 5 intervention, designed as a weight loss programme with PA components. The intervention was implemented over 12 weeks and did not include planned PA sessions, but rather included educational, goal‐setting and self‐monitoring components to increase participants' PA level. Participants (N = 47) wore the ActiGraph GT1M accelerometer over a period of 7 days during and after the intervention to quantify PA levels during the day. Participants' PA intensity was categorised as light or moderate‐vigorous or as sedentary behaviours. A significant change in sedentary behaviours was reported. In addition, a significant decrease in the percentage of sedentary behaviours was observed as well as a significant increase in light PA intensity. No significant changes were observed for light and moderate‐to‐vigorous PA or for the percentage of time spent in moderate‐to‐vigorous PA. PA levels were also assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire‐Short version (IPAQ‐S; Craig et al. 2003). The questionnaire was completed by participants with their carer's help. No changes in IPAQ‐S scores were observed. These mixed results (i.e., some results suggesting changes in PA and other results suggesting no changes) suggest that the Take 5 intervention led to small decreases in sedentary behaviours, but changes in light‐to‐moderate‐vigorous PA are inconclusive.

Melville et al. (2015) used the accelerometer data to quantify participants' PA level, before and after the Walk Well Program, as well as at a 24‐week follow‐up. Specifically, they categorised the intensity of PA as moderate‐to‐vigorous PA or as sedentary behaviours. Results from the pre‐test to the post‐test or from baseline to the 12‐week post‐test reveal no significant within or between‐group effects for moderate‐vigorous PA and sedentary behaviours. Similarly, no within‐group effect was observed after the waitlist group completed the intervention (n = 40). These results show that the Walk Well Program was ineffective in increasing participants' PA levels.

Risk of Bias and Quality Assessment

Quality assessment and risk of bias were examined for all three types of studies (cluster‐randomised controlled trials, pre‐experimental single group pre–post designs and single‐case designs) using a design‐specific tool. Detailed results of the risk of bias or quality assessment of the individual studies are presented in a Supporting Information. Results show that only one of the eight studies, a cluster‐randomised controlled trial, was found to be at low risk of bias (Melville et al. 2015). The other cluster‐randomised controlled trial was found to have a high‐risk bias, especially due to a significant amount of missing data (75%) for the PA outcomes (van Schijndel‐Speet et al. 2017). Both pre‐experimental studies were considered to have a serious risk of bias. Two single‐case design studies were considered of acceptable quality (Krentz et al. 2016; Valbuena et al. 2019). These studies used ABAB single‐case designs with multiple participants. The remaining two single‐case designs (Stanish et al. 2001; Turgeon et al. 2024) were considered of poor quality. Interrater agreement for the risk of bias and quality assessment of studies was 91%. Specifically, agreement was reached for 214 of the 234 elements (individual items, domains and overall assessment of the study) screened. The interrater agreement for the RoB 2 CRT, ROBINS‐I and Horner et al.'s (2005) quality indicators were of 92.9%, 84.2% and 96.6%, respectively.

Discussion

The main objective of this systematic review was to synthesise the results of studies examining the effectiveness of natural setting interventions to increase PA level (e.g., frequency, duration, number of steps, intensity) in older adults (≥ 40 years old) with intellectual disabilities. The eight studies included in our systematic review resulted in a combined sample of 694 adults with intellectual disabilities (M age > 40 years) that were conducted in North America and Europe. These studies relied on various designs (i.e., pre‐experimental, cluster‐randomised control trial or single‐case) and their interventions focused on information and goal setting (Melville et al. 2011, 2015), gamification (Turgeon et al. 2024), walking (Krentz et al. 2016; Valbuena et al. 2019) and person or video guided PA sessions (Bazzano et al. 2009; Stanish et al. 2001; van Schijndel‐Speet et al. 2017).

Main Findings and Future Directions

All studies were successfully implemented in a natural setting, thus supporting the feasibility of creating and implementing PA interventions within this type of environment for older adults with intellectual disabilities. This result is especially important, given that transportation, scheduling and staff support (i.e., accompanying participants to their activities) have been consistently reported as important barriers to PA for adults with intellectual disabilities (Bowers et al. 2014; Burns et al. 2022; Mahy et al. 2010; McGarty and Melville 2018; van Schijndel‐Speet et al. 2014). Most studies showed positive changes in PA levels, but these changes were generally small. However, it is worth noting that the study with the lowest risk of bias reported no changes in participants' PA level (Melville et al. 2015), whereas interventions with similar components but with a higher risk of bias reported significant increases in light PA and decreases in sedentary behaviours (Melville et al. 2011). Such differences support the need to replicate the studies using low risk of bias studies with more rigorous designs and methodologies to better understand the effects of interventions designed to increase PA levels in older adults with intellectual disabilities. The two studies that seemed to produce the largest effect sizes for PA changes used contingent reinforcement when implementing planned 1‐h walking sessions (Krentz et al. 2016; Valbuena et al. 2019). Although the research literature supports the effectiveness of contingent reinforcement for behaviour changes of individuals with intellectual disabilities (see Cló and Dounavi 2020 for a systematic review), the sustainability of such an intervention component is questionable. Specifically, if the reinforcement is tangible, it requires consistent costs and it may be hypothesised that its effect may fade over time. Moreover, both studies used single‐case designs and only measured participants' PA levels during the planned 1‐h walking sessions. As such, the effect of the tested intervention on the overall PA level of older adults with intellectual disabilities remains unknown.

All interventions used in the studies included in our systematic review were multi‐component interventions. However, assessment of the most effective, important or efficient components remains unknown as the designs or analyses used did not allow for comparing intervention components. As an example, the use of self‐monitoring is considered an important component in behaviour change pertaining to PA (Page et al. 2020). This component is especially interesting considering that many low‐cost pedometers and accelerometers are available and easy to use. However, the data available in the included studies do not allow us to understand if this component is effective when used alone or only in combination with other components such as positive reinforcement when used with older adults with intellectual disabilities. Therefore, further research is needed to fill this gap and better understand which components should be included or combined in intervention programmes to maximise feasibility, social validity and effectiveness of PA interventions for older adults with intellectual disabilities. One way to achieve this is to implement interventions with different components and compare their effectiveness. Also, in relation to intervention components, only one study included a gamification component. The use of gamification to increase PA levels among older adults in the general population has gained scientific support and interest in the last decade as it increases participants' intrinsic motivation towards PA and can be easily implemented in a natural setting (Heston 2023). As such, we suggest that future research should further explore the potential effects of gamification on PA levels of older adults with intellectual disabilities.

Finally, a pattern observed within the results of our systematic review is that interventions with planned PA seemed to produce the most positive outcomes. In fact, the two studies that did not include planned PA sessions within their intervention reported no or very few changes in participants' PA levels (Melville et al. 2011, 2015). Specifically, Melville et al. (2015) did not find a significant change in PA outcomes, whereas Melville et al. (2011) found small changes in light PA and in sedentary behaviours. Results from the other studies seem to show more consistent positive results, while, as mentioned above, the changes reported were generally of small effect size. One potential explanation for this pattern is that planned PA may increase participants' motivation to be physically active (Park et al. 2014). As such, the inclusion of planned PA sessions should be considered as an important component when developing future interventions designed to increase PA levels among older adults with intellectual disabilities.

Strength of Evidence

Elements that contribute to the strength of the evidence presented in this systematic review include the use of objective measures to assess participants' PA and the use of longitudinal designs that included baseline as a comparison point in their analyses. However, multiple factors limit the strength of the evidence presented. First, the number (K = 8) and the quality of studies included in the present systematic review both limit the strength of the evidence. Regarding the quality of the studies, only one study was found to have a low risk of bias (Melville et al. 2015). Additionally, the interventions, populations and tools used to assess PA outcomes varied greatly between studies. Heterogeneity of the indicators selected to quantify participants' PA level (e.g., frequency, duration, intensity, step count, distance travelled) also limits the generalisation of the results. Moreover, the main objective of the present systematic review was to synthesise the effects of interventions to increase PA level in older adults with intellectual disabilities. However, rather than assessing changes in participants' overall PA levels, three of the eight studies measured a specific activity (e.g., cycling behaviours, Turgeon et al. 2024; walking behaviours, Valbuena et al. 2019) and half of the studies only measured PA during the planned PA sessions (Krentz et al. 2016; Stanish et al. 2001; Turgeon et al. 2024; Valbuena et al. 2019).

Strengths and Limitations of the Systematic Review

Our study adhered to established guidelines for conducting systematic reviews that included developing a comprehensive search strategy provided by a trained librarian and ensuring methodological rigour through an interrater agreement process at each stage of the review (Page et al. 2021). Transparency was ensured throughout the review process, allowing readers to understand the methods employed and the decisions made at each step. By documenting our procedures and rationale, including changes that occurred over the two‐year period concerning the extraction process, we ensured accountability and facilitated future replication of our study, promoting trust and reproducibility. Accordingly, one limitation of our study is that the extraction process was conducted twice with different lead reviewers for each extraction. However, it is important to note that the same second reviewer completed the interrater agreement process for both extractions. The high interrater agreement values, as evidenced by both the percentage of agreement and Cohen's κ coefficient, suggest strong reliability of the results despite the changes. Another limitation is that our study aimed to study PA interventions for older adults with intellectual disabilities. However, due to the small number of studies identified during the search process, we decreased the inclusion criteria for age from 50 to 40 years. In addition, studies were included if (a) the mean age of the participants was at least 40 years or (b) at least half of the sample was aged 40 or older, resulting in seven studies that included adults younger than 40 in their sample. It is essential for readers to interpret the findings with caution, considering potential differences in the efficacy of these interventions for those under 40 and those aged 40 and above. All studies included in this systematic review were conducted in high‐income countries across North America and Europe. Thus, the findings may not be generalisable to low‐ and middle‐income countries, where the natural environments and available resources for individuals with intellectual disabilities may differ. Lastly, the heterogeneity of the study designs included in our review presents a challenge in proposing definitive conclusions.

Conclusion

The aforementioned limitations restrict the conclusions that can be drawn from this systematic review. Although the findings from most of the individual studies support the potential of certain interventions to increase PA levels in older adults with intellectual disabilities, they can't be generalised beyond the specific settings in which the interventions were implemented. The main conclusion drawn from this systematic review is that there is a need for more and higher‐quality research to understand which components of natural setting interventions can effectively increase PA levels in older adults with intellectual disabilities. Such knowledge development is urgent, given that the life expectancy of adults with intellectual disabilities has rapidly increased over the last decades and that increasing PA levels can contribute not only to this group's physical health but also lead to other mental health, psychosocial and cognitive benefits.
