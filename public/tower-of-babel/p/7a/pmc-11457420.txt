Phenotype remodelling of HNSCC cells in the muscle invasion environment
Authors: Guozhong Zeng, Yi Shen, Wei Sun, Huanzi Lu, Yujie Liang, Jiashun Wu, Guiqing Liao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTumour invading muscle in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is often associated with destructive growth and poor prognosis. However, the phenotypic functions and pathological mechanisms of muscle-invasive cancer cells in tumour progress remains unknown. In this study, we aimed to investigate the phenotypic functions of muscle-invasive cancer cells of HNSCC and their potential crosstalk with tumour microenvironment.MethodsWe obtained scRNA-seq data (SC) from GSE103322 (N = 18) and GSE181919 (N = 37), spatial RNA-seq data (ST) from GSE208253 and GSE181300 (N = 4), transcriptomics of human HNSCC samples from GSE42743 (N = 12) and GSE41613 (N = 97). Utilizing the TCGA-HNSC dataset, we conducted univariate and multivariate Cox analyses to investigate the prognostic impact of muscle-invasion in HNSCC, with validation in an additional cohort. Through Stutility and AUCell approaches, we identified and characterized muscle-invasive cancer cell clusters, including their functional phenotypes and gene-specific profiles. Integration of SC and ST data was achieved using Seurat analysis, multimodal intersection analysis, and spatial deconvolution. The results were further validated via in vitro and in vivo experiments.ResultsOur analyses of the TCGA-HNSC cohort revealed the presence of muscle-invasion was associated with a poor prognosis. By combining ST and SC, we identified muscle-invasive cancer cells exhibiting epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and myoepithelial-like transcriptional programs, which were correlated with a poor prognosis. Furthermore, we identified G0S2 as a novel marker of muscle-invasive malignant cells that potentially promotes EMT and the acquisition of myoepithelium-like phenotypes. These findings were validated through in vitro assays and chorioallantoic membranes experiments. Additionally, we demonstrated that G0S2-overexpressing cancer cells might attract human ECs via VEGF signalling. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments revealed G0S2 plays key roles in promoting the proliferation and invasion of cancer cells.ConclusionsIn this study, we profiled the transcriptional programs of muscle-invasive HNSCC cell populations and characterized their EMT and myoepithelial-like phenotypes. Furthermore, our findings highlight the presence of muscle-invasion as a predictive marker for HNSCC patients. G0S2 as one of the markers of muscle-invasive cancer cells is involved in HNSCC intravasation, probably via VEGF signalling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-024-05607-8.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) ranks as the sixth most prevalent cancer type globally [1]. HNSCC invasion and metastasis are poorly understood and account for a majority of cancer-associated deaths [2]. Over 50% of patients exhibit locoregional recurrence within 3 years of treatment [2]. The most significant prognostic factor in HNSCC is cervical lymph node metastasis, which decreases the 5-year survival rate by 50% [3]. Currently, patients with clinical and/or radiographic evidence of lymph node metastasis undergo neck dissection at the time of surgery. However, 10–20% of these patients are metastasis free upon histologic examination [4]. Therefore, a better understanding of locoregional recurrence is needed.

The oral cavity functions as a motor organ, and the tongue is a particularly important component. Muscle contraction could stimulate tumour cell dissemination and metastases through various mechanisms such as increasing elastic modulus [5], releasing ECM-sequestered cytokine [6, 7], improving tissue stiffness [7], and inducing kinetic mechanosensing and tumour cell reprogramming [8]. Tumour cell dissemination from the primary site along intrinsic and extrinsic muscle fibres leads to diverse spreading patterns related to the orientation of the involved musculature. The peculiar configuration of the extrinsic muscles, characterized by a pronounced curvature extending from the tongue base towards the oral floor, facilitates rapid tumour progression to deeper anatomical planes [9–12]. Compared to non-invasive HNSCC, HNSCC with invasion into the extrinsic skeletal muscle of the tongue, such as tongue cancer with muscle involvement, has a 50% higher local recurrence rate and a 55% lower disease-free survival rate after 96 months, with no discernible differences in the incidence of lymphatic invasion [13]. Muscle-invasion according to patient histology is an independent prognostic parameter influencing the clinical management of various cancer types [8]. A specific study on HNSCC revealed that muscle-invasion occurred in 23.3% of cases with lymph node metastasis [4]. The positive predictive value of muscle-invasion for recurrence was found to be 43.7%, surpassing that of a depth of invasion (DOI) ≥ 3 mm (40.4%) [4]. Chandler et al. showed that the local recurrence rate was greater in the group with muscle-invasion than in the group with DOI ≥ 3 mm, although the positive predictive value of muscle-invasion in regard to nodal status was slightly less than that of DOI [4]. Another retrospective study focused on identifying the presence of pathologic muscle-invasion(pMI) and its correlation with adverse pathological features [14]. The results showed that pMI was significantly associated with increased rates of lymphovascular invasion, perineural invasion, extranodal extension, and advanced tumour stage [14]. Hence, muscle-invasion has emerged as a crucial prognostic indicator for HNSCC patients. However, adequate attention has not been given to this topic.

Muscle tissue provides a rich variety of extracellular matrix (ECM) components and associated tissue structures, including vessels and nerves, to guide invasion and reprogram invading cells. An increasing of molecular markers has been elucidated to characterize the invasive front cells (leader cells) across diverse cancer types. For instance, in breast cancer, leader cells are delineated by the expression of basal epithelial genes, including cytokeratin-14 and p63, while in thyroid cancer, evidence suggests that senescent tumor cells play a pivotal role in driving collective invasion [15, 16]. However, little work has been performed to address the specific mechanisms that underlie cancer cell invasion into muscle tissue, as muscular 3D cellular organization and ECM organization are not readily recapitulated by in vitro culture models [8]. Recent advances in SC research have enabled the exploration of genetic and functional heterogeneity at a cellular resolution [17]. However, studies often lack spatial information during tissue dissociation [18]. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) can be used to map transcriptional signatures to distinct geographical regions that are vital in disease development [19], which provides the possibility to study cells with respect to their location.

We hypothesized that muscle-invasive cancer cells acquire aggressive tumor characteristics that may impact on patient survival outcomes. In this study, we integrated SC and ST data to elucidate the phenotypic functions and transcriptional features of muscle-invasive cancer cell populations. Our analysis revealed a distinct muscle-invasive cancer cells exhibiting invasive characteristics and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and myoepithelium-like phenotypes. Subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies highlighted the crucial role of G0/G1 switch gene 2 (G0S2) in promoting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of cancer cells. G0S2+ HNSCC cells might attract ECs via VEGF signalling. These findings reveal that G0S2 is a master regulator of skeletal muscle-invasion, suggesting its potential as a promising therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Patient samples and ethical statement

237 HNSCC cases at the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) were reviewed from 2012 to 2022. We captured essential data such as age, gender, TN staging, muscle-invasion, survival duration, and status. A total of 72 cases without muscle-invasion and 165 cases with muscle-invasion were identified based on the pathologic presence of muscle-invasion (Table 2) under the assistance of two professional pathological staffs.

Paraffin-embedded HNSCC tissue were obtained from the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient prior to this study, and this study was approved by the medical ethics committee of the Hospital of Stomatology Sun Yat-sen University (approval no. # KQEC-2022-77-01). All patient studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local legislation.

Pathologic review

TCGA pathologic image was download through GDC Data Transfer Tool. Muscle-invasion is defined by the presence of unequivocal microscopic evidence of tumour invasion into adjacent skeletal muscle tissue, based on the presence of tumour cells infiltrating between skeletal muscle fibers (Fig. 1A, B) as previously described [14]. At last, we got 31 patients without muscle-invasion (Muscle−) and 187 patients with muscle-invasion (Muscle+) (Table S1).

TCGA analysis and gene-set scoring

Patient metadata and bulk RNA sequencing data of the TCGA-HNSC cohort were downloaded from the TCGA Data Portal (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/, Accessed 20 Nov. 2023.) and the NCBI GEO database (GSE42743 [20], and GSE41613 [20], https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, Accessed 25 Nov. 2023.). Samples of GSE42743 were 12 HPV-negative OSCC samples with clinical information and samples of GSE41613 were 97 HPV-negative OSCC patients with clinical information. Gene-set scoring analysis was evaluated using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) based on top 50 genes differentially expressed in the RMI in each slide respectively and in the active cluster in SC (see below) in R package GSVA (version 1.36.3). The optimal quantile cut point for RMI gene set scores to allocated patients in cohorts into high and low expression groups was determined using the surv_cutpoint function in the survminer R package (Version 0.4.9). Kaplan–meier survival plots were generated using the survfit function in the survival Rpackage (Version 3.4-0) and visualized using the ggsurvplot function in the survminer R package (Version 0.4.9) to show differences in survival time. Statistical significance was then assessed using the log-rank test.

Spatial transcriptomic data processing

ST HNSCC data was downloaded from the NCBI GEO database (GSE208253 [21] and GSE181300 [22], https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, Accessed 25 Nov. 2023.). Sections with muscle-invasion were included into further analysis. In total, four sections from separate individuals, recovering a total number of 8402 spots containing tissue sequenced to 43,648 post-normalizations mean reads per spot, were chosen based on H&E image data. Then, the selected data was converted into a Seurat object using InputFromTable (STutility) [23] for normalization, quality control, dimensionality reduction, and Louvain clustering [24] in addition to custom scripts. Spots expressing less than 200 features were excluded from downstream analysis. Normalization across spots was performed using the SCTransform function. Dimensionality reduction and clustering was performed with principal components analysis (PCA) and Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Signature scoring included epithelial (KRT14, KRT1, DMKN, EPCAM, KRT5), stromal (DCN, COL1A1, COL1A2, CD10, MME, CD31, PECAM1), muscle (ACTA1, MYL1, MYL2, MYH2), immune (CD45, PTPRC, CD3D, CD3E, TRAC, CD74, CPA3, TPSAB1, CTSG, HLADRA, HLA-DPB1) was performed using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat with default parameters. Differentially expressed genes were performed using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat with average log2FC > 0.3 and adjusted P-value < 0.05 (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a Bonferroni correction). Spatial feature expression plots were generated with the STUtility R package (version 1.0.0), ggplot2 (Version 3.4.0) and ggrepl R packages (Version 0.9.2). Pearson correlation was calculated and hierarchical clustering of ST clusters was performed in the pheatmap R package. Signature scoring derived from SC (see below) or ST RMI signatures was performed with the AddModuleScore function in Seurat with default parameters.

Single cell RNA-seq data processing

Single-Cell RNA-seq Data was downloaded from the NCBI GEO database (GSE103322 [25] and GSE181919 [26], https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, Accessed 25 Nov. 2023.). Cell-level transcripts was converted into the Seurat package for normalization, quality control, dimensionality reduction, and Louvain clustering as previously described [25, 26]. 5902 cells in GSE103322 and 54,240 cells in GSE181919 were included into further analysis according to previous description [25, 26]. GSE181919 was derived from tissue specimens of normal tissue (NL, n = 9), leukoplakia (LP, n = 4), primary cancer (CA, n = 20), and metastatic tumours in lymph nodes (LN, n = 4). The differentially expressed genes for each cluster against other clusters were determined by the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat with average log2FC > 0.5 and adjusted P-value < 0.05 (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test with a Bonferroni correction). Sub-clustering analyses were performed with the Findneighbors function of the Seurat package with proper resolutions. Identified cell clusters and sub-clusters were visualized on UMAP plots. The cell clusters were annotated based on top expressed genes in each cluster and reported canonical cellular markers.

Functional enrichment analysis

The GO, KEGG enrichment analysis, Reactome and Hallmark pathway enrichment analysis were performed using clusterProfiler [27] (version 4.6.0) R package. Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. GSVA [28] in the GSVA package (version 1.36.3) was applied to assess the relative pathway activities. LIMMA package (version 3.38.3) was used to calculate the difference in signalling pathway activity between different groups. Pathways with adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered for further analysis. The upregulated and downregulated pathways were visualized by bar plot. Also, ssGSEA performed in R package GSVA (version 1.36.3) was used to represent the degree of absolute enrichment of a gene set in each sample, including KEGG, Gene Ontology Biological Process, and Reactome pathways. P-value < 0.05 was considered as statistical significance.

Determination of cell type enrichment/depletion by MIA

Multimodal intersection analysis (MIA) was an analytical method that integrated SC expression profiles with ST data [29]. This analysis was proceeded by first delineating sets of cell type-specific and tissue region-specific genes and then determining whether their overlap was higher (enrichment) or lower (depletion) than expected by chance. In the SC data, we defined the gene sets by identifying for each cell type whose gene expression is statistically higher than that of other clusters (adjusted P-value < 0.05 and average log2FC > 0.5). For the ST data, we then identified genes with significantly higher expression in each spatial region in comparison with expression in the remaining cells (adjusted P-value < 0.05, average log2FC > 0.3, pct.1 > 0.5 and pct.2 < 0.5). After obtaining two kinds of gene sets, the significance of the overlap between ST genes and cell type marker genes was queried using the hypergeometric cumulative distribution, with all genes as the background to compute the P-value. In parallel, test was done for cell type enrichment by computing − log10(1 − P).

Deconvolution

Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) [30] is a computational method that leverages cell type profiles from SC data to decompose mixtures, such as those observed in spatial transcriptomic technologies. The major cell types obtained in the SC were used to annotate the ST by utilizing RCTD R packages as previously described [30]. The predicted cell type composition for each spot was then used to cluster the spots by the k-means algorithm. The clusters were annotated based on the average cell type prediction score across all the spots in the cluster. The cell-type deconvolution results were further submitted to SpatialFeaturePlot function in Seurat R package and ggplot2 package for visualization.

AUCell

Gene signature with 28 genes for downstream analysis were obtained from the overlap of the DEGs of two representative ST slides. Then, the gene signature was set as input data for area under the curve (AUC) value calculation. Gene-expression rankings were built for each cell according to the AUC value. The AUC estimates the proportion of genes in the gene set that are highly expressed in each cell. Cells expressing more genes from the gene set will have higher AUC values than cells expressing fewer genes. Function “AUCell_exploreThresholds” was used to calculate the threshold that could be used to consider the current signature activity. Cells were identified as active- and inactive-clusters according to the threshold of AUC value.

Transcription factor prediction

Prediction of active transcription factors was performed by the ChEA3 algorithm [31] (https://maayanlab.cloud/chea3/, Accessed 28 Nov. 2023.) based on differentially expressed genes of active cluster compared to inactive cluster in SC. Predicted transcription factors were ranked based on their average rank across all transcription factor-target gene libraries (mean rank).

Cell communication

Cellchat (version 1.1.3) [32] was used to analyze the cell-cell communication networks among each cluster of tumour cells, endothelial cells and fibroblasts. Both outgoing and incoming signals of the cell clusters were calculated and then visualized on Chord diagrams. Moreover, the most prevalent VEGF signalling pathway was selected to be further visualized among the clusters.

H&E staining and immunohistochemistry

Deidentified paraffin-embedded tissues of HNSCC and normal adjacent specimens from the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University were retrieved. Then, 4 μm thick sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated according to routine procedures. The slices were then stained with H&E. For IHC, sections were then incubated in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) and microwaved for 20 min. The sections were then blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and incubated with goat serum albumin, followed by routine staining procedures with goat serum albumin (OriGene Technologies) as previously described. G0S2 (1:500; catalog no. 12091-1-AP; proteintech) and CD13 (1:10000; catalog no. 66211-1-Ig; proteintech) was used for immunostaining. Photographs were taken by a Leica DM2M microscope (Leica Microsystems) with a digital camera. The semiquantitative assessments of tumour G0S2 and CD13 immunostaining were performed by Image analysis was performed using the IHCbox [33] plugin (Immunohistochemistry Image Analysis Toolbox) in ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence

In brief, Cal27 transfected with G0S2-overexpressing plasmid or empty control plasmid were seeded onto imaging slides (Life Science Research, Millicell, PEZGS0496) and allowed to adhere overnight. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde solution. Following a wash step with PBS solution, the cells underwent treatment with Triton-X100. Subsequently, cells were subjected to overnight incubation with anti-Actin (1:200; #C1033; Beyotime) for 1 h prior to DAPI staining. Subsequent immunofluorescence images were acquired utilizing a confocal microscopy imaging system (Olympus FluoView FV1000).

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining was performed with the tyramide signal amplification (TSA) 7-color mIHC kit (CAT#10004100010, PANOVUE) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, Sect. (4-µm thickness) obtained from paraffin-embedded samples were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to 100 °C for antigen retrieval. Then, the sections were incubated with blocking antibody at room temperature for 20 min, treated with the first primary antibody for 1 h, horseradish peroxidase conjugated (HRP) secondary antibody for 20 min, and a TSA for 15 min. After washing in PBST buffer, the slides went through EDTA (pH 9.0) buffer antigen retrieval using a microwave set at 20% of maximum power for 15 min. The same process was repeated for the following four primary antibodies. Each slide was then treated with two drops of DAPI and manually coverslipped. The following proteins were detected with PPD fluorophores: Vimentin (PPD-520), CD31(PPD-520), LYVE1(PPD-520), a-SMA (PPD-570), PanCK (PPD-620), N-cad (PPD-650), G0S2 (PPD-690). DAPI was used for nuclear counterstaining. The slides were finally mounted with antifade reagent (#AR1109, BOSTER). TissueFAXS Imaging software (v7.134) was used to capture the images and identify all markers of interest. Three to four representative fields in targeted regions of the whole-slide scan images (n = 12) were selected and quantitatively analyzed by StrataQuest software (TissueGnostics, v7.0.0). The staining and analysis results of the mIHC were also checked by 2 certified pathologists.

Cell culture and cell transfection

Cell lines including Cal27, HSC6, HSC3, SCC25, FADU, SAS, HUVEC and HLEC were obtained from the Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of Stomatology of Sun Yat-sen University. The cells were validated by STR authentication and tested regularly for Mycoplasma every month.

HNSCC cell lines (Cal27, HSC6, HSC3, SCC25, FADU and SAS) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% P/S (Gibco) at 37℃ in an incubator with 5% CO2. HUVEC were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% P/S (Gibco) at 37℃ in an incubator with 5% CO2. HLEC were cultured in Endothelial Cell medium (ECM, Gibco) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), associated growth factor and 1% P/S (Gibco) at 37℃ in an incubator with 5% CO2.

For cell transfection assay, SAS and Cal27 cells was seeded in 6-well plates for 24 h without P/S before transfection. Human G0S2 gene were silenced with the reverse-transcribed cDNA from SAS cell with siRNA (HANBIO) using Lipofectamine 3000 (lot#2378914, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Human G0S2 gene were amplified with the reverse-transcribed cDNA from Cal27 cell with plasmid (GenCopoeia) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A scrambled nontargeting siRNA and plasmid were used as an empty control. The cells were harvested 36 h after transfection. The authenticity was verified by qPCR and western blot. Primers were listed in Table S7.

RNA extraction and qRT‑PCR

FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Cat: RC112-01, Vazyme) was used to extract total RNA from Cal27, HSC6, HSC3, SCC25, FADU and SAS according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of total RNA were examined by UV spectrophotometric analysis at 260 nm. The cDNAs were synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Promega, Madison, WI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mRNA expression levels were measured using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA) and the Applied Biosystems 7900HT sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems). And mRNA relative expression level was measured using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Primers (Generay) were listed in Table S7.

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated by transwell assay with Corning Transwell Inserts (8.0 μm). For the transwell migration assay, 1 × 104 Cal27 cells, transfected with G0S2-overexpressing plasmid or empty control plasmid, were seeded in a 24-well plate after transfection. 600ul DMEM with 10% FBS was filled in the lower compartment and 200ul cell suspension was added to the upper chambers containing serum-free medium. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h in the present of 5% CO2 at 37℃. The successfully translocated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in four randomly chosen fields (200×) under a microscope. For cell migration experiments, 600ul DMEM with 10% FBS was added in the 24-well plate coated with 50 µL Matrigel (CORNING) in the center of upper chamber. After Matrigel formed into a gel, 1 × 104 cell suspended in 50 µL serum-free medium were added to the upper chambers containing serum-free medium. The cells on the lower surface were fixed, stained, and photographed microscopically after 48 h. Experiments were repeated at least three times.

For the transwell migration and coculture assay, 1 × 104 Cal27 cells, transfected with G0S2-overexpressing plasmid or empty control plasmid, were seeded in a 24-well plate after transfection. 600ul DMEM with 10% FBS was filled in the lower compartment and 200ul cell suspension with 1 × 104 HUVEC or HLEC was added to the upper chambers containing 10% FBS medium. Lower compartment contains 600ul DMEM with 10% FBS without G0S2-overexpressing plasmid cells or empty control plasmid cells as control. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h in the present of 5% CO2 at 37℃. The successfully translocated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and counted in four randomly chosen fields (200×) under a microscope. Experiments were repeated at least three times.

Sphere formation assay

Cells were seeded in ultra-low attachment 96-well plates at the indicated cell density (1000/well). 48 h later, collagen type I (Solarbio) was coated and cultivated in 37℃ for 30 min. Then, DMEM with 10% FBS was added 200µL/well, and cultured for two days in the present of 5% CO2 at 37℃. The number of spheres was counted in each well under a microscope, and the diameter of sphere was measured with ImageJ software.

Colony-formation assay

To determine the colony-formation capacity of G0S2 siRNA or plasmid transfected cells and empty control transfected cells, 500 cells for each group were seeded in the coated 6-well plate and cultured until apparent colony formation. Colonies were stained by crystal violet (Beyotime biotechnology), and the total colony numbers were counted and compared under a microscope. Each assay was repeated for 3 times.

Cell vitality assay

Cell vitality of HNSCC cell lines were evaluated using a CCK-8 assay (Dojindo). Cal27 cells, transfected with G0S2-overexpressing plasmid or empty control plasmid, were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. After culturing at 37 °C for 24 h, cells were applied with puromycin (0,2,4,6,8,10 ug/ml) and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. Then cells were treated with 10% CCK-8 for 1 h. The absorbance of each well was measured with a microplate reader at 450 nm.

CAM assay for tumour angiogenesis and intravasation

All animal experimentation adhered to institutional guidelines for ethical conduct of animal studies. The procedures were executed following the previously described methodology [34]. In summary, the chorioallantoic membranes (CAMs) of 10-day-old chick embryos were delicately accessed by creating a window in their eggshells, and subsequently, CAMs were randomly allocated into distinct experimental groups (each containing 5 eggs). Cal27 cells, transfected with either empty control plasmid or G0S2-overexpressing plasmid, were applied onto the upper CAMs. Following an incubation period at 37 °C within an egg incubator for 4 days, CAMs were excised. The CAMs were visually documented in conjunction with the tumours, and the quantification of microvessels connected to the tumours was performed. The samples were handled in a blinded manner, with the researchers conducting the experiments and assessing the outcomes being unaware of the group allocations.

Flow cytometry assay

For cell cycle analysis, cells were subjected to staining with Nuclear Dye as per the guidelines provided by the manufacturer (Cat# KTA2020; Abbkine). In summary, cells were trypsinized, centrifuged, and washed twice with cold PBS. Then, cells were fixed with 100% ethanol for 16–24 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS and re-suspended in assay buffer supplemented with Nuclear Dye and RNase A. Following a 30-minute incubation period in the dark at 37 °C, the cells were subjected to analysis by BD FACS AriaFusion (BD Biosciences). Data analysis was performed utilizing FlowJo software.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted by RIPA buffer (Beyotime) and separated by 10% SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad). Proteins were transferred to 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST containing 0.1% Tween-20, probed with primary antibodies: G0S2 (1:500; catalog no. 12091-1-AP; proteintech), VEGFA (1:1000, #sc-7269, SANTA CRUZ), VEGFC (1:1000, #22601-a-AP, proteintech) and GADPH (1:1000, lot#2301, EMAR), then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and detected with ECL reagent (Millipore). Western blot was conducted from at least 3 independent experiments using different cell line passages.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with R (version 4.2.0) and GraphPad Prism 9.5. Continuous variables with normal distribution and equal variances between binary groups were assessed using Student’s t-test; otherwise, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed. The normality assumption of datasets was evaluated using quantile–quantile (QQ) plots, while Levene’s test was used to compare variances between data sets. Continuous variables with normal distribution and equal variances among ternary or more groups were compared using one-way ANOVA; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. To assess differences in multiple overall rates or component ratios, the Chi-squared test was utilized. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare overall survival (OS) outcomes between the Muscle + and Muscle- groups. The disparity in OS between these groups was evaluated utilizing the log-rank test implemented through the “survival” and “survminer” R packages. Propensity-score matching was used to identify a cohort of patients with similar baseline characteristics. The propensity score was estimated with the use of a nonparsimonious multivariable logistic-regression mode as previously described [35], with OSCC with muscle-invasion as the dependent variable and all the baseline characteristics outlined in Table S1 as covariates. Matching was conducted using a 1:1 matching protocol without replacement, employing a greedy-matching algorithm with a caliper width set at 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. P-values were calculated for all baseline covariates both before and after matching to evaluate pre-match imbalance and post-match balance. A P-value exceeding 0.05 for a specific covariate suggests minimal imbalance. All experiments were conducted a minimum of three times to ensure reliability, and the results for continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Unless otherwise stated, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Muscle invasion is correlated with a poor prognosis in the HNSCC cohort

The TCGA-HNSC cohort was categorized into patients with muscle-invasive tumours (Muscle+, N = 187) and those with non-muscle-invasive tumours (Muscle−, N = 31) based on pathological assessment of muscle-invasion, and the baseline characteristics are listed in Table S1. The effect of bias related to variations in tumour purity was excluded, as there was no significant difference in tumour purity between the Muscle+ and Muscle− groups (Fig. S1B). Our survival analysis revealed that the Muscle+ group had worse overall survival (OS) (P < 0.05) and disease specific survival (DSS) (P < 0.05) (Fig. S1A). Patients with the presence of muscle-invasion were more likely to exhibit lymph node metastasis (P = 0.0024) and lymphovascular invasion (P = 0.0321, Table 1).

Characteristics of TCGA-HNSC patients grouped by the presence of muscle-invasion

Due to the class imbalance problem between the Muscle+ and Muscle− group in TCGA-HNSC cohort, we expanded our analysis by examining data for 237 HNSCC cases at the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University. A total of 72 patients without muscle-invasion and 165 patients with muscle-invasion were identified. We observed that the muscle-invasive group exhibited a greater propensity for lymph node metastasis (P = 0.031, Table 2). To exclude the effect of N stage on patient prognosis, propensity score matching was then performed using a 1:1 matching protocol without replacement, employing a greedy matching algorithm with a calliper width equivalent to 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score (see “Methods”). Our study ultimately included 71 patients without muscle-invasion and 71 patients with muscle-invasion (Table 2). Survival analysis of the post-matched cohorts revealed a significantly poorer prognosis among patients with muscle-invasion (Fig. 1C), which corroborates the data from our TCGA analysis. We further investigated the prognosis value of muscle-invasion in the patients with or without lymph node metastasis. The results showed that in the patients with lymph node metastasis, the presence of muscle-invasion was associated with poor prognosis (P = 0.01), whereas no difference was observed in the patients without lymph node metastasis (Fig. S1C). Moreover, in the paired HNSCC cohort, both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses indicated that muscle-invasion and N stage were independent risk factors (Fig. 1D). In addition, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of primary tumour site distribution, pathological T stage or PNI (Table 1). Overall, these data indicate that muscle-invasion is an adverse prognostic factor in HNSCC patients.

Identification of specific muscle-invasive cell populations in HNSCC using spatial transcriptomic data

To characterize the profiles of specific muscle-invasive cell populations in HNSCC using spatial transcriptomic data, we analysed two ST profiles covering 20 HNSCC samples in total (GSE208253 [21] and GSE181300 [22]). The ST data from four tissue samples involving muscle-invasion were selected for further analyses via microscopic examination of H&E-stained sections (Fig. 2A and Fig. S2A-S2C). For ST, data from each individual were processed separately. After gene expression normalization and principal component analysis (PCA) for evaluating variably expressed genes, the spots were subjected to unsupervised clustering, which yielded 11 major clusters on Slide 1, 6 clusters on s Slide 2, 9 clusters on Slide 3 and 9 clusters on Slide 4 (Fig. 2A and Fig. S2A-S2C). Furthermore, considering that each spot contained multiple cells, we proposed a signature-based strategy to evaluate the enrichment degree of various cell types in each cluster as previously described [36]. The cell type occupation and enrichment were then displayed in the feature plots by scoring spots with cell type-specific signatures (Fig. 2B and Fig. S2A-S2C, see “Methods”). Notably, Clusters 2, 5, 6, 7, and 9 in Sect. 1 predominantly exhibited higher epithelial scores, which was consistent with the histological annotations of H&E-stained sections (Fig. 2A). Conversely, Clusters 3 and 10, enriched with stromal scores, corresponded to stromal regions. Moreover, Clusters 0, 1, and 4 displayed enrichments in muscle scores and were aligned with muscle regions (Fig. 2C). We also employed the same strategy for the other slides. (Fig. S2A-C). To further validate our clustering results, we explored marker genes and performed correlation analysis (Fig. 2D and E and Table S2). According to the expression of known canonical marker genes and combined H&E spatial location, high correlations were observed in each cluster among the epithelial (Clusters 2, 5, 6, 7 and 9), stromal (Clusters 3 and 10), and muscle (Clusters 0, 1 and 4) cell populations (Fig. 2E), which further confirmed our spatial annotation. Similar results could also be found in other slides (Fig. S2D-F).

In the subsequent analysis, spots in tumour histological regions were divided into region with muscle-invasion (RMI) and region of non-muscle-invasion (Non-RMI) by experts according to spatial distance from adjacent muscle under H&E staining (Fig. 2F and see “Methods”). Further differential expressed gene (DEGs) analysis revealed that the genes upregulated in RMI included keratinization-associated genes such as KRT13 and KRT4, invasion-associated genes such as MMP 1 and MMP10, and skeleton-associated genes such as ACTA1 (Fig. 2G and Table S3), in line with our H&E findings that cancer cells in mysial interphases exhibit different morphological characteristics (Fig. 2H). These findings demonstrate that epithelial cells in RMI are cancer cells involved in muscle-invasion.

The RMI gene signature is correlated with a poor prognosis in HNSCC patients

We then explored the functions of the DEGs that were enriched in pathways associated with actin binding and myofibril and epidermal development (Fig. 3A). Gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) revealed that RMI were enriched in cell motility, ECM components, keratinization and even muscle contraction, which also suggested that the cells in RMI were malignant cells invading mysial interphases (Fig. S3A). Further gene set variation analysis (GSVA) of the transcriptional profiles of the two populations using the Reactome database revealed that RMI were mainly enriched in EMT and even myogenesis pathways (Fig. 3B), further confirming our findings. Taken together, these results indicate that cancer cells in RMI exhibit enhanced cell motility, ECM communication, and skeletal remodelling. These traits align with malignant profile, consistent with the characteristics of invasive cancer cells observed in previous studies [37, 38]. Multiple studies have provided evidence of EMT changes in invading and motile cancer cells [39]. We also evaluated the EMT of malignant HNSCC cells in mysial interphases using immunofluorescence staining (IF staining). We observed N-cad and vimentin staining in targeted tumour cells, confirming the EMT status of these cells (Fig. 3C and D).

Next, we overlapped genes upregulated in two representative ST profiles (555 genes in Slide 1 and 71 genes in Slide 2) to determine the gene signature of RMI more precisely (Fig. 3E). Finally, we obtained an RMI signature of 28 genes (Table S4). As expected, the RMI signature genes were mainly enriched in extracellular matrix organization, extracellular structure organization, cell motility and muscle contraction according to Reactome analysis (Fig. 3F). The scoring results showed that the RMI signature was significantly higher in RMI than in non-RMI, further confirming the preciseness of the RMI signature (Fig. 3G and Fig. S3B). Furthermore, we observed that the Muscle+ cohort exhibited a greater RMI signature score than did the Muscle− cohort among TCGA-HNSC patients (Fig. S3C). Next, we correlated the RMI gene signature score with HNSCC patient survival. Moreover, a high RMI signature score was associated with worse OS in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 3H). The association between the RMI gene signature and poor patient survival was further validated in two independent HNSCC cohorts (GSE42743 and GSE41613) (Fig. 3H). Collectively, these data confirmed that the RMI signature has great value in the prediction of muscle-invasion and prognosis in HNSCC patients.

Identification of muscle-invasive cell populations via SC integrated with ST

We analysed SC profiles covering 25 primary HNSCC samples and classified malignant cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, monocytic cells, mast cells, T cells, B cells and myocytes as previously described [25] (Fig. S4A). To quantify the RMI signature activity in each cell, AUCell analysis, an efficient approach to identify cells with the RMI gene signature by an evaluated threshold, was conducted, and we annotated the cells with AUCell scores exceeding the threshold as “active cluster” cells or “inactive cluster” cells (Fig. 4A).

A UMAP plot revealed that certain cells included in “active clusters” were transcriptionally closer to each other (Fig. 4B), which demonstrated similar transcriptomic features among the active cluster cells. We applied different bioinformatic approaches to further validate that the active cluster comprises cells in the RMI in the ST profiles. Firstly, the active cluster signature score (Table S5) was calculated for each ST spot in RMI and non-RMI by using the AddModuleScore function (see “Methods”) in Seurat package. The results showed that the RMI exhibited a significantly greater number of active clusters in the four-slide dataset (Fig. 4C and Fig. S4B). Second, MIA was applied to determine the corresponding relationship between the SC clusters and ST clusters. We detected that the active clusters were enriched in the RMI (Fig. 4D and Fig. S4C). Thirdly, spatial adjacency, as previously described [40] was calculated based on the deconvolution results of spatial gene expression with the SC gene expression of the major cell types (Fig. 4E, see “Methods”). The results indicate that the active cluster had the most stromal cell neighbours, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells and myocytes (Fig. 4F). Taken together, these results revealed that active cluster cells invaded mysial interphases.

Muscle-invasive cancer cells exhibit EMT and myoepithelium-like transcriptional programs

To explore the functional phenotypes of active cluster cells in SC, we compared the DEGs between the active and inactive clusters. Overall, 223 genes were upregulated in the active clusters (Table S6). The active cluster exhibited increased expression of typical mesenchymal markers, including VIM, SPARC, FN1, and ITGBI, and matrix degradation and elastases, such as MMP1, MMP2, and MMP3, as well as cytokine-associated genes, including CXCL1 and CXCL2 (Fig. 5A), indicating that the active cluster promotes cell migration and tumour metastasis. Notably, patients in the TCGA-HNSC dataset with high gene signature scores in the active cluster had poorer overall survival than patients with low signature scores (Fig. 5B), which was also confirmed in two independent GEO datasets (Fig. 5B). To further explore the functional phenotypes of active cluster cells, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) of the transcriptional profiles of the active cluster was conducted, which revealed that the active cluster was mainly enriched in EMT signalling pathways (EPITHELIAL MESENCHYMAL TRANSITION), myogenesis and other muscle-related signalling (Fig. S5A). Moreover, the gene ontology (GO) demonstrated that the DEGs were mainly enriched in cell motility and even in myofibrils, extracellular matrix organization, and extracellular structure organization (Fig. S5B). Although expression of the signature was accompanied by the expression of genes in certain muscle-related signalling pathways, we did not detect the expression of muscle-encoded genes except KCNMA1, which involved in muscle activity [41]. Thus, we explored the specific upstream transcriptional regulatory network of DEGs through the ChEA3 database [31]. Our results showed that the TF PRRX1 is a master TF that is essential for myofibroblastic lineage development (Table 3) [42]. Moreover, we detected the expression of classical EMT TFs, such as TWIST1/2 (Table 3). Thus, these findings raise the possibility that active cluster cells have undergone EMT and acquired myoepithelium-like phenotypes. Taken together, our data suggest that muscle-invasive cancer cells harbour EMT and myoepithelium-like transcriptional programs and are associated with a poor prognosis in patients.

To explore certain markers and corresponding functions of the active cluster in HNSCC, we analysed DEGs between the active and inactive clusters. We found that G0S2 was one of the top genes upregulated in the active cluster (Fig. 5A). Moreover, KEGG analysis revealed that the PPAR signalling pathway was enriched in the active cluster that targeted G0S2 gene expression (Fig. S5C), which targeted G0S2 gene expression [43]. Several critical metabolic pathways, including lipid metabolism and fatty acid metabolism, were also significantly suppressed in the active cluster (Fig. S5D). ANPEP (CD13) was also one of the top genes upregulated in the active cluster (Fig. 5A). Increased expression or activity of CD13 has been described for various tumours and generally correlates with diminished disease-free and overall survival outcomes [44]. To identify muscle-invasive cells in HNSCC, we examined the location of active cluster cells by immunohistochemistry (IHC) imaging through markers that were highly expressed in our SC datasets, including G0S2 and CD13. Overall, G0S2 was predominantly expressed in muscle-invasive HNSCC cancer cells, whereas it was nearly absent in malignant cells located in other regions (Fig. 5C). However, we detected CD13 staining in stromal and immune cells but not malignant cells in HNSCC tissue (Fig. S5E), which indicated that CD13 was an unfavourable marker. Multiplex immunohistochemical staining (mIHC) showed that in muscle-invasion areas of HNSCC samples, colocalization, defined as overlap between the two signals at the pixel level, was high for the G0S2 and PanCK markers (Fig. S5F). The above results suggested that malignant cells in mysial interphases were a subpopulation of malignant cells expressing G0S2, but it was not clear whether G0S2 was expressed in other cell subsets in the TME of HNSCC. According to the UMAP plots, G0S2 was mainly expressed in a subset of epithelial cells (Fig. 5D). These findings were also confirmed in another SC dataset (Fig. 5E and G). We also found that there were more G0S2+ cells in the CA cluster than in their NL, LP and LN counterparts. The proportion of G0S2+ cells is shown in Fig. 5F. These results suggest that G0S2 is specifically expressed in muscle-invasive HNSCC cells.

Next, we determined whether G0S2+ cancer cells undergo EMT and acquire a myoepithelium-like phenotype (Fig. 5H). mIHC revealed that G0S2+ cancer cells in mysial interphases were VIM+ and N-cad+, suggesting that G0S2+ cells were in an EMT-like state (Fig. 5H and J). In addition, whole-slide scan images (n = 12) of areas with and without tumour cell muscle-invasion were captured to determine whether G0S2+ cancer cells exhibited a myoepithelium-like (a-SMA+) phenotype. Quantitative analyses using StrataQuest software (TissueGenostics) demonstrated that the proportions of both G0S2+, α-SMA+ and PanCK+ cells were more significantly enriched in muscle-invasion areas (Fig. 5K), showing that G0S2+ malignant cells located in mysial interphases have undergone myoepithelium-like transition. Taken together, our analysis identified G0S2 as a novel marker for distinguishing muscle-invasive malignant cells, which may promote EMT changes and the acquisition of myoepithelium-like phenotypes.

G0S2 promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of cancer cells in vitro and in vivo

Next, we investigated whether the expression of G0S2 has a critical function in HNSCC cells in vitro. We first detected the expression of G0S2 in a panel of six HNSCC cell lines. qPCR and western blot (WB) revealed that the G0S2 expression was greater in the SAS cell line than in other cell lines (Cal27, SCC25, HSC3, HSC6, and FADU) (Fig. 6A and Fig. S6A). To elucidate the role of G0S2 in HNSCC cells, we utilized specific siRNA to reduce G0S2 expression in SAS cells, a cell lines with high endogenous G0S2 expression (Fig. S6B). Meanwhile, we overexpressed G0S2 in Cal27 cells, a cell line characterized by lower G0S2 expression levels (Fig. S6B). The transfection efficiency of G0S2-overexpressing plasmid and G0S2 siRNA was verified through WB and qPCR (Fig. 7I and Fig. S6B). Previous results have suggested that the G0S2+ cluster acquires EMT phenotypes in SC. Consistently, G0S2-induced morphological changes and actin filament organization in HNSCC cells were analyzed by phalloidin staining. Consistently, G0S2-overexpressing Cal27 cells exhibited a spindle-shaped morphology reminiscent of a transition similar to that observed in mesenchymal cells (Fig. 6B). In addition, using colony formation assay and CCK8 assay, we found that overexpression of G0S2 dramatically increased colony formation, and puromycin resistance (Fig. 6C and D). In contrast, G0S2 knockdown significantly decreased colony formation (Fig. S6C). Moreover, flow cytometry assays revealed that G0S2-overexpressing promoted the proliferation of Cal27 cells, as evidenced by the significant increase in the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase (Fig. 6G). The effects of G0S2 on the invasion ability of HNSCC cells were evaluated using transwell and 3D sphere invasion assays, and G0S2-overexpressing substantially stimulated the migration and invasion of Cal27 cells (Fig. 6E F and Fig. 6H). In contrast, G0S2 knockdown significantly decreased the migration and invasion ability of SAS cells (Fig. S6D-F).

To assess the impact of G0S2 on HNSCC invasion in vivo, we injected G0S2-overexpressing plasmid-expressing cells or empty control plasmid-expressing Cal27 cells into a chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) angiogenesis and invasion model (Fig. 6I and J). Significant increases in blood vessel density and tumour circumference were observed in the G0S2-overexpressing plasmid CAM group compared with the control CAM group (Fig. 6J). These data suggested that G0S2 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of cancer cells.

The interaction between G0S2+ cancer cells and endothelial cells

Given the observed spatial adjacency between active cluster cells and endothelial cells (Fig. 4F), the Muscle+ cohort exhibited a greater propensity for lymphovascular invasion in HNSCC patients (Tables 1 and 2). mIHC also showed that in HNSCC samples, the relative proportions of blood vessels and lymphatic vessels were greater in the muscle-invasion regions than non-muscle-invasion regions (Fig. 7A and B). The CAM assay additionally demonstrated a significant increase in blood vessel density in the G0S2-overexpressing CAM group compared to the control group (Fig. 6J). Thus, we reasoned that adjacency in spatial location would facilitate crosstalk; thus, we analysed how cell–cell communication occurs. We first subclustered the endothelial cells into lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) and vascular endothelial cells (VECs) (Fig. 7C and D and Fig. S7A-B). Among the ligand‒receptor pairs pertaining to ECs, active cluster cells exhibited strong potential interactions with LECs and VECs via the VEGF signalling network (Fig. 7E). To model the cell-to-cell communication between G0S2+ cancer cells and ECs, we first performed transwell migration assays and coculture experiments using HUVECs and HLECs along with empty control Cal27 and G0S2-overexpressing Cal27 cells (Fig. 7F), as previously described [45]. The results demonstrated that Cal27 transfected with G0S2-overexpressing plasmid significantly attracted HUVECs and HLECs compared with empty control plasmid-transfected Cal27 cells (Fig. 7G and H). Moreover, Cal27 cells transfected with the G0S2-overexpressing plasmid had higher VEGFA and VEGFC levels than the control cells when these cells were cocultured with HUVECs and HLECs (Fig. 7I). Conversely, G0S2 silenced SAS cells had lower VEGFA and VEGFC levels than the control cells when cocultured with HUVECs and HLECs (Fig. 7I). Taken together, these data suggest that G0S2+ active cluster cells might attract ECs via VEGF signalling to trigger HNSCC metastasis.

Discussion

Mysial interphases in smooth and skeletal muscle tissue guide the invasion of cancer cells [8], yet little work has been performed to address the mechanisms that underlie cancer invasion into muscle tissue due to the lack of a proper model. Through ST analysis, we aimed to first identify markers of muscle-invasive cancer cells in HNSCC. We also aimed to explore the mechanism underlying muscle-invasion by conducting functional enrichment analysis of genes upregulated in these cells. In this study, we combined ST and SC to identify muscle-invasive cancer cells in HNSCC and investigated their transcriptomic features. We revealed that muscle-invasive cancer cells featured with promoted cell motility, enhanced ECM communication, and remodelled cellular cytoskeleton through in vitro and in vivo assays (Fig. 8). This study also revealed that G0S2 is a master regulator of skeletal muscle-invasion, suggesting its potential role as a therapeutic target.

Single-cell resolution transcriptomic technology has revealed previously unknown cell types and provided new insights into cellular heterogeneity and the tissue microenvironment [46]. However, a notable limitation of single-cell sequencing is the loss of spatial information for cells due to tissue dissociation, impeding the study of cellular interactions between tumours and their surrounding components. To address this limitation, spatially resolved transcriptomics, which is capable of preserving the spatial positioning of transcript molecules, was developed. This advancement allows for a more comprehensive understanding of complex tissues, such as the TME, facilitating a deeper exploration of disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. For spatial domain detection methods, a recent benchmarking study [47] evaluated 13 computational techniques based on criteria such as accuracy, spatial continuity, marker gene detection, scalability, and robustness and indicated that no single method was universally effective across all datasets and that the optimal method depends on the data characteristics. To overcome the limitations of single spatial transcriptomics approaches, in this study, we used three bioinformatic approaches, including Seurat analysis, MIA and RCTD, and integrated SC and ST to make the cluster annotation more convincing, which enables researchers to perform comprehensive and unbiased tissue analysis [29]. Through the integration of SC and ST, we observed that muscle-invasive malignant cells underwent EMT and exhibited myoepithelium-like phenotypes (Figs. 3C and D and 5H). Researchers may explore multiple methods to select the one that best aligns with the characteristics of their data.

G0S2 was first identified in lymphocytes during the drug-induced transition from the G0 to G1 phase of the cell cycle, associating the protein with cell-cycle regulation [48]; this protein is associated with cell cycle regulation and functions to bind lipid metabolism directly to inhibit triglyceride hydrolase activity by influencing lipid metabolism [49]. G0S2 is silenced by gene promoter methylation in a variety of solid tumours and has been associated with terminal differentiation, cell cycle withdrawal, quiescence, and apoptosis [50, 51]. However, studies have also shown that the overexpression of G0S2 can promote EMT and cell proliferation [52]. Thus, the function of G0S2 remains elusive. In this study, we found that G0S2-overexpressing promoted cell cycle progression from the G0 to the G1 phase(Fig. 6G), as previously described [48]. Moreover, we also found that the overexpression of G0S2 can promote the proliferation, drug resistance, migration and invasion of Cal27 cells both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 6C and J). In addition, a coculture assay demonstrated that G0S2-overexpressing cells attract ECs and may promote HNSCC metastasis (Fig. 7G and H); however, the underlying mechanism requires further research. In the human invasive breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231, G0S2 can promote cell survival and metastasis through integrin-mediated Hippo signalling [53]. However, this study revealed no difference in the mRNA levels of LATS1, YAP1, and CYR61 through qPCR or p-YAP and YAP1 through WB in G0S2-overexpressing Cal27 cells (Fig. S7C-D). Thus, the signal transduction pathways involved in muscle-invasion in HNSCC remain elusive. One key ECM protein, cysteine-rich angiogenic inducer 61 (CCN1/CYR61), is a key growth factor mediating the migration of smooth muscle cells and is induced and secreted by a variety of normal cell types (such as fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and neurons) as well as tumours [54]. In metastatic prostate cancer, CCN1 secreted by tumour cells induces smooth muscle dedifferentiation and responds to the biomechanical forces inherent in smooth muscle [54–56]. Therefore, it is necessary to further investigate whether tumour cells in OSCC secrete chemotactic factors that lead to muscle cell dysfunction, muscle cell atrophy, and loss of muscle barrier function [57].

The distant metastasis rate of HNSCC with muscle-invasion is high. Using mIHC, we observed a greater density of lymphatic vessels and blood vessels in the periphery of mysial interphases (Fig. 7A and B), potentially facilitating the distant metastasis of cancer cells infiltrating muscle fibres. Cellular interactions are also crucial in HNSCC. Neighboring cells such as ECs facilitate dissemination. In this study, we performed CellChat analysis on SC and revealed that the VEGF pathway is highly activated between the G0S2 + active cluster and ECs (Fig. 7E). Transwell assays revealed that Cal27 cells overexpressing G0S2 demonstrated a greater ability to attract HUVECs and HLECs compared to Cal27 cells transfected with the empty control plasmid (Figure 7G and H). Additionally, in vivo CAM model experiments corroborated these findings, demonstrating a notable augmentation in microvessel density in Cal27 cells transfected with G0S2-overexpressing plasmids than their control counterparts (Fig. 6J). Malignant cells have been widely reported to promote cancer metastasis by regulating cellular signalling via VEGF [58]. Our experiments demonstrated that G0S2-overexpressing Cal27 cells had higher VEGFA and VEGFC levels than did empty control plasmid-transfected cells under HUVEC and HLEC coculture conditions (Fig. 7I).

This study has several limitations. First, there were insufficient tissue samples in the ST analysis, which makes it difficult to obtain information on the shared characteristics of muscle-invasive cancer cells. The second limitation is the limited patient history data, which limits the generalizability of our conclusions. Third, in vivo experimental models, such as animal models, require further optimization. However, we also leveraged the CAM assay to validate the enhanced invasiveness of cells overexpressing G0S2. The fourth limitation is that we utilized only six cell lines in our study, which is insufficient for comprehensive research on HNSCC. Future research can pay attention to diverse research models, such as organoids, multicomponent live-cell culture (“organ-on-a-chip”) [59], tensile strain models [60], 3D culture of differentiated myogenic cells isolated from native tissue [61], to investigate muscle-invasive cancer cells more effectively.

Conclusions

Our study unveiled muscle-invasive cancer cell populations in HNSCC through integrating single-cell profile and spatial transcriptomes, and identified G0S2 plays key roles in muscle-invasion and serves as predictive markers for patients. Mechanistically, G0S2+ cancer cells might attract endothelial cells to enhance tumour intravasation via VEGF signalling (Fig. 8). Targeting these events may have implications in developing novel therapies to inhibit the initiation of HNSCC metastasis.
