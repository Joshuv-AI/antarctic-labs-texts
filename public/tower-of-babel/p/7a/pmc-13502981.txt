A Geospatial Study of Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY) Empaneled Healthcare Facilities across South Indian States
Authors: Christiana Amarthaluri, K. Balasubramani, Lekha D. Bhat, Manimozhi Selvaraj, Neena E. Philip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Context:To advance universal health coverage (UHC) in India, several healthcare reforms were introduced, including the Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY). The scheme provides secondary and tertiary inpatient care to eligible below-poverty-line and vulnerable populations through empaneled public and private hospitals.Aims:To examine the spatial distribution of PM-JAY empaneled hospitals and assess its alignment with equitable healthcare access.Methods:Open-access data on PM-JAY empaneled hospitals in South Indian states were retrieved from National Health Authority’s (NHA) Hospital Empanelment Module (HEM) in January 2022. Data were cleaned and analyzed using SPSS, R, and ArcGIS. Distance correlation and Spearman’s correlational analyses were performed between hospital-density variables and development indicators (Human Development Index [HDI], literacy rates) at 95% confidence interval.Results:A total of 8,946 hospitals were empaneled across South India. Public hospitals constituted 52.9% and private facilities 47.1%, with inter-state variations. Private facilities dominated in Kerala, while Karnataka had the highest public share. Distance correlation (dCor) and Spearman’s correlation analyses showed significant associations (P < 0.05) between private hospitals’ availability and development indices. Associations with literacy rates were significantly stronger for private hospitals per million population (dCor = 0.451) and per sq. km (dCor = 0.430). Geospatial analysis revealed a scattered distribution of public hospitals, while private facilities were clustered, specifically in urban pockets.Conclusion:The observed distribution patterns suggest state-specific policies for healthcare advancements. Critical gaps identified in private hospitals’ distribution need attention to mitigate challenges in underserved areas. Overall, a balanced and well-regulated mix of both sectors may be most effective in ensuring equitable and comprehensive healthcare access.

INTRODUCTION

In India, with a population of over 1.3 billion, healthcare is challenging. The country’s healthcare system includes service provision by both public and private care.[1] Over the years, though progress has been achieved in health outcomes[2], the mixed system faces persistent limitations related to affordability, accessibility, and accountability.[3] Consequently, India has implemented many reforms in its healthcare system with universal health coverage (UHC) as a guiding principle.

The launch of Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY) by the Indian government in 2018 marks one such milestone reform. The scheme aimed at marginalized sections of society, offering secondary and tertiary hospitalization care by providing beneficiaries a package of services worth INR 5 lakhs per family per year, with no cap on age, gender, or family size. Families that fall under the defined criteria of the Socio-Economic Caste Census (SECC), 2011 database, are eligible to claim the scheme’s benefits.[4]

One of the key aims of the scheme is to enhance the availability and choice of healthcare facilities for the beneficiaries.[5] Hence, the scheme empanels both public and private facilities with portability benefits. All the public facilities with inpatient provisions are automatically deemed empaneled under the scheme. Private hospitals’ empanelment is carried out by the State Health Agencies (SHAs) of respective states, in accordance with the guidelines set by the National Health Authority (NHA).[6] Within the insurance framework, empanelment is a central structural element[7] that underpins population health management by enabling health systems to improve patient experience, reduce costs, and enhance health outcomes.[89]

Given its significance, our study examined the empanelment pattern of PM-JAY hospitals at the state and district levels across the southern states of India. While PM-JAY primarily targets the vulnerable populations, examining relatively better-performing states in terms of health indices and assessing the empanelment pattern in these states can provide insights into more optimized empanelment patterns and highlight structural factors that may inform implementation in low-performing regions. Despite the expansion of healthcare infrastructure, significant disparities persist in the spatial distribution of healthcare facilities, particularly in access to secondary and tertiary care. Evidence on the geographical distribution of PM-JAY hospitals remains limited, especially at the district level using geospatial approaches. Our study made a geospatial comparison of the population density across the districts with the density of hospitals. The study also assessed the correlation between hospital availability [expressed as hospitals available per million population and per square kilometer (sq. km)] and development indicators [Human Development Index (HDI) and literacy rates]. The inclusion of these indicators was intended to examine whether the distribution of empaneled hospitals align with broader developmental indicators at the district level. Since HDI captures health, education, and income dimensions, it serves as a proxy for overall development, which may influence both availability and utilization of healthcare services. This approach helps to understand whether relatively developed districts tend to have better availability of empaneled facilities.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Research design

The study employed a secondary analysis of cross-sectional data and an innovative mapping approach to examine the distribution of empaneled healthcare facilities under the scheme. For the present study, South Indian states (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, and the Union Territory (UT) of Puducherry) were selected. Before bifurcation, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana were a single state.

Data retrieval

Data on empaneled hospitals for the present study were obtained from the official web portal of the NHA[10] in January 2022. This apex body updates the details of empaneled healthcare facilities (available to beneficiaries under PM-JAY) on the Hospital Empanelment Module (HEM) platform, and these details are revised every year in March.

Initially retrieved study data had information on hospital name, type (public/private-for-benefit/private-not-for-benefit), address, specialties empaneled, specialties upgraded, state and district of ministry hospital (a hospital run by a ministry of the central government and not state, like Railway hospitals, ECIS hospitals), hospital e-mail, hospital contact, hospital sub-type, and empanelment type. Relevant variables were selected to create a Microsoft Excel database with additional information on the state and district of hospitals, retrieved from the hospital addresses provided. Public facilities were further classified into three categories: (a) General/District/Medical college hospitals/Ministry hospitals, (b) Taluk headquarters hospitals/Area hospitals, and (c) Primary health centers (PHCs) and Community health centers (CHCs). While PM-JAY primarily targets secondary and tertiary care facilities, we included primary-level facilities in the analysis to reflect the actual empanelment landscape and to highlight deviations from the intended policy design. The locations of empaneled facilities were extracted using a location query in Google Maps. The hospital name and address were used to retrieve location coordinates (x and y), corresponding to longitude and latitude, respectively.

Data cleaning

The initial dataset contained 11,053 hospitals. In the HEM platform, each South Indian state was entered separately in the search query. However, some search results (n = 12) that included hospitals from other states (Ex: Shri Siddheshwar Cancer hospital and research Centre that is in Solapur, Maharashtra, was included in Karnataka), were filtered out. Also, some of the hospitals (n = 8) that were found to have inaccurate data were excluded (Ex: Hospital names were fdafdsaf, jbhjkj, fdsafdsf with corresponding addresses 1-5-55, xyz, Jagitial, Telangana, 1-5-55, xyz, Hyderabad, Telangana, and 1-5-55, xyz, Hyderabad, Telangana, respectively). The hospitals with duplicate records/coordinates (n = 1,890) and not classified under PM-JAY (only CGHS empaneled; n = 197) were also excluded. Post exclusions, the PM-JAY empaneled hospitals were 8,946 in number and taken in for statistical analyses. A total of 8,946 hospitals were included for geospatial assessment as well [Supplementary File 1].

Statistical analysis

A state and district-level analyses of all empaneled healthcare facilities in South India were conducted using the statistical tools, SPSS version 16.0, and R programming software version 4.6.0 (for distance correlation [dCor] analysis); the results were tabulated. Empaneled hospitals’ availability per one million population in a district was calculated by normalizing the hospital count in a district with its population; hospitals’ availability per sq. km area in a district was calculated by normalizing the hospital count with the district area. For normalization, the study considered projected population estimates for 2020 for each district; the data were retrieved from the Harvard Dataverse [Supplementary File 2]. Six normalized variables were obtained in total including a) Total empaneled hospitals available per one million population, b) Empaneled public hospitals available per one million population, c) Empaneled private hospitals available per one million population, d) Total empaneled hospitals available per sq. km., e) Empaneled public hospitals available per sq. km., and f) Empaneled private hospitals available per sq. km. The association of six variables with HDI and literacy rates was assessed using dCor and Spearman’s correlation analyses. Before the analyses, all the variables were tested for normality and were found to violate the assumption. Hospital-density variables associated with per million population were corrected for skewness when log-transformed; however, the skewness persisted in the hospital-density variables associated with area (availability per sq. km) even after log and square root transformations. Because several hospital-density variables remained non-normally distributed despite transformation, and the scatter plots [depicted in Supplementary Figure 1] suggested non-monotonic associations, Spearman’s rank correlation alone was considered insufficient. Therefore, dCor analysis was additionally performed, as it detects both linear and non-linear dependence without assuming monotonic relationships. The correlation analysis in this study does not simply examine distribution patterns in isolation. Rather, it assesses the relationship between the availability of empaneled hospitals and selected district-level developmental indicators—HDI and literacy rate. The data sources for the parameters were tabulated and presented in Supplementary File 2. Additionally, sensitivity analysis excluding primary-level facilities (PHCs and CHCs) were also performed.

Geospatial analysis

The longitude and latitude values of empaneled facilities, extracted from Google Maps, were converted to decimal coordinates (x and y) to prepare a spatial database. Google Maps enables manual verification and disambiguation of hospital identities using facility names together with available address details; this was considered important for maximizing match completeness and hence, used in the study. All relevant attributes of empaneled facilities were added to the spatial database using ArcGIS software, version 10.4.1 using the geographic coordinate system with the datum of World Geodetic System (WGS) 1984. The district-wise population data extracted from the Harvard Dataverse were also added to the spatial database and used for spatial analysis and mapping of empaneled healthcare facilities. The Kernel density tool was used to estimate the density of the hospital networks i.e. the number of hospitals per square kilometer (sq. km). The study applied environment settings of the tool with the output cell size of 5 km. The search radius (bandwidth) was computed using a spatial variant of Silverman’s Rule of Thumb, based on the spatial configuration of input points, and set as 75 km. For the present study, thematic maps (dot distribution and choropleth) were created for overlay analysis and interpretation.

Ethical consideration

This study was based on secondary analysis of publicly available data obtained from the official portal of the NHA, Government of India. The dataset comprises information on empaneled healthcare facilities and does not include any individual-level or personally identifiable data.

Although the study does not involve human participants directly, ethical principles of responsible data use, transparency, and accurate representation were strictly adhered to throughout the research process. Data were used solely for academic and research purposes, and no attempt was made to identify or link information to specific individuals or sensitive entities.

As per institutional and national guidelines for research involving publicly available, non-identifiable data, formal ethical approval was not required for this study.

RESULTS

The scheme has empaneled both public and private healthcare facilities. As of January 2022, 8,946 hospitals were empaneled under PM-JAY in the South Indian states. Government General/District/Medical college hospitals/Ministry hospitals [n = 471, (5.3%)], Taluk headquarters hospitals/Area hospitals [n = 491, (5.5%)], CHCs, PHCs, and other govt. hospitals [n = 3,772, (42.2%)] made up the public facilities. Private hospitals included private for-profit [n = 4,032, (45.1%)] and private not-for-profit hospitals [n = 180, (2.0%)] [Table 1]. Across South India, the overall proportion of private facilities [n = 4,212 (47.1%)] was slightly lower than that of public [n = 4,734, (52.9%)], with differences varying across the states. However, when stratified and compared, the proportion of private for-profit hospitals was more (45.1%) [Table 1].

Karnataka had the highest number of PM-JAY empaneled hospitals [n = 3,621, (40.5%)], followed by Tamil Nadu [n = 2,682, (30.0%)] and Andhra Pradesh [n = 1,287, (14.4%)] [Table 1]. The proportion of public hospitals was the highest in Karnataka (79.6%), followed by Andhra Pradesh (53.1%); in the rest of the states/UT, private facilities were higher. Kerala had a high share of private hospitals (74.3%), followed by Tamil Nadu (71.2%) and Telangana (66.0%). Together Andhra Pradesh and Telangana had 52.8% of private hospitals [Table 1].

The availability of PM-JAY empaneled hospitals per million population was found more in Karnataka (n = 52), followed by Tamil Nadu (n = 32) and Andhra Pradesh (n = 23). In Southern India, the average number of hospitals available per million population was 25 [Table 1].

More than half (63.4%) of the total facilities empaneled [private: 33.3%, public: 66.7%] had no specialty services. Hospitals offering one specialty, 2-5, 6-10, and ≥11 specialties accounted for 6.7%, 12.7%, 9.3%, and 7.9%, respectively. Nearly three-quarters (~76%) of facilities offering ≥11 specialties belonged to the private sector [Supplementary File 3].

While all other states accounted for the highest proportion of facilities with no specialty services, Kerala was the only state with a minimal proportion of hospitals (3.6%) with no specialty services. It also had the highest proportion of facilities with ≥11 specialties (20.6%). Hospitals offering 2–5 specialties were more common in Kerala (30.5%). In others states, hospitals offering 2–5 specialties represented the second highest category, while first in Kerala. The detailed distribution of hospitals by district is provided in Supplementary File 3.

At the district-level, dCor analysis was conducted between the six hospital-density variables and development indicators (HDI and literacy rates). The analysis showed significant associations (P < 0.05) between the all six variables and development indicators. The associations could be linear or non-linear (at 95% CI). The dCor values between HDI and total, public and private hospitals available per million population were 0.319 (P = 0.001), 0.238 (P = 0.016), and 0.319 (P = 0.002), respectively. Similar associations were observed between hospitals’ availability per million population and literacy rates with dCor of 0.342 (P = 0.001) for total, 0.211 (P = 0.037) for public and 0.451 (P = 0.001) for private hospitals. The analysis showed a relatively strong association between private hospital availability per million population with literacy rates [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, and Karnataka are among the best-performing states for the health index, ranking 1,2,3,4, and 9, respectively, in India in 2019-2020.[11] Assessing the distribution of PM-JAY empaneled hospitals among these states revealed a slightly higher proportion of public facilities (52.9%) than private facilities (47.1%), with differences varying across the states. As the empanelment of hospitals is a state subject, the distribution of public and private hospitals across the states varies. Kerala and Tamil Nadu had >70% of share by the private sector, whereas public facilities were predominant in Karnataka (~80%). Collectively, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana also had >50% share of private healthcare facilities.

In any state, the empanelment of private hospitals is governed by the strength of the state’s public health infrastructure and its ability to provide different types of treatment.[5] Kerala and Tamil Nadu’s higher private hospital empanelment reflects the acknowledgment of gaps in their public sector’s capacity to provide specialized and advanced care. Kerala created its healthcare infrastructure in such a way that made basic services accessible at the community level. It also expanded primary health care coverage improving access to a range of preventive and curative services.[12] Nevertheless, over the years, besides improvement in socioeconomic conditions,[13] the failure of public institutions to manage overcrowding, long waiting times, resource scarcity and other prevailing issues led to a compromise in quality of care at public facilities.[13] Similarly, in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, factors such as inadequate funding, poorly functioning referral systems resulting in overcrowding, and insufficient monitoring of the quality and quantity of services in public institutions, along with the state’s focus on promoting facilities for multinational industries, enabled the private sector to strengthen its dominance.[14] The sensitivity analysis of the study also revealed scheme’s inclination towards privatization in all these states.

Karnataka’s large share of public hospitals maybe attributed to an earlier ongoing insurance scheme, Arogya Karnataka (later AB-PMJAY Arogya Karnataka),[15] that promoted and reserved medical procedures (classified as simple secondary/2A procedures) exclusively for public facilities, while private hospitals serve as referrals for more complex secondary and tertiary procedures[15] and were mainly involved in specialties like cardiology, oncology, and orthopedics.[5] Also, under Arogya Karnataka, only district hospitals could refer patients to private facilities. Since there is only one district hospital per district, travelling all along to the district hospital to get a private referral makes it cumbersome for patients,[15] making them prefer public facilities. Even in the sensitivity analysis, though the proportion of private hospitals is more, Karnataka had the highest percentage of public hospitals as compared with other states, reflecting a public-centered orientation in the state.

Whilst the Rural Health Statistics (RHS), 2020-21 reported significant constraints including workforce scarcity, poor infrastructure and lack of equipment and medicines at the primary care level,[16] our study showed that ~42% of the public facilities empaneled were PHCs/CHCs/other government facilities. However, as empanelment in PM-JAY is based on standard criteria, facilities empanelment at the primary care level may have driven their improvements.[7]

Also, the scheme was designed to increase the availability and accessibility of hospitals across the country. In Southern India, our analysis showed an average of 25 empaneled hospitals available per million population, which was lower than national average 30 reported by Joseph et al.[7] The shortfall could be due to the differences in population selection methodology. While the present study used a normalized population for calculations, Joseph et al.[7] used the average family size from the 2011 SECC report to manually estimate the number of eligible beneficiaries.

Besides overall availability, the distribution of hospitals in the state serves as an indicator of service availability under the scheme.[17] In South Indian states, many facilities offered 2–5 specialties, and nearly three-quarters (~76%) of facilities offering 11 or more specialties were in the private sector. Evidence from earlier studies also suggests that private hospitals play a crucial role by delivering specialized services that are often unavailable in public healthcare.[18] However, private hospitals are making a smaller contribution to extending the geographic reach of PM-JAY to underserved areas.

The study’s statistical and geospatial analysis also infer the unevenness in the distribution pattern. The findings of correlational analyses indicate the availability of PM-JAY empaneled healthcare facilities are associated with development indices including HDI and literacy rates. The dCor captured broader and more consistent relationships compared to Spearman’s correlation. As Spearman’s correlation measures only monotonic relationships (either increase or decrease), the analysis showed weaker and less consistent associations for some variables. Also, the contrasting differences between some variables (e.g., public hospitals showing negative Spearman’s rho with HDI but positive dCor) across both the analyses suggest that several associations between hospital availability and developmental indicators may be nonlinear or non-monotonic in nature [as shown in Supplementary Figure 1] that were captured by dCor but not fully identified by Spearman’s correlation. Overall, both the analyses consistently showed a strong association of private hospitals’ density with development indicators, while public hospitals’ association with them appeared comparatively weaker and less consistent.

The Kernel density analysis also revealed that spatial skewness is more pronounced in the distribution pattern of private hospitals. As depicted in Figure 1, private hospitals were concentrated more in urban pockets. In a country where nearly three-fourths of the population live in villages,[19] the clustered distribution limits the extent of the scheme’s geographic reach to underserved areas.[72021] The concentration of private facilities in selected locations creates additional barriers, such as out-of-pocket expenses, geographical distance, travel time, and so on for those in uncovered areas.[22] Studies have shown that geographical access to healthcare facilities is one of the factors contributing that help minimize health inequalities.[2324]

Among the private, a scattered pattern of private not-for-profit hospitals (—facilities that do not make profits for their owners from the funds collected for patient services)[25] was particularly observed in Kerala, suggesting that such institutions may play a complementary role alongside the public healthcare system in expanding healthcare service availability. While this pattern is particularly evident in Kerala, it may not be entirely unique. In regions with limited public healthcare infrastructure, not-for-profit private providers may also play a complementary role in service delivery. However, the extent to which this model can be generalized depends on the historical development of the health system, regulatory environment, and the presence of a strong not-for-profit sector.

While the concentration of private empaneled hospitals in urban areas may limit geographic access, it is also important to recognize that empanelment patterns are largely influenced by the availability of eligible healthcare facilities, particularly in the private sector. Private hospitals are more likely to be established in urban areas due to better infrastructure, a larger workforce, and greater financial viability. Under PM-JAY, the minimum criterion for a private hospital to be empaneled is to have a bed strength of at least 10. This being the case, only 3% of private hospitals in the country are eligible to be empaneled under the scheme.[26]

In contrast, in rural areas, public sector hospitals continue to play a critical role as the primary providers of free healthcare services under the scheme. The observed maldistribution maybe attributed to factors such as urban concentration of private facilities, disparities in public health infrastructure, and uneven investment patterns across districts. Therefore, the observed distribution reflects broader health system characteristics and structural constraints, rather than the scheme’s design alone. A balanced interpretation of access must consider the complementary roles of both public and private providers in different settings.

CONCLUSION

The study focused on analyzing the spatial distribution of PM-JAY empaneled hospitals across the South Indian states. The top-ranking states in health indices were spanned across the empanelment spectrum with varied proportions of public and private facilities. At the extreme ends were Kerala and Karnataka—the former with a predominance of private empanelment and the latter with a stronger reliance on public empanelment. This contrast suggests that high health system performance can be achieved through different institutional pathways. The implication is that there is no single optimal model; rather, policy approaches should be context-specific. States may focus on strengthening regulatory frameworks and quality assurance where private participation is high, or on expanding capacity and efficiency in the public sector where government provision dominates. Overall, a balanced and well-regulated mix of both sectors may be most effective in ensuring equitable and comprehensive healthcare access.

The geospatial analysis highlighted significant gaps in the distribution of private hospitals, which may contribute to inequitable healthcare access in the underserved areas, underscoring the need for targeted, region-based policy interventions to address inequities in healthcare access. This also calls for allocation of resources and infrastructure development of the healthcare facilities already located in the underserved areas to mitigate the challenges.

Limitations

The study assessed only the number of specialities offered but did not provide a detailed differentiation between specialty and super-specialty services in relation to empanelment, which could be explored in future research. Also, the topographical challenges influencing hospital empanelment were not examined. Additionally, for geospatial analysis, the use of Google maps to retrieve coordinates involved manual verification; this may reduce reproducibility as compared with automated geocoding tools. Also, precise location coordinates were unavailable for a few hospitals (~2%); in such cases, approximate coordinates within the hospital’s locality were used, which may have introduced minor spatial inaccuracies. For the locations to which approximations were assigned, an administrative-unit tolerance error was applied. As the study objective was focused on broader distributional patterns, rather than facility-level accessibility estimation, minor locational uncertainty was considered unlikely to substantially alter the study findings. The study also acknowledges the limitation of the absence of utilization data (—empanelment is not equivalent to service delivery or access). The study did not investigate inter-district migration or cross-border patient flows. The use of district as the unit of analysis might obscure intra-district rural–urban heterogeneity; and the study could not distinguish between supply-driven and demand-driven clustering of private facilities.

Key messages

The study highlighted the distribution pattern of PM-JAY empaneled hospitals revealing the urban concentration of private facilities and calls for policy implications towards a balanced approach that optimizes healthcare access. While the presence of public hospitals in less developed regions reflects an encouraging expansion of healthcare access, further strengthening of workforce capacity, infrastructure, and advanced healthcare services could enhance their ability to deliver comprehensive care. Also, while primary healthcare services are essential for addressing basic health needs, access to specialized care is equally important for managing complex health conditions and providing comprehensive healthcare services. The inter-state disparity in the distribution of public and private hospitals, such as the inverse spatial relationship in Kerala suggests tailor-made and state-specific strategies for improved health systems.
