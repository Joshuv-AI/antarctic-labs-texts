MMP8 mediates CD115 cleavage-dependent elimination of large peritoneal macrophages in response to microbial exposure
Authors: Shilpi Giri, Andrew T. Martin, Alexandra Safronova, Sophia I. Eliseeva, Sara Ahmed, Samantha F. Kwok, Felix Yarovinsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMacrophages, critical for tissue homeostasis and immune regulation, undergo significant changes during microbial infections. Certain inflammatory conditions can trigger the loss of these macrophages through a process called macrophage disappearance reaction (MDR), which, in turn, affects the ongoing immune response. Here, we show that the presence of bacteria in the peritoneal cavity, which can be mimicked by Toll-like receptor (TLR) stimulation, leads to loss of cell surface CD115, also known as macrophage colony-stimulating factor receptor (M-CSFR). This loss renders macrophages unresponsive to M-CSF signaling and induces their disappearance. MMP8, an enzyme released by macrophages in response to LPS, was responsible for CD115 cleavage and its subsequent loss from the cell surface during MDR, establishing a biochemical mechanism for this phenomenon. Our findings extended to human cells, highlighting the role of MMP8 in CD115 shedding. This study enhances our understanding of MDR initiation and proposes a regulatory mechanism for inflammatory responses by restricting essential growth factors.

INTRODUCTION

Macrophages play crucial roles in maintaining tissue homeostasis and regulating immune responses, influencing both the initiation and resolution of inflammation.1–3 Microbial infections induce significant changes in macrophage populations, marked by the recruitment of monocytes to the inflammatory site, where they execute host defense programs and differentiate into monocyte-derived macrophages.4,5 There is a well-recognized yet incompletely understood host defense mechanism involving the loss of tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs), particularly well characterized in the peritoneal cavity, known as macrophage disappearance reactions (MDRs).6–8 The MDR is characterized by the disappearance of the peritoneal tissue-resident peritoneal macrophages, commonly referred to as large peritoneal macrophages (LPMs), in response to microbial infections.9,10 While this response can be triggered by bacterial LPS or yeast-derived zymosan, the molecular events leading to MDR remain incompletely understood. Recent progress in the field revealed several key elements seen during MDRs, including the rapid migration of LPMs into the omentum, cellular adhesion, macrophage-induced coagulation, and metabolic death of these macrophages due to mTOR inactivation.11,12 Nevertheless, how innate immune sensing of pathogens initiates MDRs is incompletely understood. This information is crucial, as the LPMs are embryonically seeded long-lived cells that are maintained via a macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF)-dependent mechanism,13,14 and their disappearance leads to major perturbation in the composition of immune cells in the peritoneal cavity. In addition to microbial ligands, several cytokines, including IFN-γ, can induce MDRs, leading to significant alterations in the composition of peritoneal macrophages.7,15 The distribution of inflammatory cells within the peritoneal cavity is highly dynamic, influenced by complex interactions between various mediators and target cell populations in response to specific stimuli.16,17 While the MDR is widely recognized as a key component of macrophage activation and reprogramming, critical for effective immune responses, its precise role in shaping immune dynamics remains an area of active investigation. The reaction is used to detect delayed hypersensitivity to antigens, such as those from Yersinia pestis, demonstrating its utility in evaluating immune responses post-immunization.

In this study, we aimed to decipher the requirements for triggering MDRs and to identify the molecular mechanism responsible for the loss of LPMs. Employing microbial infections and purified Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists, we discovered that the loss of LPMs is mediated by the shedding of CD115, a receptor for the essential growth factor M-CSF. Furthermore, we identified MMP8 as a key enzyme released by macrophages, leading to the cleavage of CD115. Overall, the performed experiments revealed a biochemical mechanism responsible for the MDR. Our results also suggest that distinct dependence on M-CSF regulates macrophage subsets at the site of infection, revealing a growth-factor-dependent innate immune defense pathway.

RESULTS

Microbial infection causes loss of CD115

The MDR occurs in response to infectious or inflammatory stimuli. This response involves the loss of peritoneal TRMs, commonly known as LPMs and defined as CD11b+F4/80+ or CD11b+CD102+ MHCIIlow cells (Figures 1 and S1), due to their migration, tissue adherence, and cell death.7 Additionally, CCR2+ monocytes rapidly migrate to the peritoneal cavity, contributing to the replacement of TRMs by SPMs.16 While the presence of CCL2 and other chemokines is sufficient for the recruitment of CCR2+ monocytes,18,19 how the initial microbial exposure leads to the MDR is not fully understood. Utilizing microbiologically sterile germ-free mice, we first introduced normal intestinal bacteria via the natural oral route or experimental intraperitoneal injection. We aimed to answer the question of whether the presence of microbes in adult mice is sufficient to trigger MDRs. Our experiments revealed that the oral route of microbial exposure failed to induce the loss of LPMs detected as CD11bhiF4/80hi or the recruitment of SPMs (CD11bloF4/80lo)9 to the peritoneal cavity (Figure 1A). In contrast, peritoneal injection of the same bacteria resulted in the selective elimination of LPMs (Figure 1A), reminiscent of the host’s response to pathogenic bacterium S. typhimurium (Figure 1B). The disappearance of LPMs caused by S. typhimurium infection coincided with the loss of the cell surface receptor CD115 (Figure 1C), the receptor for the essential TRM survival growth factor M-CSF.13,14 We also observed accumulation of the soluble extracellular portion of CD115 in the peritoneal cavity (Figure 1D), strongly suggesting that bacterial infection triggered the shedding of CD115. This event played a central role in the loss of LPMs, as direct inhibition of CD115 proved sufficient for both LPM loss and SPM accumulation within 2 days post-experimental treatment (Figure 1E). We also noted that the loss of LPMs, caused by microbial stimuli or blocking CD115, coincided with an increased frequency and absolute numbers of monocyte-derived SPMs (Figures 1A, 1B, and 1E).

Overall, our results demonstrated that the presence of microbes is insufficient to trigger the MDR. Instead, the MDR occurs in response to the insult caused by peritoneal exposure to bacteria.

TLR stimulation is sufficient for the loss of CD115

Bacterial infections are commonly detected through activation of TLRs, which recognize distinct components of bacteria, such as LPS, CpG, and flagellin.20–22 To determine whether TLR signaling is capable of initiating MDRs through CD115 shedding, wild-type (WT) mice were intraperitoneally injected with a single dose of LPS, CpG, or flagellin, activating TLR4, TLR9, and TLR5, respectively. The performed experiments revealed that all tested TLR agonists were sufficient to trigger the loss of cell surface CD115, strongly indicating that TLR activation is capable of triggering major changes in the composition of peritoneal macrophages (Figure 2A).

Crucially, attempts to block TNF or IFN-γ, major cytokines downstream of TLR-dependent pathogen recognition and known initiators of MDRs,15,23 did not prevent the loss of CD115 (Figure 2B). Considering previous reports implicating TNF-converting enzyme (TACE) in CD115 cleavage,24 we also investigated whether this enzyme was responsible for LPS-induced CD115 cleavage (Figure S2). However, we observed that peritoneal macrophages lost cell surface expression of CD115 regardless of the presence of TAPI-2, a potent TACE inhibitor (Figure S2A). These observations suggest that TLR activation is sufficient for triggering MDRs through mechanisms distinct from TNF- and IFN-γ-induced CD115 shedding.

LPS-induced MMP8 cleaves CD115

To elucidate the mechanism of TLR-induced CD115 shedding, we employed an in vitro system using RAW cells and peritoneal macrophages stimulated with LPS. Our experiments revealed that LPS treatment in vitro resulted in the disappearance of full-length CD115 in RAW cells, similar to peritoneal macrophages (Figures 3A and 3B). Notably, the presence of EDTA led to the retention of CD115 in LPS-stimulated cells (data not shown), prompting us to hypothesize the involvement of Ca2+-dependent matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) in CD115 cleavage. To test our hypothesis, we first examined the effect of the pan MMP-inhibitor Batimastat (BB94)25 on LPS-induced CD115 cleavage. We observed that the MMP inhibitor prevented LPS-induced CD115 cleavage and the release of its extracellular portion, implicating MMPs in CD115 shedding in response to LPS (Figures 3B and 3C). Similar results were obtained with another MMP inhibitor, marimastat (Figure S2B).

MMPs are categorized into several major classes based on their domain structure and substrate preferences, including collagenases (MMPs 1, 8, and 13), stromelysins (MMPs 3, 10, and 11), gelatinases (MMPs 2 and 9), matrilysins (MMPs 7 and 26), and membrane-type MMPs (MT-MMPs).26 Notably, while inhibition of MMP8 prevented LPS-induced CD115 cleavage (Figure S3A), other tested inhibitors failed to do so (Figure S3B).

We also noted that while multiple MMPs can be detected in the cell lysate of peritoneal macrophages (Figure 3D), we could only detect MMP8 in the cell culture supernatants collected from LPS-treated macrophages (Figure 3D). Importantly, the supernatant collected from LPS-treated macrophages was sufficient to induce MMP8-dependent CD115 cleavage in Tlr4−/− macrophages (Figure 3E). We used Tlr4−/− macrophages as responder cells to avoid secondary stimulation of macrophages with LPS retained from the initial stimulation.

Finally, we observed that genetic targeting of MMP8 prevented the loss of LPMs in response to LPS stimulation (Figure 4A). This preservation of LPMs coincided with the retention of CD115 expression in Mmp8−/− mice, whereas WT mice exhibited a significant reduction in CD115 levels on the surface of LPMs (Figure 4B). These findings formally establish MMP8 as a key mediator of MDRs. Furthermore, repetitive LPS injections, which mimic persistent infection or repeated exposure to bacterial pathogens, resulted in acute mortality in Mmp8−/− mice, whereas WT mice survived this treatment (Figure 4C). This difference suggests that the MMP-8-dependent MDR, mediated via CD115 loss, is essential for regulating macrophage responses to microbial insult.

We also explored whether the mechanism of CD115 shedding applies to human myeloid cells, as CSF1R is highly conserved across vertebrate species.27 To investigate this possibility, we examined the effects of LPS and MMP inhibitors on CD115 cell surface expression in human CD14+ monocytes isolated from peripheral blood (Figure 5). Similar to the mouse system, LPS stimulation led to loss of CD115 cell surface expression. Furthermore, the presence of pan-MMP or MMP8 inhibitors prevented the loss of cell surface CD115 in response to LPS stimulation (Figure 5). These experiments revealed that, similar to the mouse system, TLR4 stimulation resulted in MMP8-dependent cleavage of CD115.

DISCUSSION

Overall, the performed experiments established that TLR-mediated detection of microbial infection is sufficient to trigger MDRs characterized by loss of TRMs. Importantly, the location of bacteria, rather than their pathogenicity, determined the initiation of MDRs, suggesting that the loss of TRMs is an integral part of immune responses when barrier function is compromised. Our results revealed a mechanism contributing to the replacement of TRMs in the peritoneal cavity that was mediated by the MMP8-mediated cleavage of CD115, a receptor for M-CSF.

Peritoneal macrophages constitute approximately 40% of the immune cell population residing within the peritoneal cavity of mice.10,28 Fate-mapping experiments have established the distinct origins of macrophage subsets, revealing that LPMs are embryonically derived cells that not only represent a major population but are also maintained by self-proliferation throughout the lifespan of mice.1,29–31 Additional experiments have formally established that bone-marrow-derived monocytes play no role in the generation of LPMs. Instead, monocyte-derived macrophages constitute a separate population of peritoneal macrophages known as SPMs.9,32,33 While both of these populations are stable under homeostatic conditions, aging in mice, even without notable exposure to infections, leads to a partial replacement of LPMs by SPMs. These SPMs may acquire some phenotypic functions of embryonically derived macrophages, but the overall functional consequences of replacing LPMs with SPMs are poorly understood. This knowledge is crucial, as peritoneal macrophages play a central role in immune surveillance, and their functionality contributes to both physiological and pathological processes during immune responses.

The results of this study revealed that elimination of LPMs is sufficient to promote the recruitment of SPMs, although the precise molecular events regulating this process were not examined in the current study. While it is tempting to speculate that LPM loss alone is sufficient to drive monocyte recruitment to the peritoneal cavity, our recent experiments using oral infection with the common parasite T. gondii demonstrated that LPM loss and SPM recruitment can be uncoupled, particularly when the location of the microbial insult is distinct from the site of IFN-γ-mediated LPM loss.15 Taken together, these findings suggest that additional inflammatory signals or specific mechanisms underlying LPM disappearance play a critical role in regulating the replacement of embryonically seeded macrophages.

A classical responsiveness of peritoneal macrophages to inflammatory stimuli is known as MDRs and is characterized by the rapid loss of LPMs. This complex and dynamic response, triggered by inflammatory signals, is characterized by a series of dynamic changes in macrophage phenotype and function. Several key, non-mutually exclusive mechanisms have been proposed to explain the loss of LPMs, as this event is central to MDRs. Among these are the rapid migration of LPMs to the omentum, increased macrophage adhesiveness to the peritoneal wall, and trapping of LPMs within the coagulation cascade.11,12 Our recent studies identified that the Th1 cytokine IFN-γ triggers MDRs via the induction of LPM death caused by mTOR inactivation, resulting from the shedding of CD115, a receptor for the essential growth factor M-CSF.15 The current study advances previous findings by revealing that MMP8 mediates the replacement of LPMs due to CD115 shedding and a subsequent loss of responsiveness to M-CSF.

Importantly, this process played a host-protective role in the model of repetitive LPS injection, suggesting that the replacement of LPMs is a host defense mechanism that is, in part, regulated by MMP8—partly through the cleavage of CD115.

Our results not only established a biochemical mechanism contributing to MDRs but also suggested that the availability of the growth factors, in particular M-CSF, may provide a mechanism for the regulation of inflammation via the regulation of macrophage subsets present at the site of infection.

Limitations of the study

The present study is not without limitations. While our in vitro and in vivo findings provide valuable insights into the mechanism of MDRs caused by CD115 shedding and the subsequent loss of responsiveness to M-CSF by LPMs, our results do not rule out other mechanisms of MDRs triggered by pathogens or purified microbial TLR ligands. It remains to be further investigated whether there is a universal mechanism underlying the loss of embryonically seeded macrophages in response to insults or whether MDR is the outcome of multiple independent mechanisms involved in sensing microbial infections. The kinetics of MDRs caused by the loss of CD115 was not investigated in the current experiments, and the long-term effects of LPS-induced loss were also not examined. Our experiments with human cells were limited to in vitro studies, and future work is essential to determine whether the observed mechanism is translatable to in vivo systems. Lastly, the experiments performed did not address the beneficial or detrimental consequences of MDRs in the context of bacterial infections or LPS-induced CD115 loss.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice

C57BL/6 mice were originally purchased from the Jackson Laboratories and maintained in the pathogen-free animal facility at the University of Rochester of Medicine and Dentistry. Mmp8−/− (005514) mice were purchased from the Jackson laboratory. CSF1R-YFP mice were developed by crossing CSF1R Cre (Jax # 029206) with ROSA-EYFP (Jax # 006148).

All animal experimentation in this study was reviewed and approved by the University of Rochester’s University Committee on Animal Resources (UCAR), the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Cell line

RAW 264.7 cells (ATCC, TIB-71) were used in this study. Cell cultures were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. Cell line authentication was not performed beyond verification by ATCC.

METHOD DETAILS

Cell culture and treatments

Peritoneal exudate cells (PECs) were isolated via lavage with a 10 mL syringe containing PBS supplemented with 0.5% EDTA. To enrich the resident peritoneal macrophages with the minimum invasion procedures, PECs were allowed to adhere in a tissue culture grade 48 well plate for 2 h at 37°C. Samples were washed with complete cell culture RMPI media supplemented with 10% of FBS to remove non-adherent cells. RAW 264.7 cells (ATCC # TIB-71) were cultured in complete RPMI media supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were plated in a 6-well/24-well plate and treated with 10 ng/mL LPS (O111:B4, Sigma # 2630), 5 μM Batimastat (Sigma # SML0041) or 5 μM Marimastat (Sigma #M2699) for 3 h at 37°C. MMP-8 inhibitor I (Santa Cruz # sc-311436), MMP 2 inhibitor, MMP9 inhibitor (Sigma # 444293), MMP13 inhibitor (Sigma, Cat # 444283) were used at 10 μM, 50 μM and 100 μM concentrations.

Human blood samples

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from heparinized peripheral blood of deidentified healthy volunteers by Ficoll-Paque density-gradient centrifugation. All donors provided informed consent in accordance with guidelines approved by the University of Rochester Institutional Review Board. Both freshly purified PBMCs and those cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen were used for the study. Cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed in warm complete media (RPMI 1640, 10% FBS, penicillin-streptomycin, l-glutamine) in preparation for in vitro stimulation. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Human primary monocytes were subsequently isolated from PBMCs using CD14 magnetic microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Microbial infections

Mice were infected intraperitoneally with Salmonella enterica serovar typhimurium as previously described (15). Briefly, mice were infected once with 2*105 CFUs intraperitoneally. Mice were weighed every day after infection to assess the progression of infection.

To mimic intraperitoneal infection, 20 mg of fresh fecal pellets were collected from WT B6 mice and dissolved in 10 mL of PBS. The slurry was then centrifuged to settle the particulates. Subsequently, 200 μL of the clear supernatant was injected intraperitoneally.

TLR ligand and antibody treatment in vivo

WT C57BL/6 mice of 18–20 g were injected ip with LPS (100 μg) (O111:B4), CpG1826 (50 μg) and flagellin (50 μg). In some experiments. mice were treated with a single intraperitoneal dose of 300 μg of anti-CSF1R mAb (Bioxcell # BE0213).

Flow cytometric analysis

Anti-mouse CD11b-APCCy7 (101225), F4/80-BV785 (123141), CD102-AF647 (105612), CD115-PE (135505), CD115-BV421 (135513), MHCII- PECy7 (107630), Ly6C- BV421 128031, Ly6G-percp cy 5.5 127616, CD19-FITC 152404 were obtained from Biolegend. Anti mouse CD102-AF488 (53–1021-82), MHCII PerCP-eF710 (46–5320-80), F4/80-APC (17–4801-82) were purchased from eBioscience. CD19-BUV37 (612781) was obtained from BD Bioscience. Cellular fluorescence was measured using an LSRII Fortessa flow cytometer, and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

ELISA analysis

Soluble CD115 (CSF-1-R) was measured by standard sandwich ELISA kit per the manufacturer’s instructions (Abcam).

Western blotting

Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes using Trans-Blot SD Semi-Dry Transfer Cell BioRad, Hercules, CA at standard condition 25V for 30 min at room temperature. Membranes were blocked for 45 min at room temperature in 10 mM Tris-Cl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) containing 5% BSA and then incubated with a 1:200 dilution of CSF-1R/M-CSF-R Antibody (CST # 3152) or beta-Actin (CST# 13E5) overnight at 4°C with gentle rocking. Blots were then washed five times for 5 min in TBS-T. Membranes were then incubated for 30 min with HRP conjugated anti-rabbit (eBioscience, Cat# 18881633) 1:10,000 in TBS-T and washed as before. Reactive proteins were visualized by using a SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat # 34580).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All the statistical analysis was performed using Prism (Version 9.4.1; GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). p values were calculated using unpaired Student’s t test or ANOVA. Data are presented as Mean ± SD, if indicated otherwise. Statistical significance was represented as: not significant (NS): p > 0.05, *: p ≤ 0.05, **: p ≤ 0.01, ***: p ≤ 0.001, and ****: p ≤ 0.0001.
