A retrospective analysis of the promoting effect of kinesio taping on the rehabilitation of military personnel with recurrent shoulder dislocation caused by training injury
Authors: Tingting Xia, Hongxu Liu, Ran Man, Shougang Li, Shaohui Geng, Ling Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKinesio taping, as a non-invasive, non-pharmacological elastic taping technique, has become increasingly popular in sports injury management due to its safety, non-restrictive nature, and lack of interference with joint movement. This study aims to evaluate whether conventional comprehensive rehabilitation combined with kinesio taping can more effectively improve rehabilitation outcomes in military personnel following surgery for recurrent shoulder dislocation caused by training injuries.MethodsSixty military personnel with recurrent shoulder dislocation caused by training injury were selected and randomly divided into a study group and a control group, with 30 cases in each group.The control group received conventional rehabilitation therapy, including physical agent modalities (ultrasound), cryotherapy and exercise therapy. The study group received, in addition to all treatments administered to the control group, shoulder-specific kinesio taping (using I-strip, Y-strip, and fan-cut strips applied over the supraspinatus muscle, deltoid muscle, and glenohumeral joint area, changed daily). After 6 weeks of treatment, shoulder joint range of motion, UCLA(The university of California at Los Angeles shoulder rating scale)score, and Constant-Murley score were compared between the two groups.ResultsAfter treatment, the range of motion for shoulder flexion, abduction, internal rotation, external rotation, and extension increased in both groups compared to pre-treatment values (P < 0.05). Similarly, the UCLA and Constant-Murley scores also improved significantly in both groups (P < 0.05). The study group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in shoulder joint mobility (P < 0.05), UCLA score (P < 0.05), and Constant-Murley score (P < 0.05) compared to the control group.ConclusionConventional comprehensive rehabilitation therapy combined with kinesio taping can better improve the range of motion and activities of daily living in postoperative military personnel with recurrent shoulder dislocation caused by training injury.Keywords: Kinesio taping, Recurrent shoulder dislocation, Postoperative rehabilitation

Introduction

The shoulder joint, as the ball-and-socket joint with the greatest range of motion in the human body, possesses excellent mobility which also predisposes it anatomically to instability and dislocation [1]. Recurrent dislocation is a common and severe sports injury. Its primary high-risk groups include athletes involved in contact sports, as well as individuals engaged in heavy physical labour—such as construction workers and movers—who frequently have their shoulders subjected to high loads, making them prone to repeated dislocations. Especially among military personnel, due to the immense and complex stresses placed on the shoulder joint during daily training (such as tactical maneuvers, throwing, combat, horizontal/parallel bar exercises, and load-bearing high-intensity activities), recurrent shoulder dislocations caused by training injuries are particularly prevalent. This not only severely affects the individual health and training performance of service members but also poses a challenge to the overall combat readiness of the unit. Arthroscopic Bankart repair aims to reconstruct the stability of the labral-ligamentous complex and has achieved favorable clinical outcomes [2, 3].

However, the postoperative rehabilitation phase often faces a series of complications that directly impact the process and quality of functional recovery. Common issues include: early postoperative pain and inflammatory edema, which can inhibit muscle activation and restrict early movement [4, 5]; postoperative stiffness and adhesion of the joint capsule and soft tissues, leading to restricted range of motion; inhibition, atrophy, and decreased coordination of the rotator cuff and periscapular muscles, impairing dynamic stability; and the loss of joint proprioception due to the disruption from injury and surgery, affecting the precision of neuromuscular control [6, 7]. These complications—pain, muscle inhibition, stiffness, loss of proprioception, and dynamic instability—interact with each other, forming a vicious cycle that hinders functional recovery [8, 9].

Taking the treatment protocol used for the control group in this study as an example, conventional comprehensive postoperative rehabilitation typically involves a phased, multimodal intervention. The early phase focuses on controlling inflammation and pain (e.g., ultrasound, cryotherapy), gradually introducing joint mobilization to improve accessory joint motions, and performing passive and active-assisted range of motion exercises to maintain and increase joint mobility, while combining soft tissue release and specific muscle isometric contractions to preserve neuromuscular control. As tissue healing progresses into the middle and late stages, the rehabilitation focus shifts towards strengthening the rotator cuff muscles, scapular stabilisers, and deltoid muscles, aiming to rebuild dynamic stability and coordinated movement patterns of the shoulder joint through progressive resistance and functional training. However, although conventional protocols include strength and range of motion training, they often lack standardized, high-intensity, repetitive, and functionally integrated training modules specifically targeting the re-education of neuromuscular control and proprioception [3, 10]. This precise element is central to preventing re-dislocation and restoring high-demand military function.

Against this backdrop, kinesio taping, as a non-invasive, non-pharmacological elastic taping technique, has become increasingly popular in sports injury management due to its safety, non-restrictive nature, and lack of interference with joint movement [11]. Its theoretical mechanisms include: Firstly, through its elastic recoil lifting the skin, it can increase the interstitial space in the subcutaneous tissue, promoting lymphatic drainage and blood circulation, thereby assisting in reducing postoperative edema and alleviating pain and movement restrictions caused by swelling [12]. Secondly, the continuous, gentle stretch applied by the tape to skin mechanoreceptors can provide rich proprioceptive input [13]. This additional sensory feedback is believed to partially “substitute for” or “amplify” the joint proprioceptive signals weakened by injury, aiding in the central nervous system’s reconstruction of spatial perception of the shoulder joint. This, in turn, may promote more coordinated and timely muscle recruitment patterns, thereby improving dynamic stability. Furthermore, taping applied along specific muscle orientations (e.g., providing support for the supraspinatus and deltoid muscles) may help modulate fascial tension, assist in alleviating muscle inhibition, and promote the activation of target muscle groups, creating better neuromuscular conditions for strength training [13]. The compound taping protocol used in this study, involving combined application targeting the supraspinatus muscle (I-strip), the glenohumeral joint area (fan cut), and the deltoid muscle (Y-strip), aims to synergistically address multiple aspects including pain management, circulatory improvement, muscle function modulation, and proprioceptive enhancement, with the goal of optimizing the overall effect of conventional rehabilitation.

In recent years, kinesio taping has played an active role in managing various musculoskeletal disorders. For instance, it has been shown to aid in pain relief and early functional improvement following total knee arthroplasty and has demonstrated potential in enhancing proprioception after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [14, 15]. For the shoulder joint, kinesio taping has also proven effective in treating subacromial impingement syndrome, alleviating shoulder pain, and improving scapular dyskinesia [16]. However, a significant evidence gap exists regarding its application in the specific context of postoperative rehabilitation for recurrent shoulder instability caused by military training injuries.

Therefore, this study aims, through retrospective analysis, to systematically evaluate the impact of combining kinesio taping with conventional comprehensive rehabilitation therapy on postoperative functional recovery in patients with recurrent shoulder dislocation caused by military training injuries. The goal is to clarify whether kinesio taping can truly translate its theoretical advantages into measurable clinical gains superior to conventional rehabilitation in this specific scenario. We hypothesize that the combined intervention can more effectively improve patients’ shoulder range of motion and functional scores, and holds positive significance for optimizing the rehabilitation process.

Objects and methods

Study participants

This study is a single-centre, retrospective cohort study that included 60 postoperative patients with recurrent shoulder dislocation admitted to our department from May 2023 to May 2024, with 30 patients in each group (study and control). All patients were active-duty military personnel who had suffered unilateral recurrent shoulder dislocations due to training-induced injuries and had undergone arthroscopic shoulder labral repair and capsular tightening surgery in the Orthopedics Department of our hospital. Rehabilitation treatment in our department commenced 2 weeks postoperatively and lasted 6 weeks. This timing aligns with the standard rehabilitation pathway following arthroscopic labral repair, aiming to protect early tissue healing and minimize the risk of early postoperative complications [17, 18]. This study was approved by our hospital’s ethics committee, and all patients provided informed consent. Inclusion criteria: (1) Met the diagnostic criteria for recurrent shoulder dislocation; (2) Stable vital signs postoperatively without infections or other complications; (3) Able to combine conventional physical therapy, functional rehabilitation training, and kinesio taping. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients with peripheral structural fractures or nerve injuries; (2) Patients with severe organ injury or cardiovascular/cerebrovascular diseases; (3) Patients with postoperative infection or other complications; (4) Those allergic to kinesio tape, unable to tolerate the treatment, or who withdrew and could not complete the full course.

Treatment

Control group: conventional comprehensive rehabilitation therapy

(1) Physical modality therapy: Details ultrasound and cold therapy. Ultrasound therapy: continuous mode, intensity 0.8–1.2 W/cm², once daily, 15 min per session. Ultrasound therapy was administered in continuous mode, utilizing its thermal and non-thermal effects to promote tissue repair and alleviate stiffness [19]. Cold therapy: Ice pack application for 15 min after exercise to control inflammatory edema [20]. (2) Exercise rehabilitation [21]: (a) Joint mobilization: The therapist performed anterior-posterior glides and end-range mobilizations for theglenohumeral and acromioclavicular joints. (b) Passive exercise: The therapist performed passive movements, including flexion, abduction, and extension of the shoulder joint. (c) Soft tissue mobilization: Gentle mobilization of the patient’s shoulder joint capsule, pectoralis major, pectoralis minor, deltoid, and rotator cuff muscles. (d) Active exercise: Fist clenching, pendulum exercises, shoulder girdle isometric exercises, deltoid submaximal isometric contractions. At six weeks post-surgery, shoulder girdle strength training commenced (rowing exercises, shrugging exercises, chest exercises, wall push-ups), along with active resistance exercises using elastic bands. The above exercises were performed in sets of 10 repetitions, 3 sets per day.

Study group: conventional rehabilitation plus kinesio taping

The study group received kinesio taping in addition to all interventions provided to the control group. Based on the principles of enhancing postoperative shoulder joint stability, promoting local circulation, and strengthening proprioception, we adopted a composite taping strategy [22–24]. Blue tape( LI-NING AXWV089-1, Li-Ning Co.) with uniform elasticity and thickness was used, cut into I-shape, Y-shape, and lantern-shape strips. The tape was applied with light to moderate tension of approximately 10–15%, aiming to provide continuous cutaneous sensory stimulation without restricting joint movement [11]. Taping was performed by the same certified rehabilitation therapist. Patients were seated with the shoulder joint in a relaxed, neutral position, and the skin was clean and dry. The specific taping procedures were as follows:

An I-shape strip (for the supraspinatus muscle): Applied along the belly of the supraspinatus muscle, from the greater tubercle of the humerus towards its proximal origin at the scapular spine. As a key component of the rotator cuff crucial for the initial phase of shoulder abduction, the supraspinatus is prone to inhibition and atrophy post-surgery. This taping method aims to promote neuromuscular activation and assist in modulating muscle tension through continuous, low-intensity mechanical stimulation [25, 26].

A Y-shape strip (for the deltoid muscle): The forked end of the “Y” faced proximally, with the base anchored at the deltoid tuberosity. The two tails were wrapped and taped towards the lateral third of the clavicle and the scapular spine, respectively. The deltoid, the primary prime mover for shoulder motion, often exhibits dysfunction postoperatively due to pain inhibition or compensatory patterns. The direction of pull applied by the tape aligns with the deltoid’s contraction direction. It aims to assist muscle contraction during shoulder movement through the tape’s elastic recoil, especially in the early stages of insufficient strength, thereby promoting muscle activation, enhancing neural drive, and improving shoulder control during multi-planar movements [27, 28].

A Lantern-shape strip (for the glenohumeral joint area): The tape was cut into a lantern shape—narrow in the middle and wider at both ends. The midpoint was fixed with slight stretch on the anterolateral aspect of the glenohumeral joint. The two wings extended proximally and distally along the upper arm’s longitudinal axis, vertically covering the joint area. As both ends served as anchor points, the stabilisation effect was optimal. The specially cut middle section of the tape helped maintain muscle tone and promote circulation and metabolism, potentially reducing the accumulation of local inflammatory factors, alleviating soft-tissue compression, improving postoperative soft-tissue swelling, and relieving localised pain [29–31] (Fig. 1).

The tape was replaced daily and remained in place until the next rehabilitation session. Both groups received the same duration per treatment session and the same total treatment course.

Outcome measures

Shoulder joint range of motion

Including flexion, abduction, internal rotation, external rotation, and extension; flexion, abduction, extension was measured with the patient seated; internal and external rotation were measured in the neutral position. A standard universal goniometer was used for all measurements, which were performed by the same experienced physical therapist in our department before and after treatment.

UCLA score

The UCLA (University of California, Los Angeles) shoulder rating scale ranges from 0 to 35 points, with higher scores indicating better shoulder function and patient satisfaction [32]. The UCLA scores were assessed by the same trained physician in our department guiding the patients to complete the questionnaires before and after treatment.

Constant-murley score

The Constant-Murley shoulder score is a comprehensive assessment tool encompassing pain (15 points), activities of daily living (20 points), active range of motion (40 points), and muscle strength (25 points), totaling 100 points. A higher score represents better overall shoulder function. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity and is widely used in clinical practice and research [33]. The Constant-Murley scores were assessed by the same trained physician in our department guiding the patients to complete the questionnaires before and after treatment.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed that all measurement data conformed to a normal distribution; hence, they are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (). Within-group comparisons were made using the paired t-test, and between-group comparisons were made using the independent samples t-test. Count data were analyzed using the chi-square test. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study investigated the application of kinesio taping combined with conventional rehabilitation in postoperative patients with recurrent shoulder dislocation due to military training injuries through a retrospective cohort analysis. The results showed that the combined intervention led to more significant improvements in patients’ shoulder range of motion and functional scores. This indicates that kinesio taping, as an adjunctive measure, has a clear augmentative effect on conventional rehabilitation protocols. This finding not only validates the research hypothesis but also provides a new synergistic therapeutic approach for postoperative rehabilitation in this special population. From a broader clinical perspective, this finding suggests that the targeted integration of non-invasive adjunctive techniques within conventional rehabilitation frameworks could become a feasible pathway to optimize postoperative functional outcomes, particularly for enhancing rehabilitation quality in high-demand patients.

The findings of this study are generally consistent with the overall trend of results from studies applying kinesio taping to other shoulder pathologies, supporting its value as an adjunct to physical therapy. For example, the study by Shakeri et al. demonstrated that taping combined with conventional rehabilitation improved the DASH scores in patients with shoulder dysfunctions [34]; Frassanito et al. also confirmed its effectiveness for calcific tendinitis of the shoulder [35]. These studies, along with other evidence, suggest that taping has short-term benefits in alleviating shoulder pain and improving daily function.

However, there are important distinctions between the clinical context and the application logic of this study and those of the aforementioned literature. Regarding population characteristics and clinical needs, previous research has mostly focused on chronic shoulder pain (e.g., impingement syndrome, tendinitis) or non-specific dysfunction, with rehabilitation goals primarily aimed at pain relief and improvement of daily activities [35]. In contrast, the patients in this study were postoperative cases of recurrent instability caused by military training injuries. Their postoperative condition is characterized by a clear background of structural injury, significant neuromuscular control deficits, and higher standards for functional recovery. In clinical practice, we have observed that in such patients, beyond pain, a more prominent contradiction in the early postoperative phase is the conflict between kinesiophobia (fear of movement) and the necessity of early functional initiation.

Based on this clinical context, the compound taping protocol used in this study demonstrates its unique interventional value. Specifically, taping may address this conflict through two complementary pathways: First, by providing continuous and gentle external support and sensory cues. For instance, the coverage of the lantern-shaped tape over the glenohumeral joint area and the assistance of Y-shaped strips along the deltoid direction can provide additional proprioceptive input regarding joint position and movement via skin receptors when patients engage in early activities. This immediate “biofeedback” may help reduce patients’ fear of pain and re-dislocation, thus encouraging them to attempt active movements more willingly within a pain-free range. This might explain the more significant improvement in joint range of motion observed in the study group. Second, by assisting in the early re-activation of neuromuscular control. During the early postoperative phase of muscle inhibition, facilitative taping targeting the supraspinatus and deltoid muscles may provide mild mechanical assistance during muscle contraction via elastic recoil. This could not only help “awaken” muscles inhibited by pain and immobilization but also potentially optimize their activation timing, laying a better neuromuscular foundation for subsequent strength and coordination training. This potential improvement in early motor control quality might be an intrinsic factor underlying the more pronounced increase in functional scores (UCLA, Constant-Murley) in the study group.

Clinical implications of the study

The significance of this study lies in exploring the potential value of kinesio taping as an “adjunctive medium for neuromuscular control and motor relearning,” emphasizing that in this specific postoperative phase of recurrent shoulder instability, it may play a unique and more functionally-oriented adjunctive role by providing continuous sensory input and assisting early motor control. This perspective helps explain the main finding of this study: the combined intervention protocol showed comprehensive improvements in joint range of motion and composite functional scores, beyond alleviating pain symptoms.

Study limitations and future research directions

Despite the positive results, this study has several limitations. First, this study is a retrospective study, and the sample size is determined based on the actual number of available cases, without prior efficacy analysis. Although this study has obtained statistically significant results, the sample size is relatively small (n = 60), which may limit the generalizability of the conclusions and increase the risk of type II error. In the future, it is necessary to verify the reliability of the results of this study through a multi center, large sample size study.Second, the study did not include a sham taping group. The clinical effects of kinesio taping may be partly attributable to placebo effects, patients’ positive expectations, or psychological influences from the therapeutic contact itself. Future randomized controlled trials employing sham taping controls (e.g., using non-elastic tape or non-therapeutic taping) would help isolate the physiological or neuromechanical effects of kinesio taping itself more purely. Third, it should be noted that although the conventional comprehensive rehabilitation therapy used in the control group is widely accepted in clinical practice, its implementation often lacks a sufficiently refined standardized protocol. More importantly, for patients after surgery for recurrent shoulder instability, one of the core goals of rehabilitation is to restore dynamic stability and impaired proprioception. However, conventional protocols sometimes fail to place these elements in a sufficiently central and structured position, which may affect the rehabilitation outcomes of the control group [3, 36]. The intervention design of this study did not fully control or quantify this variable. Finally, regarding statistical analysis, as a retrospective study, we primarily employed within-group pre-post comparisons and between-group post-treatment comparisons. While this approach effectively addresses the core clinical question of this study, it did not incorporate longitudinal data models such as repeated-measures ANOVA, which could provide deeper insights into the dynamic pattern of efficacy over time and the specific time course of between-group differences [37]. Future prospective studies should aim to address this limitation by incorporating more frequent assessment points and more complex statistical models.

Based on the aforementioned limitations, future research should focus on: First, conducting multicenter, large-sample randomized controlled trials incorporating sham taping control groups to control for placebo effects and evaluate the specific efficacy of kinesio taping more accurately. Second, methodologically integrating objective quantitative tools, such as utilizing surface electromyography to analyze shoulder muscle activation timing and fatigue indices, isokinetic dynamometers to assess peak torque and endurance, and three-dimensional motion analysis systems to quantify kinematic parameters, thereby providing more robust objective evidence for the neuromuscular modulation mechanisms of kinesio taping. Third, future research should strive to develop and apply more standardized, clearly phased rehabilitation protocols emphasizing neuromuscular control and proprioceptive training as the control baseline, to more clearly isolate the independent contribution of kinesio taping. Fourth, establish long-term follow-up endpoints focusing on training performance after return to training, reinjury rates, and quality of life to assess the long-term benefits of the combined rehabilitation protocol comprehensively.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the combined application of kinesio taping within the standard rehabilitation protocol for postoperative patients with recurrent shoulder dislocation due to military training injuries can safely and effectively further improve shoulder range of motion and functional scores. This provides a practically valuable adjunctive option for optimizing postoperative rehabilitation strategies for this special population.
