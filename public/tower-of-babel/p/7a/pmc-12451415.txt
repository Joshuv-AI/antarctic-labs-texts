Direct Visualization of Plasmon‐ Induced Hole Injection in Electroactive Luminescent Monolayers on Gold Nanoparticles
Authors: Baptiste Maillot, Ali Dabbous, Jean‐Frédéric Audibert, Vitor Brasiliense, Fabien Miomandre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA dual and simultaneous modulation of fluorescence intensity and electrochemical current induced by plasmon activation is demonstrated using a combination of electrochemical (SECM) and fluorescence microscopies, employing fluorescent redox monolayers grafted onto gold nanoparticles. The observed electrochemical and optical responses are fully consistent with a direct hole injection from the plasmonically excited metallic nanoparticles into the grafted monolayer. In particular, an unprecedented fluorescence intensity enhancement occurs upon simultaneous electroreduction at the tip and plasmon activation, whereas each process taken separately leads to a fluorescence quenching. The electrochemical current enhancement is significantly larger for functionalized nanoparticles compared to bare ones under plasmon activation, opening the route toward new strategies in photoelectrocatalysis.Keywords: electrofluorochromism, fluorescence, plasmonics, SECM, tetrazine

Introduction

The perspective of efficient use of light to promote chemical reactions has fueled a growing interest in the development of photocatalytic systems. The use of plasmonic structures is highly appealing, opening up interesting perspectives in terms of efficient energy harnessing from photons in the visible range to catalyze different chemical transformations,[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] including electron transfer reactions.[ 4 , 5 ] Plasmon‐ driven (photo)electrochemistry is thus becoming a field of intensive research,[ 6 , 7 ] aiming to efficiently use visible light to trigger or boost electrochemical reactions.[ 8 , 9 ] Recent applications in the field of electrocatalysis[ 7 ] or hydrogen production[ 10 ] highlight the potential societal impact of the concept.

Plasmon‐ induced processes are however complex, involving mechanisms spanning different timescales and energy ranges. Indeed, following the excitation of the plasmonic modes of nanoobjects, a sequence of correlated photochemical processes takes place, with generation of hot‐carriers,[ 11 ] thermalization, heat transfer, charge accumulation on interfaces, and/or injection into solution or surface species.[ 12 , 13 ] Such processes are influenced by the intrinsic properties of the nanoobjects, but also by their environment and irradiation conditions.[ 14 ] Moreover, they are often entangled with photochemical reactions and/or photothermal processes, making the scrutinization of individual effects often difficult.[ 15 ]

Significant research efforts have been aimed at disentangling these various phenomena.[ 15 ] For fast ones,[ 16 ] ultrafast spectroscopy has been the tool of choice,[ 17 ] revealing the hot charge carrier dynamics and thermalization processes[ 18 ] which can be scrutinized with respect to their characteristic times. Slower phenomena, such as charge accumulation, NP to solution heat transfer, and interaction with adsorbed species[ 14 ] take place at similar timescales, and are, as a consequence, less easily discriminated. These are however essential for understanding reactivity under plasmon excitation, prompting the development of different methodologies for quantitatively analyzing reactions.[ 19 ] For instance, Willets et al. developed an approach based on scanning electrochemical microscopy (SECM), in which ultramicroelectrodes (UME) are positioned near plasmonic surfaces to quantify the products of plasmonic reactions,[ 20 ] while other groups have focused on identifying spectroscopic signature of products and intermediates,[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] or on characterizing the spatial dynamics of the generated products.[ 15 , 24 ]

A unified understanding of plasmon‐ driven chemistry is however yet to emerge, as plasmonic reactions result from delicate charge accumulation and balancing processes which are critically dependent on experimental conditions.[ 11 , 25 ] Indeed, while fast plasmon‐ induced processes are often dependent on the plasmonic entity and irradiation conditions, charge accumulation, and injection are regulated by the environment of the plasmonic object, such as adsorbed species, capping agents, the presence of electron/hole scavengers, or charge buffer layers. Most investigations reported in the literature so far deal with interfaces between plasmonic particles and semi‐conductors (majorly TiO2, due to its applications in photocatalysis) with direct visualization of hot carrier generation through a photocurrent boost.[ 26 ]

So far, no methodology has been able to quantify the product of plasmonic reactions while simultaneously monitoring the generation of surface charge carriers at an interface with a well‐defined molecular layer. In this paper, we propose to address this challenge by developing an approach for simultaneously analyzing surface charge accumulation and injection processes. We associate local quantification of plasmonic reaction products, based on SECM, with the use of an electrofluorochromic (EFC) probe covalently grafted on the plasmonic nano‐objects, allowing to use fluorescence microscopy in tandem with SECM.

In a preliminary analysis, we have analyzed the plasmonic response of electrodeposited gold nanoparticles with an EFC mediator in solution, providing valuable evidence on the identity of the charge carriers generated.[ 27 ] However, since plasmonic effects are mainly short‐ranged (typically shorter than 20 nm from the nanoparticle surface regarding luminescence[ 28 ]), only a very thin zone near the plasmonic surfaces was directly affected, reducing the system sensitivity by convoluting the solution and surface fluorescence signals. In such conditions, it remained difficult for the optical response to disentangle transport effects from local plasmonic phenomena, limiting our ability to separate hot carrier generation and injection. Indeed, if the EFC probes are free to diffuse, the optical response of the solution remains majoritarian with respect to the probes located near the plasmonic surface, which are those sensitive to charge injection.

We now propose to lift those limitations by localizing all the EFC probes on the surface of the plasmonic objects. Using modified tetrazines as EFC probes able to anchor to the surface by a covalent link, we have prepared a photoluminescent redox active monolayer on the top of plasmonic objects, and use it to analyze plasmon‐ driven reactions. This will benefit from our past work to optimize tetrazines for the formation of monolayers over different surfaces, such as gold[ 29 ] and ITO.[ 30 ]

By analyzing the electrochemical and fluorescence responses of EFC monolayers located on gold and ITO surfaces, while simultaneously monitoring the produced species with the SECM, we assess two different aspects of plasmon‐ induced reactivity. Through this approach, we attempt here to provide: (i) direct fluorogenic evidence of the generation of holes in the electrodeposited NPs and (ii) quantitative analysis of the role of surface species acting as charge buffers on plasmonically induced reactions.

We first analyze the response of the system with respect to a model redox mediator, cobaltocenium, whose redox potential fits the requirements to trigger the process. Our coupled fluorescence and electrochemical approach enables a quantitative analysis of plasmons as photocatalytic entities, providing new metrics for assessing the process efficiency.

Experimental Section

All commercial chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Plasmonic substrates

The plasmonic substrates on ITO were electrochemically prepared on 25×25 mm ITO plates (25–35 Ω.cm, 100 nm thickness from SOLEMS SAS (France)) by chronoamperometry using a Princeton Applied Research Versastat 3 potentiostat, according to a previously published protocole.[ 29 ] A three‐electrode setup with the ITO plate as the working electrode, a silver wire as a quasi‐reference electrode (QRE) and a platinum wire as the counter electrode was used for electrochemical experiments. The electrolyte solution was prepared from chloroauric acid trihydrate (from Sigma Aldrich > 99.9%) 2 mM and sodium carbonate (from Prolabo 0.25 M) in milli‐Q water (18.2 MΩ.cm−1). The electrodepositions were carried out at constant potential fixed at −0.9V vs. Ag QRE for times ranging from 300 to 600 seconds. The solution used for electrodeposition was prepared by dissolving chloroauric acid 2 mM in distilled water containing 0.25 M sodium carbonate.

SEM‐FEG snapshots were acquired on a Zeiss (Ultra 55) apparatus. Size distribution was calculated using ImageJ software, removing the large sizes assigned to aggregates.

Extinction Spectra

Extinction spectra of the nanoparticles were recorded by using a SD 2000 fiber optic spectrometer from Ocean Optics. The samples were illuminated in transmission mode using a Nikon inverted microscope with a 12 V 100 W halogen lamp through a 40 × objective (NA 0.6). Spectra were acquired using the SpectraSuite software in absorbance mode with an integration time of 100 ms. The presented spectra are averages of 100 raw spectra corrected for the dark signal.

Functionalization of plasmonic substrates

Before the functionalization process, ITO substrates were activated during 15 minutes in an UVO cleaner (Jetlight). The functionalization of the gold nanoparticles deposited on ITO was carried out by immersing the substrates in a 1 mM solution of thioctic tetrazine (see Figure 1A) in dichloromethane (from Carlo Erba Reagents) during 48 hours.

Grafting on ITO was carried out by immersing electrodes in a 1 mM solution of siloxane tetrazine (see Figure 1A) in dichloromethane during 48 hours with one drop of acetic acid (from Carlo Erba Reagents).

Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry experiments were performed on a Princeton Applied Research Versastat 3F potentiostat. A three‐electrode setup with a 10 µm Pt ultramicroelectrode (from Ametek) as the working electrode, a silver wire as a QRE and a platinum wire as the counter electrode was used. All potentials in this paper are reported against Ag QRE and concern the tip (substrates remained always unconnected). The tip position was controlled by a piezoelectric system (Versascan). Plasmonic substrates were illuminated by using a supercontinuum laser (SMHP‐40.2‐ A‐PP‐RC, Leukos 10 MHz – 40 ps‐fwhm). The excitation wavelength was selected by using a bandpass filter bright light fluorescence (BP650nm‐13 nm from Semrock).

For all experiments, the electrolyte solution was prepared by dissolving bis(cyclopentadienyl)cobalt(III) hexafluorophosphate (from Sigma 98%) in acetonitrile at 2 mM and tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate (from TCI > 98%) at 0.1 M in degassed (with argon) acetonitrile (from Carlo Erba Reagents).

Fluorescence Microscopy Coupled to SECM

The optical measurements were carried out on a Nikon Ti Eclipse inverted microscope with an extra‐large working distance objective (x40, NA 0.6) in wide‐field illumination. Tetrazine molecules were excited by a pulsed laser diode at 474 nm (LDH‐P‐C‐470B, Picoquant) with pulses of 230 ps and a repetition rate of 40 MHz. The plasmonic substrates were illuminated by using a supercontinuum laser (SMHP‐40.2‐ A‐PP‐RC, Leukos 10 MHz – 40 ps‐fwhm) with a bandpass filter (BP650nm‐13 nm from Semrock) with a power of 1 mW focused in a 2.5 µm diameter zone (power density of ca. 20 kW cm−2). The emitted light was collected after passing through a band‐pass filter (BP536nm‐40 nm from Semrock).

Electrofluorochromism was studied by recording the emitted light during multistep chronoamperometry experiments. Emitted light was collected by a SD 2000 fiber optic spectrometer from Ocean Optics. Spectra were acquired using the SpectraSuite software in emission mode with an integration time of 1 second.

Results and Discussion

Plasmonic substrates are prepared by electrodepositing gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) over an ITO surface, as described in detail in the Experimental Section and in a previous paper.[ 29 ] Different electrodeposition times are used in order to modulate the particle size and the surface coverage. SEM‐FEG snapshots of the plasmonic substrates electrodeposited at −0.9 V for 450 and 600 seconds are shown in Figure S2. The shortest time sample is composed of nanoparticles of small size (15–20 nm) and aggregates of diameter in the 50–70 nm range. The longest time sample is composed of nanoparticles which are larger (typically 70–80 nm) with some aggregates.

The corresponding extinction spectra are displayed in Figure S3 for all samples. The position of the plasmon resonance maximum (λmax = 640 nm) is not strongly affected, but a gradual broadening can be seen when the electrodeposition time changes from 300 to 600 seconds.

Electrochemistry

The substrates are inserted in an optically accessible electrochemical cell and mounted on the coupled SECM‐fluorescence setup, as indicated in the Experimental Section. We first analyze the role of the interfacial photoactive layer on the plasmon‐ driven reaction using a redox mediator, the Cobaltocenium/Cobaltocene (Cc+/Cc) couple, as indicated in Figure 1. We prepare two kinds of substrates, where tetrazine‐ based monolayers are selectively located onto the AuNP or ITO surface, using thiol or siloxanes anchoring groups, as indicated in Figure 1A. By comparing the optical responses of such sensors, we attempt to discriminate between the effect of hot carriers, confined to the NP surface, and global effects, such as solvent temperature enhancement, photoinjection through ITO, or photodegradation of the probes.

After filling the cell with a 2 mM Cc+ acetonitrile solution, a UME (d = 10 µm) is positioned in the focal spot of the inverted microscope, in the vicinity of the plasmonic surface (typically 5 µm). By poising the electrode at sufficiently cathodic potentials (< E°(Cc+/Cc) = −0.8 V versus Ag/AgCl), Cc+ is reduced on the tip, generating Cc which is sufficiently reducing to locally reduce the tetrazine ring into its anion radical (E°(Tz/Tz−) = −0.6 V versus Ag/AgCl). As the tip is brought near conducting surfaces, a positive feedback operates, regenerating the oxidized redox mediator, and consequently leading to an increase in the tip current. The injection of holes/electrons in the system by irradiation of the AuNPs can therefore be directly detected in this configuration by monitoring the increase/decrease in the tip cathodic current. We quantify the effect by reporting the ratio between the current values in presence and absence of plasmon excitation (r = iONtip/iOFFtip). We start by analyzing simple situations, without any tetrazine (bare ITO and AuNPs in Figure 1A). Figure 1B shows the evolution of the current time traces after polarizing the tip at E = −1.1 V, and irradiating the nanoparticles near their plasmon resonance maximum (λexc=655 nm). One can readily see that excitation of the NP LSPR generates a significant increase in the cathodic current, suggesting the production of holes in the NP. These results are in good agreement with our previous study, in which tetrazines were used as redox mediators in solution[ 27 ] and with previously reported data.[ 23 ] Interestingly, if bare nanoparticles are compared with functionalized ones (Tz@AuNP versus bare Au), we observe a small but significant increase (going from 6% to ca. 8%) in the ratio, suggesting that the Tz layer improves the hole transfer efficiency to the mediator in solution. A small effect of the red irradiation can be seen for bare ITO probably due to a small but detectable photocurrent on this semi‐conducting electrode.[ 31 ] Control experiments have demonstrated that r is as expected sensitive to the incident power of the red laser (Figure S4), the tip‐substrate distance (Figure S5) and the alignment between the laser focus and the tip (Figure S6). In particular, analyzing finely the current increase when varying the tip‐substrate distance (Figure S5C) brings valuable evidence on the role of the mediator diffusion, as the raising time is shown to vary linearly with the square of the tip‐substrate distance (Figure S5D).

In order to confirm the proposed mechanism, we now vary the tip potential while monitoring the current ratios, as shown in Figure 1C. No current enhancement is observed at small cathodic potentials, when the potential remains in a range where no electrochemical reaction occurs at the tip. Then as the tip approaches the Cc+ reduction potential, the current enhancement increases sharply before reaching a plateau. The previously observed difference between AuNP and Tz@AuNP can still be seen here in the plateau value, showing the beneficial effect of the interfacial molecular layer on the plasmon‐ induced charge transfer. Furthermore, it can be noticed that the current ratio vs. tip potential curve looks like a steady state CV, with a half wave potential (−0.8 V) very close the one actually measured by CV for the redox probe (−0.85 V, see Figure S7). These data demonstrate that the current enhancement is controlled both by the plasmon activation (hole injection process) and the electrochemical reaction occurring at the tip through the applied potential and the mediator nature.

Another parameter of great interest is the mean size of the Au NPs on the substrate, which can be monitored by the duration of the electrodeposition process. As shown in Figure S2, the mean diameter of Au NPs increases with the electrodeposition time from 21 to 52 nm, resulting in a broadening and a small red shift of the extinction spectra. The impact on the current enhancement can be seen in Figure 2, with a clear increase of the contrast ratio. Furthermore, it can be noticed that in all cases, functionalized Au NPs display a higher contrast than the non–functionalized ones, but the difference becomes larger as the electrodeposition time is longer. This result can be interpreted as an effect of the larger number of grafted EFC probes for larger particles, which makes the transduction of the plasmonic activation into the electrochemical signal more efficient.

Inset: magnification for 300 seconds. B) Difference of contrast ratio between Tz@AuNPs and Au NPs as a function of electrodeposition time.

At that point, the mechanism responsible for these observations can be considered as being a hole injection from the plasmonic nanoparticles under red‐ light excitation, leading to a positive feedback on the electrochemical current, that is, the opposite reaction on the substrate (oxidation) compared to the one occurring on the tip (reduction). Note that the opposite reaction, namely a plasmon‐triggered electron injection that would directly reduce the mediator at the substrate surface can be excluded, as no variation of r can be seen at potentials more positive than the mediator reduction.

Fluorescence

This hypothesis is further checked by monitoring the fluorescence response of the grafted fluorophores during irradiation of the Tz@AuNPs surface. At monolayer scale, although being more sensitive to plasmon activation, analysis of the fluorescence response of grafted layers must be considered carefully, as the excitation of the fluorophores leads to a significant photobleaching, which must be decorrelated from the electrochemically or plasmonically induced responses. For that, we start each experiment by carefully monitoring the photobleaching kinetics for a few seconds (typ. 5–10), before attempting to induce any changes through tip polarization or plasmon excitation. We use that early response to model the photobleaching kinetics, fitting the fluorescence intensity variation over time as an exponential function (Figure 3A), which can then be subtracted from the response under electrochemical or plasmon activation to yield the curves shown in Figure 3B.

B) Corrected fluorescence intensity (background and bleaching fit subtracted) vs. time for Tz@AuNPs with electrochemistry alone (green zone, upper curve) and plasmonic activation of Au NPs alone (red zone, lower curve). C) Corrected fluorescence intensity (background and bleaching fit subtracted) vs. time for Tz@AuNPs in presence of electrochemistry at the tip (green zone) plus plasmonic activation (red zone) as a function of electrodeposition time. Using this procedure, we first analyze the effects of electrochemical tip polarization on the emission from grafted layer in absence of plasmon activation. When Cc is generated in the tip (green zone in Figure 3B top), the fluorescence response of the surface grafted layer quasi instantaneously drops, showing that Cc is indeed able to reduce the surface grafted Tz into their nonemissive form. Interestingly, the fluorescence response is readily restored after we cease to polarize the tip cathodically, confirming the reversibility of the process even at the monolayer scale, once photobleaching effects have been subtracted.

An apparently similar response is observed under plasmon activation alone (in absence of electrochemistry) but with a smaller amplitude (red zone in Figure 3B bottom). Such decrease in fluorescence intensity has different origins, as carefully investigated in our previous work,[ 27 ] resulting from energy transfer mechanisms enabled by the spectral overlap between Tz emission and the plasmon extinction.

In short, the reasons of the fluorescence intensity variations observed in Figure 3B are reasonably understood, stemming from the electrochemically induced formation of nonemitting anion radicals on one hand, and due to energy transfer between the excited fluorophores and the Au NPs on the other.

We are therefore in a position to test the hole transfer hypothesis by simultaneously reducing the tetrazines electrochemically, and exciting the AuNPs plasmon resonance. If hot holes are indeed generated by the AuNPs, they should accumulate on the reduced Tz radical anions, leading to their oxidation and thus to a local increase in the fluorescence emission. If enough holes accumulate in the Tz layer, it could be possible that the emission increase is enough to compensate for any energy transfer quenching mechanism, allowing a measurable increase in the overall fluorescence intensity to be observed.

As shown in Figure 3C this is precisely what we observe. After the initial quenching period due to the electrochemical reaction, about 20% of the original fluorescence intensity is restored upon excitation of the AuNP plasmonic resonance. This partial fluorescence recovery in the red zone is fully consistent with the electrochemical current enhancement described in the previous section, both being due to hole injection from the plasmonic activation. The effect becomes even more visible if samples containing a larger number of NPs are used, as indicated in Figure 3D, which shows the degree of fluorescence recovery plot against the electrodeposition time.

This is a strong and direct evidence that the main consequence of the plasmon excitation is hole injection. Indeed, whereas fluorescence quenching can result from various mechanisms, it is unlikely that this fluorescence recovery could be due to anything else than the reoxidation involving the tetrazine derivatives grafted on Au NPs. In particular, photothermal effects would lead to the opposite variation by favoring nonradiative pathways. Finally, it must be emphasized that, while plasmon and electrochemical activation lead both to a fluorescence quenching when taken separately, an opposite effect is observed when both input signals are applied simultaneously. This could be of interest for applications in information coding or anti‐counterfeiting.

Another point to underline is the necessity to graft the redox moieties on the gold surface to observe the fluorescence enhancement related to the hole transfer. Indeed, when dealing with Tz@ITO substrates, no variation can be seen when electrochemical and plasmonic activations are simultaneously applied (Figure S8), while plasmon alone leads to a similar quenching as for Tz@AuNP, albeit with a smaller amplitude. This result strongly supports the plasmon‐ induced charge transfer mechanism, which becomes unlikely when the tetrazines are not directly connected to the particles.

Quantitative Analysis. Beyond these qualitative observations, the plasmonically induced hole injection process in the Tz layer can be used to quantitatively assess the efficiency of the induced charge transfer. Indeed, the fluorescence emission is a measurement of the number of emissive molecules present at a given time, therefore valuable information can be readily extracted. Two observables are of particular interest: I elec, the (normalized) intensity during the reduction of Cc+, which provides information about the proportion of Tz radical anions generated electrochemically; and I plas, the (normalized) fluorescence intensity during irradiation of the NP plasmon band, which estimates the number of reoxidized species by the plasmonic mechanism. One can readily see that both xox and ηplas increase with the electrodeposition time, showing that both factors are sensitive to the mean size and size distribution of gold nanoparticles on the substrate. As both parameters increase with the electrodeposition time (see Figure S2), it is not possible at that stage to discriminate which one is responsible for the observed variation. However, contrarily to what is observed at solid interfaces where the charge injection efficiency is larger when the plasmonic nanoparticles are smaller,[ 32 , 33 ] here we observe an opposite behavior, mainly because the crucial factor is the amount of redox‐active molecules serving as efficient relays for the electron transfers responsible for the current and light emission enhancements.

Although the plasmon‐ induced hole injection has a very low yield when compared to the total amount of absorbed photons in the active zone (not surprising when comparing with a recent literature report[ 4 ]), its effect to convert the redox moieties can be directly visualized on the fluorescence response through a clear intensity enhancement, reaching almost half of the intensity initially quenched by the electrochemical reaction at the tip. This validates the method as a direct visualization of the hole‐ injection process triggered by the plasmonic excitation.

Conclusion

The covalent grafting of EFC probes on the surface of gold nanoparticles has allowed us to highlight the role of hole injection in the plasmonic activation, thanks to a combination of SECM and fluorescence microscopies. The effect of plasmon excitation can be seen by an electrochemical current enhancement which is significantly higher for functionalized nanoparticles compared to bare ones and by a fluorescence switch‐on, which is only observed when the probe is directly grafted on the nanoparticle surface. A fluorescence intensity enhancement is observed when both the electrochemical signal and plasmon activation are applied, whereas each one taken independently leads to a fluorescence quenching, emphasizing the hole‐ injection process and allowing to visualize it through the optical output. This also demonstrates that the luminescence of a monolayer can be controlled through the combination of electrochemistry and plasmons, constituting a first example of plasmon‐mediated electrofluorochromism at such a scale. Finally, applications in the field of photoelectrocatalysis can be envisioned because this effect can be extended to other redox mediators, like dissolved oxygen. Indeed, combining electrochemical and plasmonic activations on redox functionalized nanoparticles would significantly enhance the measured current at the tip. This aspect will be the scope of further studies, in parallel through the control of the nanoparticle size and organization on the substrate.
