Risk factor analysis and construction of risk nomogram for severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome based on clinical classification
Authors: Lijuan Zhou, Huijuan Zhou, Ming Yin, Yue Wang, Mianyu Yang, Jiling Wang, Xiaoqiong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome (SFTS) is an acute infectious disease caused by the novel bunyavirus with high mortality rates. Current lack of systematic clinical classification standards and effective risk prediction tools necessitates early identification of severe patients to improve prognosis. We retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 437 SFTS patients admitted to The First Affiliated Hospital of USTC and Hefei Second People’s Hospital from January 2022 to December 2024. Patients were classified into mild (177 cases, 40.5%), severe (202 cases, 46.2%), and critical (58 cases, 13.3%) categories based on disease severity. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors for disease progression (severe+critical vs. mild). A risk prediction nomogram was constructed using R software with internal validation. Multivariate analysis revealed seven independent risk factors for disease progression: IL-6 > 50 pg/mL (OR = 7.82, 95%CI: 4.23–14.47, P < 0.001), altered consciousness (OR = 6.85, 95%CI: 3.94–11.91, P < 0.001), hemorrhagic manifestations (OR = 4.21, 95%CI: 1.85–9.58, P < 0.001), high-sensitivity CRP > 10 mg/L (OR = 3.45, 95%CI: 2.18–5.47, P < 0.001), pulmonary infection (OR = 2.94, 95%CI: 2.01–4.30, P < 0.001), AST > 200 U/L (OR = 2.13, 95%CI: 1.47–3.08, P < 0.001), and age ≥ 65 years (OR = 1.68, 95%CI: 1.15–2.46, P = 0.007). The nomogram model demonstrated good discriminative ability with a C-index of 0.806 (95%CI: 0.804–0.878) and ROC-AUC of 0.806. Calibration curves showed high concordance between predicted and actual probabilities (Hosmer-Lemeshow test P = 0.512), with sensitivity of 78.5% and specificity of 81.9%. This nomogram prediction model incorporating seven independent risk factors can effectively assist clinical identification of high-risk SFTS patients and has significant clinical utility for early intervention and personalized treatment strategies.

Introduction

Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome (SFTS) is an acute infectious disease caused by the SFTS virus (SFTSV), a novel bunyavirus primarily transmitted through tick bites1–3. Since its first discovery in China in 2009, SFTS has emerged as an important infectious disease in Asia, with outbreaks reported in China, Japan, and South Korea4–6. According to World Health Organization statistics, the annual incidence rate of SFTS in China is approximately 0.77/100,000, with case fatality rates ranging from 5.3% to 16.2%, posing serious threats to public health security7.

The clinical presentation of SFTS varies greatly, ranging from mild fever to multiple organ failure, with rapid disease progression and severe patients often dying within two weeks8,9. Currently, no specific antiviral treatment exists, and management relies primarily on supportive and symptomatic care10,11. Therefore, early identification of high-risk patients and timely intervention measures are crucial for improving prognosis12,13.

Recent studies have explored risk factors and prognostic prediction models for SFTS. Fang et al. constructed a mortality prediction nomogram with an AUC of 0.873, identifying age, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, prolonged prothrombin time, and elevated D-dimer as independent risk factors for death14,15. Liu et al.‘s multicenter study found that low lymphocyte percentage, early encephalopathy, and elevated serum LDH and BUN were independently associated with mortality.

However, existing studies predominantly use death as the endpoint, potentially underestimating the proportion of severe cases and lacking unified clinical classification standards16,17.

Nomograms serve as visual risk prediction tools with important applications in clinical decision-making18–20. This study analyzed 437 SFTS patients from two hospitals, systematically evaluated risk factors based on clinical classification standards, and constructed a practical risk prediction nomogram to provide scientific evidence for early identification of high-risk patients and individualized treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study enrolled SFTS patients admitted to The First Affiliated Hospital of USTC and Hefei Second People’s Hospital from January 2022 to December 2024. The study was approved by the ethics committees of both institutions (approval number: 2024-Research-124), with informed consent waived due to the retrospective nature.

Clinical classification standards

Based on relevant literature and clinical practice experience21,22, SFTS patients were classified into three categories:

Mild: Fever (axillary temperature 37.3–39.5 °C), platelet count 50–100 × 10⁹/L, liver enzymes < 3 times upper normal limit, no obvious organ dysfunction, relatively stable disease course.

Severe: High fever (axillary temperature > 39.5 °C) or platelet count < 50 × 10⁹/L, liver enzymes ≥ 3 times upper normal limit, single organ dysfunction (acute kidney injury, liver failure, respiratory failure), or presence of altered consciousness and bleeding tendency.

Critical: Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, disseminated intravascular coagulation, septic shock, or life-threatening complications.

Data collection

Patient information was collected through electronic medical record systems, including basic demographics, clinical manifestations, laboratory tests, virological detection, imaging studies, and outcomes. All laboratory indicators were based on first test results within 24 h of admission.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation: Based on the ten-events-per-variable (EPV) rule recommended for logistic regression models, with an anticipated 10–15 candidate predictors in the multivariate model, a minimum of 100–150 outcome events was required. Given that approximately 59.5% of enrolled patients were expected to have severe or critical disease (based on pilot data), a sample size of at least 250–300 total patients was needed to ensure adequate statistical power. The final cohort of 437 patients (260 severe/critical events) exceeded this requirement, providing sufficient power for stable model estimation.

Missing data: Missing data were handled using the following strategy: variables with > 30% missing values were excluded. For the remaining variables with < 30% missing values, missing data were handled by multiple imputation using chained equations (MICE) with 20 imputation datasets and predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic regression for binary variables. Results across imputed datasets were pooled using Rubin’s rules. Sensitivity analyses using complete-case analysis yielded consistent results (data not shown), supporting the robustness of our findings.

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics) and R Statistical Software, Version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org), with forest plots created using GraphPad Prism, Version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com/scientific-software/prism/). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range) [M(Q1,Q3)]. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) [n(%)]. For group comparisons, normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, non-normally distributed variables using Kruskal-Wallis H test, and categorical variables using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed with disease progression (severe+critical vs. mild) as the dependent variable. Variables with P < 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate logistic regression using forward LR method.

The nomogram was constructed using the R package ‘rms’ with internal validation performed using Bootstrap method (1000 resamples). C-index and ROC curves were calculated, calibration curves were drawn, and Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was performed. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 437 SFTS patients were included, aged 18–89 years with a median age of 66 years (IQR, 56–74). There were 258 males (59.0%) and 179 females (41.0%). According to classification standards, 177 cases (40.5%) were mild, 202 cases (46.2%) were severe, and 58 cases (13.3%) were critical. Significant differences were observed among the three groups in age, gender, agricultural work history, tick exposure history, and time from onset to hospital admission (P < 0.05). Critical patients were older, had higher proportions of agricultural workers, and higher tick exposure rates (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

Significant differences existed among the three groups in major clinical symptoms. Severe and critical patients had higher fever degrees, more obvious gastrointestinal symptoms, and significantly increased incidence of neurological symptoms and hemorrhagic manifestations. The incidence of altered consciousness in mild, severe, and critical groups was 8.0%, 31.3%, and 68.4%, respectively (P < 0.001); hemorrhagic manifestations occurred in 3.5%, 7.1%, and 25.9%, respectively (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Laboratory findings

Significant differences were observed in laboratory indicators among the three groups. With disease progression, inflammatory markers gradually increased and organ dysfunction worsened. Inflammatory markers such as IL-6, high-sensitivity CRP, and procalcitonin were significantly elevated in the critical group; organ function indicators including liver enzymes and creatinine also showed increasing trends (Table 3).

Univariate analysis

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified 20 variables associated with disease progression (P < 0.05 or P < 0.1), including age ≥ 65 years, agricultural work history, maximum temperature, altered consciousness, hemorrhagic manifestations, white blood cell count, platelet count < 30 × 10⁹/L, AST > 200 U/L, elevated creatinine, high-sensitivity CRP > 10 mg/L, IL-6 > 50 pg/mL, elevated procalcitonin, and pulmonary infection (Table 4).

Multivariate analysis

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of variables with P < 0.1 in univariate analysis identified seven independent risk factors for disease progression: IL-6 > 50 pg/mL (OR = 7.82, 95%CI: 4.23–14.47, P < 0.001), altered consciousness (OR = 6.85, 95%CI: 3.94–11.91, P < 0.001), hemorrhagic manifestations (OR = 4.21, 95%CI: 1.85–9.58, P < 0.001), high-sensitivity CRP > 10 mg/L (OR = 3.45, 95%CI: 2.18–5.47, P < 0.001), pulmonary infection (OR = 2.94, 95%CI: 2.01–4.30, P < 0.001), AST > 200 U/L (OR = 2.13, 95%CI: 1.47–3.08, P < 0.001), and age ≥ 65 years (OR = 1.68, 95%CI: 1.15–2.46, P = 0.007) (Table 5).

Development and validation of risk prediction nomogram and forest plot

A risk prediction nomogram and forest plot were developed based on independent risk factors identified through multivariate analysis (Figs. 1 and 2). The model exhibited good discriminative performance with a C-index of 0.806 (95% CI: 0.804–0.878). ROC curve analysis revealed an AUC of 0.806, demonstrating significantly better predictive performance compared to individual predictors (Fig. 3). Calibration analysis showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, with the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P = 0.512, > 0.05) confirming good model calibration (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study established clinical classification standards for SFTS patients and identified seven independent risk factors for disease progression using multivariate logistic regression analysis, demonstrating superior predictive performance with a C-index of 0.806 and ROC-AUC of 0.806. The nomogram model exhibits excellent clinical utility for early risk stratification of SFTS patients and enables targeted interventions to improve patient outcomes. By incorporating both laboratory biomarkers and clinical manifestations, this integrated approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of SFTS pathogenesis compared to single-marker prediction strategies.

IL-6 emerged as the most potent independent risk factor (OR = 7.82, 95% CI: 4.23–14.47), reflecting its central role in the pathogenic mechanisms underlying SFTS. The elevated IL-6 levels in severe SFTS represent cytokine storm—a dysregulated innate immune response characterized by excessive production of pro-inflammatory mediators. Mechanistically, SFTSV infection activates the monocyte-macrophage system through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), particularly toll-like receptors (TLRs) and retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), which subsequently trigger intracellular signaling cascades including NF-κB and JAK-STAT pathways22–24. These signaling cascades converge on nuclear transcription factors that orchestrate the massive transcription and release of IL-6 and other pro-inflammatory cytokines. IL-6 acts as a pleiotropic cytokine with multiple pathogenic consequences: stimulation of hepatic acute-phase protein synthesis, driving the systemic inflammatory response; promotion of neutrophil recruitment and degranulation, amplifying oxidative stress and tissue injury; facilitation of endothelial cell activation and increased vascular permeability, contributing to fluid extravasation and hypovolemic shock; and modulation of coagulation cascade activation through direct tissue factor induction. Furthermore, IL-6 trans-signaling through the soluble IL-6 receptor (sIL-6R) represents a particularly insidious mechanism, as it amplifies the inflammatory cascade specifically in target tissues that lack membrane-bound IL-6R (classical signaling occurs only in IL-6R-expressing cells), potentially explaining the widespread multi-organ involvement observed in critical SFTS cases25. Prior studies demonstrated that IL-6 levels correlate closely with both viral load and the severity of organ damage, supporting IL-6 > 50 pg/mL as a clinically practical threshold for identifying patients at imminent risk of deterioration26. Our categorical threshold approach enhances clinical applicability by providing an unambiguous decision point for bedside risk stratification, superior to continuous variable approaches that lack clear clinical guidance.

Altered consciousness emerged as the second strongest predictor (OR = 6.85, 95% CI: 3.94–11.91), reflecting critical CNS involvement in SFTS pathogenesis. SFTS-associated encephalopathy is multifactorial in origin. SFTSV can breach the blood-brain barrier through transcytosis across endothelial cells, or alternatively, infected monocytes function as ‘Trojan horses,’ transporting viral particles across the BBB to establish viral reservoirs within the CNS parenchyma27,28. Once established within the CNS, SFTSV replication induces focal neuroinflammation characterized by microglial activation, astrogliosis, pro-inflammatory cytokine production (including IL-6 and TNF-α), and focal demyelination in white matter tracts29. Additionally, systemic pathophysiologic derangements compound neurological dysfunction: hypoxemia from respiratory failure reduces cerebral oxygen delivery, hypotension from septic shock decreases cerebral perfusion pressure, and metabolic derangements (hyperammonemia, acidosis) further impair neuronal function. The emergence of confusion, altered mental status, somnolence, or agitation in an SFTS patient should trigger immediate escalation of intensive monitoring, neuroimaging (CT or MRI to exclude hemorrhage or edema), and consideration of ICU transfer for advanced supportive care and potential neuroprotective interventions.

Hemorrhagic manifestations (OR = 4.21, 95% CI: 1.85–9.58) indicate profound coagulation dysfunction, commonly observed in severe SFTS. SFTSV exerts direct vascular pathogenic effects through multiple mechanisms. Direct infection of vascular endothelial cells triggers viral-induced apoptosis and necrosis, compromising the endothelial barrier integrity and exposing subendothelial collagen and tissue factor, which initiates both extrinsic coagulation pathway activation and platelet adhesion30,31 Simultaneously, the cytokine storm downregulates thrombomodulin expression on endothelial cell surfaces, impairing the protein C anticoagulant system and tilting hemostatic balance toward thrombosis. Progressive consumption of coagulation factors (II, V, VII, fibrinogen) and platelets leads to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), manifesting as the clinically paradoxical coexistence of simultaneous thrombotic and hemorrhagic complications. This is reflected in elevations of PT/INR, APTT, and D-dimer in our cohort, with critical patients showing more profound derangements. Recognition of hemorrhagic manifestations should prompt assessment for DIC, consideration of fresh frozen plasma or cryoprecipitate transfusion, and avoidance of additional procoagulant triggers.

High-sensitivity CRP > 10 mg/L (OR = 3.45, 95% CI: 2.18–5.47) independently predicted disease progression, reflecting the intensity of the acute phase inflammatory response32,33. CRP is synthesized by hepatocytes primarily under stimulation of IL-6, and its elevation in SFTS directly mirrors the severity of the underlying cytokine storm. Compared to prior multicenter studies highlighting the CRP-to-lymphocyte ratio as a mortality predictor25, our finding that absolute Hs-CRP > 10 mg/L independently predicts disease progression underscores its utility as a simpler, single-variable risk marker accessible in all healthcare settings, particularly in resource-limited endemic regions. CRP’s role extends beyond mere marker of inflammation; elevated CRP itself may contribute to pathogenesis through complement activation via the classical pathway and amplification of systemic inflammation.

AST > 200 U/L (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.47–3.08) served as an important indicator of hepatocellular injury and potentially muscle involvement. The liver is a primary target organ of SFTSV, with both direct viral cytopathic effects on hepatocytes and concurrent immune-mediated injury driven by cytotoxic T cells and macrophages contributing to hepatocellular necrosis16–34. Importantly, markedly elevated AST (> 200 U/L) may reflect not only hepatic involvement but also concurrent extra-hepatic sources, particularly skeletal muscle involvement (rhabdomyolysis) or myocarditis, which are recognized but underappreciated features of severe SFTS. This multi-organ involvement further emphasizes the systemic nature of SFTSV infection and the rationale for intensive supportive care. Our threshold-based approach (> 200 U/L) provides a clinically actionable criterion compared to continuous variable analyses, facilitating rapid bedside risk assessment.

Age ≥ 65 years (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.15–2.46) represented an independent risk factor, consistent with well-characterized immunosenescence in the elderly. The aging immune system exhibits thymic involution with reduced naive T cell output, impaired dendritic cell antigen presentation, reduced B cell diversity, and diminished antibody affinity maturation in response to novel pathogens35. Paradoxically, concurrent inflammaging a chronic low-grade inflammatory state—impairs acute antiviral responses while predisposing to dysregulated cytokine responses. This explains why elderly patients often mount inadequate primary immune responses yet simultaneously develop excessive inflammatory complications. Our findings mirror epidemiologic data showing age-related mortality escalation in SFTS, particularly in patients > 70 years. The OR for age in our multivariate model (1.68) was lower than some prior single-variable studies, likely because our comprehensive multivariate model captures age-related susceptibility partially mediated through the other mechanistically downstream risk factors included in the model.

Pulmonary infection (OR = 2.94, 95% CI: 2.01–4.30) as an independent predictor highlights the clinical importance of secondary bacterial or fungal superinfection in SFTS progression. SFTSV-induced immunosuppression (including impaired neutrophil function and altered T cell responses) creates a window of vulnerability during which opportunistic pulmonary infections, particularly by Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Candida species, readily establish infection and accelerate the transition to respiratory failure and septic shock. This finding supports the utility of early microbiological surveillance (blood cultures, respiratory secretion cultures), empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage, and consideration of antifungal agents in high-risk patients presenting with fever persistence despite initial antimicrobial therapy.

Our comprehensive seven-factor model (AUC = 0.806) significantly outperformed single indicator predictions, demonstrating substantial value in multi-factor integration. While Fang et al.‘s mortality prediction model achieved AUC = 0.87314 and Liu et al.‘s early prediction model reached AUC = 0.92415, these studies used death as the primary endpoint—an inherently more extreme outcome that tends to inflate apparent model discriminative performance. By contrast, our model targets disease progression (severe+critical vs. mild), a clinically more actionable endpoint that enables earlier intervention before irreversible organ damage occurs32. This difference in endpoint selection explains the somewhat lower but arguably more clinically meaningful AUC in our study. Furthermore, our model’s inclusion of clinical manifestations (altered consciousness, hemorrhagic manifestations) alongside laboratory markers provides a more comprehensive and clinically practical assessment compared to laboratory-only models.

Risk stratification-based management strategies help optimize medical resource allocation and reduce healthcare costs. Early identification and aggressive intervention of high-risk patients identified by our nomogram—including ICU admission, close hemodynamic monitoring, cytokine storm mitigation strategies, and enhanced microbiological surveillance—may reduce ICU stay duration and overall medical expenses, potentially bringing significant health economic benefits in SFTS-endemic areas. The nomogram can guide clinical decision-making regarding intensity of monitoring, need for ICU transfer, consideration of plasma exchange for cytokine removal, and other advanced supportive measures.

This study has several important limitations warranting acknowledgment. As a retrospective study, selection bias and information bias cannot be entirely excluded, though both participating institutions are tertiary referral centers with systematic patient documentation. The model relied on static indicators at hospital admission and did not capture dynamic changes during the disease course (e.g., trajectory of IL-6 or CRP over time), which may underestimate the predictive value of evolving clinical trajectories and serial biomarker measurements. The sample was drawn from two tertiary hospitals in Anhui Province, potentially limiting the geographical representativeness and external validity of findings to other endemic regions with potentially different viral strains, host populations, or healthcare practices. Future prospective, multi-center studies across diverse geographic regions are warranted to validate and externally calibrate the nomogram and establish its generalizability.

Conclusion

This comprehensive study of 437 SFTS patients established evidence-based three-level clinical classification standards and identified seven independent risk factors for disease progression: IL-6 > 50 pg/mL, altered consciousness, hemorrhagic manifestations, high-sensitivity CRP > 10 mg/L, pulmonary infection, AST > 200 U/L, and age ≥ 65 years. The constructed risk nomogram model demonstrated excellent predictive performance with a C-index of 0.806 and can effectively identify severe SFTS patients at risk for rapid deterioration. This nomogram provides an important tool for early clinical intervention, enabling prioritization of ICU resources, and implementation of individualized treatment strategies tailored to patient risk profile. Future validation in prospective multi-center cohorts will solidify its clinical utility in diverse SFTS-endemic regions.
