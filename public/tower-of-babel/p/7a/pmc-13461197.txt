Clinical and Pathological Characteristics of Men with Infertility at a Single Center: A Retrospective Descriptive Study
Authors: Salah Al-Waheeb, Naser Al Azmi, Elizabet Angelaki, Sandro C. Esteves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aims of the study were to describe testicular histopathological patterns in infertile men undergoing microdissection testicular sperm extraction (mTESE) and to examine their relationship with age and clinical presentation.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included 62 infertile men who underwent mTESE at a tertiary care center between July 2019 and August 2025. Clinical presentation, age at biopsy, and histopathological diagnosis were extracted from medical records. Patients were grouped by clinical presentation and by predefined age bands (21–30, 31–35, 36–40, 41–50, and >50 years). Histopathological categories were Sertoli cell-only syndrome (SCO), hypospermatogenesis, late maturation arrest, full maturation, and complete atrophy; one case recorded as tubular atrophy was grouped with complete atrophy for analysis. Associations were assessed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and the Kruskal-Wallis test.ResultsNon-obstructive azoospermia was the most common presentation (49/62, 79.0%). SCO was the most frequent histopathological pattern (28/62, 45.2%), followed by hypospermatogenesis (14/62, 22.6%), full maturation (8/62, 12.9%), late maturation arrest (7/62, 11.3%), and complete atrophy/tubular atrophy (5/62, 8.1%). Histopathological distribution differed significantly across age groups (chi-square p = 0.0003), and age differed significantly across histopathological patterns (Kruskal-Wallis, p = 0.0127). SCO predominated in men aged 21–30 years (13/20, 65.0%) and 31–35 years (9/14, 64.3%), whereas complete atrophy/tubular atrophy occurred only in men older than 40 years. Clinical presentation was also associated with histopathological pattern (chi-square p < 0.001).ConclusionIn this single-center cohort, testicular histopathology varied significantly with age and clinical presentation. These findings support age-stratified counseling while emphasizing that the retrospective design and small subgroup sizes limit causal inference.

Introduction

Male infertility contributes to approximately half of infertility among couples and remains an important reproductive health concern. Azoospermia is commonly classified as obstructive or non-obstructive, with non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) reflecting impaired spermatogenesis and posing major challenges for prognostication and treatment [1, 2]. Testicular histopathology provides direct information on spermatogenic status and remains useful when counseling men undergoing sperm retrieval procedures. Reported patterns include Sertoli cell-only syndrome (SCO), hypospermatogenesis, maturation arrest, full maturation, and atrophic change. These categories are clinically relevant because they correlate with the likelihood of sperm retrieval and may reflect different mechanisms of testicular failure [3, 4]. Age-related decline in male reproductive function has been increasingly recognized, but the relationship between age and specific testicular histopathological patterns in infertile men remains incompletely characterized. Previous studies have examined clinical predictors of sperm retrieval and histological correlates in azoospermic men, yet data remain limited for age-stratified histopathological patterns in routine single-center practice [5–8]. This study was undertaken to describe the distribution of testicular histopathological patterns in infertile men undergoing microdissection testicular sperm extraction (mTESE) and to examine associations with age group and clinical presentation. By reporting specific comparisons and statistical testing, the study aimed to provide clinically useful information for counseling while acknowledging the limitations of a retrospective single-center cohort.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Assisted Reproduction, Royale Hayat Hospital, a tertiary care referral center, between July 2019 and August 2025.

Patient Selection

A total of 62 infertile men who underwent mTESE during the study period were included. Inclusion criteria were age 18 years or older, presentation with infertility, a clinical diagnosis requiring testicular biopsy or sperm retrieval, and availability of a complete histopathological report. Exclusion criteria were confirmed obstructive azoospermia, previous testicular malignancy or chemotherapy/radiotherapy, incomplete clinical or histopathological records, and prior testicular surgery that could alter histopathological interpretation.

Clinical Evaluation

All patients underwent clinical evaluation and laboratory assessment as clinically indicated, including medical and reproductive history, physical examination, semen analysis, hormonal testing, and genetic or imaging studies when appropriate [1]. Semen analysis was performed according to World Health Organization 2010 recommendations after 2–7 days of sexual abstinence, and at least two semen samples were reviewed when available. Detailed sperm concentration, motility, and morphology values were not consistently retrievable from the retrospective records; therefore, spermatogram parameters beyond the clinical category were not analyzed and are acknowledged as a limitation.

mTESE Procedure

mTESE was performed under general anesthesia. A transverse scrotal incision was made, and the hemiscrotum was entered. The tunica vaginalis was opened, and the testis was delivered. Under operative microscopy, the tunica albuginea was opened, and seminiferous tubules were examined. Larger or more opaque tubules were selectively sampled when present. Tissue samples were divided for embryological sperm search and histopathological evaluation. When spermatozoa were identified, they were processed according to the assisted reproduction laboratory protocol.

Histopathological Evaluation

Testicular tissue specimens were fixed in Bouin solution for 6–12 h and then transferred to 70% ethanol. Specimens were processed using standard histological techniques, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4–5 micrometers, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Slides were examined by an experienced pathologist. Approximately 70–100 seminiferous tubules were available for assessment in each biopsy.

Histopathological Classification

Histopathological patterns were classified according to established criteria [4]. The categories were (1) SCO, defined by seminiferous tubules lined by Sertoli cells without germ cells; (2) hypospermatogenesis, defined by reduced germ cells with preserved complete spermatogenic lineage; (3) late maturation arrest, defined by arrest of spermatogenesis before mature spermatozoa; (4) full maturation, defined by complete spermatogenesis with mature spermatozoa present; and (5) complete atrophy/tubular atrophy, defined by severely atrophic or hyalinized tubules with absent or minimal spermatogenesis. If mixed patterns were present, the predominant pattern across the examined tubules was recorded as the final diagnosis. No separate mixed-pattern category was created because the retrospective reports did not quantify minor secondary patterns consistently.

Data Collection

Data were extracted from medical records and pathology reports and included age at mTESE, clinical presentation, relevant medical history, and histopathological diagnosis. Patients were stratified into age groups of 21–30, 31–35, 36–40, 41–50, and >50 years. These age bands were selected a priori to provide clinically interpretable strata, to preserve adequate numbers in the younger and middle-age groups, and to isolate men older than 50 years as a distinct advanced-age group.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation or median and range. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square testing. Differences in age across histopathological categories were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test because of small subgroup sizes and nonnormal distribution in several groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Given the exploratory design and small cell counts, p values were interpreted descriptively rather than as definitive evidence of causality.

Results

Study Population

A total of 62 men underwent mTESE during the study period. The mean age was 36.8 years (SD 10.6; range 21–80 years). NOA was the most common clinical presentation (49/62, 79.0%), followed by oligozoospermia (8/62, 12.9%), normal spermatogenesis with other indications for sperm retrieval (3/62, 4.8%), and anejaculation (2/62, 3.2%) (Table 1).

Age Group Comparisons

Histopathological distribution differed significantly by age group (chi-square p = 0.0003; Table 3). SCO predominated in the 21–30-year group (13/20, 65.0%) and the 31–35-year group (9/14, 64.3%), decreased in the 36–40-year group (4/13, 30.8%), and was less frequent in the 41–50-year group (2/10, 20.0%). No SCO cases were observed in men older than 50 years. In contrast, complete atrophy/tubular atrophy was not observed before 40 years of age and was found in 2/10 men aged 41–50 years (20.0%) and 3/5 men older than 50 years (60.0%). Mean age differed significantly across histopathological categories (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.0127), ranging from 30.3 years for late maturation arrest and 32.6 years for SCO to 48.2 years for complete atrophy/tubular atrophy.

Clinical Presentation and Histopathology

Clinical presentation was significantly associated with histopathological pattern (chi-square p < 0.001; Table 4). Among men with NOA, SCO was the dominant finding (28/49, 57.1%), followed by late maturation arrest (7/49, 14.3%), hypospermatogenesis (7/49, 14.3%), full maturation (2/49, 4.1%), and complete atrophy/tubular atrophy (5/49, 10.2%). Oligozoospermia was associated mainly with hypospermatogenesis (6/8, 75.0%) and full maturation (2/8, 25.0%). All men categorized as having normal spermatogenesis manifested full maturation as seen on histology (3/3, 100%).

Discussion

Principal Findings

This study found that testicular histopathology in infertile men undergoing mTESE differed significantly by age group and clinical presentation. SCO was the most common overall diagnosis and predominated in men younger than 36 years, whereas complete atrophy/tubular atrophy was restricted to men older than 40 years. These results support age-stratified counseling but should not be interpreted as proof of a linear age-related degenerative process because the design was retrospective and subgroup sizes were limited.

Comparison with Previous Literature

The predominance of SCO in NOA is consistent with prior reports describing severe germ-cell depletion as a common substrate of non-obstructive spermatogenic failure [3, 6, 9]. The association between clinical category and histology is also biologically plausible: oligozoospermia more often showed hypospermatogenesis or full maturation, whereas NOA showed a broader spectrum that included SCO, maturation arrest, and atrophic change. These findings reinforce the role of biopsy histology in refining prognosis beyond semen analysis alone [10, 11].

Interpretation of Age-Related Patterns

The finding of more advanced spermatogenesis in some older men does not imply that aging improves spermatogenesis. Rather, it likely reflects selection and heterogeneity in indications for mTESE. Some older men underwent sperm retrieval for reasons other than severe primary testicular failure, including anejaculation or other clinical circumstances, and therefore, retained areas of full maturation. Conversely, younger men with NOA were more likely to have congenital, genetic, developmental, or early-life causes of germ-cell depletion. This interpretation is consistent with the focal and heterogeneous nature of spermatogenesis in NOA and with the rationale for microdissection-guided retrieval [12–14].

Clinical Implications

These results may assist clinicians when counseling infertile men before mTESE. Younger patients with NOA should be counseled that SCO is common in this cohort, whereas older patients should be counseled that preserved spermatogenesis can still be present in selected cases but that atrophic patterns may also occur. Because histology and sperm retrieval outcomes are not interchangeable, counseling should integrate age, clinical presentation, hormone profile, testicular volume, genetic results, and the operative findings rather than relying on age alone.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include a well-defined single-center cohort, uniform tissue handling, and standardized review by an experienced pathologist. Limitations include the retrospective design, modest sample size, single-center setting, incomplete availability of detailed spermatogram, hormonal, genetic, and testicular-volume data, and the use of predominant-pattern classification when mixed histological features were present. Small cell counts particularly limit interpretation of co-conditions and of the oldest age group. Accordingly, the statistical tests should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

In this retrospective single-center cohort of infertile men undergoing mTESE, histopathological patterns were significantly associated with age group and clinical presentation. SCO predominated among younger men, whereas complete atrophy/tubular atrophy was observed only in older men, and full maturation in some older patients likely reflected clinical heterogeneity rather than an age-related improvement in spermatogenesis. Larger prospective studies incorporating semen parameters, hormonal profiles, genetic testing, testicular volume, and sperm retrieval outcomes are needed to validate these observations.

Statement of Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Royale Hayat Hospital. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because this was a retrospective analysis of existing clinical and pathology records, patient management was not affected. Patient confidentiality was maintained throughout the study; all data were anonymized and stored securely, and only authorized research personnel had access to the study dataset.
