Assessment of community pharmacists’ knowledge on rivaroxaban drug and food interactions in Sudan
Authors: Abdulkarim Dakah, Rodaina Abdalraheem, Anwar Mohammed, Tawasul Osman, Anfal Abdalshakor, Mozdalfa Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to assess community pharmacists knowledge of Rivaroxaban drug and food interactions in Sudan addressing gaps in understanding crucial for patient safety. The use of rivaroxaban is increasing globally, ensuring pharmacists are equipped to manage its administration and interactions are vital, particularly in settings with limited prior exposure to direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). This is the first study to assess DOAC knowledge among community pharmacists in Sudan, a low-resource context with unique pharmacy practice challenges. A survey with 15 questions was conducted among 420 community pharmacists in Sudan. The questionnaire evaluated knowledge of administration requirements, herbal and drug interactions, dispensing practices, patient education strategies, and self-reported confidence levels. Key measures included correct identification of dosage-specific administration guidelines, awareness of herbal and drug interactions, patient education practices, and confidence levels in handling Rivaroxaban. Pharmacists demonstrated strong knowledge of administration for higher doses (95.2% for food requirements of 15 mg/20 mg), but confusion persisted regarding lower doses (58.5% correct for 10 mg). Awareness of herbal interactions was high (91.2%), yet 45.6% never checked drug-drug interactions and 28.9% rarely did so (74.5% never or rarely). Patient education practices were inconsistent, with only 20.9% providing written materials and 10.7% offering no counseling. Confidence levels were moderate (56.3%), highlighting gaps between knowledge and practical application. Multivariate analysis identified prior DOAC training (AOR = 3.40; 95% CI 2.10–5.52; p < 0.001), more than 10 years of experience (AOR = 2.15; 95% CI 1.42–3.25; p < 0.001), and postgraduate education (AOR = 1.85; 95% CI 1.10–3.12; p = 0.018) as significant predictors of adequate knowledge. While pharmacists exhibited solid knowledge of some aspects, significant gaps remain in dosage-specific guidelines, drug interaction monitoring, and patient education. These shortcomings pose risks to patient safety and underscore the need for targeted training programs to enhance practical application skills.

Introduction

Anticoagulation therapy plays a key role to stop and manage blood clot disorders such as venous thromboembolism (VTE) pulmonary embolism (PE), and atrial fibrillation1. These conditions add to global illness and death rates as populations age and risk factors like obesity inactive lifestyles, and diabetes become more common2. Doctors have long used traditional blood thinners like warfarin, but these need frequent checks due to their small safe dose range and how foods can affect them3. New oral blood thinners like Rivaroxaban have come up as options offering steady drug behavior in the body, set doses, and fewer check-ups, which makes life easier for patients4. Despite the growing global shift toward DOACs, concerns remain regarding healthcare professionals’ readiness to manage these therapies. A multinational survey by Papastergiou et al.5 involving 4212 pharmacists from 18 countries revealed that pharmacists felt significantly less confident in advising patients on non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) compared to warfarin, highlighting a global educational gap. More recently, a study from Saudi Arabia reported that community pharmacists possessed only moderate knowledge of DOACs (mean overall score 53.65%), with notable deficiencies in safety and patient counseling domains6. These findings underscore the need to assess DOAC knowledge in other understudied regions, such as Sudan, where pharmacy practice may face additional resource and training constraints. Rivaroxaban works by blocking Factor Xa. When it stops Factor Xa, it breaks the chain that makes thrombin and helps blood clot thus preventing clots from forming. Unlike warfarin, Rivaroxaban doesn’t change how platelets work or need to block vitamin K so it gives steady blood thinning without regular tests4,7. Doctors can prescribe Rivaroxaban to help prevent stroke due to non valvular atrial fibrillation, treat and help prevent VTE (DVT and PE), Help prevent blood clots from occurring after surgical procedures on the bones, Help lower the chances of major heart issues in coronary artery disease (CAD) or peripheral artery disease (PAD)7. Rivaroxaban has dose proportional bioavailability: 80 to 100% for 10 mg (food independent) and ≥ 80% for 15 to 20 mg when taken with food. Clinical studies have shown that taking these doses with food enhances bioavailability, increases plasma concentrations, and enhances therapeutic efficacy. For example, fasting conditions can reduce absorption from approximately 100% to 66%, potentially leading to suboptimal anticoagulant efficacy and adverse outcomes such as recurrent thromboembolism8–10. It is metabolized by CYP3A4/2J2 pathways, and it is eliminated from the body by liver and kidneys11. Elimination of rivaroxaban from plasma occurs with a terminal half-life of 5–9 h in healthy young subjects and 11–13 h in elderly subjects12. Drug-drug interaction (DDI) with rivaroxaban can increase the risk of bleeding or reduce its efficacy. The administration with Cyp3A4 inhibitors (e.g., ketoconazole) increases drug levels, but Cyp3A4 inducers (e.g., rifampin) reduces its effects. Herbal supplements such as St. John Wort reduces the level of rivaroxaban, while garlic or Ginkgo biloba increases the risk of bleeding12,13. Recent computational studies have identified novel isosteviol-based FXa inhibitors using molecular dynamics simulation and ADMET prediction, demonstrating the potential of natural product derivatives as safer anticoagulant alternatives14. Furthermore, quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) and artificial neural network (ANN) models have been successfully applied to predict the anticoagulant activity of thiourea isosteviol derivatives, identifying key molecular descriptors such as the presence of chlorine atoms and three-dimensional conformational features15. Molecular docking studies have further elucidated the binding modes of isosteviol derivatives within the FXa active site, revealing interactions with key residues such as Y99, F174, and G216, which are also targeted by FDA-approved drugs like apixaban and edoxaban16.

Food and Drink: According to the United States prescribing information, 15 mg and 20 mg doses of rivaroxaban should be taken with food when used to treat deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), as well as when reducing the risk of recurrent DVT and PE. The United States prescribing information also indicates that 15 mg and 20 mg doses of rivaroxaban should be taken with dinner to reduce the risk of stroke and systemic embolism in patients with nonvalvular AF17. Drugs: NSAIDs, antiplatelets, modulators of CYP3A4, and P-gp inhibitors influence the pharmacodynamics of Rivaroxaban13. Potentially lowering drug plasma levels and increasing stroke risk18,19. Anticoagulant drugs must be used under the guidance of community pharmacists to provide assurance of safe use dispensing medication advising patients on drug intake (e.g., food intake) monitoring DDIs, and alerting patients to the danger of bleeding20. The risks associated with anticoagulant use extend beyond bleeding to include teratogenic effects when used during pregnancy. Chaudhari et al. (2025) comprehensively reviewed drug-induced diseases and teratogenicity, noting that anticoagulants (particularly warfarin) can cause embryopathy characterized by nasal hypoplasia, stippled calcification, and skeletal deformities when exposure occurs during the critical period of 8–14 weeks of gestation21. Although rivaroxaban has not been as extensively studied in pregnancy, understanding these risks is essential for community pharmacists who may counsel women of childbearing potential. The authors emphasized that the primary method for diagnosing drug-related disorders is through thorough medication history-taking, highlighting the pharmacist’s role in identifying potential teratogenic exposures21. Their expertise is vital for bridging gaps in patient knowledge regarding DOACs like rivaroxaban. Pharmacists must stay informed about rivaroxaban’s pharmacological profile to provide effective counseling on dosage-specific food requirements, DDIs, and herbal interactions. This knowledge ensures optimal patient outcomes while minimizing adverse events20,22. There are Current Challenges in Pharmacist Education on Novel Oral Anticoagulants, Despite the growing use of DOACs like rivaroxaban, studies reveal gaps in pharmacist training regarding administration protocols, interaction monitoring, and patient education strategies. For example: Misunderstanding food requirements for specific doses (e.g., confusion between 10 mg vs. 15–20 mg dosing)13. Failure to consistently check DDIs or provide written educational materials3,13. Evaluating pharmacists’ understanding of rivaroxaban’s administration guidelines, interaction risks, and counseling practices is crucial to improving anticoagulation management in low-resource settings like Sudan. Addressing these knowledge gaps through targeted training programs can enhance patient safety by reducing preventable adverse events related to improper drug use3,20. A growing body of evidence suggests that structured, pharmacist-led anticoagulation management services (AMS) can improve clinical outcomes and patient safety. A systematic review by Egunsola et al. (2022) identified key facilitators for community pharmacist-led AMS, including enhanced patient convenience, improved clinical outcomes, and professional satisfaction, while recognizing barriers such as resistance from general practitioners and lack of funding23. In England, national audits incentivized through the Pharmacy Quality Scheme demonstrated that community pharmacists made nearly 150,000 interventions for patients on oral anticoagulants, significantly improving patient knowledge and the provision of safety alert cards24. These successful models provide a benchmark for evaluating and improving pharmacist practices in lower-resource settings. Therefore, this study aims to assess Sudanese community pharmacists’ knowledge of rivaroxaban drug and food interactions, identify specific gaps, and inform targeted educational interventions.

To date, no published study has examined DOAC knowledge among Sudanese community pharmacists, making this the first investigation of its kind in this context. Addressing these knowledge gaps through targeted training programs can enhance patient safety.

Methodology

Study design

This study employed a survey design to assess the knowledge of community pharmacists regarding rivaroxaban-drug and food interactions. The data was collected through a structured questionnaire from 420 community pharmacists. The questionnaire included multiple-choice questions (15 Questions) focusing on rivaroxaban-drug and food interactions, pharmacists’ confidence levels, and patient counseling practices. The questionnaire used in this study was developed by the research team specifically for this research. Table 1.

Questionnaire development and validation

The questionnaire underwent face validity assessment by three independent faculty members from the Department of Pharmacy Practice at Imperial University College. Subsequently, a pilot test was conducted on 20 community pharmacists (not included in the final sample) to assess clarity and comprehensibility. Internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.78). Based on pilot feedback, minor wording modifications were made to three questions.

Scoring system

A total knowledge score was calculated based strictly on 6 objective questions (Questions 1–6) related to Rivaroxaban pharmacology and interactions. Each correct answer was assigned 1 point, while incorrect or ‘unsure’ responses were assigned 0 points. Questions 7–15, which assessed practice, confidence, and attitudes, were analyzed descriptively as independent variables and were excluded from the cumulative knowledge score to maintain methodological transparency. Adequate knowledge was defined a priori as a score of ≥ 4 out of 6 (66.7%), a cut-off point consistent with established clinical pharmacy research standards6,25.

Study population

The target population comprises community pharmacists who provide patient counseling on anticoagulant therapy.

Inclusion criteria

Licensed community pharmacists actively practicing in retail or independent pharmacies.

Exclusion criteria

Hospital pharmacists and clinical pharmacists not working in community settings.

Pharmacists unwilling to provide consent for participation.

Sampling and data collection

Study period

The study was conducted between January and April 2025. We used a stratified random sampling technique across Khartoum State.

Sample size justification

Sample size was calculated using the Raosoft online calculator. Based on an estimated population of approximately 2500 community pharmacists in Khartoum, with a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 50% response distribution, the minimum required sample size was 334. We targeted 420 pharmacists to account for incomplete responses.

Response rate

The exact response rate could not be calculated because the survey was distributed through multiple channels without a definitive count of recipients. Based on the number of active pharmacists in the networks, the estimated response rate was approximately 45–50%.

Data management and analysis

Missing data handling

Questionnaires with missing responses to more than 3 items (20% of the 15 questions) were excluded.

Coding of variables for logistic regression

For the multivariate logistic regression (Table 3), the following independent variables were coded as binary categories: prior DOAC-specific training (1 = yes, 0 = no); years of experience (1 = > 10 years, 0 = ≤ 10 years); gender (1 = male, 0 = female); education level (1 = PharmD/Master/PhD, 0 = Bachelor); The dependent variable (adequate knowledge) was coded as 1 if the participant scored ≥ 4 correct out of the six knowledge-based questions (Q1–Q6), and 0 otherwise.

Data was entered and analyzed using jamovi software.

Awareness regarding basic administration rules

There was high awareness among respondents regarding basic Rivaroxaban administration guidelines by the survey. The large majority of the pharmacists (95.2%) got it correct that tablets of Rivaroxaban 15 mg and 20 mg must be ingested with food. Similarly, 93% of respondents got it correct that food increases the absorption of tablets of Rivaroxaban 20 mg, demonstrating good comprehension of this crucial interaction.

However, knowledge gaps occur in the case of lower doses. While 53% correctly identified that the 10 mg dose can be taken with or without food, a significant 41% incorrectly selected the 2.5 mg dose. Asked directly what the 10 mg dose is, 58.5% got it right, but 36.4% incorrectly responded that it is to be taken with food. This creates doubt over dosage-specific administration instructions that may impact patient care.

Knowledge of herbal interactions

Pharmacists demonstrated good levels of knowledge of herbal drug interactions with Rivaroxaban. A high percentage (91.2%) identified correctly that garlic, Ginkgo biloba, and ginger must be avoided in patients who are receiving Rivaroxaban. Additionally, 89.6% identified correctly that St. John’s Wort lowers Rivaroxaban drug levels. That high level of knowledge for herb-drug interactions is comforting since patients may not always volunteer use of herbal supplements unless asked explicitly.

Dispensing practice and patient counseling

A majority of pharmacists (84.9%) report dispensing Rivaroxaban, suggesting that the drug is being used broadly. Although 74.5% of respondents reported being aware of certain dietary limitations for patients on Rivaroxaban, this still leaves a significant 25.5% who lack this information or are unsure. This lack of knowledge may translate into suboptimal patient counseling.

The education methods reveal further issues. While 63.4% of pharmacists counsel verbally about Rivaroxaban-food interactions, just 20.9% supplement this with written materials. Notably, 10.7% of pharmacists do not typically provide any education about such interactions and could leave patients at risk for unnecessary adverse events.

Confidence and knowledge gaps

Although high rates of correct responses on some knowledge questions exist, reported confidence levels by pharmacists are different. Most pharmacists (56.3%) expressed moderate levels of confidence in their knowledge regarding Rivaroxaban-food interactions, while just 29.6% expressed high confidence. This difference between knowledge and confidence shows uncertainty in practice application.

Perhaps the most concerning finding concerns drug interaction monitoring. A substantial 45.6% of pharmacists never monitor for potential drug-drug interactions when dispensing Rivaroxaban, while a further 28.9% rarely do so. As 54.8% of them were unsure about certain drug classes that interact with Rivaroxaban, this is an important threat to patient safety. This finding represents, to our knowledge, the first quantified estimate of complete omission of DDI screening for DOACs in any community pharmacy setting globally, and it signals an urgent safety concern.

Multivariate analysis of factors associated with knowledge

The multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 3) identified several independent predictors of adequate knowledge. Prior DOAC training was the strongest predictor (AOR = 3.40; 95% CI 2.10–5.52; p < 0.001). Additionally, pharmacists with over 10 years of experience (AOR = 2.15; 95% CI 1.42–3.25; p < 0.001) and those with postgraduate degrees (AOR = 1.85; 95% CI 1.10–3.12; p = 0.018) were significantly more likely to achieve adequate scores.

Discussion

The survey results present a contradictory picture of community pharmacists’ awareness and practice concerning Rivaroxaban interactions. While some knowledge domains are adequate, our most striking and previously unreported finding is that 45.6% of pharmacists never check for drug-drug interactions when dispensing rivaroxaban—a complete omission of a basic safety step. This quantifies a critical implementation gap that has not been documented in any other setting with such clarity. The high proportion of pharmacists aware that increased doses require ingestion with food (95.2%) reinforces the general movement towards more DOAC use. An important and novel finding of this study is the marked discordance between high theoretical knowledge (e.g., 95.2% correctly identified food requirements for 15/20 mg doses) and extremely poor practical application (45.6% never check drug interactions). Most published studies report either knowledge OR practice, but our study uniquely documents both within the same sample. This discordance suggests that interventions focused solely on knowledge transfer (e.g., traditional lectures) are unlikely to change behavior. Instead, system-level supports such as mandatory checklists, electronic reminders, and workflow integration—are needed to bridge the knowledge-practice gap. Rivaroxaban accounts for 25.2% of anticoagulant prescriptions, according to an NHS England audit, representing a shift away from warfarin (which decreased from 48 to 20.8% between 2017/18 and 2021/22)26. This increasing prevalence renders the awareness of pharmacists about DOACs like Rivaroxaban very pertinent. The overall pattern of moderate knowledge with specific gaps observed in our study is consistent with findings from other countries in the Middle East and beyond. In Saudi Arabia, Basilim6 reported a mean overall awareness score of 53.65% among community pharmacists regarding DOACs, which mirrors our finding of significant knowledge deficiencies despite moderate familiarity. Notably, similar to our results, the Saudi study found that pharmacists scored higher on efficacy questions (56.10%) compared to safety (52.82%) or counseling (52.74%), suggesting a common trend where practical safety aspects are less well understood. Furthermore, the multinational iPACT survey by Papastergiou et al.5 revealed that pharmacists globally reported lower confidence in managing NOACs than warfarin, with hospital pharmacists displaying higher confidence than community pharmacists—a pattern that aligns with our observation of moderate confidence (56.3%) and inconsistent screening practices in community settings.

Our finding that 45.6% of pharmacists never check for drug-drug interactions when dispensing rivaroxaban, despite reasonable theoretical knowledge, underscores a critical implementation gap and is quite concerning. This phenomenon is not unique to Sudan. Egunsola et al.23, in their systematic review of community pharmacist-led anticoagulation management services across six jurisdictions (Canada, New Zealand, UK, Australia), identified several system-level barriers that likely apply to our context. These included organizational limits (staffing, time constraints, pharmacy layout), lack of integration with electronic health records, and inadequate remuneration for clinical services. In Sudan, additional barriers such as the absence of electronic prescribing systems, high patient volumes, and limited access to continuing education likely exacerbate this gap. The English national audit data showed that incentivized, structured programs enabled community pharmacists to make 148,376 interventions over two audit cycles, significantly improving patient safety indicators24. While the English NHS data demonstrate what can be achieved with structured, incentivized pharmacy programs, these findings must be interpreted cautiously in the Sudanese context. Unlike England, Sudan lacks a national pharmacy quality scheme, electronic health records, or routine financial incentives for clinical interventions. Most community pharmacies operate independently without integration into a centralized health system. Therefore, the intervention rates reported by Parekh et al.24 represent an aspirational benchmark rather than a directly transferable model. Nevertheless, the core principles standardized protocols, pharmacist training, and patient education materials can be adapted to Sudan’s setting with appropriate modifications for resource constraints. This suggests that financial incentives and standardized protocols can bridge the knowledge-practice divide a lesson for policymakers in Sudan. Without such support systems, even knowledgeable pharmacists may default to rapid dispensing without comprehensive safety checks. The NHS audit recognized that anticoagulants are “high risk with a potential to cause significant harm,” and therefore, they identified the key role of avoiding adverse events and ensuring pharmacists have a significant role to play26. That lack of familiarity with basic administration requirements and practice of comprehensive medication management indicates a notable gap requiring attention. Several system-level barriers may explain the observed gap between knowledge and practice in the Sudanese context. First, the absence of electronic prescribing and dispensing systems means that pharmacists rely entirely on their own vigilance or manual checks for DDI screening. Second, high patient volumes in community pharmacies (averaging 150–200 prescriptions daily per pharmacist in Khartoum) limit time for comprehensive counseling. Third, only 23% of surveyed pharmacists reported receiving any formal training on DOACs since graduation, highlighting continuing education gaps. Fourth, the lack of standardized, locally-adapted DDI screening tools or quick-reference guides leaves pharmacists without practical support systems. Similar barriers have been identified in other low-resource settings; Egunsola et al.23 highlighted organizational limits (staffing, time constraints, pharmacy layout) as key impediments to pharmacist-led anticoagulation services. Addressing these systemic barriers is as critical as improving individual knowledge. The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several independent predictors of adequate knowledge (Table 3). Prior DOAC-specific training was the strongest predictor (AOR = 3.40; 95% CI 2.10–5.52; p < 0.001). Pharmacists with more than 10 years of experience (AOR = 2.15; 95% CI 1.42–3.25; p < 0.001) and those with postgraduate degrees (AOR = 1.85; 95% CI 1.10–3.12; p = 0.018) were also significantly more likely to have adequate knowledge. Gender showed no statistically significant association (AOR = 1.05; 95% CI 0.72–1.45; p = 0.515), which is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. These findings suggest that targeted educational interventions should prioritize pharmacists without prior DOAC training, those with fewer years of experience, and those with lower educational qualifications. An Iranian cross-sectional study also observed that pharmacists possessed acceptable knowledge but low attitude regarding DOACs, and Rivaroxaban (77.4%) was the most commonly dispensed DOAC27. This concurs with our finding that although knowledge of facts is quite strong, application (as reflected in interaction checking and patient education activities) remains poor.

A survey of food-drug interactions among Jordanian pharmacists found “unsatisfactory knowledge about common FDIs,” with a median knowledge score of approximately 60%28. This is in line with our finding that most pharmacists (56.3%) are only moderately confident in their Rivaroxaban knowledge. The Jordanian study also found that university education was the primary source of FDI knowledge for 66.3% of pharmacists, which could explain why 91.1% of pharmacists in our survey expressed interest in additional training28. Similar findings have emerged from a study in Qatar, where El-Bardissy et al.25 assessed knowledge and attitudes toward DOACs among 211 hospital and community pharmacists. The overall percentage awareness score was moderate (41.6% ± 26%), and notably, 72% of participants were not satisfied with their own knowledge of DOACs. Factors associated with higher awareness included being a clinical pharmacist, male gender, and board certification findings that parallel our observation that prior DOAC-specific training (rather than years of experience) was significantly associated with adequate knowledge in our multivariate analysis. The authors concluded that educational campaigns are urgently needed to address these gaps. While our study focused on rivaroxaban, it is instructive to compare its interaction profile with that of warfarin, a traditional anticoagulant with well-documented herb-drug interactions. Laszczych et al. (2023) comprehensively reviewed interactions between warfarin and selected ‘superfoods’ (flax, Moringa, Nigella sativa), identifying mechanisms including CYP450 enzyme modulation (particularly CYP2C9 and CYP3A4), plasma protein displacement, and direct antiplatelet effects29. Rivaroxaban, unlike warfarin, does not require routine INR monitoring and has fewer dietary restrictions. However, it is still metabolized by CYP3A4 and is a substrate for P-glycoprotein. Therefore, although our pharmacists showed high knowledge of herbal interactions with rivaroxaban (91.2% correct), the potential for interactions with herbal products rich in CYP3A4 modulators remains clinically relevant. This knowledge may be particularly important in Sudan, where herbal remedies are commonly used alongside conventional medicines, and future research should explore the prevalence of concurrent herbal supplement use among patients on DOACs. From a pharmacological perspective, the molecular determinants of FXa inhibitory activity have been extensively studied. A QSAR analysis of isosteviol derivatives identified that the presence of chlorine atoms on the phenyl ring and three-dimensional molecular conformation (e.g., WHIM and GETAWAY descriptors) significantly enhance anticoagulant activity30. These findings support the notion that structural modifications of natural product scaffolds can yield potent FXa inhibitors, aligning with ongoing efforts to develop safer anticoagulants.

Other food-drug interactions have also been described in recent scientific literature that are not yet well recognized. For example, Raiola et al. found that consumption of fiber might significantly reduce the bioavailability of DOACs like Rivaroxaban31. Because only 74.5% of our survey pharmacists reported familiarity with some dietary restrictions, this new evidence may be another knowledge gap that may impact care. We acknowledge that this study has inherent limitations due to its cross-sectional, self-reported design, which may be subject to social desirability bias. Additionally, we did not assess system-level barriers such as workflow constraints or access to drug interaction screening tools, which may influence actual practice more than knowledge alone23. Future interventional studies are needed to determine whether targeted training programs can translate knowledge gains into improved patient safety outcomes.

Conclusion

This study reveals a critical dissociation between Sudanese community pharmacists’ theoretical knowledge of rivaroxaban and its practical application in drug interaction screening and patient education. The finding that a substantial proportion of pharmacists never check for drug-drug interactions represents a tangible patient safety concern. However, the strong interest expressed in additional training provides a clear pathway for intervention. Targeted, context-appropriate educational programs addressing the specific barriers identified—limited access to screening tools, high patient volumes, and absence of continuing education—could substantially improve anticoagulation management in Sudan. Pilot implementation of the recommended interventions with subsequent evaluation is urgently needed.
