HPV knowledge and willingness to receive HPV vaccine among female secondary school students in Ebonyi State, Nigeria: a pre-post evaluation of an educational intervention
Authors: Chinenye Angela Ogbu, Elvis Anyaehiechukwu Okolie, Austine Okpaga Ume, Obiageri Florence Ajah, Felicity Nneoma Okolie, Augustine Okpani Oko, Cosmas Nnadozie Ezejindu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite high Human Papillomavirus (HPV) prevalence and the rank of cervical cancer as the second leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality among Nigerian women, HPV knowledge and vaccination remain poor, especially in rural settings in Nigeria. Ebonyi State is reported among the most affected areas, with HPV prevalence reaching 71.1%. This study evaluated the effectiveness of a school-based health education intervention on HPV knowledge and willingness to receive the HPV vaccine among female secondary school students in Ebonyi State.MethodsThis study utilised a pre- and post-test design. A total of 474 female students aged 9–14 years were selected from the three senatorial zones using multi-stage random sampling. The intervention included structured health education sessions on key topics delivered by trained health educators. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was used for data collection. Data analysis, including descriptive and inferential statistics (paired samples t-test and McNemar’s test), was conducted using SPSS Version 26.ResultAt the pre-intervention stage, 83.5% of participants had low HPV knowledge. Following the intervention across the schools, 96% achieved high knowledge, with mean knowledge scores rising from 5.36 ± 6.26 to 23.41 ± 2.51 (p < 0.001). Among 193 participants who completed willingness items at both stages, willingness to receive HPV vaccine increased from 35.2% to 79.8% (p < 0.001). Only 10.6% of participants had been vaccinated. Lack of awareness, fear of side effects, vaccine unavailability, and parental objections were reported as reasons for poor vaccine uptake.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that school-based health education intervention is effective in improving HPV knowledge and willingness to receive HPV vaccine among female students. Translating these gains into actual vaccine uptake requires political commitment towards the integration of school-based health education interventions, engaging parents and guardians, and addressing barriers to vaccination, such as cost, vaccine availability, and safety concerns.Keywords: HPV, HPV vaccine, Health education, Knowledge, Willingness, Ebonyi State, Nigeria

Introduction

Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is the most common sexually transmitted infection, with high-risk subtypes being an important risk factor for cancers of the cervix, anus, penis, vulva, and vagina, head and neck [1]. HPV 16 and 18, which are high-risk subtypes, account for about 95% of all global invasive cervical cancer cases [1, 2]. Globally, cervical cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women, with an estimated 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths annually [1, 3]. It is worrisome to note that more than 90% of the global cervical cancer burden is reported in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), with countries in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) constituting a substantial proportion of these LMICs [1, 3]. Cervical cancer is the second most common cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among Nigerian women, after breast cancer, with an estimated incidence of 13,700 and 7,100 deaths in 2022 [4, 5].

In Nigeria, Kabuga et al. [6] reported a pooled prevalence of 42% among women. At the regional level, HPV prevalence was reported at 35.6% in Northcentral Nigeria, with 82.6% in Nasarawa State, 30.9% in Plateau State, and 30.3% in Abuja [7]. In Southern Nigeria, Onwuamah et al. [8] reported a 25% prevalence across the three geopolitical zones. In Ebonyi state, HPV prevalence has risen to 71.1%, with high- risk HPV 16 and 18 at 22.2% and 21.2% infection rates, respectively [9]. Disparities in HPV prevalence could be attributed to poor education, lack of employment, and lack of HPV awareness and vaccination. At the same time, some highlighted HPV risk factors include age at coitarche, multiple partners, co-morbidity such as HIV and the synergistic effect of smoking and alcohol intake [7, 8]. For every nation, especially countries such as Nigeria, where the HPV burden is high, vaccinating girls against HPV before they begin sexual activities is considered the best long-term strategy for lowering the risk of cervical cancer [10, 11]. Previous epidemiological and population-level studies have demonstrated that HPV vaccines are safe, efficacious, and highly immunogenic in the prevention of HPV infection, and other diseases and symptoms linked to HPV, such as anogenital warts, cervical lesions [10, 12].

As part of prevention and control efforts to address Nigeria’s high HPV and cervical cancer incidence, the Nigerian government, through the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA), with technical and financial support from partners such as Gavi-the Vaccine Alliance, World Health Organization (WHO), United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), and International Vaccine Access Center (IVAC) launched a two-year single-dose HPV vaccination campaign in October 2023 to protect millions of girls between 9 and 14 years [5, 13, 14]. This national HPV vaccination campaign has been successful in reaching over 12 million girls; this achievement has been largely attributed to careful planning, multistakeholder collaboration, resource and community mobilisation, and a demand-generation drive to increase uptake [14, 15]. Equally important to the recorded success are the many public awareness campaigns aimed at increasing knowledge of HPV infections and vaccine acceptance across several Nigerian states [13, 14, 16].

Despite the success of the national campaign in reaching a substantial number of girls in Nigeria, a plethora of challenges, such as misinformation resulting in vaccine hesitancy, myths and misconceptions, insecurity, difficulties in reaching girls in remote settings, and poor awareness, were prevalent during the implementation and impeded greater coverage of girls [13–15]. Again, research evidence from subnational settings such as Ebonyi state indicates that factors such as lack of knowledge about the HPV vaccine, concerns about the vaccine’s safety, and lack of awareness of the link between HPV and several HPV-induced cancers are significant contributors to poor acceptance and low vaccination uptake among the target population [17, 18]. To address these issues, these research studies recommended educational interventions through tailored outreach programmes that include community mobilisation and collaboration, integration of HPV awareness campaigns into school settings, and leveraging social media to deliver context-relevant messages on HPV vaccination [17, 18]. Despite these recommendations and the recent national vaccination campaign, there are limited reports of educational interventions targeted at HPV knowledge and willingness to receive HPV vaccine across Ebonyi state, especially in rural settings, where such interventions are needed to improve knowledge and address misinformation, vaccine hesitancy, low willingness, and poor vaccination uptake. Based on this background, this study implemented and evaluated the effectiveness of a school-based health education intervention to improve knowledge of HPV and the willingness to receive the vaccine among female secondary school students across the three senatorial zones of Ebonyi State.

Methods

Study design and study area

This study utilised a single group pre- and post-test (before-and-after) research design to assess the effectiveness of the health education intervention. Using a single group pre- and post-test represented a pragmatic approach due to resource and logistical limitations, given that we needed to sample students across different study locations. The study was carried out across the three senatorial zones of Ebonyi State (Fig. 1), one of the 36 states in Nigeria. This study was conducted between August and December 2025.

Sampling and sample size

A multi-stage sampling technique was used to select the sampling units at different levels. First, we randomly selected one Local Government Area (LGA) from each of the three senatorial zones in Ebonyi State via ballot (Fig. 1). The sampling frame included a total of 13 LGAs with four LGAs in Ebonyi North (Abakaliki, Ebonyi, Izzi, Ohaukwu), four in Ebonyi Central (Ezza North, Ezza South, Ikwo, Ishielu), and five in Ebonyi South (Afikpo North, Afikpo South, Ivo, Ohaozara, Onicha). The three randomly selected LGAs are Ohaozara LGA (Ebonyi South), Abakaliki LGA (Ebonyi North), and Ezza North LGA (Ebonyi Central). Next, three secondary schools were randomly selected from a list of all public secondary schools from each of the selected LGAs, with one school representing each of the zones: Umunaga Secondary School (Ohaozara LGA) for Ebonyi South, Girls’ High School (Abakaliki LGA) for Ebonyi North, and Community Secondary School (Ezza North LGA) for Ebonyi Central. To select the final sampling units (participants), we used class registers as a sampling frame to randomly select eligible participants, taking class and school sizes into consideration.

The study population included female secondary school students aged 9–14 years enrolled in the selected secondary schools. We excluded female students below 9 or above 14 years of age in this study, as well as those within the target group who did not assent or obtain the required written consent from their parents/guardians. Data collection instruments

Data was collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires. The questionnaire was adapted from previous studies [20, 22] that assessed similar variables, such as knowledge, vaccine uptake and willingness, that were relevant to our study. To ensure content validity, the adapted questionnaire was shared with two public health researchers who have published extensively on HPV and HPV vaccination, who evaluated the relevance and clarity of the questions to the research objective. Feedback from this evaluation by subject experts was integrated before the pretesting exercise. The questionnaire evaluated the demographic characteristics, knowledge of HPV and cervical cancer, risk factors, and methods of prevention. It also assessed awareness and knowledge of the HPV vaccine, target age and group for vaccination, barriers to vaccine uptake, willingness and attitudes towards HPV vaccine, and myths about the vaccine. The participants were required to correctly identify the risk factors, methods of HPV prevention, the right vaccination age, and perceptions about the HPV vaccine by responding with ‘yes’, ‘no’, or ‘I don’t know’ as appropriate. The questionnaire further assessed the willingness to receive the vaccine and evaluated the health education intervention programme. At the baseline, only participants who had heard of the HPV vaccine were allowed to answer questions on willingness, as we considered it inappropriate to assess willingness among those with no prior awareness at this stage. Suggestions and recommendations for improving health education programmes were also requested from the students. The questionnaire was read aloud to the students, and opportunities for clarification were provided to ensure the content was clearly understood.

Pre-intervention activities

Our study involved three health educators from the State Primary Health Care Centres and six members of the research team. The health educators were briefed on the objectives, sampling procedure, and questionnaire administration. The final version of the questionnaire was pretested among 25 students aged 9–14 years at Etiti Uburu Secondary School in Ebonyi South. Based on the results of the pretest, the questionnaire was reviewed for clarity, and necessary modifications were made to improve understanding and completion time before administration to the final study participants. A baseline survey was conducted on the first day by distributing the final questionnaires to 474 participants who assented and provided signed consent forms from their parents. Field monitoring was done by the research teams throughout data collection to ensure that questionnaires were completed.

Intervention activities

As part of the health education intervention design process, we engaged a diverse team of experts and stakeholders, including researchers experienced in implementing school-based health interventions, primary health care (PHC) workers who served as health educators and had prior experience delivering childhood vaccinations within the study communities, and selected school teachers and parents. The health education intervention consisted of structured lecture sessions that were delivered by the health educators on the second day of data collection, lasting approximately 2 h. Visual aids, such as banners and posters with HPV information, were printed, and the health educators supported the intervention with HPV stickers and hand tags. The sessions covered key topics on HPV, risk factors, methods of prevention including HPV vaccine, the right age for vaccination, target population, misconceptions about the vaccine, vaccine safety, and locations to access vaccination. The health education guide used across the schools was adopted from the Ebonyi State PHC training standards. A question-and-answer session was also included to ensure clarity, address misconceptions, and promote active participation.

Post-intervention activities

Post-intervention assessment was carried out immediately after the intervention to evaluate its impact on HPV-related knowledge and willingness to receive the vaccine. The participants were reissued the same interviewer-administered questionnaire at the end of the activity. Following this, research team debriefs were held to evaluate the implementation of the intervention and plan for data entry, analysis, and development of reports.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS Version 26 to produce descriptive and inferential statistics, and graphs were generated in Microsoft Excel. For inferential statistics, we used paired samples t-test and McNemar’s test for the pre-post variables for knowledge and willingness to determine whether the health education intervention improved knowledge of HPV and willingness to receive the vaccine. Knowledge scoring was done by assigning one (1) mark for each correct knowledge question and zero (0) for incorrect or “don’t know” responses [20]. The maximum knowledge score was 25; scores from 0 to 12 were designated as ‘low’ (< 50% of the total score), scores from 13 to 17 were designated as ‘moderate’ (50–70% of the total score), and scores from 18 to 25 were designated as ‘high’ (> 70% of the total score). The data was summarised using proportions, means, and standard deviation. The significance level (alpha) for all statistical tests in this study was set at 0.05 (α = 0·05).

The sociodemographic profile of participants in this study is presented in Table 1. A total of 474 students participated in the study and completed both the pre- and post-intervention assessments. As shown in Table 1, the 13–14 years age group accounted for more than half of the participants, 293 (61.8%), followed by the 11–12 years group, which constituted 35% (166) of the participants. The junior secondary school (JSS) class levels (JSS1-3) accounted for a significant proportion of the participants in this study, with JSS2 accounting for 42.8% (203), with JSS1 and JSS3 comprising 32.5% (154) and 19.2% (91), respectively. The majority of the participants, 271 (57.2%), were drawn from the Girls’ High School, Azuiyokwu in Ebonyi North senatorial zone.

Pre- and post-intervention HPV knowledge levels across sociodemographic variables

Table 2 shows that at the pre-intervention stage, participants in the low knowledge category ranged from 78.9% among those aged 11–12 years to 86.7% in the 9–10 years age group. Only a small proportion of the female students in the 11–12 and 13–14-year groups demonstrated high knowledge, with 5.4% and 3.1% performing well in the knowledge assessment, respectively. However, after the intervention, we observed a substantial increase in knowledge levels across the different age groups, with those classified as having high knowledge ranging from 95% in the 13–14 years group to 100% in the 9–10 years group. Similarly, in the senatorial zones, high knowledge levels ranged from 0 in Ebonyi South to 6.6% in Ebonyi North before the intervention, and improved dramatically in the post-intervention phase, with high knowledge levels exceeding 92% across the senatorial zones.

Comparison of pre- and post-intervention HPV knowledge

We conducted a paired samples t-test (Table 3) to evaluate whether participants’ HPV knowledge differed significantly before and after the health education intervention in the schools. The paired samples t-test showed that participants’ knowledge scores at post-intervention (M = 23.41, SD = 2.51) were significantly higher than at pre-intervention (M = 5.36, SD = 6.26), t(473) = 52.523, p < 0.001. These results suggest that the health education intervention conducted across the schools significantly improved participants’ knowledge of HPV, as evidenced by the mean knowledge scores, which rose from 5.36 ± 6.26 at baseline to 23.41 ± 2.51 after the intervention.

Willingness to receive HPV vaccine before and after the intervention

Among participants who answered questions about willingness at both time points (n = 193), a substantial improvement in willingness to receive the HPV vaccine was observed as the proportion of those who were willing to receive the HPV vaccine rose from 35.2% (68) before the intervention to 79.8% (154) at the endline. Table 4 presents McNemar test results assessing whether the difference in participants’ willingness to receive the HPV vaccine before and after the health education intervention was significant. The results indicated that the proportion of participants willing to be vaccinated in the post-intervention phase was significantly higher than before the intervention, χ²(1) = 84.012, p < 0.001. Of the 125 participants who reported being unwilling or unsure about receiving the HPV vaccine before the health education intervention, 86 became willing after the intervention. Equally notable is that none of the 68 participants who initially indicated willingness to receive the HPV vaccine at the pre-intervention phase became unwilling or unsure at the post-intervention phase.

Uptake of HPV vaccine

The study participants who had heard about HPV vaccines were allowed to proceed and answer whether or not they had received the vaccine. Among 208 participants who had heard of the HPV vaccine, only 22 (10.6%) had received the vaccine. For the 186 participants who had not received the vaccine, the most common reasons were: no reason (46.2%), lack of awareness (19.4%), fear of side effects (14.2%), unavailability of vaccines (10.2%), and parental objection (6.6%). Regarding who could influence vaccination, 417 (89.7%) participants indicated that their parent/guardian is a significant person who could influence them to receive the vaccine, while others mentioned health workers (4.7%), friends (3.4%), teachers (0.9%) and religious leaders (0.9%) as sources of influence.

Evaluation of health education intervention

Before the intervention, only 55 (27.5%) participants among the 209 who completed the questionnaire had participated in any health education activity related to HPV vaccination. Based on learning outcomes from the intervention, 86–91% of the participants reported learning what HPV is and how it spreads, relationship between HPV and cervical cancer, the target age for HPV vaccination, and where to access the vaccine. In contrast, only 29.1% reported understanding the possible side effects of HPV vaccines as their learning outcomes. Following the intervention, perceptions of the HPV vaccine were positive (98.3%), and participants reported a high likelihood (95.4%) of recommending HPV vaccination to their peers.

Discussion

This study assessed the effectiveness of a school-based health education intervention in improving the knowledge of HPV and willingness to receive the HPV vaccine among female secondary school students. Overall, this study demonstrates that school-based health education intervention can play an influential role in improving HPV knowledge and willingness to receive the HPV vaccine among the study group, which represents the target population for HPV vaccination in Nigeria [5, 14]. At the baseline, 83.5% of the participants had low knowledge; however, following the intervention, 96% reached a high knowledge level with mean knowledge scores rising significantly from 5.36 ± 6.26 to 23.41 ± 2.51 (p < 0.001). Similarly, among the subgroup of participants (193) that completed the willingness question before and after the intervention, a statistically significant increase was observed (p < 0.001), as willingness to receive the HPV vaccine increased from 68 (35.2%) participants to 154 (79.8%).

Despite the success of the intervention in improving HPV knowledge, the baseline finding that more than 80% of the students had low knowledge of HPV raises concerns, as the participants are part of the target group for the national HPV vaccination campaign in Nigeria [5, 16]. This finding aligns with previous studies involving secondary school students in Nigeria and even within Ebonyi State, in which poor HPV and HPV vaccine knowledge was documented [18, 22–24]. For instance, Fagbule et al. [22] in their study involving 2,530 students across six states in Nigeria observed that only 23% and 18% of the respondents knew about HPV and HPV vaccine, respectively. These findings suggest that such poor knowledge is not location-specific in Nigeria and points to the absence of integrated school-based HPV-related education programmes, which are critical to expanding HPV vaccination coverage [11, 25]. Importantly, the dramatic improvements in students’ knowledge levels following our school-based health education intervention, along with similar trends in other school-based studies, show that poor knowledge can be addressed through carefully planned interventions [26, 27]. This strengthens the case for the integration of HPV health education into the routine school health curriculum with standardized materials and sustainability of school-based interventions across the state and other parts of Nigeria, as gains from one-off or periodic programmes such as our intervention may not be sustained without mechanisms that reinforce learning [26, 28].

The substantial improvements in willingness to receive HPV vaccine from 35.2% to 79.8% among a subgroup of participants demonstrate that health education activity can shift the disposition to receive HPV vaccine substantially. Similar improvements in willingness to be vaccinated following a school-based intervention were also documented by Rabiu and Yahuza [24] in their study among students in Kano, Nigeria. However, while willingness is an important determinant, it does not always translate into the uptake of HPV vaccines [29, 30]. In a study conducted among parents in Lagos, Akinyele et al. [29] reported that despite high willingness among 79.2% of parents, only 4% had vaccinated their daughters. This finding is instructive, as the shift from willingness to receive the HPV vaccine into actual uptake is largely mediated by vaccine cost, availability, accessibility, adequate knowledge, and socio-religious factors [11, 28, 30]. Equally important is that educational interventions alone are unlikely to translate gains in knowledge and willingness into actual vaccine uptake without corresponding structural changes, including a reliable vaccine supply, sustainable financing mechanisms, integrated service delivery models, and sustained engagement of parents and guardians, to close this gap [5, 11, 28, 31].

In this study, HPV vaccine uptake was poor, as only 10% of students who had heard of the vaccine before this study had received it. This finding is unsurprising, as previous studies have documented similar poor levels of vaccine uptake [24, 28, 29]. While Rabiu and Yahuza [24] reported that no student in their study had received the vaccine, Asgedom et al. [28] found that Nigeria had the lowest HPV vaccine uptake among sub-Saharan African countries at 3.77%. Despite the widely reported success of the National HPV vaccination campaign, which is estimated to have reached over 12 million girls following its launch in October 2023 [14], the observation of suboptimal HPV vaccine uptake levels among students who belong to the target group (9–14 years) for vaccination demonstrates that more needs to be done to expand coverage. Factors such as having no reason, lack of awareness, fear of side effects, unavailability of vaccines, and parental objection were cited by participants as reasons for not being vaccinated. These reasons have been consistently reported in the literature [23, 25, 29, 30, 32], highlighting the urgent need for interventions to consider these challenges during planning and integrate mechanisms that address them. Equally important is embedding and sustaining school-based HPV vaccination within the national HPV vaccination drive to scale coverage among the target group [11, 25, 33]. This could be strengthened by leveraging parent–teacher associations and community leaders to address the myths and safety concerns identified in this study.

Our health education intervention marked the first time more than 70% of the population participated in an HPV vaccination-related health education activity. This finding reinforces the concern that school-based health education activities are limited despite the national commitment to HPV vaccination coverage among persons aged 9–14 years in Nigeria [11, 13]. In terms of learning outcomes from the intervention, while 86–91% of the participants reported learning across most HPV and HPV vaccine content domains, only 29.1% noted that they learnt about the possible side effects. This evaluation feedback is noteworthy because safety concerns and fears about unknown side effects of HPV vaccines, coupled with misconceptions of vaccines being a source of infertility, are among the predominant reasons fuelling vaccine hesitancy in Nigeria [11, 23, 29, 34]. Future interventions should allocate more time to vaccine safety content and adopt participatory delivery methods, such as myth-busting question-and-answer sessions, testimonials, and simple infographics, potentially involving health workers, community leaders, and parents directly in addressing safety concerns. In addition, co-designing safety-related materials with students may also improve comprehension and retention of this content.

Study limitations and strength

The interpretations of the findings of this study should be done with consideration of the study’s limitations. The lack of a parallel control group due to the use of a single group pre- and post- study design precludes the definitive attribution of observed improvements in knowledge and willingness among participants to the intervention, as peer discussions or a test effect following exposure to and completion of the questionnaire at baseline may also have shaped outcomes; participants may have remembered the questions or the questions triggering learning outside of the intervention [35, 36]. The post-intervention assessment, which was conducted immediately after the health education intervention, may have influenced the substantial improvements in knowledge scores; whether these gains will translate into durable knowledge change remains unknown. Furthermore, limiting enrolment to only girls in school meant that we excluded out-of-school girls who face greater barriers in access to HPV vaccination services. Notwithstanding these limitations, this study possesses several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this study appears to be the first published school-based HPV vaccination health education intervention with a pre-post evaluation design conducted across all three senatorial zones of Ebonyi State. The adoption of best research practices, including adherence to ethical standards for research involving children, pretesting and revising study tools, a considerably large sample size, use of a multi-stage random sampling technique, and employing appropriate statistical tests (paired samples t-test and McNemar’s test), further strengthens this study.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that school-based health education interventions is effective in improving HPV knowledge and willingness to receive the HPV vaccine across all sociodemographic groups among the target population. Study findings contribute to the evidence base on the critical role of school-based health education activities in improving HPV vaccine knowledge and willingness, and strengthen the case for integrating and sustaining them in Nigeria, especially in Ebonyi State, where the burden of HPV remains disproportionately high [11, 24, 26]. While improvements in knowledge and willingness are noteworthy, translating them into HPV vaccine uptake requires political commitment to engaging parents who influence vaccination decisions and addressing barriers such as cost, vaccine availability, safety concerns, and limited access. This could include coordinating scheduled school-based outreach vaccination days and parent consent drives. Vaccine safety content in particular warrants more deliberate design attention, given that only 29.1% of participants in this study reported learning about side effects. Furthermore, future school-based intervention research is needed to assess whether improvements in knowledge and willingness translate into actual vaccine uptake and the state of knowledge retention at least six months after the intervention.
