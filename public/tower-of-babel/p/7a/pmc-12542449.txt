Cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the Holistic Nursing Competence Scale (HNCS) in Iranian nurses
Authors: Yasin Jafari Rahim Abad, Kiyana Saadati, Fatemeh Ghaffari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHolistic Nursing Competence (HNC) is a key dimension of professional nursing care, requiring precise and valid assessment tools. This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Holistic Nursing Competence Scale (HNCS) for use in the Iranian nursing context.MethodsA methodological and cross-sectional study was conducted between 20 July 2024 to 15 January 2025 202, involving 697 nurses from diverse healthcare settings in Iran. The translation process followed established guidelines, and face and content validity were confirmed by an expert panel. Psychometric validation included Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGA), and Random Forest Model (RFM) analysis. Convergent and discriminant validity were examined using Fornell–Larcker criteria, while reliability was assessed via Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, composite reliability (CR), and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Measurement invariance across genders was also tested. This methodological and cross-sectional study employed SPSS, AMOS, and R software to perform EFA, CFA, EGA, and Random Forest analyses.ResultsThe EFA results confirmed the five-dimensional structure of the instrument, comprising: Staff education and management, Ethically-oriented practice, General aptitude, Nursing care in a team, and Professional development. Factor loadings for all items were above 0.40. The CFA results supported the appropriate fit of the five-factor model of the HNCS. Fit indices, including PCFI = 0.818, PNFI = 0.782, CMIN/DF = 3.17, RMSEA = 0.079, IFI = 0.909, CFI = 0.909, and GFI = 0.901, were all within the acceptable and, in some cases, desirable range, indicating that the proposed factor structure is consistent with the empirical data of the present study. Convergent and discriminant validity were confirmed (CR > 0.7; AVE > MSV). Reliability indices were reported at satisfactory levels (α and Ω > 0.9; ICC > 0.8). EGA also corroborated the five-dimensional structure. The RFM indicated ‘General Aptitude’ contributed most (41%) to predicting HNC. Measurement invariance confirmed structural stability across genders.ConclusionThe Persian version of the HNCS was identified as a valid and reliable instrument for assessing holistic nursing competence in the Iranian context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-03951-8.Keywords: Cross-cultural adaptation, Holistic nursing, Iran, Nursing care, Psychometrics, Nursing staff

Introduction

Nursing competence is a cornerstone of the nursing profession, directly influencing patient outcomes and quality of care [20, 30, 37]. Initially centered on clinical skills, its scope has expanded to encompass ethical, managerial, educational, and communicational dimensions in response to the growing complexity of healthcare systems and diverse patient needs [51]. According to Fukada [24], nursing competence is a multidimensional construct integrating knowledge, skills, professional judgment, and values to ensure safe and ethical care in varied contexts [24]. Within this framework, holistic nursing competence emphasizes addressing patients’ physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs [31] and is supported by evidence linking it to improved clinical outcomes, patient and family satisfaction, treatment adherence, and professional performance [19, 38].

Holistic competence requires abilities in evidence-based decision-making, culturally sensitive communication, teamwork, and lifelong learning [31]. Studies indicate that it also enhances job satisfaction, self-confidence, and engagement, while reducing burnout and fostering self-reflection [2, 32]. A particularly relevant concern in Iran, where burnout prevalence among nurses ranges from 30% to 60% [1, 63]. It is further associated with better clinical performance, stress management, shorter hospital stays, fewer adverse events, and higher patient satisfaction [12, 49, 61, 63].

Despite its importance, many existing nursing competence instruments inadequately capture its multidimensional nature. Widely used measures such as the Nurse Competence Scale (NCS) [22], Slater Nursing Competencies Rating Scale [57], and Cultural Competence Assessment Instruments [50] typically emphasize specific domains clinical skills [20], functional abilities [43], or sociocultural competence [46] while neglecting areas such as ethical practice, leadership, and continuous professional development. These tools have not been validated among Iranian nurses and may overlook cultural features of ethical decision-making, teamwork, and leadership relevant to Iranian healthcare contexts [3, 24, 51]. Consequently, their application risks producing incomplete assessments of actual competence, especially where organizational and cultural expectations differ from those in the instruments’ countries of origin [6].

The Holistic Nursing Competence Scale (HNCS), developed by Takase and Teraoka [54], offers a comprehensive approach by assessing competencies in clinical practice, management, education, ethical behavior, and professional development [54]. Its multidimensional structure, combined with documented cross-cultural applicability, makes it a strong candidate for assessing nursing competence in diverse settings. The HNCS has been adapted in Türkiye [2], Poland [9] and South Korea [49] with studies confirming its reliability and validity in those contexts.

However, cultural, social, and organizational differences necessitate rigorous translation and cultural adaptation before applying such instruments in new settings [6, 58]. Cultural adaptation involves accurate translation, validation of content, and alignment of items with local norms to prevent measurement errors and misinterpretation. In Iran, features such as hierarchical healthcare structures, collectivist values, and family-centered care expectations may influence how nurses perceive and demonstrate competence. Without cultural adaptation, these factors could limit an instrument’s accuracy and relevance.

Given the demanding conditions faced by Iranian nurses high workloads, limited support, and complex duties [1] an accurate, culturally adapted assessment tool is essential for identifying strengths and weaknesses, informing training programs, and enhancing patient care quality [63]. To our knowledge, no prior study has translated, culturally adapted, and psychometrically validated the HNCS for use in Iran. Therefore, the present study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and examine the psychometric properties of the HNCS among Iranian nurses. We hypothesized that the Persian version would replicate the original factor structure and demonstrate satisfactory validity and reliability, thereby providing Iranian healthcare institutions with a comprehensive tool for evaluating and developing holistic nursing competence.

Methods

Design and participants

This methodological and cross-sectional study was conducted from 20 July 2024 to 15 January 2025. This study as part of a larger project (contract code: 724135862) at the Health Research Institute, Babol University of Medical Sciences.

The study population comprised nurses working in hospitals in the west of Mazandaran Province, Iran. Approximately 1,100 nurses employed in eight hospitals affiliated with Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences were considered eligible to participate. Inclusion criteria were: employment in a hospital affiliated with the university and at least one year of clinical nursing experience. The exclusion criterion was incomplete research instruments. In the main psychometric validation, 740 nurses agreed to participate voluntarily. After excluding incomplete responses, data from 697 nurses were analyzed. According to Munro’s guideline [40], the final sample was randomly divided into two subgroups: one subgroup of 348 participants for Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and another subgroup of 349 participants for Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). Convenience sampling was used for initial participant recruitment, conducted at the end of work shifts to ensure easier access to nurses without interrupting their professional duties. The instruments were completed by participants during this time and returned to the researcher immediately. Data were collected using two instruments: a demographic information form and the HNCS:

Demographic characteristics questionnaire

This questionnaire included variables such as age, gender, work experience, work shift, job satisfaction, income adequacy, financial status, educational level, department of service, marital status, and type of employment within the organization.

Holistic nursing competence scale (HNCS)

This instrument was developed by Takase and Teraoka [54]. It consists of 36 items and 5 dimensions.

“Staff education and management” (9 items), which reflects competencies in coordinating educational activities for staff, managing human resources, and overseeing quality improvement processes; “Ethically-oriented practice” (9 items), which covers the ability to make ethical decisions, respect patient rights, and maintain professional integrity; “General aptitude” (7 items), referring to foundational personal qualities and daily professional behaviors that support safe and effective nursing practice; “Nursing care in a team” (7 items), encompassing skills for effective collaboration, conflict resolution, and contribution to team-based care [54]; and “Professional development” (4 items), addressing self-directed learning, updating clinical knowledge, and planning for career growth. It has two parts. Part one: Includes the “General aptitude” subscale, which assesses items related to the general behaviors of nurses. Scoring for this part is on a 7-point Likert scale: 1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Occasionally, 4 = Sometimes, 5 = Often, 6 = Usually, and 7 = Always. Part two: Includes the four subscales: “Staff education and management”, “Ethically-oriented practice”, “Nursing care in a team”, and “Professional development”. Its scoring is on a 7-point Likert scale: 1 = Cannot at all, 2 = Can to a very small extent, 3 = Can to some extent, 4 = Can to an acceptable extent, 5 = Can almost, 6 = Can completely, and 7 = Can to a superior extent. The final score of the scale is obtained by summing the scores of all 36 items across the five subscales. The minimum possible total score is 36 and the maximum is 252. A higher total score indicates a higher level of holistic nursing competence. The original validation study conducted by Takase and Teraoka in 2011 reported high psychometric properties, including a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.967 and a five-factor structure explaining 59.74% of the total variance [54].

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

Following Beaton et al. [6]’s six-stage process, the HNCS was adapted into Farsi as follows [6]:

Forward translation (T): Two independent bilingual translators (native Farsi) one with nursing expertise, one without healthcare background produced T1 and T2, each with a written report.

Synthesis: Both forward translators and a recording observer compared T1 and T2 with the original instrument, resolving discrepancies by consensus to produce T-12, documented in a synthesis report.

Synthesis: Both forward translators and a recording observer compared T1 and T2 with the original instrument, resolving discrepancies by consensus to produce T-12, documented in a synthesis report.

Back translation (BT): Two new native-English translators, native to the instrument’s concepts, independently back-translated T-12 (BT1, BT2), each with a written report.

Back translation (BT): Two new native-English translators, native to the instrument’s concepts, independently back-translated T-12 (BT1, BT2), each with a written report.

Face validity

In the qualitative phase, the pre-final Farsi version of the scale was reviewed by 10 clinical nurses. Inclusion criteria for this group included having at least two years of clinical work experience and a minimum education level of a bachelor’s degree in nursing. Participants were asked to assess each item for comprehensibility, clarity, simplicity, and relevance to the clinical nursing context in Iran. Written feedback was collected, and necessary revisions were made accordingly. For the quantitative face validity, the item impact score was calculated. This index reflects how important each item is from the participants’ perspective. Each nurse rated the importance of the items using a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 = not important to 5 = very important). The impact score was calculated by multiplying the frequency of ratings ≥ 4 by the mean importance score. Items with an impact score above 1.5 were retained [44, 47]. All items in the scale met this criterion and were therefore preserved.

4.Expert committee: Forward and back translators, subject experts, a methodologist, and a language professional reviewed the original instrument, T1, T2, T-12, BT1, BT2, and all reports, ensuring semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence, and producing the pre-final version.

Content validity

In this stage, the qualitative and quantitative content validity of the HNCS was examined. For the qualitative assessment, 12 experts were invited, including nursing faculty members (with at least 5 years of academic experience), psychometricians (with prior publication experience in instrument validation), and specialists in tool development. Inclusion criteria for the expert panel included: advanced academic qualifications (at least MSc or PhD), practical experience in scale development or validation, and familiarity with clinical nursing contexts in Iran. These experts reviewed the pre-final Farsi version of the HNCS for clarity, conceptual equivalence, cultural appropriateness, and conciseness of each item. Feedback was collected using a structured evaluation form, either via email or in-person review, depending on availability. Items identified as vague, redundant, or culturally inappropriate were revised accordingly and re-evaluated in the second round. For the quantitative content validity assessment, two indices were calculated:

Content validity ratio (CVR)

Experts rated the essentiality of each item on a 3-point scale (“essential,” “useful but not essential,” and “not essential”). The CVR was calculated based on Lawshe’s [35] formula, and items with CVR values above the minimum threshold of 0.56 for 12 experts were retained [35].

Content validity index (CVI)

Experts also assessed the relevance of each item on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (“not relevant”) to 4 (“highly relevant”). The CVI was calculated as the proportion of experts assigning scores of 3 or 4 to each item. Items with a CVI score ≥ 0.79 were accepted, those between 0.70 and 0.79 were revised, and items scoring below 0.70 were discarded [45, 62].

5.Test of the prefinal version: Thirty target-population nurses completed the pre-final version; cognitive debriefing confirmed item clarity and cultural relevance.

6.Submission/appraisal: The translated and culturally adapted version was not reviewed by the original developers; therefore, equivalence was ensured through expert panel assessment and pilot testing according to standard procedures.

Submission/appraisal: The translated and culturally adapted version was not reviewed by the original developers; therefore, equivalence was ensured through expert panel assessment and pilot testing according to standard procedures.

Construct validity

The aim of assessing construct validity in this study was to ensure that the instrument accurately measures the intended concept. This was examined using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), which identify the underlying factor structure and evaluate its fit with empirical data [10, 33]. Both methods were applied in sequence to establish the validity of the instrument.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

EFA is employed when the researcher aims to identify the underlying factor structure of an instrument without prior assumptions regarding the number of factors or their interrelationships [17, 52]. This approach is particularly useful in the early stages of instrument development to detect the latent constructs being measured. In this study, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were applied to assess the suitability of the data for factor analysis [33]. Factors were extracted using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or Maximum Likelihood (ML) methods, followed by Varimax rotation to achieve a simpler and more interpretable factor structure [4]. Items with factor loadings below 0.40 were removed or revised [27].

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

Following the identification of the factor structure through EFA, the primary purpose of CFA was to test and confirm this hypothesized structure using the Maximum Likelihood Estimation method [10]. This hypothesis-driven approach enables evaluation of the degree to which the proposed factor model fits the observed data. In this study, model fit was assessed using Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and χ²/df. Adequate model fit was defined as CFI and TLI >0.90 and RMSEA < 0.08 [33].

Normality, outliers, and missing data

To identify univariate and multivariate outliers, distribution plots and the Mahalanobis distance index were used. According to established statistical guidelines, data points with a Mahalanobis distance p < 0.001 are identified as multivariate outliers [53]. Furthermore, to assess univariate and multivariate normality, skewness and kurtosis indices were calculated. Data with skewness values within the ± 3 range and kurtosis values within the ± 7 range indicate a normal distribution [33]. Additionally, Mardia’s coefficient was used for multivariate normality, and a value less than 8 was accepted as an indication of normally distributed data [11]. Based on these assessments, the data in this research were consistent with the assumption of a normal distribution. Regarding missing data, the Listwise Deletion method was used for model estimation in CFA. This method is recommended when the percentage of missing data is low and the pattern of missing data is missing completely at random (MCAR)(Little [36]. In this study, the percentage of missing data was examined using multiple imputation, and then replaced with the mean response of the participants.

Convergent and discriminant validity

Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed using the Fornell and Larcker [23] method. In this method, Average Variance Extracted (AVE), Maximum Shared Squared Variance (MSV), and Composite Reliability (CR) were calculated. An AVE value greater than 0.50 indicates adequate convergent validity, and an AVE greater than MSV confirms the discriminant validity of the scale [23].

Reliability

The purpose of the reliability assessment in this study was to evaluate both the internal consistency and stability of the instrument. Internal consistency was examined using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega (Ω) for each extracted factor, with α ≥ 0.80 considered good and ≥ 0.70 acceptable [8, 59]. Stability was assessed via a two-week test–retest procedure, calculating the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) as a measure of agreement and rank-order reliability [60]. Based on Baumgartner and Chung [5], ICC ≥ 0.40 was deemed acceptable. For this purpose, 30 nurses from teaching hospitals affiliated with Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences participated in two administrations of the instrument under identical conditions [5].

Standard error of measurement (SEM)

To assess the precision and responsiveness of the measurement instrument, three key psychometric indices were utilized: SEM, Minimal Detectable Change (MDC), and Minimal Important Change (MIC). SEM reflects the extent to which an individual’s observed score may deviate from their true score; smaller SEM values indicate higher measurement precision and greater instrument reliability [18]; Educational & Testing, [21]. In this study, SEM was calculated for each dimension of the HNCS to evaluate the instrument’s precision across its subscales.

Measurement invariance

In this study, the invariance of the instrument across gender was assessed using multi-group structural equation modeling (SEM) analysis. To examine the structural consistency of the instrument between male and female groups, several levels of invariance, including configural invariance, metric invariance, and scalar invariance, were tested [14].

Exploratory graph analysis (EGA)

In this study, to investigate the factor structure of the HNCS, the EGA method was used. EGA is a novel approach in psychometrics that, by utilizing network theory, performs data clustering within a graphical framework and identifies the factor structure of the instrument [16]. In this research, the correlation matrix between the HNCS items was first calculated, and using the Graphical LASSO method, the conditional covariance matrix was obtained to identify the optimal factor structure.

Random forest model (RFM)

The purpose of applying the RFM in this study was to perform predictive analysis and identify the most influential factors in nursing competence. RFM is a non-parametric ensemble learning algorithm that constructs multiple decision trees and combines their results via bagging to enhance prediction accuracy and reduce overfitting [7]. In this study, the total HNCS score (regression) or categorical competence levels (classification) served as the dependent variable, with five major predictor variables including key dimensions of holistic nursing competence and selected demographic/occupational characteristics. Data were split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets, and 5-fold cross-validation was employed to ensure model generalizability. This method was chosen for its capacity to handle high-dimensional, non-linear data and to provide a robust ranking of predictor importance for overall competence.

Ceiling and floor effects assessment

The aim of assessing ceiling and floor effects in this study was to determine whether the HNCS could effectively distinguish between nurses with differing competence levels. Ceiling and floor effects were considered present if more than 15% of participants achieved the highest or lowest possible scores, respectively [56]. The proportion of such cases was calculated for each dimension to evaluate the scale’s sensitivity.

Feasibility and acceptability

To evaluate the operational characteristics of the HNCS prior to psychometric analyses, a field test was conducted with 30 clinical nurses from various care units. Feasibility indicators included completion rate, completion time, and the extent of required researcher assistance. Acceptability was assessed through the proportion of voluntarily completed questionnaires, participant engagement, and qualitative feedback on item clarity and content relevance [25]. Data from this field test were used exclusively for determining feasibility and acceptability and were not included in the final psychometric analysis.

Statistical analyses

EFA analyses were performed using SPSS software version 26. CFA and measurement invariance testing were conducted using the maximum likelihood estimation method in AMOS software version 24. EGA was performed using the EGAnet package in the R software environment. RFM analysis was implemented with the Random Forest package in R. Descriptive statistics, skewness and kurtosis assessment, and Ceiling and Floor Effects analysis were also calculated using SPSS. Cronbach’s alpha was estimated via SPSS, and McDonald’s omega was estimated using the psych package in R. Convergent and discriminant validity were assessed based on the Fornell and Larcker criterion [23]. Convergent validity was conducted by calculating AVE and CR in AMOS software version 24.

Results

Demographic information of participants

A total of 697 nurses participated in this study. The average age was 34.52 years (SD = 8.37) with an average of 10.04 years (SD = 7.21) of work experience. Most participants were female (76.3%) and held a bachelor’s degree (87.1%). The majority were married (67.4%) and worked rotating shifts (77.4%). Nearly one-third of nurses (30.7%) were employed in emergency departments. Regarding job satisfaction, nearly half reported low satisfaction (49.2%), while a significant portion perceived their income as less than adequate (65.1%). Official employment status was reported by 44.0% of the sample. Furthermore, 44.8% had received in-service training related to holistic nursing empowerment. In terms of clinical autonomy, 40.3% identified as semi-independent decision-makers, with 31.4% independent and 28.3% dependent (Table 1).

Face and content validity evaluation

In the qualitative assessment of face and content validity, some items were culturally adapted. The results showed that the impact score for all items was above 1.5. Furthermore, in the CVR and CVI analysis, all items scored above 0.56 and 0.79, respectively. Accordingly, none of the items were removed.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

he suitability of the data for factor analysis was confirmed by a high KMO value (0.963) and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ² = 15,675.99, p < 0.001). Promax rotation revealed a five-factor solution explaining 63.7% of the total variance, with all items loading above 0.40, indicating strong construct validity. The extracted factors were labeled as: Staff Education and Management, Ethically-Oriented Practice, General Aptitude, Nursing Care in a Team, and Professional Development. These dimensions represent distinct yet related aspects of holistic nursing competence. Among these, the Staff Education and Management factor accounted for the largest proportion of variance (15.97%), highlighting the importance of leadership and educational roles in nursing competence. The factor structure was supported by a scree plot, which clearly indicated five factors (Fig. 1). Detailed factor loadings and communalities for each item are provided in Table 2.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

The CFA results supported the proposed five-factor structure of the HNCS. The first-order model demonstrated acceptable to good fit indices: PCFI = 0.818, PNFI = 0.782, CMIN/DF = 3.17, RMSEA = 0.079 (90% CI: 0.075–0.083), IFI = 0.909, CFI = 0.909, and GFI = 0.901. All standardized factor loadings exceeded 0.40, indicating strong item-factor associations (Fig. 2). The inter-factor correlations ranged from weak to moderate, confirming the factors are related but distinct, supporting discriminant validity. The second-order CFA, assessing a higher-order factor underlying the five dimensions, also showed acceptable fit: PCFI = 0.822, PNFI = 0.785, CMIN/DF = 3.22, RMSEA = 0.080 (90% CI: 0.076–0.084), IFI = 0.906, CFI = 0.906, and GFI = 0.899 (Fig. 3). Overall, these findings confirm the structural validity of the HNCS as a multidimensional construct (Table 3).

Convergent and discriminant validity

Table 4 demonstrates that the HNCS possesses satisfactory convergent validity, with all factors exhibiting CR values above the recommended threshold of 0.7 and AVE values exceeding 0.5. These metrics confirm that the items within each factor are strongly correlated and effectively represent their respective constructs. Furthermore, the relatively high AVE values alongside clear conceptual distinctions among factors support the scale’s discriminant validity. This indicates that the identified factors measure distinct aspects of holistic nursing competence without excessive overlap.

Reliability

As presented in Table 4, the HNCS demonstrates excellent internal consistency, with α and Ω coefficients exceeding 0.9 across all factors. CR values were also above the recommended threshold of 0.7, confirming robust construct reliability. Additionally, ICC above 0.8 indicate good stability of the instrument over time or across raters. Together, these reliability indices confirm the HNCS as a highly trustworthy and consistent tool for measuring holistic nursing competence.

Feasibility and acceptability

The average completion time for the HNCS was 14 min, indicating ease of use in a clinical environment. The full response rate (without missing items) was 98%, and no researcher assistance was required, confirming satisfactory feasibility. Acceptability was supported by a 100% return rate and voluntary participation of all nurses. Qualitative feedback described the instrument as “relevant to daily activities,” “simple to understand,” and “coherent in content,” with only a few remarks on potential length. No reports of fatigue or withdrawal were recorded during completion.

Standard error of measurement (SEM)

Table 5 presents SEM, MDC, and MIC values for each factor of the HNCS. SEM values ranged from 1.34 (“Professional Development”) to 3.87 (“Ethically-Oriented Practice”), indicating low measurement error and high precision across subscales. Corresponding MDC values ranged from 3.71 to 10.73, reflecting the minimal change required to be confident that observed differences are not due to measurement error. Discussion

In this study, the process of cultural adaptation and examination of the psychometric properties of the HNCS was conducted. One of the key stages in this process was the investigation of the instrument’s factor structure, which was performed through EFA and CFA. The findings from the EFA in the present study confirmed the five-factor structure of the HNCS. The KMO index of 0.963 and the significance of Bartlett’s Test (p < 0.001) indicated sample adequacy and the suitability of the data for factor analysis, which is consistent with the criteria proposed by Kline [33] for exploratory analyses [33]. In this analysis, five main factors, including “Staff education and management”, “Ethically-oriented practice”, “General aptitude”, “Nursing care in a team”, and “Professional development”, were identified, which collectively explained 63.7% of the total variance of the scale; this value was higher than the explained variance reported in the original version of the instrument (59.74%) by Takase and Teraoka [54]. All items had factor loadings greater than 0.40, ranging from 0.469 to 0.967, which aligns with standard thresholds for acceptance in exploratory analyses [27]. The use of Promax rotation, given the moderate correlation among factors, was an appropriate approach that allowed for a better interpretation of the factor structure. This structure is consistent with the results of studies conducted in other countries such as Türkiye [2] and South Korea [49]. Additionally, in Poland, Wysokiński et al. (2023) applied exploratory factor analysis and identified a two-factor solution that explained 50.2% of the total variance. The Polish version demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.921 and 0.962 for the two subscales, respectively. Although the Polish adaptation did not replicate the five-factor structure, its psychometric properties support the scale’s reliability and cultural applicability within the Polish nursing education context [9]. The results obtained from the EFA not only clarified the internal structure of the instrument but also provided a strong empirical basis for subsequent analyses, including CFA and EGA.

The CFA results supported the appropriate fit of the five-factor model of the HNCS. Fit indices, including PCFI = 0.818, PNFI = 0.782, CMIN/DF = 3.17, RMSEA = 0.079, IFI = 0.909, CFI = 0.909, and GFI = 0.901, were all within the acceptable and, in some cases, desirable range, indicating that the proposed factor structure is consistent with the empirical data of the present study [29]. Although the RMSEA value of 0.079 demonstrates an acceptable fit, it does not meet the more stringent criterion for excellent fit (RMSEA < 0.06). This slightly elevated RMSEA may be attributed to the relatively large number of observed variables and the complexity of the model, both of which can influence this index even when the overall model fit is satisfactory. This suggests that while the model shows satisfactory construct validity, further refinement could improve its fit. These findings confirm that the dimensions identified in the exploratory factor analysis also possess internal consistency within the confirmatory model framework, and the conceptual structure of the instrument has the necessary stability. Comparison of the results of the present study with the original version of the instrument shows that the five-factor structure of the HNCS is also applicable and adaptable in the Iranian population, which supports the cross-cultural validity of the scale. Furthermore, the factor loadings of all items were greater than 0.40, which is consistent with accepted standards for confirmatory factor analysis and indicates the significant contribution of each item to its corresponding factor [33]. The alignment between the findings of the exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses also indicates the theoretical and empirical coherence of the HNCS structure and validates its utility in assessing the multiple dimensions of nursing competence in clinical settings.

The CFA results also provided a suitable platform for supplementary analyses such as the assessment of measurement invariance. In this study, using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MG-CFA), the equivalence of the HNCS factor structure between two groups of male and female nurses was examined. The results showed that at three levels of analysis, including metric invariance, structural covariances, and overall model fit indices, there were no significant differences between the groups. Specifically, changes in the comparative fit index (∆CFI) were less than 0.01 at all stages, and the ∆χ² values were also non-significant, both of which are criteria indicating the establishment of structural invariance at these levels [13]. However, at the level of residual invariance, the fully constrained model did not show an adequate fit, and differences in error variance between the two groups were observed. This may indicate minor differences in responses to some items, which could be due to individual factors, cultural aspects, or different professional experiences of male and female nurses. Additionally, residual non-invariance could reflect subtle cultural nuances influencing item interpretation or the need for further refinement and wording adjustments in certain items to enhance equivalence across groups. Nevertheless, since the fundamental levels of invariance (configural, metric, scalar) were confirmed, it can be concluded that the HNCS possesses acceptable inter-group validity for comparisons based on gender.

In this study, to investigate the internal structure of the HNCS and re-evaluate its factorial validity, EGA was employed as a novel and complementary method in analyzing the dimensional structure. The results of this analysis indicated that the five-factor structure of the instrument was well-preserved, and the clustering of items was aligned with the findings from EFA and CFA. The use of the Walktrap algorithm for identifying clusters and the leading eigenvalue method for determining the number of dimensions provided an objective framework for network structure analysis. The results from bootstrap sampling also showed that the median number of extracted dimensions was 5, and all bootstrap samples maintained this number, with this structural stability indicating high coherence of the scale’s dimensions [15]. Furthermore, the low standard error and relatively narrow confidence interval confirm that the identified structure is reliably reproducible. Network analysis, compared to traditional factor analysis methods, offers advantages such as not relying on the normal distribution of data, preventing model overfitting, and transparency in the relationships between items [26]. The use of EGA as a complementary method was particularly justified given the non-Western context of the study, where traditional factor models may not fully capture culturally influenced patterns of relationships between items.In this study, convergent and discriminant validity were assessed using the indices proposed by Fornell and Larcker [23]. The results showed that CR in all dimensions was higher than 0.90, exceeding the desirable threshold of 0.70 and indicating high internal consistency among the items. AVE values ranged between 0.610 and 0.695, confirming adequate convergent validity. In all cases, AVE exceeded MSV, supporting discriminant validity [23]. These findings demonstrate that the HNCS possesses strong construct validity in the Iranian context. Comparable results were observed in previous Korean validations, which reported similarly high convergent and discriminant validity indices [49]. Nevertheless, it should be noted that cultural factors, such as differences in professional role expectations and organizational environments, may influence perceptions of competence and partially account for variations in psychometric parameters across countries.

The Random Forest Model was applied to explore the relative contribution of each competence dimension in predicting the total HNCS score, as this non-parametric method can handle complex interactions and rank variables based on importance. The results indicated that the “General aptitude” dimension had the largest predictive importance. This dimension, which acts as the central core of the nurse’s understanding of their overall capabilities, encompasses deeper internal aspects of professional competence such as critical thinking, self-assessment, and evidence-based decision-making. The high importance of this component suggests that nursing competence is not merely limited to technical and practical skills but relies heavily on cognitive and reflective abilities. In particular, the two items with the highest weight, “Observing things/matters objectively” and “Identifying issues surrounding self by exploring the truth,” highlight the fundamental role of introspection and unbiased observation. These capabilities are key pillars in dealing with complex clinical situations, problem-solving, adapting to changing conditions, and making accurate ethical and care-related decisions. Based on these insights, training programs should place particular emphasis on cultivating critical thinking and reflective practice to enhance holistic nursing competence. This interpretation is consistent with previous research highlighting the importance of reflective and cognitive competencies in professional nursing practice [24, 41, 54].

The examination of reliability indices in the present study indicates the high trustworthiness of the HNCS instrument in assessing holistic nursing competence in the Iranian population. Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega, both used to measure internal consistency, showed values higher than 0.90 in all factors; these values exceed the proposed threshold of 0.70, which is considered the desirable level of reliability, and indicate excellent internal consistency among the items of each factor [42, 55]. CR in all factors was higher than 0.70, indicating acceptable construct reliability [48].

Furthermore, the ICC, which is used to examine temporal reliability or inter-rater reliability, was reported to be above 0.80 in all dimensions of the instrument. According to the criteria proposed by Koo & Li [34], values higher than 0.75 indicate good to excellent reliability [34]. This finding implies that the HNCS is capable of providing consistent results under different temporal conditions or among similar groups. The alignment of these three key indices alpha, omega, and ICC with each other reinforces the psychometric strength of the instrument. Numerous researchers have emphasized the importance of using multiple indices to confirm instrument reliability [28, 39]. This consistency among the indices in the present study confirms that the HNCS possesses high reliability for the accurate and stable assessment of nurses’ professional competence and can be effectively used in clinical, educational, and research contexts. However, the very high Cronbach’s alpha coefficients (>0.95) may indicate potential redundancy among certain items, suggesting that future studies could explore item reduction strategies without compromising reliability.

Cronbach’s alpha for all 36 items of the Korean version of HNCS was 0.969. Furthermore, Cronbach’s alpha values for each sub-domain were 0.933 for “Staff education and management”, 0.928 for “Ethically-oriented practice”, 0.914 for “General aptitude”, 0.919 for “Nursing care in a team”, and 0.899 for “Professional development” [49]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient in the original study for HNCS was 0.967, indicating its appropriate reliability and validity [54]. In the Turkish adaptation study of the HNCS, the internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) was reported as 0.97 for the total scale, and the coefficients for all subscales exceeded 0.80, indicating high internal consistency across all dimensions. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the two measurement times for the total scale score was 0.83 (p < 0.01), for “General aptitude” 0.76, for “Staff education and management” 0.82, for “Ethically-oriented practice” and “Nursing care in a team” 0.72, and for “Professional development” 0.61 (p < 0.01)(Aydin & Hiçdurmaz [2]. Similarly, in the Polish adaptation of the HNCS by Wysokiński et al. (2023), the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.921 for Subscale A and 0.962 for Subscale B. These values are comparable to the original Japanese version (α = 0.967), indicating that the Polish version maintains a satisfactory level of reliability within its cultural context [9]. These high reliability indices not only confirm internal consistency but also suggest that the instrument is capable of producing stable scores over time and across different respondent groups. Such consistency is critical for longitudinal assessments and for evaluating the impact of training interventions. However, it should also be noted that very high Cronbach’s alpha coefficients (above 0.90) may indicate potential item redundancy, suggesting that future research could explore whether certain items could be streamlined without compromising the scale’s reliability.

Conclusions

The HNCS, after a rigorous process of translation and cultural adaptation strictly following the guidelines of Beaton et al. [6] and comprehensive psychometric evaluation, has proven to be a valid and reliable instrument for assessing holistic nursing competence in Iran. The 36‑item scale encompasses five key dimensions: Staff education and management, Ethically‑oriented practice, General aptitude, Nursing care in a team, and Professional development. Multiple analyses including EFA, CFA, and EGA confirmed its multidimensional structure, while discriminant and convergent validity, as well as reliability, were supported by indices such as Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, CR, and ICC. Given its strong factor loadings, conceptual clarity, and applicability across genders, the HNCS can serve as a practical tool for assessment, training, and professional development within the Iranian healthcare system.

Strengths and limitations

This study, for the first time in Iran, has translated and examined the psychometric properties of the HNCS. The cultural adaptation process of this instrument was meticulously conducted according to the guidelines of Beaton et al. [6]. Among the most important methodological strengths of this study is the use of a multi-stage approach in analyzing the factor structure of the instrument; such that the instrument’s structure was first identified using EFA, then confirmed with CFA. Subsequently, for the first time in the literature related to HNCS, EGA was utilized as a novel method based on graph algorithms to identify conceptual clusters and confirm the factor structure. Also, the use of RFM in this research enabled the identification of key components and items with the highest predictive power. Another key advantage of this research was conducting the Measurement Invariance test based on gender. From a statistical perspective as well, the data were of desirable quality. The examination of normality indices (skewness, kurtosis), ceiling and floor effects, and the assessment of convergent and discriminant validity based on Fornell and Larcker’s criteria all confirmed the psychometric adequacy of the instrument. Furthermore, in the present study, the reliability of the instrument was examined using several statistical methods. Finally, the large sample size (n = 697) increased the analytical power, the validity of the results, and the relative generalizability of the findings to the nursing population.

Despite these numerous strengths, this study also has a few limitations. For instance, the use of convenience sampling may affect the generalizability of the results. It is recommended that future research use random sampling methods to reduce bias. Data collection from a specific region (Mazandaran) may subject the findings to the influence of the specific cultural and organizational characteristics of this region. Investigating this instrument in other provinces is recommended. There is a possibility of response bias in self-report data, as participants might report their performance better than reality. Additionally, although the RMSEA value indicated acceptable model fit, it was slightly above the threshold for excellent fit, suggesting potential areas for model refinement. Finally, possible uncontrolled cultural biases specific to the regional context could have influenced responses and the perceived relevance of certain items.

Implications for nursing

The application of HNCS in educational and clinical settings can play a key role in improving the processes of education, assessment, and professional development for nurses. By providing a structured framework for measuring various nursing competencies, this instrument allows nursing managers to identify the strengths and weaknesses of nurses in diverse areas such as team-based care, ethical decision-making, managerial competencies, professional growth, and cognitive competencies. The systematic use of HNCS in periodic nursing assessments can lead to the design of targeted and needs-based educational programs, which ultimately contribute to enhancing the quality of nursing services, increasing patient safety, and improving treatment outcomes. Moreover, by focusing on the assessment of holistic nursing competence, which is one of the main pillars of comprehensive and person-centered care, this instrument provides a more accurate evaluation of nurses’ professional performance and serves as a valid basis for educational, managerial, and policy-making decisions in the healthcare system. From a human resources management perspective, the continuous use of HNCS can improve the process of identifying training needs, career advancement, and individual development planning, thereby increasing the productivity and effectiveness of care teams.
