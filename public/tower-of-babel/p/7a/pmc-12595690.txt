SUPERMAN requires its EAR motif to recruit the corepressor TOPLESS and to function as a cadastral regulator in floral patterning
Authors: Anthony S. Wokasch, Owen Albanese, Joshua Terao, Maryam Yamadi, Aidan Deneen, Ashley Bastin, Sofia I. Palau, Jose Calvo Palacios, Charlotte S. Foege, Mariam R. Coulibaly, Naden T. Krogan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe spatial regulation of gene expression is crucial for the development of complex structures such as the angiosperm flower. The combinatorial actions of floral organ identity genes, which are expressed in well-defined domains, specify the fate of lateral organs arranged in concentric whorls in the flower. SUPERMAN (SUP) contributes to this regulation by establishing a boundary between floral whorls 3 and 4 in Arabidopsis, preventing both the central spread of APETALA3 and PISTILLATA gene expression and overproliferation of male reproductive organs. SUP operates as a transcriptional repressor and harbors an ERF-associated amphiphilic repression (EAR) motif.ResultsIn this work, we show that the corepressor TOPLESS (TPL) cooperates with SUP to repress APETALA3 and PISTILLATA and suppress male reproductive fate in the flower. Additionally, we demonstrate that SUP forms a physical interaction with TPL that is dependent on its EAR motif. We also specifically mutate the EAR motif of endogenous SUP in planta, showing that even a subtle change is sufficient to cause strong sup defects and interrupt TPL association.ConclusionsCollectively, our results indicate that SUP requires its EAR motif to recruit TPL and function as a cadastral regulator, with both proteins cooperatively establishing a gene expression boundary necessary for floral patterning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07553-z.

Background

The proper initiation and specification of organs in multicellular organisms requires strict spatial control of gene expression. In angiosperms such as Arabidopsis thaliana, lateral organs initiated by the floral meristem (FM) are precisely arranged in concentric whorls. According to the original ABC model of floral patterning, the fates of these organs are specified by the combinatorial action of three classes of floral organ identity genes which operate in tightly regulated spatial domains [1, 2]. In this framework, A-class specifies sepals in outer whorl 1, a combination of A- and B-class specifies petals in whorl 2, B- and C-class specifies stamens in whorl 3, and C-class specifies carpels in central whorl 4. Furthermore, APETALA1 (AP1) and APETALA2 (AP2) are posited to operate as A-class genes, APETALA3 (AP3) and PISTILLATA (PI) comprise B-class, and AGAMOUS (AG) functions as C-class in Arabidopsis. Precise boundaries of these gene activities are crucial for proper floral patterning, as ABC gene misexpression commonly results in homeotic conversions of lateral organ identities [3–7].

Numerous transcriptional regulators that delineate the borders of ABC activity in developing flowers have been identified and include the ABC genes themselves. For instance, mutual antagonism between A-class and C-class genes establishes a boundary between the periphery and center of the flower which physically separates these two functions [3, 5, 8]. Similarly, multiple cadastral regulators spatially restrict the B-class genes AP3 and PI to whorls 2 and 3 in Arabidopsis flowers. For instance, APETALA2 (AP2) associates with the Groucho/Tup1-type corepressor TOPLESS (TPL) and HISTONE DEACETYLASE19 (HDA19) to maintain the outer B-class gene expression boundary between whorls 1 and 2. Mutation of these transcriptional repressors leads to the spread of AP3 and PI expression into whorl 1 lateral organs resulting in sepal-to-petal homeotic conversions [7], as predicted by the ABC model [1, 2].

The Arabidopsis transcription factor SUPERMAN (SUP), also known as FLORAL MUTANT 10 (FLO10), is critical for establishing the inner boundary of B-class gene expression between whorls 3 and 4 [4, 9, 10]. Consistent with this cadastral role, SUP expression begins at this boundary at approximately the time B-class gene expression is initiated in floral development [10–13]. In sup flowers, AP3 and PI expression spreads centrally, stamen number increases, and carpel identity is compromised [4, 9, 10, 13]. The extra male reproductive organs arise from whorl 4 cells initially destined for female fate which instead begin to ectopically express B-class genes [13]. Typically, stamens of sup are then reiteratively amplified by the sequential initiation of supernumerary organ whorls [4, 9]. While SUP spatially restricts B-class gene expression in a cell autonomous fashion, it operates in a cell non-autonomous manner to limit stamen production. In this latter role, SUP directly represses the expression of auxin biosynthesis genes [14]. De-repression of these genes in sup results in augmented local auxin accumulation, leading to increased floral organ initiation from an enlarged FM with prolonged proliferation. Internal to the supernumerary stamens, sup flowers ultimately produce a central gynoecium displaying varying degrees of ectopic male reproductive identity, consistent with spread of B-class gene expression towards the interior of the FM [4, 9].

At the molecular level, SUP contains a C2H2-type zinc finger DNA-binding domain near its amino-terminus, a central region rich in serine and proline residues, a basic domain, and two overlapping leucine zipper-like motifs [10]. A consensus ERF-associated amphiphilic repression (EAR) motif (DLELRL) is located at the protein’s carboxy-terminus [15, 16]. The EAR motif, a potent transcriptional repression motif in plants, was first identified in members of the Ethylene Response Factor (ERF) family of transcription factors [15]. Shortly thereafter, the EAR motif of SUP was functionally characterized in plant transient expression assays, where the ability of SUP to repress target reporter gene activity was shown to depend on its EAR motif-containing carboxy terminus [16]. Consistent with its role in transcriptional repression, translational fusion of the SUP carboxy terminus to a known transcriptional activator in Arabidopsis resulted in the recapitulation of the activator’s loss-of-function phenotypes [17]. Transient expression assays further refined the minimal SUP EAR motif to a short stretch of amino acids (DLELRL) necessary and sufficient for gene repression [18]. Moreover, the mutation of specific residues in the EAR motif showed that the hydrophobic leucine residues were particularly important for this effect [18], in agreement with site-directed mutagenesis performed on EAR motifs of other transcriptional regulators [19].

Important insight into the mechanism of EAR motif-mediated repression was revealed when the corepressor TPL was identified as a physical interactor [20]. Specifically, TPL was shown to be recruited by the EAR motif of an Aux/IAA transcriptional regulator integral to the repression of auxin inducible gene expression [20]. An analogous repression module was subsequently identified in jasmonic acid (JA) signal transduction, where the EAR motif-containing protein NINJA associates with TPL, and TOPLESS RELATED (TPR) family members, to repress JA-responsive target genes [21]. In floral patterning, AP2 relies on its EAR motif to recruit TPL and, in turn, HDA19 to negatively regulate floral organ identify genes, including repression of B-class genes in whorl 1 lateral organs [7]. Therefore, multiple signaling and developmental processes are regulated by transcription factors that employ an EAR motif to physically recruit TPL/TPR to transcriptionally repress target genes.

Despite its early identification, the mechanism and significance of the SUP EAR motif in the context of floral patterning has been described in very few studies. For instance, transgenic Arabidopsis lines which overexpress SUP lacking its carboxy terminus exhibit sup loss-of-function phenotypes, suggesting a dominant negative effect in the flower possibly due to the absence of the EAR motif [16]. As yet, however, direct mutational analyses that target the EAR motif of endogenous SUP are lacking in planta. Similarly, despite the known close association between the EAR motif and the corepressor TPL in many other transcriptional contexts [22–24], such a relationship has not been described for SUP. In this study, we show that floral defects and gene misexpression displayed by tpl-1 share commonalities with that of sup mutants. We further demonstrate that SUP physically interacts with the corepressor TPL and that this interaction depends on its EAR motif. Moreover, we use CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing to show that the EAR motif of SUP is crucial for the repression of B-class gene expression at the boundary of floral whorls 3 and 4 in planta. Thus, collectively these results are consistent with a model in which SUP physically recruits TPL through its EAR motif to restrict B-class gene expression in the center of the flower.

Methods

Plant material and growth

All plants in this study were of the Landsberg erecta ecotype of Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh. Plants were grown on soil in a growth chamber cycling between 16 h light at 23 °C and 8 h dark at 21 °C. Genetic analyses used previously described mutants tpl-1 [25], tpl-2 tpr1 tpr2 tpr3 tpr4 [7], sup-1 [4] and hda19-1 [26]. Genome-edited alleles sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) were generated in this study using CRISPR-Cas9. To genotype plants for tpl-1 and sup-1 mutant alleles, DNA was isolated from plant tissue and subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis using primers in Supplementary Table S1. Because the severity of sup-1 reproductive defects varies along the acropetal axis of the inflorescence stem [4], quantitative assessments of stamen number and whorl 4 organ identity were performed on the first ten initiated flowers of each genetic background.

CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing

A SUP guide RNA (gRNA) targeting the EAR motif coding sequence (Supplementary Table S1) was incorporated into expression vector pHEE401E [27] using Golden Gate assembly [28]. The resulting pHEE401E construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 for transformation into Arabidopsis wild-type plants. Hygromycin-resistant plant transformants were genotyped by PCR using SUP primers (Supplementary Table S1), and the resulting PCR products were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing to test for disruption of the targeted region. Genome-edited lines were backcrossed to wild-type plants to remove the pHEE401E CRISPR-Cas9 transgene by segregation prior to detailed phenotypic analysis.

RNA in situ hybridization

RNA in situ hybridization experiments with digoxigenin-labeled riboprobes were performed as previously published [7]. Probe sequences used to detect expression of AP3 and PI have been reported [11, 12]. Floral stage numbers attributed to tissue sections follow published convention [29].

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays were performed in the background of yeast strain MaV203 using Invitrogen ProQuest expression plasmids (pDBLeu and pEXP-AD502) and product protocols (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The GAL4 DNA-binding domain and full-length TPL fusion construct (DBD-TPL) in pDBLeu has been described previously [20]. Coding sequences for SUP, mSUP and N-TPR were amplified by PCR (Supplementary Table S1) and cloned into the SalI-NotI sites of pDBLeu or pEXP-AD502. β-galactosidase filter assays were performed as previously reported [20]. HIS3 reporter gene activation was assessed by growing transformed yeast strains to an OD600 of 0.4, generating 10-fold serial dilutions, and spot-plating on SC-Leu-Trp control media or SC-Leu-Trp-His with 50 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole (3-AT) selective media. To verify yeast protein expression, Western blots were stained with 0.1% Ponceau S in 5% v/v acetic acid, washed in water, and analyzed using anti-GAL4 DBD (sc-577) or anti-GAL4 AD (sc-1663) antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, Dallas, TX, USA).

In vitro pull-down assays

Expression and purification of recombinant GST-N-TPL protein using Glutathione Sepharose™ High Performance slurry (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) was previously reported [20]. Streptavidin-Binding Peptide (SBP)-tagged SUP (DLELRL), mSUP (DLEARA) and mSUP (DLELL) proteins were expressed in vitro using TNT SP6 Coupled Reticulocyte Lysate System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The wild-type SUP coding sequence for the SBP expression construct was cloned from vector pENTR/D-TOPO [30]. Other expression constructs were made by PCR-amplifying the coding sequences of mSUP variants using primers with terminal SfiI recognition sites (Supplementary Table S1) and incorporating into vector pENTR223.1-Sfi [22]. The SUP genes in each pENTR construct were introduced into vector pIX-SBP::ccdB-CAMR [31] using Gateway™ LR Clonase™ II Enzyme mix (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For in vitro expression, each pIX-SBP vector (1 µg) was added to 50 µL protein expression reactions and incubated at 30 °C for 5 h. For pull-down assays, SBP-tagged SUP or mSUP recombinant protein reactions were diluted with 1 mL Extraction Buffer [32], mixed with 100 µL glutathione sepharose beads coated with GST-N-TPL or control GST protein, and incubated at 4 °C for 45 min with gentle rotation. Beads were washed four times with 2 mL of Extraction Buffer, and protein samples were eluted by adding 100 µL of 2x Laemmli Sample Buffer and boiling for 5 min. Western blots of eluted samples were stained with 0.1% Ponceau S in 5% v/v acetic acid, destained in water, and analyzed using anti-SBP-tag (MAB10764) antibody (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA). Quantification of signal intensity on anti-SBP Western blots was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA), with signal intensities of pull-down fractions normalized against input.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) on reproductive apices was performed as previously described [33]. Primer sequences used for assessment of gene expression levels are provided (Supplementary Table S1).

Microscopy

An Olympus SZX16 dissecting microscope was used to capture live plant tissue images, while an Olympus BX61 compound microscope was used to acquire RNA in situ hybridization images (Olympus, Center Valley, PA, USA). Floral tissue prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.2 M sodium cacodylate, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and dried in hexamethyldisilazane. SEM micrographs were captured using a JEOL JSM-IT100 scanning electron microscope (JEOL USA, Peabody, MA, USA).

Results

SUP and TPL restrict male reproductive fate in the center of the flower

SUP has long been recognized as a key regulator of flower development, as loss-of-function sup alleles show amplification and central expansion of male reproductive fate [4, 9]. Transient expression assays further demonstrate that SUP operates as a transcriptional repressor, a function mapped to the EAR motif located at its carboxy terminus [16]. Despite these advancements, direct experimental links between the role of SUP in floral patterning and the activity of its EAR motif are not well established. To this end, we sought a possible relationship between SUP and the corepressor TPL based on proven physical associations between TPL and EAR motif-containing transcriptional regulators [22–24]. Consistent with this possibility, TPL is expressed throughout early stages of floral development [7], exhibiting clear overlap with SUP activity domains at the boundary of floral whorls 3 and 4 [10, 13]. If SUP relies on the direct recruitment of TPL via its EAR motif to correctly pattern the flower, sup and tpl mutants may share phenotypic abnormalities. Wild-type Arabidopsis flowers typically house six stamens in whorl 3 and two fused carpels in whorl 4 (Fig. 1A, E, I, J). The tpl-1 allele, a missense mutation (N176H) that acts as a dominant negative for the TPL/TPR gene family [26], is known to affect various aspects of floral patterning. For instance, tpl-1 flowers display sepal-to-petal homeotic conversions due to ectopic floral organ identity gene expression in whorl 1 (Fig. 1B) [7]. Here, we report that the central gynoecium of tpl-1 variably consists of incompletely fused organ chimeras displaying both stamen and carpel fate (Fig. 1B, F, I). This abnormal spread of male reproductive fate into the central floral whorl is also commonly displayed by sup-1 (Fig. 1C, G, I) [4], which harbors a premature stop at codon position 22 [10]. These observations suggest that like SUP, the corepressor TPL plays a role in the repression of stamen fate in the central floral whorl.

The spread of male identity in the center of sup-1 tpl-1 double mutant flowers is more pronounced than in either single mutant background (Fig. 1D, H, I). For instance, the central-most organs produced by sup-1 tpl-1 flowers are commonly comprised exclusively of stamen fate and devoid of obvious carpel characteristics (Fig. 1D, I). Under our growth conditions, tpl-1 and sup-1 single mutant flowers consistently display at least some carpel identity in whorl 4 organs, typically evidenced by distal stigmatic tissue (Fig. 1B, C, F, G, I). When carpel identity was detected in the center of sup-1 tpl-1 flowers, this often manifested as highly reduced, sterile filamentous organ(s) with distal stigmatic papillae cells (Fig. 1H). The synergistic interaction between sup-1 and tpl-1 further suggests that both factors act cooperatively towards the repression of male fate in the center of the flower. Such genetic synergism has been observed for other floral mutant combinations involving a transcription factor and the transcriptional corepressor it physically recruits [6, 34].

Unlike sup-1 mutants, flowers of tpl-1 do not display increases in stamen number (Fig. 1J). Moreover, the average stamen number of sup-1 tpl-1 flowers is intermediate between those of each single mutant (Fig. 1J). These results suggest that TPL may not cooperate with SUP to restrict the proliferation of supernumerary stamens, or that SUP may interact with additional redundantly acting factors to perform this role. Further, interpretation of these observations may be complicated by the various other roles TPL plays in regulating floral gene expression (see Discussion).

SUP physically interacts with TPL through its EAR motif

Since the transcription factor SUP and corepressor TPL cooperatively restrict male reproductive fate in whorl 4 of the flower, they may physically interact in a regulatory complex. Moreover, if SUP associates with this corepressor, it may interact via its EAR motif, which is found in other TPL/TPR binding partners [22–24]. To test for physical association between SUP and TPL, a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay was performed using activation of the HIS3 reporter gene as a read-out for interaction (Fig. 2A). This assay demonstrated a strong interaction between the two factors. Since previous transient expression assays identified the three conserved leucine residues of the EAR motif as being important for conferring transcriptional repression [18], these were disrupted and the assay repeated. Indeed, conversion of the latter two leucines to alanine or glycine residues by site-directed mutagenesis obstructed association with TPL (Fig. 2A). These results were confirmed with Y2H assays using expression of the lacZ reporter gene as an indicator of protein-protein interaction (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Negative Y2H results with EAR motif-mutated SUP variants were not due to insufficient protein levels in yeast, as Western blot showed comparable expression from all tested SUP constructs (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

To further confirm SUP-TPL physical association, we performed in vitro pull-down assays with the amino-terminus of TPL (N-TPL) which harbors domains known to bind to the EAR motifs of other protein interactors [7, 20, 35]. Similar to Y2H, SUP was successfully pulled down by TPL in these assays (Fig. 2B). In addition, robust interaction depended on the EAR motif, as the conversion of two of its conserved leucine residues into alanine residues weakened association with TPL (Fig. 2B). Specifically, quantification of Western blot signal intensities showed that mutation of the SUP EAR motif reduced TPL binding to 14% (average from two independent replicates) relative to wild type SUP protein (Supplementary Fig. S3). Collectively, Y2H and pull-down results indicate that SUP physically interacts with TPL and that this association depends on the EAR motif of SUP. Along with our genetic analyses, these biochemical results suggest a mechanism in which SUP recruits TPL via its EAR motif to spatially control gene expression, ensuring repression of male reproductive fate in the center of the flower.

Since TPL is a member of the TPL/TPR gene family whose members exhibit partial functional redundancy in floral patterning [7], we used Y2H to test the potential of SUP to bind to the amino-terminal domains of the four TPRs (N-TPR1 – N-TPR4). These assays demonstrated that SUP can indeed physically interact with all four TPRs and that at least one of these interactions (with TPR3) depends on its EAR motif (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). These observations suggest that SUP may pattern the flower not only in conjunction with TPL, but also with TPRs, and that the involvement of the EAR motif in these different associations may vary.

SUP requires an intact EAR motif to correctly pattern the flower

Because the importance of the EAR motif in SUP-mediated floral patterning has not been specifically established, we aimed to mutate the corresponding coding sequence of the endogenous SUP gene and assess its effect on floral development. To this end, we designed a CRISPR-Cas9 gRNA expression construct predicted to cleave between the codons of the two latter leucine amino acids of the EAR motif (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S1). After transforming this construct into Arabidopsis, we isolated two genome-edited lines with in-frame deletions disrupting the EAR motif to different extents. The first was a 3 bp deletion that effectively removes the R residue of the SUP EAR motif, changing its protein sequence from DLELRL to DLELL (Fig. 3A). The second was a 42 bp deletion that eliminates the entire EAR motif along with the preceding 8 amino acid residues (Fig. 3A). The developmental defects of these novel sup alleles with disrupted EAR motifs, named sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR), resemble the strong sup-1 allele in many respects. For instance, a substantial proportion of their flowers produce a chimeric central gynoecium with characteristics of both stamen and carpel fate (Fig. 3B-F), indicative of ectopic spread of male reproductive identity into whorl 4. In addition, both genome-edited alleles have increases in stamen number relative to wild-type flowers (Fig. 3G), reflecting enhanced proliferation of whorl 3 organs. In sup-1 flowers, expansion of stamen identity corresponds with central expansion of B-class gene expression [10, 13], and this ectopic expression can be detected by RT-qPCR on floral bud tissue (Fig. 3H; two biological replicates). A similar trend in AP3 and PI expression is observed in sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) flowers (Fig. 3H). When taken together, these observations indicate that SUP function in early floral patterning strictly depends on the presence of an intact EAR motif. By extension, these results suggest that SUP performs these developmental roles, at least in part, through the physical recruitment of TPL/TPR.

An alternative explanation for the floral abnormalities exhibited by sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) is that their deletions, positioned at the 3’end of the coding sequence, negatively affect their own levels of transcription. Consistent with this notion, examination of translational reporter genes containing different regions of SUP transcript identified positive cis-regulatory element(s) in the vicinity of the EAR motif coding sequence that were necessary for early floral expression [36]. Moreover, the SUP locus is prone to epigenetic regulation [37], and local deletions may affect its methylation status and therefore overall expression. To test whether disrupting the coding sequence of the SUP EAR motif had the unintentional consequence of suppressing SUP transcription altogether, we used RT-qPCR to compare expression in floral tissue of sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) relative to wild type (Fig. 3H). Included as a control, transcript levels of sup-1, which contains a premature stop codon, were lower than wild type in both biological replicates but remained readily detectable (Fig. 3H). In comparison, the transcripts of the in-frame deletion alleles sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) showed levels similar to wild type (Fig. 3H). This suggests that these alleles display floral patterning defects, not because of a reduction in their own transcription, but because their protein products lack a functional EAR motif necessary for SUP activity.

B-class gene expression expands centrally in flowers of sup(DLELL), sup (ΔEAR) and tpl-1 backgrounds

Although RT-qPCR showed a similar trend toward increased B-class gene transcript levels in sup-1, sup (DLELL), and sup (ΔEAR) floral tissue (Fig. 3H), this technique does not capture the spatial distribution of AP3 and PI transcription in developing flowers. Thus, to more finely evaluate phenotypic similarities between EAR motif-mutated sup alleles and the strong sup-1 allele, we performed RNA in situ hybridization. Additionally, we examined B-class gene expression patterns in tpl-1 and sup-1 tpl-1, as these genetic backgrounds also exhibit expansion of stamen fate into whorl 4. AP3 and PI expression is generally restricted to whorl 2 petal and whorl 3 stamen primordia in early wild-type floral development, including in stage 5–6 (Fig. 4A, E) and stage 9 (Fig. 4I, M) flowers [11, 12]. In sup-1, stage 5–6 flowers are morphologically similar to wild type but show ectopic expression of AP3 and PI in cells internal to the first initiated whorl 3 stamens (Fig. 4B, F) [4, 10, 13]. This pattern of B-class gene misexpression is also apparent in sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) flowers (Fig. 4C, D, G, H). In stage 9 wild-type flowers, the central gynoecium is composed of two morphologically distinct carpels that form an elongate, incompletely fused tube (Fig. 4I, M) [29]. In contrast, sup-1 flowers at stage 9 (estimated based on the sizes of the floral bud and first initiated stamens) do not display differentiating whorl 4 organs due to extended proliferation of the FM (Fig. 4J, N) [4]. Instead, the interior cells which ectopically expressed AP3 and PI in stage 5–6 retain this misexpression and differentiate into a new whorl of stamens at this later stage (Fig. 4J, N) [4, 10, 13]. Initiation of B-class-expressing lateral organs internal to the original whorl 3 stamens also occurs in sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) mutant flowers (Fig. 4K, L, O, P). The clear similarities in gene misexpression between the strong sup-1 allele and the novel sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) alleles further confirm that the EAR motif is necessary for SUP to spatially restrict B-class gene expression during early flower development. The importance of the EAR motif in this context suggests that physical recruitment of TPL is required for SUP to function as a cadastral regulator in floral patterning. If so, expansion of AP3 and PI expression may be detected in the center of tpl-1 flowers. Unlike the examined sup alleles, tpl-1 does not produce supernumerary whorl 3 organs (Fig. 1J); nonetheless, whorl 4 carpel primordia of stage 5–6 tpl-1 flowers show ectopic expression of both AP3 and PI (Fig. 4Q, R, T, U) consistent with the spread of male reproductive identity into the central whorl (Fig. 1B, F, I). As previously reported, ectopic expression of AP3 and PI is also apparent in whorl 1 organs of tpl-1 flowers (Fig. 4Q, T) which commonly display partial sepal-to-petal homeotic conversions (Fig. 1B) [7]. Thus, TPL not only cooperatively controls the inner boundary of B-class gene expression with SUP but also restricts the peripheral boundary in a role it performs with AP2 [7]. Furthermore, sup-1 tpl-1 double mutants display robust misexpression of both AP3 and PI in the center of the flower (Fig. 4S, V) consistent with expanded stamen fate in this genetic background (Fig. 1D, H, I, J). Collectively, these observations show that SUP and TPL jointly repress B-class genes in the center of the flower, plausibly through an EAR motif-dependent physical association revealed by our biochemical interaction assays.

These results suggest that the central spread of male reproductive fate displayed by the dominant negative tpl-1 allele may also be evident in the tpl-2 tpr1 tpr2 tpr3 tpr4 quintuple loss-of-function mutant, which exhibits severe defects in reproductive development [7, 33]. The tpl/tpr quintuple mutant produces sterile flowers with widespread ectopic carpel identity (Supplementary Fig. 5D) due to strong misexpression of the C-class gene AG [7]. This ectopic expression of AG, which is normally repressed by the cooperative action of AP2 and TPL/TPR, appears to mask phenotypes associated with the misexpression of other genes regulated by TPL/TPR such as AP3 and PI [7]. This masking effect highlights a major challenge of interpreting tpl/tpr floral phenotypes, which are a composite of the myriad regulatory roles played by this family of corepressors during flower development [24]. Despite the obscuring effect of AG misexpression, misregulation of AP3 and PI can still be detected in whorl 1 and whorl 4 organs of developing tpl-2 tpr1 tpr2 tpr3 tpr4 flowers (Supplementary Fig. 5B, C, E, F), consistent with a role for TPL/TPR in delineating both the outer and inner boundaries of B-class gene expression.

At a mechanistic level, TPL/TPR recruits the histone deacetylase HDA19 to function as a corepressor in a variety of developmental contexts, including in floral patterning [7, 26, 38–42]. Thus, if SUP associates with TPL to repress target genes, central spread of B-class gene expression may also be expected in hda19 mutant flowers. To test, we performed RNA in situ hybridization on AP3 and PI expression in hda19-1 flowers and indeed detected ectopic expression of both genes in whorl 4 primordia (Supplementary Fig. S6). This pattern of misexpression is consistent with a model in which SUP recruits TPL, and in turn its interacting partner HDA19, to regulate the inner boundary of B-class gene expression. Although these results are preliminary, they suggest that future investigations into the relationship between HDA19 and SUP may be informative regarding the mechanism of SUP-mediated gene repression.

TPL binding is compromised by sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) mutations

Our Y2H and in vitro pull-down assays demonstrated that site-directed mutagenesis of conserved L residues of the SUP EAR motif interrupts physical interaction with TPL (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S1A). Moreover, using CRISPR-Cas9 to specifically target the EAR motif of SUP in Arabidopsis resulted in alleles with strong floral abnormalities and gene misexpression patterns closely resembling sup-1 (Figs. 3 and 4). The two analyzed genome-edited alleles differ in the extent to which the EAR motif is disrupted, with sup (DLELL) effectively missing only a single amino acid residue and sup (ΔEAR) lacking the motif altogether (Fig. 3A). At the sequence level, neither of these new alleles resemble the SUP mutant variants whose conserved L residues were altered by site-directed mutagenesis, resulting in reduced TPL binding. Thus, we sought to test whether the floral defects displayed by sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) could result from a decreased capacity of their mutant proteins to physically recruit TPL. Indeed, Y2H showed that the protein product of the coarse sup (ΔEAR) allele lacks an ability to interact with TPL (Fig. 5A). Notably, the protein product of the narrowly defined sup (DLELL) mutation was similarly deficient in TPL binding in Y2H (Fig. 5A). Negative Y2H results with the two genome-edited SUP variants were not due to insufficient protein expression in yeast, as Western blot showed robust levels from both tested SUP constructs (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Correspondingly, in vitro pull-down assays confirmed the disruptive effect of the subtle sup (DLELL) mutation on the ability of SUP to interact with TPL (Fig. 5B). Quantification of Western blot signal intensities showed that the sup (DLELL) mutation reduced TPL binding to 32% (average from two independent replicates) relative to wild type SUP protein (Supplementary Fig. S7). Properties of the sup (DLELL) allele demonstrate that even slight changes in the appearance of the SUP EAR motif can be sufficient to counteract association with TPL. More generally, these observations suggest that the severe developmental defects of sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) are caused, at least in part, by the compromised ability of these SUP variants to physically recruit TPL during floral patterning.

Discussion

It is well established that SUP limits male reproductive fate, functioning as a cadastral regulator of B-class gene expression at the whorl 3-whorl 4 boundary of developing flowers [4, 9, 10, 13]. In parallel, transient expression systems have been instrumental in characterizing SUP as a transcriptional repressor that harbors a conserved EAR motif responsible for gene repression [16, 18]. Yet, details connecting the SUP EAR motif with floral patterning, and the molecular mechanism of SUP-mediated repression in general, remain incomplete. In this work, we show that SUP and the corepressor TPL cooperate to restrict stamen identity in the center of the flower, and that both sup-1 and tpl-1 mutants exhibit ectopic AP3 and PI expression in floral whorl 4. Furthermore, at the protein level, SUP and TPL physically interact in an EAR motif-dependent manner. Our work specifically highlights the importance of the SUP EAR motif in floral patterning, as its targeted disruption by CRISPR-Cas9 in Arabidopsis results in reproductive defects and gene misexpression patterns comparable to the strong sup-1 allele. Further, the protein products of our genome-edited alleles are compromised in their ability to bind TPL, supporting a mechanism whereby SUP recruits TPL via its EAR motif to restrict B-class gene expression and male reproductive fate in the center of the flower.

Despite only missing a single residue from its EAR motif, sup (DLELL) displayed abnormalities comparable to sup (ΔEAR), whose protein product lacks the EAR motif altogether. The severe effect of this subtle EAR motif mutation on SUP function plausibly reflects the importance of the spacing between the conserved leucines. Indeed, structural data indicate that the evenly spaced leucines interact with specific residues of an amino-terminal hydrophobic cleft of TPL/TPR [35, 43]. As such, changes to the spatial positioning of EAR motif leucines would be expected to interrupt association with TPL/TPR, as observed in our biochemical assays with sup (DLELL). Thus, while it is well established that changing the identities of the EAR motif leucines by site directed mutagenesis disrupts transcriptional repression and TPL binding [18–20], our genetic and biochemical analyses suggest that the spacing of these conserved leucines is also critical for function.

Although previous work has functionally defined the SUP EAR motif as DLELRL [18], it is noteworthy that this motif is immediately preceded by an additional leucine (LDLELRL) (Fig. 3A). mSUP (LDLEARA) and mSUP (LDLELL) retain two and three evenly spaced L residues, respectively, and in both cases, these EAR-like motifs may support weak TPL interaction. This may explain why residual TPL binding was displayed by the EAR motif-mutated SUP variants tested by in vitro pull-down assays (Figs. 2B and 5B). While it remains unclear why these SUP variants did not display residual TPL binding in yeast two-hybrid assays (Figs. 2A and 5A), this may be due to differences between this in vivo assay (using full-length TPL expressed by eukaryotic cells) and the in vitro pull-down assays (involving an amino-terminal TPL fragment produced in a prokaryotic expression system). Regardless, if the protein product of sup (DLELL) does retain some ability to associate with TPL/TPR in planta, the severe developmental defects of this genome-edited line indicates that such association is insufficient for SUP function.

Under our growth conditions, sup-1 tpl-1 whorl 4 organs often lack obvious carpel identity altogether and are therefore phenotypically more severe than either single mutant (Fig. 1). This observed synergistic interaction indicates that, despite forming a protein complex, SUP and TPL may act in a partially redundant fashion to suppress male fate in the flower center. For instance, in parallel to its interaction with SUP, TPL may repress B-class genes at the whorl 3 inner boundary by physically associating with other transcription factors. Indeed, it is becoming increasingly apparent that TPL interacts with a multitude of transcription factors to regulate an ever-expanding list of developmental processes [22–24]. These interactors include the floral transcription factor RABBIT EARS (RBE), which like SUP, contains a C2H2-type zinc finger DNA-binding domain and an EAR motif [42, 44, 45]. Interestingly, RBE also functions as a cadastral regulator of floral organ identity gene expression [45] and forms an RBE-TPL-HDA19 transcriptional repressor complex to modulate petal cell size and organogenesis [42]. Thus, the growing list of its binding partners in the flower alone supports the possibility that TPL associates with transcription factor(s) other than SUP to restrict central spread of B-class genes, as it does with AP2 to control the outer border of B-class gene expression [7].

Another explanation for the synergistic interaction between tpl-1 and sup-1 may lie in the nature of these alleles. Despite sup-1 being the strongest reported sup allele [4, 10, 46], it may not be a true functional null. Consistent with this possibility, our gene expression analyses show that sup-1 mRNA is not completely cleared by nonsense-mediated decay (Fig. 3H), in agreement with previous observations [46]. In this context, the combination of sup-1 and tpl-1, which acts as only a partial dominant negative allele for the TPL/TPR gene family in floral development [7], may be expected to exhibit phenotypes more severe than either single mutant if they function cooperatively within a common protein complex.

Consistent with their shared roles in floral patterning, tpl-1 and sup-1 mutants both exhibit spread of male reproductive fate and B-class gene misexpression in the center of the flower. However, unlike sup-1, flowers of tpl-1 do not initiate supernumerary whorls of stamens (Fig. 1J). Notably, CRISPR-Cas9 alleles sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR), which have specific EAR motif mutations that disrupt TPL binding, display increased stamen numbers (Fig. 3G). While the lack of stamen overproliferation in tpl-1 is therefore somewhat unexpected, there are multiple experimental observations that offer plausible explanations for this apparent discrepancy. For instance, as noted previously, TPL plays numerous roles in floral patterning, including combining with AP2 to repress AG expression [7]. It is possible that in tpl-1, increased AG expression, which has been correlated with reduced whorl 3 organ initiation [3], counteracts the organ overproliferation normally associated with compromised SUP function. In support of this possibility, ap2 (which shows strong AG upregulation) suppresses the excessive stamen production of sup-1, while ag mutations enhance the floral organ proliferation and FM indeterminacy of sup-1 [3, 4]. As an alternative explanation, perduring activity from other members of the TPL/TPR family, which can also physically interact with SUP (Supplementary Fig. S4), may work with SUP to suppress supernumerary stamens in the tpl-1 background. TPR3 may be of particular relevance in this scenario, as our Y2H results show that its interaction with SUP, like that of TPL, is dependent on the EAR motif (Supplementary Fig. S4).

The lack of stamen overproduction in tpl-1 may also indicate that SUP assembles into different repressor complexes to perform its regulatory roles. For example, while SUP appears to associate with TPL/TPR to counteract the spread of B-class gene expression in whorl 4, TPL/TPR may not be involved in other SUP roles such as the prevention of organ overproliferation. Alternatively, SUP may consistently interact with TPL/TPR to perform its roles while concurrently using its EAR motif to recruit other regulatory proteins to perform these same functions in a redundant manner. In support of these possibilities, SUP physically interacts with the polycomb group (PcG) proteins CURLY LEAF (CLF) and TERMINAL FLOWER2 (TFL2), and its interaction with CLF appears dependent on its EAR motif [14]. Possibly in combination with CLF/TFL2, SUP represses members of the YUCCA auxin biosynthesis gene family to limit auxin accumulation at the boundary between whorls 3 and 4, thereby controlling floral organ initiation [14]. This regulatory relationship provides a plausible explanation as to why tpl-1 lacks stamen overproliferation, as SUP would still be able to recruit PcG components with its EAR motif in the absence of TPL/TPR activity. In mutant backgrounds sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) which lack an intact EAR motif, recruitment of both TPL/TPR and PcG components would be indiscriminately abolished and broad developmental defects would be expected. Indeed, the phenotypic similarities between sup (DLELL) and sup (ΔEAR) and the strong sup-1 allele support this possibility and clearly highlight the critical role that the EAR motif plays in SUP function in the flower.

It remains to be determined if the SUP-TPL interaction described in the present work is functionally conserved in other plants. TPL/TPR genes predate the evolution of land plants and are widespread throughout angiosperms [24, 47]. Similarly, SUP homologs have been functionally characterized in Petunia hybrida (petunia), Oryza sativa (rice) and the legume Medicago truncatula, and the SUP EAR motif is highly conserved in these and other plant species [48–51]. Thus, while the nature of SUP-mediated regulation displays some variability in these diverse species [48–50, 52], the strong conservation of the SUP EAR motif and broad distribution of TPL/TPR homologs suggests that SUP-TPL regulatory relationships exist beyond Arabidopsis. Furthermore, SUP-TPL function may extend into other patterning contexts, including ovule development. Genetic analyses have shown that SUP regulates the asymmetric growth of the outer integuments in Arabidopsis independent from its cadastral role [53]. Since TPL is widely expressed in developing flowers, including in ovules [7], it will be interesting to determine if SUP also cooperates with TPL to perform this role in floral tissue patterning. If so, such cooperation may also be conserved in other species, as similar ovule defects are displayed by sup mutants of P. hybrida and M. truncatula [48, 49].

Conclusions

Using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing, we show that an intact EAR motif is critical for the cadastral regulator SUP to fulfill its roles in floral patterning. We provide insight into a possible mechanism for this observation by demonstrating that SUP physically interacts with the corepressor TPL and that this interaction is dependent on the EAR motif. Furthermore, TPL and SUP cooperate to restrict B-class gene expression from the center of the flower, consistent with both factors acting in a shared complex. Our study therefore adds SUP to the list of transcription factors which use an EAR motif to recruit TPL in the flower [22–24, 42], including AP2 which complexes with TPL to repress floral organ identity genes in outer floral whorls [7]. Notably, our work shows that TPL acts as a key cadastral regulator at both the outer and inner boundaries of AP3 and PI expression, helping confine B-class to whorls 2 and 3 during early floral development. Whereas AP2-TPL binds directly to AP3 in this context [7], it remains unclear whether SUP recruits TPL (and/or other regulatory proteins) to restrict B-class gene expression in a direct or indirect manner [13]. Regardless, the common recruitment of TPL by these different floral transcription factors reflects an overarching theme for the TPL/TPR family of corepressors, which have been repeatedly co-opted into diverse plant processes [22–24].
