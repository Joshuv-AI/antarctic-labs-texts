Genome-wide association study of maternal plasma metabolites during pregnancy
Authors: Siyang Liu, Jilong Yao, Liang Lin, Xianmei Lan, Linlin Wu, Xuelian He, Nannan Kong, Yan Li, Yuqing Deng, Jiansheng Xie, Huanhuan Zhu, Xiaoxia Wu, Zilong Li, Likuan Xiong, Yuan Wang, Jinghui Ren, Xuemei Qiu, Weihua Zhao, Ya Gao, Yuanqing Chen, Fengxia Su, Yun Zhou, Weiqiao Rao, Jing Zhang, Guixue Hou, Liping Huang, Linxuan Li, Xinhong Liu, Chao Nie, Liqiong Luo, Mei Zhao, Zengyou Liu, Fang Chen, Shengmou Lin, Lijian Zhao, Qingmei Fu, Dan Jiang, Ye Yin, Xun Xu, Jian Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryMetabolites are key indicators of health and therapeutic targets, but their genetic underpinnings during pregnancy—a critical period for human reproduction—are largely unexplored. Using genetic data from non-invasive prenatal testing, we performed a genome-wide association study on 84 metabolites, including 37 amino acids, 24 elements, 13 hormones, and 10 vitamins, involving 34,394 pregnant Chinese women, with sample sizes ranging from 6,394 to 13,392 for specific metabolites. We identified 53 metabolite-gene associations, 23 of which are novel. Significant differences in genetic effects between pregnant and non-pregnant women were observed for 16.7%–100% of these associations, indicating gene-environment interactions. Additionally, 50.94% of genetic associations exhibited pleiotropy among metabolites and between six metabolites and eight pregnancy phenotypes. Mendelian randomization revealed potential causal relationships between seven maternal metabolites and 15 human traits and diseases. These findings provide new insights into the genetic basis of maternal plasma metabolites during pregnancy.Keywords: pregnancy, non-invasive prenatal test, metabolites, amino acids, elements, hormones, vitamins, genetic basis, causal relationships, long-term health

Introduction

Metabolites are small molecules that act as intermediate or end products during the metabolic processes.1 Their concentrations are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors.2,3 Metabolites can have a crucial role in disease etiology and are often targeted in therapeutic interventions.4,5 Human genetics provides an important approach to understanding the determinants of metabolite alteration and assessing the role of metabolites in disease outcomes.6 Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) of metabolites have identified thousands of variants associated with approximately 7,000 metabolites in blood plasma or urine, as reported in the latest GWAS Catalog (r2023-01-30).7 However, like many other traits, human genetic studies of metabolites have been dominated by European populations.8 Specifically, less than 300 metabolites (less than 4.29% of all metabolites) have been studied in East Asian populations, with generally small sample sizes (less than 2,000 individuals).9,10,11,12,13,14,15,16 Therefore, there is a significant gap in our understanding of the genetic effects of metabolites in underrepresented East Asian populations.

More importantly, previous genetic investigations into metabolites have not yet addressed the question of whether and how genetic effects may vary during pivotal life stages, such as pregnancy—an imperative period in human reproduction. Pregnancy encompasses a series of physiological changes and metabolic adaptations that necessitate a balanced metabolome for the well-being of both the mother and the fetus.17 Observational epidemiological studies have underscored that imbalances in metabolite concentrations, either inadequate or excessive, can result in adverse health outcomes, severe diseases, and unfavorable birth outcomes. For example, elevated vitamin A (VA) levels may induce fetal malformation18; a deficiency in folic acid (vitamin B9 [VB9]) is linked to a heightened risk of neural tube defects19; insufficient selenium is associated with an increased risk of miscarriage, pre-eclampsia, and growth restriction20,21; and vitamin D (VD) insufficiency correlates with fetal bone growth retardation.22 To delve into the causal relationship and molecular mechanisms underlying these observational associations, genetic studies probing plasma metabolite levels during pregnancy are crucial. However, maternal metabolites during pregnancy remain unexplored through GWASs, leaving the genetic underpinnings of metabolite levels during this critical period largely unknown. It is pertinent to investigate whether genetic effects on metabolites, as gauged by GWASs, may differ between pregnant and non-pregnant populations. Moreover, it is important to explore whether metabolites assayed during this early, critical phase of human life may exhibit genetic relationships with diseases and phenotypes that manifest in both early and later life. These investigations can yield critical insights into human metabolism, laying the groundwork for subsequent mechanistic studies and the identification of novel therapeutic targets.

Conducting expansive genetic analyses, particularly within underrepresented populations, remains a significant challenge. Building upon methodologies and protocols developed in our prior and recent studies,23,24 we conducted genetic analyses of 84 maternal metabolites during pregnancy. Leveraging genetic resources obtained from non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) among 34,394 Chinese pregnant women and metabolite measurements through tandem mass spectrometry (MS) (Figure 1). Specific participant numbers for each metabolite are detailed in Table S1. Quality control (QC) statistics are presented in Table S2. These 84 metabolites include 37 amino acids, 24 trace elements and heavy metals, 13 hormones, 5 hydrophilic vitamins, and 5 hydrophobic vitamins. Of these metabolites, 52 have not been studied in East Asian populations, and 11 have not been previously investigated in other human populations (Tables S3 and S4). We performed both single- and multi-trait genome-wide association analyses to identify genetic signals significantly contributing to plasma metabolite levels and to estimate SNP heritability. We compared genetic effects for identical metabolite measurements between pregnant participants and non-pregnant individuals. Additionally, we conducted Wald ratio estimation and colocalization analyses to identify pleiotropic effects within the context of the KEGG pathway and explored associations between metabolites and various pregnancy phenotypes, as reported in a companion study.25 Finally, we applied Mendelian randomization (MR) to investigate potential causal effects of pregnancy metabolites on disease traits in East Asian populations. This large-scale metabolite GWAS in an East Asian cohort aims to provide insights into the genetic basis of the plasma level for 84 metabolites during pregnancy.

Study design

A total of 34, 394 pregnant participants were recruited during their routine obstetric examinations. Among them, 16,303 participants underwent NIPT sequencing with the BGISeq500 platform, and 18,091 participants underwent NIPT sequencing with the BlackBird platform. Differential numbers of patients underwent targeted MS assays for four categories of metabolites, including 37 amino acids (n = 7,595), 24 elements (n = 10,973), 13 hormones (n = 8,225), and 10 vitamins (n = 12,034), respectively. GWAS analyses were conducted for each metabolite on each of the two sequencing platforms. Meta-GWAS analyses were performed to combine the results from the two sequencing platforms, and multi-trait GWAS analyses were carried out for all metabolites. Potential pregnancy-related genetic effects were evaluated by comparing them with those in a non-pregnant Chinese cohort. Shared genetic effects were also evaluated between each of the 84 metabolites and examined for potential associations with a hundred pregnancy phenotypes, complex traits, or diseases. Further details of the study design are provided in the main text.

Results

Study design and phenotypic distribution of the 84 metabolites

The design of the study is summarized in Figure 1. 34,394 pregnant participants from Shenzhen, South China, were recruited during routine obstetric examinations. NIPTs, offered as a standard prenatal screening test,26 were financially covered by the government and insurance. Simultaneously, MS assays of 84 metabolites, spanning four categories, were provided as an additional screening option to monitor pregnancy metabolite status using shared blood plasma (see the STAR Methods for the selection of the 84 metabolites). Participants opting for NIPT were invited to join the pregnancy nutrition program for metabolite screening. Participants had a mean age of 30 years (standard deviation [SD]: 4.9), and blood sampling occurred at a mean gestational week of 16 weeks plus 3 days (SD: 4.1 weeks) (Table S1). No substantial age differences were identified between pregnant individuals participating in the nutrition programs and a sample of 30,699 pregnant individuals without participation during the same sample recruitment period27 (Figure S1).

For NIPT, whole-genome low-pass sequencing (0.1×–0.3×) was carried out. NIPT sequencing of the 34,394 participants employed two sequencing platforms, BlackBird (BB) for 18,091 participants and BGISeq500 for 16,303 participants. Before the meta-analysis and multi-trait association study, we performed variation calling, genotype imputation, and genome-wide association tests for each platform’s data. Genotype imputation was performed using an adaptive hidden Markov model with a Chinese population reference panel, achieving imputation accuracies of 0.77 and 0.89 for BB and Seq500 data, respectively (Figure S2; see STAR Methods).

In the MS assay, we separated the samples for different assays, including amino acids, elements, hormones, water-soluble vitamins (WSVs), and fat-soluble vitamins (FSVs). Due to plasma volume limitations, not every metabolite was examined for all 34,394 participants. The mean effective sample sizes were 7,595, 10,973, 8,225, and 12,034 for amino acids, elements, hormones, and vitamins, respectively (Figure 1; Table S1; see STAR Methods for QC of the MS assay). The plasma concentration of the 84 metabolites exhibited a Gaussian distribution, with outliers observed for a few metal elements (Figure 2). The mean values for most metabolites fell within the reference ranges except for 3MHis, Al, I, Ti, ALD, and VD, potentially attributed to specific physiological statuses during pregnancy (Table S1). QC of the MS experiment is detailed in the STAR Methods. The coefficient of variation (CV) for each metabolite in the QC samples is provided in Table S2, indicating an overall CV lower than 30%, which suggests high quality in the quantification of the metabolites.

Metabolites often operate within biological pathways, leading us to hypothesize that we would observe correlations between those metabolites that functioned together in a specific pathway. Phenotypic correlation analysis revealed that metabolites belonging to the same category tended to be more phenotypically correlated than those from different categories (Figure S3; Table S5). Strong phenotypic correlations were observed between Met and His and among Phe, Ile/Leu, and Tyr/Val for amino acids; between Cd and Hg and among Al, Ga, and V for metal elements; and between A4 and T and E1 and E2 for hormones (Spearman’s R > 0.7). Phenotypic correlation was lower for metabolites from different categories, with the strongest observed between VA and PSer/Sar (Spearman’s R = 0.41) and between VE and Car (Spearman’s R = 0.42).

The detailed methodology and evaluations for utilizing NIPT data in genetic studies are provided in our companion study.24

Genome-wide association analysis unravels novel genetic associations with metabolites

Building upon the observed phenotypic correlation within metabolite categories, we conducted a multi-trait GWAS for each metabolite, utilizing information derived from GWASs of metabolites within the same category. We performed regression analyses using PLINK28 to regress quantile-transformed metabolite values on genotype dosage for BB and BGISeq500 sequencing data. The analysis was adjusted for potential confounding factors, including the first three principal components (PCs)—maternal age, gestational week of NIPT, and MS testing—and the inferred sex of the fetus. Detailed information on covariates can be found in Table S6. Subsequently, we conducted a meta-analysis using METAL29 to amalgamate GWAS summary statistics from the two sequencing platforms. Finally, we employed the multi-trait association test algorithm implemented in MTAG30 to generate trait-specific effect estimates for each SNP, using information from summary statistics of metabolites within the same metabolite categories. Power analyses indicate that the study design has 80% statistical power to identify genetic variants with a minor-allele frequency (MAF) above 0.05 and an effect size greater than 0.2 or an effect size greater than 0.09 when the MAF approximates 0.5 (Figure S4).

The genomic control lambda (GC lambda) for the 84 metabolite molecules ranged from 0.9 to 1.05, indicating the negligible inflation of association statistics. In total, we identified 43 genetic associations reaching study-wide significance by adjusting the four metabolite categories (p < 1.25 × 10−8) for 27 metabolites, including 9 amino acids, 7 elements, 4 hormones, and 7 vitamins, and an additional 10 genetic associations for 10 metabolites reaching genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8) (Figure 3; Table S7). Among the 43 loci, 20 were known to be associated with the corresponding metabolite level, while 23 were novel (Table 1; see STAR Methods for the definition of the novel locus). We present the Manhattan, quantile-quantile, and LocusZoom plots for the 34 metabolites with genetic associations in Figures S5–S38. Below, we summarize the key novel findings for each metabolite category, with a more detailed interpretation of the novel loci presented in Table S7.

Amino acids

We identified 18 independent association signals for 14 amino acids (Figures 3A; Table S7). All signals were first discovered in the East Asian population, with 12 previously reported in the European population, resulting in six novel association signals found for five amino acids in our study. Meeting study-wide significance criteria, two novel association signals were identified between the AGA locus (lead SNP rs12645096-T: beta = −0.13, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.11 to −0.16, p = 9.07 × 10−26), the ASPG locus (lead SNP rs1744294-T: beta = −0.14, 95% CI: −0.10 to −0.17, p = 1.55 × 10−16), and aspartic acid (Asp) (Tables 1 and S8). rs12645096 is a robust expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) for AGA, which encodes the aspartylglucosaminidase in various tissues, such as artery, thyroid, and lungs (p < 1.7 × 10−32) in GTEx. Similarly, rs1744294 is also an eQTL for ASPG, encoding asparaginase. Both aspartylglucosaminidase and asparaginase deficiencies can lead to an increased Asn and Asp, which explains the genetic association observed in our study. These associations had not been previously reported due to limited genetic studies on this metabolite31,32 (Table S4). While Asp has been used for the treatment of fatigue and the improvement of athletic performance and muscle strength without robust scientific evidence supporting its use,33 the identified genetic association can serve as an instrumental variable for the causal inference of its utility when combined with data from biobanks. Four additional novel signals surpassing the genome-wide significance threshold include a genetic association with 3-methylhistidine (3MHis), an amino acid biomarker for muscle protein turnover at the CDK12 locus; a genetic association for homocysteine (Hcy) at the intergenic VSTM5-HEPHL1 locus; an association between Sar and the intergenic SLC29A2-NPAS4 locus; and an association between Val and the AGXT2 locus. More interpretations of these loci are summarized in Table S8.

Elements

We identified eleven genetic associations with metal and trace elements in maternal plasma (Figure 3B), including eight novel association signals surpassing the study-wide significance threshold (Tables 1 and S8). These eight novel associations include the GIGYF2 locus, an eQTL for C2orf82 for the barium (Ba) level through lead SNP rs13424351-A (beta = −0.10, 95% CI: −0.07 to −0.12, p = 4.32 × 10−13); the CCDC170 locus (lead SNP rs9397433-A: beta = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.09, p = 1.60 × 10−9) and the LOC401312-LOC541472 locus, an eQTL for the interleukin (IL)-6 antisense RNA 1 (IL6-AS1) (lead SNP rs58879058-T: beta = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.10, p = 1.97 × 10−9) for the copper (Cu) level; the SERPINA7 locus associated with the iodine (I) level through lead SNP rs1804495-A, a missense variant (beta = −0.27, 95% CI: −0.25 to −0.30, p = 2.13 × 10−106); the AGMAT locus (lead SNP rs6679229-A: beta = −0.06, 95% CI: −0.04 to −0.08, p = 2.00 × 10−9) and the BORCS7-ASMT locus, an eQTL for AS3MT (lead SNP rs12764049-A: beta = −0.06, 95% CI: −0.04 to −0.08, p = 2.79 × 10−9), associated with the magnesium (Mg) level; and lastly, the EPRS locus, an eQTL for EPRS (lead SNP rs2647437-T: beta = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.10, p = 1.71 × 10−9) and the MORC4 locus, an eQTL for PRPS1 (lead SNP rs6616617-T: beta = 0.16, 95% CI: 0.13 to 0.18, p = 3.40 × 10−39), associated with the strontium (Sr) level in maternal plasma.

The discovery of the strong association between the missense variant rs1804495 at SERPINA7 and iodine level is noteworthy since no GWAS has been performed for iodine. SERPINA7 encodes the major thyroid hormone transport protein TBG in serum. We found that one additional copy of the A allele decreases 79.32 μg/L iodine in maternal plasma, providing evidence for the regulation of iodine metabolism through a common missense genetic polymorphism present in TBG.

Hormones

We identified 12 loci exerting genetic influence on the plasma levels of six hormones (Figure 3C), including seven novel loci surpassing the study-wide significance threshold (Tables 1 and S8). The CYP2C18 locus was found to associate with 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC) (SNP rs1926711-A: beta = −0.12, 95% CI: −0.15 to −0.10, p = 6.74 × 10−22), where the lead SNP rs1926711 is an eQTL of CYP2C19, a monooxygenase that catalyzes many reactions involved in the synthesis of cholesterol, steroids, and other lipids and in drug metabolism.

In addition to the known association between the CYP3A7-CYP3A51P locus and E1, we found four novel signals for E1 present in the SULT1B1-SULT1E1 locus (lead SNP rs1220712-T: beta = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.10, p = 2.11 × 10−13), the OASL-P2RX7 locus (lead SNP rs2708101-T: beta = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.09, p = 1.84 × 10−9), the LINC00379-MIR17HG locus (lead SNP rs74241688-A: beta = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.11, p = 3.27 × 10−9), and the VCX3A-PUDP locus (lead SNP rs6639741-A: beta = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.09, p = 3.81 × 10−9). Additionally, we identified two novel signals, including one for 11-deoxycortisol (11DOC) at the FDX1 (lead SNP rs1073527-T: beta = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.10, p = 1.47 × 10−10) locus, which plays roles in steroid, VD, and bile acid metabolism,34 and one signal for 17α-hydroxyprogesterone (17OHP) at the ZNF468 locus (lead SNP rs4801940-T: beta = −0.10, 95% CI: −0.08 to −0.13, p = 9.49 × 10−15), which is associated with certain immunodeficiency conditions.35

Vitamins

We identified 12 loci exhibiting a profound impact on the levels of vitamins in maternal plasma (Figure 3D), including six novel associations with five vitamins (p < 1.25 × 10−8) (Tables 1 and S8). Concerning WSVs, besides confirming the known association between the 5-methyltetrahydrofolate (MF5) level and the NPPB-RNU5E-1 locus, we identified a new association between MF5 and variants within the FOLR1-FOLR2 gene locus (lead SNP rs35954619-A: beta = −0.09, 95% CI: −0.06 to −0.12, p = 3.00 × 10−9), an eQTL for FOLR1 that encodes the folate receptor 1 and folate receptor beta. This locus was previously associated with asymmetrical dimethylarginine levels related to heart rate variability.34 Additionally, we also identified a new association that surfaced between VB2 and the SLC52A3 gene (lead SNP rs6140149-A: beta = −0.08, 95% CI: −0.05 to −0.10, p = 1.32 × 10−10), known for its role in WSV metabolism,35 and another between VB5 and the TCF23-SLC5A6 locus (lead SNP rs1659696-A: beta = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.07 to 0.13, p = 3.93 × 10−13), an eQTL for ATRAID, contributing to cell-cycle arrest in the all-trans-retinoic acid signal pathway.35 Despite their biological relevance, the discoveries of these three new genetic associations with WSVs were prompted by a lack of prior GWASs (Tables S3 and S4).

Regarding FSVs, we confirmed known associations between the 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 (VD3) level and three loci—GC (rs1352846, p = 1.81 × 10−33), PDE3B (rs10766189, p = 6.87 × 10−20), and DHCR7 (rs12789751, p = 1.97 × 10−16)—as well as two known associations for vitamin E (VE) at the APOA5 locus (p = 1.39 × 10−8) and vitamin K (VK) at the LOC102724279-CYP4F2 locus (p = 1.18 × 10−37). Additionally, we identified a new association for VA at the GCKR locus (lead SNP rs1260333-A: beta = 0.07, 95% CI: 0.05 to 0.10, p = 5.31 × 10−9) and two new associations for VE at the RNF215 (lead SNP rs61583714-T: beta = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.12 to 0.17, p = 2.31 × 10−28) and GGH (lead SNP rs117494536-A: beta = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.15, p = 8.16 × 10−20) loci. The RNF215 and GGH loci were eQTLs for EC14L3 in the thyroid and GGH in the whole blood, respectively, suggesting biological relevance.

SNP heritability and genetic correlation

To assess the heritability of the 84 metabolites, we employed the linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSR) technique36 to estimate SNP-based heritability and compared this estimate to the heritability computed from summary statistics of GWASs from prior investigations37 (see STAR Methods). Among the 84 metabolites, 67 (79.8%) exhibited an SNP heritability greater than zero (Figure S39; Table S9). The SNP-based heritability estimates ranged from 0.4% to 22.2%, with a median of 6.5% among the 67 metabolites. Notably, Ala (hSNP2 = 22.2%), Hg (hSNP2 = 17.7%), 17OHP (hSNP2 = 10.1%), VD3 (hSNP2 = 18.4%), and MF5 (hSNP2 = 10.9%) demonstrated the highest SNP heritability among the amino acid, metal element, hormone, fat, and water vitamin categories, respectively.

When comparing the SNP heritability of 15 metabolites from our study to publicly available GWAS summary statistics from prior investigations,37,38 our study found that all three vitamins, VD3, VA, and VE, as well as the metal element Mg and four amino acids, namely Ala, Tyr, Ile, and Val, exhibited higher SNP heritability (Table S9). However, for the remaining five amino acids and two metal elements, our study found lower SNP heritability (Figure S40).

Comparison of estimated genetic effects in a non-pregnant Chinese cohort

All but eight of the 53 genetic loci identified in this study demonstrated consistent effect directions and reached nominal significance (p < 0.05) across both sequencing technologies (Figure S41; Table S7). The eight loci that did not reach nominal significance in one of the two sequencing technologies displayed consistent effect directions. Therefore, the divergence in p values is likely attributable to a lack of statistical power. These results underscore the high reliability of our GWAS findings.

To further explore the potential existence of pregnancy-specific genetic associations, we compared effect sizes and p values for the 53 loci with an independent study involving 1,553 non-pregnant Chinese individuals (female N = 642, male N = 911) from the BGI-Shenzhen multi-omics cohort.39 These participants underwent whole-genome sequencing, and 80 out of the 84 metabolites investigated in our study were assayed using the same tandem MS technology (see STAR Methods). Notably, in the BGI-Shenzhen multi-omics cohort, amino acids, vitamins, and hormones were assayed using plasma, the same sampling material as in this study, while metal elements were assayed using whole blood. We performed linear regression, adding individual sex and the first two PCs as the covariates for each metabolite in the BGI-Shenzhen multi-omics cohort (see STAR Methods). Additionally, we conducted a linear regression using only the female individuals (N = 642). The majority of these females were of childbearing age, ranging from 20 to 45 years, with mean age of 28.5 years (Figure S42).

Interestingly, excluding the four signals for Hcy, Ba, I, and DOC that were not assayed and two signals for Hyl and Se, where the lead SNPs were not present in the non-pregnant cohort, 25 loci discovered in our study demonstrated inconsistency in effect size estimates in the full non-pregnant cohort (53.2%, p > 0.05 or effect size was opposite) (Figure S43; Table S10). When focusing on females in the BGI-Shenzhen multi-omics cohort, 31 loci demonstrated inconsistency in effect size estimates (66.0%, p > 0.05 or effect size was opposite) (Figure S44; Table S10). The inconsistency in genetic effects between the pregnant and non-pregnant cohorts varied among the four categories of metabolites. 5 out of the 16 signals in the amino acid category (31.3%), 7 out of the 8 signals in the element category (87.5%), all 11 signals in the hormone category (100%), and 2 out of 12 signals in the vitamin category (16.7%) demonstrated inconsistent genetic effects between the pregnant and the non-pregnant participants.

We inferred the reasons for the inconsistency in genetic effects for each category as follows: (1) the low consistency for the element category was likely due to the difference between plasma and whole blood sampling, as differences in mean phenotypic values of the elements between the two populations were observed (Table S10, columns I, X, and Y); (2) the low consistency for hormones between the two studies may suggest pregnancy-specific genetic effects, in accordance with the difference in phenotypic distribution between the two populations, or may be due to a lack of power in the non-pregnant BGI-Shenzhen multi-omics cohort study, as the previously known rs4646462 and rs10883790 associated with E1 and 17OHP, respectively, were not discovered in the study; (3) for amino acids, the inconsistency of the genetic effects for the ASPG locus with Asp, the SLC29A2-NPAS4 locus with Sar, and the AGXT2 locus with Val was likely due to physiological differences between the two cohorts, while the inconsistency for the KLKB1 locus with Arg and the ASS1 locus with Cit was probably due to a lack of power, as the two association signals were previously known; and (4) for vitamins, most of the association signals tended to be consistent between the pregnant and non-pregnant cohorts, and the inconsistency of the genetic effects for MF5 and VB5 either reflects a pregnancy-specific genetic effect or a lack of power in the BGI-Shenzhen multi-omics cohort study.

Genetic pleiotropy among metabolites

Metabolites are known for their intricate interactions and roles within specific pathways. We inferred the existence of genetic pleiotropy among the metabolites investigated in this study. Specifically, the 84 metabolites examined are involved in 42 KEGG pathways (Table S11).40 The two largest pathways are the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways, comprising 20 amino acids and 13 hormones investigated in our study. We aim to explore whether the genetic associations we identified exhibit pleiotropy.

Out of the 53 association signals identified, 27 (50.94%) exhibited an impact on two or more metabolites (p < 0.001; Figures S45–S49). Utilizing a Wald ratio estimator to discern potential causal effects among metabolites using a single SNP,41 we unveiled 16 genetic associations with significant effects linking 18 metabolites (Table S12).

Pathway analysis of these 18 metabolites revealed ten pathways with nominal significance (p < 0.05) (Table S13). The pleiotropic effects were visually represented within the context of the KEGG pathways (Figure 4). In the cyanoamino acid metabolism pathway, the ASPG locus (lead SNP rs1744297) demonstrated an effect from Asp on Asn, while the AGA-LINC01098 locus (lead SNP rs12645096) affected both Asn and Asp. Additionally, the CPS1-ERBB4 locus (lead SNP rs10272406) and the ZNF713 (lead SNP rs72933867) locus exhibited an effect from Ser on Gly, different from the direction suggested in the KEGG pathway (Figure 4A). In the amino-acid-related aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway, KLKB1 (lead SNP rs4253255) showed an effect from Arg on Met but not the other way around (Figure 4B). In the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway, four gene loci, including LOC101928327-DIRC3-AS1 (lead SNP rs2162000), EGR2-NRBF2 (lead SNP rs10822140), CYP3A5 (lead SNP rs1419745), and JMJD1C (lead SNP rs6479891), mutually regulated A4-T-17OHP, E1-E2, E1-E2-CORT, and E1-E2-DHEA, respectively (Figure 4C). In the ubiquinone and other terpenoid-quinone biosynthesis pathways involving vitamins, the LOC102724279-CYP4F2 locus (lead SNPs rs12462273 and rs62107763) and the APOA5 locus (lead SNP rs3741298) affected VK and VE simultaneously (Figure 4D).

Colocalization with pregnancy phenotypes

To explore potential clinical implications arising from the newly discovered genetic associations with metabolites, we performed a colocalization analysis for each of the 53 significant loci, using around 100 pregnancy phenotypes from a companion study.25 Notably, we identified six loci that demonstrated a robust signal of colocalization between maternal metabolites and pregnancy biomarkers (posterior probabilities of H4 [PPH4] > 0.5) (Figures S50–S53). These included shared pleiotropic effects involving maternal magnesium (Mg), serum creatinine (CR), and uric acid (UA) at the AGMAT locus; iron (Fe) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) at the TST and TMPRSS6 locus; and iodine (I) and free thyroxine (FT4) at the SERPINA7 locus (Figure S50). Additional associations include maternal VA, pre-albumin (PA), and serum CR at the GCKR locus (Figure S51); estrogen (E1, E2) and mean platelet volume (MPV) at the NRBF2 and JMJD1C locus (Figure S52); and 3-methylhistidine (3MHis) and urine glucose (GLU_U) at the FBXL20 and CDK12 locus (Figure S53). Most of these colocalization signals were previously unknown, contributing novel insights into the pleiotropy between maternal metabolites and biomarker traits during pregnancy.

Potential causal maternal metabolites for human complex traits

Furthermore, we conducted a bidirectional two-sample MR analysis between the five categories of metabolites and 120 phenotypes from the Biobank Japan Project (BBJ), released in the IEU OpenGWAS project42,43 (see STAR Methods).

In the MR analysis, subsequent to excluding heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, and reverse causality (see STAR Methods), we identified significant causal relationships between seven metabolites and 15 human traits and diseases from the BBJ (p < 0.01) (Figure 5; Table S14). We found that genetically predicted MF5 was associated with increased high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (beta = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.16, p = 8.56 × 10−4) and a reduced risk of ischemic stroke (odds ratio [OR] = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.73 to 0.95, p = 7.07 × 10−3). The discovery of the relation between MF5 and these two traits verified previous MR studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in the European population.44,45 Although MF5 protects against ischemic stroke, no causal effect of MF5 on cardiovascular diseases, such as coronary artery disease, was identified in the BBJ (p = 0.62), consistent with the biological function of folate in nucleic acid synthesis and DNA repair and medical evidence of folate in nervous system development.46

Furthermore, the MR analysis suggested that genetically predicted VD3 was associated with an increased risk of Graves’ disease (GD) (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.12 to 1.77, p = 3.41 × 10−3). This differs from observational studies, which suggested that patients with GD were more likely to be deficient in VD compared to the controls, based on a sample size of less than 100 individuals.47 However, RCTs did not prove that VD supplementation improves the course of GD.48 The most recent RCT observed a trend for increased risk of treatment failure and relapse of hyperthyroidism with VD supplementation.49 An MR analysis in the UK Biobank (UKBB) of European individuals suggests no significant associations between VD3 and hyperthyroidism.50 Therefore, to confirm the effect of VD3 on GD observed in this study, further replication and mechanism validation are required.

Regarding the metal elements, we found that genetically predicted plasma Cu was associated with an elevated risk of open-angle glaucoma (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.23 to 1.84, p = 7.21 × 10−5). Concerning the hormones, genetically predicted 11DOC was associated with an increased risk of smoking initiation, aligning with prior observational evidence,51 and a larger fractional shortening (FS; the size of the ventricle at the end of systole and diastole) derived from transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) data. In addition, A4 was associated with higher levels of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), total bilirubin, and blood sugar, lower levels of lactate dehydrogenase and urea nitrogen, and a decreased risk of rheumatoid arthritis, consistent with reported observational analyses.52 Lastly, E1 was associated with a lower level of chloride and aluminum, which has not been reported previously.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive investigation into the genetic basis of maternal metabolites during pregnancy, exploring their genetic relationships, connections to common pregnancy phenotypes, and associations with complex human traits and diseases. We identified 43 genetic associations, including 23 novel associations for 15 metabolites with study-wide significance and an additional 10 surpassing genome-wide significance. The robustness of our findings was confirmed by consistent effect sizes observed across two sequencing technologies. Notably, comparisons with a non-pregnant cohort revealed substantial differences in genetic effect estimates, particularly for hormone-related metabolites, highlighting the dynamic nature of genetic effects during pregnancy despite DNA stability. This study provides a foundation for future mechanistic investigations into the biological processes of pregnancy and reproduction.

We identified pleiotropic effects in over half of the metabolite-associated loci, revealing genetic determination of metabolic flux in ten KEGG pathways. Our strategy for multi-trait association analysis, rather than independent metabolite analysis, was essential in capturing pleiotropic effects. We discovered novel pleiotropic effects involving metabolites from different categories, such as shared effects between I and Sr, among three hormones (E2, E1, CORT) and Ser, and between VE and Glu. Furthermore, our investigation into shared genetic effects between metabolites and pregnancy phenotypes unveiled new instances of pleiotropy involving six metabolites and eight pregnancy biomarkers, shedding light on previously undiscovered functions of these metabolites. Employing MR analysis, we identified potential causal relationships between metabolites and 120 phenotypes from the BBJ, linking plasma folate to ischemic stroke, VD3 to GD, copper to open-angle glaucoma, and androstenedione to rheumatoid arthritis.

Beyond uniquely contributing to the genetic understanding of metabolites during pregnancy, the present studies represent the largest-scale metabolite GWASs in East Asian individuals. The reasons for the identification of the 23 novel genetic associations surpassing the study-wide genetic association threshold and an additional 7 novel genetic associations surpassing the genome-wide association can be summarized as follows: being the first study of certain metabolites (I, DOC, 11DOC, VB5), a lack of power in previous studies (e.g., Asp, all metal elements, 17OHP, MF5, VB2, VK), and a potential discrepancy in the genetic basis between East Asian and European populations. It is noteworthy that we conducted an investigation into the genetic effects of previously reported associations in each category. Our analysis revealed substantial differences in genetic effects, whether represented as Z scores or beta values (Figure S54; Table S15). The observed variations can be attributed to false discoveries and biased effect size estimations in prior studies, primarily due to limited sample sizes. For instance, those studies focusing on elements, hormones, and vitamins typically had sample sizes of less than 2,000. Additionally, discrepancies arose from unstandardized reports of genetic effects in the GWAS Catalog, where the effect sizes of several prior genetic associations were either unusually close to zero or appeared abnormally large.

These findings offer valuable insights for the exploration of new therapeutic targets and affirm the applicability of NIPT sequencing data in medical genetic research.23,24 With the global expansion of NIPT sequencing to over 10 million individuals, adopting sophisticated study designs and methodologies will facilitate the utilization of this invaluable genetic resource. The methodologies and insights from this study have the potential to advance future research, enabling the integration of multi-omics data with NIPT information to address fundamental biological and clinical questions.

Limitations of the study

Several unresolved issues require further investigation. First, the observed discrepancies in genetic effect estimates between pregnanct and non-pregnant cohorts need in-depth exploration. These differences may arise from insufficient power in the non-pregnant cohort or from biological mechanisms, potentially related to epigenetic changes induced by pregnancy. Investigating whether genes involved in pregnancy-related hormones and metabolites are crucial for healthy fetal growth is a promising research direction. Second, caution is advised for MR estimates, especially when assessing genetic effects that change during pregnancy, as time-varying genetic effects can complicate causal interpretation.53 Although numeric causal estimates may be challenging to interpret in such cases, MR can still validly test specific causal null hypotheses.54,55 This concern does not apply to metabolites with consistent genetic effects between pregnant and non-pregnant cohorts, such as many vitamins and amino acids in our study. However, for metabolites with evolving genetic effects, particularly hormones, further investigation is essential to comprehend how pregnancy-specific genetic effects might impact late-onset diseases. Such investigations should integrate findings from this study with birth cohort resources in China56 and other countries, using intergenerational MR techniques57 to elucidate the contributions of intrauterine and fetal genetics to fetal, neonatal, and teenage health.

The genetic associations identified in this study pertain to a single time point in early pregnancy. Our recent research has revealed changing genetic effects during pregnancy for certain genes, such as variants in PEARL1 associated with changing platelet counts and gestational thrombocytopenia.58 It is expected that genetic effects underlying certain metabolites will exhibit both phenotypic and genetic effect size changes during pregnancy. These time-specific changing genetic effects may be related to disease occurrences during pregnancy. The substantial changes in genetic effects compared to non-pregnant cohorts suggest that metabolites may also undergo alterations during pregnancy, necessitating multiple time point sampling in the future.

Despite being the largest metabolite GWAS in East Asian individuals, we would like to note that the new discoveries in our study may still be subject to biased estimation of genetic effects or false positives despite rigorous and evaluated analysis procedures. To enhance transparency, we have publicly shared the full GWAS summary statistics in a standardized format, providing a robust foundation for future meta-analyses, replications, and validation studies.

Experimental model and subject details

All 34, 394 participants were recruited during their routine obstetric examinations. They were engaged in the NIPT test and the pregnancy nutrition program from years 2017 to 2019. Before blood sampling, participants underwent pretest counseling and provided informed written consent. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of BGI (BGI-IRB21184) in strict compliance with regulations regarding ethical considerations and personal data protection. The selection of the 84 metabolites included in the pregnancy nutrition program was based on the following criteria: 1) knowledge from previously reported literature on metabolomic investigation during pregnancy66,67,68,69; 2) the availability of robust multiplexed metabolic quantification techniques utilizing liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) platform67,70 [4, 5]; 3) established connections between the status of nutritional metabolites and maternal health during pregnancy.67,70,71,72

Method details

Plasma sample preparation

A 5ug peripheral whole blood was drawn from each participant and preserved in EDTA anticoagulant tubes to prevent hemolysis. Plasma was obtained by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min) and was preserved at −80°C until assay. For amino acid extraction, 40 μL of plasma was mixed with 20 μL stable-isotope labeled internal standard (IS) in sulfosalicylic acid to precipitate proteins. The mixture underwent vortexing and centrifugation (4000 rpm, 4°C, 20 min). In the case of hormones, 250 μL of plasma mixed with 205 μL of IS solutions were filtered through solid-phase extraction (SPE), followed by washing with 25% methanol and eluting with 100% dichloromethane. The elution was evaporated using nitrogen and reconstituted in 25% methanol. For vitamin extraction, approximately 200 μL of plasma was mixed with 600 μL methanol/acetic acid/IS for water-soluble vitamins (WSV), or with 1000 μL methanol/acetonitrile/IS for fat-soluble vitamins (FSV), followed by vortex and centrifugation (4000 g, 4°C, 20 min). The supernatants were evaporated with nitrogen and reconstituted with either 60 μL deionized water (WSV) or 80 μL 80% acetonitrile (FSV). Regarding metal elements, 100 μL of plasma was mixed with 400 μL nitric acid/hydrogen peroxide for decomposition at 105°C for 3 h. The decomposed products were cooled and delivered to mass spectrometry after dilution with deionized water.

Sequencing assays

Details information about the sequencing protocol has been previously published.73 In brief, within 8h of blood collection, plasma was extracted from whole blood through two rounds of centrifugation. The plasma samples underwent library construction, sample quality control, and 36-cycle single-end multiplex sequencing on either the BlackBird or BGI-seq500 platform. Prior to bioinformatic analysis, the reads were trimmed to 35bp. Poor-quality reads were filtered using SOAPnuke (https://github.com/BGI-flexlab/SOAPnuke). A read was removed if it contained more than 30% low-quality bases (Q ≤ 2) or N bases. In general, each participant underwent whole-genome sequencing, resulting in 5–10 million cleaned reads per participant, representing a sequencing depth of approximately 0.06x −0.1x.

Quantitative measurement and quality control of metabolites using mass spectrometry

The targeted quantification of metabolites was performed using liquid chromatography coupled with triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (LC MS/MS), employing ACQUITY UPLC I-Class (Waters) mounted with a C18 column, Triple Quad 5500 (Sciex) and Xevo TQ-S (Waters). MS/MS spectra corresponding to metabolites were acquired in positive ion mode with multiple reaction monitoring scans. Metal elements were measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP MS/MS), specifically the 7700x ICP-MS (Agilent). Mass spectra acquired were processed using MultiQuant (V. 3.0.2, Sciex) for amino acids, hormones and FSV, MassLynx (V. 4.1, Waters) for WSV, and MassHunter (V. B.01.03, Agilent) for metal elements.

A rigorous quality control process was implemented throughout the metabolomics measurements. Sample randomization was conducted across different batches. Calibration curves, encompassing gradient concentrations of specific standards and corresponding spiked stable isotope-labeled internal standards, were constructed to quantify targeted analytes. In each experiment, encompassing every 30 samples, lower quality control (LQC) and higher quality control (HQC) samples for every metabolite were incorporated. The determination of statistical parameters, including mean (X¯), standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and values such as X¯±1SD, X¯±2SD, and X¯±3SD was based on the accumulation of data over a period of 3–5 months from the quality control samples. If a QC sample deviated beyond the X¯±3SD or if more than two QC samples exceeded the X¯±2SD limits, the testing samples associated with the QC samples underwent re-analysis to ensure the robustness and accuracy of the results.

Correlation of metabolite concentrations

We investigated the correlation among metabolite concentrations from two aspects. First, we estimated the Spearman correlation coefficient between the phenotypes. Secondly, we applied the correlation model embedded in LD score correlation (LDSC) to determine the genetic correlation between any pair of the metabolites.36 The resulting correlations were visualized through a heatmap created using R scripts.

Sequence alignment

We applied BWA63 to align the cleaned reads to the Genome Reference Consortium Human Reference 38 (GRCh38)61 and used the rmdup option in samtools62 to remove potential PCR duplicates. The GATK realignment and base quality recalibration method64 was utilized to align the reads and adjust base quality scores. After that, the alignment files were stored as bam files.

Genotype imputation

We employed STITCH (version 1.2.7)28,65 to impute genotype probabilities for individuals from the BB and BGISeq500 sequencing datasets within a five-megabase window with a 250K buffer assuming 40 ancestral haplotypes. Allele frequency information from the Chinese population (CHB+CHS+CDX, N = 301) in the 1KG impute2 reference panel74 was used as initial values for the EM optimization of model parameters. The imputed variants included 8.16 million known polymorphic sites across 22 autosomal chromosomes and chrX, with a 1KG East Asian allele frequency ≥0.01. All loci recorded in the GWAS catalog were also included for imputation. Each imputed site was assigned an IMPUTE2-style info score and a p value for violation of Hardy Weinberg equilibrium (HWE-p value in short). We applied a filtration threshold of info score greater than 0.4, and minor allele frequency greater than 0.01 to identify significantly associated variants. Imputation accuracies were assessed using Pearson’s R2 between the true genotypes obtained from high-coverage WGS (40x) of 50 participants and the imputed genotype dosage.

Genome-wide association analysis with PLINK

As the linear regression approach assumes that the trait values follow a multivariate normal distribution, we performed a rank transformation of the metabolite raw values using the empirical normal quantile transformation approach after removing outliners in the raw value distribution.75 Subsequently, we applied the linear regression model implemented in PLINK v1.9 to identify associations between the imputed genotype dosage and the transformed values of the metabolites.28 The covariates in the regression model included maternal age, gestational week upon sampling, the top three principal components of PCA, and the inferred sex of the fetus computed from the NIPT data. The regression analysis was performed independently for the BlackBird and BGISeq500 sequencing data, producing effect size, standard errors, the number of effect individuals, and p values for each of the imputed sites.

Meta-analysis and multi-trait genome-wide association test using the MTAG approach

We applied the inverse variance-based approach implemented in METAL to perform the meta-analysis integrating the effects from BB and BGISeq500.29 Given the potential shared genetic correlation among many metabolites, we conducted multi-trait genome-wide association studies based on the METAL summary statistics using the MTAG approach.30 The resulting beta values, p value, and standard errors were utilized for subsequent analysis. It is noteworthy that during the MTAG analysis, we encountered challenges related to the extensive time and memory requirements, particularly when considering all 84 metabolites due to the very large Omega and Sigma matrices. Unfortunately, we were unable to successfully complete the computation. Following communication with the MTAG author, we opted to perform the MTAG analysis for each specific category, including amino acids (N = 37), elements (N = 24), hormones (N = 13), and vitamins (N = 10), respectively.

Identification of independent significant loci and novel loci

Upon obtaining effect sizes and p values from the meta-analysis, we defined independent loci as significant variants clustered within a 1 Mbp window. The lead SNP was identified as the one with the smallest p value within this window. No multiple independent signals within a single locus were observed after conducting a conditional test using PLINK v1.9. Furthermore, LocusZoom was utilized for visualizing the loci.76 The reported loci were determined based on the conditional test following the single marker analysis, applying a significance threshold of p value ≤5 x 10−8. As the genome-wide association study was performed on quantile-transformed metabolite values (βnor), the effect on the real metabolite level was calculated using the formula β = βnor ∗ sdpheno where sdpheno refers to the standard deviation of values for specific phenotypes. Both βnor and β are presented in Tables 1 and S7. A locus was deemed novel when meeting the following criteria: (1) within a 1Mbp window centered around the lead SNP, no genetic variants from the GWAS catalog were associated with the same metabolite7 (p < 1e−6). (2) no SNPs showing LD r2 > 0.8 with the lead SNP and having significant genetic associations with the same metabolite were found in the Phennoscanner database33 (p < 1e−5).

SNP heritability

The genomic inflation factor λGC, heritability, intercept, and the ratio were calculated using the LD score regression approach based on the summary statistics.36 17 out of the 84 metabolites displayed negative heritability. Following communication with the LDSC author, this phenomenon was attributed to potential low statistical power for these traits, because of insufficient sample size. We set the heritability of these metabolites with negative heritability as zero in Figure S39 and Table S9. The heritability was compared with that reported by Kettunen et al.37 and UK Biobank.38 The UK Biobank heritability estimates were sourced from https://nealelab.github.io/UKBB_ldsc/.

Comparison with the non-pregnancy population

1,553 participants (642 females, 911 males) from the local Shenzhen area were recruited in the BGI-shenzhen multi-omics cohort, with each individual providing written consent. Whole-genome sequencing was conducted on the white blood cells of each individual, targeting a sequencing depth of approximately 30x using the BGI-seq500 platform. Similar to the pregnancy study, 80 out of the 84 metabolites were examined in each individual, following the same protocol. Amino acids, vitamins, and hormones were assayed using plasma, while the elements were assayed using whole blood. The four metabolites not investigated were I, Ba, DOC and Hcy.

The WGS data were processed by aligning and calling variants using the Picard (http://picard.sourceforge.net), BWA77 and GATK v3.8 best practice64 pipeline. SNPs meeting specific criteria, including mapping quality greater than 40, sequencing depth greater than 4, and variant quality greater than 2.0, Phred score of Fisher’s test p value for stand bias smaller than 60.0, Haplotype score smaller than 13.0 and distance of alternative allele from the end of reads greater than 8.0 were retained for subsequent analyses. We further removed SNPs deviating from Hardy-Weinberg (p value <1 × 10−5), markers with more than 1% missing genotype data and variants with minor allele frequencies smaller than 1% were removed.

Individuals with heterozygosity greater than three standard deviations were excluded, and among relatives within the 3rd degree of relationship, one individual was randomly selected for inclusion in the clean dataset. Population stratification was investigated through principal component analysis (PCA), demonstrating a typical north-to-south gradient by the first principal component. Linear regression, incorporating individual sex and the top two principal components as covariates, was performed for each significant locus identified in the pregnancy study. Additionally, regression analyses were conducted using only female individuals while adjusting for the top two principal components, and no inflation was observed in this analysis (λGC ∼1).

KEGG and pleiotropy analysis

To explore the pleiotropy among metabolites, we employed a forest plot for each of the 53 significant loci (p value ≤5 x 10−8), depicting the effect and p value for the lead SNP (Figures S45–S49). Strong pleiotropy was defined as the presence of a lead SNP affecting at least two metabolites with a significance level of 10−3. To further understand the relationship between SNP variants and multiple metabolites, we conducted Wald ratio estimation using the tsls package in R for each of the 27 variants that suggesting pleiotropy. A total of 54 tests were performed, corresponding to a p value threshold less than 0.001 after Bonferroni correction. The pleiotropic correlations were summarized and visualized in the KEGG pathway using the MetaboAnalyst website (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).

Colocalization analysis

To explore the potential shared genetic underpinnings between metabolites and pregnancy phenotypes as reported in a companion paper, we conducted a colocalization analysis. Employing a stringent Bayesian approach implemented in the coloc R package78 with default parameters, we aimed to estimate the posterior probability (PP) indicating the likelihood of both metabolites and the pregnancy phenotypes being influenced by a common causal SNP at a specific locus. SNPs within a 1Mb vicinity of the instrumental variable, possessing a MAF exceeding 0.05, were included in the analysis. Metabolites demonstrating a PPH4 > 0.5 (posterior probabilities suggesting the sharing of a single causal SNP between two traits) were considered to be colocalized.

Two-sample mendelian randomization between metabolites and 120 traits from the Biobank of Japan

To assess the potential causal influence of metabolites on human traits or diseases, we performed Mendelian randomization analyses using four methods: the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, the MR Egger method, the weighted median method, and the weighted mode method, all implemented in the TwoSampleMR R package.79 Metabolites surpassing the corrected threshold p < 0.01 were retained for further evaluation of pleiotropy and heterogeneity evaluation. Cochran’s Q statistic was employed to scrutinize the heterogeneity of the IVs, while the MR Egger approach provided intercept values for assessing horizontal pleiotropy. The MR Steiger test80 was applied to examine the directionality of the effect. Only potential causal effects passing the tests for pleiotropy, directionality, and heterogeneity (p > 0.05) were reported in our study.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Power analysis

The power analysis presented in Figure S4 was computed using the formulation by Visscher et al.81 which defines power a function of a non-centrality parameter (NCP) associated with the test statistic used to examine whether the genetic effect equals zero. For NIPT data the effective sample size is a product of the experimental sample size and the imputation accuracy. The NCP follows a chi-square distribution with 1° of freedom. When computing the power, we assume Rimp2=0.8.
