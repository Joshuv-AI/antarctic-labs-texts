Systemic and local lymph node inflammation at disease onset in sarcoidosis patients
Authors: Marie Robert, Laure Boizeau, Pierre Cappy, Etienne Villain, Slobodan Culina, Aurélie Sannier, Nader Yatim, Arthur Mageau, Nicolas Charles, Christophe Rodriguez, Nathan Peiffer-Smadja, Raphaël Borie, Darragh Duffy, Paul-Louis Woerther, Karim Sacré
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcoidosis is a chronic granulomatous disease of unknown origin, and the potential involvement of microorganisms has long been debated. We conducted the present study to explore the role of microorganisms at the onset of the disease using shotgun sequencing on lymph node samples.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study of adult patients admitted for sarcoidosis in the department of internal medicine from a tertiary referral university hospital in France (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05916638). Plasma and tissue samples were collected from sarcoidosis patients prior to the initiation of treatment. Plasma controls were healthy individuals. Tissue sample controls were patients with metastatic adenocarcinoma who underwent lymph node biopsy procedures for cancer staging.ResultsPlasma samples were collected from 19 patients with sarcoidosis and 10 control subjects. Of the 45 proteins studied using a multiplex quantification assay, the levels of 29 were significantly higher in the sarcoidosis patients. Of these, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9, lymphotoxin-α and tumour necrosis factor were also significantly upregulated at the mRNA level in affected tissues. Unbiased transcriptomic analysis of lymph node tissue revealed enrichment of the interferon (IFN)-γ signalling pathway, the interleukin (IL)-2-STAT5 pathway and the IL-6-JAK-STAT3 pathway in sarcoidosis patients. Shotgun sequencing of the lymph nodes showed no evidence of bacteria, fungi, viruses or parasites in the sarcoidosis samples.ConclusionOur findings challenge the long-held belief that sarcoidosis is caused by a microorganism. The activation of the JAK-STAT and IFN-γ signalling pathways in sarcoidosis tissue indicates that JAK inhibitors could be used to treat the condition.

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterised by the presence of granulomas, primarily affecting the lungs and lymph nodes [1]. Previous studies, including those conducted by our research group, have demonstrated that patients with sarcoidosis exhibit a systemic inflammatory profile several years prior to diagnosis and at the onset of the disease [2, 3]. The cytokines (e.g. tumour-necrosis factor (TNF)) and chemokines (e.g. C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)-9, CXCL-10) that are part of this inflammatory signature are known to play a role in the formation of granulomas [1]. The agent that initiates the granulomatous inflammatory response in sarcoidosis remains the subject of intense debate, particularly with regard to microorganisms. To date, pan-microorganism approaches do not identify a single causative agent, and the available data remain scarce and heterogeneous [4]. This heterogeneity relied on the different methods used to detect microorganisms (e.g. culture, PCR, shotgun sequencing), the variety of samples analysed (e.g. lymph nodes, bronchoalveolar lavage, skin), and differences in sampling time relative to disease onset. In addition, very few studies have simultaneously examined inflammation and the presence of microorganisms within the same tissue. As the mechanisms underlying granuloma formation in sarcoidosis remain poorly understood, we conducted the present study to explore the role of microorganisms at the onset of the disease using a gold-standard method, shotgun sequencing together with transcriptomic and proteomic analyses.

Patients and methods

Study design and data collection

We conducted a prospective cohort study of adult patients admitted for sarcoidosis in the department of internal medicine from a tertiary referral university hospital in France (MOSAR, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05916638). Sarcoidosis diagnosis was made according to current classification criteria [5], all patients underwent a biopsy that revealed noncaseating granulomas. Exclusion criteria were HIV infection and pregnancy to avoid any interference in the observed immunological phenotypes. The trial investigators used a secure web-based electronic data-capture tool (REDCap) to record trial data.

Plasma protein measurements

Plasma samples were collected from sarcoidosis patients prior to treatment initiation and healthy control subjects. Levels of plasma proteins were measured using a high-throughput multiplex absolute quantification assay, which allows the measurement of 45 proteins in 1 µL plasma (Olink Target 48 Cytokine Detection Kit, #93200; Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden) [3]. The control group comprised 10 volunteer hospital staff members who provided plasma samples prospectively during the same period. Data from 15 out of 45 proteins included in the Olink panel have already been published [3].

Archived tissue samples

Two sets of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sarcoidosis and control tissues were analysed. Tissue samples of sarcoidosis patients were collected for diagnosis purpose prior to treatment initiation and all showed noncaseating granulomas. It was not possible to match sarcoidosis patients between the plasma protein study and the tissue study. Controls were patients with metastatic adenocarcinoma who underwent a lymph node biopsy for cancer staging procedures. Stored specimens were retrieved and 3-µm cuts processed.

Transcriptomic analysis

High-throughput sequencing of RNA started quality filtration using Trimmomatic (v0.39). Then, RNA transcripts were aligned against the reference genome (hg38) using HISAT2 system (v2.2.1) [6, 7]. Transcript assembly and quantification was performed with StringTie (v2.1.6) [8] and the differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.30.1) [9].

Pathway enrichment analysis

A list of output genes by DESeq2 differential expression analysis with log2FC>|2| and adjusted to p<0.05 was used to identify enrichment in sarcoidosis and control samples.

Metagenomics

FFPE lymph node biopsies were processed using shotgun metagenomics, which is a semi-quantitative, pan-microorganism and next-generation sequencing based approach allowing the detection of any pathogen in a sample [10]. Briefly, mechanical (glass beads), enzymatic (proteinase K), and chemical (chaotropic lysis buffer) treatments were applied to FFPE samples. Total nucleic acid extractions were performed using a QIAsymphony DSP DNA Midi kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) on QIAsymphony. Then, a DNA library was constructed using the Nextera XT kit while an RNA library was built using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit with Human RiboZero ribosomal reduction (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (Illumina) [11]. The identification of microorganisms was performed using the in-house METAMIC (v2.3.0) bioinformatic pipeline [12] only on samples with a cumulative DNA+RNA read count >15 million, a threshold above the current guidelines [13, 14]. In the present study, the median number of reads was 51 944 530 (10th–90th percentiles: 19 030 626–57 636 601). Using this cut-off, nine out of the 30 initially screened samples were excluded, leaving 21 samples for analysis. We then used a pathogen read count/host read count ratio of 1×10−5 to consider viral, bacterial, fungal and parasite species, but we also checked pathogens with a lower ratio. The protocol used has been subjected to independent evaluation [12], its performance compared with other molecular approaches in various contexts and has also been validated on FFPE samples [10, 15].

Statistical analysis

Mann–Whitney U-tests were performed to compare plasma and tissue protein levels, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (adj-p). ANOVA tests were performed for metagenomics.

Study approval

All patients included in our MOSAR prospective cohort study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05916638) have received information about the study and written inform consent was required. Study protocol was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Sud-Ouest et Outre-Mer III (number 2022-A02637-36) and conforms to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Plasma profiling in sarcoidosis cases and healthy controls

We first used a high throughput multiplex absolute quantification assay to measure the levels of 45 proteins in 1 µL of plasma. Plasma samples were collected from 19 sarcoidosis cases (median age 42 years, interquartile range (IQR) 32–52 years; 47% female; supplementary table S1) prior to treatment initiation and 10 healthy control subjects (median age 41 years, IQR 33–46 years; 60% female). Of the 45 proteins studied, the levels of 29 were significantly increased in the sarcoidosis cases compared to the healthy controls (adj-p<0.05) (supplementary figure S1), including CXCL-9, lymphotoxin-α (LTA), tumour necrosis factor (TNF) (figure 1a), as well as interferon (IFN)-γ and interleukin (IL)-6 (supplementary figure S1).

Transcriptomic analyses of lymph-node biopsies

We next examined whether this systemic immune response was mirrored in the affected tissues of 10 sarcoidosis patients by analysing biopsies and fine needle aspirations from lymph nodes. Sarcoidosis tissue samples were collected for diagnostic purposes prior to treatment initiation, and all showed noncaseating granulomas. Clinical characteristics of sarcoidosis cases at the time of diagnosis are reported in table 1. As healthy lymph node samples were unavailable, biopsies from 11 patients with metastatic adenocarcinoma who underwent lymph node biopsy for cancer staging procedures were used as controls (supplementary table S2). Of the 29 proteins elevated in sarcoidosis plasma samples, CXCL9, LTA and TNF were also significantly upregulated at the mRNA level in affected tissues (adj-p<0.05) (figure 1b). An additional unbiased transcriptomic analysis of lymph node tissue revealed that there were 1009 differentially expressed coding genes between sarcoidosis and cancer patients (adj-p<0.05 and log2FC>|2|; supplementary table S3), with IFN-γ signalling, IL-2-STAT5 and IL-6-JAK-STAT3 pathway enrichment in sarcoidosis patients (adj-p<0.05) (figure 1c–e).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that the inflammatory response detected in the blood and lymph nodes of sarcoidosis patients at diagnosis can arise in the absence of active microbial presence within the affected lymph nodes. Previous studies aimed at identifying microbial triggers have produced conflicting results. This is probably due to methodological differences, variability in samples and contamination issues [4]. Nevertheless, our work does not rule out the possibility that exposure to a pathogen at an earlier stage could trigger the long-standing inflammatory signature observed years before diagnosis [2]. It is worth noting that we previously reported similar inflammatory plasma protein profiles in patients with tuberculosis and sarcoidosis before they started treatment [3]. Although Mycobacterium tuberculosis has long been suspected as a potential trigger [1], we did not detect this pathogen nor any others at the time of diagnosis, meaning the cause of sarcoidosis remains unknown. Recent findings suggest that retroviruses may play a role in sarcoidosis pathogenesis, but different methods to those used in our study are necessary to dissect their contributions [16]. If an active infection is not driving the disease, it may instead result from a dysregulated host immune response. Our data, together with findings from other studies, indicate increased levels of cytokines and chemokines (e.g. CXCL-9, CXCL-10, CXCL-11, TNF) associated with monocytes and monocyte–derived macrophages in patients with sarcoidosis. It has recently been shown that these myeloid cells can display memory, termed trained immunity. We have hypothesised recently that dysregulated trained immunity may connect past exposures (including to microorganisms), host factors, and the natural history of the disease [1].

The present study was not designed to identify biomarkers for sarcoidosis. However, from a therapeutic perspective, our pathway analyses revealed the activation of JAK-STAT and IFN-γ signalling pathways in sarcoidosis tissue samples. Notably, case reports and small retrospective studies, as well as two open-label prospective trials, have shown promising results for the use of JAK inhibitors, particularly tofacitinib, in the treatment of cutaneous and pulmonary sarcoidosis [17].

Our study has several limitations. The control groups used for blood and tissue profiling differ. The lack of matching between patients and controls may have masked potential confounding effects of age, sex and obesity. For the transcriptomic analysis of tissue samples, the results of the differential and pathway analyses may have been influenced by the fact that patients with cancer were used as controls. Nevertheless, we demonstrated a clear link between circulating and tissue immune responses, and our findings are consistent with those of previous studies. Another limitation is the small number of cases, but high-quality samples were necessary for the shotgun metagenomics analysis. The same sample also needed to provide sufficient material for transcriptomic analysis, allowing us to examine both inflammation and the presence of microorganisms within the same tissue. To confirm the absence of microorganisms, additional PCR analyses could have been of interest. However, standard real–time PCR is often affected by formalin fixation, which degrades nucleic acids. In contrast, shotgun metagenomics allows the detection of fragmented and degraded nucleic acids because it uses DNA tagmentation (transposase–based nucleic acid indexing) to introduce sequencing indices into sample DNA and ligation to incorporate sequencing indices into sample RNA. Given that granulomas can affect any organ, future studies should explore other affected organs. In addition, our results do not exclude past microbial exposure or a potential contribution of microorganisms at other sites, such as the lungs.

Overall, our findings, obtained at diagnosis and restricted to lymph nodes, challenge the long-held paradigm that sarcoidosis is caused by a microorganism. Instead, they support the hypothesis that the disease can arise from the host's own maladapted immune response. The activation of the JAK-STAT and IFN-γ signalling pathways in sarcoidosis tissue suggests that JAK inhibitors have therapeutic potential, a possibility that should be investigated in large clinical trials.
