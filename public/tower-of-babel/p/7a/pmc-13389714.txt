Community-based participatory medical education in rural Japan: analysis of medical student learning needs through reflection sheets
Authors: Shuntaro Matsuda, Ikki Nokita, Manabu Hayakawa, Yoshiro Itou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Rural Japan is facing rapid aging, depopulation, and shortages of physicians. Community-based medical education (CBME) was introduced to cultivate future physicians in rural and underserved regions. The impact of participatory clerkships, in which students actively join healthcare teams should be defined.Methods: From 2015 to 2017, fifth-year medical students (n=132) from a Medical University completed clerkships at a rural hospital. The students participated in outpatient clinics, inpatient wards, home care, and case conferences alongside residents and attending physicians. Daily reflection sheets were collected and analyzed qualitatively, and the learning needs were categorized into four domains.Results: A total of 486 comments were analyzed. Most learning needs concerned general clinical knowledge and skills (65%), followed by patient communication (10.2%); community-specific medicine (14%); and other reflections, including self-attitude (10.7%). Students emphasized medical interviewing, clinical reasoning, and basic procedures as core areas for further learning in general clinical knowledge and skills.Conclusion: Participatory CBME in a rural hospital provides rich opportunities for clinical learning, equivalent to those in urban university hospital rotations, while fostering awareness of rural practices and community care. Our findings suggest that participatory clerkships effectively enhance both clinical competencies and motivation for rural practice.

Introduction

Japan is facing an unprecedented demographic shift, characterized by rapid aging and population decline, particularly in rural and remote areas. These changes have exacerbated physician shortages and weakened the healthcare systems in many regions. To sustain community health care, it is critical to foster physicians capable of delivering comprehensive care. Community-based medical education (CBME) was introduced across Japanese medical schools as part of the model’s core curriculum to expose students to rural practices. Previous studies in Japan have shown that CBME enhances student motivation to pursue rural practice1, 2). However, they reported that students learning focused more on clinical knowledge than on communities and families, raising concerns about CBME outcomes3). Therefore, we implemented a participatory CBME program in a rural hospital and analyzed reflection sheets to clarify its educational impact.

Patients and Methods

Setting: Kushima Municipal Hospital is a 80-bed general hospital and the only emergency hospital in Kushima City, with a population of 18,000. From 2015 to 2017, all fifth-year medical students (n=132) at the University of Miyazaki participated in clerkships.

Clerkship Program: The clerkship was conducted over a 5-day period. The students participated in outpatient clinics, inpatient wards, home care, and case conferences. They were assigned electronic health record accounts, case discussions, and family meetings under supervision. This participatory model was consistent with the framework of “community-based generalist education” reported in a Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare-funded study in Kushima City, where medical students, residents, and general medicine fellows engaged in comprehensive care including outpatient, inpatient, and home-based practice under supervision of generalist physicians4).

Data Collection: Students completed one reflection sheet per day during clerkship, consisting of three open-ended questions about their successful experiences, challenges, and goals. Two authors independently coded the reflection sheets, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached.

Data Analysis: A total of 486 comments related to students learning needs were extracted and included in the analysis. The reflection entries were coded into four categories: general clinical knowledge and skills, patient communication, community-specific medicine (learning related to the local community context and rural practices), and others. Furthermore, entries categorized under general clinical knowledge and skills were subdivided into specific domains, including medical interviewing, procedures, medical knowledge, physical examination, and clinical reasoning.

This study was primarily descriptive. Statistical analyses were not performed because no comparable data were available for quantitative comparisons.

Discussion

Although the data analyzed in this study were collected between 2015 and 2017, documenting these findings remains valuable as baseline data to evaluate the continuity and reproducibility of participatory CBME outcomes. Our study demonstrated that medical students who engaged in participatory CBME in a rural Japanese hospital primarily expressed learning needs related to their general clinical knowledge and skills. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that rural placement can provide robust opportunities for clinical training comparable to those in university hospitals5, 6). While some earlier studies interpreted the predominance of clinical knowledge acquisition as a limitation of CBME3), our results suggest that such outcomes should be valued, and that participatory clerkships enable students to consolidate essential competencies while simultaneously experiencing rural practice.

The participatory model, in which students are assigned electronic health record accounts and participate in supervised responsibilities within the healthcare team, likely enhances their engagement and professional identity formation. Although community-specific learning accounted for a smaller proportion 14% of reflections, experiences such as home visits, family meetings, and interprofessional collaborations may serve as important foundations for long-term interest in rural medicine. This relatively small proportion may reflect the developmental stages of fifth-year medical students. In addition, the relatively short duration of the clerkships may limit opportunities for deeper community-oriented learning.

Notably, our findings are aligned with a Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW)-funded case study in Kushima City, Miyazaki Prefecture, which demonstrated the feasibility of community-based generalist education led by rural general practitioners4). In this program, medical students, residents, and fellows engaged in outpatient, inpatient, and home-based practices as active team members. The MHLW report further emphasized broader outcomes, such as increased involvement in home-based care, strengthened interprofessional collaboration, and enhanced community engagement, indicating that participatory clerkships can simultaneously improve learner competencies and contribute to the sustainability of rural healthcare systems.

Additionally, previous reports from Japan and abroad3, 5, 6) consistently showed that students primary learning needs in rural clerkships centered on medical knowledge and clinical skills, similar to those emphasized in university hospital rotations. Thus, the essential pattern remained unchanged: students biomedical interests were reinforced regardless of the practice setting. Differences among studies arise mainly from the outcomes selected for evaluation, such as whether biomedical competencies, communication skills, or community orientation are prioritized. Therefore, the interpretation of the effective CBME effectiveness is highly contingent on the definition of educational outcomes.

Taken together, these findings highlight the dual benefits of participatory CBME: strengthening clinical competencies at the individual level while contributing to healthcare delivery in underserved rural communities. Importantly, this model also supports the mission of rural medical education to cultivate physicians who are not only clinically competent but also motivated to sustain community-based healthcare systems. Our ongoing follow-up analyses of subsequent cohorts suggest the reproducibility of these findings, reinforcing the potential of participatory clerkships as a sustainable approach for rural physician workforce development.

Therefore, the documentation of earlier data provides an essential reference for ongoing and future comparative analyses. This study had several limitations. First, owing to the absence of comparable control or baseline data, statistical analysis could not be conducted. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than inferential. Although the data were collected several years ago, we consider it important to publish these findings as baseline data. Preliminary comparisons with more recent community-based clinical training outcomes suggest that the effectiveness of participatory clerkships in rural settings has remained largely unchanged.

Conclusion

Participatory CBME in a rural hospital strengthened student clinical competencies while cultivating their awareness of rural practices. This model contributes to sustaining rural healthcare systems and to motivating general generalist physicians. Importantly, this field report demonstrates that a rural community hospital-based participatory clerkship, rather than a clinic-based model, can provide comprehensive educational value.
