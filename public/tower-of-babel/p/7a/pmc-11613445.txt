Biomechanics Model to Characterize Atomic Force Microscopy-Based Virus–Host Cell Adhesion Measurements
Authors: Jiajun Wang, Matthew Ziarnik, X. Frank Zhang, Anand Jagota
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We present a model for virus–cell adhesion that can be used for quantitative extraction of adhesive properties from atomic force microscopy (AFM)-based force spectroscopy measurements. We extend a previously reported continuum model of viral cell interactions based on a single parameter representing adhesive energy density by using a cohesive zone model in which adhesion is represented by two parameters, a pull-off stress and associated characteristic displacement. This approach accounts for the deformability of the adhesive receptors, such as the Spike protein and transmembrane immunoglobulin and mucin domain (TIM) family that mediate adhesion of SARS-CoV-2 and Ebola viruses, and the omnipresent glycocalyx. Our model represents receptors as a Winkler foundation and aims to predict the pull-off force needed to break the adhesion between the virus and the cell. By comparing the force–separation curves simulated by the model and experimental data, we found that the model can effectively explain the AFM pull-off force trace, thus allowing quantification of the adhesion parameters. Our model provides a more refined understanding of viral cell adhesion and also establishes a framework for interpreting and predicting AFM force spectroscopy measurements.

Introduction

The emergence and spread of viral-related diseases represent an ever-present and evolving threat to human health. The world has witnessed the devastating impact of viral outbreaks throughout history, from the Spanish flu1 in 1918 to the more recent challenges posed by viruses such as HIV,2,3 Ebola,4−6 and SARS-CoV-2.7−10 Viral infection has become one of the major concerns to public health. It can cost billions of dollars, cause economic damage, and countless loss of human life. Understanding how viruses invade our body is the key to finding therapies. In the lifecycle of an enveloped virus, where most emerging viruses belong, viral contact and attachment to the host cell membrane are followed by endocytosis, which results in viral particle uptake and viral–endosomal membrane fusion.11 After releasing the viral ribonucleoprotein complex into the cytoplasm, replication of viral particles is followed by budding from the host cell to spread the virus and repeat the cycle.12−14 The early stage of adhesion between the viral particle and receptors on the cell membrane surface is a crucial event for infection. Understanding the mechanics of this phenomenon holds the potential to provide insight that can help develop treatments, cures, or strategies to block the attachment of viral particles to the cell membrane.

In previous work,15 we developed a continuum model to encompass crucial aspects of viral–cell adhesion in which the viral particle was modeled as a rigid cylinder or sphere, the cell membrane carried tension and bending stiffness, and adhesion was represented solely by the work of adhesion. However, the actual biological context is more complicated. For example, for both SARS-CoV-2 and Ebola, adhesive receptors take the form of elongated structures: the Spike and TIM receptors, respectively. The continuum approach we employed represents these structures through a single number: the adhesion energy density. While there is considerable literature that follows this approach, it neglects some essential characteristics.16,17 For example, the surface of most cells in the human body displays a bush-like complex layer comprising sugars along with the proteins and lipids to which they are connected, called the glycocalyx.18 The glycocalyx is believed to play an important role in various cellular and organismic events, such as leukocyte adhesion19 and viral infections.20

Therefore, it is important to improve our understanding of ligand–receptor adhesive interactions, for example, to explore how AFM-based force spectroscopy measurements can be integrated with other physical properties like the stiffness of the receptor layer and the breaking of ligand–receptor bonds. Here, we can borrow from the notion of cohesive zones representing the adhesion, for which there is considerable literature in fracture mechanics.21

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) has emerged as a versatile tool in life sciences and biomedical research, offering an unparalleled capacity to characterize the surface of biological samples at the nanoscale.22−24 It operates by employing an ultrasharp tip attached to a flexible cantilever. The interaction forces between the tip and the sample can be detected down to picoNewton (pN) levels, allowing, for example, the study of adhesion between the viral particle and their receptors on the host cell membrane.

Here, we present a continuum model for the force–displacement trace in AFM measurements by introducing a Winkler foundation represented by a cohesive law for a deformable interface. We are able to align experimental data with theoretical predictions using our model. This is of interest because such analyses, by matching the results of AFM force spectroscopy measurements, can be used to extract parameters of the cohesive model that represent the adhesive interface.

In the following sections, we will first outline how the model is formulated for both cylindrical and spherical viral particles. In later sections, we use the formulation to model AFM-based experimental data. The Supporting Information contains details of derivations.

Methods

Description of Theoretical Model

In this study, we have developed a biomechanics model for AFM force–displacement measurement as described next. The adhesion force measurements are typically conducted in AFM force spectroscopy mode.25−27 In the experiment, the viral particle is immobilized on the functionalized cantilever, and its corresponding receptors are attached to the cell surface or a sample substrate (Figure 1). During the experiment, the cantilever is lowered onto the substrate surface to allow binding between the virus particles and their receptors (Figure 1a). After the virus particle has been pushed in sufficiently after making contact with the substrate, the cantilever is retracted. An adhesive pull-off force can be observed if any binding has occurred. For example, the retraction trace in Figure 1 shows several jumps (c), which represent several receptor–ligand breakages. We consider two versions of this problem: a cylindrical and a spherical virus particle. We use Ebola virus-like particles (cylinder) and lentivirus (sphere) as examples of these two in this work.

In most previous continuum15 models, the adhesive interactions between ligands and receptors have been represented by a single number, the adhesion energy density. In order to better model the adhesion process, in this work, we replace the adhesive energy with a cohesive zone model, which contains two parameters, characteristic cohesive stress, and opening. Such models are well-known in the literature on fracture mechanics.21 They offer a regularization of crack-tip singularities. This can be interpreted as a depiction of receptors that form a Winkler foundation.28 We assume that receptors act independently of each other and that their response to ligands is proportionate to the ligand concentration. Simulating the process of ligand–receptor bond breaking during AFM force measurements entails employing a cohesive law to model the rupture process, providing a more comprehensive representation of the interfacial interaction dynamics. To develop the model, we consider three main elements: the viral particle, the receptor bush, and the cell membrane.

To align the AFM experiment data with the model, we solve the problem for a range of values for the opening parameter σ and spring stiffness kf. For each given σ and kf, we calculate the root-mean-square (RMS) difference between our model’s output and AFM data. The pair of σ and kf values that result in the lowest RMS error indicate the optimal choice of these two cohesive zone parameters. See also the Supporting Information.

Experimental Methods

In this study, we performed AFM force measurements for two different virus particle cases. We use the Ebola virus as representative of the cylindrical case and the Lentivirus as the spherical case. Because EBOV is classified as a BSL-4 pathogen, we opted to employ a suitable model system for our research, which utilizes virus-like particles (VLPs), a nonpathogenic reagent suitable for investigation in BSL-2 laboratories. These VLPs are generated through the overexpression of the EBOV VP40 protein in mammalian cells. The expression of VP40 alone is adequate for the assembly of VLPs that closely resemble the size and the cylindrical shape of EBOV.37 Previous research has demonstrated that these VLPs interact with and are internalized by host cells in a manner similar to wild-type EBOV.38,39 The AFM force measurements were performed using a homemade AFM in force spectroscopy mode.25−27 During the AFM experiment, the functionalized cantilever was lowered onto the sample coated with TIM proteins, facilitating the interaction between TIM and phosphatidylserine (PS). After a brief contact, the cantilever was subsequently withdrawn from the surface. Binding between TIM and PS would cause a tensile rupture force, which can be determined by the deflection of the cantilever.

Immortalized HEK 293 T cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were cultured in DMEM medium (ATCC) supplemented with 4 mM l-glutamine, 4500 mg/L glucose, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1500 mg/L sodium bicarbonate, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 10% fetal bovine serum. Cell growth was carried out in T-25 flasks (Corning) maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atm. For experimental purposes, the cells were plated in 35 mm Cyto-One culture dishes (USA Scientific). Colonic epithelial cells were obtained by Dr. Yaling Liu’s lab at Lehigh University. These cells were incubated until they reached at least 50% confluency. The cell media consisted of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (ATCC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Treated cells were tested in a solution of 0.5 U/mL of heparinase (Millipore Sigma) and 50 μM of sodium chlorate (Millipore Sigma) during testing. Heparin-biotin (Millipore Sigma) was purchased for a noncellular AFM-based single-molecule force spectroscopy experiment with the epithelial cells.

The HEK 293 T cells were split 1 day prior to transfection and were seeded in 35 mm Cyto-One culture dishes (USA Scientific) at 50% confluence. 200 μL of Opti-MEM (1×) reduced serum medium (Life Technologies) was added to a 0.5 mL tube. Transfection was performed by introducing 1 μg of TIM-1 expression plasmid40,41 into the cells using MegaTran 1.0 transfection reagent (Origene Technologies) with a DNA: MegaTran ratio of 1:3 based on manufacturer’s instructions.

To functionalize AFM cantilevers (MLCT-BIO–DC, Bruker, USA) with VLPs40,41 of EBOV (a kind gift from Dr. Wendy Maury, University of Iowa) or BFP lentivirus particles, the cantilever was first silanized with (3-aminopropyl)-triethoxysilane (APTES). Ebola VLP or Lentivirus particles at 1:10 dilution were immobilized onto the silanized cantilever using a heterobifunctional PEG cross-linker, Acetal-PEG-NHS (2000 MW, Creative PEGworks, USA), according to the detailed protocol developed by Dr. Hermann J. Gruber, Johannes Kepler University.42,43 Functionalized cantilevers were stored and tested in PBS and used for the AFM-based single-molecule force spectroscopy experiment within 8 h.

The single-molecule force measurements of EBOV VLP—TIM-1 interactions and lentivirus–cell interactions were carried out using a homemade AFM apparatus. The AFM measurement data were collected at a cantilever retraction speed of 3.7 μm/s. All measurements were conducted at a temperature of 25 °C in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) with the addition of 5 mM CaCl2. The contact time and indentation force between the cantilever and the sample were minimized to ensure accurate measurements of the unitary unbinding force. The lentivirus AFM measurements were conducted at 25 °C in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Results and Discussion

In this study, we employ this biomechanics model presented in Section 2 to characterize and quantify the AFM force measurement by applying biophysical parameters identified from single-molecule studies related to the mechanics of virus adhesion. In previous work,15 we have developed a continuum model illustrating the adhesive interaction between viral particles and cell membrane by introducing the adhesion property: work of adhesion, the production of adhesion energy of each ligand pair, and the density of ligand pairs. To capture the ligand–receptor breakage process, we bring two different parameters, kf and σ, to represent the interfacial adhesion properties.

In the AFM force measurement experiment (as shown in Figure 5), the binding ligands are pulled and stretched when the cantilever moves away from the sample. Then, at some point, the ligand–receptor pairs start to break, and we can observe the rupture forces from the force–displacement curve. Given that our models are tailored to accommodate two distinct viral particle shapes, we conducted AFM force measurements separately for EBOV as a cylindrical and lentivirus as a spherical virus particle and matched the pull-off curve for each, separately.

In order to characterize and quantify the force–displacement measurement, our biomechanics model includes various physical property parameters that can be adjusted to align with the retract trace. To achieve the alignment with the TIM-PS AFM force–displacement curve, we fixed the bending rigidity, κ, to be 17 kBT,27 membrane tension, T, to be 0.5 mN/m,33,34 and the viral particle radius of EBOV to be 40 nm.27 However, Filovirus virions exhibit a variety of shapes, a characteristic known as pleomorphism.44 These shapes can appear as U-shaped, 6-shaped, or in various other configurations. Therefore, the effective radius during AFM experiments is larger than 40 nm. In Figure 5, although the displacement ranges of the virus particles in (a) and (b) differ significantly, the peak force values are nearly same. For optimal alignment, a radius of 400 nm was used. Subsequently, we iteratively loop the opening parameter σ and spring stiffness, kf, to compute the force–displacement curve until the minimum Euclidean distance between our model and AFM experiment retract trace is identified.

The values extracted are opening parameter σ = 30 nm and spring stiffness (details in the Supporting Information). The spring stiffness kf represents the density or resistance provided by a layer of receptors and glycocalyx. In the context of cohesive stress theory, the opening parameter usually quantifies the distance between the two interfaces of a crack. In our model, It is a measure of separation between the virus and the layer of receptors and glycocalyx during the retraction phrase.

Summary and Conclusions

We have developed a biomechanical model designed to characterize and quantify the pulling procedure in AFM force spectroscopy measurements of viral particle–cell adhesion. The principal novelty of this model is to replace the single parameter characterizing adhesion in conventional models, the adhesion energy density, with a cohesive zone model that represents the adhesive interaction by a cohesive function which contains two independent parameters: a characteristic opening and a characteristic pull-off stress. We show that the model can capture AFM pull-off force traces and, by optimizing the process, can extract cohesive zone parameters from the experiment. We achieve this by finding optimal values of receptor layer stiffness kf and opening parameter σ. The spring stiffness can be seen as a measure of how tightly packed or resistant the receptor bush is to deformation. The opening parameter is a critical measure that ensures the virus does not create a separation too large for effectively simulating the breakage of the receptor ligands.
