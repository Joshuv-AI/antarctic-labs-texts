Clinical Outcomes After Ulnar Collateral Ligament Reconstruction With Palmaris Longus Versus Gracilis Autograft in Baseball Players: A Cohort Analysis
Authors: Michael C. Ciccotti, Brooks Martino, Frank Cautela, Nathaniel Tchangou, Daniel Kwak, Eric DiDomenico, Harrison Fellheimer, Christopher C. Dodson, Steven B. Cohen, Michael G. Ciccotti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) reconstruction (UCLR) is a common procedure for overhead athletes, particularly baseball players sustaining partial or full UCL tears. Palmaris longus (PL) and gracilis (GR) autografts are the most frequently used graft sources, yet limited data compare clinical outcomes in baseball athletes.Purpose:To compare patient-reported outcomes after UCLR with PL versus GR autografts in baseball players.Study Design:Cohort study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:Patients who underwent isolated UCLR with either PL or GR autograft and a minimum of 2 years of follow-up were identified. All reconstructions were performed using the Modified Jobe or Docking techniques, followed by a standardized rehabilitation protocol. Demographic characteristics, playing level, preoperative elbow function, and operative details were obtained. Included patients completed patient-reported outcomes (PROs), including the Kerlan-Jobe Orthopedic Clinic Shoulder & Elbow score (KJOC), the Timmerman and Andrews elbow score, the Conway-Jobe scale, and custom patient satisfaction and return-to-play (RTP) questionnaires.Results:A total of 71 athletes (50 GR, 21 PL) met the inclusion criteria. Groups did not differ in age, body mass index, level or position of play, preoperative range of motion, valgus/varus stability, magnetic resonance imaging tear thickness, ultrasound gapping, or preoperative KJOC scores. Surgical technique, use of internal brace, nerve management, and intra- or postoperative complications were similar between groups (P≥ .421). At ≥2-year follow-up, PROs were comparable: KJOC (GR: 77.6 ± 22.4 vs PL: 75 ± 22.6; P = .584), Timmerman-Andrews (GR: 89.9 ± 13.2 vs PL: 87.2 ± 8.16; P = .093). RTP outcomes by Conway-Jobe scale were similar (P = .867). Time to RTP did not differ between groups (GR: 12.5 ± 2.7 months vs PL: 10.8 ± 3.8 months; P = .170). The only postoperative difference was greater elbow flexion in the PL group (148° vs 144°; P < .001). Extension, supination, and pronation did not differ (P≥ .133).Conclusion:PL and GR autografts demonstrate equivalent clinical and RTP outcomes after UCLR in baseball players.

Ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) reconstruction (UCLR) is a well-established procedure for overhead athletes, particularly baseball pitchers, who sustain full tears of the UCL or partial tears that are recalcitrant to nonoperative treatment and inappropriate for UCL repair.6,11,14,36 The incidence of UCL injury has continued to rise in recent years, with reconstruction performed as a routine intervention for those seeking to return to competitive play.6,12,13 In fact, nearly one-third of all UCLR procedures performed on Major League Baseball players between 1974 and 2015 have occurred 12 after 2010. Since the initial reconstruction performed by Jobe, a variety of technical modifications have been made, including exposure, management of the ulnar nerve, tunnel size/location/configuration, fixation, and graft source. 25

The graft source remains a critical consideration, with the palmaris longus (PL) and gracilis (GR) autografts representing the 2 most commonly utilized options.6,20,33 Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that both graft sources provide similar tensile strength.30,35 The PL tendon is often favored because of its proximity to the surgical field, but there is a theoretical risk of injury to the median nerve, and it may be absent in up to 25% of individuals, necessitating the use of alternative grafts such as the GR tendon. 24 Furthermore, GR autograft may be favored in some patients, such as in the case of revisions where the ipsilateral PL has been previously harvested or in patients with deficient native UCL after intraligamentous bony excision or large bony proximal ulnar overgrowth/enthesophyte.15,33 The GR autograft, while consistently available and typically larger in diameter, requires additional dissection in another extremity and may be associated with increased postoperative discomfort, injury to branches of the saphenous nerve, and donor-site weakness.5,27,32,34 In baseball players, the decision to harvest the hamstring tendon from the landing (contralateral) or drive (ipsilateral) leg is an additional consideration, with no consensus in the literature regarding the optimal choice.

Despite the widespread use of both grafts, limited literature has directly compared their clinical outcomes in baseball players. Previous retrospective studies suggest similar return-to-play (RTP) rates between graft types, but variations in recovery timelines, subsequent injury profile, and long-term performance metrics remain incompletely understood.16,18,20 Arner et al 2 investigated the outcomes of the modified Jobe versus the UCLR docking technique in throwing athletes. They also reported that PL autograft resulted in significantly higher Kerlan-Jobe Orthopaedic Clinic (KJOC) scores than GR autograft (82.3 ± 20 vs 57.9 ± 21.2; P = .001), prompting the present study to confirm whether this difference truly existed. No study has examined differences in patient-reported outcomes (PROs) between UCLR with GR versus PL autografts as the primary outcome across all levels of baseball players. This study aimed to compare PROs after UCLR with PL versus GR autografts in baseball players, hypothesizing that both grafts would yield comparable outcomes.

Methods

Patients who underwent an isolated UCLR (Current Procedural Terminology 24346) with either PL or GR autograft between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2022, and with a minimum 2-year follow-up were identified from the authors’ home institution database. The cohort included patients treated by 6 board-certified, fellowship-trained orthopaedic surgeons at a single academic institution (including M.G.C., M.C.C, S.C., C.C.D.). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) injuries unrelated to baseball; (2) <2 years of postoperative follow-up; (3) autograft other than PL and GR or allograft; (4) underwent UCL repair rather than UCLR; or (5) received concomitant procedures of the elbow (ie, ulnar nerve transposition, osteophyte resection, osteochondritis dissecans). Two comparison groups were identified: UCLR with PL autograft and UCLR with GR autograft.

UCL tears were diagnosed preoperatively by physical examination and imaging modalities, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance arthrography (MRA). The classification system introduced by Joyner et al 26 was clinically used to determine tear type based on MRI/MRA. Inconclusive imaging or partial tear on MRI/MRA was frequently followed by stress ultrasound to evaluate ulnohumeral joint gapping with applied valgus stress, a technique that has been extensively validated.3,4,8, 9,10,23,29,31 All patients underwent UCLR via the Modified Jobe or Docking techniques. 7 After surgery, all patients underwent a standardized UCLR rehabilitation, as detailed by Hadley et al. 21

The electronic medical record was also reviewed for sport participation and baseline demographic and anthropometric characteristics, including age at the time of surgery and body mass index (BMI). Clinic notes were reviewed for preoperative elbow function, and operative details were extracted from a review of the operative report. Included patients were contacted via REDCap (Vanderbilt University) or by phone to complete PRO surveys. These included the KJOC Shoulder & Elbow score, the subjective portion of the Timmerman and Andrews elbow score, the Conway-Jobe scale, and custom patient satisfaction and RTP questionnaires.1,11,37 Those who filled out the PRO surveys incompletely were excluded.

Descriptive statistics, including mean, range, and standard deviation, were calculated. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare continuous variables among the 2 groups with nonparametric data, while the Student's t test was used to compare continuous variables among 2 groups with parametric data. The Fisher exact or chi-square test was used to compare categorical data. P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences Statistics Software 26 (IBM Corp).

Results

We identified 71 patients (50 GR, 21 PL) who underwent primary UCLR at our institution from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2022, and completed all postoperative PRO surveys. The 2 groups did not differ significantly in any demographic or anthropometric data, including age (P = .099), BMI (P = .462), level of play (P = .451), or position played (pitcher vs nonpitcher) (P≥ .999) (Table 1).

Discussion

The principal finding of the present study is that there was no significant difference in outcome for baseball players undergoing UCLR, performed with PL or GR autograft, as measured by multiple PROs at a minimum 2-year follow-up. These findings confirm conclusions suggested by several previous clinical investigations evaluating graft selection in UCLR.2,16,17,20 Erickson et al 16 reported in a large cohort of professional baseball players that graft choice and surgical technique did not significantly influence RTP rates or clinical outcomes after UCLR. Similarly, Griffith et al 20 evaluated professional pitchers undergoing UCLR and found that graft type did not significantly affect postoperative performance metrics or RTP outcomes. A subsequent study by Erickson et al 18 comparing PL and hamstring tendon autografts likewise demonstrated no meaningful differences in outcomes or subsequent injury rates between graft sources. Collectively, these studies suggest that graft source alone may not be a primary determinant of clinical success following UCLR.

Notably, Arner et al 2 had previously reported higher KJOC scores in patients undergoing reconstruction with PL autograft compared with GR autograft in a retrospective cohort study primarily evaluating differences between the Modified Jobe and Docking techniques for UCLR. This finding regarding the difference in outcome by graft source was derived from a secondary subgroup analysis. This study included a heterogeneous cohort of throwing athletes undergoing multiple surgical techniques, which may limit direct comparison with more uniform cohorts. The present study specifically evaluates a more homogeneous cohort of baseball athletes undergoing isolated UCLR with standardized postoperative rehabilitation and validated PROs. By focusing on a uniform patient population, our findings provide additional evidence supporting the clinical equivalence of PL and GR autografts in baseball players.

One of the primary concerns when selecting a graft for UCLR is its biomechanical integrity and performance. Although the GR tendon is typically larger in diameter at the time of harvest than the PL tendon, 33 our findings suggest that differences at the time of harvest do not translate into superior clinical outcomes at the minimum 2-year mark. This supports previous biomechanical studies indicating that both grafts provide adequate tensile strength to restore valgus stability of the elbow.30,35 Furthermore, the similar PROs noted between the 2 cohorts in this study suggest that the theoretical advantages of a larger diameter graft may not play a significant role in patient-perceived function or symptom resolution.

In the present cohort, 2 patients in the gracilis group experienced recurrent UCL failure requiring revision surgery at 16 months and 5 years postoperatively, while no revisions occurred in the palmaris cohort. Given the small sample size and low event rate, it is not possible to attribute these failures solely to graft selection. Multiple factors beyond graft selection may contribute to failure after UCLR, including but not limited to throwing workload, rehabilitation adherence, pitching mechanics, and biologic healing capacity.

Another important consideration is donor-site morbidity. The PL tendon is often preferred because of its proximity to the surgical field, whereas the GR tendon harvest may be associated with medial thigh pain, numbness, and potential weakness.5,27,32,34 Although PL harvest is generally considered safe, the proximity of the PL to the median nerve and the palmar cutaneous branch creates a risk of iatrogenic nerve injury. Rare but devastating events of inadvertent median nerve harvest have been reported in previous literature; thus, careful attention to the nerve's course at the distal forearm is crucial to mitigate this risk. 28

Another surgical consideration during UCLR is the management of the ulnar nerve. Ulnar nerve transposition may be performed selectively in patients with preoperative ulnar neuritis/neuropathy or nerve instability. In the present study, patients undergoing concomitant ulnar nerve transposition were excluded to isolate outcomes related specifically to graft selection. Postoperative neurologic symptoms could confound PROs independent of graft choice.

While both graft options appear to yield similar outcomes, certain clinical scenarios may still influence graft selection. The absence of the PL tendon in approximately 25% of individuals 24 or the case of a previous ipsilateral PL tendon harvest necessitates the use of an alternative autograft, with the GR tendon being a readily available option. Additionally, surgeons may prefer the GR tendon when a larger graft diameter is desired, such as in some cases of revision surgery. However, our findings suggest that in primary UCLR, when both grafts are available, they are equally viable for patient outcomes.

For baseball pitchers undergoing GR autograft harvest, an additional consideration is whether to harvest from the landing (contralateral) or drive (ipsilateral) leg. The drive leg plays a critical role in generating force and rotational power during the pitching motion, while the landing leg is primarily involved in deceleration and stabilization. Electromyographic analysis by Erickson et al 19 demonstrated that hamstring muscle activity is generally greater in the drive leg compared with the landing leg throughout the pitching cycle, suggesting a higher functional demand on the ipsilateral hamstrings during pitching. Based on these findings, some surgeons preferentially harvest the graft from the contralateral (landing) leg to avoid potential disruption of the musculature most involved in force generation. In the present study, 49 of 50 pitchers underwent GR autograft harvest from the contralateral leg, with only 1 pitcher undergoing an ipsilateral harvest. While this distribution aligns with electromyography-based biomechanical considerations, graft harvest laterality remains largely influenced by surgeon preference, and there is no consensus in the literature regarding the optimal harvest site. Epidemiologic data suggest that the landing leg may be more susceptible to hamstring injury, likely because of the eccentric demands of deceleration. 22 However, previous studies of professional baseball players undergoing UCLR with hamstring tendon autograft have demonstrated no significant differences in RTP rates, performance metrics, or subsequent lower-extremity injury rates based on harvest side. 17 These findings suggest that although biomechanical differences exist between the drive and landing legs, the clinical implications of hamstring harvest laterality remain unclear. Further research is needed to definitively determine whether the side of graft harvest influences postoperative performance, injury, and RTP metrics.

This study has several strengths, including the use of multiple, validated PROs with a minimum 2-year follow-up to assess clinical outcomes. This time period allows us to assess short- to mid-term outcomes sufficiently. However, we are limited in our ability to assess potential long-term differences in graft durability or the risk of reinjury beyond 2 years. Additionally, PROs, while valuable, may not fully capture subtle biomechanical differences or performance-related outcomes, such as pitching velocity or workload tolerance. Future studies with longer follow-up, objective performance measures, and biomechanical comparisons between PL and GR autografts, as well as between GR autografts harvested from the landing versus drive leg, may further elucidate potential differences between these graft options.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that at 2-year follow-up, baseball players undergoing UCLR with PL and GR autograft achieve comparable clinical outcomes. These findings suggest that both grafts provide effective solutions for restoring elbow stability in overhead athletes, allowing for individualized graft selection based on patient anatomy and surgeon preference.
