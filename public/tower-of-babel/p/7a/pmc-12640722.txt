Utility of Multimodal Large Language Models in Analyzing Chest X-Rays with Incomplete Contextual Information
Authors: Choonghan Kim, Seonhee Cho, Joo Heung Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in clinical practice, but their performance can deteriorate when radiology reports are incomplete. We evaluated whether multimodal LLMs (integrating text and images) could enhance accuracy and interpretability in chest radiography reports, thereby improving their utility for clinical decision support. Specifically, we aimed to assess the robustness of LLMs in generating accurate impressions from chest radiography reports when provided with incomplete data, and whether multimodal input could mitigate performance loss.MethodsWe analyzed 300 radiology image–report pairs from the MIMIC-CXR database. Three LLMs—OpenFlamingo, MedFlamingo, IDEFICS—were tested in text-only and multimodal formats. Chest X-ray impressions were generated from complete text reports and then regenerated after systematically removing 20%, 50%, and 80% of the text. The effect of adding images was evaluated using chest X-rays, and model performance was compared using three statistical methods. Hallucination rates were quantified.ResultsIn the textonly setting, OpenFlamingo, MedFlamingo, and IDEFICS demonstrated comparable performance (ROUGE-L: 0.23 vs. 0.21 vs. 0.21; F1RadGraph: 0.20 vs. 0.16 vs. 0.16; F1CheXbert: 0.49 vs. 0.41 vs. 0.41), with OpenFlamingo performing best on complete text (p < 0.001). All models exhibited performance decline with incomplete data. However, multimodal input significantly improved the performance of MedFlamingo and IDEFICS (p < 0.001), equaling or surpassing OpenFlamingo even under incomplete text conditions. Regarding hallucination, MedFlamingo showed a lower false-negative rate in multimodal compared with unimodal use, while false-positive rates were similar.ConclusionsLLMs may produce suboptimal outputs when radiology data are incomplete, but multimodal LLMs enhance reliability and may strengthen clinical decision-making support.

Introduction

Collecting and maintaining complete clinical data is critical for research, yet errors frequently occur during this process. In many cases, available datasets are incomplete or misleading. Radiographic image interpretation errors occur in approximately 3%–5% of cases [1], and this rate has not substantially improved despite ongoing systematic quality initiatives [2]. Similarly, simple chart reviews have revealed that error rates in electronic health records may reach 9%–10% [3].

The introduction of artificial intelligence (AI), and more recently large language models (LLMs), into medicine has generated broad interest due to their potential to address long-standing challenges in patient care, often being described as revolutionary [4,5]. LLMs’ ability to process complex unstructured data has enabled applications that improve patient experience, such as virtual consultations, reduce physician workload through voice-recognition charting, and generate automated radiology reports [6].

Nevertheless, some researchers caution that the rapid adoption of LLMs in medicine may be premature given uncertainties in real-world clinical performance. Issues such as hallucinations [7], falsification, bias, and plagiarism have been reported. These problems are exacerbated when LLMs are trained on incomplete or low-quality data, leading to biased or inaccurate outputs. Such limitations raise concerns about the evaluation of LLMs trained on clinical data, including radiology reports [8], where objective evaluation methods remain insufficient.

Our research question was whether corrupted text data would result in proportionally worse LLM performance, thereby underscoring the risks of deploying LLMs without assessing data integrity. We further hypothesized that multimodal LLMs would generate higher-quality text outputs than unimodal (text-only) models, demonstrating the advantage of integrating multiple data modalities. Finally, we anticipated that statistical methods could provide an objective framework for evaluating LLM-generated radiology reports.

Methods

Database

We used MIMIC-CXR, a de-identified, publicly available chest radiograph database collected between 2011 and 2016 [9]. MIMIC-CXR includes 227,835 imaging studies from 65,379 patients, with each study paired with a radiology report consisting of findings and impressions.

Data Preprocessing

Three exclusion criteria were applied to remove inappropriate data: (1) findings containing fewer than three words, (2) findings shorter than the corresponding impressions, and (3) findings with more than three masked words. The dataset was then merged and filtered by removing the shortest and longest 25% of entries. This produced training, validation, and test sets containing 62,613, 1,000, and 1,000 entries, respectively. For efficient experimentation, 300 samples were randomly selected from the test set. This split reflected the structure of the original MIMIC-CXR dataset, which included approximately 64,586 patients for training, 500 for validation, and 293 for testing.

To evaluate LLM robustness, random words or phrases were deleted from the findings at rates of 20%, 50%, and 80% using a sub-word masking approach similar to BERT (bidirectional encoder representations from transformers), as illustrated in Figure 1. Sub-word masking divided words into smaller components, with higher masking levels producing greater performance degradation. This method was comparable to those of Rychalska et al. [10], who corrupted words from levels 1 to 5, and Ye et al. [11], who tested word or character attacks with probabilities ranging from 0.1 to 1.0.

Large Language Models

We selected three unimodal and three multimodal models with comparable characteristics to determine whether multimodal data improved performance relative to text-only input. Because the study required models suitable for few-shot learning, we used OpenFlamingo [12], MedFlamingo [13], and IDEFICS [14]. These models were all based on the Flamingo [15] model, which handled sequences of image-text pairs using cross-attention layers known as “GATED XATTN-DENSE layers.”

OpenFlamingo is the publicly available version of Flamingo, showing similar performance on comparable tasks. MedFlamingo, built upon OpenFlamingo, was pretrained on medical textbooks and fine-tuned using 4,721 medical textbooks and the PMC-OA dataset (1.64 million image–caption pairs from PubMed Central). IDEFICS is also a vision–language model derived from Flamingo, trained on the OBELICS dataset [14], which contains 353 million images and 115 billion text tokens from general-domain sources. A detailed summary of these models is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Study Workflow

The workflow (Figure 2) began with chest X-ray findings from the MIMIC-CXR database. Text was corrupted at different levels using sub-word masking to evaluate model robustness. Each LLM first processed the data in text-only format, followed by multimodal evaluation using paired chest X-ray images. The generated impressions were compared to reference impressions using ROUGE-L, F1RadGraph, and F1CheXbert.

Prompt Engineering

Few-shot learning techniques were applied to enhance LLM performance. In few-shot learning, a small number of examples are provided to guide the model in adapting to a specific task. To select appropriate examples, we used BM25 for text retrieval, which calculates relevance between a query and documents. The top two examples, ranked by BM25 scores, were chosen to balance memory efficiency with computational cost while remaining within the LLM’s input limit of 2,048 tokens.

Evaluation of the LLM

To evaluate the model, we used three common metrics for radiology reports. ROUGE-L (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) uses longest common subsequence (LCS) statistics to assess summary quality, producing an F-score that balances recall and precision.

F1RadGraph [16] is an F-score-based metric that measures the factual accuracy, coherence, and thoroughness of radiology reports compared to a reference. To evaluate hallucinations in clinical text generation, we measured factual inaccuracies using the false positive rate (FPR) and false negative rate (FNR) based on RadGraph. Computational Cost of LLMs

We recorded each model’s parameter size, GPU hours, infrastructure usage, token cost, and inference time. OpenFlamingo contained 3 billion parameters, while MedFlamingo and IDEFICS each had 9 billion. All models required 30 GB of GPU memory, increasing to 40 GB during inference on an NVIDIA A6000 GPU. Budget estimates were compared against Amazon Web Services (AWS) and current practice benchmarks [21].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the scipy.stats library in Python 3.11. p-values were calculated from ROUGEL and F1RadGraph scores for the 300 test samples. When normality assumptions were not satisfied, bootstrap methods were applied, and significance was determined accordingly. For the five conditions evaluated with F1CheXbert, only average performance was reported due to small sample sizes, without p-values. All analyses were conducted in Python 3.11.

Results

A total of 3,146 data labels (2.46%) were found to be grossly incomplete and unsuitable for analysis. The remaining dataset included positive, negative, and uncertain labels for various conditions. In the training set, the most frequent conditions were no finding (47.6%), lung opacity (15.5%), and pleural effusion (12.4%). Other notable conditions included atelectasis (11.2%), edema (8.4%), and cardiomegaly (7.2%). The test set showed a similar distribution, with no finding (47.5%), lung opacity (15.1%), and atelectasis (11.4%) being most common. Additional conditions included pleural effusion (10.5%), cardiomegaly (7.6%), and edema (7.6%).

Using three evaluation metrics—ROUGE-L, F1RadGraph, and F1CheXbert—the text-only versions of OpenFlamingo, MedFlamingo, and IDEFICS achieved average scores of 0.23, 0.21, and 0.21 for ROUGE-L; 0.20, 0.16, and 0.16 for F1Rad-Graph; and 0.49, 0.41, and 0.41 for F1CheXbert, respectively, when tested with full-text input. Because MedFlamingo and IDEFICS were both based on the LLaMA 7B model, their text-only results were identical.

When text data were combined with images, the multimodal models—OpenFlamingo, MedFlamingo, and IDEFICS—scored 0.40, 0.41, and 0.46 on ROUGE-L; 0.33, 0.40, and 0.41 on F1RadGraph; and 0.51, 0.61, and 0.62 on F1CheXbert, respectively (Supplementary Table S2).

MedFlamingo and IDEFICS consistently outperformed their unimodal counterparts when tested with full findings and with 20% and 50% levels of data corruption. These improvements were statistically significant across all three metrics (Figure 3). OpenFlamingo exceeded its text-only performance only at the 80% corruption level, showing significant gains in ROUGE-L and F1RadGraph.

Table 1 compares fine-tuned and general models in the multimodal setting. MedFlamingo, the clinically fine-tuned model, outperformed the generalist OpenFlamingo in all but two cases: ROUGE-L and F1RadGraph at the 80% corruption level.

For F1RadGraph, MedFlamingo demonstrated superior performance compared to OpenFlamingo under full-text, 20%, and 50% corruption conditions, with statistically significant differences (Supplementary Fig. S1). MedFlamingo also outperformed OpenFlamingo in the five representative disease categories (Supplementary Table S3). Correct and incorrect output samples of MedFlamingo in multimodal settings were illustrated for each of the five diseases (Figure 4).

For hallucination analysis, FPR values were similar between unimodal and multimodal versions of MedFlamingo. In contrast, FNR showed notable differences: unimodal Med-Flamingo had consistently higher FNRs than its multimodal version across all corruption levels, reflecting a greater number of omission errors (Figure 5).

Discussion

In this study, we observed that the yield and accuracy of all three LLMs decreased proportionally as the degree of corruption in radiography report text increased. Importantly, we also demonstrated the superiority of the multimodal approach, in which image processors were combined with text input, compared to unimodal text-only models. This multimodal design not only improved performance but also showed cost-effectiveness relative to human interpretation.

While LLMs hold strong potential in healthcare research, there are rising concerns regarding their inappropriate behaviors when applied to critical decision-making or social determinants of health. For example, when an LLM was tasked with generating resuscitation guidelines, its semantic content was inaccurate and inconsistent, raising the risk of harmful effects in real-world use [22]. Similarly, large discrepancies have been reported between LLM-generated estimates of race and gender distributions and their actual prevalence, indicating that general-domain model training on existing clinical corpora fails to reflect true conditions and is biased by input data [23]. Beyond LLMs, deep neural networks have been shown to identify patients’ race from chest X-ray images, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear [23]. Despite differences in methodology, these studies converge on a critical point: the quality and adequacy of training data profoundly influence downstream model performance. Accordingly, clinical LLMs trained solely on text data may carry substantial risks. By contrast, multimodal foundation models that leverage both text and imaging data may help bridge this gap, as demonstrated in our findings.

LLMs are increasingly valued for their ability to capture highly semantic representations of complex statistical relationships across diverse healthcare data. However, their use comes with pitfalls, particularly when input data are corrupted, leading to degraded model performance [24]. Such corruption is difficult to avoid, especially in the setting of automated data de-identification [25]. Our dataset also exhibited inherent corruption, including a “frameshift” issue in which content was misplaced between report columns—for instance, impressions appearing in the findings field, findings replacing impressions, or impression fields left empty. To account for these issues, we applied incremental corruption at random data points, thereby directly testing the relationship between input data quality and LLM performance. Our results underscore the importance of rigorous quality control in maintaining model reliability when faced with incomplete data and highlight multimodal LLMs as a viable solution.

In radiology, fine-tuning of LLMs is generally understood to improve contextual understanding [26], while prompt engineering can further enhance performance. However, our findings indicate that blind fine-tuning does not guarantee improved outcomes when training data are heavily corrupted. Although fine-tuning can effectively embed clinical knowledge into deep learning models, its success critically depends on the quality of training data. In fact, we found that fine-tuning on corrupted data not only failed to mitigate degradation but could even reduce performance when the model was later exposed to uncorrupted data. These results suggest that developing robust models requires ensuring data quality alongside fine-tuning. This interpretation aligns with recent work, such as Flamingo-CXR [27], which demonstrated higher absolute performance on MIMIC-CXR, likely due to larger model size and task-specific fine-tuning. In contrast, our study specifically emphasized robustness under corrupted input. In clinical practice, employing multimodal LLMs and systematically evaluating robustness through statistical and semantic metrics may enhance the overall credibility and reliability of radiographic report generation workflows.

Comparison between multimodal and unimodal LLMs indicates that multimodal approaches substantially improve performance, particularly under conditions of data corruption. When applied without additional clinical training, multimodal LLMs require few-shot learning, which provides the model with relevant examples. Unlike text-only models, however, few multimodal LLMs currently support few-shot learning. Many state-of-the-art models, such as BLIP-2 [28] and LLaVA [29], are limited to handling multiple text pairs with a single image. These constraints explain why we restricted our experiments to Flamingo variants. For robust performance under corrupted data conditions, future multimodal LLMs should be designed to process multiple image–text pairs, integrated seamlessly not only into backend report-generation pipelines but also into interactive interfaces at the radiologist’s workstation.

Our cost analysis underscored the efficiency of LLMs. Prior discussions of AI’s cost-effectiveness in radiology highlight that generating text with LLMs can be achieved for approximately $40.96 per GPU hour, markedly lower than the $115.00 per hour cost of a radiologist’s interpretation [30]. This suggests that multimodal approaches could provide both cost savings and time efficiency relative to human-generated impressions. Models such as OpenFlamingo and IDEFICS were often cheaper and faster than their unimodal versions, demonstrating that well-optimized multimodal systems may deliver greater clinical utility. Future work should explore their integration into real-time pipelines once quality assurance of LLM-based readings is firmly established.

The safety of LLMs in clinical use remains a pressing concern. Data poisoning and misinformation have the potential to drastically impair model performance. Additional risks include prompt injection during instruction learning and backdoor attacks, both of which currently lack effective countermeasures. Our findings showed that text-only models are particularly vulnerable to corrupted input, while multimodal models demonstrated greater resilience, thereby partially addressing these safety challenges. Although definitive solutions to LLM safety concerns are still lacking, the research community must remain vigilant in recognizing these vulnerabilities and developing mitigation strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, our data were derived from a single publicly available source, which may limit the generalizability of performance trajectories across different environments. Second, our study did not include some of the most efficient and widely recognized general models, such as GPT-4, LLaMA 3, or Med-PaLM 2. Since the Flamingo architecture is mechanistically similar to GPT-3, we assumed that semantic construction would not differ substantially. Nevertheless, the use of GPT-4 and Med-PaLM 2 was restricted in our academic setting due to limited API access. Importantly, MedFlamingo—used in this study—was the state-of-the-art medical LLM for in-context learning as of September 2023 [13], surpassing all other available models, making it the most clinically relevant choice. Third, our multimodal approach was limited to images, specifically chest radiographs. The generalizability of our findings to other imaging modalities (e.g., computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging) or broader clinical domains remains uncertain. Further validation in diverse settings is needed. Fourth, clinical data include multiple types beyond text and images, such as structured numeric data or unstructured physiologic waveforms; our results may not extend to these modalities. Nonetheless, our approach remains directly relevant to imaging interpretation tasks. Finally, as this was a pilot feasibility study, unforeseen ethical implications must be acknowledged. Despite our evaluation of hallucinations, the generative nature of LLMs may still produce unfounded content that could influence radiologic impressions and downstream clinical decisions.

In conclusion, the feasibility of LLM-based analytic solutions in real-world scenarios must be carefully evaluated, particularly when data are prone to varying degrees of noise and corruption. Our findings demonstrate that multimodal LLMs outperform unimodal (text-only) LLMs across both statistical and semantic metrics, even under degraded input conditions, while maintaining cost efficiency. Future research should extend this framework to additional imaging modalities, nontraditional data sources, and real-time sequential pipelines to further validate and expand clinical utility.
