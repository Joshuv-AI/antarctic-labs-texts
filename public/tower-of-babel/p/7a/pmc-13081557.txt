Impact of weekend admission on in-hospital mortality in chronic kidney disease: a nationwide analysis of 184 299 hospitalisations in Poland (2017–2021)
Authors: Agnieszka Mazurek, Mateusz Jankowski, Justyna Grudziąż-Sękowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe weekend effect refers to the observation that patients admitted to the hospital on the weekend experience poorer clinical outcomes than patients admitted during the workweek. This effect has not been previously studied among patients hospitalised for chronic kidney disease (CKD) in Poland. This study aimed to evaluate the association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality among patients hospitalised for CKD in Poland.MethodsThis was a retrospective study of nationwide administrative hospital discharge records of adult patients admitted with a primary diagnosis of CKD between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2021, in Poland. The association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality and the effect modification by admission mode were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression. Sensitivity analyses using alternative weekend definitions were conducted to test the robustness of the findings.ResultsIn total, 184 299 hospitalisations were analysed, of which 24 945 (13.5%) occurred on the weekend. Overall, weekend admission was found to be associated with higher adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality compared with weekday admission (aOR:1.501[1.419–1.587]; p < 0.001). This association differed by admission mode (p for interaction = 0.003), with higher adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality among emergency admissions (aOR:1.541[1.454–1.633]; p < 0.001), and no statistically significant association among scheduled admissions. The results of sensitivity analyses were largely consistent with the primary results.ConclusionsA significant association between weekend admission and increased odds of in-hospital mortality among patients hospitalised for CKD in Poland was observed. This association was statistically significant among emergency admissions but not scheduled admissions. These findings highlight the need to evaluate weekend hospital care pathways and further investigate the pre-hospital and in-hospital factors that may contribute to the observed disparities in outcomes in this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04885-2.Keywords: Emergency, Hospitalisation, Mortality, Renal, Weekend effect

Background

The weekend effect is the association between admission to the hospital on the weekend, as opposed to a weekday, and a higher risk of experiencing worse clinical outcomes due to disparities in care delivery [1, 2]. This association was formally described by Bell and Redelmeier 2001 following a study of emergency hospitalisations for over 100 different conditions in Canada [3]. Since then, the weekend effect has been described for a range of acute and chronic conditions across international healthcare settings [2, 4–7].

The causes underlying the weekend effect are thought to be multifaceted [8]. Proposed explanations include reduced hospital staffing levels and specialist availability, leading to limited access to diagnostic and time-sensitive procedures on weekends [8–10]. Notwithstanding, the existence of a true weekend effect is often contested, as many studies have found that the effect largely disappears after properly accounting for patient case severity, given that weekend admissions tend to comprise a sicker patient population [11–13].

In Poland, hospital services during off-hours (including weekends, public holidays, and nights) are considerably limited compared with regular working hours on weekdays. Staffing levels are often reduced to legally mandated minimums, with most personnel rotating through up to 24-hour shifts. Non-surgical wards are often covered by a single physician who is not required to be a specialist [14]. Consequently, essential life-saving interventions and supportive care are often prioritised over diagnostic and therapeutic procedures [15]. These operational constraints could potentially create disparities in outcomes among patients with conditions particularly susceptible to delays in care [16].

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is one such condition in which delays in care may lead to poorer outcomes. Although CKD is primarily managed in the outpatient setting, hospital care is often necessary. Common reasons for hospitalisation in CKD include acute decompensation, such as superimposed acute kidney injury, refractory electrolyte disturbances, and uraemia, as well as planned interventions, such as initiation of renal replacement therapy or other treatment modifications [17, 18]. Therefore, hospital admissions attributed to CKD generally represent episodes of clinical deterioration or escalation of care, as opposed to routine disease management.

CKD was one of the conditions included in the study by Bell and Redelmeier, where weekend admission was found to be associated with an 8% increase in adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality compared with weekday admission [3]. In Poland, the weekend effect has been investigated in several acute non-renal conditions [9, 19–21] with an association between weekend admission and higher mortality risk identified for ischemic stroke [20] and myocardial infarction [19]. However, there appear to be no studies evaluating the weekend effect in CKD among Polish patients. Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality among patients hospitalised for CKD in Poland.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective observational study based on data from hospitalisations occurring between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2021, in Poland. The data were obtained from the Nationwide General Hospital Morbidity Study (NGHMS).

The NGHMS is an ongoing initiative by the Department of Population Health Monitoring and Analysis at the National Institute of Public Health-National Institute of Hygiene (NIPH-NIH) to collect and analyse hospitalisation data derived from hospital discharge records [22]. The NGHMS maintains a participation rate of approximately 96%, making it a comprehensive source of information on hospitalisations in Poland [22]. All entities providing day-case and inpatient hospital care (except psychiatric care) are obliged to participate in the NGHMS. Notably, day cases involving dialysis therapy or chemotherapy services are excluded from reporting requirements.

The NGHMS collects data using a standardised reporting form (Form MZ/Szp-11). The section relating to clinical information is completed by the physician caring for the patient on the last day of hospitalisation in a given ward. Diagnoses are coded using the International Classification of Diseases (10th revision; ICD-10). The NGHMS contains data from up to five hospital wards for each hospitalisation.

Study population

The study population was adult patients (≥18 years old) hospitalised with a primary diagnosis of CKD, identified using ICD-10 codes N18.1, N18.2, N18.3, N18.4, N18.5 and N18.9. The primary diagnosis was the first diagnosis in the initial hospital ward. According to the NGHMS reporting standards, the first diagnosis in a given ward is the condition that the treating physician considered, on the basis of clinical assessment, to be the cause of admission to that ward. Therefore, for the purpose of this study, the primary diagnosis was treated as the reason for admission to the hospital, consistent with other studies utilizing NGHMS data [23, 24].

Records with missing sex (N = 1) or those with admission dates in the year 2016 (which appeared in the dataset due to discharge occurring in 2017 or later; N = 498) were excluded from analysis. Due to the NGHMS data being anonymised, each hospitalisation was treated as an independent record.

Variables and definitions

The outcome variable was in-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause during the hospitalisation.

The primary exposure variable was weekend admission, defined as an admission occurring on either Saturday or Sunday. Alternative definitions of weekend admission, including Saturday only, Sunday only, public holiday only, and any non-working day (Saturday, Sunday, and public holidays) were also evaluated in sensitivity analyses.

The covariates were age, sex, the presence of comorbidities, admission mode (emergency or scheduled), admission year, admission season (warm: April-September; cool: October-March; included to account for potential seasonal variation in mortality and case-mix), and hospital location. Scheduled admissions were defined as planned admissions with referral, whereas emergency admissions included all non-scheduled (urgent and emergency) admissions. The hospital location was determined according to the 16 administrative units of Poland (voivodeships).

Regarding comorbidities, the NGHMS provides up to five diagnosis codes per ward for up to five wards. Therefore, six binary variables were created to represent the presence (or absence) of at least one diagnosis code from the following ICD-10 chapters: neoplasms (C00-D49), diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs (D50-D89), endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases (E00-E89), and diseases of the circulatory (I00-I99), respiratory (J00-J99), and digestive (K00-K95) systems [25]. These conditions were selected based on a prior study identifying them as factors associated with in-hospital mortality in this cohort [26].

Length of stay (reported for descriptive statistics) was calculated as the difference in days between the dates of admission and discharge. Hence, hospitalisations with a reported length of 0 days may refer to day-case admission, same-day discharge, inter-hospital transfer, or death occurring on the day of admission.

Statistical analysis

The Python programming language, with the pandas library [27, 28] and statsmodels module [29], was used to conduct all statistical analyses.

All baseline characteristics of the study population were categorised and described using counts and percentages. The differences between weekend and weekday admission groups were assessed using the chi-square test of independence.

The association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression. Multivariable models were adjusted for the covariates outlined above. Due to suspected differences in care processes between emergency and scheduled admission modes, all primary multivariable logistic regression analyses were repeated with the inclusion of an interaction term between weekend admission and admission mode to assess effect modification.

The results of logistic regression were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Crude odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs from univariable models were also reported. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model fit was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the McFadden pseudo-R-squared. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) with 95% CIs (DeLong method [30]). Model calibration was assessed visually using calibration plots (Additional file 3: Figures S1 and S2). These metrics were reported for the primary multivariable models only.

Ethical approval

The Ethics Committee at the Centre of Postgraduate Medical Education (Warsaw, Poland) approved the study protocol (decision no. 401/2023; August 23, 2023). As the analysis was conducted using fully anonymised data from the NGHMS (administrative hospital discharge database), individual informed consent was not required.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 184 299 hospital admissions with a primary diagnosis of CKD were included in the analysis. Of those, 86.5% were weekday admissions and 13.5% were weekend admissions. The mean annual number of hospitalizations for CKD decreased by nearly 30% during 2020–2021 compared with 2017–2019 (Table 1). Both weekend and weekday admissions decreased proportionally during this period.

Characteristics of hospital admissions by weekend versus weekday admission

There were significant differences in several baseline characteristics between patients admitted on weekends and weekdays. Weekend admissions had a higher proportion of patients aged 70 years or older (47.6% vs. 45.1%; p < 0.001) (Table 1). Patients admitted on weekends were more often admitted as emergencies (64.8% vs. 55.1%; p < 0.001), had more ward transfers, and had longer lengths of stay (Table 1). In addition, weekend admissions had a lower prevalence of diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs, endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases, and circulatory diseases, and a higher prevalence of respiratory and digestive diseases (Table 1).

In-hospital mortality

The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 4.9% (Table 1). Patients admitted on weekends had a higher mortality rate than patients admitted on weekdays (7.7% vs. 4.5%; p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality

In univariable analysis, weekend admission was significantly associated with higher odds of in-hospital mortality (OR: 1.764, 95%CI: 1.674–1.859, p < 0.001) (Table 2). This association remained significant after adjusting for covariates (aOR: 1.501; 95%CI: 1.419–1.587; p < 0.001) (Table 2). Female sex (aOR: 1.095; 95%CI: 1.047–1.146; p < 0.001), age ≥ 70 years (aOR: 2.808; 95%CI: 2.670–2.954; p < 0.001), emergency admission (aOR: 4.946; 95%CI: 4.611–5.305; p < 0.001), admission in the cool months (aOR: 1.172; 95%CI: 1.121–1.226; p < 0.001), as well as the presence of neoplasms (aOR: 3.823; 95%CI: 3.556–4.110; p < 0.001), and diseases of the circulatory system (aOR: 1.794; 95%CI: 1.712–1.880; p < 0.001), respiratory system (aOR: 4.158; 95%CI: 3.905–4.428; p < 0.001), and digestive system (aOR: 1.827; 95%CI: 1.677–1.990, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with higher odds of in-hospital mortality (Table 2). The presence of diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs (aOR: 0.655; 95%CI: 0.616–0.697; p < 0.001) was significantly associated with lower odds of in-hospital mortality (Table 2).

Effect modification by admission mode

In multivariable analysis including an interaction term between weekend admission and admission mode, the association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality differed by admission mode (p for interaction = 0.003). Weekend admission was significantly associated with higher odds of in-hospital mortality among emergency admissions (aOR: 1.541; 95%CI: 1.454–1.633; p < 0.001), but not among scheduled admissions, for which no significant association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality was observed (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses

In sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of weekend admission, Saturday admission (aOR: 1.382; 95%CI: 1.286–1.486; p < 0.001), Sunday admission (aOR: 1.514; 95%CI: 1.403–1.634; p < 0.001), holiday admission (aOR: 1.299; 95%CI: 1.128–1.496; p < 0.001) and non-working day admission (aOR: 1.492; 95%CI: 1.414–1.575; p < 0.001) were all significantly associated with higher odds of in-hospital mortality (Additional file 1: Table S1).

In sensitivity analyses incorporating an interaction between weekend admission and admission mode, the interaction was significant for Sunday admission (p for interaction < 0.001), holiday admission (p for interaction = 0.010), and non-working day admission (p for interaction < 0.001), but not for Saturday admission (p for interaction = 0.961) (Additional file 2: Table S2). Emergency admissions had significantly higher odds of in-hospital mortality than scheduled admissions with admission on Sunday (aOR: 1.591; 95%CI: 1.469–1.723; p < 0.001), holiday (aOR: 1.390; 95%CI: 1.200-1.609; p < 0.001), or non-working day (aOR: 1.540; 95%CI: 1.455–1.629; p < 0.001) (Additional file 2: Table S2). No statistically significant association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality was observed among scheduled admissions (Additional file 2: Table S2).

Discussion

This study found a significant association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality among adult patients hospitalised with a primary diagnosis of CKD in Poland. This association differed significantly by admission mode, with a statistically significant association observed for emergency, but not for scheduled admissions. Collectively, these results identify variation in outcomes between patients admitted to the hospital for CKD in Poland on weekends versus weekdays, with evidence of effect modification by admission mode.

The present study examined hospitalisations in which CKD was the primary diagnosis in the initial hospital ward, indicating that CKD was considered the reason for admission to the hospital. These admissions may represent a subgroup of patients with acute decompensation or advanced disease, rather than all patients presenting to the hospital with CKD as a comorbidity. Therefore, the findings of the present study should be interpreted primarily in the context of hospitalisations attributable to CKD and not all hospitalisations of patients with CKD.

A marked reduction in the number of hospitalisations for CKD was observed during 2020–2021 compared with 2017–2019, coinciding with the changes in healthcare utilisation associated with the COVID-19 pandemic during that period [31]. Nevertheless, the observed decrease was proportional across weekend and weekday admissions, and admission year was included as a covariate in all multivariable logistic regression models to account for year-over-year variation in the number of admissions during the study period.

Overall, weekend admission was associated with 50.0% higher adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality compared with weekday admission. This finding aligns with those of many other studies reporting an increase in odds of short-term mortality for weekend compared with weekday admissions across a range of conditions [4, 5, 7]. In a study of patients with end-stage renal disease, the adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality were 8% higher for the weekend cohort [32]. Nonetheless, some studies have reported that the association between weekend admission and mortality is no longer evident after adjustment for case-mix [33].

In the present study, weekend admissions were characterised by an older patient population, higher prevalence of respiratory and gastrointestinal comorbidities, more ward transfers, and longer hospital stays. Nearly two-thirds of weekend admissions were emergency cases. Among patients with CKD, advanced age is generally associated with more advanced disease and greater comorbidity burden, and is known to increase the risk of disease exacerbation and subsequent hospitalisation [17]. Accordingly, the older age profile of weekend admissions may indicate greater disease severity at the time of admission. Higher inter-ward transfer rates and longer lengths of stay may also represent greater clinical complexity. Furthermore, emergency admissions are typically associated with increased case severity at presentation and poorer outcomes [7].

Notwithstanding, in the present study, weekend admission was associated with significantly higher odds of in-hospital mortality in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses. The persistence of this association following adjustment for age, the presence of comorbidities, and admission mode, which served as proxies for clinical severity in the absence of detailed clinical data, suggests that factors beyond differences in case-mix between weekend and weekday cohorts may contribute to the higher odds of in-hospital mortality associated with hospital admission for CKD occurring on the weekend.

There seem to be no studies directly comparing the risk of in-hospital mortality following weekend admission between emergency and scheduled admissions among patients hospitalised for CKD. In the present study, effect modification by admission mode was formally evaluated, and a statistically significant difference in the association between weekend admission and in-hospital mortality between emergency and scheduled admission modes was observed. Specifically, weekend admission was associated with 54.1% higher adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality among emergency admissions, whereas no significant association was observed among scheduled admissions. This finding raises the possibility that constraints in access to essential care on weekends may also be relevant to the higher odds of in-hospital mortality associated with weekend admission for CKD.

Weekend-related differences in the delivery of care may disproportionately affect time-sensitive emergency procedures. A study of hospitalisations for end-stage renal disease in the U.S. found that patients admitted on weekends experienced longer wait times to initiate renal replacement therapy and were overall less likely to receive it within 24 h of admission compared with patients admitted on weekdays [32]. Similarly, constraints in access to timely care on weekends have been cited as a potential contributor to the observed association between weekend admission and higher odds of in-hospital mortality among non-surgical patients in Poland [34].

Nonetheless, evidence suggests that emergency weekend admissions may comprise a population with greater baseline clinical severity than weekday admissions [12], despite these differences not being fully captured in administrative data. This phenomenon is sometimes attributed to patient self-selection [9, 35], as well as higher clinical admission thresholds on weekends [13, 34]. The present study found that admissions for CKD were not evenly distributed across all days of the week, with only 13.5% of all admissions, and 15.6% of emergency admissions, occurring on the weekend. This finding suggests that a case-mix-driven explanation for the higher adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality associated with weekend admission among emergency admissions for CKD cannot be ruled out.

The findings of this study may inform clinical decision-making, planning of hospital care during weekends, and future research on the weekend effect among patients hospitalised for CKD. Weekend admissions for CKD may more often involve clinically complex cases, which clinicians could anticipate and plan management accordingly. Patients admitted to the hospital for acute decompensation of CKD may be particularly vulnerable to differences in care between weekends and weekdays. Therefore, it may be beneficial to review pathways of emergency weekend admissions to attempt to identify potential causes of the apparent disparities in outcomes. Increasing staff coverage during weekends may be considered to ensure proper continuity of care. Future investigations should focus on pre-hospital factors that may contribute to greater disease severity at presentation among patients admitted on weekends.

Limitations

This was a retrospective observational study based on administrative data and ICD-10 codes, which may have resulted in misclassification, as CKD diagnoses could not be clinically verified. Furthermore, differentiating between CKD stages was not possible.

The study was restricted to hospitalisations in which CKD was the apparent reason for admission to the hospital, rather than a comorbidity present on admission. This restriction may have introduced selection bias by including a heterogeneous subgroup of CKD patients, presenting with either more severe CKD or with comorbidities for which coding practices favoured CKD as the primary diagnosis. In consequence, the findings of the present study may be of limited generalisability to hospitalisations in which CKD is an existing comorbidity but not the reason for admission.

Data on clinical characteristics, medications, and procedures were not available. Therefore, patient case severity could only be assessed using proxy measures, such as age, comorbidity count, and admission mode. Data on hospital staffing levels and processes were not available. As a result, it was not possible to conclude whether the discrepancies in outcomes between weekend and weekday admissions were due to differences in case-mix, care delivery, or both.

The analysed outcome was all-cause in-hospital mortality rather than CKD-specific mortality. Therefore, the findings of this study should not be interpreted as representing in-hospital mortality directly caused by CKD.

Due to data anonymisation, repeat admissions of the same patients could not be tracked. Given that recurrent hospitalisations are common among patients with CKD, especially in advanced stages when CKD itself may be the reason for admission, multiple hospitalisations from the same patient may have been treated as independent observations. As a result, the assumption of independence of observations in the logistic regression analysis may not have been met, which could have led to an underestimation of the standard error and an overestimation of statistical significance.

Finally, the cause for the absence of a statistically significant interaction between Saturday admission and admission mode in the sensitivity analysis could not be determined based on the available data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, admission to the hospital on the weekend, as opposed to a weekday, was associated with higher odds of in-hospital mortality among adult hospitalisations with a primary diagnosis of CKD. This association appeared to vary by admission mode, as a statistically significant association was observed among emergency admissions, but not among scheduled admissions. While these findings support the possibility of a weekend effect among hospitalisations for CKD, it is unclear whether the observed association is attributable to differences in care processes or differences in case-mix between weekend and weekday admissions. These findings may be relevant to risk stratification and planning of care for patients admitted for CKD on weekends. Future studies should incorporate clinical and operational data to better understand the factors that might contribute to the weekend effect in this patient population.
