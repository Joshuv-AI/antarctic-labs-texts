Development of national post-fire restoration system to assess net GHG impacts and salvage biomass availability
Authors: C Smyth, M Fellows, S Morken, M Magnan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn light of the recent unprecedented wildfires in Canada and the potential for increasing burned areas in the future, there is a need to explore post-fire salvage harvest and restoration and the implications for greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Salvage logging and replanting initiatives offer a potential solution by regrowing forests more quickly while meeting societal demands for wood and bioenergy.This study presents a comprehensive modeling framework to estimate post-fire salvage biomass and net GHG emissions relative to a ‘do-nothing’ baseline for all of Canada's harvest-eligible forests. Forest ecosystem carbon emissions and removals were modeled at 1-ha spatial resolution for Canadian forests using the Generic Carbon Budget Model (GCBM) from 1990 to 2070 using several forest inventory data sources with future harvest and wildfires.Building upon previous research, our work integrated the Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System fire intensity to estimate fire severity of future wildfires. For 2024 to 2070, we assessed the changes in ecosystem carbon, emissions from harvested wood products, and substitution benefits from avoided emissions-intensive materials, relative to a forward-looking baseline. Our prototype system provides a comprehensive framework, configuration files, links to datasets to quantify the net GHG of post-fire restoration, and sample results for validation.•Developed spatially explicit forest carbon modeling system for all of Canada's forests.•Assessed the net GHG reduction from post-fire restoration.•Used system approach to consider forests, wood products and substitution benefits.Keywords: Wildfire, Climate change mitigation, Gcbm, Spatially explicit carbon budget modelMethod name: Spatially explicit carbon modeling of future post-fire restoration in Canada

Background

The motivation for this study is to examine the net GHG impacts of post-fire restoration in Canada. We provide information on a prototype spatially explicit forest carbon dynamics system for all of Canada's forests that includes future wildfire projections and future harvest in eligible areas. We build on previous analyses that considered salvage harvest after wildfires in one region of Canada [[1], [2], [3]]. We also improve on previous analyses by better representing future wildfiref severity through the integration of a fire behavior prediction system. We explain how the methodology can be used through a sample simulation of one potential wildfire future and provide information on all open source datasets and models that were used, along with code repository information.

Method details

Forest carbon dynamics were simulated using the Generic Carbon Budget Model (GCBM), which is an open-source, spatially explicit carbon dynamics model that builds on the Carbon Budget Model of the Canadian Forest Sector (CBM-CFS3) [4]. GCBM is built on the open-source Full Lands Integration Tool (FLINT) that was jointly developed by experts from Australia and the Canadian Forest Service and is distributed through the moja.global organization (http://moja.global).

Forest ecosystem spatial layers

Spatially explicit forest age and leading species information was compiled from four sources into two separate composite inventories.

In the first composite inventory, hereafter Inv 1, stand age and leading species information was from provincial and territorial agencies that has been standardized and distributed via the Common Attribute Schema for Forest Resource Inventory (CASFRI) [5]. Extracted information from CASFRI included the year of stand origin which was converted to age, and leading species from the highest (overstory) forest layer attribute. Disturbance information is included in CASFRI but was found to be incomplete for some datasets and was not used. Additional information on processing is provided in Appendix A. This dataset is not spatially complete, and remotely sensed information was used to gap-fill stand age [6] and leading species [7]. All data sources are listed in Appendix C.

The second composite inventory, hereafter Inv 2, used high-resolution remotely sensed information from the National Terrestrial Ecosystem Monitoring System (NTEMS) for Canada for leading species and stand age [7,8], with missing information for stand ages greater than 150 years old gap-filled with nearest neighbor hybrid photo plot and remotely sensed age information [9].

The two composite inventories had different areas, and an additional attribute was included in the simulations to enable comparisons for all areas and overlapping areas only.

Historical wildfires

Information from burned areas from 1950 from 1989 was taken from the Canadian National Fire Database (CNFDB). This is a collection of forest fire data from various sources and includes fire perimeters (polygon data) as provided by Canadian fire management agencies. In addition to the historical wildfires from 1950 to 1989, burned areas with three wildfire severity classes (low, moderate, and high) for 1990 to 2020 were taken from the Canada Landsat Burned Severity product [14]. This product is a geospatial database of burn severity for wildland fires across forested regions of Canada at 30 m resolution. It is based on Landsat imagery, using values of pre-fire to post-fire differences in normalized burn ratios (dNBRs) for nearly 60 Mha of area burned across Canada's forests. Fire perimeters based on hotspot data were used for 2021 to 2023, which were compiled by the Canadian Wildland Fire Information System. A hotspot is a satellite image pixel with high infrared intensity, indicating a heat source, and is available daily, in advance of the burned area polygons or dNBR estimates. Hotspots from known industrial sources are removed, and the remaining hotspots represent vegetation fires, which we have overlayed with forests. Not all fires can be identified from satellite imagery, either because the fires are too small or because cloud cover obscures the satellite's view of the ground. Multiple data sources are used for hotspots: Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) imagery, Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) imagery and Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) imagery. It is anticipated that burned areas from 2021 to 2023 will be updated in the future.

Historical pests

Historical insect infestations were included in the Province of British Columbia for mountain pine beetle, balsam bark beetle, spruce beetle, and Douglas-fir beetle with three to four severity classes for each. These data polygons are based on annual provincial aerial surveys by the Government of British Columbia. Each polygon is labelled by the year of survey, forest health factor (disturbance agent), severity class and host.

Historical clearcuts

Harvest cutblocks were based on available provincial datasets in British Columbia and Quebec, and from remote sensing. For British Columbia, the consolidated cut blocks dataset spatially combines forest harvesting data from multiple datasets (Reporting Silviculture Updates and Land Status Tracking System, Forest Cover Inventory and Landsat satellite change detection). For Quebec, the harvest map is produced as part of the ecoforestry inventory of Quebec by the Ministère des Ressources naturelles et des Forêts, private forest development agencies and major landowners. For the remaining areas, Landsat-derived annual forest change data were from a national terrestrial ecosystem monitoring system for Canada, and were characterized from stand replacing disturbance types including harvest, and with lower confidence wildfire and harvest [15].For clearcut harvest, 85 % of the carbon in merchantable-sized trees was transferred to wood products (utilization rate), except for Alberta (94 %) and Quebec (97 %), with the remainder assumed to decay on site. Slashpile burning does occur in some regions, but there is no available spatial information, and it was not simulated.

Adjusting inventory age to common baseline year

Inventory age was adjusted to a 1990 baseline year using a spatially explicit rollback tool [16], which combines spatial information from a forest inventory for a post-1990 inventory year with data on disturbances and then rolls back the inventory from its reference year to 1990. The rollback tool adjusts the inventory age, spatial disturbances, regeneration delays, and historical stand-establishment from the original vintage year to 1990 as described by the procedures below.

For older stands, the stand age was reduced by the difference between the inventory vintage and 1990.

For young stands (established post-1990), a corresponding stand-replacing disturbance is required. If the stand-establishment year inferred from the age was inconsistent with disturbance information, the disturbance information was assumed to be accurate, and the stand age was adjusted accordingly. Any missing stand-replacing disturbances were set to harvest or wildfire, and the pre-disturbance age was determined based on the timing of the previous burn or clearcut.

Missing stand-replacing disturbances were assumed to be wildfire before 1950, and clearcut harvest after 1950 with exceptions for northern regions where missing disturbances were assumed to be wildfires because industrial-scale harvesting was limited. The pre-disturbance age for these gap-filled disturbances was set to a single value for each region: 60 years for wildfire and harvest age was set by RU and ranged from 55 to 205 (average 92) based on the median BGI age of stands harvested between 2010 and 2015. Typically, missing information uses a random draw from an age-class distribution, but not in our study because random selection would result in differences in the forest ecosystem carbon balance between the baseline and restoration scenarios and impact our net GHG results.

Future harvest eligibility

Land management categories were used to identify areas eligible for future salvage or clearcut harvest, and were based on nine classes categorized according to ownership, protection status and tenure that were compiled from federal, provincial and territorial geospatial datasets [17]. We assumed that eligible areas for harvest included tenured (short-term or long-term), treaty, and private lands, and ineligible areas included protected, federal reserves, Indian reserves, restricted, and other lands (Fig. 2). Forests in other lands may have some harvesting but not at an industrial scale, and forestry operations in these lands currently place relatively small volumes of timber into the supply chain.1 However, in Labrador, other lands were considered eligible for clearcut harvest in order to achieve annual allowable cut (AAC) harvest volumes.

Salvage harvest mask based on (a) ownership and other options which were examined but not selected were (b) buffered paved roads, or (c) buffered harvest areas.

For salvage harvest, a previous analysis [1] considered salvage harvest suitability based on proximity to roads, site index and forest type. We considered using road proximity in our analysis but could not find an unpaved road layer for Canada and found that proximity to paved roads or previous harvest locations resulted in very different eligible areas and the potential for salvage harvest in remote regions (Fig. 2).

Restoration after wildfire

In the restoration scenario, salvage harvest and replanting, or underplanting in young stands, was simulated after moderate or severe wildfires in suitable areas. Suitability considered pre-burn forest type and harvest eligibility, and restoration activities were limited to softwood-leading stands in harvest-eligible areas. Restoration included post-fire salvage harvest and replanting if the pre-disturbance stand age was greater than 40 years, and underplanting without salvage harvest in younger stands.

Future clearcut level and forest management units

Future clearcut harvest was based on annual allowable cut (AAC) levels for each forest management unit (Fig. 3), for forests eligible for harvest. AAC and forest management units were gathered from online sources (Table 3). Where no AAC information was available for an FMU, contemporary harvest volumes were assumed to be constant in the future. Because AAC is consistently higher than actual harvest in some regions, future harvest levels in Quebec and Ontario were adjusted downward based on contemporary ratios of AAC to actual harvest from 2010 to 2020. For Alberta, a coarser resolution Land-use Framework Planning Regions dataset was used because some of the AAC information was unavailable for some FMUs.

Future clearcut harvest schedule

Future harvest cutblocks were based on the allowable harvest volumes (described above) and a spatial harvest scheduling tool. The clearcut harvest cutblocks from 2021 to 2070 were developed using a spatially explicit forest planning model called the Forest Management Tool2 (FMT). FMT is an open-source forest management library that provides a generic approach to forest planning.

Forest planning inputs included projected annual harvest volumes, yield tables, inventory information (age, leading species) and inventory age. A simplified inventory was provided to the FMT to speed up processing: stand age was binned into 10-year age classes, and spatial information was resampled to 0.005° bins using GDAL nearest neighbor routine.

The inventory age in 2024 was selected for use because of the unprecedented levels of wildfires in 2023 that affected roughly 5 % of Canada's forests. Optimization was based on a MaxMin objective with even flow harvest volume, and an assumed lifespan of 500 years. Constraints were applied to restrict harvest to eligible areas and a 40-year minimum stand age.

Harvested wood product emissions and substitution benefits

Carbon transferred from forest ecosystems to products and bioenergy was tracked through manufacturing, export, use, and post-consumer treatment using the same model as the National Forest Carbon Monitoring, Accounting and Reporting System for Harvested Wood Products (NFCMARS-HWP) run using an Abstract Network Simulation Engine. Versions of these models have been described in earlier analyses [[18], [19], [20]], and only a brief description is provided here, with parameters listed in Appendix C.

Emissions are the result of harvesting in Canada and are estimated using the simple decay approach. The model has 15 flow networks for 10 provinces and two territories in Canada, two other countries (United States and Japan), and an additional country network that combines all other countries importing Canadian wood products. Flow parameters were assumed to be constant for the future, with most roundwood processed domestically and converted into commodities (sawnwood, panels, pulp and paper, and a small amount for other industrial roundwood, manufactured logs and pellets) with the remaining portion as mill residues. Default lifetimes were assumed for commodities: sawnwood and other industrial roundwood had a 35-year half-life, panels had a 25-year half-life, and pulp and paper had a 2-year half-life. Post-consumer commodities were sent to landfills, incinerated, or used for energy. Within anaerobic landfills, a portion of solid wood and paper products was assumed degradable with half carbon dioxide and half methane emissions, and some of the methane was captured and flared and/or used for energy.

Substitution benefits between solid wood products and alternative materials considered fossil emissions associated with extraction, transportation of raw materials, and manufacturing, and were previously estimated for Canada assuming a series of end-use products (e.g. single-family homes, furniture, etc.) weighted by consumption, and emissions from alternative non-wood end-use products [21]. Substitution benefits assumed a broad range of end-use products and a range of non-wood alternatives, and displacement factors were 0.54 tC emissions avoided per tC of sawnwood used, and 0.45 tC emissions avoided per tC of panels used. No substitution factors were applied to pulp, paper, manufactured logs, pellets or other industrial roundwood, and bioenergy from mill residues was assumed to be used for wood product manufacturing.

Future wildfires zones

Homogenous fire regime zones (HFRZ) and associated changes with future climate were taken from Boulanger et al. [22] who delineated 16 zones. A fire regime is a set of fire-related characteristics of a geographic region connected to a set of fire events that are relatively homogenous in time and space, and zonation is a useful generalization that aids in understanding fire dynamics at large spatial scales. Projections were not available for the Western Subarctic region, and we assumed values for the Eastern Subarctic region based on analyses by Erni et al. [23].

Future spatial wildfires from statistical fits

Using methods by Metsaranta et al. [1], we developed spatial fire layers for a Monte Carlo simulation-based assessment. Projected wildfires for 2024 to 2070 were derived from statistical fits with an assumed increase in burned area.

Lognormal statistics were fit to the historical area burned and number of fires (1950–2020) for each HFRZ. A small percentage (0.25 %) of the observed area burned was outside the HFRZ and was not considered for future fire projections: cumulative area burned in the NFDB/NBAC datasets was 121.70 Mha, and was reduced to 121.39 after the cumulative area burned was joined with HFRZ. For each zone, we estimated the annual area burned (AAB), the maximum AAB, and the cumulative area burned. Log-normal distributions were fit to AAB and fire size and the mean and standard deviation on a logarithmic scale were derived for each zone.

Post-fire recovery for future wildfires

Post-fire recovery for natural regeneration was based on White et al. [24] and their estimates of the return of forests following wildfires between 1986 and 2012. We assumed the natural regeneration delay applied to all future high-severity wildfires within GCBM. Average values were estimated for each RU (Table 5), with substitutions from nearby areas for regions with low burned areas (< 5 kha per RU), and rounded values were applied using transition rules after severe wildfires in GCBM. Restored stands, whether through underplanting for young stands or salvage harvesting and replanting stands, were assumed to have no regeneration delay.

Future fire severity

Future fire severity was determined based on fire intensity estimated using the Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System (CFFDRS) with assumed summer fire weather conditions. CFFDRS rates the risk of forest fires in Canada and produces estimates of fire potential, which are used as guides in a wide variety of fire management activities. Although this system is predominantly used for fire-fighting purposes, we used it to categorize fires into low-intensity surface fires, partial crown fires, or full crown fires. It has two subsystems: the Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index (FWI) System; and the Canadian Forest Fire Behavior Prediction (FBP) System.

The Canadian Fire Weather Index (FWI) is a fire danger rating system that assesses the potential behavior of a fire and includes six components: the Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC), the Duff Moisture Code (DMC), the Drought Code (DC), the Initial Spread Index (ISI), the Buildup Index (BUI), and the Fire Weather Index (FWI). Each component represents different aspects of fire behavior and is used to assess the overall fire danger. The three moisture code values rise as the moisture content decreases: FFMC considers litter and other fine fuels; DMC is for loosely compacted organic layers including medium-size woody material of moderate depth; and DC represents deep, compact organic layers including large logs. The Buildup Index (BUI) is a numeric rating of the total amount of fuel available for combustion and is based on the DMC and the DC.

For fire danger ratings, the FWI is typically applied to daily weather, but the GCBM operates on an annual timestep. We assumed fire-conducive weather conditions and used the 95th percentile of FWI for the summer months (June, July and August) as estimated from available data daily layers from 1981 to 2021 (Fig. 6).

Build Up Index (BUI) and Initial Spread Index (ISI) estimated from 95th percentiles of daily values from 1981 to 2021 for summer months.

Fire intensity was estimated using the Fire Behavior Prediction System, which includes 16 major fuel types and accounts for the influences of topography and location. Fuel beds (Appendix B) were assigned at the national level based on an existing rule set (canopy cover, leading species, region) [25] which was modified to use GCBM-available attributes age and leading species within each RU.

Wildfire severity was determined from the crown fraction burned: low severity for CFB ≤ 0.1, moderate severity for 0.9 < CFB > 0.1, and high severity for CFB ≥ 0.9. CFB was based on the rate of spread (ROS) exceeding a critical spread rate for crowning and assumed Canopy Base Height (CBH) (m) with values of 2, 3, 8, 4, 18, 7, 7, and 6 m for fuel beds C1 to C7 and M, respectively [26].

Equations for FWI and FBP systems were coded within GCBM to assign wildfire severity during run-time, and output values were validated through comparisons to published test cases [27].

In addition to mortality, wildfire combustion emissions were modeled in GCBM also using a disturbance matrix, which defines how C is transferred from one pool to other pools or to the atmosphere due to a disturbance [4]. Matrix coefficients for each fire severity class and ecozone define how wildfires emit C from live and dead pools to the atmosphere and an example is given in [2: Supplementary material, Fig. 1.]

Limitations

This prototype system is capable of modeling forest ecosystem carbon emissions and removals for all of Canada's forests. However, additional refinements are needed to move beyond the validation results presented in this study. The available stand age and stand-replacing disturbance information are composite layers from multiple data sources, and there are discrepancies between the establishment date (inferred from stand age) and stand-replacing disturbances for some young stands. We resolved these discrepancies by assuming the disturbance information was accurate and adjusted the stand age to match, and also added stand-replacing events for any missing establishment disturbances. However, these inconsistencies could be investigated and corrected in the various data layers.

Carbon dynamics are based on stand growth (removals), turnover and decay (emissions), natural disturbances and harvesting (emissions), and regrowth after disturbances (removals). The net ecosystem productivity is the small difference between large fluxes of opposite sign [13] and errors in either of these terms can result in a large error in the magnitude and sign of the carbon flux. Better yield information with appropriate merchantability limits is needed to accurately simulate primary productivity [29]. In addition, updated soil decay parameters which are calibrated to carbon stock plot data [30,31] could be used, along with improvements in combustion rates from stand-replacing wildfires that impact carbon stock spin-up [4]. Peatland dynamics have not been considered in this analysis but have been modeled at the national scale [32] using the same carbon budget model and could be included.

Sensitivity and uncertainty analyses have been assessed previously for growth and decay coefficients [18,33] and uncertainty analyses at the national level have considered parameter uncertainty as well as uncertainty in disturbance impacts [28]. These analyses have not yet been integrated into our spatially explicit climate change mitigation analyses, which are focused on specific activities and implementation levels [34], but such analyses would strengthen our understanding.

The climate change mitigation activity in this study considered post-fire salvage and regrowth of planted stands. We assumed a regeneration delay would occur after all future stand-replacing wildfires, but this assumption could be refined to consider a proportion of stands that would experience a natural regeneration delay after a wildfire (e.g. 25 percent of burned areas [20]). In addition, regeneration failure could be included in areas that experience multiple stand-replacing fires in a short period of time, or a transition from conifer-dominated landscapes to broadleaf deciduous species [35] could be included. We could consider additional factors related to restoration costs such as proximity to roads [1] or proximity to mills or communities [3]. Growth of planted stands associated with restoration activities does not consider adaptive planting (climatically suitable, genetically selected seedlings [2]), nor have we considered reduced growth due to the environmental impacts on the soil of both burning and harvesting activities. Additional salvage harvest constraints could be included to consider soil erosion and compaction that are based on slope, soil texture and/or post-fire nutrient levels.

Finally, low-severity and medium-severity wildfire impacts assumed simple ecozone-level combustion and mortality proportions based on existing stand-replacing wildfire impacts. However, these impacts could be improved based on information from field studies or other modeling efforts. Future fires had a lower proportion of severe wildfires relative to contemporary conditions, and further refinements could be needed to fuel bed assignments and burned area estimates.

Wildfire Fuel Beds

The Canadian Forest Fire Behavior Prediction (FBP) System provides quantitative estimates of potential head fire spread rate, fuel consumption, and fire intensity, as well as fire descriptions.

The FBP System takes the relative indices of the FWI System and converts them to stand-specific, physically recognizable and interpretable predictions of fire behavior. The system includes 16 major fuel types and accounts for influences of topography, location, and time of year on fire behavior. Descriptions of the fuel beds are available at FBP Fuel Type Descriptions (https://cwfis.cfs.nrcan.gc.ca/background/fueltypes/c1).
