Alcohol consumers’ receptivity to artificial intelligence–generated alcohol–cancer risk messages: An experimental study
Authors: Taghrid Asfar, Eric Christopher Brown, Frank J. Penedo, Ian Abrams, Jayanthi Jayakumaran, Olusanya Joshua Oluwole, Tarana Ferdous, Timothy Naimi, Daniela Avendano, Wasim Maziak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlcohol is a group 1 carcinogen linked to seven cancers, yet awareness of this risk remains low in the United State. Identifying effective alcohol–cancer communication strategies is a public health priority. The objective of this study was to test the effects of message specificity (general vs. cancer‐specific) and visual intensity (text‐only, neutral pictorial, graphic pictorial) on message receptivity (attention, emotional reactions) and precursors of behavior (harm perception; intentions to reduce, limit, or stop drinking) among moderate and heavy alcohol consumers.MethodsEight evidence‐based text messages were created across two topics: general and cancer‐specific harm. Artificial intelligence was used to generate pictorial versions at two intensities: neutral (symbolic, no visible disease) and graphic (explicit health consequences). In a 2025 online within‐subject/between‐subject crossover experiment, 639 US adult consumers (aged 21 years and older; 49.3% female) each viewed two randomly selected messages (one general, one cancer‐specific) presented in three formats (text‐only, neutral, and graphic; for six total formats), with counterbalanced order. Linear mixed‐effects models were used to estimate differences by intensity, specificity, and drinking levels, reporting regression coefficients (β, 95% confidence intervals).ResultsCancer‐specific messages produced greater attention, emotional reactions, and intentions to reduce drinking than general messages (β = 0.10–0.19; p < .05). Graphic pictorials outperformed neutral images on emotional and behavioral outcomes (β = 0.19–0.22; p < .05). Moderate consumers showed stronger perceived harm and message responsiveness than heavy consumers (β = 0.29–0.77; p < .05).ConclusionsArtificial intelligence–generated alcohol–cancer messages are feasible and effective in strengthening precursors to behavior change. Cancer‐specific content and higher visual intensity enhance impact, particularly among moderate consumers, highlighting the importance of tailoring alcohol–cancer communication strategies to different audience characteristics.

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol is the third leading cause of cancer after tobacco and excess body weight, with risk increasing as consumption rises. 1 Classified as a group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer since 1987, alcohol is causally linked to seven cancers: mouth, throat, larynx, esophageal, liver, colorectal, and breast. 2 In the United States, alcohol contributes to approximately 75,000 cancer cases and 19,000 deaths (3.2%) annually, 3 yet public awareness remains low, with only 37.1% of adults recognizing this risk. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 In response, the 2025 Surgeon General’s Advisory calls for updating alcohol warning labels to include cancer risks and making them more visible and effective in raising awareness. 9

Low public awareness of alcohol’s link to cancer highlights a key prevention gap and the need for effective risk communication. 10 Evidence from tobacco control demonstrates that health warning messages can drive population‐level behavior change, 11 , 12 , 13 and emerging research on alcohol–cancer messaging is promising. Nearly 40% of European adult consumers reported considering reducing intake after exposure to such messages, 14 and reviews indicate that warnings improve knowledge, awareness, and sometimes drinking behavior, particularly among women and high‐risk consumers. 15 Two Canadian quasi‐experimental studies demonstrate that alcohol–cancer warnings increase self‐reported reductions in consumption (adjusted odds ratio, approximately 2.4–3.7). 16 , 17 Raising awareness can aid physician counseling, especially for high‐risk groups like cancer survivors, 18 stimulate community discussions, and build support for policies reducing alcohol‐related harm. 19

Communicating alcohol–cancer risk presents several challenges. Misconceptions persist that moderate drinking is safe or beneficial, despite growing evidence to the contrary. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 In addition, unlike tobacco, 25 , 26 , 27 alcohol’s social acceptability and low risk awareness may limit the effectiveness of highly graphic warnings, which can provoke reactance rather than behavior change. 25 , 28 For example, studies have demonstrated mixed responses to alcohol–cancer messaging: in Australia, messages increased fear and negative emotions 26 ; in the United Kingdom, they raised risk perceptions but also triggered reactance 27 ; and, in Switzerland, they had little effect, largely because of beliefs about alcohol’s benefits and social norms. 29 Also, although general cancer messages (e.g., alcohol increases your risk) appeal to everyone, those at higher risk may respond better to cancer‐specific messages that mention types like breast cancer. 15 , 30 Message specificity further influences message effectiveness, with evidence indicating that naming specific cancer types (e.g., colon cancer) is more effective than using general cancer terms. 26 These complexities highlight the importance of testing variations in message design, including graphic intensity and specificity.

For the current study, we examined different approaches to alcohol–cancer risk communication in a national sample of US adult alcohol consumers. Message varied by: (1) message intensity, comparing text‐only messages with pictorial messages that featured neutral imagery (symbolic, graphs, charts) or graphic imagery (realistic images of diseased organs and human suffering), and (2) message specificity, comparing general cancer messages (i.e., noncancer type–specific) with cancer‐specific messages (i.e., identifying a specific cancer type). Guided by the Impact Message Framework, 12 primary outcomes included message receptivity (attention, reaction) and changes in precursors to behavior (harm perception; intentions to reduce, limit, or stop drinking). Considering our primary outcomes, we hypothesized that: (1) pictorial messages would outperform text‐only messages, (2) graphic messages would outperform neutral messages, (3) cancer‐specific messages would outperform general cancer messages, and (4) moderate consumers would be more receptive than heavy consumers. The findings inform campaigns, warning labels, and patient–provider counseling.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study protocol received approval from the University of Miami Institutional Review Board and included two phases, as described below.

Phase 1: Message development

Step 1: Developing the text messages

Initially, researchers developed evidence‐based texts from a literature review of scientific public health references on alcohol‐cancer risk by major agencies like the national and international health agencies, such as the International Agency for Research on Cancer and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC 2 , 7 , 31 ; e.g., heavy alcohol consumption doubles the risk of colon cancer). 32 Guided by established health communication literature, these messages were grouped into two specificity levels (general cancer and cancer‐specific). 27 , 30 General cancer risk messages focused on the link between alcohol and seven types of cancer, the benefits of reducing alcohol consumption according to the CDC guidelines, the correlation between consumption levels and cancer risk, and US statistics on alcohol–cancer risk awareness. Cancer‐specific messages focused on throat, breast, liver, and colon cancers. Mouth, esophageal, and laryngeal cancers were combined under throat, and rectal cancer was grouped with colon cancers, consistent with common public health communication practices. 33 , 34 For each message, we created three variants that convey the same core evidence‐based information with slight wording and structural differences. One message was then selected for clarity and accuracy. The final selected messages served as the standardized text stimulus for step 2 of message development and were tested in the online experiment study (Figure 1; messages tested in the online experiment).

Message stimuli tested in the online experiment are illustrated across two domains: (1) message specificity (general cancer vs. cancer‐specific) and (2) message intensity (text‐only vs. neutral pictorial vs. graphic pictorial). The finalized text‐only messages were developed in step 1, and the artificial intelligence–generated pictorial versions were created in step 2. Each message was produced in three formats—text‐only, neutral pictorial, and graphic pictorial—yielding 24 total messages. Neutral images were symbolic and noninjurious, whereas graphic images depicted visible alcohol‐related disease (e.g., cancer damage). Messages were categorized as general cancer or cancer‐specific. General messages addressed four themes: (1) alcohol′s link to seven cancers, (2) the benefits of reducing alcohol intake (according to Centers of Disease Control and Prevention guidelines), (3) the dose–response relationship, (4) and US awareness statistics. Cancer‐specific messages focused on four leading sites: throat, breast, liver, and colon. M1 through M8 indicates 1 through 8, respectively.

Step 2: Generating pictorial message intensities using artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (AI) tools were used to generate two pictorials for each text message, corresponding to two levels of intensity: neutral and graphic. A global social marketing firm led development using Adobe Firefly and Copilot for image generation and Adobe Photoshop for refinement. 35 , 36 Guided by a chain‐of‐thought framework, images were created through sequential steps: defining behavioral objectives and target audience (US adults aged 21 years and older who consume alcohol), integrating evidence‐based alcohol–cancer content from finalized text messages, applying neutral or graphic visual descriptions, conducting cross‐platform testing, and iterative human review by the research study team to ensure quality and consistency. 37 , 38 Prompts required clear communication of the causal alcohol–cancer link, systematic variation in intensity and specificity, and suitability for public health contexts while maximizing clarity and emotional engagement without stigmatizing or misleading content. 39 Neutral images were symbolic or illustrative and did not depict visible harm, whereas graphic images explicitly portrayed alcohol‐related disease outcomes (e.g., visible cancer damage). Thus both conditions included text and images but differed in the explicitness of health consequences. This process yielded eight message topics, each presented in three formats: text‐only, text with a neutral pictorial, and text with a graphic pictorial, for a total of 24 experimental messages (Figure 1).

Phase 2: Testing the AI‐generated messages in an online experiment

Study design

In March 2025, we conducted an online, within‐subjects/between‐subjects crossover experiment to examine the comparative effectiveness of AI‐generated pictorial messages versus text‐only messages. We recruited a convenience national panel‐based sample of US adults (aged 21 years and older), who were current alcohol consumers (one or more drink[s] in the past 30 days), 40 and were fluent in English (n = 639; 49.3% women; 49.3% moderate users; 50.7% heavy users). Panel‐based sampling has been widely applied in health communication research to efficiently recruit large national samples. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 41 , 42 Within‐subject factors included message intensity (text‐only, neutral pictorial, graphic pictorial messages) and message specificity (general vs. cancer‐specific messages). The between‐subjects factor was drinking level (moderate vs. heavy), defined according to CDC guidelines: moderate (one drink or less per day for women or two drinks or less per day for men) and heavy (greater than seven 7 drinks per week for women and greater than 14 drinks per week for men). 43 , 44 Participants were assigned to two groups (moderate, heavy) at enrollment to ensure balanced sizes. Each participant viewed six messages: one general and one cancer‐specific message, each presented in three formats (text‐only, neutral pictorial, graphic pictorial). The two messages were randomly selected from a pool of eight and counterbalanced to minimize carryover effects. Text‐only messages were developed in step 1 and displayed in dark blue text on a white background. Participants completed a baseline assessment and a postexposure assessment after viewing each message. Primary outcomes included message receptivity (attention, emotional reaction) and precursors to behavior (perceived harm, intentions to reduce, limit, or stop consumption). Upon completion, participants received a $20 incentive.

Participants and recruitment

Recruitment was done through Qualtrics, a behavioral research company with access to a panel of 95 million diverse participants. 45 Qualtrics generated study‐specific, poststratification survey weights using a computer algorithm to ensure balanced recruitment (1:1 ratio) across sex (men vs. women) and alcohol use levels (moderate vs. heavy). Participants were invited by email, which included a link to complete the screening. Eligible participants were then directed to complete an online informed consent form detailing the study.

Sample size was calculated using PASS22 46 (NCCS Statistical Software) based on a small effect size (Cohen f = 0.12) for intention to reduce drinking reported in an Australian study of alcohol–health risk messages. 47 For a repeated‐measures design (one between‐subject factor, two within‐subject factors), 300 participants per group (moderate and heavy consumers; N = 600 total) were required to achieve >80% power at α = .05 to detect main and interaction effects, assuming a within‐subject correlation of 0.5, nonsphericity correction of 1, and Bonferroni adjustment for 24 comparisons (8 messages × 3 intensities). 47

Procedures

Eligible participants completed a 30‐minute online session on a responsive survey platform accessible by smartphones, tablets, or computers, with no device restrictions. Message stimuli were formatted according to standard online experimental practices. Before fielding the study, the research team reviewed the message stimuli internally to ensure readability across devices. Participants could enlarge content using standard device functions. After completing the baseline assessment, participants were randomly assigned by stratified randomization to view two messages: one from four general messages and one from four cancer‐specific messages. This approach ensured that each participant saw one message from each category, creating 16 possible pairs. Assignments within each category were randomized by the survey platform (Qualtrics), ensuring equal probability of exposure to each message within its respective group. The order of message presentation was also randomized at the individual level to minimize potential order and carryover effects. 48 Participants were instructed to review each message carefully before responding: “Please examine the text and image, then answer questions about your attention, reactions, and how the message influenced your views and intentions regarding alcohol.” Each message was presented on a separate screen, and participants were required to view and respond to all items before proceeding, ensuring complete data capture and preventing contamination. 49 A series of questions assessing reactions, perceptions, and behavioral intentions was completed postexposure to each message.

Measures and outcomes

The baseline assessment included sociodemographics (age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, marital status), and medical history (e.g., cancer history), the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, 50 use of tobacco and other substances, 51 alcohol social norms and beliefs, 29 perceived benefits of alcohol, 52 knowledge of alcohol‐related cancer risk, 40 and the desire for alcohol. 53 The postexposure assessment included questions about message receptivity, including attention, 42 , 54 reaction (believability, 55 fear, 54 relevance, anticipated social interactions, 42 and avoidance 56 ), alcohol harm perception, 57 and intention to reduce, commit to a drinking‐limit goal, or stop alcohol use in the next month. 58 , 59 The primary outcomes were message receptivity (attention and reaction) and changes in precursors to behavior (harm perception, intention to reduce, commitment to a drinking‐limit goal, and stopping alcohol use in the next month). All primary outcome questions were measured on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (a lot; see Supporting Materials).

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc.), with a significance level set at α = .05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sample characteristics. Frequencies and proportions were calculated for categorical variables, whereas means and standard deviations (SDs) were used for continuous variables (Table 1). To examine differences in postexposure responses across message intensities for the main outcomes, we fitted separate linear mixed‐effects models for each outcome, with fixed effects for message intensity (three levels) and message specificity (two levels) and a subject‐specific random intercept to account for repeated ratings within participants. The dependent variables were postexposure ratings (i.e., state‐level responses to each message). Pairwise contrasts were conducted to compare the three intensity levels (Table 2). For these comparisons, we used the Tukey–Kramer adjustment, which, like Bonferroni adjustment, controls the family‐wise error rate but is less conservative for pairwise contrasts. 60 A similar analysis was conducted to examine differences in postexposure responses across the message specificity for the main outcomes (Table 3). Between‐group differences by drinking level (moderate vs. heavy) were examined using a linear mixed‐effects model, with alcohol use levels (moderate, heavy) as a fixed between‐subject factor and message intensity (graphic, neutral, text‐only) and message specificity (general, cancer‐specific) as fixed within‐subject factors (Table 3). Models adjusted for age, sex, race, education, smoking, and other drug use were included as fixed‐effect covariates. The results are reported as the regression coefficient beta (β) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Pairwise contrasts were specified a priori and tested within a linear mixed‐effects framework. Because message intensity was modeled as a within‐subject factor with each participant serving as their own control, the risk of type I error inflation from multiple independent tests was reduced; therefore, post‐hoc multiple‐comparison adjustments were not applied. Composite scores for multi‐item outcomes (e.g., message receptivity) were analyzed alongside individual items to align with the message impact framework (Tables 2 and 3). Interaction effects (intensity × specificity) and moderation by drinking level (moderate, heavy) were tested using factorial models (Tables 4 and 5). Overall effectiveness was summarized descriptively as the mean ± SD (see Tables S1, S2, and S3).

RESULTS

Overall, 3380 respondents entered the survey; 653 were deemed ineligible at screening, and 1784 were excluded for over quota, leaving 943 participants (see Figure S1). Among these, 207 participants were identified as duplicates and removed during quality checks; 736 completed the survey, but 97 were excluded for data‐quality issues, leaving 639 participants for analysis.

Alcohol–cancer risk awareness

No missing data were observed because survey completion was required. Two participants were excluded because of implausible age values. In total, 639 were enrolled in the study (mean ± SD age, 43.3 ± 16.0 years; 49.30% female; 50.7% heavy consumers; Table 1). Awareness of alcohol–cancer risk varied by cancer type and was higher among moderate versus heavy consumers. Liver cancer was most recognized (62.3%), followed by colon (34.4%) and throat (31.8%) cancers, whereas rectum and larynx cancers (26.6% each) and breast cancer (20.8%) were least recognized.

Differences by message intensity

Both graphic and neutral pictorial messages outperformed text‐only messages across nearly all outcomes (Table 2). Graphic images further exceeded neutral ones, generating greater attention (β = 0.13; p < .0001), fear (β = 0.20; p < .0001), and avoidance (β = 0.14; p < .0001) and increasing perceived harm (β = 0.12; p < .0001) and motivation to reduce intake (β = 0.19; p < .0001), set drinking limits (β = 0.22; p < .0001), and stop drinking (β = 0.02; p < .0001).

Differences by message specificity

Compared with general messages, cancer‐specific messages elicited greater attention (β = 0.11; p = .0016); were more informative (β = 0.14; p < .001), believable (β = 0.10; p = .009), and frightening (β = 0.19; p < .0001); and produced stronger intentions to reduce drinking (β = 0.10; p = .009), set a limit (β = 0.19; p < .0001), and stop drinking in the next month (β = 0.11; p = .010). These effects were more pronounced among moderate consumers, with cancer‐specific messages outperforming general messages across all outcomes (Table 3). Interaction analyses indicated that the relative effectiveness of cancer‐specific versus general messages varied by visual intensity. Cancer‐specific content had stronger effects when delivered in pictorial formats (graphic or neutral) than in text‐only format, suggesting a synergistic interaction between message specificity and visual intensity (Table 4).

Differences by alcohol consumption level

Compared with heavy consumers, moderate consumers were more receptive to the messages (β = 0.17; p < .0001) and reported greater positive behavioral intentions (β = 0.20; p = .0183). They also perceived the messages as more personally relevant (β = 0.77; p = .0058), more avoidance‐provoking (β = 0.61; p < .0001), more likely to prompt social interaction (β = 0.65; p = .0012), and more effective in increasing harm perception (β = 0.29; p = .0093; Table 3). Interaction analyses indicated that the effects of message intensity varied by drinking level, with stronger effects observed among moderate versus heavy consumers (Table 5).

Message ratings based on overall effectiveness

Graphic message 6 (mouth and throat cancer) received the highest attention rating (mean ± SD, 4.19 ± 1.04; see Tables S1 and S2) and the highest overall effectiveness score (4.10 ± 0.86). It was followed by neutral message 6 (4.01 ± 1.04) and graphic message 5 (colon cancer; 3.95 ± 1.04; see Table S3).

DISCUSSION

Our current results demonstrate that knowledge of the alcohol–cancer link remains alarmingly low in the United States, particularly for breast cancer. Both neutral and graphic pictorial messages outperformed text‐only messages in capturing attention, eliciting emotional responses, and increasing intentions to change drinking, with graphic images producing the strongest effects. Cancer‐specific messages were also more effective than general messages, rated as more engaging, credible, frightening, and informative. Heavy consumers were less responsive than moderate consumers. Together, these findings highlight effective strategies for alcohol–cancer communication across campaigns, warning labels, and clinical settings and underscore the promise of AI in message development and tailoring.

Knowledge of alcohol–cancer risk in the United States remains low, particularly for breast, rectal, and laryngeal cancers, with only about one quarter recognizing these links. Our findings align with prior surveillance. In the 2019 Health Informational National Trends Survey, only 33% of US adults identified alcohol–cancer risk. 61 In addition, only 20% of women in our sample recognized the link between breast cancer and alcohol, which is similar to the 28% reported in 2022. 62 This is concerning because there is evidence that even low alcohol intake increases breast cancer risk. 63 This knowledge gap likely reflects limited warning labels and public messaging, 64 along with alcohol’s social acceptability. 65 It is encouraging to note that both our study and prior research indicate that targeted messaging can effectively increase knowledge. 66

Visual messages were more effective than text‐only messages across outcomes, in contrast to a prior US study. 28 However, that study’s sample included underage consumers, who can be less receptive to risk messaging, especially those that may happen in later life (e.g., cancer). 67 Graphic images outperformed neutral visuals in capturing attention, increasing perceived harm, and motivating change, especially among moderate consumers. Consistent with Brennan et al., 47 graphic messages caused more avoidance than neutral ones but more strongly affected moderate consumers' intentions to change behavior than heavy consumers. This suggests that avoidance among moderate consumers might actually reflect engagement with the message 68 ; whereas, for heavy consumers, these messages elicited a defensive response of denial and resistance. 25 The psychological reactance theory suggests that, when warnings prompt avoidance and resistance, individuals may try to restore their perceived loss of freedom by acting contrary to the warning, such as consuming more alcohol rather than less. 69 These results are consistent with earlier findings that higher alcohol consumption leads to more defensive processing of messages about alcohol's harms. 70 , 71 To better engage heavy consumers, future messaging should reduce defensiveness through nonstigmatizing, 71 harm‐reduction approaches 72 ; emphasize incremental change 73 , 74 ; and use tailored delivery in supportive settings, such as clinical interactions. 9

Consistent with prior research, 27 , 66 , 75 cancer‐specific messages outperformed general messages in attracting attention, conveying new information, and enhancing credibility, resulting in stronger intentions to reduce, limit, or stop drinking. Effects were stronger among moderate than heavy consumers. According to the extended parallel process model, a person's response to a warning is influenced by two appraisals: the perceived threat, which assesses the severity of risk and personal vulnerability, and efficacy, which encompasses confidence in one’s ability to mitigate the threat. 76 Thus it is likely that cancer‐specific messages registered better with both moderate and heavy consumers, but the perceived efficacy of behavioral change made moderate consumers more responsive. Messages about throat, colon, and breast cancers were rated most effective, possibly because of their prevalence and salience in the public consciousness. 66

This study has several strengths, including the use of AI to generate diverse messages, a rigorous within‐subject/between‐subject crossover design, a large national sample, and a robust analytical approach. It also examined a broad range of alcohol–cancer warning strategies developed with input from experts in health communication, alcohol policy, and AI. Several limitations should be noted. Messages were not developed with direct input from the target population and were tested in a single, controlled exposure, limiting conclusions about real‐world and longer term effects. Future studies should assess repeated exposure in naturalistic settings. The use of a panel‐based convenience sample may affect generalizability despite weighting and a large, diverse sample. The higher educational profile of participants warrants validation in lower education populations. 77 Although randomization reduces bias, residual order effects cannot be fully excluded. Effect sizes were modest, which is typical for brief communication interventions, but may still be meaningful at the population level. Finally, although AI was used carefully with multidisciplinary oversight, its potential biases remain an important consideration for future research. 37

Conclusions

The current findings support effective alcohol cancer risk communication by considering message content, design, and consumers' traits. They identify promising messages for health campaigns, warning labels, and counseling and highlight the role of AI in tailoring each message. Heavy consumers, who are at greatest risk and less responsive, may benefit from harm‐reduction, nonstigmatizing messages that emphasize incremental change delivered in supportive settings (e.g., patient–provider interactions). Future research should explore how drinking levels affect messaging strategies and their long‐term effect on behavior and mediators.
