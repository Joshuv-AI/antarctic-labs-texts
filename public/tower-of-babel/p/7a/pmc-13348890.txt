Cloning and functional determination of PpyASR1 during fruit development in Pyrus pyrifolia
Authors: Biying Zhao, Shanting Liu, Minwu Zhou, Guodong Chen, Kaijie Qi, Xiao Wu, Junsen Peng, Zhimei Xu, Xianrong Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbscisic acid, stress and ripening-induced genes (ASRs) play crucial roles in fruit maturation, and the isolation, identification, and functional characterization of these genes have been extensively studied in plants. ‘Guanyang’ snow pear is a high-quality pear cultivar with distinct regional characteristics in Guangxi. However, the gene responsible for ASR1 and its specific functional attributes remain unidentified.ResultsIn this study, PpyASR1 was successfully isolated from ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits. This gene was found to exhibit specific expression during fruit development, with its expression level following an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease. Subcellular localization analysis revealed that the protein product of the PpyASR1 gene predominantly localizes within the nucleus, supporting its role as a transcription factor regulating maturation and senescence in fruit tissues. Virus-induced gene silencing experiments demonstrated that suppressing of PpyASR1 gene expression in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit significantly delayed senescence-associated deterioration. Compared to the control group, the treated fruits exhibited higher firmness, lower ABA content, reduced ethylene production, lower soluble solid content, and higher titratable acidity. Transient overexpression of PpyASR1 in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear resulted in markedly accelerated fruit senescence and postharvest deterioration. Compared to controls, these fruits showed reduced firmness, increased ABA content and ethylene production, and lower titratable acidity, whereas no significant difference in soluble solids content was detected.ConclusionThe current findings provide valuable insights into the storage characteristics of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear and establish a theoretical foundation for understanding the potential role of PpyASR1 in regulating fruit senescence and postharvest deterioration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08922-y.

Introduction

ASR (ABA-insensitive stress-responsive) proteins, a class of plant-specific transcription factors, are activated in response to ABA, stress, and maturation signals [1]. These proteins play crucial roles not only in plant responses to drought, high salt, low temperature, and abscisic acid stress, but also in regulating various metabolic pathways associated with plant development, maturation, and sugar metabolism [2–4]. With advancements in molecular biology and transcriptome sequencing technologies, the ASR gene was first identified in tomato fruit in 1993 [5], and since then, the functional roles of ASR protein have been identified and characterized in various monocot and dicot plants, including rice, apple, pine, corn, grape, banana, and lily [6–9]. Studies have shown that most ASR proteins share similar physical properties. They are small plant-specific hydrophilic proteins that feature two highly conserved regions, one rich in histidine at the N-terminal and another containing the highly conserved C-terminal DNA binding domain with the ABA/WDS structural domain [10, 11]. These structural features suggest that ASR proteins may have important physiological roles in the abscisic acid signaling pathway in plants.

The ASR gene exhibits a wide range of expression profiles across different plant species, with family members showing distinct patterns of expression. For example, in tomatoes and grape, ASR genes are mainly expressed in fruits and leaves [6, 12], while in maize and rice, ASR genes are predominantly expressed in leaves and roots [13, 14]. Additionally, ASR expression patterns vary under different abiotic stresses and during plant growth and development. In tomatoes, ASR1 is highly expressed during fruit ripening and water stress, while ASR2 is primarily expressed in roots and leaves under abiotic stress conditions [12]. Under drought stress, SiASR3 and SiASR4 are highly expressed in leaves, while SiASR1, SiASR2, SiASR5, and SiASR6 are highly expressed in roots [10]. Therefore, the widespread expression of ASR genes in various plant tissues contributes to the plant’s ability to resist a range of biotic and abiotic stresses. Specifically, the ASR protein SoDip22 plays a crucial role in sugarcane’s adaptation to drought stress through the ABA-mediated signaling pathway [15]. Similarly, the ASR gene ScDS2 is specifically induced in potatoes under dehydration conditions, while ASR expression is also induced in rice root tips and lateral roots under water stress [13, 16].

Abscisic acid (ABA) plays a crucial role in initiating the ripening process in both climacteric and non-climacteric fruits and is involved in regulating the ripening process of fruits. ASR proteins are key transcription factors in the ABA signaling pathway, significantly influencing fruit ripening and the formation of fruit quality. For instance, Canel et al. detected high levels of ASR peptides in immature, low-acid pomelo juice and found a close association between ASR gene expression and fruit ripening [17]. Subsequently, research on the role of ASR proteins in regulating fruit ripening has expanded significantly. Cakir et al. discovered that the transcription level of the ASR gene was highest during the fruit setting and color transformation stages in grapes, decreasing during the color change phase but increasing again in later stages of fruit maturity, consistent with patterns of ABA and sugar accumulation [6]. In strawberries, the content of endogenous ABA, along with ASR gene expression and protein abundance, significantly increased at the onset of fruit ripening [18]. Similarly, in tomatoes, ASR1 expression continued to rise during fruit development [3]. In potatoes, a decrease in ASR gene transcription was correlated with a reduction in hexose transport proteins in the plasma membrane and glucose content in parenchyma cells, indicating that glucose accumulation is closely linked to ASR gene expression [16]. Moreover, exogenous ABA treatment in immature strawberries significantly increased endogenous ABA levels, ASR gene transcription, and protein expression, ultimately accelerating fruit ripening [18]. These findings underscore the importance of ASR proteins as key components of the ABA signaling pathway that regulate downstream gene expression, affecting both fruit ripening and quality.

The Pyrus pyrifolia cv. ‘Guanyang’ (Guanyang Xueli, commonly known as ‘Guanyang’ snow pear) cultivar is a highly esteemed variety within the sand pear system of China. In 2009, it was officially registered as a geographical indication product by the Ministry of Agriculture. This fruit exhibits exceptional storage resistance and a long shelf life. However, the underlying mechanisms responsible for its storage tolerance remain poorly understood. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the storage tolerance mechanisms of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear to enhance the storage capacity of early-maturing sand pear varieties. In this study, we successfully identified the transcription factor PpyASR1, which encodes an ABA, stress and maturation-induced gene in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear. Additionally, we observed its involvement in fruit senescence and postharvest deterioration, as well as its regulatory effect on ethylene production. These findings provide valuable insights into the storage characteristics of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit.

Results

Isolation and sequence analysis of PpyASR1 in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear

Based on the published ASR sequence and the reference Dangshansu pear genome, specific primers were designed to isolate and clone the ASR gene ‘Guanyang’ snow pear. Following sequencing, the complete ASR protein sequence was obtained. Sequence analysis revealed that PpyASR1 consists of 203 amino acid residues, with a calculated molecular weight of 22.35 kDa. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the ASR protein sequences from ‘Guanyang’ snow pear, apple, rice, soybean, corn, millet, wheat, and tomato. The results indicated that PpyASR1, MdASR1, and SoASR1 cluster in the same branch, with PpyASR1 being most closely related to MdASR1 (Fig. 1A). Multiple sequence alignments of eight ASR amino acid sequences from diverse plant species (Supplementary file 1) revealed that PpyASR1 shares the highest sequence similarity with MdASR1, with 94.58% identity, followed by SoASR1 (52.10% identity) and CoASR1 (36.76% identity). The remaining four sequences (LOC_Os04g34600.1 from Oryza sativa, Solyc06g069400.4.1 from Solanum lycopersicum, Seita.7G291500 from Setaria italica, and TraesCS3D01G517400 from Triticum aestivum) also showed conserved homology with PpyASR1, consistent with the evolutionary conservation of the ASR protein family (Fig. 1B). Additionally, structural analysis of PpyASR1 revealed a histidine-rich region at the N-terminal, a conserved ABA/WDS super-domain at the C-terminal, and a signal sensing region, all of which are characteristic features of the protein (Fig. 1B).

Expression and subcellular localization analysis of PpyASR1

To explore the potential role of the PpyASR1 gene in fruit ripening, quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was employed to measure the mRNA abundance of the gene during the ripening process of the ‘Guanyang’ snow pear, with samples collected at seven specific developmental stages. The results demonstrated that the mRNA abundance of PpyASR1 gradually increased from stage S1 to S5, with a peak mRNA abundance observed at S5, and then its abundance level of PpyASR1 gradually decreased (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, ABA content in pear fruit showed a continuous increasing trend across the seven developmental stages, which was consistent with the expression pattern of PpyASR1 (Fig. S1). This result suggests that PpyASR1 may be involved in ABA accumulation during fruit development and ripening.

To investigate the subcellular localization of PpyASR1, the open reading frame without the stop codon was fused to the N-terminus of green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the control of CaMV 35 S promoter. The fusion plasmid (PpyASR1-GFP) and an empty vector (35 S-GFP) were transiently transformed into tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) leaves, respectively, and green fluorescence was examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Zeiss LSM 780). The results showed that the green fluorescence of the control (35 S-GFP) was distributed throughout the entire cell, whereas the green fluorescence of the PpyASR1-GFP fusion protein was observed exclusively in the nucleus. Moreover, this finding was consistent with the nuclear dye staining pattern, confirming that PpyASR1 resides in the nucleus (Fig. 2B).

Silencing of PpyASR1 affects fruit phenotype and firmness in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear

The ASR1 gene plays a critical role in fruit development and ripening, and the expression of the PpyASR1 variant is notably elevated in fruit tissue. The abundance of PpyASR1 transcript increases progressively during fruit growth and ripening, leading us to speculate that this gene may also be involved in the ripening process. To test this hypothesis, Agrobacterium-mediated transformation was performed to generate ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits with reduced PpyASR1 expression by using the PpyASR1-TRV2 construct. After a 15-day treatment period, the abundance of PpyASR1 transcripts was quantified by qRT-PCR. The results showed a significant reduction in PpyASR1 transcript levels in PpyASR1-TRV2-treated fruits, which reached approximately 9% of the levels in the control group (Fig. 3B).

For the following experiments, the effects of PpyASR1-TRV2 and CK on the fruit color and firmness of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear were investigated. Both PpyASR1-TRV2 and CK fruits were stored in a chamber under greenhouse conditions. After 15 days of storage, the fruit color of CK fruits darkened significantly, with the brown color deepening compared to the PpyASR1-TRV2 fruits (Fig. 3A). Further measurements of fruit surface color parameters revealed significant differences between CK and PpyASR1-TRV2 fruits after 15 days of storage, CK fruits exhibited a notable decline in lightness (L*), whereas PpyASR1-silenced fruits maintained significantly higher brightness (L* = 79.12 ± 1.09b). Furthermore, CK fruits displayed a marked increase in redness (a* = 8.92 ± 0.72c) compared to PpyASR1-TRV2 fruits (a* = 4.21 ± 0.51b). Correspondingly, the yellow-blue chromaticity value (b*) was significantly higher in PpyASR1-TRV2 fruits (17.85 ± 1.02b) than in CK fruits (29.91 ± 1.15a) after storage (Table S1). Furthermore, the firmness of CK fruits decreased markedly, reaching only 53.7% of the firmness observed in PpyASR1-TRV2 fruits (Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that silencing PpyASR1 expression significantly delays the senescence-associated deterioration processes in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear.

Silencing of PpyASR1 affects hormone levels and fruit quality‑related indexes in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits

Abscisic acid (ABA) and ethylene are key regulators of fruit development and ripening, and their endogenous levels are important physiological indexes reflecting fruit maturity. Meanwhile, titratable acidity and total soluble solids are critical indicators for evaluating fruit flavor quality and ripening process. To investigate the regulatory role of PpyASR1 in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear ripening, virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) was performed to suppress PpyASR1 transcription in pear fruits. After 15 days of storage, the contents of ABA and ethylene were both significantly lower in PpyASR1-TRV2-silenced fruits than in CK fruits. The ABA content in PpyASR1-TRV2 fruits was only 73.06% of that in CK fruits, and ethylene production in CK fruits was 1.7 times higher than that in the silenced group (Fig. 4A, B). For fruit quality indexes, the titratable acidity in PpyASR1-TRV2 fruits was about 1.4 times that in CK fruits, whereas the accumulation of total soluble solids was notably slower in silenced fruits. At 15 days, the total soluble solids content in CK fruits was approximately 1.2 times higher than that in PpyASR1-TRV2 fruits (Fig. 4C, D). Taken together, these results demonstrate that PpyASR1 silencing markedly inhibits the accumulation of ABA and ethylene, and delays the changes of titratable acidity and soluble solids during fruit ripening, suggesting that PpyASR1 acts as an important regulator in modulating hormone metabolism and quality formation in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits.

Effect of transient overexpression of PpyASR1 on phenotype and firmness in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit

To further investigate the impact of PpyASR1 on fruit phenotype and firmness, transient overexpression of PpyASR1 was performed in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits. The abundance of PpyASR1 transcripts was evaluated in both PpyASR1-OE and CK fruits by qRT-PCR after 15 days. The results showed a significant increase in PpyASR1 transcript levels in PpyASR1-OE fruits, with expression levels approximately 9.8 times higher than those in CK fruits (Fig. 5B). After 15 days of storage under greenhouse conditions, PpyASR1-OE fruits exhibited pronounced yellowing and discoloration compared to CK fruits (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis of fruit surface color parameters confirmed these phenotypic differences, after 15 days of storage, PpyASR1-OE fruits showed a significantly higher lightness value (L* = 96.57 ± 1.87c) than CK fruits (L* = 90.87 ± 1.54b), accompanied by a marked increase in redness (a* = 15.94 ± 1.85a vs. 11.46 ± 1.72b in CK) and a dramatic elevation in yellowness (b* = 34.68 ± 1.09c vs. 21.76 ± 1.95b in CK) (Table S2). In line with the accelerated senescence phenotype, the firmness of PpyASR1-OE fruits decreased significantly, reaching only 72.2% of the firmness observed in CK fruits (Fig. 5C). These results demonstrate that overexpression of PpyASR1 significantly accelerates fruit senescence and senescence-associated deterioration in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear.

Effect of transient overexpression of PpyASR1 on hormone levels and fruit quality-related indexes in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit

To further verify the regulatory function of PpyASR1 in fruit ripening, transient overexpression of PpyASR1 was conducted in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits. After 15 days of storage, ABA content and ethylene production were both significantly elevated in PpyASR1-OE fruits compared with CK fruits (Fig. 6A, B). Specifically, ABA accumulation was markedly higher in overexpressed fruits, and ethylene production in PpyASR1-OE fruits was approximately 1.4 times that in control fruits. For fruit quality traits, both CK and PpyASR1-OE fruits showed a gradual decrease in titratable acidity during storage. Notably, the titratable acidity content in PpyASR1-OE fruits was only 76% of that in CK fruits at 15 days after injection, with a significantly greater reduction (Fig. 6C). In contrast, there was no significant difference in total soluble solids content between PpyASR1-OE and CK fruits at the same stage (Fig. 6D). Taken together, these results demonstrate that PpyASR1 overexpression significantly promotes the accumulation of ABA and ethylene and accelerates the reduction of titratable acidity during fruit ripening, further confirming that PpyASR1 serves as a positive regulator in modulating hormone metabolism and senescence-associated deterioration in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits.

Discussion

ASR (ABA-insensitive stress-responsive) genes represent a novel class of transcription regulators recently discovered in plants, playing a pivotal role in environmental stress responses and fruit ripening [10, 19, 20]. These genes are involved in plant adaptation to a wide array of environmental stressors and regulate various developmental processes, including fruit ripening and senescence, pollen maturation, and glucose metabolism [21, 22]. Notably, ASR genes also participate in ABA-regulated responses to both biotic and abiotic stressors [7, 23]. However, research on the role of ASR genes in regulating the ripening and senescence processes of pear fruit remains limited. In this study, we successfully isolated and cloned the PpyASR1 gene from the fruit of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear, a variety with distinctive characteristics from Guangxi, using a combination of bioinformatics, molecular biology, and advanced techniques. Most ASR proteins share common physical properties; they are small, hydrophilic, plant-specific proteins (the PpyASR1 protein in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear consists of only 203 amino acids) and possess two highly conserved regions in their amino acid sequences. The first, a histidine-rich region, is located at the N-terminus, while the second, a conserved C-terminal DNA-binding domain, contains an ABA/WDS domain, similar to findings in previous studies [24].

Sequence alignment of amino acid sequences revealed that the PpyASR1 protein is highly homologous to ASR1 proteins from apple, rice, and other plant species, exhibiting conserved ABA/WDS domains. The N-terminus is enriched with histidine residues, while the C-terminus contains a distinct nuclear targeting signal. These structural features suggest that PpyASR1 may play a significant physiological role in the plant’s abscisic acid signaling pathway. Previous research has shown that the C-terminal region of ASR proteins harbors a highly conserved nuclear localization sequence, indicating that ASR proteins function as nuclear-localized transcription factors. In tomato, it has been confirmed that the ASR1 protein localizes to the nucleus [25]. Our study also confirmed that PpyASR1 is localized in the nucleus, as evidenced by confocal laser microscopy, supporting prior findings. ASR genes are widely expressed across various plant tissues, with particularly high expression levels in fruit tissues [26].

To elucidate the expression pattern of PpyASR1 during the development of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit, qRT-PCR was used to investigate the expression dynamics of PpyASR1 at seven distinct post-flowering stages. The results revealed that PpyASR1 expression peaked at 150 days post-flowering. ‘Guanyang’ snow pear, a widely cultivated climacteric fruit, undergoes a complex process of growth and development, mainly comprising five distinct stages: cell differentiation, cell expansion, fruit development, maturation, and senescence. These stages represent key milestones in the fruit lifecycle, with ripening being a crucial process that directly affects fruit quality, market value, and industrial applications [27]. Ripening involves multiple physiological changes, such as softening, increased respiration, sugar accumulation, and color transformation, which serve as indicators of maturity and aging. The transcription factor ASR1 is a key regulator of these processes, with its expression levels directly influencing fruit ripening and senescence. Canel et al. demonstrated a significant correlation between ASR peptide abundance and fruit ripening in low-acid grapefruit juice, highlighting the role of ASR proteins in regulating fruit maturation [17]. This discovery led to an increased focus on the role of ASR proteins in regulating fruit ripening. Additionally, the expression of VvASR peaked during grape fruiting, just before color change, before slightly declining in later maturity stages [28]. Similarly, in strawberry, endogenous ABA content and ASR expression levels significantly increase at the onset of ripening [3, 18]. In addition, the application of exogenous ABA to immature strawberry fruits can significantly increase the content of endogenous ABA hormones, as well as raise the transcription level and protein expression of ASR, thus accelerating the ripening of strawberry fruits [3, 29].

To investigate the functional role of PpyASR1 in the ripening and senescence of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit, gene silencing and overexpression techniques were employed to monitor dynamic changes in key physiological and biochemical indices. Consistent with the conserved functions of ASR proteins, silencing of PpyASR1 significantly delayed fruit senescence, as evidenced by maintained fruit firmness, increased organic acid content, and reduced accumulation of ABA and ethylene, as well as slower degradation of soluble solids. In contrast, transient overexpression of PpyASR1 accelerated fruit senescence and postharvest deterioration, characterized by decreased fruit firmness, reduced organic acid levels, and enhanced production of ABA and ethylene. Collectively, these gain- and loss-of-function analyses demonstrate that PpyASR1 acts as a critical positive regulator of senescence-associated deterioration in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear, likely through its capacity to modulate hormone homeostasis. Given that PpyASR1 encodes a nuclear-localized protein and exhibits transcriptional regulatory features, its regulatory role in fruit senescence may be mediated through the modulation of ABA and ethylene biosynthesis pathways. ABA biosynthesis is primarily governed by 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED), the rate-limiting enzyme whose expression is tightly regulated by key transcription factors during fruit ripening [30]. In strawberry and grape, ASR genes have been functionally linked to ABA signaling and ripening [6, 18], suggesting that PpyASR1 may directly target and upregulate NCED family members to promote ABA accumulation, a mechanism consistent with the elevated ABA levels observed in PpyASR1-overexpressing pear fruits. For ethylene biosynthesis, two rate-limiting enzymes—1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) synthase (ACS) and ACC oxidase (ACO)—catalyze the committed steps [31]. Functional studies in rice have revealed that ASR proteins physically interact with transcription factors (e.g., OsERF3) to modulate ethylene emission by repressing OsACS2, OsACS6, and OsACO2 [32]. In tomato, SlASR1 has been shown to regulate ethylene-mediated fruit ripening by controlling the expression of ACS and ACO orthologs [3]. Given the high sequence homology between PpyASR1 and these functionally characterized ASR proteins, it is highly likely that PpyASR1 exerts its regulatory role in pear by directly binding to the promoter regions of ACS and ACO genes, thereby enhancing their transcription and accelerating ethylene production. Future chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and dual-luciferase reporter assays are needed to verify these potential downstream targets and elucidate the precise molecular mechanism of PpyASR1 in regulating fruit ripening.

Conclusion

In summary, PpyASR1 was successfully isolated and cloned from ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression of PpyASR1 initially increased and subsequently decreased during fruit growth and development. Subcellular localization further provided strong evidence of its nuclear function. Gene silencing experiments demonstrated that inhibiting PpyASR1 expression significantly delayed fruit senescence and postharvest deterioration compared to the control group, as evidenced by slower fruit softening, reduced ABA content and ethylene production, decelerated organic acid degradation, and lower soluble solid content. Conversely, overexpression of PpyASR1 in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit resulted in a pronounced acceleration of senescence‑associated fruit deterioration, characterized by decreased fruit hardness, increased ABA content and ethylene production, reduced organic acid concentration. No significant difference in soluble solids content was observed between overexpression and control fruits. The findings of this study indicate that PpyASR1 plays a critical role in regulating fruit senescence and postharvest quality changes by modulating ABA and ethylene levels. This research significantly enhances the understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of pear fruit senescence and deterioration by ASR proteins.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Pear fruits (‘Guanyang’ snow pear) and tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) were used in these experiments. Tobacco plants were grown in a potting soil mixture (rich soil: vermiculite = 2: 1, v/v) and kept in growth chambers with a light/ dark regime of 14/10 h, at 25/23°C with 75% relative humidity, and a light intensity of 800 µmol·m− 2·s− 1. Pear fruits were harvested from the Guangxi Special Crop Research Institute experimental base in Guilin, Guangxi, China. The tobacco seeds were obtained from Center of Pear Engineering Technology Research, State Key Laboratory of Crop Genetics and Germplasm Enhancement, Nanjing Agricultural University. Ripening fruits were harvested at maturity from 10 trees, and transported to the experiment center on the same day of harvest.

Identification and sequence alignment of PpyASR1

The protein sequences of OsASR1 and MdASR1 were used as queries to perform a BLAST algorithm-based search against Chinese white pear genome databases (http://peargenome.njau.edu.cn/). Potential PpyASR1 protein sequences were reciprocally queried against the apple database to identify the best ASR protein hits, which were then analyzed using Pfam database to confirm the presence of an ABA/WDS domain [33]. According to the ASR gene putative sequence in the pear genome, the open reading frame sequences of the perfect PpyASR1 gene were cloned from ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits using gene-specific primers. The PpyASR1 protein sequence was determined in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear. An unrooted phylogenetic tree was generated by MEGA 6.0 software using the neighbor-joining method and bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates [34], and alignment of multiple ASR amino acid sequences from pear, apple, rice, soybean, corn, millet, wheat, and tomato was performed using ClustalW program [35].

Total RNA isolation and qRT-PCR assays

Total RNA was independently extracted from ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruits at different stages of development. The first sample was collected on June 26th (S1), which coincided with the 110th day after flowering (DAF). Subsequent samples were harvested on S2 (120 DAF), S3 (130 DAF), S4 (140 DAF), S5 (150 DAF), S6 (160 DAF), S7 (170 DAF). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (TaKaRa, Biotech, Shanghai, China). Primers for specific PpyASR1 and housekeeping Actin genes were designed using Oligo 7 software (Table S3), and the specificity of these primers was verified against the pear genome via the Primer Search-Paired program.

For the qRT-PCR assay, the reaction system (total volume of 20 µL) included 10 µL of Roche’s LightCycler 480 SYBRGREEN I Master Mix (Basel, Switzerland), 2 µL of cDNA template (80–100 ng), 1 µL of each primer (200 nM of each primer), and 6 µL of deionized water (ddH2O). The reaction was performed in a CFX96 Real-Time System (Roche), following a standard three-step protocol (40 cycles of 10 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 60 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C), followed by a melt curve analysis. Each qRT-PCR assay was conducted with three biological replicates and three technical replicates. The data were analyzed and graphed using Office 2010 (Microsoft Word for Mac) and GraphPad Prism 8.

Subcellular localization of PpyASR1

The full-length of PpyASR1 sequence was cloned by RT-PCR from cDNA of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit using specific primers. The PCR product was resolved by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the target band was recovered using the gel kit. The PpyASR1 was then inserted into the modified PC1301 vector containing the CaMV 35 S promoter (Clontech, Beijing, China) using the Homologous Recombination Enzyme Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The recombinant plasmids 35 S: PpyASR1-GFP and the control plasmid 35 S: GFP were independently transformed into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101-mediated transformation. The recombinant plasmids were cultured in a bacterial solution with an OD600 of 0.8-1.0. The bacterial solution was collected, and resuspended in infection solution (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, pH = 5.7, and 200 mM acetylbutanone), then slowly inject the 50 µL solution into the back of the tobacco leaves using a 1-mL sterile syringe, and use the tobacco leaves injected with the empty carrier as a control. Transformed leaves were stained with double-distilled water supplemented with 10 µg/ml DAPI (Invitrogen) for 15 min. GFP signals were visualized using the Zeiss LSM Image Browser (Zeiss, Germany) 3 days after transformation.

Silencing of PpyASR1 in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit

Silencing of PpyASR1 in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit were performed using a transient transformation system based on previously reported methods with minor modifications [36, 37]. The full-length of PpyASR1 sequence was isolated and subsequently inserted into the pTRV2 vector containing the CaMV 35 S promoter. The recombinant plasmid PpyASR1-pTRV2, the empty vector pTRV2, and pTRV1 were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101, and then incubated at 28 °C until the OD600 reached 0.8-1.0. The bacterial solution was collected and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, pH 5.5, and 150 µM acetosyringone), and then induced for 3 h on a shaking table (TY-80B). Equal volumes of PpyASR1-pTRV2 and pTRV1 bacterial solutions (OD600 = 0.8-1.0) were mixed, and 100 µL of the mixture was injected into the flesh tissue of six positions on each fruit using a sterile 1-mL syringe. Controls were prepared by injecting equal volumes of pTRV2 and pTRV1 bacterial solutions into the fruits. All samples were transferred to a culture chamber incubator at 25 °C, and samples were taken on the 0th and 15th days, respectively.

Overexpression of PpyASR1 in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit

Overexpression of PpyASR1 in ‘Guanyang’ snow pear fruit were performed using a transient transformation system based on previously reported methods with minor modifications [38, 39]. The complete of PpyASR1 sequence was cloned into the pSAK277 vector with the CaMV 35 S promoter. The recombinant plasmid PpyASR1-pSAK277 and empty vector pSAK277 were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 and incubated at 28 °C until the OD600 reached 0.8-1.0. The bacterial strain was centrifuged and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, pH 5.5, and 150 µM acetosyringone), and then 100 µL bacterial solution was slowly injected into the flesh tissue at six positions on the fruit using a sterile 1-mL syringe. Fruits injected with pSAK277 were used as controls. All samples were transferred to a culture chamber incubator at 25 °C, and samples were taken on the 0th and 15th days, respectively.

Pericarp color measurements

Pericarp color indices were analyzed using ImageJ 1.53k software. Fruit images were taken under uniform diffused light, and RGB images were converted to CIE Lab* stacks to obtain grayscale layers for relative lightness (L), red-green (a), and yellow-blue (b) components. For each sample, three regions of interest were randomly selected on the equatorial peel, and the mean gray values were measured as relative L, a, and b values. The average of triplicate measurements was used for statistical analysis, representing relative color changes during fruit ripening rather than absolute colorimeter readings.

Ethylene measurements

Ethylene production was determined utilizing a gas chromatography system (GC2010, Shimadzu, Japan) and a 1000 µL rheodyne injector (Gaoge, Shangshai, China), following previously described methods [39]. Five fruits from each treatment and control group were placed in 5 L sealed jars and stored at room temperature, sealed with plastic wrap. A 1 mL gas sample was collected from each jar and injected into a gas chromatograph equipped with a 30 cm glass column (3.2 mm ID). The gas flow rates for nitrogen, hydrogen, and air were set at 40, 40, and 500 mL/min, respectively. The oven, injector, and flame ionization detector temperatures were set at 300 °C, 60 °C, and 260 °C, respectively. After the measurement, the volume and mass of each fruit were used to calculate the ethylene release rate per unit volume and mass of ‘Guanyang’ snow pear. Each sample was measured three times, and the experiment was replicated three times.

ABA measurements

The ABA content of fruit sample was determined by ELISA method. A total of 1 g fruit from each treatment and control group was collected and added to 2 mL of pre-cooled 80% methanol solution. The samples were homogenized under ice bath conditions and the homogenate was transferred to a 10 mL centrifuge tube, then placed at 4 °C for 4 h. After centrifugation at 1000 g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected and injected into a C18 solid-phase adsorption column for ABA determination using a reagent kit. Each sample was measured three times, and the experiment was repeated three times to ensure accuracy.

Firmness measurements

Fruit firmness was measured using a penetrometer. At least three random points on specific surfaces of each pear were selected for data collection. Each group required at least two pear fruits for measurement, and firmness was determined based on the probe’s penetration of 0.5 cm into the flesh.

Soluble solid measurements

Soluble solids content was measured using a portable digital refractometer. Prior to measurements, the meter was calibrated to zero. Fresh juice was extracted from three carefully selected fruits for an accurate reading. Each fruit was replicated three times, and the average value obtained was used to represent the soluble solids content for each fruit.

Titratable acidity measurements

Titratable acidity was measured by NaOH titration method. A 2 g sample of fruit was frozen in liquid nitrogen, followed by the addition of 3 mL of distilled water and soaking in a 75 °C water bath for 30 min. The sample was centrifuged for 10 min, and the supernatant was diluted to 10 mL with deionized water. 1 mL of the sample was titrated with 0.1 mol/L NaOH standard solution until a slight red color appeared and persisted for 30–60 s. The experiment was performed three times for technical and experimental consistency.
