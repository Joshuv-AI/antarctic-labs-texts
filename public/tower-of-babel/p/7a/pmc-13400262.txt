Deep brain stimulation in pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy: Implications for the developing connectome
Authors: Karim Mithani, Thiemo F Dinger, Carolina Gorodetsky, George M Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpilepsy is increasingly recognized as a disorder of brain networks, and intracranial neuromodulation has emerged as a therapeutic strategy for individuals with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). In pediatric populations, however, ongoing brain development raises important questions regarding safety, efficacy, and long-term consequences of chronic neuromodulation. In this review, we examine the emerging role of deep brain stimulation (DBS) in the treatment of DRE in children and adolescents. We synthesize current evidence supporting the neurobiological rationale for DBS in pediatric epilepsy, with particular attention to thalamic and limbic targets and their associated networks. Available clinical data are critically reviewed, including findings from emerging prospective studies, with emphasis on seizure outcomes, quality of life, neurodevelopmental trajectories, and safety. We also discuss surgical, clinical, and ethical considerations unique to the application of chronic intracranial stimulation in the developing brain. Finally, we outline future directions for the field, including network-informed target selection, comparisons to responsive stimulation paradigms, and the need for developmentally relevant, longitudinal outcome measures. Although pediatric DBS remains largely off-label and supported by limited high-quality evidence, it is increasingly incorporated into specialized epilepsy care. Continued progress will depend on coordinated multicenter efforts that prioritize both seizure control and long-term neurodevelopmental and psychosocial outcomes.Keywords: Epilepsy, Neuromodulation, Deep brain stimulation, Pediatrics, Neurodevelopment

Overview of Pediatric Neuromodulation for Drug-Resistant Epilepsy

Despite advances in antiseizure medications, approximately one-third of children and adolescents with epilepsy continue to experience uncontrolled seizures [1], leading to long-term neurological morbidity, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality [[2], [3], [4]]. For a subset of these individuals with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), resective or disconnective epilepsy surgery offers the potential for seizure freedom [5]. However, many children are not candidates for curative surgery because of multifocal or generalized seizure onset, involvement of eloquent cortex, or underlying genetic and developmental epileptic encephalopathies that are not amenable to resection or disconnection [6]. In this context, neuromodulation has emerged as a potential therapeutic option aimed at modulating brain regions and networks involved in seizure initiation and propagation [[7], [8], [9]]. The rapid expansion of pediatric neuromodulation technologies – ranging from established modalities supported by growing clinical evidence to novel, investigational approaches – underscores the need for a contemporary review to inform current clinical practice and identify priorities for future research.

Currently, the most common neuromodulation options for DRE in pediatric populations include extracranial devices, most notably vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), and intracranial devices, including deep brain stimulation (DBS) and responsive neurostimulation (RNS) [10]. Historically, VNS has dominated clinical practice due to its relative technical simplicity, favorable safety profile, and extensive evidence supporting clinically meaningful seizure reduction in certain pediatric populations [11]. By contrast, intracranial neuromodulation occupies a more controversial and less clearly defined role in pediatric epilepsy care. Although DBS is an established therapy for adult movement disorders [12,13] and has received regulatory approval for adult focal epilepsy targeting the anterior nucleus of the thalamus [14], its application in pediatric epilepsy has been limited by a paucity of high-quality evidence, ethical concerns, and practical challenges unique to the developing brain [15,16]. Published data consist largely of small case series, heterogeneous cohorts, and mixed indications, spanning a range of intracranial targets [7,9,17]. Outcomes are reported using inconsistent metrics, follow-up durations are variable, and neurodevelopmental endpoints are inconsistently captured [18].

Although the evidence base for DBS or RNS in treating DRE is significantly more substantial in adults, its translation to pediatric populations is not straightforward. Pediatric brains are dynamic systems undergoing continuous structural, functional, and neurochemical maturation [[19], [20], [21]]. The effects of chronic electrical stimulation on developing neural circuits are incompletely understood, raising concerns regarding long-term cognitive, behavioral, and psychosocial outcomes [[22], [23], [24]]. Relatedly, the ideal timing for DBS or RNS in pediatric populations, particularly for genetic conditions or certain epilepsy syndromes where the natural history is not known, remains elusive. Pediatric-specific surgical considerations such as variable skull thickness, hardware durability, and the potential need for repeated revisions further complicate implementation in children [25,26]. As a result, clinicians face uncertainty when considering DBS for a child with refractory seizures, particularly when weighing potential benefits against invasive neurosurgical intervention in a vulnerable population. These challenges underscore the need for a careful, evidence-informed synthesis of the current literature.

In this review, we critically examine the role of DBS in pediatric epilepsy within the broader framework of neuromodulation. We explore the neurobiological rationale, approach to target selection, emerging clinical outcomes, safety profile, and ethical considerations unique to children with DRE. By integrating mechanistic insights with clinical experience, this review seeks to clarify where pediatric DBS currently stands, where it falls short, and where it may realistically fit within future treatment algorithms for DRE. Ultimately, advancing the field will require coordinated multicenter efforts, standardized outcome measures, and long-term follow-up that prioritize not only seizure reduction, but also neurodevelopmental trajectories, quality of life, and everyday functioning.

Historical Evolution of Pediatric Deep Brain Stimulation

The development of DBS in pediatric neurology is inextricably intertwined with the broader history of functional neurosurgery and the evolution of implantable neurotechnologies in adults. In contrast to adults, however, pediatric applications of DBS have lagged behind, emerging only in recent years as a potential exploratory treatment option for neurological disorders of childhood (Fig. 1). Early stereotactic interventions in the mid-20th century, including lesioning procedures for movement disorders and epilepsy, established the effects of altering deep brain structures but were largely eschewed in children because of irreversibility and concern for neurodevelopmental consequences [[27], [28], [29]]. The emergence of chronic, adjustable electrical stimulation in the late 1980s and 1990s, however, fundamentally shifted the risk-benefit profile of functional neurosurgery and laid the groundwork for DBS as a potential intervention for neurological disorders, even in younger populations [30,31].

Historical overview of events leading to the emergence of pediatric DBS for DRE. Relative to other indications, DBS for DRE emerged relatively recently. For example, the first randomized controlled trial of DBS for DRE was published nearly a decade after regulatory approval for Parkinson’s disease, and over two decades after its initial use in movement disorders. Pediatric DBS for DRE emerged even more recently (red ticks), with the first clinical trial published in 2024. Despite encouraging early results, DBS for DRE has lagged behind its adoption in other neurological conditions, particularly in children.

Initial pediatric applications of DBS occurred not in epilepsy but in severe childhood movement disorders, particularly dystonia [[32], [33], [34]]. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, Philippe Coubes and colleagues published reports describing globus pallidus internus stimulation in children with genetic and acquired dystonia, demonstrating that DBS could be performed safely in the developing brain and could produce durable functional improvements [35,36]. These early successes were pivotal, as they challenged prevailing assumptions that implanted neurotechnologies were inherently incompatible with growth, neuroplasticity, or long-term pediatric care. Importantly, they also generated practical experience with pediatric-specific surgical considerations, including lead placement in smaller brains, hardware anchoring, and long-term device management.

The extension of DBS to epilepsy, and pediatric epilepsy in particular, emerged more cautiously. Some of the earliest work on the use of electrical stimulation for the treatment of DRE involved cerebellar stimulation, albeit with uncontrolled studies and equivocal results [37,38]. Despite these mixed outcomes, neurostimulation proved technically feasible in individuals with DRE, with its titratability and reversibility distinguishing it from ablative surgery. Surmising that mixed outcomes from cerebellar stimulation were related to the inadequacy of stimulating the cerebellar cortex through relatively small surface electrodes, Velasco and colleagues set out to identify alternate targets and focused on the thalamus based on mounting evidence from animal studies [39]. Starting in the late 1980s, Velasco and colleagues reported promising clinical outcomes with neurostimulation of the centromedian nucleus of the thalamus, performed using externalized leads and portable, external neurostimulators, including in a few adolescents [40]. While these studies laid the groundwork for contemporary approaches to DBS for DRE, a major inflection point in the field occurred with the growing recognition of epilepsy as a disorder of large-scale brain networks [[41], [42], [43]]. Advances in neuroimaging, electrophysiology, and connectomics in recent decades provided a conceptual framework that aligned naturally with DBS as a tool for modulating distributed brain networks [44,45]. This shift helped legitimize DBS as a potential therapeutic strategy for children whose epilepsies could not be localized to a single resectable focus [[46], [47], [48]]. The concurrent publication of randomized controlled data supporting thalamic neurostimulation for adult epilepsy [49,50], and the subsequent regulatory approval in adults, further catalyzed interest in pediatric translation, despite the absence of pediatric-specific trials.

Until recently, the evidence base for pediatric DBS for DRE was dominated largely by case reports and small retrospective series, where the intervention was performed under compassionate-use circumstances [7,9,15]. These studies varied widely with respect to patient age, epilepsy etiology, stimulation targets, programming parameters, and outcome metrics, thereby limiting meaningful cross-study comparisons. While some reports described clinically meaningful seizure reductions and ancillary improvements in cognition, alertness, or behavior, others demonstrated modest or inconsistent benefit. Collectively, this heterogeneity reinforced uncertainty regarding patient selection, optimal targets, and expected outcomes, and contributed to persistent skepticism about the generalizability of DBS in pediatric epilepsy. Only more recently have prospective pediatric studies begun to emerge, enabled by multicenter collaborations and registries designed to overcome the limitations of single-institution experience [51,52].

Regulatory and ethical considerations have also played an important role in shaping the pace of adoption. To date, no DBS system carries approval from the FDA for pediatric epilepsy, and nearly all pediatric usage remains off-label [53]. This has contributed to institutional variability in practice, reliance on individualized ethical review, variable funding and reimbursement patterns, and a frequent delay in the use of DBS even after exhausting other treatment options. Nevertheless, accumulating institutional experience and longer-term follow-up at select centers have repositioned DBS from an experimental, “last-resort” measure to an increasingly integrated element of pediatric epilepsy care. While pediatric DBS remains far from mainstream, its historical evolution reflects a gradual reframing from an investigational extension of adult practice to a distinct, albeit nascent, subspecialty within pediatric neurosurgery [54,55].

Neurobiology of Developmental Neuromodulation: Rationale and Controversies

The neurobiological rationale for DBS in pediatric DRE rests on an evolving understanding of epilepsy as a disorder of dysfunctional neural networks rather than a condition arising solely from discrete brain foci [56]. Seizures emerge from abnormal synchronization within distributed circuits that span cortical and subcortical structures, with the thalamus, basal ganglia, limbic system, and brainstem acting as key hubs for seizure initiation and/or propagation [57]. DBS targets many of these subcortical areas directly, positioning it as a network-level intervention rather than a focal treatment [46,58]. This network-based framework is particularly relevant since generalized, multifocal, or poorly localized seizure onsets – often in the context of genetic etiologies, malformations of cortical development, or developmental and epileptic encephalopathies – are more common among pediatric populations than adults [[59], [60], [61]]. These conditions frequently involve widespread abnormalities in neuronal connectivity and physiology [62], limiting the effectiveness of focal interventions. DBS thus offers a means of modulating pathological network dynamics in the absence of a single, resectable hypothesis.

Although the concept of using DBS to modulate epileptic networks is promising in principle, neurodevelopmental considerations and the chronicity of stimulation add critical layers of complexity in its application to children and adolescents. The pediatric brain undergoes continuous structural remodeling and functional reorganization throughout childhood and adolescence, which present both opportunities and risks (Fig. 2) [[63], [64], [65], [66]]. On the one hand, neuromodulation during development may leverage this heightened plasticity to promote more adaptive network configurations, potentially leading to sustained seizure reduction beyond periods of active stimulation [67,68]. On the other hand, chronic neurostimulation could interfere with the typical maturation of cognitive, emotional, or sensorimotor circuits, particularly when applied to subcortical hubs that subserve these processes [69,70]. In pediatric epilepsy, where disease duration often spans decades, the long-term interaction between stimulation and evolution of brain networks remains poorly understood. Furthermore, whether DBS primarily suppresses seizures through acute desynchronization, induces longer-term plastic changes, or both, remains unknown with direct implications for timing, target selection, and programming strategies in children [71,72].

Opportunities and risks of modulating the developing brain. The developing brain exhibits heightened neuroplasticity, mediated by processes such as neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, long-term potentiation, and synaptic pruning (center). This plasticity creates theoretical opportunities for neuromodulation to reshape pathological circuits, including disruption or weakening of epileptogenic networks and prevention of maladaptive connectivity between seizure foci and otherwise healthy brain regions (left). At the same time, intervention during active development carries theoretical risks, as chronic modulation of highly connected hubs may interfere with the normal maturation, refinement, or stabilization of typical neural networks (right). These competing effects underscore the dual potential of neuromodulation in pediatric populations to either promote adaptive network reorganization or inadvertently perturb healthy developmental trajectories.

Age-dependent differences in neurotransmitter systems further distinguish the pediatric context from adults. The balance between excitation and inhibition evolves over development, influenced by changes in GABAergic signaling, glutamatergic receptor composition, and neuromodulatory inputs [[73], [74], [75]]. More specifically, the developmental trajectory of GABAergic signaling is shaped in part by maturational changes in chloride transporter expression. Early in development, relatively higher expression of the sodium-potassium-chloride cotransporter NKCC1 contributes to elevated intracellular chloride concentrations, such that GABAergic signaling may be less hyperpolarizing, or even depolarizing, in immature neurons. With maturation, increasing expression of the potassium-chloride cotransporter KCC2 lowers intracellular chloride and supports the transition toward more classically inhibitory GABAergic transmission [[76], [77], [78]]. Disruptions in this process, for example due to reduced expression or altered function of the KCC2 cotransporter, can increase intracellular chloride, resulting in paradoxical enhancement of neuronal activity and synchronization of epileptiform networks with GABA signaling [[79], [80], [81]]. Developmental maturation of neurotransmitter systems can therefore influence seizure susceptibility, network synchrony, and the response of immature circuits to electrical stimulation [82]. Accordingly, stimulation paradigms optimized in adults may not produce equivalent physiological or behavioral effects in children, particularly across developmental windows characterized by rapid changes in inhibitory signaling.

Taken together, the neurobiological rationale for DBS in pediatric epilepsy is compelling but remains incomplete. Although it is grounded in network neuroscience and supported by preclinical and adult clinical data, the developing brain introduces uncertainties that demand caution and underscore the need for pediatric-specific mechanistic studies. A clear understanding of how DBS interacts with maturing neural circuits is essential to refining its role as a therapeutic strategy for children and adolescents with DRE.

Deep Brain Stimulation Targets for Pediatric Epilepsy

Unlike resective surgery, where localization of a discrete epileptogenic zone is paramount, DBS ostensibly targets subcortical structures that function as hubs within seizure-related networks. Currently, these targets are typically chosen based on their anatomical connectivity, physiological role in synchrony and excitability, and prior evidence from animal models, adult epilepsy trials, and limited pediatric experience (Table 1) [8,83]. In children, target selection also requires consideration of developmental implications and pediatric-specific epilepsy syndromes [72], though this is often limited by the scarcity of systematically acquired outcome data (see Table 2).

Anterior nucleus of the thalamus

The anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) is among the most extensively studied DBS targets for epilepsy, and the only brain region currently approved for stimulation by regulatory bodies in North America and Europe [84]. Neuroanatomically, the ANT occupies a key position within the limbic circuit, receiving inputs from the mammillary bodies and projecting to cingulate and prefrontal cortices [85] (Fig. 3). Through these connections, it is thought to participate in seizure propagation and network synchronization, particularly in focal epilepsies with frontal, temporal and limbic involvement [[85], [86], [87]]. Stimulation of the ANT is thought to disrupt this pathological synchrony and reduce the likelihood of seizure generalization rather than directly suppress seizure onset [88].

Connectivity of thalamic targets for DBS in the treatment of drug-resistant epilepsy. The anterior nucleus of the thalamus (ANT) is a key node of the limbic circuit, receiving afferent inputs from the mammillary bodies via the mammillothalamic tract and from hippocampal-parahippocampal regions, and projecting predominantly to the cingulate gyrus, retrosplenial cortex, and medial prefrontal cortex through the cingulum bundle. Through these connections, ANT stimulation is thought to modulate limbic network synchrony and seizure propagation in focal epilepsies. In contrast, the centromedian nucleus of the thalamus (CM) is embedded within diffuse thalamocortical and cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical networks. The CM receives ascending neuromodulatory inputs from brainstem nuclei and to a lesser extent from the basal ganglia, and sends widespread projections to frontal, premotor, and sensorimotor cortices, as well as to the striatum. These broad connections position the CM as a regulator of arousal, attention, and generalized cortical synchrony, supporting its role as a DBS target in generalized and multifocal drug-resistant epilepsy.

Evidence supporting ANT-DBS derives primarily from adult clinical trials, most notably the pivotal Stimulation of the Anterior Nucleus of the Thalamus for Epilepsy (SANTE) trial [49] – a multicenter, randomized, double-blind study of 110 adults with long-standing drug-resistant focal epilepsy. During the 3-month blinded phase, active stimulation produced a 29% greater reduction in seizure frequency compared with controls, with unadjusted median seizure reductions of 40.4% in the stimulated group versus 14.5% in controls. Benefits were most pronounced for focal seizures with impaired awareness and patient-identified “most severe” seizures, and seizure-related injuries were significantly less frequent in the stimulated cohort (7% vs 26%). By 2 years, the median seizure reduction reached 56%, with 54% of patients achieving ≥50% seizure reduction and approximately 13% experiencing seizure-free intervals of at least 6 months. Long-term follow-up from these cohorts has shown progressive improvement over time [50,89]. At 7 years, median seizure frequency reduction increased to 75%, with a 74% responder rate, suggesting cumulative or plastic effects of chronic ANT stimulation. Real-world data from the Medtronic Registry for Epilepsy (MORE), which included 170 adults treated with ANT-DBS across 25 centers in 13 countries, reinforced these findings in routine clinical practice [90]. At 2 years follow-up in MORE, median seizure frequency was reduced by 33%, with a 32% responder rate, increasing to 55% median reduction and a 53% responder rate among patients followed for 5 years. Together, these studies have established ANT-DBS as a viable network-targeted therapy in adults with focal epilepsy.

Although high-quality evidence in adults has strongly influenced pediatric practice, there are currently no pediatric-specific trials, and thus, ANT stimulation in this population remains extrapolative and off-label [91]. Most reports of ANT-DBS in pediatric populations involve adolescents with long-standing focal or multifocal epilepsy who were not candidates for resection and had failed multiple prior therapies, including VNS [84,92]. Case series describe variable but often meaningful reductions in seizure frequency, with some patients demonstrating improvements in seizure severity and quality of life rather than complete seizure control [7,9,93]. However, outcomes are inconsistent, and younger children are underrepresented. Limitations of the existing evidence base for pediatric use of ANT-DBS include small sample sizes, predominantly retrospective evidence, heterogeneous epilepsy syndromes, and reliance on adult-derived stimulation parameters that may not optimally engage developing networks.

From a pediatric neurobiological perspective, stimulating the ANT presents both unique strengths and challenges. Its central role in limbic circuitry makes it attractive for network modulation, but its proximity to circuits involved in memory, attention, and affect raises concerns about cognitive and behavioral effects during development. While adult data suggest relative cognitive safety, long-term pediatric-specific neuropsychological outcomes remain to be characterized.

Centromedian nucleus of the thalamus

The centromedian nucleus (CM) of the thalamus has emerged as a particularly relevant target for pediatric epilepsy, especially for generalized epilepsies and epileptic encephalopathies such as Lennox-Gastaut syndrome [40,[94], [95], [96], [97]]. Unlike the ANT, which is more closely linked to limbic networks, the CM has widespread projections to frontal, sensorimotor, and parietal cortices and the basal ganglia, positioning it as a regulator of arousal and diffuse cortical synchrony (Fig. 3) [98,99]. Its involvement in attention, vigilance, and motor control aligns closely with the clinical features of generalized and multifocal seizures, making it particularly well-suited for these semiologies [99].

Similar to ANT-DBS, the initial rationale for the use of CM-DBS in pediatric populations was extrapolated from adult studies. The most notable of these was the Electrical Stimulation of Thalamus for Epilepsy of Lennox-Gastaut phenotype (ESTEL) trial, a double-blind, randomized study of CM-DBS in young adults with Lennox–Gastaut syndrome [94]. Investigators found a trend toward greater seizure reduction based on seizure diaries among the active stimulation group compared to controls, with 50% of stimulated participants experiencing ≥50% seizure reduction compared to 22% of controls, although this difference was not statistically significant. They did, however, note a significant difference in changes in electrographic seizures between the groups, with 59% of the stimulation group experiencing ≥50% reduction at the end of the blinded phase compared with none of the controls. Despite variability in outcomes, the significant benefit observed in many participants provided the first high-quality evidence supporting the potential of CM-DBS in generalized epilepsy, laying the foundation for translation to pediatrics.

Unlike other DBS targets, however, CM stimulation is supported by greater pediatric-specific evidence [93]. The recently published Add-On Deep Brain Stimulation versus Continued Vagus Nerve Stimulation for Childhood Epilepsy (ADVANCE) study [100], a prospective, non-blinded, partially randomized patient preference trial, was the first clinical trial of thalamic DBS for DRE in pediatric populations. All participants in the ADVANCE trial had previously undergone at least 1 year of VNS optimization without meaningful seizure reduction, and all but one patient assigned to the treatment arm underwent CM-DBS for the treatment of generalized seizures (the remaining single patient underwent ANT-DBS). Altogether, the study reported 51.9% reduction in seizure frequency in the add-on DBS group, which was significantly greater than the 12.3% reduction observed with ongoing VNS optimization alone. Furthermore, during a 1-year open-label follow-up phase, 3 children in the continued VNS arm elected to undergo DBS implantation (in all cases targeting the CM) and demonstrated on average 66.1% reduction in seizure frequency. This provided the first high quality evidence of the efficacy of CM-DBS in pediatric populations.

These findings are supported by a recent prospective observational cohort study of 22 children who underwent CM-DBS as part of the Child & Youth CompreHensIve Longitudinal Database for Deep Brain Stimulation (CHILD-DBS) [101]. Participants exhibited a bimodal distribution in seizure outcomes, with 10 children experiencing substantial benefit (mean reduction of 66.7%), one exhibiting modest improvement (37.5% reduction), and 11 children experiencing no change at 1-year follow-up [101]. Interestingly, most patients (73%) in this cohort experienced a clinically important reduction in seizure severity, including six children who had no change in seizure frequency, emphasizing the importance of multimodal evaluation of treatment outcomes after CM-DBS. Children who experienced a reduction in seizure frequency also exhibited improvements in overall quality of life, as well as school attendance, one year after DBS.

Importantly, CM stimulation may have cognitive and behavioral effects as well, although these outcomes are variably reported. Multiple studies describe improvements in alertness and attention after CM-DBS [102,103], potentially reflecting reduced seizure burden or modulation of arousal networks, while some report the onset of new aggressive behavior with stimulation [70]. Other studies note minimal cognitive change [104], underscoring the difficulty of disentangling stimulation effects from disease severity and developmental trajectory. In addition, short-term stimulation-related symptoms, including paresthesias, nonspecific somatosensory sensations, disequilibrium, or changes in arousal, may also occur and often require contact review or parameter adjustment [26,101,105,106]. These effects are particularly important in children with limited communication, in whom caregiver observations may be the earliest indicator of stimulation-related adverse effects.

Altogether, mounting prospective evidence consistently suggests that CM-DBS may be particularly effective in reducing seizure frequency and severity in generalized epilepsy syndromes, and can impart meaningful benefits in quality of life and daily functioning – sometimes even in the absence of a reduction in seizure burden.

Hippocampus and limbic targets

Although contemporary approaches to intracranial neuromodulation for DRE in both adult and pediatric populations have increasingly converged on thalamic targets as described above, several experimental approaches have also explored chronic stimulation of deep or mesial cortical structures [107]. While these areas are not canonical deep gray targets for DBS, they warrant brief discussion because of their potential relevance for select individuals, as well as pediatric-specific concerns regarding cognition, memory, and emotional regulation in the context of ongoing neurodevelopment.

Prominent among these exploratory stimulation targets are the hippocampus and related limbic structures, investigated primarily in the context of mesial temporal lobe epilepsy [108]. The hippocampus plays a central role in seizure generation in this syndrome, making it an intuitively appealing target. In adults, hippocampal RNS [[109], [110], [111], [112], [113]] and DBS [[114], [115], [116], [117], [118]] have demonstrated seizure reduction in select patients, particularly when resection is contraindicated due to bilateral involvement or memory concerns. In pediatric epilepsy, however, hippocampal neuromodulation is used sparingly. Mesial temporal epilepsy is uncommon in young children, and the hippocampus is critical for memory consolidation and emotional development during childhood and adolescence. The risk of disrupting normal neurodevelopmental processes has limited enthusiasm for chronic stimulation of this structure in younger patients. Reported pediatric cases are rare and typically involve older adolescents with well-characterized temporal lobe epilepsy [[119], [120], [121], [122]].

When employed, hippocampal neurostimulation may offer seizure reduction while preserving tissue integrity compared with resection. In contemporary practice, RNS may be particularly attractive when chronic intracranial recordings could help lateralize bilateral mesial temporal seizures [123], track seizure burden [124,125], or guide subsequent resection or ablation [126]. DBS, by contrast, may be considered when the goal is continuous or scheduled modulation of a mesial temporal seizure network, when reliable event detection is less central to the therapeutic strategy, or when institutional experience supports this approach [118,119,127,128]. Although data are still accumulating, preliminary meta-analyses suggest comparable results between RNS and DBS targeting the mesial temporal lobe [129,130]. Nonetheless, concerns about long-term memory outcomes, emotional regulation, and the interaction between stimulation and ongoing hippocampal maturation remain substantial barriers to adoption in pediatric populations [131,132].

Other limbic and paralimbic seizure-network targets, including the amygdala, insula, and cingulate, have also been considered in selected cases, particularly when seizures arise from or propagate through distributed mesial or operculo-insular networks [[133], [134], [135]]. However, pediatric evidence for chronic stimulation of these structures remains extremely limited, and their proximity to networks supporting affective regulation, interoception, salience processing, language, and cognition raises additional developmental concerns. At present, these targets should be considered purely investigational in children and are best examined within individualized, network-based, hypothesis-driven surgical planning.

Emerging and experimental targets

Beyond established thalamic and limbic targets, there is growing interest in alternative subcortical structures implicated in seizure modulation. These include the subthalamic nucleus, pulvinar, and components of the basal ganglia, each of which participates in cortico-subcortical loops influencing excitability and synchronization [107]. Evidence for these targets in pediatric epilepsy largely consists of isolated cases or extrapolation from adult and preclinical studies.

The application of network-guided and connectomic approaches to target selection is an emerging area of ongoing interest [46,133]. Advances in diffusion imaging and functional connectivity analysis have enabled more individualized mapping of seizure networks, raising the possibility that DBS targets could be selected based on patient-specific network topology rather than fixed anatomical landmarks [136]. This approach is particularly appealing in pediatric epilepsy, where etiologies and network architectures are highly heterogeneous.

Open-loop, Adaptive, and Responsive Neuromodulation in the Developing Connectome

An increasingly important question in pediatric intracranial neuromodulation is whether developing brains are best served by continuous or duty-cycled open-loop stimulation, adaptive DBS, or fully responsive stimulation. Conventional DBS delivers stimulation independent of real-time seizure detection, either continuously or according to a programmed duty cycle [137]. This may be advantageous for generalized, multifocal, or rapidly propagating epilepsies in which seizures are difficult to detect reliably from one or two recording sites, and where modulation of a central hub such as the CM or ANT may remodel seizure propagation networks [[138], [139], [140], [141], [142], [143]]. In children with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, open-loop thalamic DBS may therefore be attractive when the therapeutic goal is broad modulation of thalamocortical synchrony rather than interruption of a discrete seizure focus [101,144]. However, conventional DBS also raises concerns in the context of targeting developing populations. In this paradigm, electrical stimulation is agnostic to real-time neural activity, meaning that developing circuits may be exposed to chronic stimulation during periods of normal learning, sleep, or emotional regulation, potentially interfering with typical brain maturation. The clinical significance of this exposure remains uncertain, but it underscores the need for longitudinal neuropsychological monitoring and developmentally relevant outcome measures [23,53]. Potential adverse effects may include changes in attention, arousal, sleep, mood, memory, or behavior, although these are difficult to distinguish from effects of seizure burden, antiseizure medications, and underlying neurodevelopmental disorder [102,145,146].

Responsive neurostimulation offers a complementary strategy. RNS detects electrographic activity and delivers stimulation in response to predefined patterns, while also providing chronic intracranial recordings that may clarify seizure laterality, temporal dynamics, treatment response, and network evolution over time [147]. These features may be particularly useful in focal, bilateral mesial temporal, eloquent cortical, or multifocal epilepsies with one or two dominant nodes [110,111,[148], [149], [150], [151]]. In children, RNS may also offer theoretical developmental advantages by reducing unnecessary stimulation outside periods of epileptiform activity [[152], [153], [154], [155]]. Compared with conventional open-loop DBS, RNS is intentionally designed around epilepsy-relevant sensing, detection, and responsive stimulation paradigms. The real-time, longitudinal, and actionable feedback acquired through RNS can help enhance epilepsy care in developing populations, where seizure networks and developmental state evolve over time. Notably, emerging state-dependent analyses suggest that the therapeutic effects of RNS may not depend solely on stimulation during high-risk epileptiform periods, but may also involve stimulation delivered during lower-risk network states [156]. Sensing-enabled DBS may also offer a promising avenue for acquiring clinically meaningful neural data in epilepsy and other pediatric neurological disorders, although its clinical efficacy beyond continuous or cyclic stimulation remains to be determined.

Practically, the relative utility and indications of thalamic-targeted DBS and RNS remain uncertain [157]. Emerging reports suggest that both open-loop and responsive stimulation of the thalamus are feasible and effective in treating generalized and multifocal epilepsy [100,101,158,159]. Whether the continuous modulation of thalamocortical synchrony of DBS, compared to the sensing-enabled features of RNS, translates into meaningful differences in seizure reduction, neurodevelopmental safety, cognitive or behavioral outcomes, programming burden, or durability remains unknown. Ongoing prospective studies of responsive thalamocortical stimulation in Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and related developmental and epileptic encephalopathies [160], together with multicenter efforts to compile retrospective and prospective comparative data, may help clarify whether these modalities offer distinct benefits or instead act through overlapping thalamocortical network mechanisms.

Clinical Indications and Patient Selection

Appropriate patient selection is a critical determinant of outcome for DBS in pediatric DRE. Given the invasive nature of the therapy, the limited pediatric-specific evidence base, and the ethical complexity of implanting neuromodulatory devices in developing brains, DBS must be reserved for carefully selected patients in whom the potential benefits clearly outweigh the risks. Unlike adult epilepsy, where indications for ANT-DBS are increasingly standardized, pediatric indications remain individualized and highly context-dependent.

Candidates for pediatric DBS meet criteria for drug-resistant epilepsy, defined as failure of adequate trials of two or more appropriately chosen and tolerated antiseizure medications [161], and should generally be considered only after a comprehensive evaluation has excluded alternative, curative surgical options [6]. Failure or intolerance of other neuromodulatory therapies also weighs heavily in the decision-making process. In particular, most pediatric clinicians would recommend upfront VNS rather than DBS due to its lower surgical risk and established pediatric safety profile [72,162]. Lack of meaningful seizure reduction with VNS, or plateauing benefit after an adequate trial, commonly precedes consideration of DBS [157].

Certain epilepsy syndromes appear more biologically and clinically suited to treatment with DBS. Children with generalized epilepsies, developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, and syndromes characterized by diffuse network involvement, such as Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, are frequently considered candidates, particularly when seizures are dominated by bilateral tonic clonic or atonic events that are disabling and injury-prone [6,101]. Multifocal epilepsies and bilateral temporal lobe epilepsies may also be appropriate indications when resection would carry unacceptable functional risk [91]. In contrast, children with well-localized focal epilepsies amenable to surgical cure are generally poor candidates for DBS, given the superior outcomes associated with resection or ablation.

Neurodevelopmental function and the presence of psychiatric or neurocognitive comorbidities are also important considerations. Severe cognitive impairment or behavioral dysregulation does not preclude DBS, and in some cases may strengthen the appeal of neuromodulation if seizure burden is contributing to developmental stagnation or regression. Although some clinicians may be concerned about the feasibility of surgery and long-term device management in children with severe cognitive or behavioral concerns, emerging evidence has demonstrated that DBS is safe, feasible, and effective for the treatment of severe behavioral disturbances comorbid with pediatric neurodevelopmental conditions, including profound autism [105] and drug-resistant epilepsy [163].

Conversely, in children with relatively preserved cognition, the potential impact of DBS on attention, mood, and learning requires careful consideration. In adult cohorts, long-term follow-up from the SANTE trial suggests that objective cognitive performance and depressive symptom scores are generally stable over time [164,165]. Notably, some patients did report subjective memory decline or mood changes as adverse events, but these were generally not accompanied by objective, long-term neurobehavioral changes. This underscores the importance of holistic, multimodal longitudinal assessments, including both qualitative patient and caregiver reports and objective metrics, especially in still developing patients. In practice, this supports a protocolized approach to monitoring neuropsychological effects of DBS in pediatric populations that includes: (i) pre-operative neuropsychological testing with attentional, memory-related, and executive function measures; (ii) routine post-surgical screening for new cognitive concerns as well as possible mood and anxiety symptoms; and (iii) systematic monitoring of functional outcomes (e.g. school attendance, academic functioning, participation in recreational activities) to detect changes that may not be captured on clinical instruments.

Given these complexities, decisions regarding pediatric DBS should never be made in isolation. Multidisciplinary epilepsy surgery conferences provide the appropriate forum for integrating neurological, neurosurgical, neuropsychological, psychiatric, and ethical perspectives. Such conferences allow for critical appraisal of alternatives, alignment of expectations, and consensus-building among clinicians and families. In the absence of robust guidelines, this multidisciplinary deliberation remains the cornerstone of responsible patient selection for DBS in pediatric epilepsy.

Surgical, Technical, and Programming Considerations in Children

The surgical and technical aspects of DBS in children differ in important ways from adult practice, reflecting both anatomical constraints and the substantially longer period of time over which implanted devices must function. Successful DBS in pediatric populations requires anticipation not only of immediate operative risks, but also of how growth, development, and disease evolution will interact with implanted hardware and chronic stimulation over many years.

Anatomical considerations include differences in head size, skull thickness, and brain volume, all of which vary substantially across childhood [25]. Younger children present particular challenges, including thinner calvaria for applying stereotactic frames and anchoring hardware and shorter distances between cortical and subcortical structures [[166], [167], [168]]. Ongoing brain growth sometimes raises concerns about lead migration or changes in relative target position over time [169,170], although in our experience clinically significant displacement appears to be uncommon when leads are well secured. Nonetheless, growth-related changes reinforce the need for meticulous surgical planning and long-term surveillance.

Accurate lead placement is essential, particularly when targeting poorly demarcated nuclei such as the centromedian thalamus. Given variability in age-related brain development and head sizes in pediatric populations, surgical targeting relies predominantly on direct targeting using high-resolution pre-surgical MRI [8,106,171], rather than indirect targeting approaches derived from adult experience [172]. Novel sequences, including fast gray matter acquisition T1 inversion recovery (FGATIR) or magnetization-prepared 2 rapid acquisition gradient echo (MP2RAGE) sequences enhance edge detection and facilitate targeting [173]. In contrast to adult movement disorder surgery, microelectrode recording is used inconsistently in pediatric epilepsy, reflecting concerns about added operative time, limited pediatric normative data, and less well-defined electrophysiological signatures of epilepsy targets, particularly under general anesthesia. As a result, imaging accuracy and careful anatomical localization are paramount. Advances in tractography and connectivity-informed targeting hold promise but remain technically demanding and variably available.

Hardware selection must account for the realities of long-term implantation in growing patients. Implantable pulse generators are typically placed in subclavicular subcutaneous pockets, although subfascial pockets can be used in very small children to reduce the risk of wound dehiscence [168]. Battery longevity is also an important issue, as the risk of replacements increase cumulatively. Rechargeable systems may mitigate this burden by offering a longer battery life, increasing the interval between replacements from 2 to 5 years to >10 years [174,175]. Rechargeable systems also carry a smaller profile and longer battery life, which can reduce the risk of hardware erosion over the chest wall and the cumulative burden of multiple battery-replacement surgeries – a particular concern in young children [176]. Notably, cranially implanted RNS systems avoid a chest-wall pulse generator altogether, further mitigating these concerns. Although studies in adults have demonstrated high satisfaction rates [177,178] and cost-effectiveness [179,180] with rechargeable IPGs, the feasibility and acceptability in pediatric populations remains unexplored.

Programming DBS in children is both technically and conceptually challenging, largely employing parameters adapted from adult experiences. Clinicians must balance seizure reduction against potential effects on cognition, mood, sleep, and behavior, often requiring slow, iterative adjustments [70,145,181]. Compared to adults, relatively lower amplitudes and conservative titration are commonly employed initially, particularly in younger children, with gradual escalation guided by clinical response rather than predefined targets. Parameter selection may be further complicated by age-dependent neurobiology, as aforementioned. Small changes in parameters can have exaggerated effects on arousal, attention, or affect in children, which may be difficult to capture in children with intellectual disabilities or developmental and epileptic encephalopathies. As a result, stimulation adjustments require close monitoring and collaboration between neurologists, neuropsychologists, and families. Objective outcome measures should be supplemented by caregiver reports, particularly in children with limited communication.

Another important consideration in pediatric DBS is the use of continuous vs. cyclic stimulation protocols. Presently, DBS programming for DRE often involves scheduled intermittent (“duty-cycled”) stimulation rather than continuous protocols, mirroring paradigms used in seminal clinical trials (e.g., the SANTE regimen of 1 min ON/5 min OFF at high frequency) [181]. Duty-cycling offers lower total charge delivery than continuous stimulation at the same amplitude/frequency, which in some cases may improve tolerability and can materially prolong battery longevity – a nontrivial issue in children given multiple expected device replacements over the lifespan. However, our institutional experience favors continuous stimulation, which could offer an advantage in seizure reduction [182,183]. Direct head-to-head comparisons are sparse and often confounded by target choice, contact location, and simultaneous adjustment of multiple parameters. In practice, clinicians may trial adjustments to duty cycle (e.g., increasing ON time, shortening OFF time, or moving to continuous stimulation) when seizure control is suboptimal or in the presence of stimulation-related adverse events.

Finally, long-term device management represents an especially unique aspect of pediatric DBS compared to many adult indications. Children implanted at a young age may live with DBS systems for decades, requiring multiple battery replacements, potential lead revisions, and ongoing reprogramming as seizures, development, and life circumstances evolve. Transition of care from pediatric to adult epilepsy services introduces additional complexity, emphasizing the need for robust documentation, continuity planning, and patient education. Ultimately, the technical success of pediatric DBS depends not only on surgical precision, but on sustained, multidisciplinary engagement across the lifespan.

Safety of Pediatric Deep Brain Stimulation for Epilepsy

DBS implantation in children has historically been considered higher risk than adults. However, in a large prospective multi-center analysis of 130 children and adolescents, we demonstrated that surgical risks are disproportionally concentrated among patients treated for dystonia rather than epilepsy [26]. Among children treated for epilepsy, the prevalence of major (actionable) complications was 4.3%. Minor complications occurred in 15% and included superficial surgical site infections managed with oral antibiotics, high lead impedance that resolved with device reprogramming, incidental intracranial hemorrhage detected on imaging but not requiring intervention, a transient perioperative increase in seizure frequency, and postoperative headache with nausea and vomiting. In contrast, both major and minor complication rates were significantly higher among children treated for movement disorders.

In a separate, retrospective study of 54 participants who underwent intracranial neurostimulation (either DBS or RNS) for DRE, 6 out of 54 (11.1%) patients experienced post-operative complications [184]. Of these, 5 required surgical revision or device explantation. No patient in this series experienced hemorrhage, lead malposition, device malfunction, or long-term stimulation-induced sensory or cognitive adverse effects. Altogether, the available data suggest that DBS for DRE is safe and feasible in pediatric patients, with complication rates that are comparable to adults.

Importantly, the potential adverse effects of DBS on children and youth extend beyond perioperative, device-related, or stimulation-associated complications. Chronic neuromodulation during childhood has raised concerns about how sustained alteration of neural activity may influence identity formation, emotional regulation, and social development [22]. While these concerns are often raised in ethical discussions, the current empirical data suggest that DBS may restore conditions for development by improving quality of life and daily functioning [23]. Whether or not these findings translate into durable developmental gains remains uncertain.

Ethical Considerations in Pediatric Deep Brain Stimulation

Issues of autonomy and consent are particularly complex in pediatric DBS [185]. Children often lack capacity to provide informed consent, placing responsibility on caregivers to make decisions on their behalf. Informed consent in this context must extend beyond surgical risk to encompass the palliative nature of DBS, uncertainty regarding benefit, and the long-term commitment to device management. When developmentally appropriate, assent from the child should be actively sought, recognizing that understanding may evolve over time. Ongoing dialogue is therefore essential, as the child’s capacity for participation may change with maturation.

Ethical considerations also include questions of equity and access [185]. DBS is resource-intensive, requiring specialized surgical expertise, advanced imaging, and long-term multidisciplinary follow-up. Access is therefore unevenly distributed, often limited to tertiary centers in high-resource settings. Families from disadvantaged backgrounds may face additional barriers related to travel, time off work, or the demands of device maintenance, which can influence both candidacy and long-term success.

Finally, the concept of long-term responsibility is central to ethical decision-making in pediatric DBS. Implantation commits not only the child but also caregivers and healthcare systems to years or decades of follow-up, revisions, and reassessment. Clinicians must consider whether appropriate infrastructure exists to support this commitment and whether transition to adult care can be reliably managed [185]. Ethical use of DBS in pediatric epilepsy therefore depends not only on technical feasibility, but on sustained engagement across the lifespan.

Future Directions

The future of DBS in pediatric epilepsy will be shaped by advances in implantable neurostimulation technology, network neuroscience, and a broader shift toward precision-based medicine. At present, pediatric DBS remains largely exploratory and extrapolative, lagging behind innovations in adult functional neurosurgery. Moving beyond this phase will require rethinking both how stimulation is delivered and how candidates are selected, studied, and followed over time in a pediatric-specific context. Emerging and ongoing pediatric-specific clinical trials, such as the Children’s Adaptive Deep brain stimulation for Epilepsy Trial (CADET; ClinicalTrials.Gov Identifier: NCT06924086), will be critical in answering these lingering questions about pediatric DBS for DRE [18].

The evolution from continuous, open-loop systems toward closed-loop or adaptive DBS paradigms will also shape the future of DBS in children with DRE. Adaptive DBS seeks to modulate stimulation in response to physiological markers of seizure susceptibility or network state, potentially reducing unnecessary stimulation and ostensibly side-effects [186,187]. This approach is especially attractive in pediatric epilepsy, where seizure patterns, network organization, and medication regimens often change with development. However, implementation faces substantial hurdles. Reliable biomarkers suitable for deep brain targets have not been established in children, and seizure-related signals may differ across ages and epilepsy syndromes. In trials of responsive neurostimulation targeting thalamic structures, outcomes are comparable to those of open-loop stimulation [155], and may in fact be driven by stimulation during periods with less epileptiform activity [156]. Acquiring seizure data from subcortical structures such as the thalamus, for example through the emerging practice of thalamic-targeted stereoelectroencephalography [188,189], may enable the identification of reliable biomarkers to inform adaptive DBS in this population. Importantly, the relative roles of continuous or duty-cycled open-loop DBS, adaptive DBS, and responsive neurostimulation remain uncertain in pediatric DRE, and may differ between distinct sub-populations. Well-designed comparative studies will be critical to determine whether specific epilepsy syndromes, developmental stages, or network architectures are better suited to certain stimulation approaches.

Another important focus of opportunity is network-informed targeting using connectomics. Increasingly sophisticated diffusion and functional imaging techniques suggest the possibility of moving beyond atlas-based targeting toward individualized network models. In this framework, DBS targets are selected not simply for their anatomical location, but for their position within a patient-specific seizure network [136]. This may include conventional, non-invasive neuroimaging, including diffusion tensor imaging and functional magnetic resonance imaging [46,48,190,191], but also more novel, emerging approaches using invasive monitoring. For example, SEEG can provide complementary information through ictal onset and propagation patterns, interictal network activity, resting-state intracranial connectivity, and stimulation-evoked responses [136,141,[192], [193], [194], [195], [196], [197], [198], [199]]. These electrophysiological approaches may help identify network hubs, propagation patterns, or thalamocortical nodes that are not apparent from structural or functional imaging alone. Such approaches are particularly relevant in pediatric epilepsy, where heterogeneous etiologies and diffuse network abnormalities are common. Connectomic analyses may also help explain variability in DBS response and guide parameter selection [97,143,200]. Future network-guided DBS paradigms will likely integrate multimodal neuroimaging with invasive electrophysiology when clinically available.

Progress in pediatric DBS will also depend on improved clinical evidence. Traditional randomized controlled trials are difficult to conduct due to small patient numbers, absence of equipoise, and syndrome heterogeneity. As a result, prospective multicenter registries are likely to play a pivotal role in advancing the field. Well-designed registries can capture real-world outcomes, enable longer follow-up, and support comparative effectiveness analyses across targets and stimulation paradigms. Standardization of outcome measures, particularly for neurodevelopment, cognition, and quality of life metrics, is essential if such data are to be interpretable and clinically useful.

Together, these future directions underscore both the promise and the unresolved complexity of DBS in pediatric epilepsy. Advancing the field will require coordinated, multidisciplinary efforts that prioritize mechanistic insight, longitudinal data, and ethical responsibility alongside technological innovation.
