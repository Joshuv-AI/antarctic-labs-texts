Development and validation of KASP markers for powdery mildew resistance in Korean naked barley using QTL-seq
Authors: Jin-Cheon Park, Young-Mi Yoon, Chang-Hyun Lee, On-Sook Hur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPowdery mildew (PM), caused by Blumeria graminis f. sp. hordei, is a destructive fungal disease affecting barley (Hordeum vulgare L.), particularly under warm and humid conditions. Developing resistant cultivars through molecular marker-assisted selection (MAS) represents an eco-friendly and sustainable strategy for disease control. However, validated molecular markers for PM resistance in naked barley remain limited. In this study, quantitative trait locus-sequencing (QTL-seq) was conducted using two independent parental combinations, Nurichal (susceptible) and Jeonju144 (resistant), to identify genomic regions associated with PM resistance in Korean naked barley. A consistent QTL region was detected on chromosome 1 H, within which 40 Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR (KASP) markers were developed based on gene-associated sequence variants. Stepwise validation using an F₂ segregating population (n = 244) identified two markers, NPM32 and NPM43, that showed strong and consistent associations with PM resistance. Among them, NPM43 exhibited complete concordance between genotype and phenotype in the evaluated population, indicating its high diagnostic reliability. The associated sequence variant was located within an uncharacterized gene in the barley reference genome. Validation of the KASP marker NPM43 revealed stable performance and a strong association with PM resistance, highlighting its potential utility for MAS in breeding PM-resistant naked barley. This study provides useful genomic resources for marker-based breeding and supports the development of precision breeding strategies for disease resistance in barley.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11032-026-01649-6.Keywords: Naked barley, Powdery mildew, Resistance, QTL-seq, KASP marker, Marker-assisted selection

Introduction

Barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) is among the earliest domesticated cereal crops and ranks fourth in global production after maize, wheat, and rice (Wang et al. 2019; FAO, 2023). Barley is cultivated worldwide for multiple purposes, including use as an animal feed, malting, and human consumption. Approximately 60% global barley production is used for livestock feed, approximately 40% for malting and brewing, and only a small portion for direct human consumption (Ullrich 2010; Ross et al. 2023). Hulled barley, which retains the lemma and palea after threshing, is the predominant type cultivated worldwide. In contrast, naked barley (also referred to as hulless barley) is widely consumed as a staple food in several Asian and North African regions owing to its easy dehulling and relatively high nutritional properties (Abdelazim et al. 2019; Meints et al. 2021). In the Republic of Korea, naked barley is mainly consumed as a mixed grain in daily diets. In 2025, its cultivation area reached 13,384 ha, accounting for more than half of the total barley-cultivation area (25,234 ha) (KOSIS, 2025). Therefore, naked barley plays a crucial role in the Korean barley industry and is represented as an important cereal crop for human consumption.

Powdery mildew (PM), caused by the obligate biotrophic fungus Blumeria graminis f. sp. hordei, is a major disease affecting barley (Dreiseitl 2011). PM infection is favored by moderate temperatures of approximately 15 °C and high humidity, under which fungal conidia germinate and colonize the leaf surface (Bilstein-Schloemer, Bocianowski 2014; Tratwal, Bocianowski 2014; Bilstein-Schloemer et al. 2025). During the early stages of infection, host-derived invertase activity is transiently suppressed owing to interference of fungal effectors with plant sucrose metabolism, thereby disturbing normal accumulation of photosynthetic assimilates (Kuska et al. 2018). This metabolic disruption impairs energy utilization and negatively influences photosynthetic processes. Subsequent formation of fungal hyphae and conidia on the leaf surface further reduces chlorophyll content and photosynthetic efficiency, ultimately resulting in yield loss (Tian et al. 2024). In addition, PM infection often causes lodging and deterioration of grain quality, leading to yield reductions by 5%–10% on average and exceeding 30% under severe epidemic conditions (Mansour et al. 2023; Marzani et al. 2023). In the Republic of Korea, PM frequently occurs under warm and humid spring conditions, and recent climate trends have accelerated its spread, posing an increasing threat to stable barley production (Jeong et al. 2024).

Various genes conferring resistance to PM, including Mla, Mlk, and Mlg, have been identified in barley; nevertheless, the effectiveness often varies depending on the host genetic background and the virulence spectrum of pathogen races. In contrast, mlo has demonstrated long-lasting and broad-spectrum resistance across multiple barley genotypes for several decades (Dreiseitl 2025). Although fungicide application remains a common and effective approach for controlling fungal diseases and maintaining grain quality, their excessive use has accelerated the emergence of fungicide-resistant pathogen populations and raised environmental and health-related concerns. Consequently, the development of resistant cultivars through the deployment of resistance genes is considered a sustainable and economically efficient strategy for managing fungal diseases such as PM (Dracatos et al. 2023). Extensive efforts have been made to identify genetic variations associated with PM resistance, leading to the development of several molecular markers, including cleaved amplified polymorphic sequence (CAPS) (Řepková et al. 2009; Park et al. 2025) and restriction fragment length polymorphism (Kurth et al. 2001). However, most previous studies have focused primarily on hulled or malting barley, whereas research on naked barley remains limited, highlighting the need for further investigation to identify novel resistance loci and develop molecular markers applicable to naked barley backgrounds for effective resistance breeding.

Quantitative trait locus sequencing (QTL-seq) is an analytical approach that combines bulked segregant analysis (BSA) with whole-genome resequencing (WGS) using next-generation sequencing technology (Takagi et al. 2013). BSA is a simple, rapid, and cost-effective method for identifying trait-associated loci by comparing allele frequency differences between two contrasting phenotypic bulks, such as resistant and susceptible groups (Majeed et al. 2022). QTL-seq is typically applied to segregating populations such as F₂, recombinant inbred lines, or doubled haploid lines, in which individuals exhibiting extreme phenotypes are selected and pooled into two bulks to enhance the efficiency of QTL detection (Topcu et al. 2024). This approach has been recognized as a time- and cost-efficient alternative to conventional QTL mapping and has been successfully applied to identify genomic regions underlying both qualitative and quantitative traits in various crops (Illa-Berenguer et al. 2015). Representative examples include early flowering in cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) (Lu et al. 2014), days to flowering and leaf shape in pigeon pea (Cajanus cajan [L.] Millsp.) (Singh et al. 2022), heading time in bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) (Komura et al. 2024), resistance to dirty panicle disease in rice (Oryza sativa L.) (Riangwong et al. 2023), and pod length and width in soybean (Glycine max [L.] Merr.) (Xie et al. 2021). Nevertheless, the effectiveness of QTL-seq is maximized when a strong and consistent phenotypic contrast exists between extreme bulks. PM resistance typically exhibits a clear and reproducible phenotypic separation between resistant and susceptible individuals, making it well-suited for QTL-seq-based locus identification. However, QTL-seq studies focusing on PM resistance in naked barley remain limited.

Kompetitive allele-specific polymerase chain reaction (KASP) is a fluorescence-based genotyping technology designed for the precise detection of single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and insertion/deletion (InDel) variants. This system employs allele-specific oligonucleotide extension combined with fluorescence resonance energy transfer, enabling homogeneous and highly specific genotyping (He et al. 2014; Semagn et al. 2014). Owing to its high reproducibility, cost-effectiveness, and flexibility across platforms, KASP is recognized as a high-throughput genotyping technology capable of rapidly and accurately analyzing a large number of samples (Zhao et al. 2017). Furthermore, the integration of KASP markers into marker-assisted selection (MAS) programs enables efficient selection of target traits in early breeding generations and helps maintain valuable genetic resources by preserving genetic diversity (Wang et al. 2025). Consequently, KASP-based genotyping has been widely applied in various crops, including cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz), maize (Zea mays L.), and wheat (T. aestivum L.) for the improvement of agronomic, stress tolerance, and quality-related traits (Esuma et al. 2022; Jagtap et al. 2020; Tian et al. 2024).

Because PM drastically affects barley yield and grain quality, elucidating the genetic basis of PM resistance and establishing a MAS system based on reliable diagnostic markers are essential for improving the efficiency of barley breeding programs. Therefore, this study aimed to develop barley cultivars with resistance to PM to ensure stable production of high-quality naked barley in the Republic of Korea. Significant SNP variations associated with PM resistance were identified through QTL-seq analysis, and KASP markers were designed and validated based on these loci. Furthermore, the relationship between genotypes determined by the developed KASP markers and phenotypic resistance levels was analyzed to evaluate the effectiveness of the candidate markers. This study aimed to provide a technical foundation for future application of marker-assisted breeding aimed at improving PM resistance and achieving sustainable barley production.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

A susceptible naked barley cultivar ‘Nurichal’ (Lee et al. 2018) and a resistant elite breeding line ‘Jeonju144’ were used as parental lines in this study. Both genotypes were developed by the Winter Crop Research Division, Department of Crop Science, National Institute of Crop and Food Science, Rural Development Administration (RDA), Republic of Korea. All plant materials were grown in institutional research fields, and no wild plant materials were collected; therefore, no collection permits were required. Formal identification of the plant materials was conducted by professional barley breeders of the RDA. To generate a segregating population for genetic analysis of PM resistance, ‘Nurichal’ (female parental line) was crossed with ‘Jeonju144’ (male parental line). The resulting F1 plants were self-pollinated to produce an F2 segregating population, which was used for phenotypic evaluation, QTL-seq analysis, and marker validation.

Evaluation of phenotypic response and inheritance of PM resistance

PM resistance was examined in the parental lines (‘Nurichal’ and ‘Jeonju144’), their F₁ hybrids, and 244 F₂ individuals. Seedlings were inoculated with Blumeria graminis f. sp. hordei and grown in a growth chamber under controlled conditions for approximately 10 days to ensure uniform disease development. The disease reaction of each plant was evaluated according to the infection type (IT) scale that classifies symptom severity from IT0 (highly resistant) to IT4 (highly susceptible), as previously described (Mains and Dietz 1930). Plants rated as IT0 exhibited complete immunity with no visible fungal growth, whereas IT1 showed localized necrosis owing to hypersensitive cell death, limiting pathogen invasion. IT2 was characterized by restricted lesions with sparse sporulation, and IT3 and IT4 displayed extensive formation of white conidia without necrotic tissue, indicating full susceptibility. Based on these criteria, plants with IT0 and IT1 were classified as resistant, and those with IT2–IT4 were classified as susceptible (Fig. 1). The segregation pattern of resistance in the F2 population was analyzed using a chi-squared (χ2) test to determine whether the observed distribution conformed to the expected Mendelian ratio.

Genomic DNA extraction

For QTL-seq analysis, genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from leaf tissues of the parental lines and 244 F2 individuals. Leaf sampling was conducted at the two-leaf stage prior to PM inoculation and phenotypic evaluation. Based on the IT scores obtained from the seedling assay, 20 highly resistant and 20 highly susceptible F2 individuals showing extreme phenotypes were selected for bulk DNA construction. gDNA was isolated using a Plant DNA(II) Extraction Kit (SMARTGENE INC., Daejeon, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a DS-11 + Spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, USA), and DNA integrity was confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Equal amounts of DNA from the 20 resistant plants were combined to generate the resistant bulk (R20), and DNA from the 20 susceptible plants was pooled to generate the susceptible bulk (S20).

Whole-genome resequencing and QTL-seq

The quality and quantity of extracted gDNA were assessed using an Agilent 2200 TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using a TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and subjected to paired-end WGS on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Prior to analysis, raw sequencing reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases, resulting in high-quality reads for subsequent processing. Clean reads were aligned to the H. vulgare cv. Morex V3 reference genome (Mascher et al. 2021) obtained from Phytozome v.13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/HvulgareMorex_V3).

QTL-seq analysis was performed as previously described (Takagi et al. 2013) and implemented with the QTL-seq software package v.2.2.3 (https://github.com/YuSugihara/QTL-seq). SNP-index and Δ(SNP-index) values were calculated using a 2-Mb sliding window with 100-kb increments to examine allele-frequency differences across the genome. Genomic regions exhibiting significantly higher average Δ(SNP-index) values (p < 0.01) than those of the surrounding regions were considered as candidate QTLs associated with PM resistance. Two independent analyses were performed: one using Jeonju144 and the two bulks, and the other using Nurichal and the same bulks. Variants located within these QTL intervals were extracted from the variant calling file, and only significant ones (p < 0.01) were retained. Common significant variants detected in both analyses were designated as putative loci for further investigation.

Variant analysis and selection

Chromosomal positions and functional effects of the identified variants were annotated using SnpEff v.5.0e (Cingolani et al. 2012). Variants located within genic regions, including the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions, exons, and introns, were classified as genic, whereas those outside annotated gene boundaries were defined as intergenic. Variants within coding sequences (CDSs) were further classified according to the predicted effects, including synonymous and nonsynonymous substitutions, frameshift mutations, start-gained/lost, stop-gained/lost variations, and codon insertions or codon deletion variants, which were likely to affect protein structure or function. Functional annotations of the genes harboring these variants were retrieved from the gene description file of the H. vulgare Morex V3 reference genome available on Phytozome v.13. To identify high-confidence candidate variants, a multi-step filtering process was applied: (1) variants showing clear differences (p99) between the resistant and susceptible bulks were retained; (2) variants that were homozygous and polymorphic between the parental lines were selected; (3) variants homozygous in the susceptible bulk were considered; and (4) variants predicted to alter the amino-acid sequence of the encoded protein were preferred.

Design of KASP primers

Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR primers were designed for selected protein-altering variants, including nonsynonymous substitutions, frameshift mutations, stop-gained variants, and codon InDels, identified by QTL-seq. For each target, ± 100-bp flanking sequences were retrieved from the barley reference genome and used to design the primers. The allele-specific primers were arranged so that their 3′ termini directly overlapped the diagnostic base; where beneficial, a single intentional mismatch was introduced near the 3′ end to sharpen allelic discrimination. Designs targeted short amplicons (80–150 bp) with primer melting temperatures ranging between 60 and 65 °C and GC content between 40 and 60%. Candidate primers were filtered to avoid 3′ complementarity and strong self/cross-dimers, and in-silico specificity checks against the reference genome were performed; designs with off-target matches were discarded. The allele-specific forward primers were synthesized with 5′ universal tails (FAM tail: 5′-GAAGGTGACCAAGTTCATGCT-3′; HEX tail: 5′-GAAGGTCGGAGTCAACGGATT-3′), and the common primer was synthesized without a tail. All primers were synthesized by Macrogen (Daejeon, Republic of Korea).

Genotyping of the KASP assay

KASP reactions were performed in 10 µL volumes containing 5.0 µL of 2× KASP Master Mix, 0.14 µL primer mix, and 4.86 µL of 20 ng/µL gDNA. Amplifications were carried out on a Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 real-time PCR system using the following thermal cycling program: pre-read at 30 °C for 60 s, initial denaturation at 94 °C for 15 min, 10 touchdown cycles at 94 °C for 20 s with the annealing/extension temperature decreasing from 61 to 55 °C (− 0.6 °C per cycle) for 60 s, followed by 35 cycles at 94 °C for 20 s and 55 °C for 60 s, and a post-read at 30 °C for 60 s. Genotypes were called based on FAM/HEX fluorescence scatter plots generated by the instrument software. Marker validation was performed in three stages. First, the parental lines and the two bulks were genotyped (Nurichal [S], Jeonju144 [R], S_bulk, and R_bulk) to confirm clear cluster separation. Second, 20 susceptible and 20 resistant individuals used for bulk construction were genotyped along with non-template controls; markers showing distinct three-cluster separation, high call rates, and phenotype–genotype concordance were retained. Finally, validated markers were applied to the entire F2 population. Samples showing ambiguous amplification or baseline signals were designated as “No Call” and reanalyzed.

Statistical analysis

Segregation analyses were conducted using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Phenotypic segregation in the F2 population was tested for goodness-of-fit to a 3:1 (resistant: susceptible) expectation under a single dominant-gene model using a χ2 test (df = 1, α = 0.05). Genotypic segregation of codominant KASP markers was tested against the 1:2:1 (AA: Aa: aa) expectation using a χ² test (df = 2, α = 0.05). Only F2 individuals with unambiguous genotype calls were included in the analyses; parental controls and samples with “No Call” genotypes were excluded.

Results

Phenotypic assessment and genetic determination of PM resistance

Seedling assays were performed using seeds from F2 individuals grown under controlled conditions. IT was assessed and classified as follows: IT0 (111 plants), IT1 (66), IT2 (2), IT3 (31), and IT4 (34) (Fig. 2). When grouped into resistant (IT0–IT1) and susceptible (IT2–IT4) categories, 177 plants exhibited resistance, whereas 67 were susceptible. This segregation pattern suggested that PM resistance was controlled by a single dominant locus (Table 1). Phenotypic evaluations of the parental lines, F1 hybrids, and F2 progeny supported this conclusion. The susceptible parental line exhibited complete susceptibility; the resistant parental line showed full resistance, and all F1 plants displayed resistance, indicating dominant inheritance of the resistance trait. A χ2 test yielded a value of 0.787 with a corresponding p-value of 0.370, showing no significant deviation from the expected 3:1 segregation ratio. Collectively, these results confirm that PM resistance in this cross is governed by a single dominant gene.

QTL-seq analysis

Using the QTL-seq pipeline, sequence variants identified in two comparative sets were analyzed relative to the reference genome. In the Jeonju144–S20 vs. R20 comparison, 11,142,696 variants were detected in Jeonju144; 14,547,538 in S20; and 14,361,934 in R20; of which 3,169,407 were significant (Online Resource 1, Table S1). In the Nurichal–S20 vs. R20 comparison, 12,168,544 variants were detected in Nurichal; 14,025,069 in S20; and 13,858,810 in R20; with 3,076,817 being significant (Online Resource 2,Table S2).

To identify genomic regions associated with PM resistance, SNP-index values between the resistant and susceptible bulks were calculated using the Morex reference genome. The SNP index represents the proportion of non-reference alleles within each bulk; a value of 0 indicates the absence of a variant, whereas a value of 1 indicates that the variant is composed entirely of non-reference alleles among individuals in the bulk.

Based on these data, SNP-index distribution plots and Δ(SNP-index) variation patterns were generated for each chromosome (Online Resources 3–6, Figs. S1–S4). QTL candidate regions significantly associated with PM resistance (p < 0.01) were identified. In the Jeonju144 combination, major QTL signals were detected on chromosomes 1 (1 bp–19.4 Mb) and 7 (119.4–121.6 Mb), whereas in the Nurichal combination, a similar signal was observed on chromosome 1 (0.9–20.5 Mb) (Table 3). Certain genomic regions with low SNP density were excluded from further analysis. Within the significant region (99% confidence interval) on chromosome 1, mean Δ(SNP-index) values ranged from 0.4788 to 0.7085 in the Jeonju144 combination and from − 0.6602 to − 0.5228 in the Nurichal combination (Online Resources 7, 8; Tables S3, S4). A common QTL candidate region showing significant association at p < 0.01 in both combinations was located between 0.9 Mb and 19.4 Mb on chromosome 1 (Fig. 3). Within this region, a total of 3,005 common variants were identified; comprising 2,352 SNPs and 653 InDel variants (Table 4).

Detection and classification of variants in candidate QTL regions

Next, we identified the variants located in CDSs of the common sequence variants within the candidate QTL region. The variants predicted to affect protein sequences, such as NON_SYNONYMOUS, CODON_DELETION, CODON_CHANGE_PLUS_CODON_DELETION, FRAME_SHIFT, STOP_GAINED, START_GAINED, and CODON_INSERTION, were prioritized for KASP marker design. This prioritization was a practical decision to delimit the scope of marker development and validation in the present study and does not imply that only nonsynonymous or coding-region variants are suitable for marker-assisted selection. Although any stable polymorphism can theoretically serve as a genetic marker, the present analysis focused on coding-region variants causing amino-acid changes to enable efficient screening and stepwise validation within the candidate QTL region. During the development of KASP markers, the designed primers were screened for self- and cross-complementarity, GC balance, and melting temperature to prevent primer-dimer formation and ensure efficient amplification. Variants that failed to align with the reference genome (‘Morex’) during BLASTN comparison or exhibited non-specific flanking sequences were excluded to maintain locus specificity and amplification accuracy. Following this filtering process, 40 KASP markers were successfully developed for high-throughput fluorescence-based genotyping of allelic variants (Online Resource 9, Table S5). Of these, 37 markers corresponded to nonsynonymous SNPs that altered amino acid residues, 2 were classified as START_GAINED, and 1 as STOP_GAINED, which may influence translational initiation or cause premature termination of protein synthesis.

In silico exploration of putative candidate genes within the PM resistance locus.

To identify genes potentially associated with PM resistance in naked barley, an in silico survey was conducted using 40 KASP markers corresponding to nonsynonymous SNPs within the overlapping QTL-seq locus on chromosome 1 H (2.4–15.4 Mb). Based on physical positions and reference genome annotations, 21 genes were annotated within this interval and are considered putative candidates linked to the PM resistance locus. These genes were associated with diverse predicted biological functions, including defense response, signal transduction, and cell wall modification, according to available functional annotations. Several candidate genes corresponded to markers associated with disease resistance and signaling pathways, includingNPM02 (G/A) and NPM04 (C/T), annotated as disease resistance protein RGA4 isoform X2; NPM15 (T/C), PHOTOSYSTEM I ASSEMBLY 2, chloroplastic; NPM19 (T/C), methionine S-methyltransferase isoform X1; NPM20 (C/G) and NPM21 (C/A), GDSL esterase/lipase At5g45910-like; NPM22 (C/T), serine/threonine-protein kinase 19 isoform X2; NPM25 (G/A) and NPM26 (G/C), disease resistance protein RGA5-like; NPM27 (A/G), putative fatty acyl-CoA reductase 7; NPM28 (G/A), WAT1-related protein At5g64700-like; NPM30 (G/A) and NPM31 (G/A), phosphoinositide phosphatase SAC7-like; NPM34 (G/T) and NPM35 (C/T), growth hormone-regulated TBC protein 1; NPM45 (G/C), hypothetical protein; NPM50 (G/A), kinetochore protein SPC25 homolog; NPM60 (A/G), replication protein A 70 kDa DNA-binding subunit C-like; NPM65 (A/G) and NPM66 (G/C), wall-associated receptor kinase 3-like; NPM68 (G/T), G-type lectin S-receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase; NPM75 (G/A), phragmoplastin interacting protein 1-like; NPM76 (T/A), zinc finger protein ZAT12-like; NPM77 (C/A) and NPM78 (A/C), UPF0481 protein At3g47200-like; NPM79 (A/G), protein HGH1 homolog isoform X1; NPM85 (C/G), CSC1-like protein At3g21620; as well as several loci annotated as uncharacterized proteins (Online Resource 10, Table S6).

KASP genotyping and segregation analysis of candidate markers

To identify KASP markers suitable for MAS, candidate markers were subjected to a stepwise validation process based on assay performance and allele discrimination. Validation was conducted in three sequential stages: primary, secondary, and tertiary screening.

In the primary screening, the four samples used for QTL-seq analysis—Nurichal, Jeonju144, the susceptible bulk (S_bulk), and the resistant bulk (R_bulk)—were genotyped to assess basic assay performance and allele discrimination. Because PM resistance exhibits dominant inheritance and resistant phenotypes may include both homozygous and heterozygous genotypes, no fixed genotypic assumptions were applied to parental lines or bulks. Instead, markers were evaluated based on their ability to generate clear, reproducible fluorescence signals and to display consistent allele-frequency differences between phenotypically contrasting bulks. Based on these criteria, 11 markers (NPM02, NPM06, NPM21, NPM22, NPM24, NPM26, NPM31, NPM32, NPM43, NPM66, and NPM68) produced interpretable fluorescence patterns and were retained for secondary screening. The remaining 29 markers, which exhibited ambiguous, inconsistent, or undetectable fluorescence signals, were excluded from further validation, as they require additional assay optimization or may reflect complex genetic features beyond the scope of this study (Online Resource 11, Table S7; Online Resource 12, 13, Figs. S5 and S6).

In the secondary screening, the 11 retained markers were evaluated in 40 F2 individuals (20 susceptible and 20 resistant), used to construct the S_bulk and R_bulk. Several markers showed genotype–phenotype inconsistencies, including heterozygous or alternative allele calls in susceptible individuals or inconsistent allele calls in resistant individuals, and were therefore excluded from further analysis. These markers were considered candidates for future optimization and genetic evaluation. Among these, NPM32 and NPM43 consistently distinguished susceptible and resistant individuals and produced clearly separated genotype clusters in fluorescence scatter plots (Online Resource 14, Table S8; Online Resource 15, Fig. S7), and were advanced to tertiary screening.

In the tertiary screening, NPM32 and NPM43 were genotyped in 244 F₂ individuals previously phenotyped for PM resistance. For NPM32, 66 individuals carried the susceptible allele, 57 carried the resistant allele, and 121 were heterozygous, conforming to the expected 1:2:1 segregation ratio (χ² = 0.680, p = 0.712). However, genotype–phenotype discrepancies were observed in seven individuals (Table 5; Online Resource 16, Table S9). For NPM43, 67 individuals carried the susceptible allele, 57 carried the resistant allele, and 120 were heterozygous, also fitting a 1:2:1 segregation ratio (χ² = 0.885, p = 0.642), with no observed genotype–phenotype discrepancies (Table 5; Online Resource 17, Table S10). Fluorescence scatter plots showed clearly separated genotype clusters for NPM43, whereas NPM32 exhibited minor cluster overlap in a small number of individuals (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, KASP markers associated with PM resistance were developed and validated in an F2 segregating population of naked barley. Because PM frequently occurs under warm and humid conditions and can cause substantial yield and quality losses, the development of reliable molecular markers for rapid identification of resistant genotypes is essential for efficient breeding. Conventional gel-based markers, including randomly amplified polymorphic DNA, sequence-characterized amplified regions, variable number tandem repeats, and CAPS markers, are limited by low throughput and labor-intensive detection, which restricts their utility in large-scale breeding programs (McDermott et al. 1994; Park et al. 2025). In contrast, the fluorescence-based KASP markers developed in this study enabled clear discrimination of codominant genotypes with high reproducibility. Among the tested markers, NPM43 showed segregation consistent with the expected 1:2:1 ratio and exhibited strong concordance between genotype and phenotype, supporting its reliability as a diagnostic marker for PM resistance. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the superior accuracy and automation efficiency of KASP genotyping compared with gel-based methods (Ayalew et al. 2019; Alvarez-Fernandez et al. 2021; Sayadi Maazou et al. 2021).

QTL-seq is a rapid and cost-effective strategy for detecting major-effect loci in early segregating populations, such as F2. In the present study, QTL-seq was applied using two independent parental combinations: Jeonju144–S20 vs. R20 and Nurichal–S20 vs. R20 to identify genomic regions associated with PM resistance in naked barley. This dual-comparison approach allowed cross-validation across different parental genetic backgrounds, thereby reducing background noise and minimizing false-positive signals that may arise from single-parental analysis. Through this approach, a major PM resistance–associated QTL spanning 0.9–19.4 Mb on chromosome 1 H was consistently detected in both analyses, providing a robust basis for subsequent variant filtering and marker development.

The identified QTL region partially overlaps with the distal region of chromosome 1HS harboring the well-characterized Mla resistance locus. The Mla locus comprises a complex cluster of nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) genes with extensive allelic diversity and structural variation, which complicates precise allele-level resolution using short-read whole-genome resequencing alone (Dreiseitl 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). Given the relatively broad physical interval detected in this study and the inherent resolution limits of QTL-seq–based mapping, the resistance signal could not be unequivocally assigned to a specific Mla allele. Moreover, the barley reference genome ‘Morex’ represents a PM-susceptible haplotype, and resistance-conferring alleles present in genotypes such as ‘Jeonju144’ may be absent from or structurally divergent relative to the reference sequence (Hoseinzadeh et al. 2019; Mascher et al. 2017). Consequently, genes annotated within the QTL interval should be regarded as putative candidates inferred from in silico annotation rather than confirmed resistance genes. Moreover, the primary objective of this study was not to clone or functionally characterize the causal resistance gene, but to develop and validate robust diagnostic markers tightly linked to PM resistance. In this context, the strong and consistent association observed between NPM43 and the resistance phenotype demonstrates its practical value for MAS in naked barley, regardless of the precise molecular identity of the underlying gene. Although the NPM43-associated variant resides within a gene annotated as encoding an uncharacterized protein, this reflects current limitations in genome annotation and reference haplotypes rather than evidence of a novel resistance mechanism. Similar situations have been reported in other crops, where loci initially annotated as unknown were later linked to disease resistance following fine-mapping and functional validation (Gogoi et al. 2023).

Within the candidate QTL region, numerous nonsynonymous SNPs were identified, and these variants were preferentially selected for KASP marker development to facilitate efficient assay design and stepwise validation. Although any stable polymorphism can serve as an effective marker for MAS, coding-region variants were prioritized as a pragmatic strategy to reduce marker redundancy and enhance locus specificity during high-throughput genotyping. The use of nonsynonymous SNPs in this context does not imply a causal role in PM resistance, but rather reflects a marker-design consideration aimed at improving assay robustness and transferability. This approach is consistent with previous marker-development studies emphasizing its reliability and reproducibility in breeding applications (Mammadov et al. 2012; Salgotra and Stewart 2020).

In the Republic of Korea, naked barley accounts for more than half of the total barley cultivation area and is widely used for mixed grains and barley-based food products. Owing to its high β-glucan content, naked barley is valued for its nutritional and functional properties; however, PM infection can severely compromise grain quality and market value. Breeding for PM resistance in naked barley is therefore essential to ensure stable productivity and quality. Breeding efficiency in naked barley is often lower than in hulled barley because the hulless trait is recessive, making phenotypic selection difficult in early segregating generations when crossed with hulled types. Therefore, the development of naked barley-specific molecular markers linked to PM resistance is particularly valuable, as they enable early and accurate selection of resistant individuals independent of phenotypic evaluation. The validated KASP marker NPM43 provides an effective tool for accelerating the development of PM-resistant naked barley adapted to domestic cultivation environments.

NPM43 is based on a nonsynonymous variant reflecting functional polymorphism in resistance-related genes, ensuring high biological reliability. Although NPM43 was validated using an F2 population, this genetically diverse population represents an appropriate stage for early marker screening. Further validation in diverse germplasm collections and advanced breeding lines will be necessary to confirm its broader applicability. Additionally, integration of complementary approaches, such as transcriptome profiling, stage-specific quantitative real-time PCR, and targeted genome editing, may facilitate functional characterization of the underlying genes and support the development of precision MAS strategies based on functional markers (Adhikari et al. 2022).

One limitation of this study is the relatively broad genomic interval encompassed by the identified QTL, indicating the need for relatively highly refined genetic analysis to pinpoint the actual resistance-related genes. Mapping precision is strongly influenced by the number of recombination events captured, and limited population sizes can result in broad QTL intervals with reduced resolution (Shen and Messer 2022). Future studies should therefore employ larger recombinant populations and fine-mapping approaches to narrow the QTL region and identify the causal variant(s) underlying PM resistance (Yang et al. 2012). Such efforts will be essential for elucidating the genetic mechanisms of resistance and further strengthening the molecular basis of PM-resistant barley breeding.

Conclusions

The KASP markers developed in this study demonstrated high accuracy and reproducibility, supporting their suitability for establishing a rapid and reliable MAS system for early and efficient identification of PM resistance in naked barley. Among these markers, NPM43 showed a strong and consistent association between genotypic and phenotypic segregation in an F2 population, highlighting its practical value as a diagnostic breeding marker. Although the underlying resistance gene was not resolved, the validated marker provides an immediately applicable tool for improving selection efficiency in naked barley breeding programs. Future studies focusing on fine-mapping, expanded population sizes, and integrative functional analyses will further refine the resistance locus and contribute to the development of high-precision breeding strategies based on robust molecular markers for disease resistance in barley.
