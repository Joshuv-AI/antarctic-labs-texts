Migration strategies, connectivity and corridor features of the partial migrant little bustard (Tetrax tetrax) across the Iberian Peninsula
Authors: David González del Portillo, Beatriz Arroyo, Eladio L García de la Morena, Gerard Bota, João Paulo Silva, Ana Teresa Marques, João Gameiro, Filipa Coutinho Soares, Manuel B Morales
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study of migration ecology is crucial for understanding the factors and pressures affecting migratory species. Here, we studied the migratory ecology of the little bustard (Tetrax tetrax), a steppe bird that has suffered a sharp decline over recent decades, mainly due to agricultural intensification. Using 105 adult birds tagged across the main Iberian regions where the species is present (Alentejo, Extremadura, Ebro Valley, Northern Plateau, Southern Plateau and Guadalquivir Valley), we analysed the ratio of migratory and resident birds in each population and assessed their connectivity during the three main migratory periods (summer, winter and pre-breeding). Additionally, we describe the features of the migrations recorded in terms of length, duration and day period. Our results corroborate that little bustards can be considered partial migrants across Iberia, although the proportion of residents versus migrants varied between populations: the Alentejo (94.74%) and Northern Plateau (93.75%) had the highest proportion of migrants, followed by Guadalquivir Valley (81.82%), Extremadura (65.38%), Southern Plateau (55.56%) and Ebro Valley (25.93%). Migratory connectivity varied between periods: the pre-breeding and summering migrations showed a trend to move northwards, while birds moved southwards for winter. Regarding the migratory corridors obtained from the 253 migrations identified, we found three main routes: one corridor that connects the Northern Plateau with the western part of the Southern Plateau and Extremadura, another one that connects the Southern Plateau, Extremadura, Alentejo and Guadalquivir Valley, and one corridor that concentrates migrations within the Ebro Valley, and between the Ebro Valley and the Southern Plateau. Finally, analyses showed that little bustards migrate at night through areas dominated by herbaceous cover (avoiding tree-covered land and water bodies) and of low elevation and terrain roughness. Our results highlight the importance of developing an international and inter-regional conservation strategy to protect not only the breeding and wintering quarters, but also this endangered species’ migratory corridors, thus supporting the viability of the metapopulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-025-00595-w.Keywords: Brownian bridge kernel, GPS tracking, Habitat characterization, Migration patterns, Net squared displacement, Step selection function, Telemetry

Introduction

Large-scale movement ecology is a cornerstone of animal species’ ecology, essential for understanding their habitat requirements, population dynamics and current distribution [23]. In environments with seasonal variation, it is common to find migrant animals that move between different areas to meet their needs during the whole year (e.g. winter vs breeding quarters; [13]). Conversely, in areas with more stable conditions throughout the year, sedentary species are more frequent [13]. Migratory behaviour can vary among and within species [44], thus, species or populations can be classified as annual migrants (if all the individuals move between the wintering and breeding quarters, partial migrants (if some individuals migrate while others are sedentary and differential migrants (when the migration varies in timing and/or extent among different age/sex classes; [63]). Each species has specific ecological requirements for migration. If these requirements are not very flexible, their migratory routes tend to converge in the same regions, known as migratory corridors, which are characterized by distinct topographic and land-use features. Moreover, the migratory strategy involves the connectivity between different populations, as individuals from different breeding regions may share the wintering quarters or vice versa [70]. From a conservation perspective, it is crucial to consider the migration features of migratory species and the resulting geographical connectivity between the different areas visited by moving individuals throughout their annual life cycle. Protecting their habitat requires ensuring the preservation of breeding grounds, non-breeding quarters and the corridors connecting them [6, 62]. Otherwise, carry-over effects resulting from threats to wintering areas may have significant repercussions on breeding populations in distant regions, and vice versa, potentially compromising population viability in the long term [29, 46].

Migration has been widely studied across different taxa and scales [17]. Recent advances in wildlife tracking have led to the development of more precise, cost-effective tags that are lighter, have more battery and include additional sensors such as accelerometers [20, 24, 33, 64]. These advances allow researchers to tag smaller animals (without exceeding 3% of their body weight) and obtain more detailed information about their behaviour. Along with this progress, the emerging statistical tools, primarily within the R software environment [32, 51], has significantly enhanced our understanding of species that were previously understudied due to morphological constraints (e.g. small size) or ecological characteristics (e.g. cryptic species).

One of these species is the little bustard (Tetrax tetrax), a farmland bird that was historically distributed throughout the Palearctic but has nowadays two disjoint sub-ranges: a western one, whose main stronghold is in the Iberian Peninsula (but encompasses populations in France and Sardinia), and an eastern one that includes populations mainly from Russia and central Asia [38]. Its migratory behaviour is well documented, particularly in Europe, where some populations are considered resident, while others partially or fully migratory [41]. The Asian populations have been much less studied but are in contrast considered mainly migrant [41]. Beyond this general pattern, studies in Europe based on tagged birds with trackers have shown significant intraspecific variation in the timing and distance of migration, even among birds from the same breeding populations ([26], authors unpublished data). García de la Morena et al. [26] identified four migratory strategies in Iberia: typical summer migrants, winter migrants, summer–winter migrants (encompassing various strategies with different migration timings) and sedentary birds. This diversity of strategies is common in species with wide distributions, where the environmental conditions differ between populations and individuals [44]. Indeed, such diversity in migratory patterns has also been described for other steppe birds with similar habitat requirements, such as great bustards (Otis tarda, [39]) or Eurasian stone-curlew (Burhinus oedicnemus, [22]). These differences in the movement patterns among populations and individuals usually lead to low migratory connectivity due to the mix between migrant and resident birds in the non-breeding areas [44, 70]. Low migratory connectivity influences the general population trends since different breeding populations share non-breeding habitat and therefore threats [21].

Although little bustard migratory ecology has been assessed in the past using VHF radio transmitters and PTT satellite devices [3, 26, 53], their migratory corridors remain undescribed. Knowing the topographic and land cover features of these areas is crucial for developing conservation strategies that encompass the species’ entire annual cycle and movement range.

The aims of this study were to: a) describe the migration patterns of little bustards in the Iberian Peninsula, including the ratio of residents vs. migrants in each area, as well as movement features such as distance travelled and whether migration occurs by day or night, b) describe the migratory connectivity between the different regions with little bustard presence, c) identify the main corridors used in the migration movements, and d) characterise the topographic and land use features of the areas used by birds during migration. To achieve these objectives, we analyse data from 105 little bustards tagged with GPS tracking devices covering as much of the species’ range in the Iberian Peninsula as logistically possible. Our results may help to identify habitat requirements and threats associated with migration, thereby supporting the preservation of suitable habitat conditions within migration corridors. This, in turn, contributes to ensuring connectivity between the species’ populations and across areas critical during different phenological periods. Furthermore, in the current context of land-use change including renewable energy expansion in Iberia [56], identifying and characterizing migration corridors is essential to minimize present and future conflicts with new infrastructures, such as wind turbines and high-tension power lines.

Material and methods

Study species

The little bustard is a medium-sized steppe bird whose populations are suffering sharp declines across Europe [38]. Its original habitat was natural herbaceous steppes [16], however, it now occupies cultivated areas where cereals and legumes are mixed with natural herbaceous vegetation such as grasslands or fallows [25, 28]. It is a plumage-dimorphic bird, especially in the breeding season when males exhibit a black and white collar, with sexual differences in microhabitat selection (breeding males seek conspicuousness and visibility in open areas, while females prefer enough cover and resources to breed the chicks, [40]), although both sexes tend to occupy the same areas at landscape scale [27, 28]. Outside the breeding season, all individuals gather in mixed-sex flocks of adults and juveniles. Thus, there seems to be no sex or age-related difference in habitat selection outside the breeding season. No sexual differences in migration patterns have been described so far [41], suggesting that the results obtained for males, which are easier to capture and therefore constitute the majority of tagged individuals, can be extrapolated to the whole population.

Agricultural intensification is the main threat for the species, leading to the current decline of European populations [30, 38, 65]. As a result, the little bustard is classified as “Endangered” in Spain [55], “Vulnerable” in Europe [7] and “Near threatened” globally [8].

Study area

This study focuses on the Iberian Peninsula as a whole, which is considered to host the population core of the little bustard’s western distribution range [38]. Little bustards were captured and tagged in 6 Iberian natural regions (Fig. 1a): Southern Alentejo, Ebro Valley, Extremadura (which includes Extremadura and Northern Alentejo due to their proximity), Guadalquivir Valley, Northern Plateau and Southern Plateau. These regions are dominated by agricultural land uses, mainly arable crops, mosaic farmland (cereal with interspersed olive groves and vineyards) or grasslands devoted to livestock farming. However, intensive or super intensive woody crops (e.g. irrigated olive groves and vineyards) are exponentially taking over in some regions (e.g. Guadalquivir Valley, Southern Plateau, [61]).

Map of the localities where little bustards were tagged (a) and the migration segments registered and used in the analyses (b)

Regarding climate, the Iberian Peninsula is characterized by a high diversity of local climates [31]. The little bustards included in this study were tagged under a wide variety of Mediterranean-type climate conditions, all of them characterized by a more or less pronounced summer drought [4, 34]. The mean annual temperature and precipitation vary widely across these areas, ranging from 12.5 to 22.5 °C and 400 to 700 mm respectively. These climatic differences are reflected in ecosystem primary production, which decreases from northwest to southeast [2].

Little bustard tagging

Little bustards included in this study were all adult (since capture and tagging method cannot be safely used with juveniles) and captured during the spring between 2014 and 2024 using different techniques: leg nooses, funnel traps, and spring traps remotely activated by the capturers. Capture methods are described in Ponjoan et al. [49] and González del Portillo et al. [27].

Once trapped, the birds were immediately tagged, minimizing handling time to less than 15 min. The transmitter models used were Ornitela OT-15, Ornitela OTE-10, Ornitela OT-20 and Movetech MT25g, attached to the bird with a thoracic Teflon harness. Captured birds weighted between 740 and 975 g [10], and transmitters weighted between 10 and 25 g. The weight of the transmitter plus the harness was usually between 1.5 and 2.5% of the weight of the bird and did not exceed 3% of the bird’s weight in any case. After tagging, individuals were released where they were captured.

Statistical analyses

All the data manipulation and statistical analyses were carried using R software v4.3.2 [52].

Population connectivity and individual classification

We first divided the data obtained from the tags annually, considering the day of capture as the beginning of the monitoring period. Using all the locations, we then calculated the net squared displacement (NSD, defined as the squared straight-line distance between each location and the first recorded location; [67]) for the annual trajectories. This allowed us to graphically classify tagged little bustards as resident or migratory (based on clear movement patterns between two or more areas corresponding to the breeding, wintering or summering period, Fig. 2) and to determine the distances travelled between breeding, summering and wintering quarters. In the case of migratory birds, we further classified them as summer migrants (those that leave the breeding area during the summer and return to it at the end of this period), winter migrants (those that spend the winter in a different region from that used during breeding) or summer–winter migrants (individuals that use three different areas for breeding, summering and wintering, respectively; see [26] for the same classification, Fig. 2). To test whether the proportion of migratory individuals differed among breeding regions, we fitted a generalized linear model (GLM) with a binomial error distribution and a logit link function. The response variable was the proportion of migratory and resident individuals per population, and the explanatory variable was the breeding region. To explore pairwise differences between populations, we performed post-hoc comparisons using estimated marginal means.

Examples of resident and migratory movement patterns based on from the net squared displacement calculated from all the GPS locations provided by some tagged little bustards

Secondly, for each bird (resident and migrant) we identified its breeding, summering and wintering quarters among the main natural regions of the Iberian Peninsula (Alentejo, Ebro Valley, Extremadura, Guadalquivir Valley, Northern Plateau and Southern Plateau) based on the coordinates provided by their tags. Using this categorization, we evaluated their migratory connectivity, defined as the degree to which migratory individuals from the same area spread into different regions after migration [70]: strong migratory connectivity implies that most of the individuals from one breeding population concentrate in the same non-breeding quarters,meanwhile, a weak (or diffuse) migratory connectivity implies that individuals from a breeding population share different non-breeding quarters (the more non-breeding quarters are shared, the weaker migratory connectivity). For this purpose, we calculated the percentage of tagged birds that stayed in same region during different seasons or moved between Iberian regions. To quantify migratory connectivity, we computed the migratory connectivity coefficient as originally proposed by Cohen et al. [14], which provides a measure of the strength of the connection between regions, and ranges between 0 (no connectivity) and 1 (maximum connectivity). We calculated a global migratory connectivity coefficient that considers the movements during breeding, summering and wintering periods, as well as separate coefficients for each period to evaluate how migratory connectivity varies across the annual cycle. For each origin region and period, we calculated the percentage of migratory individuals (excluding residents) moving to each destination, so that the total percentage of movements from each origin sums to 100%. This allows a standardized comparison of movement patterns regardless of the number of individuals tagged in each region. Since independent estimates of population size were not available for all breeding regions, we used the proportion of GPS-tagged individuals from each area as a proxy for relative abundance, following previous studies where census data were lacking [14].

Finally, for the matrix obtained from the recorded migrations, we computed the eigenvector centrality score for each region, which informs about the influence of each node in the region network based not only on the number of its connections but also on the importance of the nodes it is connected to [9]. This measure helps identify key regions that are not only highly connected but also linked to other influential areas, potentially acting as hubs essential for maintaining the structure, stability and viability of the metapopulation network [69]. We made these analyses for the breeding-summering, the summering-wintering and pre-breeding movements observed.

Identification and characterization of migration periods

Using the NSD plots, we visually identified the day of the start and end of the migration. To detect the hour of departure and arrival, we used the distance between the bursts (defined as consecutive locations recorded less than one minute apart) recorded by the transmitters (which outside the migrations are generally below the 2 km threshold, see Additional Fig. 1). After filtering the bursts to discard locations recorded less than 1 min apart from the previous one and selecting the most accurate fix (based on the number of satellites or the Horizontal Dilution of Precision, which is a measure of the quality of GPS locations—[35]), we identified the departure hour excluding the locations until a step of at least 2 km were registered, for the hour of arrival, we kept the locations until the GPS device recorded a step less than 2 km far from the previous one. In total, 253 migrations and 71,437 locations were recorded, with an average of 238.5 ± 519.8 locations per migration (mean ± standard deviation).

To describe the patterns of little bustard’s migration we calculated the total distance travelled, the number of days migrating and the timing of the migration to test whether it occurs during the day or at night as found for little bustards migrating from France to winter in the Iberian Peninsula [41, 68]. For this test, we characterised each day as “Migration” or “No migration” and the daily period of each location as either “Day” or “Night”, using its timestamp, the hour of sunrise and the hour of sunset (using bioRad v0.7.3 package; [19]). After that, we selected only those days with at least 3 locations during both periods (“Day” and “Night”), and calculated the distance travelled during “Day” and “Night” by summing all the movements that start and end in the same period. Movements that began during the day and ended at night, or vice versa, were excluded, as the exact distance travelled during each period was unclear (e.g. if locations were recorded every two hours, and one fix was taken 1 h before sunset and the next one an hour after, it would be unclear whether the movement occurred before or after sunset). This approach resulted in the exclusion of 4.24% of the locations. Finally, we implemented a linear mixed-effects model, with the total distance travelled (logarithmically transformed to fit a gaussian distribution) as the response variable, and day period (“Day”, “Night”), migration period (“Migration”, “No migration”) and the interaction between them as fixed terms. We included bird identity as a random effect.

Description of migratory corridors and their habitat characterization

To group the migrations identified and define corridors in the Iberian Peninsula (understood as areas where migratory trajectories of different individuals converge), we performed a Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS hereafter). For this analysis, we first calculated a dissimilarity matrix from the start and end coordinates of the trajectories. We then performed the NMDS using the metaMDS function (vegan v2.6-6.1 package; [47]) to reduce dimensionality and visualize similarities among migrations. To identify natural groupings within the NMDS space, we applied k-means clustering to the NMDS scores. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow and silhouette methods. This classification allowed us to assign each migration to a corridor and summarize spatial patterns of migratory connectivity across the Iberian Peninsula.

After that, we computed Brownian bridge kernel models on these migratory trajectories, selecting those with at least 7 recorded locations to maintain as many trajectories as possible while ensuring that the corridors obtained were robustly fitted. All the locations recorded during these migrations and retained after filtering the bursts were included in the analysis. Standardization of the frequency at which GPS locations were recorded was not necessary, as the corridors obtained were not compared among themselves. To delineate the corridors, we used the kernelbb function from the adehabitatHR v0.4.21 (Calenge and Fortmann-Roe [11]). This function considers not only the recorded locations, but also the path travelled by the animal, inferred as the straight line connecting two successive locations. For this analysis, it is necessary to define two smoothing parameters, sig1 and sig2. The first parameter, sig1, is related to the animal’s speed, controls the width of the bridge between successive locations and can be estimated by the liker function from the adehabitatHR v0.4.21 package (Calenge and Fortmann-Roe [11]). The second parameter, sig2, is related to the imprecision of the recorded location (i.e. location error). For this parameter, we used two alternative methods, depending on the tag: (i) the Horizontal Dilution of Precision values recorded by the transmitter, in the case of the Ornitela tags, and (ii) a 10-m error estimate for the Microwave tags, already empirically tested by Acácio et al. [1]. Both variables are related to GPS accuracy, which varies depending on the number of satellites connected, influenced by factors like cloud cover or obstacles such as trees or power lines). To define to which corridor each individual migration trajectory corresponded, we used the 95% isopleth of the resulting heat map.

To describe the topography and land uses of these corridors, we used Step Selection Functions (hereafter SSF). These functions are widely used to correlate environmental variables with high-frequency locations by comparing the steps between real and random locations [58]. The results provide insight into the animal’s preferences for the environmental conditions represented by the variables included in the model. For this purpose, we followed the five-step approach of the amt package [58]:

Selection of environmental variables: We used both topographic and land-use variables. For topography we chose elevation, slope, and roughness. They were obtained from a ETOPO1 Elevation raster downloaded from the Environmental Data Automated Track Annotation System on Movebank (resolution of 908 × 908 m; [18]). The land use variables were obtained from Corine Land Cover (CLC, Copernicus land monitoring service [15]). Since CLC has 42 land-use categories, some of which are rare in the Iberian Peninsula, we decided to unify them into the following categories: Artificial, Herbaceous, Shrubland, Tree-covered land, Water and Bare ground (more details on the reclassification can be found in Additional Table 3).

Results

Variation in migratory patterns and population connectivity

A total of 105 adult little bustards were included in this study (2014–2024), consisting of 96 males and 9 females, covering a large part of the species’ distribution range in the Iberian Peninsula, although some sectors in the south-east could not be covered (Fig. 1a and Additional Table 1).

We obtained a total of 183 annual segments corresponding to 55 little bustards classified as residents and 105 as migrants (Table 1). In 23 cases we could not determine the behaviour of the birds due to the animal’s early death or tag failure (Table 1).

The percentage of residents and migrants varied between regions (Table 1): Alentejo and the Northern Plateau had the highest proportion of migrants (94.74% and 93.75% respectively), followed by the Guadalquivir Valley (81.82%), Extremadura (65.38%), Southern Plateau (55.56%) and Ebro Valley (25.93%). According to our model, these proportions differed significantly between breeding populations. Based on pairwise comparisons, breeding populations were grouped into three clusters according to the proportion of migrants and residents. The Ebro Valley formed a distinct group with the lowest proportion of migrants. A second group included Alentejo, Extremadura, and the Northern Plateau, which did not differ significantly from each other and showed intermediate to high proportions of migrants. Finally, Guadalquivir Valley and Southern Plateau formed a third group, with migrant proportions not significantly different from either the second group or the Ebro Valley, indicating an intermediate status. Results of the post hoc analyses are presented in Additional Table 2.

The migrations registered varied seasonally (Fig. 3): Alentejo, the Southern Plateau and the Northern Plateau received birds from more southern regions during summer; while Extremadura and the Southern Plateau were the main destinies as wintering quarters of birds from further north; the pre-breeding migrations tended to occur towards the north, with the Northern Plateau as the main destination followed by Extremadura. The percentage of birds that remained in each region differed between seasons, indicating that movements are more common between the summering and wintering quarters than between the breeding and summering areas (Fig. 3). These findings are supported by migratory connectivity coefficients obtained. When calculated across the full annual cycle, migratory connectivity showed a moderate value (0.59), indicating some regional fidelity but also a mixed movement pattern throughout the year. However, when examining specific periods, strong connectivity was observed between summering and wintering grounds (0.89), as most individuals tend to winter in the South Plateau. In contrast, intermediate levels of connectivity were found for summer and pre-breeding movements (0.72 and 0.67, respectively), reflecting a broader dispersal across the Iberian Peninsula during these times.

These seasonal differences in the movements between regions were also supported by the eigenvector centrality scores (Table 2). According to these results, Alentejo is the region with the highest eigenvalue score among the regions where we tagged little bustards for the breeding-summering movements, followed by the Northern and Southern Plateaus. In the case of the summering-wintering movements, Extremadura was the most important node in the migratory network, followed by far by the Southern Plateau. Finally, regarding the pre-breeding migrations between wintering and breeding quarters, Extremadura still was the most important node followed by the Northern Plateau (also by far). On the contrary, the Ebro Valley had the lowest eigenvector centrality scores for the three periods, which is consistent with the higher proportion of resident individuals and the regional isolation shown by Fig. 3 and Table 2.

Migration periods identification and characterization

From the 105 year-round trajectories classified as migrant we could identify 257 migrations: 146 post-breeding and 111 pre-breeding migrations. Post-breeding migrations were also classified as summering or wintering, depending on whether the movements occurred from July to August or from September to November: 85 were considered summer migrations and corresponded to individuals that moved between their breeding and summering quarters, while 61 were classified as winter migrations and occurred from breeding or summering grounds to wintering quarters. We identified more migrations than year-round trajectories because some birds moved between breeding, summering and wintering quarters more than once in a single season or do not migrate in a single direct movement.

Overall, little bustards completed these migrations quite quickly in all the seasons: summering migrations in 2.3 ± 2.7 days, wintering migrations in 2 ± 2.4 days and pre-breeding migrations in 1.8 ± 2.2 days; with an average distance travelled of 94.7 ± 88.2 km, 123.9 ± 118.9 km and 117.5 ± 105.4 km respectively (mean ± standard deviation) between the departure and arrival sites. The linear mixed-model (with random intercepts and slopes for individuals) analysing the distance across time periods yielded significant differences in distances travelled between migration and non-migration movements, especially at night (Fig. 4). During the migration periods, little bustards travel longer distances during night-time than during daytime (Fig. 4). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Tukey’s adjustment were conducted to explore differences between groups, and the results are represented in Fig. 4.

Migratory corridors description and habitat characterization

Out of all the migrations identified, only 152 of these periods successfully converged, providing polygons that define the areas used by the little bustards during migration (Fig. 5). According to the NMDS, the migration routes followed by individuals seem to converge in three main flyways, likely forming three different migratory corridors in the Iberian Peninsula (See Fig. 5 and Additional Table 4). One corridor connects the Northern Plateau with the western part of the Southern Plateau and Extremadura (with 98 migrations, mainly from birds tagged in the Northern Plateau), a second one connects the Northern Plateau, Extremadura, Alentejo and the Guadalquivir Valley (with 138 migrations, mainly from the individuals tagged in Extremadura); and, finally, a third corridor connects different zones inside the Ebro Valley and the Ebro Valley with the Southern Plateau (18 migrations, mainly from individuals that move inside the Ebro Valley, Fig. 5).

The SSF results showed significant effects for the topographic variables included in the model (Table 3 and Fig. 6). Little bustard occurrence was negatively related to elevation and terrain roughness. In terms of land use, little bustards preferentially migrated through regions dominated by herbaceous vegetation (mainly cereal farmland and pastures), while avoiding water bodies and tree-covered land. We did not record locations on terrain classified as “Artificial” nor on “Bare ground”, so we could not include these land-uses in the final Step Selection Function. However, the fact that zero out of 71,437 locations were obtained on those cover categories, strongly indicates their avoidance by little bustards during their migration movements.

Discussion

The ratio resident versus migrant varied among regions: in the Ebro Valley, most little bustards were residents, whereas in the other regions, most little bustards tagged were migrants (Table 1). Seasonal movements revealed a moderate migratory connectivity among regions except for the wintering period, as little bustards gathered in the mid-south of the Iberian Peninsula during wintering. This result has conservation implications, emphasizing the importance of preserving wintering grounds [70]. Failure to protect wintering sites in these regions could negatively impact breeding populations in distant areas, which, despite being protected, may result in population declines [70]. These movements varied seasonally (Table 2, Fig. 3): pre-breeding and post-breeding summer migrations showed a tendency to move northwards, whereas at the end of the summer birds tended to move southwards.

The coexistence in the same breeding regions of individuals that abandon those areas after breeding with others that stay there year-round (e.g. [43]) shown in this study corroborates the previously described migration status of the little bustard in Iberia as a partial migrant [26, 41]. Partial migration has been also reported for other long-lived steppe birds, such as the great bustard (Otis tarda, [39, 48]) or the stone-curlew (Burhinus oedicnemus, [22]). Inter-individual variability in migratory strategies is influenced not only by intrinsic factors, such as genetic heredity or sex, but also by body condition, age, individual experience, and environmental conditions [12, 50, 57]. This variability is particularly common in species with summer post-breeding movements previous to migration to their wintering grounds [37, 45], which is the case for most of the little bustards tagged in this study (54.4%, Table 1). Since changes in environmental conditions can trigger migration [53], different movement patterns can be expected in species with broad distribution areas that encompass a wide range of local environmental conditions [57]. This might be case for Iberian little bustards, which can experience a wide range of environmental conditions, both in summer and winter [34].

The three little bustard migration corridors identified (Fig. 5 and Additional Table 4) run through a significant portion of the species’ Iberian range. The corridor connecting Extremadura, the Northern and the Southern Plateaus is well defined while the other two are more diffuse. In the case of the corridor connecting the Alentejo, Extremadura, Guadalquivir Valley and the Northern Plateau this may be due to the lack of geographical obstacles that create bottlenecks (Fig. 5). On the other hand, the identified corridor connecting the Ebro Valley and the Southern Plateau may be influenced by the lower proportion of migratory birds, resulting in a small sample size (Additional Table 4). Moreover, despite our joint and comprehensive tagging effort, some Iberian geographical sectors remained uncovered. Particularly, the southeast, which comprises areas of eastern Andalusia and the southeastern fringe of the Southern Plateau. Future little bustard tagging in this sector may reveal new connections with the natural regions considered in this study.

Our analyses showed that little bustards migrate through areas of low elevation and terrain roughness, probably because such landscapes offer greater visibility and safety in flight (bustards have limited frontal vision, [36]), and also allow them to land more easily and safely if necessary. However, on a larger scale, the topography of the three corridors identified is quite different. The corridor that connects both plateaus and Extremadura crosses the Central System Mountain range, while the other two present flatter relief profiles. Due to the threats associated with migration, the survival rate of migrants tends to be lower than that of resident individuals, especially in the case of longer-distance migrants [42, 54]. Moreover, as threats vary across migration routes, the survival rate of migratory birds could also be different between populations. This might be the case of wind farms in relation to little bustard migration: in the Central System Mountain range, wind turbines are located along mountain summit lines, which are avoided by migrating birds according to our model results (Table 3, Fig. 6). Conversely, in the eastern corridor, wind farms are located on páramos (high elevation but flat terrain of little slope and roughness) whose topographic features are preferentially used by little bustards during migration. Therefore, birds that use the latter corridor crossing plateau areas with high concentration of wind farms by night when the visibility of turbines is low might be exposed to higher collision risk (both with turbines and associated power lines), which may potentially select against the migratory strategy in populations like that from the Ebro Valey.

Regarding the landscape composition comprised by migration corridors, our results showed a dominance of herbaceous cover (arable crops and grassland), while birds avoided water bodies and high tree-covered land (Table 3, Fig. 6), which is consistent with the species’ general habitat preferences at landscape scale (see a thorough review in [66]). This selection pattern along flyways is consistent with our topographic results and is also possibly linked to the preference of little bustards for areas with high visibility and low vegetation, reducing the predation risk while migrating and where they can land if necessary.

On the other hand, according to the results of SSF little bustards avoid water bodies and tree-covered land, as well as bare and artificial ground (i.e. no locations were found in these two categories). The ultimate driver of this avoidance is likely the total unsuitability of water bodies and tree-covered land for landing and/or resting during migration. In the case of artificial land, nocturnal light could also play a role, since bustards migrate by night [59, 68]. In any case, little bustards have been shown to generally avoid urban areas [5]. Likewise, bare ground (which includes bare rocks and sparsely vegetated areas), as well as high, slopy and rough terrain, is far from being in the range of little bustard suitable habitats (see review in [66]), which is consistent with their avoidance during the migration, despite their tree-less structure.

The species’ dependence on herbaceous vegetation (basically, arable crops and grasslands) during migration, also found by Alonso et al. [3], makes little bustards even more sensitive to land use change than previously assessed from general breeding or post-breeding habitat preferences [60]. In the light of our results, the ongoing conversion of annual crops into intensive woody crops (olive groves, vineyards), can be considered as a threat to little bustard migration and thus to population connectivity, since they may function as barriers for flyways forcing birds to seek new routes in areas with more open and tree-less vegetation cover.

Conclusions

This study describes the migratory behaviour of birds from different areas, the connectivity between the main natural regions hosting little bustard populations in the Iberian Peninsula (the species’ European stronghold), as well as the migratory corridors used by this endangered steppe bird. Our results showed that all these regions are interconnected, resulting in a moderate migratory connectivity as individuals from the same breeding population disperse across different non-breeding areas and vice versa. These results showed that little bustards largely move across natural and administrative regions, which underlines the need for an integrated conservation strategy encompassing the entire species’ range to ensure the integrity and viability of the metapopulation.

Our results showed that little bustard preferences are similar during migratory and non-migratory periods. Specifically, herbaceous crops (e.g. cereals and legumes) and grasslands should be maintained along migration corridors, avoiding their transformation into artificial ground (e.g. urbanized areas, infrastructures), large water bodies (e.g. reservoirs) or afforestation. On a wider conservation framework, the preservation of suitable habitats along flyways of migratory species seems critical to reduce the migratory threats such as mortality from collision with human-made infrastructures (e.g. wind turbines) and favour metapopulation persistence [71].
