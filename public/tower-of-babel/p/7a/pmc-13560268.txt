Comparative analysis of MinION and MiSeq using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing in human gut microbiome
Authors: Chong Zhu, Tao Wang, Wenbo Zhang, Anjie Guo, Shushen Ji, Chunyu Liu, Rongyu Ma, Li Song, Yue Wang, Peng Yao, Ling Lin, Lejun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The genetic classification of microbial populations at high taxonomic resolution is crucial for clinical diagnosis and treatment. The classification of complex bacterial communities in the human gut was examined in this study by utilizing amplicon sequencing of the 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene. The influence of different sequencing platforms and amplicon regions on this classification was investigated. Nineteen human fecal samples were analyzed using both Nanopore MinION and Illumina MiSeq platforms.The main objective of the analysis was to assess how effectively these platforms can characterize gut microbiota at the Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV), genus, and species levels, taking into account various amplicon regions within the 16S rRNA gene. The findings reveal significant disparities between the two platforms. In particular, the MinION platform demonstrates higher values in terms of ASV and species richness, as well as diversity (both alpha and beta diversity) compared to the MiSeq platform. These variations are dependent on the specific targeted amplicon region of the 16S rRNA gene. However, despite these differences in richness and diversity, there is a notable level of consistency observed in detecting the presence of dominant microbial taxa across both platforms, although their relative abundances varied significantly. Notably, the study emphasizes that the choice of primers used for amplification exerts the most significant impact on the classification results at the genus level, surpassing the influence of the sequencing platform itself. This research provides valuable insights into the strengths and limitations of long-read and short-read sequencing in the context of classifying complex gut microbiota, particularly in clinical microbiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12896-026-01182-6.

Introduction

In recent years, high-throughput sequencing technology has significantly advanced the analysis of complex microbial ecosystems, particularly in clinical microbiota. This technological advancement has yielded valuable insights into the dynamics of microorganisms in both healthy and diseased states [1, 2]. The Illumina MiSeq sequencing platform is widely acknowledged as a leading tool for characterizing microbiomes, specifically in the identification of clinical bacteria and the discovery of novel pathogens using next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology [3, 4]. The MiSeq platform excels in generating 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) reads, with lengths reaching up to approximately 600 base pairs.

Despite the high quality of raw data produced by the MiSeq platform, the relatively elevated cost per base remains a significant economic challenge for many research projects [5]. As a cost-effective alternative that has gained widespread acceptance, the ONT MinION sequencing platform from Oxford Nanopore Technologies is increasingly favored due to its affordable initial investment and the remarkable potential for virtually unlimited sequence read length [6, 7].

The primary limitation of the MinION sequencing platform lies in its lower base-calling accuracy, estimated at around 95%, compared to the impressive 99.9% accuracy achieved by the MiSeq platform [8]. The relatively high error rate in ONT sequencing poses challenges in accurately distinguishing bacterial species-level taxonomic classifications using 16S rRNA sequences, which typically require a minimum similarity threshold of 97%. Subsequent advancements have further raised the threshold to 98.65%. However, advancements in the MinION platform, particularly with the R10.4 version of the flow cell, now produce data with a modal read accuracy exceeding 99% [9]. Furthermore, the ONT R10.4 flow cell exhibits exceptional proficiency in generating nearly complete microbial genomes from isolates or metagenomes, obviating the need for short-read data or reference-based polishing [5]. These advancements underscore the substantial improvement in sequence accuracy achieved by the MinION platform, rendering it an invaluable tool for a wide range of genomic applications, including microbiome studies.

A commonly employed approach for investigating microbial structure, organisms, and composition patterns involves amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene, a highly conserved and universally present genetic marker spanning approximately 1500 base pairs [10, 11]. The 16S rRNA gene consists of nine variable regions known as V1–V9, which are commonly targeted using various primers to analyze microbial diversity and community structure. These primers can be used individually or in combination with specific variable regions of the 16S rRNA gene [11–14].

The choice of sequencing platforms and primers significantly impacts microbiome studies [3, 14–16]. Evidence increasingly supports MinION’s superiority over MiSeq in taxonomic classification, spanning genus to species levels, across diverse environments, including agricultural soil, building dust, and human feces [17–19]. In the field of clinical microbiology, MinION’s application in full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing has demonstrated remarkable taxonomic precision, particularly when utilizing optimized primers to identify specific bacterial taxa at the species level [17]. However, there remains a substantial knowledge gap concerning the influence of platforms and primers on microbial classification within this domain.

This study aims to elucidate whether sequencing platforms and primers influence: (1) species richness, (2) the relative abundances of genera, and (3) the optimal choice of regions or platforms for taxonomic classification in the context of clinical bacteria, furthering our knowledge in this crucial area.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction

Nineteen fecal samples were obtained from healthy volunteers and preserved in polypropylene tubes (FLMedical, Padova, Italy). These samples were promptly stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. DNA extraction from these samples was performed using the PowerMax Extraction Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000c (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

PCR amplification

To amplify specific 16S rRNA gene regions, universal primer pairs were thoughtfully selected to align with research objectives [20]. The primer pair 341F (5′-CCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 805 R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′) was applied to both the MinION and MiSeq platforms, targeting the V3–V4 region. Additionally, the primer pair 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806 R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) was used with both platforms to amplify the V4 region of the 16S gene. For the MinION platform, the study also utilized the universal primer pair 27F (5′-AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492 R (5′-TACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) to target the full-length 16S rRNA gene, enabling comprehensive analyses. According to the standard dual-indexing protocol, specific barcode sequences and sequencing adapters were incorporated into the primers to facilitate multiplex amplification and sequencing on the Illumina platform.

PCR amplification of 16S rRNA genes was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions, using LongAmp® Taq 2X Master Mix M0287 (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA, United States).The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 25 µL of LongAmp® Taq 2X Master Mix M0287 (New England BioLabs), 1 µL of forward primer (10 µM), 1 µL of reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of template DNA (approximately 10–50 ng), and 21 µL of nuclease-free water, yielding a total volume of 50 µL. For the V3–V4 region, the PCR reaction followed this temperature profile: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 minute, then 21 cycles of 95 °C for 20 seconds, 55 °C for 30 seconds, and 65 °C for 1 minute, concluding with a final extension step at 65 °C for 5 minutes. For the V4 region, the PCR reaction conditions were: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 minute, followed by 21 cycles of 95 °C for 20 seconds, 55 °C for 30 seconds, and 65 °C for 1 minute, and a final extension at 65 °C for 5 minutes. For the V1–V9 region, the initial denaturation time in the PCR reaction was extended to 2.5 minutes.

Library preparation and sequencing

Following amplification, the DNA was purified using AMPure® XP Beads (Beckman Coulter). For DNA quantification, the NanoDrop® 2000c spectrophotometer was used for the MinION platform, as it provides sufficient accuracy for high-concentration long-read libraries. Conversely, the highly sensitive Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed for the MiSeq platform to ensure highly precise quantification necessary for short-read library pooling.

For ONT MinION sequencing, the purified amplicons targeting the V1–V9, V3–V4, and V4 regions were used to generate sequencing libraries using the SQK-LSK109 Kit. These libraries were loaded onto the R9.4.1 flow cell (FLO-MIN106; Oxford Nanopore Technologies) for sequencing on the MinION MK1C device. The ONT MinKNOW software ver. 1.11.5 was employed for the collection of raw sequencing data. For Illumina MiSeq sequencing, the purified amplicons targeting the V3–V4 and V4 regions were evaluated for quality and fragment length using gel electrophoresis. The sequencing libraries were then prepared for Illumina MiSeq pair-end sequencing on a 2 × 250 bp platform.

Bioinformatics analysis

The Guppy software ver. 4.4.0 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) was utilized to perform base-calling on the MinION sequencing data originating from FAST5 file. During this step, a minimum quality score filter (–min_qscore 7) was applied to retain only high-quality FASTQ format pass reads. Precise removal of adapter and barcode sequences was meticulously performed using Porechop ver. 0.2.4 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies), (i.e., applying an adapter alignment threshold of 90%, a barcode threshold of 75%, and an extra end trim of 2 base pairs). The reads underwent size-based filtration using SeqKit software ver. 2.5.1, retaining sequences within the specified range of 1300–1950 base pairs for the V1–V9 region, 350–600 base pairs for the V3–V4 region and 250–350 base pairs for the V4 region. These size thresholds were strategically determined based on the distribution of 16S rRNA gene sequences found in SILVA database ver. 138 [21].

To eliminate reads originating from human DNA, each read was aligned against the human genome (GRCh38) using minimap2 ver. 2.14, incorporating the map-ont option [22]. Reads that did not align with human DNA were identified as bacterial in origin. For taxonomic classification, each read underwent a minimap2 search against the SILVA database. Subsequently, the species demonstrating the highest minimap2 score were assigned to the query sequence. In cases where multiple species displayed similar similarity scores, the reads were classified within a higher taxonomic rank encompassing all identified species. Taxonomic assignments were rooted in the NCBI taxonomy database [23].

Data analysis

Qiime2 ver. 2023.2.0 was utilized to carry out DADA2 processing of the raw sequencing data. The initial data processing involved rigorous quality filtering, which encompassed the removal of adapter and barcode sequences, as well as trimming (specifically, truncating the forward reads at 220 bp and reverse reads at 200 bp for both V3–V4 and V4 regions) to an appropriate length, with the maximum expected errors (maxEE) set to 2 and maximum ambiguous bases (maxN) set to 0, to exclude sequences with an average quality score below 20. Due to the fundamental differences in sequencing technologies, these parameters were distinct from the length-based filtration used for the long-read MinION platform (which applied specific size ranges for V1–V9, V3–V4, and V4 regions as detailed previously). Following this, sequences underwent crucial steps, including dereplication, sequence variant evaluation, merging, and chimera checking, adhering to the standard DADA2 procedure. The filtered representative sequences and biom-formatted tables were subsequently annotated with taxonomic information from the SILVA ver. 138 database. The result yielded a biom-formatted table enriched with taxonomic information, accompanied by a separate text file containing comprehensive taxonomy details for subsequent analysis.

Before calculating diversity indices, the dataset was normalized by rarefying to the minimum library size among all samples to account for uneven sequencing depths. Specific alpha-diversity indices (including Observed richness, Chao1, ACE, Shannon, and Simpson) and beta-diversity indices were computed and visualized using the “microeco” package ver. 0.20.0. The calculation of beta diversity involved assessing the dissimilarities and distances between microbial communities using the Bray-Curtis index, a metric that quantifies compositional dissimilarity. The resulting distances were effectively visualized using Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA). To evaluate the dissimilarities in beta diversity across diverse sample groups, we conducted a permutation-based multivariate analysis of variance using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). The similarities and dissimilarities among samples were assessed using Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient. The distances between samples were computed based on the Bray–Curtis beta diversity matrix. Rarefaction curves were generated using “microeco” package to assess ASV richness across different sequencing depths.

The sequence data (fastq files) were deposited in the NCBI BioProject under accession number PRJNA1058165. The SRA records will be accessible with the following link after the indicated release date: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1058165.

Microbial diversity in long-read and short-read sequencing platforms

Figure 1 illustrates the disparities in species richness through rarefaction curves generated from MinION and MiSeq sequencing outcomes. These curves, computed for samples analyzed on both sequencing platforms, vividly portray the increasing numbers of raw sequencing reads. Notably, MinION sequencing revealed a significantly higher capacity for discovering species compared to MiSeq (Fig. 1). The rarefaction curves for MiSeq results reach a saturation point earlier than those for MinION, indicating that the MiSeq platform generally exhibited detects only a limited number of new sequences as sequencing depth increases. Importantly, analysis of the sequence abundance distribution revealed a substantially higher proportion of singletons (sequences appearing only once) in the datasets generated by the MinION platform compared to those from the MiSeq platform. Specifically, among the total ASVs identified by the MinION platform, singletons accounted for 19.91% (V1–V9), 28.69% (V3–V4), and 27.11% (V4), respectively. In sharp contrast, singletons in the MiSeq V3–V4 and V4 datasets accounted for only 0.22% and 1.14% of the ASVs, respectively. This continuous accumulation of singletons directly contributes to the lack of early saturation in the MinION rarefaction curves. Discussion

The present study aimed to assess the taxonomic classification capabilities of two sequencing platforms, namely the long-read MinION and short-read MiSeq, using 19 fecal samples obtained from healthy volunteers. To ensure a comprehensive evaluation, different amplicon variable regions and universal primer pairs were employed for library preparation on both platforms. Specifically, on the long-read MinION platform, we sequenced 16S rRNA amplicons derived from three regions: V1–V9 (using primers 27F/1492 R which cover nearly the entire sequence of the 16S rRNA gene), as well as V3–V4 (primers 341F/805 R) and V4 (primers 515F/806 R). For the short-read MiSeq platform, 16S rRNA amplicons specific to the V3–V4 (primers 341F/805 R) and V4 (primers 515F/806 R) regions were sequenced.

The long-read MinION platform, when compared to the short-read MiSeq platform, demonstrates comparable capabilities for microbial taxonomic classification at both the genus and species levels. Our findings align with a study by Matsuo et al. [17], which also reported that full-length 16S rRNA sequencing provides similar results for classifying microbial genera in clinical fecal samples when compared to standard short-read 16S rRNA sequencing. Their research highlights that the full-length MinION platform achieves improved discrimination among specific taxa, such as Bifidobacterium, through the utilization of optimized primer sequences. However, our investigation revealed that the MinION platform may encounter challenges in classifying certain taxa, such as Bifidobacterium, due to sequence mismatches with commonly used PCR primer 27F [24]. Additionally, our study demonstrated that the MinION platform detected a significantly higher number of new sequences with increasing sequencing depth compared to the MiSeq platform (Fig. 1). This observation is closely associated with the generation of singletons (sequences appearing only once in the dataset). We noted a notably higher number of singletons in the MinION data. While this contributes to the identification of a greater number of distinct species (Fig. 4), it must be interpreted with caution due to the intrinsic high error rate of the MinION platform. The higher per-base error rate of Nanopore sequencing compared to Illumina can artificially inflate sequence diversity by generating spurious sequence variants that manifest as singletons, potentially leading to an overestimation of rare taxa. In conclusion, these findings collectively indicate that while long-read sequencing platforms hold the potential to discover a larger number of new sequences for species identification, rigorous bioinformatics filtering is essential to distinguish true biological diversity from sequencing artifacts.

The selection of primers plays a pivotal role in shaping the outcomes of human gut microbial studies [25, 26]. A comprehensive investigation by Soergel et al. [27] established that the choice of primer pairs and read lengths significantly influences the rate and accuracy of taxonomic classification. Their work, based on 374 viable pairings of 22 forward and 22 reverse primers in paired-end reads, emphasized that the most informative sequence region may vary, even within samples from the human gut. Abellan-Schneyder et al. [28] delved into the human gut microbial profile using various primer pairs, encompassing regions such as V1–V2, V1–V3, V3–V4, V4, V4–V5, V6–V8, and V6–V8. Their research underscored the substantial impact of primer choice on microbial composition and called for independent validation of profiles generated with different primers. Notably, they found that primers targeting the V3–V4 region exhibited commendable performance for human gut samples. Our current study corroborates this observation, indicating that the utilization of V3–V4 region primers demonstrates favorable performance in human gut samples when employing the MinION platform (Fig. 5). Moreover, considering the MinION system’s cost-effectiveness, with low initial costs and affordable per-sample expenses (approximately 50 USD, compared to around 20 USD for MiSeq), it emerges as a practical choice for taxonomic classification in clinical microbiology, particularly when focusing on the V3–V4 region.

Numerous diseases, including allergies, obesity, and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), have been associated with reduced Bifidobacterium genus levels [29]. Some Bifidobacterium species exhibit positive probiotic effects [30], emphasizing the pivotal role of Bifidobacterium in the human gut microbiota. Precisely, our study found that most samples sequenced with the long-read MinION platform using the V1–V9 region displayed non-significant correlations at the genus level when compared to the short-read MiSeq platform targeting both the V3–V4 and V4 regions (Fig. 5). This observation may stem from challenges in amplifying certain genera, particularly Bifidobacterium, using “universal” primers designed for the full-length 16S rRNA gene (the 27F and 1492 R primers). Previous research has documented that these full-length primers possess sequence mismatches, failing to amplify more than 40% of purified Actinobacteria isolates [31]. In contrast, the analyses targeting the V3–V4 and V4 regions employed different primer pairs (341F/805 R and 515F/806 R) that do not exhibit this specific bias against the Actinobacteria phylum. Consequently, when these sub-regions were targeted, the underrepresentation of Bifidobacterium was circumvented on both the MiSeq and MinION platforms. Nonetheless, for future full-length sequencing studies, tailored primers can be designed to enhance classification outcomes for specific challenging taxa such as Bifidobacterium [17, 32]. Therefore, the development of optimized primer sequences capable of effectively covering a broad spectrum of bacterial species is paramount [33]. In conclusion, these research results suggest that the rational selection of PCR primers is of crucial importance in clinical microbiology research. Although sequencing platforms with long read lengths such as MinION can identify more novel ASVs and provide higher classification resolution at the genus level, when using primers like 27F/1492 R that cover the entire V1–V9 region, their inherent amplification bias may lead to underestimation or missed detection of key clinical-related taxa such as Bifidobacterium. Therefore, researchers need to make a balance between the species-level resolution brought by the full-length sequence pursuit and the potential deviations introduced by the primers themselves. For the accurate and economical characterization of the gut microbiome, the use of optimized primers (such as targeting the V3–V4 region) still holds significant value.
