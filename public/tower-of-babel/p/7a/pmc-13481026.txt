Quality of Life among Iraqi Ostomy Patients and Its Associations with Demographic and Clinical Characteristics of Patients
Authors: Alice Khachian, Belqis H. A. Al-Zamili, Tahereh A. K. Khoshnazar, Shima Haghani, Masoud Rezaei, Saeb F. Al-Saadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The surgical creation of an ostomy can substantially affect patients’ daily functioning and quality of life (QoL). However, no previous study has evaluated QoL among ostomy patients in Iraq, and the influence of demographic and clinical factors remains insufficiently explored.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional study included 200 ostomy patients recruited from three oncology centers in Baghdad, Iraq. QoL was assessed using the City of Hope–Quality of Life Ostomy Questionnaire (CoH–QoL–OQ). Associations between demographic and clinical variables and QoL were analyzed using multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA), adjusting for age and sex.Results:Based on the total QoL score, none of the patients had a good QoL; 92.00% had moderate QoL and 8.00% had low QoL. Patients aged ≥48 years had lower overall QoL compared with younger patients (η² =0.05, p = 0.001). Lower QoL was also observed in patients with malignant tumors before surgery (η² =0.10, p < 0.001) and those receiving chemotherapy after surgery (η² = 0.07, p = 0.001). Social QoL differed significantly across marital status, ostomy type, tumor type, and treatment methods (all p < 0.01).Conclusions:Iraqi ostomy patients experience low-to-moderate QoL. Age, marital status, ostomy type, tumor malignancy, and postsurgical treatment methods are significant determinants of QoL in this population.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is a significant global health concern, ranking as one of the most common and deadly cancers worldwide.[1] The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was 19.5 per 100000, with higher rates observed in developed countries due to lifestyle factors and aging populations.[1] Colorectal cancer is currently the sixth most common cancer among men and the fifth among women in Iraq, with the median age at diagnosis around 55 years.[2] One of the common treatment approaches for patients with colorectal cancer is colostomy. Colostomy or fecal ostomy is a surgically produced incision in the abdomen that permits intestinal contents to bypass the anus, permanently or temporarily, which includes colostomy, ileostomy, and urostomy.[3] Patients with colostomy use a pouch collecting system as a result of the inability to control stool flow.[3] Therefore, colostomy and changes in patients’ appearance due to the surgery might be associated with mental and sexual problems, physical dissatisfaction, tiredness, and low self-esteem in these patients.[4] In addition, colostomy may have a significant effect on health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) in these patients.[5]

There are different definitions for QoL. However, in the healthcare system, it is defined as the well-being of an individual in terms of health and happiness.[6] For patients, it is defined as a patient’s perceived health status in relation to physical, psychological, social, and spiritual aspects.[6] Therefore, researchers who assess the QoL of ostomy patients should focus on physical health, psychological status, social relationships, and spiritual aspects of patients in their daily life. A large number of studies have shown a low score of QoL in ostomy patients.[789] Current evidence reveals that several factors, such as ostomy management difficulties, access to medical coverage, and adequate reimbursement for services and supplies, can affect the QoL of patients with an ostomy.[7] Besides, the incidence of ostomy complications due to the lack of standard care might negatively affect the QoL of patients.[789] Life limitations in either physical or psychosocial areas for patients and lack of family and social support are other factors that lead to a significant reduction in the ostomy patients’ QoL, particularly social, psychological, and spiritual aspects.[10]

Despite the evidence, the influence of other factors, such as demographic and clinical factors on patients’ QoL, is of great importance. In a 2019 systematic review, Kristensen et al.[11] reported that colostomy had more impact on younger patients than older ones and QoL in women was reduced more than in men. Most studies in the systematic review were from Western countries. The evaluation of QoL in ostomy patients and determining the factors affecting it are important in the Middle East, particularly in Iraq, where the prevalence of colorectal cancer is high.[2] However, until now, no study has assessed QoL in Iraqi ostomy patients. Different cultures and beliefs among Iraqi people profoundly influence self-esteem and overall QoL in ostomy patients, particularly through social norms, religious practices, and community attitudes.[12] Iraqi society places significant emphasis on bodily integrity, social participation, and religious observance, which can make adapting to an ostomy challenging for many individuals.[13] Therefore, the current study was conducted to assess QoL and its subscales (physical, psychological, social, and spiritual aspects) in Iraqi ostomy patients. Another aim of this study was to assess the associations of demographic and clinical variables with QoL in these patients.

Materials and Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted among inpatients and outpatients referred to oncology centers of three hospitals including the Martyr Ghazi Hariri Hospital for Specialized Surgery, Private Nursing Home Hospital, and AL-Amal National Hospital for Cancer Management in Baghdad, Iraq, from 2022 to 2023. These hospitals are located in the center of the governorates of Baghdad and each contains specialized centers for cancer treatment. In total, 250 ostomy patients were invited to participate in the current study. However, 200 patients accepted the invitation (response rate: 80%). Patients were selected using the convenience sampling method. Inclusion criteria were: age range of ≥18 years, colorectal cancer diagnosed by a physician for more than 6 months, having undergone ostomy for at least the last 3 months, being able to read, speak, and understand Arabic, and having healthy vision and hearing. Patients who had cognitive impairment based on physician diagnosis and those who were not willing to participate in the current study were excluded. The setting was the oncology outpatient clinic or hospital ward, as patients were recruited during their visits. The researcher introduced herself, explained the study purpose, and asked participants to complete the self-administered questionnaires within 15 to 20 minutes during clinic visits or hospital stay. If participants were unable to complete the questionnaire alone, a female assistant helped them. After the completion of each questionnaire, the female assistant checked the completed questionnaire and if needed, she asked the participant to complete the missed information. Therefore, we had no missing data for the 200 patients.

To calculate the minimum sample size needed to assess the QoL in patients with ostomy at a 95% confidence interval (with a type 1 error rate of 5%), an accuracy margin of d = 0.15, and a standard deviation of 1.07 8, a total of 200 participants were necessary.

We assessed QoL in ostomy patients using the City of Hope–Quality of Life Ostomy Questionnaire (CoH–QoL–OQ).[14] This questionnaire contains 43 questions on different aspects of QoL including physical health (questions 1–11), psychological health (questions 12–24), social health (questions 25–36), and spiritual health (questions 37–43). The questions of this part are scored on a Likert’s rating scale from 0 to 10; the scores are subsequently applied to measure the QoL mean score. To calculate the total scores of QoL and its subscales, scores of some questions, including 1–12, 15, 18, 19, 22–30, 32–34, and 37, should be reversed. Subscale scores are calculated by adding all the scores of each subscale and dividing their sum by the number of items in that subscale. A total QoL score is calculated by adding the scores on all 10-point items and dividing by the total number of items.

The Arabic translation of the English CoH–QoL–OQ was conducted according to the forward–backward translation guidelines recommended by the World Health Organization. The translation was conducted by a five-person team consisting of a urology surgeon, a colorectal surgeon, and three nurses who possessed postgraduate degrees. It is noteworthy that all members of the team were native Arabic speakers and demonstrated a high level of proficiency in the English language. Each individual translated independently. The Arabic version underwent revisions in accordance with recommendations provided by the participants. This process was repeated iteratively until a consensus was reached regarding the adequacy of the content. The questionnaire was translated from Arabic into English by an independent translator. The translation process yielded only minor discrepancies, which were subsequently resolved through consensus within the panel. To conclude the process, the English back-translated rendition was juxtaposed with the original CoH–QoL–OQ English version to verify the preservation of the questionnaire’s comprehensive meaning and contextual nuances.

The validity of the Arabic tool was determined based on content validity. After translating the questionnaire and using the opinions of the research colleagues and the statistical consultant, it was provided to 10 members of academic nurses and surgeons. The content validity of the instrument was confirmed after using their suggested comments and corrections. Questionnaires were completed by 10 eligible clients. In the end, Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.95 and correlation coefficients for physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions of QoL expectancy were 0.82, 0.88, 0.83, and 0.78, respectively.

We used a research-made questionnaire to collect data on sex (men/women), age (year), marital status (single/married), education (university or nonuniversity educated), occupation (unemployed/employed), residence of the participant (urban/rural), duration of the colostomy (6 months/6–12 months/>12 months), type of cancer tumor before the surgery (benign/malignant), treatment of the participant after surgery (chemotherapy/radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy), and type of the colostomy (permanent/temporary).

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 21 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), under a licensed agreement.

Categorical variables were expressed as number and percent. Continuous variables were reported as mean and standard error (SE). To assess the distribution of categorical variables between men and women, we used the Chi-square test. The multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was used to assess differences in QoL scores among the different levels of demographic and clinical variables. To do MANCOVA, we examined the normal distribution of total QoL score and its subscales using Q–Q plot and skewness values. Fortunately, the skewness values for QoL scores were between −1 and +1 and, therefore, the continuous variables had normal distribution. In addition, in the MANCOVA, we controlled for age and gender, which are the most important confounders in terms of QoL.[15] Due to the limited sample size, we could not adjust for other variables. p value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical considerations

All subjects provided written informed consent forms before study enrolment. The Bioethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, approved the study (IR.IUMS.REC.1402.373).

Results

In addition, 58.50% of patients had malignant colorectal cancer before the surgery. Moreover, stoma was permanent among 46% of recruited patients. The associations of demographic and clinical variables with QoL scores among ostomy patients are shown in Table 3. Ostomy patients with an age range of ≥48 years had lower total QoL scores compared with patients aged <48 years (5.03 ± 0.08 compared with 5.38 ± 0.07, η²: 0.05, p = 0.001). This significant difference was also seen for the social subscale of QoL. In addition, married patients had lower QoL scores in social subscale than single patients (4.45 ± 0.18 compared with 5.16 ± 0.13, η²: 0.05, p = 0.001). Such a difference was also seen for ostomy type, so that patients with temporary ostomy had lower social scores compared with those who had permanent ostomy (4.55 ± 0.14 compared with 5.35 ± 0.15, η²: 0.07, p < 0.001). In terms of treatment type after the surgery, the total score of QoL, as well as scores of psychological and social subscales, was lower among patients under chemotherapy than those who used radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy. For other variables including sex, education, occupation, residential area, and duration of ostomy, we found no significant difference in total QoL and its subscales.

Discussion

We found that none of the ostomy patients who participated in the current study had a good QoL; however, 92% and 8% of patients had a moderate and low QoL score, respectively. Our findings were in line with those obtained from the study of Zewude et al.,[9] in which only 11.00% of Ethiopian ostomy patients had a good QoL. Additionally, in a cross-sectional study on Iranian ostomy patients, Mahjoubi et al.[16] reported that having a colostomy was associated with a high level of emotional and sexual function impairment. In another study, Anaraki et al. concluded that 70% of ostomy patients were dissatisfied with sexual activities and more than 50% of patients reported feelings of depression following stoma surgery.[17]

In contrast, a cross-sectional study revealed that >50% of ostomy patients had high QoL that was associated with stoma sites.[16] However, the high QoL in the patients was due to an appropriate stoma site and patients with an inappropriate stoma site had lower QoL. In a systematic review of 14 prevailingly descriptive cross-sectional studies, Vonk-Klaassen et al.[5] concluded that a colostomy influenced the QoL of patients negatively. This was also reported in a narrative review.[7] Overall, based on our findings and those obtained from the previous studies, it seems that colostomy can adversely affect the QoL of patients with colorectal cancer. However, the type of ostomy might have a role in this regard.

Comparisons with studies from Ethiopia, Iran, and Europe strengthen the external validity of these findings but differences in cultural, healthcare, and socioeconomic contexts may influence QoL outcomes. For example, in Iraq, strong cultural emphasis on bodily integrity, social participation, and religious practices may exacerbate social and spiritual challenges for ostomy patients compared with populations where social stigma is less pronounced.[1213] Healthcare access and availability of ostomy care supplies also vary widely between these regions, affecting patients’ physical and psychological well-being.[13] Therefore, cross-cultural differences should be explicitly considered in devising interventions to improve QoL.

Unlike the overall QoL, some subdomains ‒ physical, psychological, social, and spiritual ‒ had patients with good scores. This contrast may reflect that while patients can maintain well-being in specific facets of life, the overall burden of living with an ostomy, including managing complex physical care and psychosocial adjustments, dampens their total QoL score.[5] It is also possible that positive experiences in particular domains do not fully compensate for challenges in others, such as social stigma or spiritual distress, leading to no patients reaching the threshold for a good overall QoL. This aligns with previous research indicating that QoL in ostomy patients is multifactorial and domain-specific interventions are necessary to improve overall well-being.[57]

The discussion here benefits from integrating well-established QoL frameworks that differentiate physical (e.g., pain, fatigue), psychological (e.g., depression, anxiety), social (e.g., isolation, social roles), and spiritual (e.g., meaning, faith) domains. Clear articulation of how ostomy impacts each domain allows for more targeted clinical interventions. The current findings highlight that social QoL is particularly affected, consistent with the literature showing that social isolation and reduced confidence are central issues for ostomy patients.[1718] Embedding these results in a biopsychosocial-spiritual model emphasizes the need for multidisciplinary care including psychological counseling and spiritual support alongside physical rehabilitation.

Many problems, such as poor placement of stoma, use of the improper appliance, development of a hernia, and leakage from stoma, might be involved in the low QoL in ostomy patients.[1920] Another problem reported by the patients was a change in clothing style due to stoma surgery.[21] Ostomy patients, for reasons such as location of ostomy, weight changes, and changes in body appearance, were forced to change their clothing style, which itself reduced their QoL.[22] In the current study, the highest percentage for low QoL was for the social subscale. However, colostomy had the highest impact on social subscale compared with other subscales. This might be explained by the fact that physical and psychological disorders resulting from a stoma may gradually reduce a person’s confidence and his social relations.[2324] These factors go hand in hand and lead to some degree of social isolation.[24]

In the current study, we found that higher age was associated with lower QoL in overall and in social subscale. In a cross-sectional study in Poland, Chrobak-Bień et al.[25] reported a significant inverse association between age and QoL in ostomy patients. Older patients may suffer from other chronic diseases compared with younger ones and also they may have less tolerance to the complications of stoma. In addition, we found that married patients had lower scores for the social subscale of QoL compared with single patients. Married patients may have more relationships with other people than single patients and, therefore, they may be more exposed to negative views of other people.[26] This exposure may result in some degrees of social isolation in these patients.[26] Additionally, marital relationships could be strained by changes in physical appearance and sexual function postsurgery, further reducing social QoL.[27] Future studies should investigate these mechanisms through qualitative methods or longitudinal designs to clarify causal pathways. For example, examining the dynamics within marital relationships postostomy could elucidate how social support or strain affects social QoL.[23]

In the current study, patients with malignant tumors before surgery had lower QoL compared with those who had benign tumors. In a review article, Chow et al.[28] concluded that patients with benign sinonasal tumors had a higher QoL pre- and postoperatively compared to those with malignancy. It should be noted that patients with malignancy have worse psychological distress compared with patients with benign tumors.[28] In addition, malignant tumors can provide more physical, psychological, and social complications compared with benign tumors.[29] In this study, patients under chemotherapy after the surgery had lower QoL than those who used radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy. This was also reported in a European multicenter study, in which long-term HR-QoL was in favor of patients treated with chemoradiotherapy compared with patients treated with chemotherapy.[30] This disparity might be explained by different side effects, prices, and efficacy of these treatment methods.

Regarding the nonsignificant differences observed for sex, education, occupation, residence, and duration of ostomy, these null findings are consistent with some prior studies where these demographic variables did not predict QoL outcomes, indicating that other factors, such as age, type of tumor, and treatment methods, might play more substantial roles in QoL variations.[9] The lack of association with education and occupation may reflect cultural or healthcare system factors where these variables exert less influence on patients’ daily experiences with ostomy care.[7] However, further research with larger sample sizes is needed to confirm these relationships and explore potential mediators.

Our study had some strengths. This was the first study that assessed QoL among Iraqi ostomy patients. Furthermore, we recruited ostomy patients from three oncology centers in Baghdad, to increase the generalizability of our data. In addition, all patients in the current study provided complete data and the drop-out rate was zero. However, some limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting our results. The design of our study was cross-sectional prohibiting inferring a causal link between demographic/clinical variables and QoL in ostomy patients. The sample size of this study was small and future studies should recruit more patients to assess QoL of ostomy patients. Although we controlled for age and sex in the statistical analyses, further control for other residual confounding variables such as medications and dietary intakes might be needed to reach an independent association between demographic/clinical variables and QoL in ostomy patients. In addition, despite the use of a validated questionnaire for QoL assessment, some degree of measurement errors and misclassification may occur and we cannot entirely exclude this possibility from our study.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between demographic and clinical variables and QoL outcomes. Second, the relatively modest sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect associations for some variables. Third, although age and sex were adjusted for in the analyses, residual confounding by factors, such as medication use, dietary intake, and comorbidities, cannot be excluded. Finally, QoL was assessed using a self-reported questionnaire, which may be subject to reporting bias. Future longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes are recommended to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

We found that ostomy patients in Iraq had a low-to-moderate QoL and none of the patients had a good QoL. Some demographic and clinical variables, including age, marital status, ostomy type, type of cancer tumor before surgery, and treatment methods after surgery, may affect QoL in these patients. Healthcare professionals in Iraq need comprehensive training in ostomy care to support physical and psychological rehabilitation. Besides, policymakers should ensure affordable access to ostomy supplies and psychosocial services, and patient support organizations must develop culturally sensitive programs to reduce stigma and enhance social reintegration. This multifaceted approach can help address the complex factors influencing QoL among Iraqi ostomy patients.
