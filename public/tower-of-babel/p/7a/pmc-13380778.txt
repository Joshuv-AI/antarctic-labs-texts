Total Hip Arthroplasty Frequently Alters Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee Classification: Implications for Knee Arthroplasty Planning
Authors: Noriyuki Aibara, Takeaki Hashimoto, Tatsuaki Matsumoto, Kazuya Kaneda, Akihito Oya, Shu Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal hip arthroplasty (THA) can alter lower-limb alignment. Although the coronal plane alignment of the knee (CPAK) classification describes constitutional alignment and is associated with outcomes after total knee arthroplasty, whether CPAK classification is preserved following THA remains unclear. We investigated changes in CPAK classification after THA and factors associated with CPAK transition and postoperative alignment.MethodsThis retrospective study evaluated 76 knees undergoing THA. Preoperative and 1-year postoperative standing long-leg radiographs were analyzed. Lateral distal femoral angle, medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), arithmetic hip–knee–ankle angle (aHKA), and joint line obliquity (JLO) were measured, and CPAK type was determined preoperatively and postoperatively. Multivariable logistic, multinomial logistic, and linear regression analyses were performed.ResultsCPAK classification changed in 43 knees (56.6%) after THA. Lateral distal femoral angle increased from 86.7° to 88.2° and JLO from 173.6° to 175.6°, whereas aHKA decreased from 0.15° to −0.81°; MPTA showed no significant change. Valgus knees shifted toward varus CPAK types (21 knees), whereas varus knees shifted toward valgus types (9 knees). The most common transition was type 2 to type 1 (6 knees). Preoperative aHKA and JLO independently predicted postoperative changes. In 54 unilateral cases, greater leg length difference correlated negatively with ΔMPTA and ΔaHKA.ConclusionsMore than half of knees demonstrated a CPAK change after THA. These transitions were driven by femoral alignment–related changes rather than tibial parameters. It suggests that surgical sequencing of THA and total knee arthroplasty should be carefully considered in patients with concomitant hip and knee osteoarthritis.Keywords: Total hip arthroplasty, Coronal plane alignment of the knee, CPAK classification, LLA, Joint line obliquity, Arithmetic hip–knee–ankle angle

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) has been widely recognized as an effective surgical intervention for end-stage hip osteoarthritis (OA), providing reliable pain relief and functional improvement [1]. In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward lower-limb alignment (LLA) following arthroplasty [2]. Choi et al. reported that THA influences ipsilateral LLA and demonstrated a tendency for the arithmetic hip–knee–ankle angle (aHKA) to shift toward varus following THA [3]. However, its influence on LLA remains unclear.

In the context of the LLA, the coronal plane alignment of the knee (CPAK) classification has been introduced to better characterize individual variations in native LLA based on the combination of femoral and tibial coronal alignment parameters [4]. Although originally developed for native LLA, CPAK has increasingly been used to describe global LLA patterns and has been associated with biomechanical loading characteristics and clinical outcomes after reconstructive procedures [[4], [5], [6]]. Also, concerns remain about the accuracy of CPAK classification from standard two-dimensional (2D) radiographs, as the overall agreement with upright three-dimensional (3D) computed tomography (CT) was only 48.5% according to Sasaki et al., highlighting the limitations of 2D measurements [6]. However, whether CPAK classification is preserved after THA remains unknown.

In THA, restoration of femoral anteversion, the hip center, femoral offset, and leg length inevitably alters lower-limb biomechanics [2,3]. These changes may secondarily affect coronal alignment parameters of the femur and tibia, potentially resulting in a transition of CPAK classification after surgery [2,3]. However, most existing studies on alignment changes following THA have focused on isolated parameters, such as aHKA or femoral offset, rather than integrated alignment patterns [3,5,[7], [8], [9]].Therefore, understanding CPAK changes after THA may have important implications for arthroplasty planning, particularly in patients with coexisting hip and knee osteoarthritis.

Furthermore, patient-specific anatomical factors, including acetabular dysplasia and preoperative alignment characteristics, may influence postoperative alignment adaptation. [5,7]

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate changes in CPAK classification before and after primary THA and to identify factors associated with CPAK transition. We hypothesized that a substantial proportion of patients would demonstrate a change in CPAK category following THA and that preoperative characteristics would be associated with these alignment transitions.

Material and methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective observational study evaluated changes in CPAK classification following THA. A total of 76 knees that underwent THA for hip osteoarthritis, developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), osteonecrosis of the femoral head, or post-traumatic arthritis at a single tertiary referral center in 2024 were screened for eligibility.

The inclusion criteria were (1) availability of standardized preoperative and postoperative full-length standing radiographs of the lower extremities and (2) complete radiographic data allowing CPAK classification both before and after THA. Exclusion criteria included a history of femoral or tibial osteotomy, neuromuscular disorders affecting LLA, and inadequate radiographic quality.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Keio University School of Medicine (approval number: 2023-1011-2). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were primary THA performed by a single hip surgeon with approximately 2 decades of clinical experience using a standardized technique through a minimally invasive anterolateral (OCM [Orthopädische Chirurgie München]) approach in the lateral decubitus position. Implant selection, surgical approach, and perioperative management followed institutional protocols. No attempt was made intraoperatively to intentionally modify coronal alignment.

The OCM approach is a minimally invasive anterolateral technique performed in the lateral decubitus position [10]. This approach differs from the anterior-based muscle-sparing and anterolateral minimally invasive approaches, which are typically performed in the supine position [11,12].

Radiographic assessment

Standardized standing long-leg anteroposterior radiographs were obtained preoperatively and at 1 year postoperatively. Radiographs were acquired with patients standing in a neutral position with both patellae facing forward.

•Medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA)

All measurements were performed digitally using calibrated imaging software. Each measurement was obtained by a single independent observer blinded to the clinical data. To assess measurement reliability, 20 randomly selected radiographs were remeasured after a 2-week interval by the same observer, and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to evaluate intraobserver reliability.

Ipsilateral knee osteoarthritis was assessed using the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system based on preoperative weight-bearing radiographs of the ipsilateral knee. Each knee was graded from 0 to 4 [13]. Patients who had previously undergone total knee arthroplasty (TKA) were recorded separately and excluded from KL grading.

Postoperative leg length discrepancy and femoral offset were measured on anteroposterior radiographs of the hip. In unilateral THA cases, leg length discrepancy was calculated as the difference in total limb length between the operated and contralateral limbs, with positive values indicating that the operated limb was longer than the contralateral limb. Femoral offset was measured as the perpendicular distance from the femoral shaft axis to the center of the femoral head for both the operated and contralateral sides. In bilateral THA cases, in which no native contralateral reference was available, only the absolute side-to-side differences in leg length and femoral offset between the 2 operated hips were calculated.

CPAK classification

CPAK classification was determined according to the original description. Knees were categorized into one of 9 CPAK types preoperatively and postoperatively [4]. A CPAK change was defined as any transition from the preoperative CPAK category to a different postoperative category.

Assessment of hip dysplasia

Hip dysplasia was evaluated using the center-edge (CE) angle measured on preoperative radiographs. In addition to using the CE angle as a continuous variable, dysplasia was treated as a categorical variable and defined as present or absent according to established threshold of 20 degrees.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the proportion of knees demonstrating a change in CPAK classification after THA. Secondary outcomes included the direction and pattern of CPAK transitions and the association between CPAK change and preoperative factors.

Data analyses

Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Comparisons between knees with and without CPAK change were performed using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables, and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent factors associated with postoperative CPAK change. Variables entered into the model included preoperative alignment parameters and the CE angle. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported.

To address whether the magnitude of hip restoration influenced postoperative knee alignment changes, Pearson correlation analyses (with Spearman correlation as a sensitivity check) were performed between postoperative leg length difference and signed femoral offset difference (operated minus contralateral side) and changes in knee alignment parameters (ΔLDFA, ΔMPTA, Δjoint line obliquity [JLO], and ΔaHKA) in the 54 unilateral THA cases. Because this analysis was exploratory and involved 8 pairwise comparisons, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate–adjusted q values are reported alongside raw P values; the interpretation is based on raw P values.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 30.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A 2-tailed P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

More than half of knees (43/76, 56.6%) demonstrated a change in CPAK classification after THA. Preoperatively, the mean LDFA was 86.7 ± 3.2°, MPTA was 86.9 ± 2.6°, JLO was 173.6 ± 4.3°, and aHKA was 0.2 ± 3.9°. The mean CE angle was 14.2 ± 11.5°, and DDH was present in 20 patients (26.3%).

Ipsilateral knee OA was assessed using the KL classification: grade 0 in 28 knees (36.8%), grade 1 in 28 knees (36.8%), grade 2 in 11 knees (14.5%), grade 3 in 3 knees (3.9%), and grade 4 in 1 knee (1.3%). Five knees (6.6%) had previously undergone TKA and were not assigned a KL grade. The majority of knees (73.7%; 56/76) had KL grade 0 or 1, indicating no or minimal concomitant ipsilateral knee OA.

The cohort comprised 54 unilateral and 11 bilateral THA cases. In unilateral cases (n = 54), with the unaffected limb serving as the anatomical reference, the mean signed leg length discrepancy was 2.2 ± 5.8 mm (range: −10 to 27 mm; absolute mean, 3.9 ± 4.8 mm). In bilateral cases (n = 11), in which no native contralateral reference was available, only between-side absolute differences could be characterized: the mean absolute leg length discrepancy was 2.0 ± 2.4 mm (range: 0–8 mm), and the mean absolute femoral offset difference between the 2 operated hips was 4.3 ± 2.6 mm (range: 1–7 mm).

The stable group was defined as knees whose CPAK classification remained unchanged postoperatively. Among knees with a CPAK transition, those showing a shift toward varus, defined as a decrease in aHKA, were classified as the varus group, whereas those showing a shift toward valgus, defined as an increase in aHKA, were classified as the valgus group.

Preoperative aHKA differed significantly among the 3 groups (P = .045). Knees in the varus shift group demonstrated more valgus preoperative alignment, whereas those in the valgus shift group exhibited more varus alignment. The stable group showed intermediate preoperative alignment. No other significant difference was observed among the groups.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with the direction of CPAK transition, using the stable group as the reference category. Increasing age was independently associated with a lower likelihood of undergoing a varus-directed CPAK shift. Preoperative aHKA showed a borderline association with varus shift. No significant predictors were identified for valgus shift.

Association between hip restoration parameters and knee alignment changes

To examine whether the magnitude of hip restoration influenced postoperative knee alignment changes, Pearson correlation analyses (with Spearman correlation as a sensitivity check) were performed in the 54 unilateral THA cases between postoperative leg length difference and signed femoral offset difference and changes in each knee alignment parameter (ΔLDFA, ΔMPTA, ΔJLO, and ΔaHKA) (Table 8). Greater leg length difference was associated with smaller ΔMPTA (r = −0.38; 95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.59 to −0.13; P = .004), ΔaHKA (r = −0.35; 95% CI: −0.56 to −0.09; P = .010) which remained statistically significant after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment. Greater leg length difference was also associated with smaller ΔJLO (r = −0.29; 95% CI: −0.52 to −0.02; P = .034). Greater signed femoral offset difference was associated with smaller ΔMPTA (r = −0.28; 95% CI: −0.51 to −0.02; P = .038) and ΔJLO (r = −0.28; 95% CI: −0.51 to −0.01; P = .039). ΔLDFA was not associated with either leg length difference (r = 0.02; P = .890) or femoral offset difference (r = −0.11; P = .417). Spearman sensitivity analyses showed similar directionality but did not reach statistical significance.

Measurement reliability

Intraobserver reliability was excellent for all alignment parameters: preoperative LDFA (ICC = 0.958, mean absolute difference [MAD] = 0.80°), MPTA (ICC = 0.953, MAD = 0.62°), JLO (ICC = 0.970, MAD = 0.95°), and aHKA (ICC = 0.935, MAD = 1.02°). All parameters exceeded the ICC threshold of 0.90, supporting the reliability of the measurements.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study was that more than half of knees (56.6%) demonstrated a change in CPAK classification following primary THA, despite the absence of any intentional intraoperative modification of coronal LLA. This result supported our hypothesis that THA alters global LLA and highlighted that CPAK classification, originally developed to describe constitutional LLA, is not necessarily preserved after THA. Although previous studies have suggested favorable outcomes when kinematic alignment respects native CPAK classification, CPAK alignment may be altered by proximal joint reconstruction such as THA. [14,15] Consequently, in patients with hip osteoarthritis, CPAK-based alignment concepts should not be assumed to represent immutable constitutional alignment and should be applied cautiously.

The observed changes in CPAK classification appeared to be driven by systematic postoperative alterations in femoral alignment–related factors rather than tibial factors. Specifically, LDFA and JLO increased significantly after THA, whereas MPTA did not change significantly. These findings suggest that postoperative alignment modification after THA occurs predominantly at the femoral level.

Collectively, these findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that THA can influence ipsilateral LLA and knee loading patterns [3]. Furthermore, investigations comparing 2-dimensional radiographic measurements with 3-dimensional assessments have reported that postoperative alignment alterations are predominantly observed in femoral parameters, whereas tibial parameters such as MPTA remain relatively unchanged [6]. Together, these observations support the concept that THA mainly affects femoral alignment-related factors.

One possible explanation is that restoration of femoral anteversion, hip center position, femoral offset, and leg length during THA alters lower-limb mechanics. Among these factors, restoration of femoral anteversion may play a particularly important role because changes in femoral rotation can directly influence the apparent LDFA measured on coronal radiographs. Sasaki et al. demonstrated that rotational changes of the femur can substantially affect coronal alignment measurements [6]. Therefore, rotational correction at the hip level may represent a key mechanism underlying the observed changes in LDFA and subsequent CPAK transitions.

With regard to the distribution of CPAK classification, the preoperative and postoperative CPAK distribution in our cohort appeared broadly comparable to those reported in healthy Korean and Taiwanese populations, in which varus-oriented phenotypes are commonly observed. [16,17] While direct comparison is limited by differences in study populations, our findings do not contradict previously described alignment patterns in Asian knees.

Importantly, no preoperative demographic or radiographic factor independently predicted CPAK transition in multivariable logistic regression analysis. It suggested that CPAK transitions after THA may not be easily predictable based on preoperative parameters alone.

However, when CPAK transitions were analyzed according to directionality, distinct patterns emerged. The finding that 21 of 76 knees (27.6%) demonstrated a varus-directed change in CPAK classification is consistent with previous reports showing that THA can induce varus shifts in ipsilateral LLA [3]. Choi et al. reported that the mean aHKA shifted from −1.4° preoperatively to −2.7° after THA, indicating a postoperative drift toward varus alignment [3]. Similarly, in the present study, the mean aHKA changed from 0.15° ± 3.93° preoperatively to −0.81° ± 3.88° postoperatively.

Consistent with this tendency, the most common CPAK transition was from type 2 to type 1 (6 knees). Because both types share near-neutral joint line orientation but differ mainly in overall limb alignment, this transition likely reflects a mild varus shift in global alignment after THA rather than a change in joint line morphology.

Patients who experienced a varus CPAK shift tended to have more valgus preoperative alignment, whereas those who shifted toward valgus CPAK types exhibited more varus alignment preoperatively. This regression-to-the-mean phenomenon was further supported by linear regression analyses demonstrating a strong negative association between preoperative aHKA and postoperative change in aHKA. These results suggested that greater preoperative varus alignment was associated with a larger magnitude of correction. Age was also independently associated with the magnitude and direction of aHKA change, implying that age-related factors such as soft tissue compliance and muscle weakness may influence postoperative realignment behavior.

Changes in JLO followed a similar pattern. Preoperative JLO was the dominant predictor of postoperative JLO correction, explaining nearly 40% of the variance in ΔJLO. This finding indicated that knees with more varus-oriented joint lines preoperatively underwent greater postoperative correction. Notably, joint line correction appeared largely independent of changes in global limb alignment.

The role of acetabular dysplasia in CPAK transition was of particular interest. [9] Although DDH was common in the present cohort, neither the presence of dysplasia nor the CE angle was significantly associated with CPAK change or transition direction. This finding suggested that, once hip reconstruction is achieved, postoperative LLA adaptation may be governed more by femoral alignment-related factors than by acetabular factors. Nevertheless, the observed trend toward increased CPAK change in patients with smaller CE angles needs further investigation in larger cohorts. Importantly, Pearson correlation analyses in the 54 unilateral THA cases revealed that greater leg length difference and greater signed femoral offset difference (operated minus contralateral) were each associated with more varus-directed knee alignment changes, particularly at the tibial (ΔMPTA) and global limb (ΔaHKA) levels, whereas the distal femoral angle (ΔLDFA) was unrelated to either hip parameter. This pattern suggests that the magnitude of hip geometry restoration partially drives postoperative knee alignment behavior; however, because LDFA—the principal femoral contributor to CPAK reclassification in this cohort—was independent of hip restoration metrics, CPAK reclassification after THA likely reflects a combination of measurable hip-restoration effects (tibial-side and global alignment changes) and additional biomechanical adaptations such as femoral rotational changes that are not captured by leg length or offset alone.

With regard to concomitant knee osteoarthritis, the majority of knees in the present cohort (73.7%; 56/76) had KL grade 0 or 1, indicating no or minimal ipsilateral knee OA at the time of THA. This finding suggests that severe concomitant knee OA was uncommon and is unlikely to have substantially confounded the radiographic alignment measurements or CPAK classification in this study.

Regarding the adequacy of hip geometry restoration, postoperative leg length discrepancy and femoral offset were measured retrospectively to characterize the degree of anatomical reconstruction achieved. In unilateral cases, the mean signed leg length discrepancy was 2.2 ± 5.8 mm and the mean femoral offset was 45.1 ± 6.9 mm on the operated side vs 41.5 ± 7.6 mm on the contralateral unaffected side (mean absolute side-to-side offset difference, 5.4 ± 4.9 mm); in bilateral cases, the mean absolute leg length discrepancy was 2.0 ± 2.4 mm and the mean absolute femoral offset difference between the 2 operated hips was 4.3 ± 2.6 mm. On average, these values indicate satisfactory restoration of hip geometry; however, individual variability was present, and systematic preoperative templating data were unavailable for formal comparison. Future prospective studies with standardized preoperative planning and intraoperative documentation of hip geometry restoration targets would help clarify how the degree of anatomical reconstruction influences postoperative LLA changes.

These findings have several important clinical implications. First, CPAK classification should not be considered a fixed constitutional alignment once hip reconstruction has been performed. Second, postoperative LLA and loading patterns after THA may differ substantially from preoperative ones. These findings underscore that the sequence of TKA and THA should not be regarded merely as a matter of surgical preference. In clinical practice, the decision to perform THA before TKA is typically guided by multiple clinical factors, including the severity of hip symptoms, referred knee pain attributable to hip pathology, and the prospect of improved hip mobility following arthroplasty [18,19]. The potential influence of THA on LLA and CPAK classification should therefore be regarded as an important supplementary consideration in this decision-making process.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This study was retrospective and conducted at a single center with a relatively modest sample size, which may have limited statistical power to detect small but clinically relevant associations. Secondly, radiographic measurements were performed by a single observer, precluding evaluation of interobserver reliability, although intraobserver reliability was excellent for all alignment parameters (ICC range 0.935–0.970; MAD range 0.62°–1.02°). In addition, clinical outcomes and knee symptoms were not assessed. Moreover, postoperative radiographs were obtained at 1 year, and longer-term alignment adaptation could not be evaluated.

Furthermore, the predominance of female patients in our cohort (82.9%) reflects the epidemiology of hip osteoarthritis in the Japanese population but may limit the applicability of these findings to male patients and to populations with different sex distributions, as hip geometry and LLA parameters are known to differ between sexes [20,21]. In addition, while postoperative leg length discrepancy and femoral offset were measured to characterize the quality of hip reconstruction, formal comparison against preoperative templating targets was not possible due to the retrospective design of the study. Future prospective studies with standardized documentation of hip reconstruction goals and outcomes are warranted.

Another limitation of this study is that CPAK classification was based on 2D standing radiographs, which may have limited accuracy compared with 3D imaging modalities [6]. Although upright 3D CT may offer superior precision in assessing coronal knee alignment, it remains largely a research tool available only at specialized institutions and has not yet been adopted into routine clinical practice. In contrast, weight-bearing 2D radiography is widely accessible, cost-effective, and continues to represent the clinical standard for preoperative arthroplasty planning, as it effectively captures functional alignment under physiological loading conditions. Therefore, this limitation is considered inherent to current clinical practice rather than a fundamental methodological flaw.

Lastly, the correlation analyses between hip restoration parameters and knee alignment changes were exploratory and involved multiple pairwise comparisons; while we report Benjamini–Hochberg–adjusted q values for transparency, the manuscript's interpretation is based on raw P values, and the borderline significance of several pairs warrants confirmation in larger cohorts.

Despite these limitations, this study provides new insight into the dynamic nature of CPAK classification following THA. By demonstrating that CPAK transitions are common and primarily driven by femoral alignment–related factors, the present findings contribute to a better understanding of lower-limb biomechanics after hip arthroplasty. Future studies incorporating patient-reported outcome measures, longer follow-up periods, and assessment by multiple observers are warranted to further clarify the clinical implications of CPAK changes.

Conclusions

Primary THA frequently altered CPAK classification, with postoperative changes observed in 56.6% of knees. These transitions appeared to be driven mainly by femoral alignment–related changes and may, at least in part, reflect changes in hip or femoral rotation after THA. Accordingly, CPAK classification based on 2-dimensional radiographs may not always reliably represent underlying constitutional alignment after hip reconstruction. Because these changes may influence subsequent knee arthroplasty planning, the sequence of THA and TKA should be considered carefully in patients with concomitant hip and knee osteoarthritis.
