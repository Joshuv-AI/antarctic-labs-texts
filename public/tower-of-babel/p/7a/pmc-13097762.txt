The lysine methyltransferase KMT5a and p53 regulate the expression of the key autophagy adaptor p62/SQSTM1 in glioblastoma
Authors: Rosa Della Monica, Michela Buonaiuto, Mariella Cuomo, Denise Drongitis, Maria Giuseppina Miano, Davide Costabile, Giulia de Riso, Federica Trio, Marta Sabbarese, Sara Ferraro, Lorenza Oliva, Nives Piacentino, Rosaria Cappiello, Gianluca Scotti, Vito Alessandro Lasorsa, Mario Capasso, Alberto De Bellis, Marialaura Del Basso De Caro, Lorenzo Chiariotti, Roberta Visconti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKMT5a, a histone lysine methyltransferase responsible for histone H4 lysine 20 monomethylation (H4K20me1), plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of various human diseases, including cancer. In glioblastoma, KMT5a is frequently overexpressed, with elevated expression levels correlating with poor clinical outcomes, and its inhibition restrains cell growth. The KMT5a-induced H4K20 methylation regulates the expression of several oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, but its transcriptional targets in glioblastoma remain poorly understood. Through RNA-sequencing, we found that the expression of the key autophagy adaptor p62/SQSTM1 was strongly repressed by KMT5a.MethodsTo investigate the regulation of p62/SQSTM1 by KMT5a, both pharmacological inhibition and genetic silencing of KMT5a were performed in U251 glioblastoma cells. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were used to confirm KMT5a binding to the p62/SQSTM1 promoter and to examine histone modification states. The role of additional methyltransferases KMT5b and KMT5c in establishing higher-order methylation (H4K20me2/3) was also assessed. The dependency on p53 status was tested using various glioblastoma cell lines with differing p53 backgrounds (U251, SW1088, and U87MG), as well as through genetic manipulation of p53 expression. This dependency was also examined in primary glioblastoma cells and glioblastoma stem cells.ResultsInhibition or knockdown of KMT5a led to a strong upregulation of p62/SQSTM1 transcription in U251 glioblastoma cells. ChIP assays confirmed direct binding of KMT5a to the p62/SQSTM1 promoter and deposition of the repressive H4K20me1 mark, which was reversed by the demethylase PHF8. Full repression required sequential methylation by KMT5b and KMT5c, indicating a multi-step repression mechanism. Importantly, p62/SQSTM1 induction was observed only in p53-deficient cells and was suppressed by reintroduction of wild-type p53. Conversely, dominant-negative p53 expression in p53-proficient cells restored p62/SQSTM1 induction. Starvation-induced expression of p62/SQSTM1 also required both H4K20 demethylation and p53 displacement from the promoter.ConclusionsKMT5a acts as a key epigenetic repressor of p62/SQSTM1 in glioblastoma through a mechanism dependent on histone methylation and p53 status. Given the emerging roles of both KMT5a and p62/SQSTM1 in glioblastoma progression, these findings also suggest that KMT5a inhibition could serve as a therapeutic strategy specifically in p53-proficient glioblastomas, where p62/SQSTM1 remains repressed. p53 status may therefore serve as a predictive biomarker for the efficacy of KMT5a-targeted therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02665-x.

Introduction

KMT5a, also known as PR-set7, SET8 or SETD8, is a lysine methyltransferase playing a critical role in the pathogenesis of various human diseases [1]. In cancer, a notable substrate of KMT5a methyltransferase activity is p53. KMT5a methylates p53 at lysine 382, thus inhibiting its tumour suppressing functions [2, 3]. Moreover, KMT5a-mediated methylation of Numb at lysine 158 and 163, within its PTB domain, disrupts Numb/p53 interaction, leading to p53 degradation [4]. Several other KMT5a substrates have been identified in cancer cells, including proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and c-MYC [5, 6]. In summary, KMT5a-induced methylation promotes cancer cell proliferation, suggesting that targeting KMT5a could be an effective strategy in cancers driven by its substrates [7].

Of note, KMT5a is the only mammalian enzyme that specifically induces monomethylation of histone H4 at lysine 20 (H4K20me1) [8]. Subsequently, H4K20me1 can be further di- and trimethylated by other methyltransferases but these modifications are more specifically catalysed by KMT5b and KMT5c [9, 10]. Remarkably, H4K20 methylation orchestrates DNA damage repair, thus ensuring genome stability [11]. The KMT5a-induced monomethylation of H4K20, and its subsequent conversion to dimethylation, is essential for the recruitment of 53BP1 to sites of DNA double-strand breaks [12]. Consistently, we have recently demonstrated that the KMT5a inhibitor UNC0379 suppressed glioblastoma cell growth by impairing DNA damage repair, leading to DNA damage accumulation and activation of the genome-safeguarding cell cycle checkpoints. By inhibiting the cell cycle checkpoint kinase Wee1 with adavosertib, UNC0379-DNA damaged, cell cycle arrested cells bypassed checkpoints, progressing to mitosis, where they died by mitotic catastrophe. Notably, UNC0379 synergized with adavosertib in restraining glioblastoma growth also in xenograft mice. Furthermore, we found that KMT5a expression was significantly higher in glioblastomas compared to both normal brain tissues and low-grade gliomas. High KMT5a levels of expression also correlated with poorer prognosis, further highlighting KMT5a potential as a therapeutic target in glioblastoma [13].

However, beyond its role in DDR, KMT5a-induced H4K20me1 is part of a broader histone code regulating transcription. Critically for cancer progression, KMT5a-induced H4K20 methylation exerts predominantly pro-tumorigenic effects by repressing tumour suppressor genes and activating oncogenes [7]. Thus, to investigate the transcriptional consequences of KMT5a inhibition in glioblastoma, we performed RNA sequencing of U251 cells and identified p62/SQSTM1 among the top genes upregulated by UNC0379. p62/SQSTM1 encodes for a multifunctional adaptor protein that plays a central role in selective autophagy, functioning as a receptor that links ubiquitinated cargo to the autophagy machinery. Through its ubiquitin-binding domain and LC3-interacting region, p62/SQSTM1 enables the sequestration of misfolded, damaged, or aggregated proteins into autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation [14]. Subsequent analysis of the p62/SQSTM1 promoter in glioblastoma cells revealed that H4K20 methylation is indeed a histone modification that dictates p62/SQSTM1 transcriptional repression. Finally, we show that p53 activity is required for the KMT5a-mediated repression of p62/SQSTM1.

Methods

Cell cultures and chemical treatments

U251 and U87MG glioblastoma cell lines were cultured in DMEM-GlutaMAX (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, cat. 32430-027) supplemented with 10,000 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 15140-122), 100 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 11360-039), and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, cat. A5256701). SW1088 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 31870082), supplemented with 10% FBS, 2mM GlutaMAX (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 35050061), and 1% non-essential amino acids (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, cat. 21875-034). SW1088 cells were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). U251 cells were obtained from CLS Cell Lines Service GmbH (Eppelheim, DE). Glioblastoma primary cells, established from resected tumor tissue, were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 11320033) supplemented with 2mM GlutaMAX, 15% FBS and 10,000 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. The procedures have been approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Campania Nord (San Giuseppe Moscati-Avellino; Prot. 359 dated 15 February 2020). Informed consent from the patient was obtained by AORN Sant’Anna and San Sebastiano (Caserta, Italy). SD2 glioblastoma stem cells were cultured as described [15, 16].

KMT5a was chemically inhibited using UNC0379 (Selleckchem, Houston, TX, USA, cat. S7570). Cells were treated with 5 µM UNC0379 for 48 h, unless otherwise specified. KMT5b and KMT5c were chemically inhibited using 5 µM A-196 (Selleckchem, cat. S7983) for 48 h. Untreated control cells were treated with 0.1% DMSO (Sigma Aldrich, cat. D4540). Cells in starvation conditions were grown in enriched DMEM without FBS for 48 h.

Characterization of glioblastoma primary and stem cells

Targeted somatic mutations in specific genes were assessed by next generation sequencing (NGS) using the Diatech Myriapod NGS Cancer Probe Plus kit (Diatech Pharmacogenetics, Jesi, IT, cat. NG103). Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted from cells using DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Hilden, DE, cat. 69504) and then analyzed using the NG103 kit and MagnisDx NGS Prep System, following the manufacturer’s instruction. Sequencing was performed using V3 reagent kits on the Illumina MiSeq system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Expression vector and siRNA transfections

Transfections of expression vectors were performed using linear polyethylenimine (L-PEI; Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA, USA, cat. 23966-1) and 0.25 M NaCl (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. 7647-14-5). siRNAs targeting human KMT5a were purchased from Dharmacon Inc. (Lafayette, CO, USA). For siRNA treatments and complementation experiments, U251 cells were transfected with non-targeting (cat. D-001810) or specific siRNAs (siKMT5a, cat. L-031917); 12 h later, cells were transfected with a Myc-Flagged pcDNA-KMT5a expression vector (OriGene, Rockville, MD, USA, cat. RC208219). The pcDNA-PHF8 expression vector was also obtained from OriGene (cat. RC230633). dCas9 and p62/SQSTM1 gRNAs were purchased from VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL, USA). The pCMV-p53WT and pCMV-p53R273H expression vectors were purchased from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA; cat. 16434 and 16439, respectively). The EGFP-LC3 plasmid was purchased from Addgene (cat. 11546).

Analysis of RNA-Seq data and differential expression

The RNA sequencing data were first assessed for quality using “fastp” (v0.20.1) and then analyzed with Hisat2 (v.2.2.1) and Cufflinks v2.1.0 [17]. To reads mapping, Hisat2 was run with default options using the reference genome and its related RefSeq reference transcriptome (assembly GRCh37/hg19). Cufflinks was then used to assemble the mapped reads into possible transcripts and to assemble the final transcriptome. Gene-level expression was reported as normalized FPKM counts (Fragments Per Kilobase of Exon Per Million Fragments Mapped). Cuffdiff was subsequently used to identify differentially expressed genes between the two experimental conditions: UNC0379-treated U251 cells (U251T) and untreated, control cells (U251 CTRL) cells. The results were checked and explored using the R-Bioconductor package CummeRbund (v2.16.0). Genes were considered differentially expressed genes if they showed a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and a log2-transformed fold change greater than + 1 (up-regulated) or lower than − 1 (down-regulated). The list of the differentially expressed genes is reported in Additional file 1.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cell pellets using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, cat. 74106), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, 2 µg of RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed using the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, cat. 205313), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentrations were measured using Nanodrop™ One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Real-time PCR was performed as previously described [18].

Click-iT and chip experiments

Nascent mRNA expression was assessed using the Click-iT™ Nascent RNA Capture Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. C10365). Cells were treated with DMSO or 0.2 mM ethylene uridine (EU) for 16 h, followed by RNA extraction. Subsequent steps were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Biotinylated RNA was reverse-transcribed, and real time PCR was performed.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were carried out using Pierce™ Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. 26157X), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-Time PCR was then performed on the ChIP-derived DNA. IgG-immunoprecipitated DNA served as negative control.

Western blot analysis

Cells were collected and lysed in 0.1% NP-40 (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. I8895) in PBS, supplemented with PhosSTOP™ and Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche Life Science, Basel, CH, cat. HY-K0011). Lysates were incubated on ice for 20 min and centrifuged twice at 14,000 × g for 10 min. Protein concentration was determined using SmartSpec™ Plus spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with the Protein Assay Dye Reagent Concentrate (Bio-Rad, cat. 5000006). A Bovine Serum Albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. A2153) curve was used for quantification. Lysates were then boiled and loaded onto a polyacrylamide gel. Precision Plus Protein™ All Blue Standards (Bio-Rad, cat. 1610373) were used as a molecular weight marker.

Immunofluorescence procedure

For immunofluorescence analysis, U251 cells were transfected with a vector encoding EGFP fused to LC3. Cells were then seeded onto coverslips and treated with DMSO or UNC0379. After treatment, cells were processed as previously described [19]. DNA was stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. D9542). Samples were examined and imaged using an Axiovert 200 M inverted microscope equipped with an ApoTome slider module and 40× and 63× objectives (Zeiss, Oberkochen, DE).

Results

p62/SQSTM1 gene is repressed by the H4K20 methyltransferase KMT5a

mRNA sequencing reveled that p62/SQSTM1 was among the top upregulated transcripts in U251 glioblastoma cells treated with UNC0379, a specific KMT5a inhibitor (Supplementary Fig. S1, Additional file 1) [20]. p62/SQSTM1 encodes a protein essential for selective autophagy, the process responsible for degrading misfolded, damaged, or aggregated proteins in lysosomes [14]. RT-PCR confirmed that p62/SQSTM1 mRNA levels were increased in UNC0379-treated U251 cells. As a control, in U251 cells, UNC0379 did not change the levels of either KMT5a or the H4K20 demethylase PHF8 (Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis demonstrated that UNC0379 treatment increased p62/SQSTM1 protein levels, consistent with enhanced transcription, and reduced H4K20 monomethylation, confirming the efficacy of the inhibitor (Fig. 1B). To further validate the specificity of UNC0379, we silenced KMT5a using siRNAs. KMT5a knockdown markedly upregulated p62/SQSTM1 expression. As expected, siRNAs reduced KMT5a mRNA levels without affecting PHF8 mRNA expression (Fig. 1C). To determine whether KMT5a inhibition directly affected p62/SQSTM1 transcription, we analyzed nascent RNA by capturing and amplifying newly synthesized transcripts. Both UNC0379 treatment and KMT5a knockdown led to a robust increase in nascent p62/SQSTM1 RNA levels, without affecting GAPDH or PHF8 levels (Fig. 1D and E). Importantly, the effect of KMT5a silencing was rescued by transfection of a plasmid expressing KMT5a cDNA, which is resistant to the siRNAs, targeting the 3′ untranslated region of the gene (Fig. 1E). Together these data demonstrate that p62/SQSTM1 gene expression is induced by KMT5a inhibition. Since KMT5a is the only mammalian enzyme capable of monomethylating histone H4 at lysine 20, our results suggest that H4K20 methylation mediates transcriptional repression of p62/SQSTM1. To further support this hypothesis, we overexpressed the H4K20 demethylase PHF8 in U251 cells. PHF8 overexpression significantly increased both p62/SQSTM1 mRNA and nascent RNA levels (Fig. 1F and G), providing additional evidence that H4K20 methylation is a key epigenetic mark repressing p62/SQSTM1 transcription.

KMT5a-induced methylation of H4K20 at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter represses transcription

To definitively demonstrate that KMT5a regulates p62/SQSTM1 expression, we closely analyzed the p62/SQSTM1 promoter in U251 cells, as showing in Fig. 2A, using the promoter of RPL5, a known KMT5a-regulated gene [21], as positive control in all experiments. KMT5a was detected at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter (Fig. 2B). KMT5a inhibition led to a significant decrease in H4K20 methylation and a concurrent increase of H3K27 acetylation at the promoter region (Fig. 2C), indicating chromatin opening. To further confirm that H4K20 methylation acts as a repressive mark for p62/SQSTM1 expression, we guided on the p62/SQSTM1 promoter a catalytically active fusion protein comprising death caspase-9 and the H4K20 demethylase PHF8. Delivery of PHF8 to the promoter rapidly induced p62/SQSTM1 expression (Fig. 2D). Moreover, under basal condition, PHF8 occupancy at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter was notably higher than that of KMT5a (Fig. 2E). However, upon KMT5a overexpression in U251 cells, its occupancy at the promoter increased whereas PHF8 levels decreased, suggesting that the presence of the two enzymes is mutually exclusive at the promoter (Fig. 2E).

Together, these data demonstrate that H4K20 methylation is a critical epigenetic switch for p62/SQSTM1 transcription: methylation by KMT5a represses gene expression whereas demethylation by PHF8 promotes transcription.

Regulation of p62/SQSTM1 transcription requires di- and trimethylation of H4K20

KMT5a catalyzes monomethylation of H4K20; this mark can then be further methylated to form H4K20me2 and H4K20me3 by KMT5b and KMT5c, respectively [9, 10]. To investigate whether H4K20 di- and trimethylation play a role in regulating p62/SQSTM1 expression, we treated U251 cells with A196, a potent and selective inhibitor of KMT5b and KMT5c [22]. A196 further enhanced p62/SQSTM1 expression when combined with UNC0379, suggesting that H4K20 di- and trimethylation are required for the repression of p62/SQSTM1 transcription (Fig. 3A). Co-treatment of U251 cells with UNC0379 and A196 also strongly increased p62/SQSTM1 nascent mRNA levels, without affecting GAPDH and KMT5a transcription (Fig. 3B). The specificity of A196 was confirmed by western blot analysis, which showed a significant reduction in H4K20me2 levels, but not in H4K20me1, in A196-treated U251 cells (Fig. 3C). Additional evidence for the role of H4K20 di- and trimethylation came from analysis of the p62/SQSTM1 promoter. All three H4 methylation states were detected at the promoter (Fig. 3D). Inhibition of KMT5a by UNC0379 lead to a reduction not only in H4K20me1 but also in H4K20me2 and H4K20me3 at the promoter, consistent with the fact that H4K20me1 serves as a substrate for further methylation (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, treatment with A196 also reduced H4K20me1 levels at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter, suggesting that blocking the progression to di- and trimethylation may lead to the rapid removal of the monomethyl mark (Fig. 3E). Supporting this hypothesis, A196 treatment was associated with increased recruitment of the H4K20 demethylase PHF8 to the promoter (Fig. 3F).

Together, these findings demonstrate that p62/SQSTM1 repression requires a sequential methylation process: initial monomethylation of H4K20 by KMT5a, followed by di- and trimethylation by KMT5b and KMT5c, respectively.

KMT5a repression of p62/SQSTM1 requires p53 transcriptional activity

Our data demonstrate that KMT5a represses p62/SQSTM1 transcription in U251 cells. To validate our findings across different glioblastoma cell lines, we treated SW1088 and U87MG cells with UNC0379. Western blot analysis demonstrated that UNC0379 reduced H4K20 monomethylation in both cell lines, confirming the efficacy of the treatment (Supplementary Fig. S2A). As expected, UNC0379 strongly induced p62/SQSTM1 transcription and protein levels in SW1088 cells, without significatively affecting KMT5a or PHF8 levels, thus confirming the results obtained in U251 cells (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. S2A). Surprisingly, however, p62/SQSTM1 expression and protein levels were not affected by KMT5a inhibition in U87MG glioblastoma cells (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S2A). This unexpected result led us to hypothesize a role for p53 in p62/SQSTM1 repression, as U87MG cells retain functional p53 transcriptional activity, while U251 and SW1088 have lost it. This hypothesis was further supported by in silico analysis identifying a p53 binding site at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter (data not shown). To validate our hypothesis, we transfected U251 cells, heterozygous for p53 mutations, with a plasmid encoding wild type p53. In this context, p62/SQSTM1 expression was completely abolished, even in presence of UNC0379 (Fig. 4C). Conversely, expression of a dominant negative mutant p53 in U87MG cells induced p62/SQSTM1 transcription, which was further increased by UNC0379 treatment (Fig. 4D). Western blot analysis confirmed both the efficiency of p53 transfection and the ability of UNC0379 to reduce H4K20 monomethylation levels in U251 and U87MG cells (Supplementary Fig. S2B and S2C).

To further validate our model, we utilized glioblastoma primary cells established from resected tumor tissue, which harbor, among other alterations, a mutation in the p53 gene (Fig. 5A) and express the glial marker GFAP (Fig. 5B). In these p53-mutated glioblastoma primary cells UNC0379 treatment induced p62/SQSTM1 expression without affecting KMT5a expression (Fig. 5C).

An oncogenic role for KMT5a has been clearly demonstrated in pancreatic and colorectal cancer stem cells [23, 24]. Therefore, we also conducted experiments on SD2 glioblastoma stem cells, established from resected tumor tissue and propagated in neural stem cell media [15, 16]. We first verified that these cells harbor, among other alteration, a mutation in the p53 gene (Fig. 5D). UNC0379 induced p62/SQSTM1 mRNA and protein levels also in SD2 glioblastoma stem cells, without affecting KMT5a expression and stemness (i.e., SOX2 expression) (Fig. 5E and F).

UNC0379-treatment resulted in p62/SQSTM1 mRNA upregulation also in tumor tissues obtained from mice inoculated with p53-mutated U251 cells, as reported in our previous publication [13], suggesting that, also in vivo, KMT5a and p53 repress p62/SQSTM1 expression (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Together, these findings demonstrate that p53 acts as a strong repressor of p62/SQSTM1 expression and is required for KMT5a-mediated repression of p62/SQSTM1 transcription.

Starvation-induced transcription of p62/SQSTM1 is inhibited by KMT5a and p53

Upon amino-acid and serum starvation, p62/SQSTM1 is initially degraded by autophagy. However, during prolonged starvation, its levels are restored through transcriptional upregulation, although this occurs only in certain cell types [25]. In U251 glioblastoma cells, serum starvation was sufficient to induce p62/SQSTM1 expression to levels comparable to those induced by UNC0379 (Fig. 6A). Treatment of starved cells with UNC0379 further enhanced p62/SQSTM1 expression (Fig. 6A). Consistently, in starved cells H4K20me1 levels at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter were significatively lower than in non-starved cells (Fig. 6B). Starvation was confirmed by phosphorylation of AMPK (Fig. 6C). To define the role of p53, we also subjected the p53-proficient U87MG cell line to serum deprivation. Starvation induced p62/SQSTM1 transcription in U87MG cells, as observed in U251 cells, without significatively affecting KMT5a or PHF8 levels (Fig. 6D). Activation of p62/SQSTM1 transcription was accompanied by H4K20 demethylation and loss of p53 binding at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter (Fig. 6E and F).

Altogether, these findings reveal a differential regulation of the p62/SQSTM1 promoter in starved p53-deficient versus p53-proficient cells. In p53-deficient U251 glioblastoma cells, p62/SQSTM1 transcription is activated by starvation, effectively restoring its levels following autophagy-mediated degradation. Importantly, our findings also indicate that inhibition of KMT5a-mediated H4K20 methylation is required to induce p62/SQSTM1 expression and to promote its restoration during autophagy. In p53-proficient U87MG cells, demethylation of H4K20 alone is not sufficient to induce p62/SQSTM1 transcription; the displacement of p53 from the promoter is also required. This further supports the role of p53 as a transcriptional repressor of p62/SQSTM1.

Discussion

Our study identifies KMT5a-mediated H4K20 methylation as a key epigenetic mark that represses transcription of the p62/SQSTM1 gene in glioblastoma cells. Transcriptomic analysis initially revealed p62/SQSTM1 as one of the most upregulated genes in U251 glioblastoma cells following KMT5a inhibition with the small-molecule inhibitor UNC0379. These findings were subsequently validated at both the mRNA and nascent RNA levels in cells treated with either UNC0379 or KMT5a-targeting siRNAs. The specificity of this regulatory mechanism was further confirmed by phenotypic rescue experiments using siRNA-resistant KMT5a constructs.

Previously, repression of p62/SQSTM1 by KMT5a had only been observed in the RAW264.7 murine macrophage cell line under lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, as a model for inflammatory bowel disease [26]. In that context, H4K20me1enrichment at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter was significantly reduced in siKMT5a + LPS-treated cells. Although functional relevance was suggested by evidence that UNC0379 worsened dextran sodium sulfate-induced colitis in mice, no further studies on the p62/SQSTM1 promoter were performed in that model. In contrast, our chromatin immunoprecipitation assays confirmed that KMT5a directly binds the p62/SQSTM1 promoter and catalyzes H4K20 monomethylation at this site. The H4K20 demethylase PHF8 functions in opposite way: its overexpression or targeted delivery to the p62 promoter was sufficient to activate p62/SQSTM1 transcription. The mutual exclusivity between KMT5a and PHF8 binding at the promoter further supports a dynamic methylation-demethylation balance that regulates p62/SQSTM1 expression.

While KMT5a catalyzes monomethylation of H4K20, the subsequent di- and trimethylation steps are mediated by KMT5b and KMT5c, respectively. Combined pharmacological inhibition of all three enzymes using UNC0379 and A196 leads to a more pronounced induction of p62/SQSTM1 compared to KMT5a inhibition alone. This suggests that not only H4K20me1 but also its higher methylation states contribute to the repression of p62/SQSTM1, underscoring the role of sequential H4K20 methylation in establishing a transcriptionally repressive chromatin environment at this promoter. The notion that H4K20 methylation acts as a repressive histone mark at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter is further supported by the observation that KMT5a inhibition is associated with increased H3K27 acetylation, an epigenetic mark of open chromatin.

Unexpectedly, p62/SQSTM1 regulation by KMT5a was dependent on p53 activity. In glioblastoma cell lines with impaired p53 function (U251 and SW1088), KMT5a inhibition robustly induced p62/SQSTM1 expression. In contrast, p62/SQSTM1 transcription remained unaffected in p53-proficient U87MG cells upon KMT5a inhibition. Re-expression of wild-type p53 in p53-deficient U251 cells abolished p62/SQSTM1 expression, whereas inhibition of p53 function in U87MG cells enabled p62/SQSTM1 induction upon KMT5a inhibition. These results suggest that functional p53 is necessary to maintain repression of p62/SQSTM1, possibly by collaborating with KMT5a or stabilizing repressive chromatin structures at the p62/SQSTM1 promoter.

Intriguingly, p53 is a direct target of KMT5a methyltransferase activity. In colorectal cancer and neuroblastoma, KMT5a-mediated methylation of p53 at lysine 382 impairs its tumor suppressor functions. Consistently, therapeutic targeting of KMT5a reduces tumor growth and metastasis in animal models [24, 27]. Taken together, these findings, along with our data, suggest that, in a broader context, KMT5a inhibition may regulate the transcription of p53 target genes through a dual mechanism: by promoting p53 demethylation and activation, and by inducing chromatin opening at target promoters. This hypothesis warrants further investigation to fully elucidate the effects of KMT5a inhibition in cancer treatment.

KMT5a inhibition strongly induces DNA damage in glioblastoma cells, as H4K20 methylation is a key epigenetic mark required for recruiting the DNA damage repair machinery [11–13]. Treatment of U251 cells with UNC0379 led to the expected increase in DNA damage, as indicated by the accumulation of phospho-gH2AX. In these p53-deficient cells, p62/SQSTM1, LC3-II, and LC3 puncta levels were also elevated, and AMPK was activated, suggesting that the DNA damage triggered autophagy (Supplementary Fig. S4). Further studies will be needed to definitively determine whether UNC0379-induced p62/SQSTM1 expression is a direct response to DNA damage.

Conclusions

Our results elucidate a novel epigenetic mechanism whereby KMT5a, through H4K20 methylation, represses p62/SQSTM1 transcription in a p53-dependent manner (Fig. 7). This repression can be reversed by specific pharmacologic inhibitors. KMT5a is frequently overexpressed across a wide range of cancers, with elevated expression levels generally correlating with poor clinical outcomes. Its dual activity on histone and non-histone substrates drives tumor progression, making it a compelling target for therapeutic intervention. Our findings, however, indicate that KMT5a inhibition may lead to increased transcription of p62/SQSTM1. P62/SQSTM1 is frequently upregulated in cancer, where its accumulation reflects impaired autophagy and actively contributes to tumor progression [28]. By sustaining pro-survival pathways such as NF-κB and mTORC1, p62/SQSTM1 enhances cell proliferation, stress resistance, and metabolic adaptation [29, 30]. Elevated p62/SQSTM1 levels are therefore associated with more aggressive disease and poorer prognosis across multiple tumor types [28]. P62/SQSTM1 role in glioblastoma is complex, but accumulating evidence highlights its involvement in promoting tumor progression and therapy resistance [31, 32]. Taken together, these findings suggest that targeting KMT5a could represent an effective therapeutic strategy for tumors with functional p53, where p62/SQSTM1 transcription is expected to remain unaffected by KMT5a inhibition, thus positioning p53 mutations as a potential biomarker for therapy selection.
