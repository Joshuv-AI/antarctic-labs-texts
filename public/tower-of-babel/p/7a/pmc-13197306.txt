Efficacy of low-concentration nitrous oxide gas anesthesia in routine cataract microsurgeries
Authors: Kaori Morii, Kotaro Ishigami, Shinji Miura, Ritsuko Fujiwara, Azusa Akashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the sedative effects of low-concentration nitrous oxide (LCNO) anesthesia during cataract microsurgery (CMs) using bispectral index (BIS) monitoring.MethodsThirty patients undergoing sequential CMs were randomized into the following three groups: Group A (LCNO for the first eye, oxygen for the second), Group B (oxygen for the first eye, LCNO for the second), and Group C (oxygen for both eyes) (n = 10 per group). BIS values, blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation were recorded at four time points: upon entering the operating room (T1), at the start of surgery (T2), at the conclusion of surgery (T3), and upon exiting the room (T4). A linear mixed model analysis was performed, with participants as the variable factor and group (LCNO-first, O2 first, both O2), duration (first and second eye), time (T1, T2, T3, and T4), interaction terms, and the initial values of each parameter (at entry) as fixed factors.ResultsBIS values at T2 and T3 were significantly lower in groups under LCNO compared to those under oxygen-only conditions (p < 0.001). No significant changes in heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation were observed in both groups.ConclusionLCNO provided mild sedation during CMs, enhancing patient comfort without causing adverse effects.

Introduction

Cataract microsurgery (CM) is a common procedure, with over 20 million surgeries performed annually [1, 2], highlighting its importance in preserving visual health. It is typically performed under local anesthesia, such as eye drops [3]. However, certain patient populations, including infants or young children, patients unable to maintain a supine position, and extremely fearful or nervous patients, may require additional sedation. In such cases, general anesthesia or conscious sedation is often considered.

Nitrous oxide (N₂O) is widely used across multiple medical disciplines, including ophthalmology, owing to its analgesic and sedative properties, rapid onset, and favorable recovery profile. N₂O sedation has been well established as a method for procedural sedation to reduce pain and anxiety in dentistry. In obstetrics and gynecology, N₂O—commonly referred to as “gas and air”—is frequently used during labor to alleviate contraction-related pain. In gastrointestinal endoscopy, it is employed for procedural sedation and analgesia and is generally better tolerated from a cardiovascular standpoint compared with some forms of intravenous sedation. In pediatric practice, N₂O is commonly administered in wards and emergency departments to facilitate both diagnostic and therapeutic procedures in children. N₂O is valuable across these settings because it exerts minimal effects on cardiopulmonary function while allowing patients to remain conscious and cooperative during procedures [4–6].

Low-concentration nitrous oxide (LCNO) has shown promise as a mild sedative agent in various medical settings [6], yet its effects on CMs remain underexplored. Studies have demonstrated the efficacy of LCNO in reducing anxiety and discomfort during ophthalmic procedures, including CMs, ptosis surgeries, and intravitreal injections, using subjective indices such as questionnaires [7, 8]. However, the sedative effects of LCNO during CMs have not been thoroughly investigated using objective monitoring methods.

The bispectral index (BIS) is a widely accepted, objective measure of sedation, ranging from 0 (deep anesthesia) to 100 (full alertness) [9–12]. Previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of various anesthetic agents in providing sedation during CMs, with BIS values serving as reliable indicators of sedative effects.

This study employed BIS monitoring to objectively assess the sedative impact of LCNO during CMs.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective case series included 30 patients aged ≥ 30 years scheduled for sequential CMs in 2021 at Asagiri Hospital. Exclusion criteria included nasal obstruction, history of cerebral infarction, pacemaker implantation, or antipsychotic drug use. Ethical approval (No. 2020-9) and UMIN registration (UMIN000049081) were obtained, and the study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients were randomized into three groups using block randomization with stratification by age and sex (stratified randomization).

Randomization and group allocation were concealed from patients and the surgical team to minimize potential biases.

Intervention

Gas was administered via a nasal cannula at a rate of 6 L/min. The LCNO concentration was set at 30%, and gas administration (LCNO or oxygen alone) was controlled using a gas regulator (Sedant PSYCORICH T-70, Sekimura, Japan). Five minutes after gas administration, CMs were performed. All procedures were performed by a single operator (K.M.) using standard techniques, including topical anesthesia (4% xylocaine), and intracameral anesthesia (1% xylocaine), cataract removal with phacoemulsification and aspiration, and intraocular lens implantation in the capsular bag. At the end of surgery, patients inhaling a mixture of LCNO and oxygen were switched to oxygen alone. Gas administration was discontinued 5 min after surgery, and patient exited the operating room. An independent anesthesiologist monitored anesthesia administration to ensure patient safety.

BIS explanation

The Bispectral Index (BIS) monitor (Aspect Medical Systems, USA) (Fig. 1) is widely used as an electroencephalography (EEG)-based monitoring tool for assessing the depth of sedation and anesthesia during surgery. BIS is a processed index derived from frontal EEG signals continuously recorded over the preceding minute via surface electrodes placed on the patient’s forehead. The BIS ranges from 0 to 100, where 100 represents a fully awake state and 0 corresponds to an isoelectric (flat) EEG. In clinical practice, BIS values of 60–80 indicate light sedation, 40–60 correspond to an appropriate level for general anesthesia, and values below 40 are interpreted as deep anesthesia. BIS monitoring is commonly applied in operating rooms and intensive care units as an objective indicator of central nervous system depression during sedation and anesthesia [9–12].

Monitoring

BIS values, systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP, respectively), heart rate (HR), and oxygen saturation (SPO2) were recorded with recordings taken at four distinct time points: upon entering the operating room (T1), at the start of surgery (T2), at the conclusion of surgery (T3), and upon exiting the room (T4).

BIS electroencephalogram (EEG) electrodes were attached to the forehead upon reclining in the surgical bed, and the face was covered with an ophthalmic surgical drape (Fig. 1). CM was performed without interfering with the electrodes. BIS values were automatically calculated using a computer algorithm based on EEG signals.

To evaluate the sedative effect of LCNO during CMs, BP, HR, and SPO2 were monitored throughout the procedure using a BP-608EV II (Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd., Japan). The BIS index values were obtained using a BIS monitor with electrodes attached to the patient’s forehead.

Statistical analysis

Estimates were calculated for each parameter (BIS, HR, SPO2, SBP, and DBP) by group (LCNO-first, O2 first, both O2) × time period (first and second eye).

For statistical analysis, a linear mixed model was used, with participants as the variable factor and group (LCNO first, O2 first, and both O2), duration (first eye and second eye), time (T1, T2, T3, and T4), their interaction terms, and the initial values of each parameter (at entry) as fixed factors. Estimates for each group (LCNO and O2) were calculated and compared, with statistical significance set at p < 0.001.

The mixed-effects model included fixed effects for group, time, eye (first vs. second), and their interaction terms (group × time, group × eye), with participants treated as random effects to account for within-subject correlation.

Complications

No intraoperative complications were reported.

Discussion

Although rare complications such as laryngospasm, aspiration, or thromboembolic events related to recreational misuse have been reported in the literature, none occurred in our monitored clinical setting with 30% LCNO and supplemental oxygen [13–17].

Our study revealed that the BIS values significantly decreased during CMs under LCNO gas anesthesia, exhibiting rapid induction and recovery. In contrast, inhalation of oxygen alone did not affect the BIS during CMs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first prospective case series to demonstrate the mild sedative effect of LCNO for CMs using an objective measure, the BIS value, with an emphasis on the substantial reduction of BIS values during procedures under LCNO.

Noguchi et al. and Sasajima et al. assessed the effects of LCNO for CMs or ptosis surgeries using subjective indices such as visual analog scale scores or questionnaires [7, 8]. These studies demonstrated that LCNO subjectively suppressed anxiety and pain in patients, but did not evaluate the actual depth of anesthesia during the procedures. The use of the BIS value, an established measure of sedation, lends credibility to our findings and distinguishes our study from previous investigations. Furthermore, the marked decrease in BIS values during surgeries under LCNO underscores the potential benefits of this anesthetic method.

Our results indicate that LCNO provided mild and safe sedation for patients during CMs. Notably, no learning effect was observed in patients concerning BIS values, because the same BIS-lowering effect occurred when LCNO was used for the first or second eye surgery. A previous study suggested that the level of anxiety and pain in patients could vary between the first and second eye surgeries. However, Sasajima et al. demonstrated that anxiety levels and pain scores were significantly reduced in patients who underwent CMs under LCNO, after adjusting for various effects in a linear mixed model [6]. Our findings highlighted the consistency of the sedative effects of LCNO, regardless of whether it was used for the first or second CM, and suggested that its sedative effects did not diminish or intensify over time or across multiple surgeries.

This study has several limitations. First, no sample size was prespecified, which may have affected the statistical power of the results. Second, the relatively small sample size may have limited the generalizability of the findings. Third, LCNO produces mild sedation during CM, thereby increasing patient comfort without causing adverse effects. However, this study did not directly measure patient comfort (e.g., no subjective anxiety or pain scores). Furthermore, the phrase “without side effects” is too broad. This study only reported vital signs and did not assess other possible side effects (e.g., nausea, dizziness, or impact on cognitive function). An inherent time lag is present between the cortical EEG changes and displayed BIS values, particularly in short procedures. BIS may also show artifactual fluctuations; therefore, trends rather than absolute values should be interpreted.

Future studies with larger sample sizes and prespecified sample size calculations are needed to corroborate our findings and explore the potential benefits of LCNO in CMs.

This study highlights LCNO’s potential to provide mild and effective sedation during CMs. BIS monitoring confirmed its sedative effects without compromising safety. These findings align with previous subjective assessments of LCNO in ophthalmic settings but extend knowledge by employing objective BIS values.

LCNO offers a safe and effective sedation option for CMs, enhancing patient comfort and surgical outcomes. However, larger randomized trials are required to validate these findings and establish LCNO’s broader applicability in ophthalmic procedures.
