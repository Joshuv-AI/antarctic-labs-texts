HDL‐mimetic peptide treatment reverses APOE4‐induced transcriptomic and lipidomic alterations in the brain of humanized APOE mice
Authors: Allison Chang, Minwoo Kim, Matthew Glittenberg, Wenhui Qu, Danni Li, Ling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4) allele is the strongest genetic risk factor for late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD). ApoE4 has reduced lipidation capacity and impaired lipid transport, disrupting neuronal maintenance. The high‐density lipoprotein (HDL)‐mimetic peptide 4F offers a potential therapeutic strategy.METHODSTo investigate how APOE4 alters brain gene expression and lipid metabolism and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of 4F, we performed dual‐omics analysis in APOE4/4 and APOE3/3 mice treated intraperitoneally with D‐enantiomer of 4F (D4F) or vehicle for 12 weeks from 10 to 13 months of age.RESULTS APOE4/4 mice showed widespread transcriptomic and lipidomic alterations, including downregulation of lipid metabolism and synaptic pathways, increased ceramides, sphingomyelins, and cholesteryl esters, with decreased diglycerides and triglycerides. D4F treatment shifted relevant gene expression and lipid profiles toward APOE3/3 levels.DISCUSSIONThese findings reveal molecular mechanisms underlying APOE4‐driven dysregulation and support the therapeutic potential of HDL‐mimetic peptides to mitigate APOE4‐associated alterations in AD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, APOE, brain, HDL‐mimetic peptide, lipidomics, transcriptomics

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) poses a significant global health challenge, with prevalence expected to rise dramatically in the coming decades. 1 Among the risk factors identified for late‐onset AD, the apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4) genotype stands out as the most potent genetic risk factor, present in approximately two‐thirds of AD cases, despite comprising only a quarter of the population. 2 APOE4 carriers have increased AD risk, with homozygotes facing up to 15‐fold higher odds, while the APOE2 allele is protective and APOE3 is largely neutral. 3

Disruption of lipid metabolism has emerged as a key feature of AD pathogenesis. Abnormal levels of cholesterol, ceramides, and sphingomyelins have been observed in AD brains, contributing to amyloidogenic processing, neuroinflammation, and synaptic function. 4 Elevated cholesterol promotes lipid raft formation, facilitating clustering of β‐ and γ‐secretases, which enhances Aβ production. 5 Abnormal cholesterol levels also destabilize synaptic structures, progressively impairing neuronal communication and accelerating disease progression. 6 Ceramides are likewise increased in AD brain and serum, where they promote mitochondrial apoptosis and correlate with Aβ pathology. 7 Blood‐based studies further underscore the predictive value of these lipids. For example, higher plasma sphingomyelin/ceramide ratios were associated with slower cognitive decline in AD patients, 8 whereas in cognitively normal adults, low baseline levels predicted existing memory impairment and higher levels were linked to future decline, 9 suggesting these lipids may mark distinct stages of disease progression.

ApoE, the primary lipid carrier in the central nervous system, functions crucially at the intersection of lipid metabolism and AD pathology. The three major isoforms (ApoE2, ApoE3, ApoE4) differ in their lipid transport ability. Although ApoE4 has stronger lipid binding compared to ApoE3, 10 it is less effective at facilitating cellular lipid trafficking and efflux, resulting in reduced lipidation after secretion. 11 , 12 Properly lipidated ApoE facilitates delivery of lipids to neurons, supports synaptogenesis and repair, and regulates Aβ aggregation and clearance. 13 , 14

High‐density lipoprotein (HDL) has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for AD, as higher HDL and its main protein, apolipoprotein A‐I (ApoA‐I), are linked to lower risk and improved cognition. 15 In transgenic mouse models, ApoA‐I overexpression or infusion reduces neuroinflammation, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), and cognitive deficits. 16 , 17 Conversely, ApoA‐I deficiency exacerbates amyloid burden, CAA, astrogliosis, and amyloid‐associated astrocyte reactivity. 18 , 19

Because therapeutic use of native ApoA‐I is limited by production costs and bioavailability, synthetic peptides mimicking HDL's properties have been developed. 20 One such peptide, 4F, is an 18‐amino acid amphipathic structure that emulates ApoA‐I activities despite lacking sequence homology. Its D‐enantiomer (D4F) has superior bioavailability and longer half‐life than the L‐form, making it more suitable for in vivo use. 21 We previously demonstrated that 4F effectively crosses the blood‐brain barrier, 22 improves ApoE secretion and lipidation in astrocytes and microglia, 23 and reduces neuroinflammation, Aβ deposition, and CAA in transgenic AD mouse models. 24 , 25 , 26

While transcriptomic and lipidomic analyses have been performed in APOE targeted replacement (TR) mice, 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 such studies in the more recently developed APOE knock‐in (KI) mice from Jackson Laboratory are lacking. Until recently, only one transcriptomic study was conducted on this model, and it focused exclusively on young mice aged 2‐4 months. 32 A recent comparison of humanized APOE mice (aged 3‐4 months) by biochemical and transcriptomic analyses found that both the APOE genotype and the source of the mice affect ApoE levels, lipid profiles, and molecular pathways, highlighting the importance of model selection. 33

In this study, we examined brain transcriptomic and lipidomic alterations in middle‐aged humanized APOE4/4 (E4/4) compared to APOE3/3 (E3/3) KI mice and assessed the impact of D4F treatment on these changes. This approach aimed to elucidate mechanisms by which APOE4 increases AD risk and to evaluate HDL‐mimetic therapy as a potential intervention. Our data reveal widespread transcriptomic and lipidomic dysregulation in E4/4 brains compared to E3/3, with D4F treatment reversing key gene expression and metabolic changes. These findings highlight the molecular underpinnings of APOE4‐driven AD risk and support HDL‐targeted therapeutics as a promising strategy for intervention.

METHODS

Animals

APOE4/4 (JAX Stock No. 027894) and APOE3/3 (JAX Stock No. 029018) KI mice were purchased and bred to establish the colonies for this study. All mice were maintained under standard laboratory conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the University of Minnesota Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol # 1908‐37310A and 2207‐40221A).

D4F treatment protocol

Male and female E4/4 and E3/3 mice (aged 10.5 ± 0.1 months) received daily intraperitoneal injections of D4F or vehicle (phosphate buffered saline [PBS]) for 12 weeks to 13.2 ± 0.1 months of age. D4F was administered at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg body weight, based on prior studies demonstrating efficacy and tolerability in mouse models of atherosclerosis and AD. 26 , 34 Control groups received an equivalent volume of PBS. The 12‐week (∼ 3‐month) treatment window in 10‐13 months old mice was chosen to model a mid‐life intervention period relevant to preclinical AD stages, allowing assessment of both prevention and early reversal of APOE4‐driven molecular alterations.

RNA sequencing and analysis

RNA extraction and sequencing were performed on samples from each group (n = 7‐8 mice (males and females) per group), as previously described. 35 Total RNA was extracted from ∼50 mg cortical tissue using the Aurum™ Total RNA Fatty and Fibrous Tissue Kit (732‐6830; Bio‐Rad) according to manufacturer's instructions. RNA quantity and quality were assessed using the RiboGreen RNA Quantification assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), respectively. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 20 million 150‐bp paired‐end reads per sample. Raw reads were quality‐trimmed using Trimmomatic 36 and aligned to the mouse reference genome GRCm38 using HISAT2. Gene‐level read counts were obtained with FeatureCounts. 37 Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed one sample as an outlier, which was subsequently excluded from downstream analysis.

Differential gene expression and pathway analysis

Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis was performed using DESeq2 (1.44.0) 38 with default normalization and filtering methods. Differentially expressed genes were identified using adjusted p‐value thresholds of false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 or < 0.1, depending on the comparison. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the gseGO function from the ClusterProfiler package (4.12.6). 39 Visualization of results included volcano plots generated with ggplot2 (3.5.1) and hierarchical clustering of DEGs using pheatmap (1.0.12) with default parameters. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2).

Weighted gene co‐expression network analysis

To explore the interactions between genes, we employed weighted gene co‐expression network analysis (WGCNA), a computational framework that transforms gene expression correlation patterns into quantifiable network relationships. This analysis was performed using the WGCNA R software package (1.73). 40 The optimal β value was identified utilizing the R function PickSoftThreshold. Hierarchical clustering was used to identify gene modules, with a merging threshold of 0.40 applied to combine similar modules when each module contained at least 30 genes. Module eigengenes were correlated with experimental traits using Pearson correlation coefficients, with significance indicated by asterisks. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were performed for modules using the R package clusterProfiler (4.12.6).

Gene set enrichment and AD biological domain mapping

Differential gene expression analyses were performed using DESeq2 (1.44.0). Separate contrasts were constructed to evaluate the genotype effect (E4/4 vs E3/3) and the treatment effect in E4/4 mice (E4/4 D4F vs E4/4 PBS). Differential expression results were ranked using the signed test statistic (Wald statistic) and subjected to gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) against the GO Biological Process (BP) gene sets. The enrichment analysis was performed using the GSEA function in the clusterProfiler package (4.12.6). To interpret transcriptomic changes at the systems level, each GO term was mapped to one of 19 curated Alzheimer's disease–relevant biological domains, which were developed by aggregating genes and GO terms associated with evidence of Alzheimer's disease risk from large‐scale multi‐omic consortia. 41

Lipidomic analyses

Flash‐frozen left frontal cortex samples from each APOE genotype and treatment group (n = 3 females/group) were homogenized in LC‐MS grade isopropanol at a fixed tissue:solvent ratio of 1:6 (w/v). Samples processing was performed using the protocol for the MaxQuant™ p500 Kit (Biocrates Innsbruck, Austria). A total of 630 lipids and metabolites were measured on a triple‐quadrupole mass spectrometer. Analyte concentrations (M) were determined in the tissue extract using the Biocrates MetIDQ pipeline based on internal standards and multi‐point calibration curves. These values represent concentrations proportional to the initial tissue mass, adjusted for the extraction volume. Quality control filtering removed analytes with > 50% of values below the limit of detection (LOD) or with missing values. For remaining samples, values below LOD were set to the minimum LOD value. Data were median‐normalized and log2‐transformed for normalization prior to PCA, heatmap visualization using pheatmap (1.0.12), and statistical analysis. Group comparisons were performed using Student's t‐test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction in R (version 4.5.2). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Immunoblotting

The experimental procedures for protein assay and immunoblotting have been previously described. 35 , 42 Briefly, protein concentrations from total brain tissue homogenates were determined by the Bradford assay (Thermo Fisher 23246). Equal amounts of protein lysates (20 µg) were resolved on 8% or 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gels and transferred to Immobilon‐P polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer containing primary antibodies against sortilin‐related receptor 1 (SORL1; 1:1000; Cell Signaling, D8D4G), sterol regulatory element‐binding protein 2 (SREBP2; 1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc‐13552), low‐density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) (1:1000; Abnova, PAB8804), ATP‐binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1; 1:1000; Novus Biologicals, NB400‐105), SYT1 (1:1000; Cell Signaling, D33B7), APOE (1:1000; Novus Biologicals, NB110‐60531), glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; 1:5000; Invitrogen AM4300), and Tubulin (1:5000; Thermo Fisher, A11126). Membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies, followed by chemiluminescent detection using Clarity or Clarity Max ECL substrates (Bio‐Rad). Signals were visualized using the iBright Western Blot Imaging System (Thermo Fisher). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software. Male and female samples were run on separate gels. To enable normalization across gels, a pooled control sample containing equal amounts of protein from each sample was included on every gel. Band intensities were first normalized to the corresponding housekeeping protein (GAPDH or tubulin), and then further normalized to the pooled control sample intensity to account for inter‐gel variability.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted as previously described. 42 Briefly, the posterior half of the brain was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and sectioned in PBS at 50 µm thickness using a vibratome (Leica). Two sections per animal, spaced 300 µm apart, were selected for analysis. Sections were washed three times in PBS‐T before blocking (5% normal donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X‐100 in PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature.

Following blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. Primary antibodies included: rabbit anti‐perilipin‐2 (PLIN2; 1:500; Novus Biologicals, NB110‐40877); mouse anti‐MAP2 (1:500; Invitrogen, MA5‐12826); chicken anti‐glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; 1:500; Aves Labs AB‐2313547); goat anti‐IBA1 (1:500; Abcam, ab5076); and goat anti‐CD31 (1:100 R&D Systems, AF3628). Negative control sections in which primary antibodies were omitted were included. After primary antibody incubation, sections were washed in PBS‐T and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with species‐appropriate secondary antibodies. Secondary antibodies included: donkey anti‐mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) Alexa Fluor 568, donkey anti‐chicken IgG Alexa Fluor 594, donkey anti‐rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488, and donkey anti‐goat IgG Alexa Fluor 647 (all 1:400; Thermo Fisher). Sections were then washed three times in PBS‐T and incubated with DAPI (1:1,000; Thermo Fisher) for 20 minutes at room temperature, followed by three additional PBS washes. Sections were mounted onto glass slides, allowed to dry for 15 minutes, and coverslipped using ProLong Diamond antifade mounting reagent (Invitrogen).

Image Acquisition and Quantification

Images were acquired using a Keyence all‐in‐one fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ‐X810). Z‐stack images (10 µm total thickness, 1 µm step size) were obtained using a 100× oil immersion objective with the multi‐stack module. At least three randomly selected fields of view per cortical and hippocampal region were imaged for each section and used for quantitative analysis. Image processing and analysis were performed in ImageJ. Background subtraction was applied to PLIN2 images using the rolling ball method, and auto‐thresholding was used to obtain regions of interest for MAP2‐, GFAP‐, IBA1‐, and CD31‐positive cells. PLIN2‐positive puncta were quantified within cellular marker‐defined regions, in both cortex and hippocampus. For each animal, two sections were analyzed and averaged. Six animals per genotype were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise specified. Transcriptomic and lipidomic data were analyzed as described in the relevant sections above and figure legends. All statistical analyses for transcriptomic and lipidomic analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2). For immunoblotting and immunofluorescence experiments, statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 11). Comparisons among groups were conducted using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Unless otherwise stated, a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

APOE4 suppresses lipid catabolic and synaptic gene networks

To establish how APOE genotype influences gene expression in the brain, we performed RNA‐seq on cortical tissue from E3/3 and E4/4 mice (13.2 ± 0.1 months of age). Principal component analysis revealed clear separation between genotypes along PC1, with additional clustering by sex (Figure 1A). Differential expression analysis identified 2215 significantly upregulated genes and 2666 significantly downregulated genes in E4/4 mice compared to E3/3 mice (Figure 1B). Hierarchical clustering of the top differentially expressed genes revealed distinct expression patterns between genotypes. Although principal component analysis showed some sex‐based separation along PC2, differential expression patterns did not show sex‐specific expression signatures (Figure 1C).

Gene ontology enrichment analysis revealed suppression of lipid metabolism pathways in E4/4 mice (Table S1), including the acylglycerol catabolic process (GO:0046464) and neutral lipid catabolic process (GO:0046461). Several synaptic‐related processes were also downregulated, including regulation of trans‐synaptic signaling (GO:0099177), synaptic organization (GO:0050808), and protein localization to synapse (GO:0035418) (Figure 1D). Reactome pathway enrichment (Table S2) further highlighted widespread transcriptional alterations, including suppression of key neurotransmitter systems such as the glutamate neurotransmitter release cycle and potassium channel activity. Synaptic signaling pathways were also downregulated, including synaptic adhesion molecules, neurexins and neuroligins, and protein–protein interactions at synapses. In addition, lipoprotein/HDL remodeling pathways were significantly suppressed in E4/4 mice (Figure 1E). Suppressed expression of genes involved in synaptic structure and signaling indicates a vulnerability to impaired connectivity and reduced plasticity in E4/4 brains. At the same time, downregulation of lipid remodeling pathways may contribute to dysfunctional cholesterol transport and membrane maintenance, exacerbating neuronal stress and vulnerability. Overall, E4/4 brains exhibited a loss of function compared with E3/3 brains.

D4F treatment reverses synaptic and lipid‐related gene expression in APOE4/4 mice

To evaluate the transcriptional impact of D4F treatment on APOE4‐associated changes, we treated E4/4 mice with daily intraperitoneal injections of D4F from 10 to 13 months of age and compared gene expression profiles to PBS‐treated E4/4 and E3/3 controls. Principal component analysis showed primary separation by genotype along PC1, with PC2 capturing additional variation related to sex and treatment (Figure 2A). Differential expression analysis between D4F‐treated and PBS‐treated E4/4 mice identified 31 significantly altered genes, with 25 upregulated and 6 downregulated with treatment (Figure 2B). Notably, several genes involved in synaptic vesicle trafficking (Synj1, Nsf), dendritic transport (Kif3b), and cellular signaling (S100a16, Scn2a, Gabrb2) were significantly modulated by D4F (Table S3), suggesting that the treatment partially reverses neuronal and intracellular communication pathways disrupted in E4/4 mice.

To further explore the biological relevance of these transcriptional changes, we examined synaptic and lipid‐related pathways identified through GSEA comparing E4/4 and E3/3 mice. Notably, D4F treatment shifted the expression of both pathway types toward patterns observed in E3/3 mice (Figure 2C). This included changes in synaptic pathways such as neurexin/neuroligin signaling, synapse organization, and regulation of trans‐synaptic signaling. Similarly, lipid‐related pathways including lipoprotein remodeling, neutral lipid catabolism, and cellular lipid export showed expression patterns that more closely resembled E3/3 mice following D4F treatment. These results indicate that D4F treatment partially reversed APOE4‐associated transcriptional changes, particularly in pathways related to synaptic signaling and lipid metabolism.

To validate and contextualize our transcriptomic findings within a broader AD framework, we leveraged a recently published large‐scale reference dataset from the TREAT‐AD consortium. 41 This harmonized bulk RNA‐seq dataset comprises 1699 postmortem brain samples collected across seven major brain banks and represents one of the most comprehensive human LOAD transcriptomic resources available to date. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed on our APOE mouse data and subsequently mapped to 19 curated AD‐relevant biological domains 41 (Figure 2D). In the E4/4 vs E3/3 comparison, many domains were significantly downregulated in E4/4 mice. These pathways clustered predominantly in domains related to synapse, structural stabilization, and proteostasis, suggesting that APOE4 disrupts neuronal signaling, cytoskeletal integrity, and protein homeostasis at the transcriptional level. Additional downregulation was observed in lipid metabolism, apoptosis, immune response, vasculature, and endolysosome domains, reinforcing the multifaceted systems‐level impact of APOE4 across key biological processes implicated in AD. Notably, following D4F treatment in E4/4 mice, domain‐level enrichment patterns shifted. Several pathways in the synapse, lipid metabolism, and proteostasis domains showed positive normalized enrichment score (NES) values, suggesting transcriptional activation or restoration of regulatory and repair mechanisms.

Although human APOE KI mice do not develop overt amyloid or tau pathology, they model early, pre‐pathological vulnerability associated with APOE4. To further evaluate the translational relevance of these findings, we cross‐referenced genes that were downregulated in E4/4 mice and shifted toward E3/3 levels following D4F treatment against differentially expressed genes from a large‐scale human AD snRNA‐seq dataset. 43 Of the 16 overlapping genes identified, 12 showed concordant dysregulation in human AD brains and E4/4 mouse brains and were shifted in the opposite direction by D4F treatment (Table S4). These included multiple synaptic genes involved in synaptic transmission and plasticity, as well as lipid‐related genes including Sorl1, a key AD risk gene involved in lipid trafficking and amyloid processing. Collectively, these results indicate that APOE4‐driven transcriptional alterations in this mouse model recapitulate disease‐relevant pathways observed in human AD and that D4F treatment shifts these signatures toward a more homeostatic, E3/3‐like state.

WGCNA reveals gene co‐expression networks affected by APOE4 and D4F treatment

To identify coordinated gene expression changes associated with APOE genotype and D4F treatment, we performed WGCNA, a systems biology method that groups genes into modules based on their expression patterns across samples. 44 This approach allows the detection of functionally related gene networks that may be overlooked by traditional differential expression analyses. WGCNA identified several gene modules with significant correlations to experimental groups (Figure 3A). The brown module exhibited a strong positive correlation with the E3/3 genotype and a negative correlation with E4/4, suggesting that genes in this module are dysregulated in E4/4 mice. Gene ontology analysis revealed this module was enriched for synaptic functions, including regulation of membrane potential, synapse assembly, and cognition‐related processes (Figure 3B). Conversely, the darkgreen module, which was positively correlated with E4/4 and negatively with E3/3, was enriched for immune response pathways, consistent with the known pro‐inflammatory profile associated with APOE4. 45 The darkgrey module showed positive correlation with E3/3 and was enriched for cellular processes including protein synthesis and energy production, potentially reflecting more robust cellular maintenance in these mice.

Notably, D4F treatment showed a strong trend to modulate gene co‐expression patterns in E4/4 mice, particularly within the lightcyan and greenyellow module. Although neither module was significantly correlated with treatment, both exhibited expression profiles that shifted toward those of the E3/3 group, suggesting partial normalization of gene network activity. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed that the lightcyan module was enriched for developmental and neurogenesis‐related processes, including Wnt signaling and neural precursor cell proliferation. The greenyellow module was enriched for metabolic processes, notably retinoic acid and isoprenoid biosynthesis. The treatment‐induced shift in these modules suggests that D4F may modulate distinct biological pathways disrupted by APOE4, including those involved in neurodevelopment and lipid metabolism.

D4F treatment shifts lipid‐related gene expression in APOE4/4 mice

To investigate how D4F treatment may affect APOE4‐driven transcriptomic alterations in lipid metabolism, we examined expression patterns across major pathways involved in the synthesis, breakdown, and transport of lipids. Hierarchical clustering and heatmap visualization of these genes revealed that E4/4 mice displayed distinct gene expression patterns compared to E3/3 mice, with D4F treatment shifting the expression of some key genes toward E3/3 levels, indicating partial remodeling of lipid‐associated transcriptional profiles in E4/4 mice (Figure 3C).

Within the cholesterol pathway, D4F modulated transcriptional programs associated with cholesterol biosynthesis, intracellular transport, and lipid sensing, including components of the mevalonate pathway 46 and cholesterol trafficking machinery. 47 These coordinated changes suggest that D4F may influence both sterol production and subcellular cholesterol distribution that are disrupted in APOE4 brains. In the sphingolipid pathway, D4F treatment shifted expression patterns within genes regulating ceramide turnover and sphingosine metabolism. These pathways are critical for maintaining sphingolipid homeostasis and regulating signaling molecules linked to inflammation and cell death. 48 Phosphatidylcholine metabolism was also impacted by D4F, with observed shifts in gene expression related to de novo synthesis 49 and membrane translocation. 50 These changes may reflect restoration of membrane lipid composition and distribution. Within the triglyceride pathway, D4F modulated genes involved in lipid droplet (LD) formation 51 and fatty acid handling, 52 suggesting that D4F treatment may help reduce aberrant lipid storage and improve overall lipid metabolism in the APOE4 brain. Together, these data demonstrate that APOE4 mice exhibit broad transcriptomic alterations across multiple lipid metabolic pathways, and that D4F treatment can modulate expression of relevant genes toward a more homeostatic, E3/3‐like state.

APOE4 alters brain lipid profiles

To investigate further the impact of APOE4 on lipidomic profiles, we performed targeted lipidomics on cortical tissue from 13‐month‐old E4/4 and E3/3 mice. The analysis encompassed 12 major lipid classes comprising 339 molecular species. Principal component analysis demonstrated clear separation between the genotypes (Figure 4A). Differential abundance analysis identified 18 significantly altered lipids (Figure 4B). Examination of major lipid classes revealed distinct patterns between genotypes (Figure 4C). Total cholesteryl esters (CE), ceramides (Cer), hexosylceramides (HexCer), dihexosylceramides (Hex2Cer), trihexosylceramides (Hex3Cer), phosphatidylcholines (PC), and sphingomyelins (SM) were increased in E4/4 brains. Whereas, diglycerides (DG), triglycerides (TG), and lysophosphatidylcholines (lysoPC) were reduced in E4/4 brains compared to E3/3. Analysis of the significant individual lipid species showed genotype‐specific alterations consistent with the total levels (Figure 4D). Several key metabolites, including specific ceramides and phosphatidylcholines, displayed consistent elevation in E4/4 brains compared to E3/3 controls, while di‐ and triglyceride species were reduced. The quantitative comparison of significant lipid species in each of the lipid classes is further illustrated by bar plots (Figure 4E).

To explore broader metabolic alterations captured by the MaxQuant™ p500 platform, we examined small molecule metabolites across genotypes. Differential analysis identified 10 metabolites significantly altered between E4/4 and E3/3 mice (Figure S1). These included amino acids and amino acid‐related metabolites, biogenic amines such as dopamine and choline, and free fatty acids. The metabolite profile in E4/4 mice treated with D4F shifted toward E3/3 levels following D4F treatment (Figure S1). These findings demonstrate broader disruption of metabolic pathways in E4/4 brain, including those linked to lipid metabolism, membrane composition, and neurotransmitter‐associated processes, and the potential of D4F treatment to mitigate APOE4‐associated metabolic alterations.

D4F treatment reverses APOE4‐associated lipid alterations in the brain

To evaluate whether D4F treatment could modify APOE4‐associated lipid dysregulation, we performed targeted lipidomics on cortical tissue and compared lipid profiles of D4F‐treated E4/4 mice with PBS‐treated E4/4 controls in reference to E3/3 mice. Principal component analysis demonstrated clear separation between all three groups (Figure 5A). Differential abundance analysis comparing D4F‐treated versus PBS‐treated E4/4 controls identified 33 significantly altered lipid levels, with 14 increased and 19 decreased following treatment (Figure 5B). Examination of the abundance of the major lipid classes revealed that D4F treatment substantially altered the lipid profile in E4/4 mice (Figure 5C). Importantly, further analysis of individual lipid species that were significantly different between E4/4 and E3/3 genotypes revealed that D4F treatment shifted most of these lipids in E4/4 mice toward E3/3 levels (Figure 5D).

Targeted protein validation supports disruption of lipid and synaptic pathways in APOE4 brain and rescue by D4F treatment

To provide orthogonal validation of pathways identified by transcriptomic data, we assessed protein levels of key targets involved in cholesterol metabolism, lipoprotein trafficking, and synaptic function in brains from E4/4, E4/4 D4F‐treated, and E3/3 mice (Figure 6A‐B).

We first examined lipoprotein‐related receptors involved in cholesterol uptake and intracellular trafficking, including LDLR and SORL1. LDLR is a major receptor for ApoE‐containing lipoproteins and plays a central role in cholesterol uptake and redistribution in the brain, 53 and reduced LDLR expression may contribute to disrupted lipid delivery and membrane lipid composition. In addition, LDLR deficiency accelerates whereas LDLR overexpression inhibits amyloid deposition in the brain of AD mice. 54 , 55 LDLR protein levels were significantly reduced in E4/4 mice compared to E3/3 controls, consistent with impaired lipoprotein uptake and cholesterol homeostasis. SORL1 is a sorting receptor that regulates intracellular trafficking of ApoE‐containing lipoproteins and a genetic risk factor of AD, 56 and SORL1 deficiency has been shown to alter phosphatidylcholine and sphingolipid metabolism. 57 In the present study, SORL1 protein levels showed a strong trend toward reduction in E4/4 mice compared to E3/3 (p = 0.0587). Importantly, D4F treatment increased the expression of both LDLR and SORL1 proteins to equivalent levels in E3/3 mice (Figure 6A‐B). These coordinated changes suggest that APOE4 disrupts both lipoprotein uptake and intracellular lipid trafficking pathways, and that D4F improves these receptor‐mediated processes.

We next assessed SREBP2, a master regulator of cholesterol homeostasis that regulates genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis and uptake, including LDLR. 58 Consistent with suppression of transcriptomic lipid metabolic and lipoprotein remodeling pathways in E4/4 brains, SREBP2 protein levels were significantly reduced in E4/4 mice compared to E3/3 controls. D4F treatment significantly increased SREBP2 levels in E4/4 mice, shifting expression toward E3/3 levels (Figure 6A‐B).

In addition to receptor‐mediated lipid handling, we examined proteins involved in cholesterol/lipid efflux. ABCA1, a key regulator of cholesterol/lipid efflux, was increased in E4/4 relative to E3/3 mice (Figure 6A‐B). This apparent paradox likely results from impaired intracellular trafficking and function as recently reported. 59 While D4F treatment did not significantly change the levels of ABCA1 in E4/4 mice, it appeared to have improved its function. This notion is supported by the finding that ApoE protein levels were reduced in E4/4 compared to E3/3 but increased/normalized following D4F treatment (Figure 6A‐B). This is consistent with prior work showing altered ApoE stability and turnover in APOE4 brains 60 and D4F enhances ApoE secretion and lipidation through ABCA1 in astrocytes. 23 Together, these findings demonstrate that cholesterol/lipid metabolism is impaired in the brain of E4/4 mice and D4F treatment modulates the expression and function of key proteins involved in cholesterol/lipid homeostasis, normalizing toward the levels in E3/3 mice.

To evaluate synaptic alterations shown by transcriptomic analyses, we measured SYT1, a presynaptic vesicle protein critical for neurotransmitter release. 61 SYT1 protein levels were significantly reduced in E4/4 mice relative to E3/3 mice and partially restored by D4F treatment (Figure 6A‐B), aligning with downregulation of synaptic pathways identified by RNA‐seq in E4/4 mice and reversed by D4F treatment.

To further assess whether transcriptomic alterations in lipid metabolism were reflected functionally at the tissue level, we performed immunofluorescence staining for PLIN2, a LD‐associated protein that stabilizes LDs and regulates lipase access. 62 PLIN2 is a well‐established marker of LDs in the brain, with expression increasing with aging and in Alzheimer's disease. 63 In our transcriptomic data, several LD‐associated genes, including members of the perilipin family, were increased in E4/4 mice, while the lipid hydrolytic enzyme Pnpla2 was decreased (Figure 3C). PLIN2‐positive puncta were quantified in specific cell types in the hippocampal and cortical regions using co‐staining with MAP2 (neurons), GFAP (astrocytes), IBA1 (microglia), and CD31 (endothelia) (Figure 6C‐D). In the hippocampus, E4/4 mice exhibited a significant increase in the number of PLIN2‐positive puncta in GFAP+ cells compared to E3/3 controls, indicating increased LD accumulation in astrocytes. D4F treatment reduced PLIN2 puncta toward E3/3 levels. Similarly, PLIN2 puncta in CD31+ endothelial cells were elevated in E4/4 mice and showed a strong trend of reduction following D4F treatment (p = 0.0636). These findings suggest altered lipid handling and redistribution in E4/4 brains at the cellular level. Interestingly, no significant differences were observed in PLIN2 puncta in MAP2+ neurons or IBA1+ microglia, and no significant changes were detected in cortical regions (Figure S2). These results indicate that LD accumulation in E4/4 brains at this age is region‐ and cell type‐specific, with prominent effects in astrocytes and endothelial cells in the hippocampus, and that D4F treatment partially mitigates these alterations.

Together, these results demonstrate that APOE4‐associated disruptions in lipid metabolism and synaptic pathways observed at the transcriptomic and lipidomic levels are reflected at the protein levels. Importantly, D4F treatment rescues key regulators of cholesterol handling, lipid trafficking, and synaptic function, supporting a coordinated normalization of lipid homeostasis and neuronal signaling.

DISCUSSION

The current study investigates how the APOE4 genotype alters brain gene expression and lipid homeostasis and assesses the therapeutic potential of HDL‐mimetic D4F in mitigating these changes. While previous work from our group demonstrated that D4F reduces amyloid‐related pathology in AD mouse models, 22 , 24 , 25 , 26 its effects on APOE genotype‐specific transcriptomic and lipidomic signatures remain unclear, representing a key gap given APOE4’s strong influence on lipid metabolism and neurodegeneration.

Our transcriptomic analyses revealed widespread gene expression changes in APOE4/4 mice compared to APOE3/3 controls, including suppression of lipid metabolic pathways, lipoprotein remodeling, and synaptic signaling processes. Immunoblot analyses validated these data, showing that key proteins involved in maintaining cholesterol/lipid homeostasis and synaptic function, including LDLR, SORL1, SREBP‐2, ABCA1, APOE, and SYT1, are dysregulated in E4/4 mice compared to E3/3 mice. These changes indicate that ApoE4 disrupts both lipid handling and synaptic integrity, processes critical for maintaining neuronal health and resilience with age.

Synaptic dysfunction is a primary early pathogenic event in AD, occurring before overt neurodegeneration. 64 Consistently, carriers of the APOE4 allele exhibit altered synaptic morphology and function. 65 , 66 APOE4 targeted replacement mouse models show similar impairments in synaptic function and dendritic structure. 67 Our transcriptomic findings align with these prior observations and reveal suppression of genes involved in synaptic adhesion, signaling, and structural maintenance in APOE4 KI mice.

WGCNA further identified coordinated gene network disruptions associated with APOE4. The brown module negatively correlated with E4/4 mice and was enriched for genes involved in synaptic function and membrane potential. In contrast, the darkgreen module was positively correlated with E4/4 mice and enriched for immune‐related pathways, suggesting elevated inflammatory signaling. These findings highlight a broad systems‐level transcriptional shift in E4/4 brains, characterized by suppressed neuronal function and enhanced immune activation.

The effects of D4F on brain transcriptional programs are only beginning to be explored in AD mouse models 26 and its broader impact on the brain of humanized APOE mice has not been characterized previously. In the present study, we observed that D4F treatment partially normalized certain pathways and gene modules, including those involved in synapse formation and neuronal signaling, suggesting that improving lipid homeostasis may yield downstream synaptic benefits. This is especially relevant given the central role of synaptic dysfunction in AD progression. 68 GSEA using curated AD‐relevant biological domains 41 revealed that E4/4 mice exhibited coordinated downregulation across multiple domains. D4F treatment partially reversed many of these changes, particularly within the synapse and proteostasis domains, indicating that this HDL‐mimetic may modulate interconnected biological networks disrupted by APOE4. These findings provide further support for D4F's potential to counteract early transcriptomic signatures of AD vulnerability. This notion is supported further by the results of immunoblot analyses showing the rescue of cholesterol/lipid‐ and synaptic‐related molecules by D4F treatment in E4/4 toward E3/3 at the protein level.

WGCNA identified additional D4F‐responsive modules whose expression shifted toward the E3/3 profile in E4/4 mice. For example, the greenyellow module included genes related to retinoic acid and isoprenoid biosynthesis pathways, both of which are implicated in AD. Retinoic acid isomers have been shown to enhance APOE expression and lipidation in astrocytes via activation of retinoid X and retinoic acid receptors. 69 Isoprenoid biosynthesis, part of the mevalonate pathway, generates lipid intermediates such as farnesyl pyrophosphate that are essential for the prenylation of small GTPases involved in synaptic signaling and membrane trafficking. 70 This pathway is under feedback control by sterol regulatory element‐binding proteins (SREBPs), which adjust gene expression based on intracellular cholesterol levels. 71 Immunoblot analysis showed that SREBP2 protein levels were reduced in E4/4 brains and were shifted toward E3/3 levels following D4F treatment. As SREBP signaling plays a central role in coordinating cholesterol synthesis and isoprenoid production, these data suggest that dysregulation of this axis may contribute to impaired lipid homeostasis and downstream effects on membrane dynamics and synaptic function in E4/4 brains. Given prior work linking dysregulated prenylation to AD pathogenesis, 35 , 70 , 72 these findings may help inform future mechanistic studies.

The lightcyan module also showed a modest shift toward the E3/3 profile in treated E4/4 mice. This module was enriched for pathways related to neurodevelopment, including WNT signaling, neural precursor cell proliferation, and axon guidance. WNT pathways regulate synaptic structure and plasticity and have been implicated in AD‐related processes such as Aβ accumulation and synapse loss. 73 The observed transcriptional shift suggests that D4F may influence neurogenic and plasticity‐related programs, hinting at broader effects beyond lipid regulation.

We next examined whether these transcriptional changes were mirrored at the lipid level. Our lipidomic analysis revealed substantial alterations in brain lipid profiles of APOE4/4 mice compared to APOE3/3 controls. The observed increases in ceramides, sphingomyelins, and cholesteryl esters, coupled with decreases in triglycerides and diglycerides, suggest a fundamental disruption in lipid homeostasis. These results partially align with prior lipidomic findings in the entorhinal cortex of APOE targeted replacement mice 29 and are consistent with previous reports of elevated ceramide and sphingolipid levels in the brains of APOE4 carriers. 74 The accumulation of ceramides and sphingomyelins is particularly notable given their roles in apoptosis, inflammation, and neurodegeneration. 75 In AD brains, carriers of ceramide proteins co‐localize with amyloid, and may promote pathogenic Aβ production through a feed‐forward mechanism involving increased sphingomyelinase activity. 76 The therapeutic relevance of this pathway is supported by studies showing that sphingomyelinase‐2 knockout in AD mouse models results in reduced ceramide generation and improved cognitive outcomes. 77

Total cholesteryl esters were also elevated in APOE4/4 brains, with CE 14:0 being affected most significantly. While these changes are often interpreted in the context of cholesterol biosynthesis, the particular increase in CE 14:0 may also reflect altered substrate availability or esterification dynamics. In support, studies in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐derived astrocytes show that APOE4 disrupts intracellular lipid homeostasis by promoting lysosomal cholesterol accumulation, impairing efflux, and increasing LD formation, 78 , 79 , 80 Large‐scale lipidomic analyses further demonstrate APOE4‐dependent CE accumulation in human astrocytes and AD brain tissue. 81 Furthermore, post‐mortem studies of APOE4/4 brains report increased CE species in white matter regions, where cholesterol sequestration in oligodendrocyte LDs is associated with reduced myelination. 82 These human data reinforce our observation of CE elevation in E4/4 mice and support the broader relevance of CE accumulation as a lipid signature of APOE4‐associated dysfunction.

Intriguingly, D4F treatment shifted levels of ceramides and cholesteryl esters in APOE4/4 mice toward those observed in APOE3/3 mice, indicating that the HDL‐mimetic peptide helps re‐establish lipid homeostasis in E4/4 brains via genetic and metabolic modulation. Restoration of lipid homeostasis may underlie some of the beneficial effects previously observed with D4F in amyloid models of AD, 22 , 24 , 25 , 26 although this remains to be more thoroughly investigated.

Transcriptomic analysis paralleled these lipid‐level changes, revealing broad alterations in key lipid synthesis and transport pathways in E4/4 mice. D4F treatment shifted the expression of several pathway‐level genes toward E3/3‐like profiles. In the cholesterol pathway, this included modulation of genes involved in sterol biosynthesis and intracellular lipid transport, suggesting improved cholesterol distribution. Similarly, genes involved in ceramide turnover and sphingosine recycling were altered in a direction consistent with reduced ceramide accumulation. Importantly, immunoblot analyses validated the impact of APOE4 and the rescue by D4F treatment on cholesterol/lipid‐related pathways at the protein level.

Lipid droplet biology provides an additional link between APOE4 and lipid dysregulation. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed LD accumulation in astrocytes and endothelial cells in E4/4 mice. Astrocytes are main producers of ApoE and central regulators of brain cholesterol and fatty acid homeostasis, and they sequester excess fatty acids into LDs under oxidative or metabolic stress. 83 Our observations are consistent with prior studies that showed APOE4 astrocytes accumulate more LDs than APOE3 astrocytes, 84 and emerging evidence that brain endothelial cells become lipid‐laden with aging and exhibit altered blood‐brain barrier‐related transcriptional signature. 85 These findings suggest that LD accumulation in E4/4 brains is most prominent in astrocytic and endothelial compartments at this stage and may contribute to blood‐brain barrier dysfunction, a feature implicated in AD. 26 Attenuation of these changes by D4F further supports a role for lipid‐targeted interventions in modulating APOE4‐associated lipid imbalance.

This study has some limitations. While our RNA‐sequencing sample size (n = 7‐8/group) is typical for bulk transcriptomic studies, lipidomic analysis used a smaller subset of samples (n = 3 per group), which may reduce robustness and prone pathway or network interpretations to false positives and inflated significance. Furthermore, lipidomics was performed only in female mice based on our transcriptomic analyses that did not reveal significant sex‐specific differences in relevant pathways identified in this study. In addition, APOE4 is associated with greater AD risk and more pronounced pathological effects in females. 86 Future studies with larger cohorts of both sexes will be important to confirm and extend these findings and to directly test the mechanisms underlying D4F effects, which are inferred here from our multi‐omics data and prior literature.

In conclusion, our results highlight a profound dysregulation of lipid metabolism and synaptic function in the brain of APOE4/4 mice and demonstrate that D4F treatment can partially restore lipid homeostasis and modulate some AD‐relevant molecular pathways. These findings suggest that targeting lipid homeostasis with HDL‐mimetic peptides like D4F may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating APOE4‐associated AD risk.
