Enhanced stratification of male pattern hair loss using AI through novel loss region ratio analysis
Authors: Haonan Xi, Xuhai Yuan, Hao Yuan, Kaibin Lin, Chong Wang, Zheng Wang, Jianglin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMale pattern hair loss (MPHL) is a common dermatological condition with significant psychological and clinical impacts. Traditional grading systems, such as the Norwood-Hamilton and Basic and Specific (BASP) classifications, rely on subjective assessments, limiting accuracy and reproducibility. This study introduces an AI-based grading framework that incorporates a novel area ratio metric to provide a more objective and standardized assessment of MPHL severity. A total of 761 images from 257 patients were analyzed to develop and evaluate an AI-based framework for grading MPHL. The framework utilizes the area ratio metric as a quantitative alternative to the traditional BASP length ratio. Model performance was assessed using precision and recall metrics, with bounding box and mask evaluations validating the accuracy and consistency of hair loss region identification. The AI model achieved an average precision of 97.6% in bounding box evaluations and 96.1% in mask assessments, demonstrating high accuracy in identifying hair loss regions. However, challenges in detecting smaller regions were reflected in slightly lower recall values for mask evaluations. Stratification accuracy was consistently high for Grades I, II, IV, and VI, with variability observed in intermediate grades due to boundary overlap. The novel area ratio metric outperformed the BASP length ratio, particularly in higher MPHL grades, offering a more reliable and objective framework for hair loss classification. This study shows that AI can improve MPHL diagnostics by using a data-driven and standardized grading system. The area ratio metric enhances precision and consistency, especially in advanced MPHL grades, offering better diagnostic accuracy and supporting personalized hair loss treatments. Introduction

Male pattern hair loss (MPHL), also known as androgenetic alopecia, is the most prevalent form of non-scarring progressive hair loss. It affects up to 80% of men during their lifetime, making it a significant dermatological and public health concern1,2. This condition’s pathogenesis primarily involves genetic predisposition and androgen status, although other factors like persistent stress and imbalanced diet al.so contribute3. While not life-threatening, MPHL can significantly impact psychological well-being, often leading to depression and anxiety4–6. Characteristically, MPHL begins with bitemporal recession of the frontal hairline and progresses to diffuse thinning at the vertex. The Norwood-Hamilton classification, the most commonly used method for MPHL assessment, categorizes the condition into seven levels based on severity4,7,8, guiding treatment strategies that range from no intervention at level 1 to medication, hair transplantation, or wigs for more advanced stages9 (depicted in Table 1). However, the reliance on visual observations and the Hamilton-Norwood scale for diagnosis and classification is subjective and time-intensive, highlighting the need for more objective and efficient assessment methods.

Historically, MPHL was classified into two predominant patterns: vertex (crown) involvement and frontal recession2,10. This early dichotomous classification reflected clinical observations that follicular sensitivity to androgenic influences varies across scalp regions, with vertex follicles exhibiting greater susceptibility to miniaturization. However, this binary approach oversimplified the complexity of clinical presentations, as mixed or multifocal patterns involving both frontal and vertex regions are frequently encountered in practice. Recognition of these complexities led to the development of integrated classification systems, such as the Norwood–Hamilton and Basic and Specific (BASP) classifications, designed to provide a more comprehensive assessment of MPHL. The BASP classification, in particular, represents a systematic and universal framework for evaluating patterned hair loss, incorporating both anterior hairline shape and hair density variations across frontal and vertex regions. This classification takes the proportion of the recession length as a criterion to classify MPHL, which is useful and enlightening11, as depicted in Table 1.

Recent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) have significantly improved the diagnosis and classification of hair diseases, particularly alopecia. Kapoor et al. devised a model employing a feedforward artificial neural network and backpropagation algorithm, achieving a notable 91% accuracy in differentiating between alopecia patients and those without the condition, thereby providing an invaluable adjunct to clinical decision-making12. Jeong et al. introduced AI-ScalpGrader, an integrated system that combines a portable imaging device, a mobile app, and cloud-based AI to diagnose and classify ten different scalp conditions. Utilizing the EfficientNet Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model, this system attained accuracies ranging from 87.3% to 91.3%13. Kim et al. further refined diagnostic accuracy by incorporating data preprocessing, augmentation, and an ensemble of deep learning models, resulting in an impressive 95.75% accuracy and an F1 score of 87.05%14. Additionally, Shakeel et al. applied machine learning techniques, specifically Support Vector Machine (SVM) and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), to a dataset comprising both healthy and alopecia areata-affected hair images, achieving accuracies of 91.4% and 88.9%, respectively15. Finally, Mittal et al. introduced a CNN-based approach that surpassed traditional machine learning models, attaining a 98% accuracy rate in detecting alopecia areata16.

However, existing studies on MPHL grading exhibit significant limitations. Although extensive research has explored MPHL pathogenesis, there remains limited development of AI-driven stratification frameworks, especially those effectively integrating technological innovation with clinical applicability12–16. This disparity hampers the translation of algorithmic advances into standardized and clinically relevant diagnostic tools. Moreover, many AI-based studies inadequately replicate real-world clinical diagnostic procedures, resulting in findings with limited interpretability and practical applicability. Such discrepancies arise from insufficient integration with clinical workflows; thus, while AI methods may accurately predict classifications from learned datasets, they often fail to capture the inherent complexities and subtleties observed in dermatological practice. This highlights a critical need for advanced methodologies explicitly aligned with clinical realities to ensure both relevance and utility in practical dermatology12–17.

To address these limitations, we propose an objective area ratio approach leveraging Mask R-CNN, a model renowned for its robust instance segmentation capabilities and effectiveness in handling complex scenarios17–22. This approach is further enhanced through the integration of deep learning techniques to enable a region-based assessment of severity. Finally, the proposed method will be systematically evaluated against traditional approaches to rigorously assess its performance, with the aim of demonstrating its superiority in terms of accuracy and efficiency.

Methods

All images were captured using a standardized frontal perspective, with patients seated and positioned according to the Frankfort horizontal plane, ensuring consistent visualization of the frontal hairline and vertex region. Images were obtained using a Canon EOS 90D camera equipped with a 60 mm lens, positioned at a fixed distance of 50 cm under controlled diffuse LED lighting conditions. Prior to photography, patients’ hair was gently combed to fully expose the scalp surface without creating artificial partings; no topical substances (e.g., water, gels) were applied, maintaining natural hair distribution and minimizing occlusion of hair-loss regions. This standardized imaging protocol facilitated a consistent and accurate assessment of hair loss severity22–25. Notably, the study intentionally excluded Hamilton–Norwood grade VII patients due to the comparatively low prevalence of this grade, which represents approximately 4.6% of alopecia cases across the 0–80-year age range and is even rarer (approximately 1%) among patients younger than 40 years22.

Hairline annotation

To achieve accurate prediction and segmentation of the hairline through supervised learning, a structured workflow (Fig. 1) was employed for the automatic segmentation and grading of male pattern hair loss regions. Initially, standardized scalp images were acquired using a rigorously controlled imaging protocol. Subsequently, the acquired images underwent professional annotation by three dermatologists specialized in hair disorders. Each dermatologist independently analyzed and classified the images according to the Hamilton–Norwood classification system10, and subsequently reached a consensus on the precise delineation of the hairline boundaries. To ensure consistency and clinical validity, hairline annotations were standardized at the intersection of the scalp’s horizontal plane and the facial vertical plane, positioned approximately four finger-widths (approximately 8–10 cm) above the eyebrows23–25.

(D) The algorithm selects the most confident instance mask for precise stratification, utilizing the area ratio between the hair-covered and hair loss areas for grading severity.

Following annotation, a Mask R-CNN model was fine-tuned using pre-trained weights from the Common Objects in Context (COCO) dataset. After training, the optimized model could automatically process new input images, segment the balding regions, and quantify the ratio of hair loss area relative to the total scalp area.

Overall framework

The proposed model employs a Mask R-CNN backbone to objectively quantify MPHL severity based on a computed area ratio, integrating deep learning-based segmentation with quantitative image analysis to enhance grading accuracy and clinical efficiency. As illustrated in Fig. 1D, the computational framework applies Mask R-CNN to delineate hair loss regions precisely from patient scalp images. The model was developed using a dataset of 761 images collected from 257 patients. In our training and evaluation, “healthy” and “unhealthy” regions are each represented by a separate binary mask: one mask labels intact hairline pixels, and the other labels areas of follicular loss. This dual-mask approach ensures that the model learns to distinguish and accurately delineate both preserved and balding scalp regions.

The dataset was divided into training (180 patients, 540 images; 70%), validation (28 patients, 84 images; 10%), and independent test sets (49 patients, 137 images; 20%), with patient-level separation to prevent data leakage. Test images were randomly selected from the cohort, excluded from training and validation, and met strict quality criteria, including full scalp exposure and absence of motion artifacts. To improve model generalizability, data augmentation was applied using grayscale conversion, horizontal flipping, and random cropping. The model was trained with 5-fold cross-validation over 200 epochs, using a batch size of four. The combined loss function included RPN classification, RPN bounding box regression, and Mask R-CNN segmentation. Optimization employed the Adam optimizer (β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 1 × 10−⁸, weight_decay = 0) with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule (initial lr = 1 × 10−⁴, T_max = 200 epochs, minimum lr = 1 × 10−).

Post model-development (shown in Fig. 2), the segmentation results distinguish between areas of hair presence and absence. In the ensuing post-processing phase, these segmented regions are quantitatively delineated to calculate the area ratio—crucial for the classification of hair loss severity. This ratio is visually articulated on the post-processed images, with color differentiation denoting specific areas; hair regions are annotated in red, with hair loss areas highlighted in green. The segmented images undergo mask fusion and area calculation, which likely involves overlaying the segmented mask on the original image to accurately calculate the affected area. The process culminates in the classification of hair loss severity into six categories, as shown by the different illustrations of hair loss patterns on the heads on the right side of the flowchart. The color coding of red for hair region and green for hair loss area helps visually differentiate between healthy and affected areas, which is instrumental in assessing the degree of hair loss.

Statistical analysis

To assess the performance of the Mask R-CNN-based segmentation technique in conjunction with the objective area ratio metric for hair loss classification, we implemented a comprehensive statistical evaluation protocol. Model performance was quantified using AP and recall metrics calculated at multiple IoU thresholds. In addition, a confusion matrix was constructed to report the classification accuracy for each of the six MPHL severity stages (I–VI), with results presented to two decimal places. Comparative analyses of distribution density and measures of central tendency were conducted to elucidate differences and overlaps between severity levels, providing insight into the discriminative capacity of the area ratio-based grading system relative to traditional classification methods.

Results

This study presents a quantitative area ratio metric for severity grading, leveraging the segmentation capabilities of Mask R-CNN to enable automated identification of hairless regions for the classification of male pattern baldness. The model is optimized for segmentation tasks and evaluated using a comprehensive framework based on AP and recall metrics across multiple IoU thresholds. In bounding box evaluation, the model achieved a precision of 0.976 and a recall of 0.953, indicating high localization accuracy. Robustness of the approach was further demonstrated through five-fold cross-validation, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.933 and a recall of 0.914.

For grade I, the model achieved 100% accuracy, demonstrating perfect identification of this stage. The accuracy for grade II was 98%, with a minor misclassification of 2% as grade III. In grade III, the true positive rate was 81%, but there was noticeable confusion, with 10% of cases misclassified as grade II and 9% as grade IV, highlighting the challenges in distinguishing intermediate grades. Grade IV achieved an accuracy of 92%, with 8% misclassified as grade III. For grade V, the model achieved 86% accuracy, with 11% misclassified as grade IV and 3% as grade VI. Lastly, grade VI demonstrated a strong performance with a 95% accuracy rate, with 5% misclassified as grade V.

In contrast, the ‘Area’ method exhibits less variability, implying more consistent assessments, especially notable in the higher severity stages V and VI. The findings highlight the relative robustness of the ‘Area’ assessment in consistently gauging hair loss severity, offering a refined metric over BASP for clinical application. Furthermore, the less dispersed distribution profiles at the initial stages as illustrated by our method suggest a precision in early MPHL grading that may be instrumental in advancing clinical evaluations and interventions.

The model demonstrates the capacity to classify MPHL into severity stages I through VI through a structured and quantitative image analysis approach. The progression across stages reflects the model’s ability to detect subtle variations in hair recession and to compute corresponding area-based metrics. Specifically, the area ratios representing the proportion of hair loss relative to the total scalp area were 0.0%, 14.4%, 24.6%, 34.2%, 40.3%, and 65.9% for stages I through VI, respectively. This consistent increase across stages underscores the model’s capacity to provide an objective and systematic quantification of hair loss severity. The precision of the segmentation results, as reflected in the consistent correlation between clinical observations and computed area ratios, supports the validity of the deep learning framework for translating visual scalp features into accurate, data-driven severity classifications.

Discussion

Our study proposes an innovation in MPHL stratification by integrating deep learning-based segmentation with a quantitative surface-based metric, a method we term the Area-Segmentation Integration (ASI). Unlike traditional grading systems such as the Basic and Specific (BASP) classification, which rely primarily on visual assessment of hairline shape and recession length, our approach combines AI-driven image segmentation with objective area ratio analysis to provide a reproducible and fine-grained assessment of hair loss severity. Using a dataset of 761 images from 257 patients, our model demonstrated robust performance across varying MPHL stages, achieving high average precision and recall in both bounding box and mask evaluations. The area ratio metric showed a more consistent and narrower distribution than the BASP length ratio, particularly at higher severity stages, suggesting enhanced reliability in clinical grading.

The clinical implications of this approach are significant. By offering a data-driven and objective measure of hair loss severity, the framework enhances diagnostic precision and facilitates more accurate evaluation of treatment efficacy. Furthermore, the versatility of the Mask R-CNN model suggests broader applicability in dermatology, where it may be extended to assess various skin conditions beyond MPHL. In this manner, the study underscores the potential of deep learning techniques to transform dermatological diagnostics by providing quantitative, reproducible, and clinically informative results.

To promote practical implementation in clinical settings, we propose developing a mobile application (APP) that streamlines and standardizes the image capture process for dermatologists. This APP would enforce consistent imaging protocols, including standardized angles and distances, thereby ensuring uniform data quality. Once images are acquired, the APP would automatically process them through our AI model, delivering an immediate and objective assessment of MPHL severity. We believe that this targeted deployment strategy will not only bolster clinical utility but also pave the way for future research aimed at refining the predictive capabilities of our framework through expanded datasets and advanced modeling approaches.

This quantitative framework directly addresses the long-standing reproducibility limitations of visual classification systems by substituting subjective pattern recognition with pixel-level analysis. Moreover, graphical tools such as violin plots emphasized the stability of our metric, particularly in early-stage detection. In contrast to immune-mediated conditions like Alopecia Areata (AA)—which involve acute, binary-pattern hair loss—MPHL follows a gradual, androgen-mediated progression characterized by follicular miniaturization at the individual follicle level over time1,26. While our current model captures macroscopic changes effectively, future integration with validated trichoscopic parameters (e.g., hair shaft diameter < 30 μm, density < 100 follicles/cm²) may enable early detection of miniaturization, bridging macro- and micro-scale diagnostic insights.

Limitations

This study significantly advances the assessment of MPHL using deep learning, yet it has several limitations. First, our imaging system (Canon EOS 90D; 32.5 MP resolution; pixel size: 3.2 μm; 60 mm lens) cannot resolve micro-scale follicular structures smaller than 200 μm. Consequently, precise metrics like follicular miniaturization (20–50 μm) or hair density require specialized methods such as trichoscopy or phototrichogram analysis. Incorporating trichoscopic techniques into future AI frameworks could enable more comprehensive severity assessments at a follicular level. Second, our frontal-focused analysis, while accurate for Norwood–Hamilton patterns, may have limited applicability to frontal-sparing alopecia types, such as Ludwig patterns2. Additionally, frontal follicles typically exhibit lower regrowth potential than vertex follicles, meaning that treatment outcomes based exclusively on frontal metrics might underestimate overall improvement1. To address these limitations, future studies will incorporate multi-region analysis (e.g., vertex, occipital regions) and adopt standardized approaches such as the Scalp Coverage Scoring (SCS) method introduced by Van Neste et al.27, enhancing objectivity and comprehensiveness in severity evaluation.

Furthermore, our dataset of 761 images from 257 patients lacks representation of Hamilton–Norwood grade VII cases, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Stratification accuracy in intermediate MPHL grades is hindered by boundary overlap and mixed-pattern characteristics, complicating precise categorization. The limited dataset size and diversity may also restrict the model’s applicability across different ethnicities and age groups. Moreover, traditional expert grading methods exhibit inherent inter-observer variability, with dermatologists achieving only 65–78% reproducibility for Norwood–Hamilton staging7. Although our AI-based method reduces subjectivity through quantitative area ratios, residual variability persists, particularly in intermediate stages (grades III and V). Future studies incorporating 3D scalp mapping and refined algorithmic approaches for smaller regions could further improve model accuracy and robustness.

Conclusion

Unlike previous AI-based approaches that focus primarily on classification tasks, the proposed ASI framework introduces a novel fusion of geometric area quantification with instance segmentation, establishing a new paradigm for objective and reproducible assessment of MPHL severity. Despite its promise, integrating AI-driven methodologies into routine clinical practice presents several challenges. These include the need for acceptance and adaptation by healthcare professionals, seamless incorporation into existing clinical workflows, and the requirement for consistent performance across diverse patient populations and clinical environments. Additionally, the limited size and diversity of the current dataset raise concerns regarding potential model overfitting to specific hair loss patterns, which may restrict generalizability. To address these limitations, future research should focus on expanding the dataset to include a wider range of MPHL presentations, refining algorithmic architectures to improve model robustness, and developing standardized protocols to facilitate reliable and scalable clinical implementation.
