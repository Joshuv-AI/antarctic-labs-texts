Strain echocardiography for assessment of cardiac graft function following donation after circulatory death heart transplantation
Authors: Duc M. Giao, George E. Olverson, Ameen Basha, Abraham Sonny, Vipin Mehta, Ibraheem Hamzat, Rashid Walker, Dane C. Paneitz, Ruby Singh, Kamila Drezek, Alyssa Ramsay, Ioannis Mastoris, Kemar Brown, S. Alireza Rabi, Eriberto Michel, David A. D’Alessandro, Asishana A. Osho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDonation after circulatory death (DCD) heart transplantation demonstrates preserved conventional ventricular and valvular function through 1-year follow-up. However, strain imaging identifies subtle but persistent abnormalities in myocardial deformation, suggesting distinct myocardial recovery patterns and possible subclinical ischemia-related graft injury after DCD transplantation.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study compared DCD and Donation after Brain Death (DBD) heart transplant recipients from 2016 to 2022, at 1-, 3-, 6-, and 12-month post-transplantation. Conventional echocardiographic variables, valvular function, and speckle-tracking strain analysis, including left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LVGLS), right ventricular global longitudinal strain (RVGLS), and right ventricular free wall strain (RVFWS) were compared between DCD and DBD recipients. Multivariable linear regression, chi-squared/Fisher Exact testing, and linear mixed-effects modeling were used to evaluate longitudinal outcomes. Exploratory multivariable analyses evaluated associations between GLS and perioperative variables.ResultsFifty-one DCD and 201 DBD hearts were transplanted. Conventional echocardiography demonstrated no significant differences in LVEF or presence of valvular dysfunction at any timepoint. The treatment group-by-time interaction was not significant (p = 0.566). However, DCD recipients demonstrated significantly less negative LVGLS at 3 months (−13.9±3.54 vs −18.0±3.19, p<0.0001) and 12 months (−16.0±3.08 vs −18.1±3.17, p = 0.0007). Early RV dysfunction was also observed in DCD recipients at 3 months by RVGLS and RVFWS analysis. Longitudinal mixed-effects modeling demonstrated a significant donor type-by-timepoint interaction for GLS (p = 0.034). Exploratory multivariable modeling demonstrated that DCD donor status (β=1.52, p = 0.021) and total ischemic interval (β=0.0057, p = 0.034) remained independently associated with impaired GLS.ConclusionDCD heart transplantation demonstrates preserved conventional ventricular and valvular function through 1-year follow-up. However, strain imaging identifies subtle but persistent abnormalities in myocardial deformation, suggesting distinct myocardial recovery patterns and possible subclinical ischemia-related graft injury after DCD transplantation.

Recipients of heart transplantation remain at risk for graft dysfunction from multiple etiologies including rejection, ischemia-reperfusion injury, cardiac allograft vasculopathy, and perioperative injury.1, 2, 3 Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) remains the primary noninvasive modality for surveillance of graft function after transplantation.4, 5 Although left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is routinely used to assess systolic function, strain imaging using speckle-tracking echocardiography may provide greater sensitivity for detecting subclinical myocardial dysfunction.6, 7, 8, 9, 10

Donation after circulatory death (DCD) heart transplantation has emerged as an important strategy to expand the donor pool and has demonstrated survival outcomes comparable to donation after brain death (DBD) transplantation.11, 12 However, longitudinal functional data evaluating ventricular mechanics after DCD transplantation remain limited. Given the additional warm ischemic exposure associated with DCD procurement, strain imaging may identify subtle myocardial dysfunction not apparent on conventional echocardiography. Therefore, we compared longitudinal ventricular and valvular function in DCD and DBD heart transplant recipients using standard echocardiography and strain analysis.

Methods

All adult patients at a single tertiary institution undergoing isolated orthotopic heart transplantation between 2016 and 2022 using donor hearts procured from DCD or DBD donors greater than 18 years old were selected. This retrospective data analysis was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board. Informed consent was waived.

Patient baseline characteristics were compared using two-sample Student’s t-tests with equal variances for continuous variables and Chi-squared tests or the Fisher Exact test when expected cell counts were less than 5 for categorical variables, and mean comparisons of all ECHO variables were done using two-tailed t-tests. F-tests were performed to assess for equal variances. Continuous normal variables are presented as mean (standard deviations) and non-normal variables as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentages). A 2-sided p-value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

ECHO reports data

Post-operative observations were measured using ECHO at 1-, 3-, 6-, and 12-months post transplantation. LV dynamics and valvular function evaluated using ECHO were compared between DCD and DBD donor heart recipients at each of the 4 post-operative time points. Valvular function was recorded as none/trace, mild, moderate, or severe.

To account for the expected positive correlation between measurements of the same individuals across different post-operative occasions and heterogenous variability on each occasion, linear mixed-effect modeling was used. The estimate of the treatment group and time interaction effect was studied.12 ECR models were adjusted for recipient age, recipient blood group, recipient weight, recipient pre-transplant LVEF, donor LVEF prior to procurement. These variables were selected based on statistical comparisons of baseline characteristics between the 2 study groups. In addition to these variables, the occurrence of aortic regurgitation (AR) was adjusted for at the 3-month time point due to infrequency.

Time interaction was considered as a categorical variable and represents the time points of measurement post-transplantation (i.e., 1 month, 6 months, etc.). A linear mixed model with random intercepts was compared with a linear mixed model with random intercepts and slopes using minimization of the negative double log likelihood and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).13 Finally, a two-way repeated measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed and the group by time interaction was studied to assess for convergence of results among the above models. Missing data was excluded without imputation or other correction due to the intrinsic ability of linear mixed-effects models to handle unbalanced data.

ECHO strain analysis

ESA, post-operative observations were measured at approximately 3-, 6-, and 12-months post transplantation. Follow-up echocardiograms were categorized according to these predefined timepoints using transplant date as the reference. When multiple ECHOs were available within a follow-up interval, the study nearest to the target timepoint was selected for analysis. Patients were excluded from strain analysis if image quality was inadequate for endocardial border tracking or if all required views (4-chamber, 2-chamber, and 3-chamber) were not available.

Speckle tracking echocardiography was performed using the TomTec platform (TomTec Imaging Systems, Unterschleissheim, Germany). Variables of myocardial deformation included left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LVGLS), right ventricular global longitudinal strain (RVGLS), and right ventricular free wall strain (RVFWS). GLS values were analyzed and reported in their conventional negative form, where less negative values indicate relatively impaired myocardial deformation.

For descriptive comparisons between DCD and DBD recipients, strain variables at each timepoint were summarized as mean (standard deviation) and compared using independent two-sample Student’s t tests assuming equal variances. Only patients with non-missing values at the corresponding timepoint were included in each analysis.

To evaluate longitudinal changes in GLS after transplantation, a linear mixed-effects model was constructed including donor type, timepoint, and a donor type-by-timepoint interaction, with patient-level random intercepts to account for repeated measures within subjects. Exploratory multivariable mixed-effects modeling was additionally performed to assess whether donor type remained independently associated with GLS after adjustment for donor organ LVEF, total ischemic interval, and primary graft dysfunction (PGD).

Exploratory univariable analyses within the DCD cohort evaluated associations between perioperative variables and GLS at each follow-up timepoint. Variables included donor organ LVEF, warm ischemic time, cardiopulmonary bypass time, total ischemic interval, PGD, and time-matched rejection episodes.

Rejection episodes were identified from adverse event records containing the term “rejection” within event descriptions. Time-matched rejection variables were generated such that only rejection episodes occurring on or before the corresponding echocardiographic assessment were included in the 3-, 6-, and 12-month analyses. Timing was calculated from transplant date to adverse event date.

Exploratory longitudinal analyses of right heart catheterization (RHC) data were also performed using thermodilution-derived cardiac index measurements obtained during routine post-transplant surveillance. Cardiac index trajectories over time were evaluated using linear mixed-effects modeling with donor type, timepoint, and donor type-by-timepoint interaction terms.

Results

Comparison of baseline patient characteristics

Two hundred-one DBD donor heart recipients and 51 DCD donor heart recipients were included in the main analysis. The DBD and DCD treatment groups were similar in terms of proportion of males (71.1% vs 76.5%, p = 0.56), distribution of race (p = 0.51), and mean body mass index (BMI) (28.16 [12.53] vs 28.10 [4.22] p = 0.96) (Table 1). In comparison to the DBD reference group, DCD donor heart recipients were more likely to have blood group O (67% vs 43%, p = 0.005), had a higher mean pre-transplant LVEF (29.1% vs 23.6%, p = 0.03), and more likely to have higher LVEF hearts from DCD donors (62% [6.35] vs 56% [10.67], p<0.0001). All DCD grafts were transported on the OCS. Perfusion time of DCD grafts was 250.5 ± 8.41 min. Initial arterial lactate was 6.35 ± 0.19 and initial venous lactate was 6.31 ± 0.20. Final arterial lactate was 4.90 ± 0.23 and final arterial lactate was 4.77 ± 0.25. Results of linear mixed-effects modeling

DCD recipients had no statistically significant difference with DBD hearts in the pattern of change in LVEF over time (p = 0.57) (Table 3). To further account for longitudinal follow-up of patients, the AIC comparison was performed where the linear mixed model with both random slopes and intercepts fit the observed data better than the linear mixed model with random intercepts only (3976.8 vs 3995.4). To confirm findings from the linear mixed-effects models, a repeated ANOVA was performed, and the interaction term was not significant (p = 0.09) indicating no difference in LVEF across all time periods (Table 4). Exploratory multivariable mixed-effects

In an exploratory multivariable mixed-effects model including all recipients and repeated GLS measurements at 3, 6, and 12 months, DCD donor status remained independently associated with impaired GLS after adjustment for timepoint, donor organ LVEF, total ischemic interval, and PGD (β = 1.52, p = 0.021). Total ischemic interval was also independently associated with GLS (β = 0.0057, p = 0.034), whereas donor organ LVEF showed a borderline association (β = −0.048, p = 0.057). PGD was not significantly associated with GLS in the adjusted model (β = 0.60, p = 0.432) (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

The results of this retrospective cohort analysis of 252 isolated orthotopic cardiac transplant recipients determined that although LVEF increased over time for both groups, there was no significant difference between DCD and DBD donor hearts at any time point. Linear mixed-effects modeling accounting for the longitudinal data structure still failed to identify significant effect measure modification of LVEF change over time between DCD and DBD recipients. Confirming model convergence, an equivalent ANOVA model also failed to demonstrate a significant treatment group by time interaction. However, a trend toward significance was noted, suggesting that with increased data, longer follow-up, and more precise measurements, there may be a significant change in LVEF over time.

Advanced analysis STE assessed global longitudinal strain (GLS). This measures the shortening of the myocardium in the longitudinal axis (from base to apex), and is reported as the average of all of the segments of a 17-segment heart model.13 A more negative GLS value correlates to a higher degree of myocardial deformation in that given axis. Longitudinal strain was chosen (rather than radial strain or circumferential strain) due to abundance of data on the impact of the variable and GLS being the only strain measurement described in current American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) guidelines.14, 15

Regarding LV GLS, DCD recipients demonstrated significantly less negative strain values overall, with the largest differences observed at 3 months and persistent differences again at 12 months post-transplantation. Interestingly, the between-group difference attenuated at 6 months before re-emerging at 12 months, which was further supported by the significant donor type-by-timepoint interaction observed in longitudinal mixed-effects modeling. Together, these findings suggest that DCD allografts may follow a distinct trajectory of myocardial deformation recovery over the first post-transplant year despite preserved conventional systolic function.

Importantly, although these differences reached statistical significance, both DCD and DBD groups generally maintained GLS values within conventionally accepted normal ranges. Thus, the abnormalities identified likely represent subclinical alterations in myocardial mechanics rather than overt graft dysfunction. Nevertheless, GLS may still provide clinically meaningful physiologic insight because strain imaging is more sensitive than LVEF for detecting subtle myocardial dysfunction and may identify early alterations in graft adaptation not appreciable with conventional echocardiographic metrics alone.

Notably, exploratory multivariable mixed-effects modeling demonstrated that DCD donor status remained independently associated with impaired GLS even after adjustment for donor organ LVEF, total ischemic interval, and PGD. Total ischemic interval was also independently associated with GLS, supporting the biologic plausibility that ischemic exposure contributes to persistent alterations in myocardial deformation after transplantation. In contrast, PGD was not independently associated with GLS in adjusted analyses, suggesting that the observed strain abnormalities may not simply reflect clinically overt early graft dysfunction. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that DCD grafts may experience subtle myocardial injury or altered recovery patterns that are incompletely captured by conventional perioperative outcomes.

In this context, our results support interpreting GLS as a sensitive physiologic marker of subclinical myocardial performance rather than as an isolated indicator of clinically actionable dysfunction. Accordingly, while these data do not by themselves justify routine GLS surveillance specifically for all DCD recipients, they suggest that strain imaging may serve as a useful adjunct to standard echocardiographic assessment and warrant further study in larger cohorts with longer follow-up and clinical outcome correlation. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of clinically significant graft dysfunction.”

Existing reported evidence

Longitudinal functional data in recipients of DCD donor hearts remain limited in the published literature. In a retrospective analysis with 5-year follow-up, Kumud Dhital et al. evaluated outcomes in 32 DCD heart transplant recipients compared with recipients of DBD donor hearts and demonstrated similar survival between groups.15 However, detailed comparisons of ventricular or valvular function were not reported. Similarly, the Royal Papworth Hospital transplant group reported comparable 5-year survival between DCD and DBD heart transplant recipients.16

Our findings extend these prior observations by demonstrating preserved conventional echocardiographic measures of ventricular and valvular function in DCD recipients while additionally identifying subtle differences in myocardial deformation using strain imaging. Prior work from our group demonstrated non-inferior short-term clinical and hemodynamic outcomes in DCD recipients, including apparent resolution of early right ventricular dysfunction within the first several weeks after transplantation.12 In contrast, the present study suggests that subclinical abnormalities in right ventricular myocardial deformation may persist beyond the early postoperative period despite recovery of conventional hemodynamic parameters and preserved LVEF.

Limitations

This study has several notable limitations. First, this was a retrospective chart review and, therefore, subject to missing data, misclassification, and potential abstraction errors. Additionally, standard ECR data regarding right ventricular function were limited, restricting further comparison with strain-based findings, particularly given prior reports of early right ventricular dysfunction following DCD transplantation. Although multivariable adjustment was performed, residual confounding from unmeasured variables remains possible, as adjustment could only be performed for observed covariates. Furthermore, while longitudinal mixed-effects modeling was used to account for repeated measurements within patients, more complex hierarchical or multilevel clustering structures were not evaluated due to sample size limitations. Inter-reader variability in echocardiographic assessment may have influenced measures of ventricular and valvular function. Although standard echocardiographic parameters such as LVEF and strain measurements are widely used, they remain operator-dependent and can vary based on image acquisition, reader experience, and post-processing techniques. In particular, strain analysis requires precise endocardial border tracing and vendor-specific software, potentially introducing additional variability across readers. Furthermore, the lack of blinding to donor type during echocardiographic interpretation introduces potential observer bias. Knowledge of donor status could consciously or subconsciously influence qualitative assessments of ventricular function or valvular abnormalities, as well as selection of images or contours used for strain analysis. Together, these factors may have affected the internal validity of the findings and should be considered when interpreting observed differences, particularly subtle changes identified on strain analysis.

Conclusion

This longitudinal analysis of DCD and DBD heart transplant recipients demonstrated comparable LVEF and valvular function during the first post-transplant year using conventional echocardiography. However, strain analysis identified persistently less favorable myocardial deformation profiles in DCD recipients, particularly involving LV GLS and early RV strain parameters, despite preserved conventional systolic function. Longitudinal and exploratory multivariable analyses further suggest that DCD grafts may exhibit distinct myocardial recovery patterns partially associated with ischemic exposure and not fully explained by rejection, PGD, or invasive hemodynamic differences. These findings support strain imaging as a sensitive adjunctive tool for characterizing subclinical graft dysfunction after DCD heart transplantation and warrant further investigation in larger studies with longer follow-up and clinical outcome correlation.
