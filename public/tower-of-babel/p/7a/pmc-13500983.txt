Patient-Reported Outcomes of Concomitant Deltoid and/or Syndesmotic Reconstruction With Broström for Global Chronic Ankle Instability: The Role of Deltoid Stability
Authors: Steven M Hadley Jr, Eric H Durudogan, Rachel Bergman, Sarah J Westvold, Carolyn J Hu, Tanya Kukreja, Muhammad Y Mutawakkil, Milap Patel, Cesar de Cesar Netto, Anish R Kadakia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Medial and syndesmotic instability are risk factors for failure to return to pre-injury activity following Broström. This investigation aims to understand the extent to which superficial deltoid reconstruction (DR) and/or syndesmotic reconstruction (SR) affect global ankle stability in patients with global chronic ankle instability (GCAI) as measured by the validated Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT) and Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS).Methods:All 574 patients who underwent lateral ankle ligament reconstruction at a single institution (2013-2024) were retrospectively reviewed. Revisions, allografts, nonanatomic reconstructions, flexor digitorum longus transfers, progressive collapsing foot deformity, hindfoot arthrodesis, ankle arthroplasties, fractures, those without minimum 1-year follow-up, and those who did not complete preoperative CAIT were excluded. Forty-three of 71 patients with concurrent deltoid and/or syndesmotic injury completed postoperative PROMIS/CAIT. GCAI was determined via history, examination, imaging, and intraoperative findings. Primary outcome was postoperative change in CAIT.Results:For patients who underwent concomitant DR (13/43), the mean CAIT score improved by 16.0 ± 8.9 points (P < .001). The mean PROMIS physical function (PF) was 53.8 ± 6.8, the mean PROMIS pain interference (PI) was 46.7 ± 8.5, and the mean postoperative CAIT score was 22.0 ± 7.8. For those who had SR (16/43), the mean CAIT improved by 8.0 ± 12.6 (P = .02). The mean PROMIS PF, PI, and postoperative CAIT scores among those who underwent associated SR were 49.3 ± 8.2, 51.3 ± 8.9, and 16.0 ± 9.1, respectively. For patients who underwent both SR and DR (14/43), average CAIT improved by 17.7 ± 7.0 (P < .001). Mean PF, PI, and postoperative CAIT were 51.9 ± 6.3, 48.2 ± 6.7, and 22.3 ± 7.0, respectively. Change in CAIT was significantly higher in DR and DR+SR compared to SR alone (P = .05).Conclusion:Concomitant DR/SR in patients undergoing Broström may be associated with significant improvements in ankle stability from preoperative baseline. Those with DR and DR+SR had the largest improvements in CAIT, which may suggest the role of deltoid stability in GCAI. Therefore, addressing global stability should be considered in some GCAI patients.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective comparative study.Keywords: ankle instability, chronic ankle instability, deltoid repair, syndesmotic reconstruction, Broström, global ankle stability, global instability, multidirectional instability, multidirectional chronic ankle instability, global chronic ankle instability

Introduction

Prior literature indicates that concurrent untreated medial and syndesmotic instability are risk factors for higher rates of postoperative dissatisfaction and failure to return to pre-injury activity following Broström for chronic ankle instability.1,2 Because of the relatively worse function of these patients, the concept of multidirectional or global ankle instability was developed to explain the combined ligamentous injuries in addition to the lateral ligaments that contribute to ankle instability.3-5 Global chronic ankle instability (GCAI) refers to instability occurring in multiple planes (coronal, sagittal, and rotational) and reflects injuries to multiple ligament complexes—lateral, medial, and syndesmotic. 3

Despite evidence supporting the concept of multidirectional chronic ankle instability, limited studies have evaluated patient-reported outcomes following Broström with concurrent deltoid repair/reconstruction and/or syndesmotic reconstruction (SR).5-13 Regarding concurrent DR, Mansur et al 8 and Buchhorn et al 9 reported improved American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS) and the visual analog scale (VAS) with concomitant DR at time of index Broström. Vega et al 10 studied 8 patients who had improved AOFAS scores following deltoid plication with concomitant lateral ligament repair. Although Rigby et al 11 suggested that patients with associated DR had improved Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) and AOFAS scores, this study is limited by average follow-up of less than 1 year. Yasuda et al 12 further demonstrated that combined medial and lateral reconstruction is an effective surgical management strategy that enables athletes to return to sport following lateral and medial ligamentous injury. For SR, Hua et al 13 demonstrated significant improvements in AOFAS scores when performed concomitantly with Broström; however, this study only evaluated 6 patients with syndesmotic injuries and instead largely focused on patients with osteochondral lesions.

It remains unclear to what extent Broström with concurrent DR/SR improves ankle stability. No studies have used the validated Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT) in patients who have undergone DR/SR in patients undergoing Broström for GCAI. CAIT is specifically validated in ankle instability and further understanding of how DR/SR bolsters stability, as measured by CAIT, would provide valuable information to optimize outcomes for patients suffering from GCAI.14-16 This investigation therefore aims to determine whether concomitant deltoid reconstruction with superficial deltoid imbrication and/or SR bolsters global ankle stability in patients suffering from GCAI as measured by CAIT.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This was an institutional review board–approved retrospective study of all 574 patients who underwent lateral ankle ligament reconstruction at a single institution (2013-2024). Two investigators independently evaluated all records and excluded revisions, allografts, nonanatomic reconstructions, flexor digitorum longus transfers, progressive collapsing foot deformities, concomitant hindfoot arthrodesis, associated total ankle arthroplasties, concomitant fractures, those without minimum 1-year follow-up, and those who did not complete preoperative CAIT. 93 total patients underwent surgery for global ankle instability. 72 completed preoperative CAIT scores. Three of 93 (and 1/72) did not have 1-year follow-up. Therefore, 71 patients with GCAI who had completed preoperative CAIT scores were identified for the study. These 71 were contacted to complete postoperative surveys containing CAIT and PROMIS. CAIT was collected preoperatively as part of routine practice at the authors’ institution. PROMIS and CAIT were subsequently collected at the same time postoperatively as part of the aforementioned postoperative survey. Forty-three patients, out of 71, completed surveys and were included for final analysis.

We identified GCAI through a combination of history, examination, imaging, and intraoperative findings. GCAI was defined as having associated deltoid and/or syndesmotic injury. Deltoid injury was identified preoperatively via characteristic MRI changes including edema at the medial malleolus and signal changes at the deltoid origin.17,18 Syndesmotic injury was determined via direct operative visual examination or preoperative MRI with evidence of damage to the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament and posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament. 19 Confirmation of deltoid and syndesmotic instability was achieved with direct visual examination intraoperatively after provocative testing, as direct visual examination is known to be most accurate to identify these ligamentous injuries.20-22 If either preoperatively or intraoperatively patients had evidence of syndesmotic and/or deltoid injury, associated DR/SR was performed; notably, in addition to direct visual examination, the ability to put a 3-mm probe in the distal tibiofibular space during manual external rotation was diagnostic for syndesmotic instability and a 4-mm probe in the medial gutter for deltoid insufficiency.23,24

All operations were performed by fellowship-trained foot and ankle orthopaedic surgeons (n = 4). All patients who underwent surgery failed nonoperative management including ankle bracing and formal physical therapy for a minimum of 6 months. 25 After failing conservative treatment, such imaging findings including subtle signal changes in the setting of patients’ symptomatic instability were consistent with chronic injury. All Broström procedures were performed with suture anchor fixation augmented with the InternalBrace (Arthrex) with DR/SR being performed after Broström. Regarding DR, the anterior one-half of the superficial deltoid was imbricated using suture anchors reinforced with 0-vicryl. 18 Regarding SR, if preoperative or intraoperative coronal plane syndesmotic instability was observed, a TightRope (Arthrex) was used to reconstruct the syndesmosis. 22 If there was additional rotational and/or sagittal syndesmotic instability, an InternalBrace (Arthrex) was placed to reconstruct the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament. 22

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was interval change in the CAIT score relative to the preoperative baseline. Secondary outcomes evaluated raw score postoperative CAIT, PROMIS physical function (PF), and PROMIS pain interference (PI) using age-matched means. Additionally, outcomes in this cohort were further compared to previously published results of 84 patients who underwent isolated Broström at our institution (same eligibility criteria, all procedures similarly done between 2013-2024) who completed the same patient-reported outcome surveys (of 127 patients who completed all surveys, 84/127 had isolated Broström without evidence of GCAI on examination or imaging or intraoperatively). 16 These comparisons were evaluated to determine whether additional incisions to address global instability resulted in higher incidence of complications at 1 year and whether patients with GCAI were able to achieve similar outcomes to patients with isolated lateral ankle instability. Complications were verified via chart review, and complications evaluated in the study were defined as those occurring within the first postoperative year. All patients in the study had at least 1-year follow-up, so all met this time point. The group of 84 patients who underwent isolated Broström was not used as a control, as patients in this cohort lacked apparent evidence of GCAI, and thus would not serve as an appropriate control group.

Higher scores on CAIT indicate greater ankle stability with a maximum score of 30.14,15 The CAIT minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is a change of ≥3 points.14,15 Traditionally, a CAIT of 28 to 30 indicates a perfectly functionally stable ankle, whereas a score of <26 suggests chronic ankle instability, although the literature supports evaluating the entire clinical context, as well as patients’ subjective stability compared to baseline scores.15,16,26 PROMIS was developed for a US population mean score of 50, with higher PF scores indicating better physical function, whereas conversely higher PI scores designate more pain.27-32

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R, version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The normality of continuous data was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Within-group comparisons of preoperative vs postoperative CAIT scores were performed using paired t tests. Between-group differences were assessed using Kruskal-Wallis test. Statistical significance was set at P <.05. This study was at 80% power to detect large effect sizes (Cohen f = 0.50) but underpowered to detect small effects. Analysis was exploratory and thus hypothesis-generating, rather than confirmatory.

Results

A total of 574 patients underwent lateral ankle ligament reconstruction at a single academic institution between 2013 and 2024. After applying eligibility criteria (please see Methods for full criteria), 71 patients with evidence of concurrent GCAI were eligible for study inclusion. Forty-three of 71 patients responded to the survey and were included for final analysis. There were no significant differences with the numbers available between those who responded to the survey and those who did not in terms of baseline characteristics (Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

Patients undergoing concomitant deltoid reconstruction with superficial deltoid imbrication and/or SR with Broström lateral ligament repair for GCAI demonstrated statistically and clinically significant improvements in ankle stability, as measured by CAIT, compared with preoperative baseline scores. These improvements were significantly greater in the DR and DR+SR groups compared with the SR group. Notably, both the DR and the DR+SR groups demonstrated significantly larger changes in CAIT scores from the preoperative baseline compared with the SR group alone, despite all 3 groups having similar degrees of preoperative instability. This difference exceeded the CAIT MCID, indicating that concomitant DR may be associated with both statistically and clinically meaningful improvements in ankle stability.

As the first study to evaluate the validated CAIT in patients undergoing DR and/or SR in conjunction with Broström, our findings highlight the importance of routinely assessing deltoid and syndesmotic integrity in patients presenting with lateral ankle instability. Concomitant DR and/or SR may provide substantial benefit for patients with multidirectional chronic ankle instability. Therefore, medial-sided (deltoid) instability appears particularly relevant to global ankle stability. 33 In patients undergoing Broström with SR alone, subtle deltoid injury that is not readily detected on MRI may contribute to persistent GCAI.4,12 These patients may experience a ceiling effect in CAIT scores when subtle deltoid insufficiency is left unaddressed. Greater attention to the assessment and management of multidirectional chronic ankle instability, including routine evaluation of deltoid integrity, may therefore offer an opportunity to further optimize clinical outcomes in this population.

The pathophysiology of deltoid injury and syndesmotic injury are interconnected.3,4 Disruption of one increases strain on the other, thereby predisposing to further injury and persistent instability, but often they are injured in combination at the index rotational injury frequently secondary to an external rotation mechanism.3,4 The significant relative improvement in CAIT scores in the DR+SR group relative to the isolated SR group therefore suggests that patients with subtle syndesmotic injury may benefit from DR, likely due to DR’s contribution to syndesmotic—and thus global—ankle stability.3,4

These findings further suggest that conventional MRI may be insufficient for accurately diagnosing subtle medial-sided injuries. Alternative diagnostic approaches, such as ankle arthroscopy for direct visualization of deltoid ligament integrity and dynamic instability, or the development of more sensitive imaging criteria, should be considered.5,12 Prior literature has reported the concept of functional instability with negative radiographic findings.34-36 Functional instability develops as recurrent ankle sprains produce neuromuscular and proprioceptive deficits that lead to instability not readily detectable on examination or imaging.34-36 However, functional and mechanical instability are related and contribute to the development of the multidirectional chronic ankle instability proposed by de Cesar Netto et al. 3 Functional instability likely represents a continuum in which neuromuscular deficits and repetitive microtrauma contribute to progressive laxity of the lateral, medial, and syndesmotic ligament complexes. Consequently, many patients with persistent pain and instability after rotational ankle injuries may display occult subtle structural and dynamic insufficiency of the deltoid and syndesmotic ligaments. These subtle injuries may often manifest clinically as global ankle instability despite the absence of clear radiographic or MRI findings. In particular, medial-sided instability may especially be overlooked, and medial-sided ligamentous injury may be an important factor in the development of functional instability with negative radiographic findings. 4

In essence, ankle stability is often a subjective experience for patients and can persist functionally even in the absence of objective radiographic findings. This highlights the value of patient-reported tools such as CAIT in detecting subtle functional instability, quantifying its severity, and evaluating treatment success. Subtle global or multidirectional ankle instability may be frequently missed in current practice due to the lack of major radiographic evidence and the common failure to assess stability in all 3 ligamentous planes. 5 Moreover, injury to one ligament complex can have deleterious effects on the others, thereby predisposing the ankle to progressive instability. 3 For this reason, a more complete and detailed evaluation of all 3 ligamentous complexes and multidirectional stability, both with advanced imaging and arthroscopically during surgical treatment, may be considered an effective approach to optimize outcomes in patients suffering from GCAI. 37

When comparing patients who underwent concomitant DR/SR with Broström to those who underwent isolated Broström, the improvement in CAIT from preoperative baseline to postoperative follow-up was greatest in the DR+SR group and closely followed by the DR group. These improvements were not only statistically significant but also exceeded MCID criteria, indicating clinically meaningful enhancements in ankle stability. Given the similar baseline instability across all groups, the superior CAIT improvements observed with concomitant DR+SR suggest that addressing global ankle stability represents an effective surgical strategy for patients with GCAI. These results do not suggest that all patients suffering from ankle instability require DR and/or SR, as those with GCAI and those with isolated lateral ankle instability are 2 different populations. Rather, the results underscore that consideration should be given to the evaluation of all ligament complexes, and that if the extra pathology of GCAI is present, satisfactory PROs may still be achieved when such additional pathology of deltoid and syndesmotic injuries is addressed.

Our results demonstrate that patients with GCAI can achieve excellent postoperative outcomes and ankle stability following concomitant procedures, often comparable to or better than those undergoing isolated Broström for lateral ligamentous injury alone. The significantly greater improvements in CAIT scores from baseline observed in the DR and DR+SR groups compared with the isolated Broström and SR groups further suggest that DR plays a particularly critical role in restoring global ankle stability. Notably, in our cohort, patients with subtle syndesmotic injuries achieved improved outcomes when the deltoid was also addressed, which may underscore the deltoid’s important contribution to both syndesmotic stability and overall global ankle stability. 4 These patients may benefit from comprehensive evaluation of all ligament complexes and from all aspects of fixation—not just limited to syndesmotic fixation. Ultimately, further research should evaluate the relationship between syndesmotic and deltoid injury in GCAI.

Moreover, patients who underwent concomitant DR and/or SR achieved approximately population mean PROMIS scores, indicating that restoration of global stability results in satisfactory functional outcomes. Because PROMIS exhibits extensive reliability among patients with foot and ankle pathologies—especially compared to other commonly used patient-reported outcome metrics—the findings of this study indicate that patients may achieve satisfactory functional outcomes relative to the population mean.27-32 Ankle stability therefore may not be merely isolated to the strength and function of the lateral ankle ligaments. Consequently, achieving global stability—lateral, medial, and syndesmotic—should be a consideration in patients suffering from GCAI.

Finally, both the cohorts of 43 patients with concomitant DR/SR and the 84 with isolated Broström had low complication rates at 1 year, including notably not significantly different, with the numbers available, deep wound infection incidences (1.2% isolated vs 2.3% DR/SR), deep vein thrombosis incidences (1.2% isolated vs 0% DR/SR), and reoperations (3.6% isolated vs 4.6% DR/SR). These values are consistent with reported complication rates for isolated Broström repairs.38-40 These low complication rates at 1 year suggest that concomitant DR/SR in patients undergoing Broström repair is safe and may not lead to markedly increased complication rates at 1 year compared to Broström alone.

This study has some limitations. First, the study lacked a true control group of patients with GCAI who underwent isolated Broström repair, making it difficult to directly compare the relative benefit of concomitant DR/SR. At our institution, DR/SR is routinely offered to patients with GCAI who show clinical or radiographic evidence of medial and/or syndesmotic instability. Although comparison with the isolated Broström group suggests that patients with GCAI can achieve similar or superior functional outcomes to those with isolated lateral instability, this group is not an ideal control, as these patients presumably lacked evidence of multidirectional instability. Future studies should adopt a prospective, randomized design. Second, although preoperative CAIT scores were available for comparison with postoperative values, this study lacked preoperative PROMIS scores to quantify baseline function and pain. Nevertheless, PROMIS scores were collected at the time of survey completion and provided a reliable postoperative evaluation of patient function and pain. Potential variability in how patients interpreted survey questions may have introduced measurement bias. Additionally, patients completed the postoperative surveys at varying postoperative time intervals. However, there were no significant differences with the numbers available in outcomes based on duration of follow-up beyond 1 year.16,41-43 Third, the relatively small sample size and potential nonresponse bias (43/71 patients, 60% survey response rate) may have limited the generalizability of the findings. However, the sample size remains larger than those in prior studies evaluating concomitant DR/SR with Broström repair, the response rate compares favorably with other studies assessing Broström procedures, and baseline characteristics between responders and non-responders were similar as evidenced by prior publication of the same cohort of patients.6-8,10,16,38,41-47 Fourth, all patients in the DR and DR+SR groups underwent augmented repair, which may have been associated with the improved outcomes observed in these groups.16,43,48 Fifth, prior research indicates that fear of reinjury can significantly limit patient function after Broström repair. 41 Future studies should therefore include validated assessments of fear of reinjury in this population. Prior literature suggests that chronic ankle instability patients with concomitant cavovarus deformity suffer worse outcomes.42,43 Although we did not specifically assess influence of deformity on outcomes in this study, the proportion of patients with cavovarus deformity was similar across groups, which likely mitigates potential bias from their inclusion. Although patients improved significantly from their preoperative state, many patients did not exceed the CAIT threshold for a functionally stable ankle. The purpose of these surgeries is not to make patients “normal” or “perfect,” but rather to improve the patients from their preoperative state, as many of these patients had life-limiting ankle instability.

Despite its limitations, this study provides valuable insight into the role of DR/SR in improving outcomes following lateral ankle reconstruction. It is the first and largest study to evaluate CAIT in patients with GCAI undergoing concomitant DR/SR and the only study to assess PROMIS in this population with a minimum 1 year follow-up. Access to preoperative CAIT scores allowed characterization of the improvement in ankle stability from baseline to the postoperative period. Furthermore, both CAIT and PROMIS are validated specifically in this patient population, strengthening the validity of the findings. These results therefore also may suggest a potential role for CAIT and PROMIS in clinical evaluation of patients suffering from GCAI.

Conclusions

In conclusion, concomitant deltoid reconstruction with superficial deltoid imbrication and/or SR performed with Broström lateral ligament repair in patients with GCAI was associated with statistically and clinically significant improvements in ankle stability, as measured by the CAIT, compared with preoperative baseline scores. In patients who underwent DR or DR+SR, CAIT improvement was significantly greater than in those who underwent SR alone. These findings suggest that addressing medial and syndesmotic instability may bolster global ankle stability in patients with GCAI.

Medial-sided (deltoid) instability appears particularly relevant to overall global ankle stability. In patients undergoing Broström repair with SR alone, subtle deltoid injury that is not readily detected by current imaging may contribute to persistent GCAI. Therefore, thorough evaluation of all 3 ligament complexes (lateral, medial, and syndesmotic) with the goal of achieving comprehensive global stability should be considered in the surgical management of patients with GCAI.
