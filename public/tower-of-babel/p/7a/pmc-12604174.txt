Prevalence and factors associated with physical inactivity among older persons in Malaysia: Findings from the National Health and Morbidity Survey 2023
Authors: Norizzati Amsah, Nurfatehar Ramly, Lim Kuang Kuay, Noor Ul-Aziha Muhammad, Muhammad Fadhli Mohd Yusoff, Hamizatul Akmal Abd Hamid, Halizah Mat Rifin, Nor’ain Abd Wahab, Nurul Huda Ibrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical activity is crucial for maintaining health and preventing chronic diseases, particularly among older persons. However, various sociodemographic and health-related factors may influence physical activity levels in this population. This study aims to identify the factors associated with physical inactivity among older persons aged 60 years and above in Malaysia.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2023, a nationwide population-based survey. Older persons aged 60 years and above were included. Variables assessed included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, functional limitations, and health conditions. Physical activity status was categorized as active or inactive. Complex sample logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with physical inactivity, with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsA total of 2,979 older persons were included in the analysis. Several significant factors associated with physical inactivity were identified. The prevalence of physical inactivity among older persons aged 60 years and above was 43.2% (95% CI: 40.1–46.4). Physical inactivity was more prevalent among females, urban residents, those with no formal education, low household income (B40), individuals with chronic conditions (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia), and those reporting disabilities or functional difficulties. Complex sample logistic regression analysis that diabetes mellitus (AOR: 1.39; 95% CI: 1.14–1.70), overall disability (AOR: 1.96; 95% CI: 1.46–2.62), walking difficulty (AOR: 1.78; 95% CI: 1.09–2.90), difficulty in self-care (AOR: 7.05; 95% CI: 2.96–16.82), and sedentary behavior more than 4 h per day (AOR: 1.80; 95% CI: 1.27–2.11) were significantly associated with increased odds of physical inactivity. Sociodemographic factors such as sex, education level, and residential area were not significantly associated with physical inactivity.ConclusionThis study highlights a substantial prevalence of physical inactivity among older persons in Malaysia. Key factors significantly associated with physical inactivity include the presence of chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, functional limitations, self-care difficulties, overall disability, and sedentary behavior of more than 4 h. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions and policies that address health conditions and functional impairments to promote active ageing. Strengthening support systems and rehabilitation programs may help improve physical activity levels and overall well-being among older persons in Malaysia.

Introduction

The world’s population is growing in both established and developing countries, especially due to the increasing number of older persons [1]. The world’s population of older persons aged 60 years and above was over 1 billion in 2019, representing 13.2% of the world’s 7.7 billion total population. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes healthy aging as the process of developing and maintaining functional abilities that enable well-being in older age [2]. Like many other Asian developing nations, Malaysia is also experiencing a sharp increase in its older population. Similarly, it is predicted that by 2030, 14% of Malaysia’s population will be 60 years and above, resulting in an aging country [3]. However, the burden of diseases among older persons also increases with the world’s aging population, which raises concerns for the global healthcare system, notably in developing countries [4].

Globally, physical inactivity is the fourth leading cause of mortality [5]. It has become a major public health concern worldwide and has been identified as a significant contributor to disability and poor health outcomes [6] About three million fatalities annually and 6–10% of the prevalence of major non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are linked to a lack of physical activity [7]. One of the growing burdens of disease among older persons is NCDs such as diabetes mellitus, heart disease, cancer, and chronic respiratory conditions [3] Moreover, it has been suggested by previous research that the most significant modifiable risk factors for NCDs include a lack of physical activity, unhealthy diet, tobacco use, and excessive alcohol intake [8]. In addition, about 10% of occurrences of breast, colon, and rectal cancer, 16% of cases of diabetes mellitus, and 22% of cases of ischemic heart disease have been identified to be associated with physical inactivity [9].

Globally, the prevalence of physical inactivity has increased, and about 31.1% of individuals worldwide are physically inactive, ranging from 17.0% in Southeast Asia to about 43.0% in the eastern Mediterranean and the Americas [10]. According to the previous studies, the prevalence of physical inactivity among older persons was between 5% and 29% in Europe [6], 50.7% in Brazil [11], and 63.1% in China [12]. However, according to the National and Health Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2023, it has been reported that 29.9% of the adults Malaysian population was physically inactive [1]. A report in 2015 revealed that the number of older persons aged 60 years and above in Malaysia who are not physically active, estimated to be 48.8%, which is approximately half of the older population in Malaysia [3].

Several studies have examined factors associated with physical inactivity among older persons [13, 14]. Globally, levels of physical inactivity increase with age, suggesting that fewer individuals over 60 years fulfil worldwide recommendations compared to younger adults [15]. Previous studies in Malaysia have shown that the prevalence of physical inactivity increases with age, with individuals over 75 years having the highest prevalence [1]. Compared to other countries, physical inactivity also increases significantly as age increases [16, 17]. Another study has reported that gender, particularly females, was significantly associated with higher levels of physical inactivity compared to males [13, 18]. Other sociodemographic factors, such as education levels, marital status, ethnicity, and locality, have also been reported by previous research to be significantly associated with physical inactivity [1, 13, 16].

One of the key objectives of the WHO Active Ageing Framework is to reduce the global burden of NCDs by encouraging older persons to engage in more physical activity and exercise. Therefore, understanding the prevalence and factors associated with physical inactivity among older persons is crucial for targeted public health interventions. Considering the increasing number of this understudied population, particularly older persons, which has the potential consequence of being the most physically inactive group in society, it is important to understand the knowledge gap by focusing on physical inactivity among older persons. Thus, this study aims to identify the factors associated with physical inactivity among older persons aged 60 years and above in Malaysia using data from the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2023. Based on the findings from this study, policymakers can develop strategies to improve physical activity levels among older persons in Malaysia.

Methodology

Study sample

This study was part of the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2023, which examined non-communicable diseases and their risk factors in Malaysia. It used a cross-sectional, population-based design with a two-stage stratified random sampling method to select a representative sample of adults aged 18 and older. The stratification included all states and federal territories as the primary stratum, with urban and rural areas as the secondary stratum. The Primary Sampling Unit (PSU) comprised 499 Enumeration Blocks (EBs) randomly selected based on the National Population and Housing Census 2022, proportionate to each state’s population. The Secondary Sampling Unit (SSU) involved randomly selecting 12 Living Quarters (LQs) from each EB. Detailed information regarding the data collection procedures is reported in the National Health and Ministry Survey (NHMS) 2023 report [19].

Sample size

Sample sizes were calculated using both single a proportion formulas for prevalence study. It was adjusted to the projection population on 2024, design effect and response rate. The sample size based on proportion formula was calculated using a prevalence of physical inactivity of 62.7% [20].

The total sample of 2,979 was deemed sufficient to achieve the desired power and significance levels, ensuring the robustness and reliability of the results.

Data collection

The data was collected through face-to-face interviews conducted between July and September 2023. Out of 13,616 participants, 10,852 engaged with the physical inactivity module, which included 2,979 older persons. All qualifying respondents provided their consent before participating in the study. This study protocol was approved by the Medical Review and Ethics Committee (MREC), Ministry of Health, Malaysia. The inclusion criteria are (1) Older persons aged 60 years or above, and (2) staying in a non-institutionalised LQ for at least 2 weeks before data collection. It is essential only to collect data from eligible households in this population-based study. The exclusion criteria are (1) institutionalised people, such as those staying in hotels and hospitals. All questionnaires were in the Malay and English languages, including sociodemographic information.

Instrument

This study used the validated Malay version of the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) to calculate total physical activity across three domains: work-related, travel-related, and recreational [21]. The results were reported in Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET) minutes per week. A person is considered physically active if they engage in at least 30 min of moderate-intensity activity or walking per day for at least five days in a typical week. Alternatively, they can be active by doing 25 min of vigorous-intensity activity per day on at least three days a week. Additionally, a combination of walking and moderate or vigorous-intensity activities for five days can also qualify, provided the total physical activity level reaches a minimum of 600 MET minutes per week [22]. The GPAQ showed acceptable evidence of short- and long-term test-retest reliability by activity category and modest validity evidence [23].

Variable definitions

The dependent variable in this study was defined as physical activity status, which was categorized into two groups: physically inactive and physically active. The analysis included all categorical independent variables, which comprised sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, health-related conditions, and functional limitations.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Residential location was classified into urban and rural settings; sex was male and female; ethnicity included Malay, Chinese, Indian, or other ethnicities; marital status was grouped as either married or not married (which included individuals who were single, widowed, or divorced). Educational attainment was categorized into no formal, primary, secondary, or tertiary education. Income was stratified into B40, M40 and T20 groups [24].

Lifestyle factors

Lifestyle variables included fruit and vegetable intake, smoking and alcohol consumption, and time spent in sedentary activities. Dietary intake was assessed by WHO STEPS criteria (2015), categorizing fruit and vegetable consumption as either adequate (≥ five servings/day) or inadequate (< 5 servings/day) [25]. Smoking and alcohol use were each classified as either “never” or “former/current” users. Daily sedentary time was grouped into quintiles: less than 2 h, 2 to 4 h, and more than 4 h.

Health-related conditions

Health-related conditions included diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertension. Diabetes was identified either through capillary fasting blood glucose levels ≥ 6.1 mmol/L or random blood glucose levels > 11.1 mmol/L [26] or by self-reporting a prior medical diagnosis. Hypercholesterolemia was determined by a fasting capillary total cholesterol level ≥ 5.2 mmol/L [27], or through self-reported diagnosis. Both glucose and cholesterol levels were measured using the validated CardioCheck® PA Analyzer. Blood pressure was measured twice using a calibrated OMRON HEM-907 digital device with a 10-minute interval between readings. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic pressure ≥ 90 mmHg [28] or as previously diagnosed hypertension reported by the respondent.

Sedentary behaviour

Sedentary behaviour (SB) and physical activity were assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) SB was evaluated based on the question: “On a typical day, how much time do you usually spend sitting or reclining, including time spent at work, at home, during leisure, and while traveling, but not including time spent sleeping?” Participants were asked to report the total time spent sitting or reclining during daily activities, such as working at a desk, socializing, commuting by car, bus, or train, reading, playing games, or watching television [29]. Time spent sleeping was excluded from this estimate.

Functional limitations

Functional limitations were assessed using the short version of the Washington Group Questionnaire (WGQ), which captures difficulties in basic activities of daily living across six functional domains [30]. There were six questions asking whether people have difficulty performing basic activities in six functional domains: seeing (even while wearing glasses), hearing (even while using a hearing aid), walking, cognitive status, self-care and communication. Each question has four possible responses: (1) No, no difficulty, (2) Yes, some difficulty, (3) Yes, a lot of difficulty, (4) Cannot do it at all. In this study, for each type of functional limitation, respondents who answered they either have “some difficulty”, “a lot of difficulty’, or “cannot do it at all” were categorized as “Yes”, while those who answered they have no difficulty were categorized as “No”. Overall difficulty was defined as having ‘some difficulty’ or ‘a lot of difficulty’ or ‘cannot do at all’ in any domain. Overall disability was defined as having ‘some difficulty’ in two domains or ‘a lot of difficulty or cannot do at all’ in one domain. The WGQ does not provide a score in the sense of a numerical sum that directly indicates a level of disability. Instead, it uses a threshold approach to identify individuals with functional limitations.

The WGQ was developed through extensive expert consultation and cognitive testing to ensure it covers key functional domains and is culturally neutral. Its focus on activity limitations aligns with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework [31]. The WGQ has demonstrated high test-retest reliability, meaning individuals consistently report their functional difficulties over time [32].

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted using a complex survey design, including weighting and stratification, utilizing SPSS version 27.0. To account for the unequal probability of selection and non-response, as well as to ensure the representativeness of our sample to the target population, sampling weights were applied. These sampling weights were calculated as the inverse of the probability of selection for each respondent, further adjusted for non-response within specific strata. Specifically, post-stratification weights were developed based on the known population distributions of list key demographic variables such as, age group, gender, and geographical region, which were obtained from the Department of Statistics Malaysia. This ensured that our sample statistics accurately reflected the demographic composition in Malaysia. Stratification was incorporated into the analysis to account for the clustered sampling design used in data collection. Our survey design involved implicit stratification by list stratification variables, by state. This means that separate sampling frames were drawn from these distinct strata to ensure adequate representation and to reduce sampling variability.

All statistical analyses were performed using complex sample analysis. The functions correctly incorporate the specified weights and stratification into the calculation of descriptive statistics (weighted means, proportions) and inferential statistics (standard errors, confidence intervals, p-values). This approach ensures that variance estimates are appropriately adjusted for the survey design, yielding unbiased population estimates and valid statistical inferences. Complex sample logistic regression was employed to identify significant predictors of physical inactivity. A final model was developed, incorporating all factors significantly associated with physical inactivity, with a p-value < 0.05. The adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and their 95% confidence intervals were reported to assess the associations between the predictors and physical inactivity. All the interactions and multicollinearity were checked.

Results

A total of 2,979 older persons aged 60 years and above were included in the analysis. The mean age of the respondents was 68.1 years (95% CI (Confidence interval): 67.8 –68.5). The overall prevalence of physical inactivity was 43.2% (95% CI: 40.1 - 46.4). The number of physically inactive was 1,263, and the number of physically active participants was 1,716 older persons. Discussion

Several epidemiological studies and clinical trials have demonstrated that any physical activity can reduce the incidence of chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and stroke. Because physical inactivity increases the risk of frailty and impairment among older persons, it is important to encourage them to perform and maintain regular exercise throughout their aging process [33]. Thus, this study provides information on the prevalence and factors associated with physical inactivity among older persons aged 60 years and above in Malaysia.

The overall prevalence of physical inactivity among older persons in Malaysia was 43.2%, showing a decrease compared to 48.8% in 2015 [3]. This encouraging decline suggests a favorable trend in physical activity levels among older Malaysians over recent years. Several factors may contribute to this observed reduction in physical inactivity in Malaysia since 2015. Over the past decade, Malaysia has seen an increased national focus on healthy ageing and the prevention of non-communicable diseases. Government initiatives and campaigns by the Ministry of Health, such as the National Healthy Community Program (KOSPEN) and various initiatives, have likely played a role in raising public awareness and encouraging greater participation in physical activities among older persons [34].

In comparison, the prevalence of physical inactivity among older persons was 63.1% in China and 33.7% in Singapore [12, 20]. This community-based study was conducted in a district in China and involved 3,920 adults aged 40 years and older. When comparing our findings with other regional contexts, it was shown that physical activity patterns among older persons vary significantly. For instance, a community-based study in China reported a physical inactivity prevalence of 63.1% among adults aged 40 years and older [12]. However, it is crucial to consider the broader age range included in this study, which involves those 40 years old and above, and the data focused on a district setting.

In contrast, a comprehensive, nationally representative cross-sectional study by Aithal, Visaria, and Malhotra (2022) in Singapore reported a significantly higher prevalence of insufficient physical activity at 62.7% among community-dwelling older adults aged more than 60 years [30]. This figure for Singaporean older adults is markedly higher than that of our Malaysian population. A notable strength in comparing these two studies lies in their shared use of the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) for measuring physical activity and sedentary behavior. This common methodological tool enhances the comparability of findings, suggesting that the observed difference in inactivity prevalence (62.2% in Singapore vs. 43.2% in Malaysia) may reflect genuine disparities in activity levels, rather than solely measurement variations.

Aithal et al. (2022) identified several sociodemographic and health correlates of insufficient physical activity in Singapore, including older age, female sex, Malay ethnicity, lower education, self-reported poor health, chronic diseases, and unemployment. While our study also explored similar correlates of physical inactivity in Malaysia, the distinct socio-cultural environments, urban infrastructure, public health initiatives, and prevailing health burdens in each country could contribute to these observed differences in overall population-level physical activity.

Based on the findings, we observed that older persons with diabetes mellitus were significantly associated with higher physical inactivity levels than older persons without diabetes mellitus. This finding is consistent with a recent study among older persons in Malaysia, which found that older persons with diabetes mellitus were associated with a significantly greater likelihood of physical inactivity compared to those without diabetes mellitus [3]. A previous study found that older persons with diabetes mellitus were less likely to engage in physical activity at the recommended levels [35]. Additionally, compared to those without diabetes mellitus, older persons with diabetes were more likely to perform poorly in walking speed and balance performance, which may raise their risk of falling and increase the likelihood of hospitalization for fall-related injuries [36]. The risk of hypoglycaemia during exercise and symptoms like fatigue and peripheral neuropathy can be significant barriers to physical activities among older persons with diabetes mellitus [37]. This heightened risk could be a factor contributing to reduced physical activity among older persons with diabetes mellitus [38].

In addition, the current study’s findings showed that sedentary more than four hours was significantly associated with physical inactivity among older persons in Malaysia. The findings are supported by studies done in Malaysia and Ireland among older persons, which reported that sedentary time was strongly associated with physical inactivity [3, 39]. This could be because long periods of sitting with low levels of physical activity were more common among the older persons, and this choice of lifestyle may increase their risk of related non-communicable diseases [40]. Therefore, older persons with these behaviors (highly sedentary and inactive) should be consulted by healthcare professionals to start a new exercise regimen to ensure activities are safe and appropriate based on their health status and relevant to their condition. Moreover, government and public health agencies should implement strategies to promote physical activity among older persons that emphasize approaches in community, healthcare, and home settings, thereby improving their health and well-being [41]. However, a study in Malaysia has shown that other factors, such as unemployment, are also significantly associated with higher levels of sedentary behaviour [29]. The condition may be explained by the lack of structured daily activities and diminished opportunities for social interaction that are often afforded through employment.

Out of the six functional limitations assessed, only walking and self-care difficulties were significantly associated with physical inactivity. Older persons with walking difficulties were more likely to be physically inactive. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that mobility impairments are significantly linked to physical inactivity among older persons [3]. This could be because older persons with difficulties walking and moving around typically struggle to perform any physical activity. Thus, they can improve their walking difficulties by engaging in low-impact activities like chair-based exercise, using assistive technology like walkers, and attending physiotherapy sessions to strengthen their muscles [42]. Other than that, task-oriented motor learning exercise is an essential component of training to improve motor skills, and may be a beneficial approach to improving walking in older persons [43].

Other functional limitations, such as self-care difficulties, showed that older persons with self-care difficulties are significantly associated with physical inactivity. A previous study conducted in Japan revealed that older persons who were physically active had substantially higher self-care agency scores compared to their inactive counterparts [44]. This might result in reduced functional capacity, loss of self-confidence when carrying out activities, and deterioration in the desire to perform and maintain exercise [45]. This creates a vicious cycle where a lack of self-care leads to reduced physical activity, contributing to health deterioration and making self-care even more challenging. Older persons should be supported with environmental modifications such as grab bars, adapted equipment, and caregiver assistance to break this cycle and promote better self-care. These interventions can help maintain independence in daily living tasks, as self-care practices have been shown to promote physical health among older adults [46].

Strengths and limitations

This study has notable strengths. First, this study provides valuable insight into the prevalence and factors associated with physical inactivity among older persons in Malaysia. Second, examining multiple demographics, health-related conditions, and functional limitations provides a comprehensive understanding of the issues. Lastly, the study’s results can guide targeted intervention and policy recommendations to encourage active aging and enhance mental well-being. This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, self-reported data on physical activity levels could lead to recall bias affecting the accuracy of the data results. A notable limitation of our study is the scope of variables collected. We did not assess conditions such as stroke. Similarly, key geriatric considerations like depression, frailty, history of falls, and nutritional status were not captured. For future research, it is strongly recommended that these critical areas be included to provide a more holistic and nuanced picture of participants’ health and functional capabilities.

Conclusion

The prevalence of physical inactivity among Malaysian older persons was 43.2%. This study highlights a substantial prevalence of physical inactivity among older persons in Malaysia. Key factors significantly associated with physical inactivity include the presence of chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, functional limitations, self-care difficulties, overall disability, and sedentary behavior of more than 4 h. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions and policies that address health conditions and functional impairments to promote active ageing. Strengthening support systems and rehabilitation programs may help improve physical activity levels and overall well-being among Malaysia’s older population.
