A case of budding speciation in the California Floristic Province and infraspecific divergence in Abronia villosa
Authors: Eli J. Allen, Eric F. LoPresti, Matthew G. Johnson, Oscar M. Vargas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremiseBudding speciation occurs when peripheral populations adapt to local ecological conditions and “bud” off from a widespread progenitor species. Traditionally regarded as rare because of its sympatric and parapatric nature, budding speciation is largely understudied. Moreover, recent evidence signals that this type of speciation is prevalent in plants, especially in biodiversity hotspots. In this study, we tested for budding speciation in the narrow endemic Abronia villosa var. aurita from the California Floristic Province (CFP).MethodsWe used high‐resolution phylogenetics, biogeography, and niche modeling to test the predictions of budding speciation for A. villosa var. aurita from its putative progenitor, the more widespread A. villosa var. villosa. Specifically, we tested for nested phylogenetic relationships of sister taxa, asymmetrical range size, and ecological divergence.ResultsOur results revealed that A. villosa var. aurita is a recently budded taxon, which phylogenetically nested within another variety of the same species. Abronia villosa var. aurita showed a clear signal of niche divergence into a montane environment and signals of genetic structure between two geographically divided populations, indicating further local adaptation and divergence potential.ConclusionsWe elucidated a case of budding speciation in the CFP, adding evidence to a growing number of studies that support budding speciation as a prevalent driver of diversification in plants. Furthermore, our analyses uncovered a nuanced history of within‐species divergence, illustrating the importance of bridging microevolutionary and macroevolutionary mechanisms.

Speciation and the maintenance of species boundaries between closely related taxa are contentious but important concepts for understanding diversification across the tree of life (e.g., Rieseberg and Brouillet, 1994; Gottlieb, 2004; Kay, 2006; Butlin et al., 2008; Hörandl and Stuessy, 2010; Bock et al., 2023). Allopatric speciation, which occurs due to geographic barriers that prevent gene flow between populations, has been considered the most common driver of species divergence in sexual organisms (e.g., Dobzhansky, 1970; Mayr, 1942, 1970; Coyne and Orr, 2004) because if gene exchange between populations is blocked for a sufficiently long time, species divergence can occur (e.g., Grant, 1981; Coyne and Orr, 2004). Sympatric speciation (which occurs when there are overlapping ranges without spatial barriers) and parapatric speciation (which occurs between adjacent ranges that lack spatial barriers) were both thought to be highly unlikely, because gene flow would prevent reproductive isolation so that speciation would be unlikely (e.g., Jordan, 1905, 1908; Mayr, 1942, 1959, 1963). However, increasing evidence for the existence of sympatric and parapatric speciation has led evolutionary biologists to rethink the relative importance of different speciation modes in diversification, particularly for plants (e.g., Crawford, 2010; Otero et al., 2022; Bock et al., 2023; Ivey et al., 2023). For example, comparative investigations of plants, fungi, and animals indicate that sympatric speciation has played a significant role in the genesis of biodiversity (Barluenga et al., 2006; Fitzpatrick et al., 2008, 2009; Hernández‐Hernández, et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2025).

Budding speciation, either in sympatry or parapatry, has been shown to occur in vertebrates, invertebrates, and plants (Barraclough and Vogler, 2000; Malay and Paulay, 2010; Claremont et al., 2012; Anacker and Strauss, 2014; Grossenbacher, et al., 2014). In budding speciation, a small population within or on the periphery of the range of a larger “progenitor” species adapts to a new ecological niche, locally producing a “derivative” species (Stebbins and Major, 1965; Rieseberg and Brouillet, 1994; Baldwin, 2005; Schluter, 2009; Crawford, 2010; Sianta and Kay, 2022). Studies on budding speciation in plants have focused on comparing sister‐taxon pairs (using this phrase broadly, where one species at least initially remains nested phylogenetically in another, see Olmstead, 1995) by searching for highly asymmetrical range sizes and divergence in ecological traits (Harrison, 2012; Anacker and Strauss, 2014; Grossenbacher, et al., 2014; Otero et al., 2022; Ivey et al., 2023). While early studies on budding speciation were macroevolutionary and often only a single individual was sampled per taxon (e.g., Fitzpatrick and Turelli, 2006; Anacker and Strauss, 2014; Grossenbacher, et al., 2014), recent studies at shallower taxonomic levels are providing more refined details on the ecological and genetic mechanisms underlying the “budding off” process (Otero et al., 2022; Sianta and Kay, 2022; Ivey et al., 2023; Juárez, et al., 2023). These studies not only confirmed the presence of budding speciation, but they also provided evolutionary snapshots of how budded sister pairs continue to evolve.

Budding speciation may be an important process leading to increased species richness in biodiversity hotspots. A strong signal of budding speciation across disparate plants has been found in the California Floristic Province (CFP), one of two biodiversity hotspots in the United States (Gottlieb, 2004; Baldwin, 2005; Grossenbacher, et al., 2014; Harrison and Noss, 2017; Ivey et al., 2023). A phylogenetically comparative meta‐analysis of 71 CFP sister pairs from 12 families found signatures of the “progenitor‐derivative” relationship in 80% of the sister pairs analyzed (Anacker and Strauss, 2014). This high signal for budding speciation could be attributed to the heterogeneity of climate, geology, topography, and soil in the CFP, which may provide ample microhabitats for local ecological specialization to drive the origin of neoendemic species from widespread progenitor species (Raven and Axelrod, 1978; Thorne et al., 2009; Kraft et al., 2010). The estimated high level of CFP plant endemism (an estimated 42%; Burge et al., 2016) matches the expected proportion of narrow endemics. If 80% of sister pairs diverged via budding speciation (Anacker and Strauss, 2014), then half of this percentage, 40%, should be derivative species, assuming equal rates of survival. Confirming the impact of budding in the development of species‐rich floras requires testing this speciation mode for multiple taxa at shallower evolutionary scales.

Here we chose the narrow endemic Abronia villosa var. aurita (Nyctaginaceae) (Abrams, 1905; Jepson, 1914) as a test case for budding speciation in the CFP. It displays signs of being a recently budded taxon derived from the closely related variety, the widespread A. villosa var. villosa. The two varieties show strong range asymmetry, with the rare var. aurita having a much smaller range that is on the periphery of the more common A. var. villosa (Figure 1A, B). The two varieties differ ecologically: var. aurita is montane (200–2000 m), but var. villosa is found primarily on the low desert floor (0–1000 m a.s.l.) (Curtis, 1977; Galloway, 2003). However, toward the coast, var. aurita is found in sandy washes and river valleys across increasingly lower westward elevations (farther west than var. villosa occurs in CA), with one population reaching the Pacific Ocean (Figure 1C). This geographic distribution suggests that var. aurita has evolved with respect to a wetter niche on the mountains and slopes facing the Pacific Ocean. Where their ranges abut, the two varieties are separated by stark elevational differences, with their only range overlap being found in a long mountain canyon (Coyote Canyon) connecting adjacent populations (Figure 1D). This range asymmetry (with var. villosa being widespread and var. aurita restricted to mountains and Pacific slopes) and the ecological divergence (differences in elevation and precipitation) between the two varieties are both suggestive indicators of budding speciation (Anacker and Strauss, 2014; Sianta and Kay, 2022). Because their ranges abut and their morphological differences are minimal (see Materials and Methods: Study system), we hypothesize that this infraspecific divergence represents an early stage of budding speciation. We use the broad term “budding speciation” here despite the early stage because given enough time, these two varieties may evolve into two fully distinct species. Note that in Nyctaginaceae, as in many North American plant families, the rank of variety is used entirely instead of subspecies (Hamilton and Reichard, 1992).

Although range asymmetry and ecological divergence between sister taxa are indicators of budding speciation, they do not provide conclusive evidence of it (Sianta and Kay, 2022). Progenitor‐derivative relationships are most definitively detected by using phylogenetic analysis (Crawford, 2010): with budding speciation supported by observing a nested phylogenetic relationship in which the derivative taxon is monophyletic but nested within a paraphyletic progenitor species (Rieseberg and Brouillet, 1994; Baldwin, 2005). The major goal of our study is to test for a signal of budding speciation within Abronia villosa. We hypothesize that A. villosa var. aurita was derived from A. villosa var. villosa. To test for a phylogenetic signal of budding speciation, we employed target‐capture sequencing using the Angiosperms353 probe set, which uses 353 low‐ to single‐copy protein‐coding genes that are informative at shallow and deep taxonomic levels across the angiosperms (Johnson et al., 2019; Baker et al., 2021). The Angiosperms353 loci are ultraconserved, but the noncoding flanking regions and introns that are also captured during sequencing of each locus can evolve very quickly, making them very useful for inferring infraspecific relationships (Baker et al., 2021; Slimp et al., 2021). In the context of budding speciation, we aimed to specifically test (1) whether the focal taxon (var. aurita) is nested phylogenetically in a progenitor taxon (var. villosa), with the latter therefore paraphyletic; (2) for evidence of moderate to strong genetic structure within the species as a whole through analysis of single nucleotide polymorphisms; (3) for parapatry and range asymmetry of the two varieties using a curated occurrence data set; (4) for ecological divergence via niche comparison between the two varieties. We also discuss the implications of our findings in the contexts of reproductive isolation and speciation in biodiversity hotspots.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Code and intermediate files used in this study can be found at Github: https://github.com/alleneli/abronia_budding_sp.

Study system

The genus Abronia, commonly known as sand verbenas, comprise 20 species of xerophytic plants that are adapted to coastal dunes and arid, sandy environments across western North America (Galloway, 1975; Wilson, 1972, 1976). Eight of the species are native to the CFP, five of which are considered rare or contain rare varieties (Wilson, 1972; Murdock, 2012). Abronia villosa var. aurita, a rare variety with a complicated taxonomic history (Appendix S1), is one of only two Abronia taxa endemic to California (Wilson, 1972; Murdock, 2012). The two varieties of Abronia villosa differ in both elevational range and morphology (Murdock, 2012). Curtis (1977) delineated the ecotypes corresponding to var. villosa and var. aurita as desert and nondesert taxa, respectively, and distinguished them morphologically. Abronia villosa var. aurita has distinctly larger floral characters (perianth tube and limb), more flowers per umbel, and longer peduncles than var. villosa. Its range spans southern California and parts of the southwestern United States (southern Nevada, eastern Arizona), northern Mexico, and Baja California (Galloway, 1975; Wilson, 1972, 1976). Abronia villosa var. aurita is endemic to Riverside and San Diego counties, and is a mostly montane species found on the western side of the San Jacinto Mountains (Curtis, 1977). However, part of its range has expanded farther west to the Pacific Ocean in annually flooding sandy washes of the Santa Margarita River Valley (Figure 1B) (Curtis, 1977; Murdock, 2012). Although the varieties are mostly geographically isolated by the Peninsular Ranges (San Jacinto and Santa Rosa Mountains), they are connected by Coyote Canyon, which is a canyon network between the Anza‐Borrego Desert and the mountain town of Anza that contains the populations of var. villosa that are geographically closest to var. aurita (Figure 1D). There are also populations of var. aurita along the Santa Margarita River valley that are separated from the rest of its range both geographically and in terms of elevation, with some populations reaching the coast at the outlet of the Santa Margarita River (Figure 1B). At this outlet, var. aurita has significant range overlap with its coastal congener, Abronia umbellata var. umbellata, which has been found to hybridize wherever it occurs sympatrically with a congener (Tillett, 1967; Van Natto, 2020). Morphological similarities and an ITS phylogeny support the hypothesis that A. villosa and A. umbellata are sister taxa comprising a monophyletic group that is itself sister to the Baja California species Abronia gracilis (Tillett, 1967; Galloway, 1975; Nosratinia, 2013).

Phylogenetic analysis

We included a total of 50 individuals (Appendix S2) in our phylogenetic analysis belonging to A. villosa var. aurita and its allies (A. villosa var. villosa, A. umbellata, and A. gracilis) and employed Abronia latifolia as an outgroup taxon. Three of the A. umbellata samples belong to the autogamous variety A. umbellata var. breviflora, but the rest belong to the outcrossing var. umbellata (Spellenberg and Poole, 2003; Samis and Eckert, 2007; Darling et al., 2008). Two putative hybrid samples of intermediate morphology between A. villosa var. aurita and A. umbellata var. umbellata were collected from the outlet of the Santa Margarita River (Figure 1B). Most samples were collected in the field and were dried on silica gel for subsequent DNA extraction. However, two samples were grown from field‐collected seeds in a greenhouse (by author E.L.), four were from herbarium specimens, and two were obtained as raw Angiosperms353 reads (by coauthor M. G. Johnson). Specimen vouchers for newly sequenced specimens were placed in the HSC herbarium. A record for each sample collected was uploaded to iNaturalist (www.inaturalist.org). The raw reads of all samples have been uploaded to the National Center for Biodiversity (NCBI) Short Read Archive (Appendix S2).

DNAs were extracted from all samples using the NucleoSpin Plant II kit (Macherey‐Nagel, Düren, Germany) and the manufacturer's protocol, with 5 μL proteinase K (20 mg/mL) added to the digestion step. DNA extractions were quantified with broad‐range Qubit Fluorometric Quantification (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and DNA quality was visualized via gel electrophoresis using 1% agarose gels. Dual‐indexed Illumina library preparation and target‐capture sequencing using the Angiosperms353 probe set (Johnson et al., 2019) were performed by Rapid Genomics (Gainesville, FL, USA) on a NovaSeq. 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 150‐bp paired‐end reads.

The raw reads were cleaned, assembled, extracted, and aligned with the Captus 0.9.89 pipeline (Ortiz et al., 2024). Trimming and quality filtering of raw reads were performed using the Captus function clean with default settings, utilizing BBduk from the BBTools suite 39.10 (Bushnell et al., 2017). De novo assembly of paired reads into contigs was performed using the Captus function assemble with the default settings via MEGAHIT 1.2.9 (Li et al., 2015). Target genes were extracted using a custom reference data set for Abronia compatible with the Angiosperms353 probe set (Johnson et al., 2019). Targets were extracted using the Captus function extract with a minimum nuclear identity threshold of 90% (‐‐nuc_min_identity 90) and Abronia latifolia as the outgroup (‐‐out), matching the assembled contigs to the Abronia custom references (‐n angiosperms353.abronia.fasta) via Scipio 1.4 (Keller et al., 2008). Extracted markers were aligned using the Captus function align, with flanking regions included (‐f ALL), via Mafft 7.505 (Katoh et al., 2002) and then trimmed via ClipKIT 1.3.0 (Steenwyk et al., 2020) with Captus default settings.

The alignments for each given locus obtained from Captus were further trimmed to remove gaps with trimAl 1.2 (Capella‐Gutiérrez et al., 2009). We removed positions from alignments when 50% or more of sequences contained a gap (trimAl ‐gt 0.5). Alignments of some loci contained a portion of samples with problematic regions that are consistent with pseudogenes or cryptic paralog capture (corresponding to deeply divergent sequence blocks within species), particularly in flanking regions for both. This pattern was revealed by visual inspection for approximately one third of alignments. The proportion of putative pseudogene sequences per locus varied between 3 to 25 sequences but was often low. The alignments of individual loci were retained but trimmed under increasingly stringent thresholds to minimize misleading signal as follows. Alignments were categorized into three groups based on visual assessment of problematic regions: (1) bad = many samples with problematic regions; (2) questionable = few samples with problematic regions; (3) good = no samples with problematic regions. After this categorization, 218 alignments were marked as good, 61 as questionable, and 66 as bad. Good alignments were not trimmed further. Questionable and bad alignments were trimmed further using the trimAl option ‐automated1. Bad alignments received additional trimming by removing positions with a similarity score lower than 0.005 (trimAl ‐st 0.005), removing columns in the alignments with doubtful homology among sequences. For both questionable and bad alignments, the extra trimming steps caused some sequences (rows) to become entirely gaps; these sequences were fully excluded from those alignments (trimAl ‐resoverlap 0.20 ‐seqoverlap 20).

We inferred phylogenetic trees using a concatenated maximum likelihood (ML) approach and a coalescent‐consistent approach. For the first approach, individual gene trees were produced for each trimmed alignment using IQ‐Tree v2.2.2.6 (Nguyen et al., 2015) with a GTR + G model and 1000 ultrafast bootstraps. We identified outlier long branches using Treeshrink 1.3.9 (Mai and Mirarab, 2018), with the alpha setting ‐q 0.30. Four gene trees (from alignments that recovered very few taxa) contained only long branches, and these alignments were removed from the analysis before the following step. Long‐branch taxa identified with Treeshrink were removed from the original Captus alignments using a script modified from remove_long_branched_taxa_from_fasta_005.py (Vargas et al., 2020). The alignments with long branch samples removed were then used to infer a concatenated tree using IQ‐Tree 2.2.2.6 (Nguyen et al., 2015), using the option ‐p. In addition, a second concatenated tree was inferred without any putative hybrid samples of clear intermediate morphology between A. villosa var. aurita and A. umbellata var. umbellata. This second tree was inferred to ensure that putative hybrids would not have an undue effect on the topology, because hybridization disagrees with the fundamental assumption of strictly bifurcating tree inference. To calculate the number of gene trees in concordance with each given node in our concatenated tree, we employed PhyParts (Smith et al., 2015) and visualized the results using phypartspiecharts.py (https://github.com/mossmatters/MJPythonNotebooks). For the coalescent‐consistent approach, we generated new gene trees from alignments with long‐branch taxa removed (again with a GTR + G model and 1000 ultrafast bootstraps), and the output trees were used to calculate a coalescent‐consistent tree in Astral 5.7.1 (Mirarab et al., 2014).

Genetic structure analysis

To explore the genetic structure of Abronia villosa var. aurita and its allies, we created a matrix of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) using BWA v0.7.17 (Li and Durbin, 2009), Picard Toolkit v3.1.1 (Broad Institute, 2019), Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.4.0.0 (McKenna et al., 2010), and Plink (Purcell et al., 2007). Two taxa were excluded from this analysis: A. latifolia because it is an outgroup, and Abronia gracilis due to low sequence recovery. First, we created a custom reference assembly of 345 target loci with a total of 477,308 bp from the longest Abronia villosa var. aurita sequence in each of our 345 trimmed Captus alignments. Our cleaned paired reads (produced by the Captus function clean) were mapped to the custom reference with BWA mem, and duplicate reads were marked and removed with Picard Tools. The SNPs were then called for each sample using GATK HaplotypeCaller. The SNPs for all samples were combined into a single data set using GATK CombineGVCFs, from which variants were jointly genotyped using GATK GenotypeGVCFs. Genotyped variants were selected to include SNPs and to exclude any nonvariants using GATK SelectVariants. Plink was then used to filter SNPs by removing individuals missing genotype data for more than 20% of their genotyped markers, removing variants with an allele frequency less than 10%, and applying linkage disequilibrium filtering with a window size of 50 and an LD threshold (r 2) of 0.1, finalizing the SNP matrix for downstream analyses.

We assessed genetic structure using fastStructure 1.0 (Raj et al., 2014) and used Structure_threader v1.3.11 (Pina‐Martins et al., 2017) to automate and parallelize multiple runs of genetic clustering. Taxa were grouped into five groups using Structure_threader ‐‐ind, which aesthetically divided taxa into groups only for downstream visualization and did not affect clustering. The optimal number of predicted genetic clusters (K) was determined after assessing values of K ranging from 1 to 10 with the fastStructure function chooseK.py. Plots were visualized using Structure_threader. To further visualize genetic distance between species and varieties, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. Plink was used to prune the GATK‐SNP data set and execute a PCA using the same linkage disequilibrium filtering as above. The PCA was visualized in R 4.3.2 (R Core Team, 2023) using the R package tidyverse 1.3.2 (Wickham et al., 2019).

Absolute genetic divergence (D xy) and nucleotide diversity (π)

We determined the relative degrees of divergence between taxa by calculating average absolute genetic divergence (D xy) and within taxa by calculating the average nucleotide diversity (π) using pixy 0.95.02 (Korunes and Samuk, 2021). We prepared a SNP matrix following the same protocol as our genetic structure analysis but including pixy‐specific caveats at the end of the pipeline. When variants were jointly genotyped using GATK GenotypeGVCFs, nonvariant sites were included instead of excluded, because they are necessary for the correct computation of D xy and π. The data set was then filtered to include only biallelic SNPs using bcftools 1.22 (Danecek et al., 2021), following best practices outlined in the pixy manual. We then used pixy to calculate D xy and π using non‐overlapping 10‐kb windows, performing two runs of pixy, one in which each variety was considered as a population, and one in which A. villosa var. aurita was split into two populations representing its western lowland vs. eastern montane occurrences. Outputs were then averaged in R (R Core Team, 2023) by combining across windows and summing raw counts to compute the ratio of pairwise differences to comparisons, as recommended in the pixy manual (i.e., how many base pairs differ between populations [π] or population pairs [D xy] divided by the number of comparisons made).

Climatic niche and range‐size comparisons

Geographic occurrences for both varieties of A. villosa were downloaded from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF.org, 2021) and California Consortium Herbarium portal (CCH Consortium of California Herbaria 2022). We filtered occurrence data for duplicate collections and erroneous identifications outside the known ranges of each variety (see Appendix S1) (Curtis, 1977; Murdock, 2012). Because dense populations of var. villosa in the Anza Borrego Desert were generally oversampled, samples of the variety outnumbered those of var. aurita by an order of magnitude in collections, so we reduced sampling bias by randomly removing collections of var. villosa with localities within 0.1° latitude or longitude of each other. After filtering, our data set comprised 119 occurrences of var. aurita and 528 occurrences of var. villosa.

We downloaded 19 bioclimatic variables at a resolution of 3 min of a degree from WorldClim 1.4 (Hijmans et al., 2005), bounded within our study region (–113° to –119° longitude, and 32° to 37° latitude). The climatic niches of each variety were compared in a PCA by projecting their bioclimatic variables into a niche space generated from pseudoabsence data within our study region using the R package ecospat v1.3.9 (Di Cola et al., 2017). We visualized the resulting environmental space using a modified version of the function niceOverPlot (Fernández‐lópez and Villa‐Machío, 2017) in the R package ggplot2 3.4.2 (Wickham, 2016) and uploaded it to the repository of our study. We used Shoener's index (D) as a calculation of niche overlap (Schoener, 1968; Warren et al., 2008) and tested for niche equivalency and similarity with ecospat. Niche equivalency evaluates whether two taxa occupy niches that are statistically distinguishable from a null case by randomly switching occurrences between their respective ranges (Warren et al., 2008; Broennimann et al., 2012). Niche similarity measures whether the observed niche overlap is greater or less than expected by chance, by randomly shifting one taxon's occurrences within its own environmental space while holding those of the other taxon constant (Schoener, 1968; Warren et al., 2008). In other words, niche equivalency tests whether the niches of two species are effectively identical by randomly swapping occurrences between them, while niche similarity tests whether their niches are more similar than expected by chance given their respective environmental backgrounds. We assessed niche equivalency using the option overlap.alternative = lower, and for niche similarity, we randomly shifted ranges using the option rand.type = 1. In both cases, we performed the tests with 1000 replications.

To quantify range asymmetry, we divided our filtered occurrence data into a series of cells from grid lines based on latitude and longitude using the R package raster 3.6‐31 (Hijmans, 2019). We calculated range asymmetry as the total area of grid cells occupied by A. villosa var. aurita divided by the total area of grid cells occupied by A. villosa var. villosa (Fitzpatrick and Turelli, 2006; Vargas et al., 2020). We used two sizes of cells to account for spatial scale: a fine spatial scale (0.05 decimal degrees, approximately 33 km2) and a coarse spatial scale (0.1 decimal degrees, approximately 131 km2).

RESULTS

Phylogenetic analysis

Newly sequenced samples yielded roughly 206 million reads consisting of 31 billion base pairs. A total of 16,301 target regions were extracted from contigs with Captus function extract, averaging 326 target regions per sample. The Captus function align produced a total of 345 alignments from extracted target regions. After subsetting and extensive trimming with trimAl, the resulting alignments had a remaining 14,840 target regions (averaging 297 per sample) consisting of 21.1 million base pairs.

The concatenated ML tree inferred 100% bootstrap support for the monophyly of each species of Abronia (Figure 2). The coalescent‐consistent tree likewise recovered strong posterior probability support (PP = 1) for the monophyly of each species (Appendix S3). At the variety level, there was 100% bootstrap support and strong posterior probability (PP = 1) for the monophyly of both A. villosa var. aurita and A. umbellata var. breviflora. In both trees, A. villosa var. aurita is phylogenetically nested within A. villosa var. villosa (100% bootstrap support, PP = 0.96), making var. villosa a paraphyletic group. Abronia villosa var. aurita also comprises two reciprocally monophyletic groups that align with the respective geographic isolation of the localities of samples in each subclade; one subclade represents higher elevation montane populations (475–1500 m a.s.l., San Jacinto Mountains and Hemet) and the other subclade consists of lower elevation coastal populations (6–350 m, Santa Margarita River Valley) (Figure 1B–D). The autogamous variety A. umbellata var. breviflora is likewise phylogenetically nested within A. umbellata var. umbellata, matching the findings of Van Natto and Eckert (2022). One putative hybrid sample (EEA0175) from the outlet of the Santa Margarita River, where A. villosa var. aurita and A. umbellata var. umbellata co‐occur, had conflicting placement in both trees, either being sister to A. villosa or a clade comprising A. umbellata and A. gracilis (putative hybrids are highlighted in light green in Figure 2; Appendix S3). The secondary concatenated tree, made without any putative hybrid samples, recovered the same underlying topology for all major groups and the majority of minor relationships (Appendix S4). The PhyParts analysis inferred low phylogenetic signal across gene trees (Appendix S5), as expected for infraspecific phylogenies inferred from protein‐coding genes. Because of the low signal found in independent gene regions, we focus our discussion on the concatenated analysis, where all the phylogenetic signal has been added together, producing a robust phylogenetic hypothesis.

Genetic structure analysis

Filtering for fastStructure resulted in a SNP matrix of 559 variants. The genetic structure of A. villosa and A. umbellata inferred by fastStructure that best explains the data comprises three ancestral genetic clusters: at this value of K, the varieties of A. villosa are clustered in two distinct genetic groups, with A. umbellata making up the third (K = 3; Figure 3A). Filtering for PCA with Plink yielded 707 SNPs (the procedure for PCA in Plink limits depth of filtering, resulting in more SNPs than the SNP matrix used for fastStructure), recovering results that are consistent with the fastStructure analysis (Figure 3B). Both the PCA and fastStructure showed evidence of hybridization between A. villosa var. aurita and A. umbellata var. umbellata for two samples from the outlet of the Santa Margarita River; likely early‐generation hybrids based on admixture proportions.

Absolute genetic divergence (D xy) and nucleotide diversity (π)

The mean nucleotide diversity within taxa (π) is 0.0115 with A. villosa var. aurita split into two populations, and 0.0121 without splitting. In both split and unsplit runs of pixy, A. umbellata var. breviflora had the lowest π (0.00443), A. villosa var. villosa the highest (0.0185), and A. villosa var. aurita had roughly half the π‐value of A. villosa var. villosa, whether split into two populations (montane = 0.00918, lowland = 0.00810) or not (0.00906) (Table 1). Mean nucleotide diversity between taxa (D xy) totals 0.0219 and 0.0225 in runs with var. aurita split and unsplit, respectively. Between varieties of the same species, D xy was relatively low (0.0130–0.0172), while between species it was relatively high (0.0276–0.0300). Valuses for comparisons between putative hybrids of A. umbellata and A. villosa with other taxa were more intermediate (0.0185–0.0222) (Table 2). When A. villosa var. aurita was split, the montane and lowland populations had the lowest D xy of any two taxa compared. Values calculated for D xy between var. aurita and other taxa changed only negligibly when splitting var. aurita into two populations (Appendix S6), and values for D xy between any two taxa besides var. aurita were unaffected by its splitting. Similarly, splitting had no effect on π values for other taxa.

Climatic niche and range‐size comparison

In our PCA of bioclimatic variables projected into a niche space defined by pseudoabsence data, the first two principal components explained 74.24% of the total variability in the study region (PC1 = 46.84% and PC2 = 27.4%). The variables contributing most to PC1 were annual mean temperature (bio1), max temperature of warmest month (bio5), and mean temperature of warmest quarter (bio10); the variables contributing most to PC2 were temperature seasonality (bio4), temperature annual range (bio7), and precipitation seasonality (bio15). Overall, A. villosa var. aurita occupies a wetter, colder niche than A. villosa var. villosa. Visualization of their climatic niches in an environmental space (Figure 4) shows no overlap; however, observing the PC axes individually does show some minimal overlap. The metric for niche overlap (Schoener's D) for the two varieties of A villosa is 0.046, indicating that they largely occupy different climatic niches. While niches were not equivalent (P = 0.001), they trend toward being more similar than expected by chance (P = 0.073; this level of niche similarity that trends toward but does not meet the traditional statistical threshold [P = 0.05] is anticipated for closely related taxa, especially those recently diverged; Warren et al., 2008; Otero et al., 2022; Juárez et al., 2023).

Our range‐size comparison analysis found the sister varieties to have strongly asymmetrical range sizes, with the range of A. villosa var. villosa being over five times larger than that of A. villosa var. aurita. At fine spatial scales, var. villosa had a range 5.75 times larger than var. aurita and at coarse spatial scales, 5.085 times larger.

DISCUSSION

A hypothesis for the evolutionary history of Abronia villosa var. aurita

Our results showed a clear signal of budding speciation within Abronia villosa, with var. aurita being derivative of var. villosa. The tree topologies infer A. villosa var. aurita to be monophyletic but nested within A. villosa var. villosa, causing var. villosa to be paraphyletic (Figure 2; Appendix S3). This nested pattern meets the expectation for budding speciation, which is further supported by the range asymmetry and the geographic peripherality of var. aurita relative to var. villosa (Rieseberg and Brouillet, 1994; Baldwin, 2005; Anacker and Strauss, 2014). Reconstruction of geographical ranges shows that the two varieties are parapatric, with var. aurita covering a geographical area five times smaller than that ofvar. villosa. Our niche comparison demonstrated that the two varieties occupy different bioclimatic niches, indicating ecological divergence (Figure 4). Our structure analysis showed that var. aurita and var. villosa cluster separately from each other (Figure 3A, B), providing evidence of strong genetic structure within the species and supporting the classification of these two lineages as distinct taxa (Abrams, 1905; Jepson, 1914; Curtis, 1977). Moreover, the lower nucleotide diversity observed within var. aurita compared to var. villosa (π = 0.00906 and π = 0.0185, respectively) indicates that var. aurita has a smaller effective population size than the widespread var. villosa, and the moderate absolute divergence between them (D xy = 0.0172) indicates recent divergence between them. These patterns are consistent with a scenario of budding speciation with recent parapatric divergence. Additionally, we recovered evidence of recent hybridization between A. villosa var. aurita and A. umbellata var. umbellata, consistent with the focal clade of this study comprising recently diverged taxa, with substantial but not total genetic isolation.

We hypothesize that A. villosa var. aurita is an ecologically diverged variety of A. villosa that colonized wetter, colder mountain habitats, likely spreading from the desert floor around Borrego Springs into the Peninsular Ranges (San Jacinto and Santa Rosa Mountains), via Coyote Canyon (Figure 1D). In our phylogeny, the sample of A. villosa var. villosa that is the sister group of var. aurita (Figure 2, EEA0110) was grown from seed collected in Borrego Springs near the base of Coyote Canyon, showing that the closest living relative to var. aurita in our study was from this region—strong evidence for our hypothesis. At some point, this montane transition was accompanied by an enlargement of floral characters compared to the lowland var. villosa, with a longer perianth tube, larger perianth limb, more flowers per umbel, and longer peduncles. It is highly unlikely that this divergence was instead caused by the uplift of the San Jacinto mountains because this upheaval predated the diversification of Abronia by at least 10 million years (Spotila et al., 2020; Cunha Neto et al., 2022). Thus, our evidence supports the current taxonomic rank of var. aurita. Elevating it to a unique species is unwarranted. While patterns of budding speciation between the varieties of A. villosa are clear, they are still nascent and ongoing. We also hypothesize that after its expansion into the Peninsular Ranges, var. aurita dispersed westward to lower elevations along annually flooded sandy washes and may currently be initiating the process of subdivergence between an eastern montane clade and a western low‐elevation clade. It is notable that the western lowland clade extends all the way to the Pacific Ocean: no other populations of Abronia villosa reached the coast in the United States.

Tillett (1967) hypothesized that if Abronia villosa reached the Pacific Ocean via river outlets, it would hybridize with A. umbellata anywhere they overlap in range. Indeed, our study found multiple lines of evidence of a hybrid zone between A. villosa var. aurita and A. umbellata var. umbellata at the outlet of the Santa Margarita River in Camp Pendleton, CA (Figure 1C). Two samples collected from the outlet had morphology intermediate between A. villosa and A. umbellata. One of the samples (EEA0075) had conflicting placements in the concatenated and coalescent‐consistent trees (Figure 2; Appendix S3), alternatively being sister to all Abronia villosa or a clade comprising A. umbellata and A. gracilis. The other sample (EEA0074) was consistently sister to all samples of A. umbellata. Their conflicting placements, and tendency to be sister to their parent clades in phylogenetic inferences, support them being hybrid individuals. Evidence of hybridization is further supported by our analysis of genetic structure using SNPs. In the fastStructure analysis, the hybrid samples had mixed ancestry of A. umbellata and A. villosa var. aurita, that is consistent with early‐generation hybrids (Figure 3A); in our PCA, the hybrid samples were distributed in an intermediate manner between the clusters for A. umbellata var. umbellata and A. villosa var. aurita along the PC1 axis (Figure 3B). The hybrid samples had higher nucleotide diversity (π = 0.0164) than either of their parent taxa (A. villosa var. aurita, π = 0.00906; A. umbellata var. umbellata π = 0.0122), which may reflect the retention of divergent alleles from parent lineages. Furthermore, the absolute genetic divergence values are similar when comparing the hybrid to either parent lineage (A. villosa var. aurita, D xy = 0.0185; A. umbellata var. umbellata D xy = 0.0187), again indicating that these are early‐generation hybrids and not a population that has experienced extensive backcrossing. The formation of hybrids is a strong indication that the divergence of A. umbellata and A. villosa is recent (Levin, 2012).

Subdivergence within Abronia villosa var. aurita

Finding evidence that A. villosa var. aurita comprises two reciprocally monophyletic groups is an unexpected and novel discovery (Figure 2; Appendix S3). These two subclades showed a degree of geographic and ecological isolation, with the more montane populations (475–1500 m a.s.l.) from the San Jacinto Mountains and Hemet, California forming one subclade (Figure 1B), and the more coastal and lower‐elevation populations (6–350 m) found in sandy washes along the Santa Margarita River Valley comprising a second subclade (Figure 1C). While not shown in the best K (K = 3) of the fastStructure analysis (Figure 3A), all values of K from 4 to 10 showed these two subclades clustering separately to some degree (Appendix S7). However, interpretations beyond inferences based on the best value for K should be taken with caution. In contrast to these findings, montane and lowland subclades had similar levels of nucleotide diversity (π = 0.00918 and π = 0.00810, respectively), and their absolute genetic divergence was low (D xy = 0.0099) and only marginally higher than their π values, suggesting relatively weak genetic structure between them. Together, our results indicate that while there is some degree of geographic or ecological divergence, these subclades are not yet strongly genetically isolated and show a pattern of very recent incipient divergence where shared ancestral alleles still dominate. The expansion of var. aurita into the Peninsular Ranges seems to have facilitated access to lower‐elevation niches on the western side of the mountains, a large geographic area that var. villosa does not occupy. Abronia villosa seeds are known to be transported long distances by flash floods (Wilson, 1976; Curtis, 1977; Murdock, 2012), and so expansion into lower elevations was likely via downstream seed transportation along seasonally flooding sandy washes. Downstream dispersal is a common mechanism for plant migration and has been shown to increase species richness in riparian communities (Honnay et al., 2001; Jansson et al., 2005). The newly found subdivergence supports the hypothesis that topographic and climatic complexity of the CFP promotes the generation of species by reducing gene flow and may also enable the persistence of lineages through elevational climatic buffering (Stebbins and Major, 1965; Richerson and Lum, 1980; Kruckeberg, 1986; Loarie et al., 2008).

Reproductive isolation in Abronia villosa

Studies on reproductive isolation in plants have shown that prezygotic barriers such as flowering time, pollinator interactions, and ecogeography are often the first reproductive barriers to occur in advance of speciation (Lowry et al., 2008; Sobel and Streisfeld, 2015; Christie and Strauss, 2019; Christie et al., 2022). The fact that the Abronia species commonly hybridize (Tillett, 1967; Van Natto, 2020) may be a sign that species are recently diverged and that postzygotic isolation has yet to occur. In the case of A. villosa, we hypothesize that timing of flowering and pollinator interactions act as prezygotic barriers between varieties. Elevational gradients strongly affect the timing of flowering, and differences in phenology are a well‐documented prezygotic barrier (e.g., Savolainen et al., 2006; Givnish, 2010; Frei et al., 2014). While the varieties overlap in flowering time, the peak bloom time of var. villosa is in March, and flowering largely tapers off before the montane var. aurita reaches peak bloom in May (based on observations from www.inaturalist.org). Additionally, the larger floral characters of var. aurita, particularly the longer perianth tube, may reflect some degree of pollinator divergence from the more widespread var. villosa. Species of Abronia are pollinated primarily by long‐range nocturnal moths (Tillett, 1967; Jabis et al., 2011; Doubleday et al., 2013). The perianth tubes of var. aurita are nearly double that of var. villosa (17.7–21.1 mm and 10.5–12.5 mm, respectively) (Curtis, 1977). Among hawkmoth‐pollinated plants, longer perianth tubes are known to be a specialization for specific hawkmoths, while shorter perianth tubes are a sign of a generalist (Johnson et al., 2017). The longer perianth tube length in var. aurita could be a signal of adaptation to a specific hawkmoth, which could contribute to reproductive isolation from var. villosa.

Speciation in biodiversity hotspots: the ephemeral speciation model

The ephemeral speciation model predicts that speciation events are common and even rapid, but that relatively few of these new species persist across evolutionary time scales (Rosenblum et al., 2012). In A. villosa, we found evidence of budding speciation followed by further divergence into two subclades in the derived taxon (var. aurita), observations that are consistent with it being in the initial phase of the model prediction of rapid and common divergence. Of course, we do not know whether the intraspecific lineages of A. villosa will continue diverging or go extinct. A macroevolutionary study of the CFP flora revealed low extinction rates (Lancaster and Kay, 2013), supporting a model of moderate speciation with low extinction rates as an explanation for the CFP's high floral biodiversity. Their results deviate from the ephemeral speciation model that predicts a high rate of speciation coupled with a high rate of extinction; however, their study employed phylogenies with a single individual per species, and as a result did not capture infraspecific variation. Based on our findings here, and the high proportion (~30%) of infraspecific taxa found in the CFP (Burge et al., 2016; Baldwin 2019), we speculate that infraspecific divergence is common in Abronia and other lineages and contributes to the rapid speciation described by the ephemeral speciation model. However, species in the CFP may be less “ephemeral” and persist more often than elsewhere (as shown by Lancaster and Kay, 2013), resulting in greater overall floral biodiversity in the region. Testing these hypotheses would necessitate calculating diversification rates using high‐resolution phylogenies that capture infraspecific divergence.

CONCLUSIONS

We found strong evidence that A. villosa var. aurita was derived from A. villosa var. villosa via budding speciation, adding to the growing evidence of the importance of budding speciation in the assemblage of biodiversity hotspots. Our phylogeny showed a pattern in which var. aurita is monophyletic and nested in the paraphyletic var. villosa. The more montane range of var. aurita is asymmetrically smaller and peripheral to the widespread desert range of var. villosa, suggesting an upslope ecological divergence into the Peninsular Ranges by var. aurita. We documented microevolutionary patterns at play during the divergence process, demonstrating the existence of paraphyletic progenitor taxa (Rieseberg and Brouillet, 1994; Olmstead, 1995; Hörandl and Stuessy, 2010) and highlighting an alternative process to allopatric speciation—budding speciation—as a major player in plant diversification (Gottlieb, 2004; Baldwin 2005; Malay and Paulay, 2010; Claremont et al., 2012; Parins‐Fukuchi, 2021; Otero et al., 2022). The novel finding that var. aurita itself seems to be diverging indicates that incipient speciation is a continuous and complex process. This process can be better understood by closing the gap between population‐level and species‐level studies by integrating phylogenomics, genetic structure analysis, climatic niche comparisons, and geographic data to yield insights into the mechanisms that underlie the early stages of plant speciation.
