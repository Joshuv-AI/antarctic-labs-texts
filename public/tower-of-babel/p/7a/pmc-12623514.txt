Selective S1P Receptor Modulation in Multiple Sclerosis Alters CXCL13:CXCR5-Associated Immune Activities Without Impacting Anti-SARS-CoV-2 Immunity
Authors: Andrea Harrer, Richard F. Radlberger, Beate Buchegger, Brooke Slade, Thomas Haybaeck, Hannes Oberkofler, Konstantin E. Thiel, Ferdinand Otto, Tobias Moser, Peter Wipfler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSphingosine-1-phosphate receptor (S1PR) modulators are effective therapies for multiple sclerosis (MS) that block lymphocyte egress from secondary lymphoid organs. This migration inhibition carries the risk of reduced infection-control as reported for the non-selective S1PR modulator, fingolimod. CXCL13:CXCR5-associated immune activities play a key role in protective antibody-based immunity but are also linked to inflammation in MS. Utilizing the ongoing SARS-CoV-2 (severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2) pandemic, we aimed to determine whether selective S1PR modulation with ozanimod acts on the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis for modulating MS activity and whether this impacts anti-viral immune responses.MethodsThis 1-year observational study included 20 patients with MS receiving ozanimod and 10 healthy probands. CXCR5+ T cells, B cells, serum CXCL13, anti-SARS-CoV-2 serostatus, and SARS-CoV-2-spike protein (ProtS)-reactive T cell responses were measured at 3-month intervals.ResultsCXCR5+ T and B cell frequencies and serum CXCL13, but not anti-SARS-CoV-2 responses, declined after ozanimod initiation. Anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibody and ProtS-reactive T cell responses peaked after recall vaccinations and break-through infections. Notably, ProtS-reactive T cell frequencies shifted from CD4+ to CD8+ T cell responses in patients treated with ozanimod compared to controls.ConclusionSelective S1P receptor modulation with ozanimod affects the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis by reducing circulating CXCR5+ lymphocytes and serum CXCL13, which may contribute to reduce meningeal inflammation in MS. Moreover, the anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune defense appeared to be preserved during treatment with SARS-CoV-2-reactive CD8+ T cells, possibly compensating the lack of CD4+ T cell responses. Our immunological data may well apply to other viral infections and underscore the favorable safety and efficacy profile of ozanimod.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40120-025-00831-w.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic-progressive, neuroinflammatory disease and the leading cause of neurological disability in young and middle-aged adults in the developed world. While disease course and symptoms are heterogeneous, the disorder is characterized by immune-mediated inflammation, demyelination, and axonal damage in brain and spinal cord [1].

Recent evidence suggests a role for B cells in sustaining inflammation throughout the central nervous system (CNS) in patients with MS [2]. Brain dissections revealed accumulation of B cells in form of simple aggregates or resembling tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) at sites of meningeal inflammation, together with microglia/macrophage activation and subpial demyelination. Importantly, such B cell-dominated immune cell infiltrates may already occur in early MS. They have been detected in the meninges of brain and spinal cord and are suspected to contribute to smouldering pathological processes leading to progression independent of relapse activity [2–5].

Chemokine C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 13:C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 5 (CXCL13:CXCR5)-associated immune activities mediate B cell accumulations and the formation of TLS at sites of chronic inflammation such as the meninges in MS [4–6]. Physiologically, the chemokine CXCL13, together with its cognate receptor CXCR5, controls the organization of B cells and follicular T cells in secondary lymphoid organs (SLO) [7, 8]. Both follicular T cells and B cells express CXCR5, allowing them to co-localize to CXCL13-rich areas within lymph nodes. This is highly relevant for the formation of B cell follicles, functional germinal centers (GC) and protective, high-affinity-maturated antibody-based immune responses [9]. The quality and magnitude of these antibody responses depend on tightly controlled interactions of follicular CXCR5+ T cells with B cells, particularly of follicular helper T (Tfh) cells and associated Tfh1-, Tfh17-, Tfh1.17-, and Tfh2-like subsets. Follicular regulatory T (Tfr) cells, on the other hand, contribute as negative regulators. Follicular CD8+ T (Tf8) cells were initially described as fighters against viral pathogens infecting B cell follicles, as for instance human immune deficiency virus-infected Tfh cells, but may also assist B cells to produce sufficient antibody responses [8, 10]. Circulating counterparts of CXCR5+ follicular T cell-like subsets can be found in peripheral blood, and may include activated cells formed during GC activity as response to infection or vaccination [11, 12].

In MS, CXCL13 has been found increased in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) during disease activity, and higher CXCL13 levels have been associated with a higher risk of developing more severe cortical damage [6]. Interfering with intrathecal CXCL13:CXCR5-related immune activities may hence explain the effectiveness of B cell-depleting therapies or sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor modulation, which both reduce CSF levels of CXCL13 [13, 14].

The primary therapeutic mode of action of S1P receptor modulators is to impede the re-egress of lymphocytes from SLO into the blood [15], and consequently migration into the CNS. However, this mechanism—leading to peripheral lymphopenia—may impair immune surveillance and reduce protective immune responses. In fact, fingolimod (FTY), the first approved S1P modulator that non-selectively binds to S1PR1, S1PR3, S1PR4, and S1PR5 [16] has been shown to increase the risk of mild infections [17, 18], and to blunt antibody responses following severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) vaccination [15, 19].

Reduced antibody responses indicate decreased CXCL13:CXCR5-related immune activities relevant to humoral immunity, which may be less in the case with newer-generation and more selective S1PR modulators such as ozanimod (OZM). With the CXCL13:CXCR5 chemokine axis relevant to both MS activity and protective immunity, and OZM having just reached EU market authorization when the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic started to ravage, we initiated a real-life study for monitoring the circulating components of the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis as well as SARS-CoV-2-related immune responses in peripheral blood of patients with MS treated with OZM. The aim was to determine whether and to what extent the selective S1P receptor modulator OZM mitigates CXCL13:CXCR5-associated immune activities detectable in blood, and whether this functionally translates to anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibody responses, anti-SARS-CoV-2T cell reactivity, and/or evidence of MS disease activity.

Methods

Study Design, Probands, and Consent

In this 1-year observational monocenter study, we included treatment-naïve or interferon/glatiramer acetate pre-treated patients with relapsing–remitting MS according to the 2017 revised McDonald criteria [20], who had opted for OZM (Zeposia®, Bristol-Myers Squibb Pharma, Dublin, Ireland) and were willing to participate in the study and to donate blood routinely drawn at routine laboratory tests from baseline (pre-treatment) to 12 months follow-up. The recruiting phase was from November 2021 to March 2023, thus within the public health emergency of international concern for SARS-CoV-2 according to the World Health Organisation [21].

Blood was collected at baseline (T0, pre-treatment) and at 3-month intervals (T3M, T6M, T9M, and T12M) during routine visits for clinical assessments [including expanded disability status scale (EDSS), MS relapses and infection state] and laboratory safety parameter measurements (white blood cell counts, lymphocyte counts and liver enzymes). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and cervical spine was performed at baseline (within 3 months before or after treatment initiation) and 12 months later. Patients were dichotomized according to disease course into subgoups of evidence of disease activity (EDA) and no evidence of disease activity (NEDA) during the observation period, the latter defined as having no MS relapses, no disability worsening according to the EDSS, and no MRI activity. In addition, we included 10 healthy donors (controls), who consented to donating venous blood drawn 3 months apart (T1 and T2). Baseline characteristics for each individual are listed in the supplements (Supplementary Table 1).

All the procedures performed in the studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. All patients and healthy probands provided written informed consent for participation in non-interventional biomedical basic research in MS and clinically isolated syndrome approved by the local ethics committee of the Bundesland Salzburg (415-E/1612/11-2018). The study was carried out according to the Helsinki protocol in its current version.

Sample Collection and Processing

Peripheral venous blood was collected in Vacutainer™ serum tubes and cell preparations tubes (CPT; Becton Dickinson, Basel, Switzerland). Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were enriched from blood collected in CPT tubes according to the manufacturer’s protocol within 1 h after sampling, washed twice in RPMI (Roswell Park Memorial Institute, cell culture medium, ThermoFisher) and adjusted to a target cell concentration of 1 × 107 lymphocytes/ml in medium [RPMI supplemented with 5% fetal calf serum (FCS)] after counting with an automatic hematology analyzer (Sysmex XT-4000i; Sysmex Austria, Vienna, Austria). To reach target concentrations, we upscaled the amount of blood collection tubes for PBMC enrichment dependent on the degree of lymphopenia of individual patients. Serum tubes were centrifuged for 10 min at 2000g and frozen aliquots stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

Flow Cytometry

PBMC were blocked as a 100-µL cell suspension (2.5 × 106 PBMC/ml in flow buffer [phosphate buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 2.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 5% FCS and 2 mM EDTA)] with human FcR Blocking Reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) prior to staining with monoclonal detection antibodies (listed in Supplementary Table 2) for 15 min at ambient temperature protected from light. After adding Lyse-Fix mixture (25 µL undiluted IOTest 3 Fixative Solution in 1 ml VersaLyse; both Beckman Coulter, Vienna, Austria) for another 10 min and a final wash, cells were immediately acquired on a CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter). Flow cytometric data were analyzed using the FCS Express 7 (De Novo Software, CA, USA). The gating strategy is illustrated as Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2.

SARS-CoV-2 Spike-Reactive T Cells

SARS-CoV-2 spike protein-reactive T cells were assessed using the human Prot_S Complete T Cell Analysis Kit (PBMC) of Miltenyi Biotec according to manufacturer’s instructions with following modification. PBMC were seeded at a target concentration of 1 × 106 lymphocytes/100 µL in triplicate into a 96-well plate and rested overnight at 37 °C 5% CO2 under humidified conditions. In brief, for SARS-CoV-2 spike protein reactivity, stimulation was performed with 2 µL of PepTivator Prot S aliquots [6 nmol PepTivator—consisting mainly of 15-mer sequences with 11 amino acids overlap peptides covering the complete protein coding sequence of the spike glycoprotein ("S") of SARS-CoV-2—dissolved in 200 µL sterile water/10% DMSO], and, as negative control, 2 µL of sterile water/10% DMSO were added and, as positive control, 2 µL of CytoStim™. After 2 h, 2 µL Brefeldin A was added and the cells were incubated for another 4 h before being carefully detached by adding PEB (PBS pH 7.2, supplemented with 0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA) and harvested for intracellular cytokine staining. After centrifugation (5 min, 300g) cells were first stained in Viobility 405/452 Fixable Dye, fixed, permeabilized, and finally stained in an 8-color mix containing recombinant (REAfinity™) anti-human monoclonal antibodies against lineage markers [CD3-APC (clone REA613), CD20-VioBlue (clone REA780), CD14-VioBlue (clone REA599), CD4-VioBright B515 (clone REA623), CD8-VioGreen (clone REA734)], activation markers CD154 (CD40L)-APC-Vio 770 (clone REA238) and intracellular cytokines interferon gamma [IFN-γ, IFN-γ-PE (clone REA600)] and tumor necrosis factor alpha [TNF-α, TNF-α-PE-Vio 770 (clone REA 656)] produced in response to the virus-specific peptide stimulation. SARS-CoV-2-reactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were immediately acquired by flow cytometry with a CytoFLEX S (Beckman Coulter).

Anti-SARS-CoV-2-Specific Serostatus

Anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein IgG was measured using the SARS-CoV-2 IgG II Quant assay and anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein IgG was measured using the SARS-CoV-2 IgG assay from thawed serum aliquots on the ARCHITECT i2000sr platform (all Abbott Core Laboratory Systems, Lake Forest, IL, USA). Anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein IgG ≥ 42.3 AU/ml and an anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid IgG Index > 1.4 were rated as positive. The assays were performed in collaboration with the Department of Laboratory Medicine of the Paracelsus Medical University.

CXCL13 ELISA

CXCL13 concentrations were analyzed from thawed serum samples by Euroimmun CXCL13 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s recommendation. This involved a 1:2 dilution of sera in a special sample dilution buffer (ZE 1100-101001; Euroimmun) not provided in the kit. For avoiding inter-assay variabilities, all samples of one patient were assayed in one plate. Photometric measurements (450/620 nm) were made with a Tecan Sunrise™ reader (Tecan Group, Männedorf, Switzerland) and the results multiplied by the dilution factor 2.

Statistical Analyses

Data were checked for consistency and normality using quantile–quantile plots and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Given the limited sample size, we subsequently applied robust nonparametric tests to analyze longitudinal data, even though some markers appeared to be normally distributed. In detail, we applied Friedman’s nonparametric repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s multiple comparisons as post hoc tests for investigating the longitudinal course of cellular and humoral parameters (frequencies of target cells, concentrations of CXCL13, readouts of antibody testing) associated with CXCL13:CXCR5- and SARS-CoV-2-immune activity. The two time-points of controls were compared using Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test. For subgroup analyses, investigating active versus stable MS disease courses, and positive versus negative anti-SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid serostatus at baseline, Mann–Whitney U tests were performed. Contingency table analyses with Fisher’s exact test were used to investigate the distribution of MS courses versus SARS-CoV-2 disease activity and a potential association of grade III lymphopenia with occurrences of breakthrough disease. A p value (two-sided) < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis and graphs were done with GraphPad Prism, Version 10.1.2 (324) (1992–2023 GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Clinical Outcome and Serological Findings of the COVID-era Study Cohort

Performing a real-life study during the COVID-pandemic requires additional considerations. These included that COVID-19 vaccination status (unvaccinated, n = 4; ≥ 3 vaccination, n = 16), vaccination time to study entry (median 169 days, range 9–491) and vaccine types were highly variable. During the study period, nine patients (45%) reported ten breakthrough infections and four patients had recall vaccinations (20%). Controls reported only one breakthrough infection (10%) and four recall vaccinations (40%). but their 3-month involvement in the study was significantly shorter. Furthermore, half of the patients (50%) and all controls (100%) had their measurements completed during the official COVID-19 pandemic. None of the COVID-19 infections was severe requiring hospitalization.

Based on anti-SARS-CoV-2 serostatus testing (discussed below), we were able to also delineate information regarding subclinical COVID infections, which is relevant for the interpretation of our immunological study data. Serological results revealed evidence of COVID infections (nucleocapsid protein IgG index > 1.4) at study entry in seven patients, laboratory-confirmed absence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune responses during OZM treatment in only six patients and one seronegative patient from entry to exit. Serology furthermore confirmed vaccination states, breakthrough infections and recall vaccinations. In controls, too, anti-SARS-CoV-2 serology detected two additional subclinical infections at T2 and matched reports except for one missing anti-spike IgG response to recall vaccination between the two measurements.

During the 1-year observation period 3 of the 20 patients treated with OZM (15%) were diagnosed with a relapse, of whom 2 had to be treated with high-dose cortisone and 1 had an EDSS-progression. New or expanding lesions occurred in 8 patients (40%) and an active lesion in 1 (5%). The cohort hence comprises 11 (55%) NEDA and 9 (45%) EDA patients based on clinical and/or imaging evidence of disease activity.

Since both SARS-CoV-2- and MS-related disease activity involve the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis, we assessed the coincidence of disease conditions and obtained following mosaic: three patients were double-negative, eight patients were single-positive for anti-SARS-CoV-2 responses, three patients were single-positive for MS disease activity, and six patients were double-positive for both (Fig. 1a). The reduction was about 67% and involved remitting grade III lymphopenia (500–200/µL) at least once across all time-points in more than half of the patients (12/20; 60%) but no grade IV lymphopenia (< 200/µL). Selective S1P Receptor Modulation Alters the Composition of Circulating CXCR5+ T and B Cells

To determine whether and to what extent the selective S1P receptor modulator, OZM, exerts any effects on CXCL13:CXCR5 axis-associated immune cells in blood, we performed flow cytometry to systematically monitor CXCR5-expressing T and B cells at 3-month intervals before and on treatment.

We first analyzed parent T cell populations and found a significant decrease of total CD4+ and reciprocal increase of total CD8+ T cell frequencies upon treatment intitiation (Table 1), which is consistent with published data on S1P receptor modulation [22]. For investigating CXCR5+ subsets, we next differentiated regulatory T cells (Treg) according to their CD127lowCD25high phenotype from total CD4+ T cells and found a significant and lasting decline of both, cTfh subset frequencies within (non-Treg) CD4+ T cells and cTfr subset frequencies within Tregs (Fig. 2a, b; Table 1). With declining cTfh and cTfr subset frequencies, the cTfh/cTfr ratio remained unchanged. We further subtyped CD4+ T cells according to chemokine receptors CCR6 (CD196) and CXCR3 (CD183) expression into Th17-like (CD196+CD183−), Th1-like (CD196−CD183+) and Th1.17-like (CD196+CD183+) T cell and cTfh subsets. Our intention was to determine whether selective S1P modulation impacts the redistribution of potential pro-inflammatory CXCR5+ T cell subsets described as B cell helpers for antibody production and roughly related to autoimmunity (Th17-like) or adaptive immune defense (Th1-like) [9, 12]. According to our data, the treatment-mediated decrease in cTfh frequencies did not associate with a pronounced qualitative redistribution within this CXCR5+ T cell subset, except for a slight decrease of Th1.17-like cTfh (Fig. 2d; Table 1).

The majority of cTfh cells usually are in a resting state [23] but activated PD1hiICOShi forms, which are efficient B cell helpers promoting antibody responses upon antigen re-exposure [24] also occur in blood [25]. With the COVID-pandemic present, we finally also investigated activated cTfh cells and found increased PD1hiICOShi cTfh cell frequencies under selective S1P recptor modulation (Fig. 2e; Table 1).

Regarding B cells, we found a treatment-induced decrease of total CD19+ B cell frequencies according to published evidence [26]. This decrease is primarily caused by the predominant fraction of conventional CXCR5+ CD19+ B cells and accompanied by a reciprocal increase of CXCR5low/− CD19+ B cell frequencies (Fig. 2, f–h; Table 1). Beyond comprising CXCR5− plasmablasts, whose frequency distributions remained unchanged under selective S1PR modulation (Fig. 2i; Table 1), the observed increase of circulating CXCR5low/− CD19+ B cell frequencies may indicate a redistribution towards B cells primed for plasma cell differentiation in the blood [27]. We furthermore observed a qualitative change within the subset of conventional CXCR5+ CD19+ B cells toward increased CD27−IgD+ naïve B (Fig. 2j; Table 1) and reciprocal decrease of CD27+IgD+ memory cells.

Selective S1P Receptor Modulation Decreases CXCL13 Levels in Blood

We next investigated the chemokine part of the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis and observed a clear decrease of serum CXCL13 levels after three and six months after treatment initiation (Fig. 2k; Table 1). This effect, however, subsided at T9M and T12M. Serum CXCL13 levels reportedly indicate GC activity in response to infection and vaccination [28] and our cohort of patients treated with OZM comprises patients with subclinical and breakthrough COVID infections. We hence separately analyzed CXCL13 levels in patients with (n = 14) and without (n = 6) anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune activity and found evidence of a decrease in response to selective S1P modulation only in the subgroup without anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune activity (Fig. 2, l, m; Table 1). Since MS disease activity may also affect CXCL13 levels we separately analyzed NEDA (n = 11) and EDA (n = 9) subgroups, which revealed a trend to decreasing CXCL13 in the NEDA group only (Fig. 2, n, o; Table 1).

To exclude subclinical SARS-CoV-2 infection at study entry as covariate of the observed decline, we categorized patients into subgroups with positive (n = 7) versus negative (n = 13) anti-nucleocapsid protein IgG index at T0 and compared (1) baseline CXCL13 levels (negative index group: median 56.5 pg/ml, range 20.4–262.5; positive index group: median 108.3 pg/ml, range 11.5–183.7; p = 0.43) and (2) changes of CXCL13 levels between T0 and T3M (delta negative index group: median 29.2 pg/ml, range – 61.1–94.5; delta positive index group: median 24.8, range -8.6–60.0; p = 0.76) but found no differences. CXCL13 levels furthermore did not differ between patients at study entry (T0; median 77.9 pg/ml, range 11.5–262.5) and controls (T1; median: 85.7 pg/ml, range 18.6–169.9), nor between T1 (median: 85.7 pg/ml, range 18.6–169.9) and T2 (median: 54.1 pg/ml, range 12.0–244.6) of controls.

Selective S1P Modulation Neither Accelerates the Decline nor Dampens SARS-CoV-2-Specific Immune Responses

This comparison is possible, since four controls had had recall vaccinations and two subclinical or reported SARS-CoV-2-related immune activities between the two blood donations.

As outlined above, the dynamics of antibody responses matched well with documented breakthrough infections and recall vaccinations both in patients and controls. Anti-SARS-CoV-2 immunity hence is active, neither accelerated in decline nor depressed during selective S1P receptor modulation.

Less pronounced but also heterogenous were the responses of SARS-CoV-2 ProtS-reactive total CD3+ T cells according to combined analysis of all three investigated representative readouts of T cell activation, i.e., CD154 upregulation and intracellular production of proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IFN-γ (Fig. 3, upper panel c; Table 1). Since CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subpopulations have different roles in adaptive immunity, most simplistically termed “CD4 helpers” and “CD8 killers”, we next investigated ProtS-reactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cell patterns. We focused on recall vaccinations, which we considered as the most comparable medical intervention for COVID-associated immune activation related to ProtS reactivity, and which occurred in equal numbers in patients and controls (n = 4). We furthermore refrained from statistical data interpretation, because the observational character alone is informative, and, in response to recall vaccination, the four patients treated with OZM lacked all three modes of ProtS-reactive CD4+ T cells. Controls showed variable responses and increased frequencies of CD154+, TNF-α+ and IFN-γ+ CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4, middle panel a–c; Table 1). Regarding CD8+ T cells, two patients but not controls showed evidence of ProtS-reactive CD154+CD8+ T cell frequencies. Moreover, patients showed a robust upregulation and similar patterns of reactive TNF-α+ and IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cell frequencies, which was less the case in controls and probably represents a compensation mechanism (Fig. 4, lower panel a–c; Table 1).

We hence selected for measurements performed after evidence of COVID-related immune activities (breakthrough infections, subclinical infections, recall vaccinations) and compared those with the medians of measurements from the samples of T3M-T12M of patients without.

We have no explanation for the variabilities observed in several of the other parameters which may relate to any immune activation, from unreported or subclinical infection to allergy or MS disease activity, reflecting the variability of individual immune systems. Hence, the factor “individual patient” is very well exemplified by the exceptional courses of several parameters (marked as red dots in several plots) of a patient who had breakthrough infection at T6M on top of a stable MS disease course (group NEDA). Discussion

We have investigated whether selective S1P receptor modulation with OZM, beyond causing peripheral lymphopenia, also affects the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis-associated components in blood, which could translate to blunted humoral or cellular anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune responses and MS disease activity.

The rise of the COVID-19-pandemic was the time when concerns regarding lymphocyte-depleting agents went viral based on early reports that the majority of COVID-patients had lymphopenia [30]. Consequentially, recommendations to temporarily delay these drugs were issued [31]. At that time, it was already well understood that S1P1 receptor modulation leads to internalization of the S1P receptor expressed on lymphocytes [32], and hence limits their mobilization, specifically the (re-)egress from circulating lymphocytes from lymph nodes into the peripheral blood.

In contrast to the pivotal Phase III clinical studies [33, 34] reporting a lesser extent lymphopenia of about 60%, we observed a decrease of roughly 70% in our patients treated with OZM. This was more then the pronounced lymphopenia typically seen in patients treated with FTY and similar to other post-marketing clinical studies involving OZM [26, 35, 36].

Although lymphopenia is generally associated with increased infection risk [37], no one in our cohort had a severe course of the breakthrough SARS CoV-2-infections while on treatment, which is in line with recently published studies [15, 38]. This raises the question regarding the underlying immunology of S1P receptor modulation apparently allowing for both sufficient immune defense for controlling SARS-CoV-2 infection and MS activity. The reason why selective and non-selective S1P receptor modulators might differ with regard to more or less blunted antibody responses likely relates to the S1P receptor biology itself. In contrast to FTY, OZM does not bind to S1P3 and S1P4, which are both widely expressed on various immune cells, including B cells and dendritic cells, and possibly involved in antibody-based immunology [15].

The CXCL13:CXCR5–chemokine axis and associated immune activities are likely involved in both SARS-CoV-2 immune defense and controlling MS activity, and hence the center of our investigations. Our data show that treatment with OZM alters the circulating CXCR5+ T and B cell repertoire and leads to decreased serum CXCL13 levels. The observed changes in peripheral blood were not associated with blunted anti-COVID immune defense as both anti-SARS-CoV-2-antibody and Prot S-reactive T cell responses remained dynamical and apparently intact in comparison with controls.

Our immune phenotyping results describing compositional changes, i.e., frequency distributions instead of absolute counts[26, 36], add the following pieces of knowledge about the impact of S1P receptor modulation in peripheral blood. First, they allow direct comparisons with treatment effects described for the non-selective S1P modulator FTY, showing that OZM-mediated decreases in total CD4+ T cell frequencies, reciprocal increases of total CD8+ T cell frequencies, and decreases in B cell frequencies are similar [22, 39]. Second, we provide first insights into the redistribution of CXCR5+ T and B cells under selective S1P modulation, which partly deviate from that of FTY described by Huber et al. [22]. Whereas both substances decrease cTfh, cTfr, cTf8, and memory B cell frequencies and increase activated cTfh and naïve B cell frequencies [22, 40], the effect of OZM on the redistribution within cTfh cells is less pronounced than that of FTY. We observed a decrease only in the least abundant cTfh1.17 subset, which did not cause a further redistribution within the other subsets. In contrast, FTY affected a pronounced increase of cTfh1 cells with a concomitant reciprocal decrease of both cTfh17 and cTfh1.17 cell frequencies [22]. Since cTfh1 T cells are non-efficient B cell helpers [9, 12], the lacking shift together with the observed increase of circulating CXCR5− B cells (comprising plasmablasts and B cells presumably primed for plasma cell differentiation [27]) may explain why anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibody responses in our cohort of patients treated with OZM were not blunted. As circulating counterparts capable of efficient B cell help in recall antibody responses [12, 41, 42] cTfh cells represent a long-term and stable memory pool [41], and may disseminate pathogen-specific adaptive humoral immune responses across SLO.

Investigating cellular anti-SARS-CoV-2 reactivity against the spike protein showed that total CD3+ T cells of patients treated with OZM responded to activation with ProtS-derived peptides, although activated CD154+ T cell frequencies declined. Separate analysis of CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses from recall vaccinated patients and controls identified lack of CD154 upregulation of CD4+ T cells responsible for the decline, which has also been described in patients with MS treated with FTY [43]. According to our data, CD4+ T cells of patients with MS also lacked TNF-α and IFN-γ responses, which was neither the case in recall vaccinated controls nor in studies with patients with COVID-19 [44]. Instead, the robust response in cytokine producing ProtS-reactive CD8+ T cells relative to controls seems to at least partially substitute for the failure of reactive CD4+ T cells in blood. Notably, CD8+ T cells responded by partly upregulating CD154, a key costimulatory molecule also known as CD40L of CD4+ T cells, which reportedly only occurs in cases when CD4+ T cell help is compromised [45]. Alternatively, instead of failure, the SARS-CoV-2-reactive CD4+ T cell fraction may have remained sequestered in the lymph nodes capable of responding from within there, whereas the exceedingly robust ProtS-reactive CD8+ T cell response in blood compared to controls may have resulted from a treatment-mediated predominance of circulating SARS-CoV-2 specific effector memory CD8+ T cells in blood. Importantly, neither explanation precludes the existence of an effective anti-SAR-CoV-2T cell defense and might explain why FTY treatment is also not associated with a worse prognosis following SARS-CoV-2 infection [15].

The answer of whether the observed OZM effects on circulating CXCL13:CXCR5-associated compounds are associated with a reduced MS disease activity or decelerated disease progression is speculative, though probably it is yes. After all, reduced frequencies of circulating CXCR5+ lymphocytes implicate fewer opportunities for contributing to meningeal inflammation, and lower serum CXCL13 levels may indicate dampened autoreactive GC activity. Increased CXCL13 levels in the blood of patients with MS compared to controls are fairly established across several studies and corroborate the latter assumption [46, 47]. The published data on immunomodulatory MS treatments affecting blood CXCL13 levels is, however, rare and less conclusive. Two studies reported that FTY does not alter CXCL13 concentrations after 1 year and 6 months, respectively [39, 48]. Here, we present data of decreasing CXCL13 serum levels under selective S1P receptor modulation. As the decline is transient, we propose that infection-related immune activation due to three recall vaccinations and three (subclinical) breakthrough infections at T9M caused the decrease to subside. Infection-related immune activation may similarly also explain why CXCL13 levels of untreated patients and controls were about the same.

This brings us to the limitations which primarily relate to the immune biological challenge posed on “cross-investigating” treatment effects on circulating parameters of the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis in MS against the COVID-19-pandemic background. Crucially involved in adaptive humoral immune responses, CXCL13-associated immune activities are relevant to both but unspecific. Whereas 20 patients generally is a reasonable cohort size for longitudinal studies, we did not anticipate the additional layer of complexity due to recall vaccinations and breakthrough infections resulting in a mosaic of patient subgroups with MS and/or anti-SARS-CoV-2 immune activities. This is further topped by additional unknown effects related to an exceptional individual immune activity or other subclinical infections and methodological issues related to low cell numbers for subset analyses. The availability of sufficient cells also for in vitro activation experiments under S1P receptor modulation is difficult and perhaps a confounding factor that T cell responses under FTY reportedly are impaired [49].

Accordingly, our longitudinal data appear quite heterogenous and we cannot explain all of it, as exemplified by the one individual with the exceptional but interrelated data. We furthermore show that underlying anti-SARS-CoV-2-related immune activity may contribute to increased anti-spike IgG, SARS-CoV-2-reactive T cells and cTfh1.17 frequencies, which is biologically meaningful. Finally, for detecting true differences but at the expense of sensitivity, we used non-parametric statistical methods together with conservative post hoc tests. Being so much on the safe side, we might have missed the opportunity to present significant differences, as is the case regarding the reported trend instead of a definitive decrease of CXCL13 levels in the NEDA subgroup.

Conclusion

Taken together, our findings show that selective S1P receptor modulation with OZM affects the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis by altering the composition of CXCR5+ T and B cells and CXCL13 levels in blood. The T and B cell changes are similar to those reported for FTY except for the shift from Tfh17 towards Tfh1 cells. This difference within the cTfh subset may add a piece to the puzzle regarding blunted antibody responses under FTY, corroborated by our data that anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibody responses appeared intact under selective S1P modulation with OZM. Together with the robust response of SARS-CoV-2-reactive CD8+ T cells possibly compensating for the lack of ProtS-reactive CD4+ T cells, this may represent the cellular basis why the course of COVID infections under OZM was not more severe. In conclusion, OZM treatment affects the CXCL13:CXCR5 axis with a possibly modulating effect on MS activity, but without interfering with adaptive immune defense and immune surveillance against viral infections.
