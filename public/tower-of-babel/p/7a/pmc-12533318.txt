Efficacy of hydration combined with rectal non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) versus NSAIDs alone for the prevention of post-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography pancreatitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Huimin Li, Changfeng Miao, Wei Fang, Yuan Deng, Xiong Li, Ting Du, Hongwei Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe most prevalent complication of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is post-ERCP acute pancreatitis (PEP). Both hydration and rectal non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can effectively prevent PEP; however, there is currently no consensus on whether combination therapy is more effective than either intervention alone.MethodsThe PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, Embase and Cochrane Library (last updated in October 2024) were systematically searched to identify and screen potentially eligible studies, from which relevant data were extracted, Review Manager 5.3 was used for meta-analysis to determine the preventive utility of hydration + rectal NSAIDs for PEP.ResultsFive studies comprising 1344 patients were included in the meta-analysis. Patients were divided into 2 groups (hydration + NSAIDs [n=654] and NSAIDs alone [n=690], a total of 107 patients experienced PEP (hydration + NSAIDs [n=40] and NSAIDs alone [n=67]). Results of meta-analysis revealed no significant difference in the incidence of PEP between patients receiving rectal NSAIDs + hydration versus rectal NSAIDs alone (odds ratio [OR] 0.57, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.25–1.30; P = 0.18).ConclusionCompared with rectal NSAIDs alone, the combination of hydration and rectal NSAIDs does not appear to have a significant synergistic effect in the prevention of PEP. However, considering their distinct mechanisms of action and the observed reduction in PEP incidence with combined use, we believe that this approach still holds considerable clinical relevance. Further large-scale studies are needed to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04192-4.

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) has become the standard surgical procedure for most pancreaticobiliary tract diseases for which the most prevalent complication is post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP), with a reported incidence of 2%−15% [1–3]. Although most cases PEP are only of mild or moderate severity, the occurrence of severe and/or necrotizing pancreatitis can lead to severe morbidity and mortality. In the United States, annual medical costs attributed to PEP are as high as $150 million [3, 4]. The mechanism of PEP may include mechanical, hydrostatic, chemical, microbial, neuronal, thermal and electrical injury [2, 5].

Presently, the most commonly used methods for the prevention of PEP include the implantation of pancreatic duct stents, rectal NSAIDs and hydration [5–7]. However, there are difficulties and complications associated with the placement of pancreatic duct stents, in addition to high cost [5]. Several studies have reported that rectal NSAIDs effectively reduce the incidence of PEP by inhibit inflammation [3, 5, 8, 9]. Even among high-risk patients, the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) recommends that all patients routinely use rectal NSAIDs before ERCP [3]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that active hydration can improve blood perfusion and stabilize pancreatic microcirculation by weakening tissue acidification, thereby improving patient’s prognosis and reducing the incidence of PEP [5, 7, 10, 11].

Interestingly, although rectal NSAIDs and hydration reduce the risk of PEP through different mechanisms, there is currently no consensus on whether combination therapy is more effective than either intervention alone. Many studies do not recommend combining rectal NSAIDs and hydration to reduce the incidence of PEP [5, 6]. In contrast, however, some studies have suggested that the combination of rectal NSAIDs and hydration is more effective [12, 13]. In view of this controversy, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of the existing literature to re-evaluate whether the combined use of rectal NSAIDs and hydration can effectively reduce PEP.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

Using a combination of Mesh words and free words, 2 authors independently searched PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, Embase and Cochrane Library for relevant studies published up to October 2024. Keywords used in the literature search included (“Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography” OR “ERCP”), (“Pancreatitis”), (“Hydration” OR “Normal Saline” OR “Ringer’s lactate” OR “Ringer’s solution” OR “Lactated Ringer”), (“nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications” OR “NSAIDs”). The details of the search strategies are presented in Appendix 1.

Eligibility and exclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria for potentially eligible studies were as follows: (a) All patients underwent ERCP, and the effects of hydration combined with rectal NSAIDs and rectal NSAIDs alone on PEP were compared; (b) All patients were adults; (c) Relevant raw data, more specifically, the number of patients with PEP in the NSAIDs group and the NSAIDs + hydration group were reported; (d) When data appears in multiple articles, the most detailed or up-to-date were included; (e) Hydration as encompassing both aggressive hydration and standard hydration/maintenance intravenous fluids; (f) All researches were randomized controlled trials (RCT). Duplicate studies, reviews, trial registrations, wall newsletters, case reports, conference summaries, animal studies, meta-analysis, letters and other irrelevant studies were excluded.

Data extraction

The 2 authors (H.M.L. and C.F.M.) designed and unified the format of the data extraction tables, which was then used by both to extract the following data from all included studies: author(s), year of publication, country, type of study (randomized controlled trial or retrospective study), number of patients, age, sex ratio, NSAIDs used, type of hydrated fluid, number of cases of pancreatic duct intubation, pancreatic duct stent, sphincterotomy, difficult intubation and sphincter of Oddi dysfunction (SOD), operative Duration; trial scheme; the number of PEP cases in the 2 groups, and definition of PEP. Any differences were resolved by consensus discussion with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using RevMan version 5.3. The measure of association was the odds ratio (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The DerSimonian and Laird method was used to pool dichotomous outcomes. In accordance with the latest Cochrane guidelines for meta-analyses, a random-effects model was applied for the analysis of outcomes. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Finally, a forest plot was created by meta-analysis of the occurrence of PEP using Review Manager 5.3, and the results were obtained.

Risk of bias assessment

Two authors (H.M.L. and C.F.M.) assessed the quality of all included studies using Review Manager 5.3, and assessed the risk of bias of the included randomized controlled trials using the Version 2 of the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2).

The level of evidence

The certainty of the overall body of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. The assessment covered five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The certainty of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Definition of PEP

The PEP standards included in the studies included the Cotton’s Consensus Criteria or the Atlanta criteria. Cotton’s Consensus Criteria from 1991 defines PEP as new onset or increased upper abdominal pain, and pancreatic amylase (or lipase) elevation of 3 times greater than the upper limit of normal at 24 h after ERCP and resulting hospitalization or prolongation of ongoing hospitalization of 2 or more nights [14]. The Atlanta classification of acute pancreatitis (AP) updated in 2012 defines PEP as the presence of 2 of the following 3 criteria: (1) abdominal pain consistent with AP (acute onset of a persistent, severe, epigastric pain often radiating to the back); (2) serum lipase (or amylase) level at least 3 times greater than the upper limit of normal; and (3) characteristic findings of AP on contrast-enhanced computed tomography or MRI or transabdominal ultrasonography [15].

Results

Study selection

The systematic literature search of PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase databases retrieved 646 potentially relevant studies, of which 250 were Duplicated and excluded. Further screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts resulted in exclusion of 386 studies for the following reason: (1) unrelated studies (n=239); (2) animal studies (n=10); (3) reviews, trial registrations, wall newsletters, case reports, conference summaries, animal studies, meta-analysis, letters (n=136); (4) the raw data (number of PEP patients in the NSAIDs and NSAIDs + hydration groups) could not be extracted (n=2). (5) non RCTs (n = 3). Ultimately, 5 studies fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis. Findings

The 5 eligible studies comprised 1344 patients, with 654 in the NSAIDs + hydration group and 690 in the NSAIDs alone group. A total of 107 patients had PEP, with 40 in the NSAIDs + hydration group and 67 in the NSAIDs alone groups. Results of meta-analysis revealed a no significant difference in the incidence of PEP between rectal NSAIDs + hydration and rectal NSAIDs alone groups (OR 0.57, 95%CI 0.25–1.30; P = 0.18). Discussion

Many studies and meta-analysis have confirmed that rectal NSAIDs and liquid hydration significantly prevent PEP [3, 19–21]. However, research specifically focusing on the combination of these two interventions remains relatively limited. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive search of all available RCTs and performed a high-quality meta-analysis to address this clinical question. Our findings indicate that, although hydration and rectal administration of NSAIDs act through different mechanisms to prevent PEP, their combined use does not appear to confer a significant synergistic effect in reducing the incidence of PEP (OR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.25–1.30; P = 0.18).

Sharma et al. [17] proposed that, based on the theory that early disturbances in pancreatic microcirculatory perfusion are associated with the severity of acute pancreatitis, aggressive fluid resuscitation During the early phase of the disease can significantly prevent clinical deterioration. The benefit of intensive hydration appears to be most pronounced within the first 24 h of acute pancreatitis, as proinflammatory cytokines are believed to induce various physiological changes that lead to impaired pancreatic perfusion. Furthermore, most patients undergoing ERCP are required to fast for at least 8 to 12 h prior to the procedure, and this relative volume depletion may increase their susceptibility to PEP [22]. Buxbaum et al. [23] conducted a meta-analysis including 13 studies comparing the incidence of PEP between aggressive and standard hydration groups. Their findings showed that aggressive hydration significantly reduced the overall risk of PEP (OR 0.47; 95% CI, 0.34–0.66). Similarly, Radadiya et al. [24] performed a meta-analysis that included only RCTs, comparing aggressive versus standard hydration. The results also demonstrated a significant reduction in the overall risk of PEP with aggressive hydration (OR 0.44; 95% CI, 0.28–0.69; P = 0.0004).

In terms of pharmacological prophylaxis for PEP, NSAIDs are currently the most widely and evidence-based method [25–27]. A landmark study conducted by Elmunzer et al. [28] in 2012 provided strong evidence supporting the efficacy of rectal NSAIDs in preventing PEP, corroborating findings from numerous prior randomized controlled trials. The administration of a 100 mg suppository of indomethacin or diclofenac rectally within 30 min prior to the procedure has since become the standard of care, and is recommended by all major international professional societies [28].

Previous studies have suggested that NSAIDs may prevent PEP by inhibiting prostaglandins and phospholipase A2, both of which are considered to play key roles in the pathogenesis of PEP [1, 29]. In contrast, the mechanism of hydration is believed to involve improvement of pancreatic microcirculation. Makhzangy et al. [12] proposed that intravenous fluid administration can replenish intravascular volume, reduce drug concentrations in the blood, and enhance tissue perfusion, thereby preventing systemic inflammatory response syndrome, multiple organ failure, and reducing mortality. Therefore, hydration and rectal NSAIDs exert their prophylactic effects against PEP through fundamentally different mechanisms. Currently, there is no clear evidence or rationale to suggest that their effects negate one another. Our meta-analysis showed that although the combination did not reach statistical significance compared to NSAIDs alone, it was associated with a lower incidence of PEP.

Several previous studies have suggested that the combination of intravenous hydration and rectal NSAIDs is effective in reducing the incidence of PEP, although the underlying mechanism remains unclear [12, 13]. In contrast, a network meta-analysis [27] by Akshintala et al. reported that among rectal NSAIDs, pancreatic duct stenting, intravenous hydration, and their combinations, rectal diclofenac alone demonstrated the greatest efficacy in preventing PEP. They further proposed that combination prophylaxis might be more effective; however, supporting evidence for this approach is currently very limited.

It should also be noted that the risk factors of PEP depend not only on the ERCP procedure, but also on the patient condition. Some studies [3, 30] have reported a significant difference in the incidence of PEP between low-risk and high-risk patients. Pasha et al. [3]. reported that the incidence of PEP is 2–10% among patients receiving ERCP, but it will rise to 8–40% in high-risk groups, and that patient-related risk factors include age less than 60 years old, previous history of PEP, normal serum bilirubin level, and SOD. As a result, Pasha et al. [3] conducted a single-center randomized clinical trial in 2020 with 281 patients who were divided into high-risk and a low-risk groups, suggesting that hydration combined with NSAIDs had a synergistic effect on patients in the high-risk group, but not in the low-risk group. We could not agree more with the results of Pasha et al. [3], unfortunately, however, all the studies we included did not classify patients risk factors, as such, we were unable to perform further subgroup analysis to further study the significance of hydration combined with NSAIDs for the prevention of PEP in high-risk or low-risk groups. Consequently, we call for more studies investigating the prevention of PEP and grouping patients according to the risk of PEP. The results of a network meta-analysis by Du et al. also showed that for high-risk patients, hydration combined with NSAIDs may be the best prevention and treatment of PEP [31].

We acknowledge the limitations of our meta-analysis included the following: First, the types of NSAIDs (including diclofenac or indomethacin) and the hydrated liquids (including normal saline and Ringer’s lactate) and intervention of hydrated were different among the included studies. Second, to enhance the quality of evidence in this meta-analysis, we included only RCTs, however, this strict inclusion criterion resulted in a limited number of eligible studies and an insufficient overall sample size. In addition, the definition of PEP included in our meta-analysis is also different, including both Cotton’s Consensus Criteria and Atlanta criteria, which may lead to potential selection bias.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the combination of intravenous hydration and rectal NSAIDs does not appear to exert a significant synergistic effect in the prevention of PEP. However, given their distinct mechanisms of action and the observed reduction in PEP incidence with combined use, this approach may still hold meaningful clinical value. Further large-scale studies are warranted to confirm these results.
