Loss of tissue-state plasticity: a conceptual framework for age-associated regenerative failure
Authors: Takayoshi Otsuka, Hideaki Matsui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTissue regeneration depends on the activation of resident stem and progenitor cells, dynamic interactions with the extracellular matrix (ECM), and tightly regulated growth factor signaling, including fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β). Numerous therapeutic strategies have aimed to enhance repair through exogenous growth factor delivery or stem cell transplantation. However, despite the presence of abundant regenerative signals, including those derived from the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), regenerative capacity declines with age across multiple organs. This discrepancy suggests that age-related regenerative decline reflects not a reduction in signal availability, but a profound alteration in how tissues interpret and respond to those signals. Here, we propose a conceptual framework centered on the concept of “tissue state”—a metastable, tissue-wide organizational mode that determines how incoming signals are interpreted and executed. Tissue state is shaped by the integration of cellular composition, ECM properties, inflammatory context, and intracellular stress responses. Within this framework, exogenous signals are interpreted as context-dependent inputs, and their biological outcomes are determined by the pre-existing tissue state. We further discuss how this perspective reshapes our understanding of regenerative medicine, suggesting that restoring tissue-state flexibility may be a more effective therapeutic goal than simply augmenting signal availability.Keywords: Tissue-state plasticity, Aging, Tissue regeneration, Fibrosis, FGF/TGF-β signaling

Background

Tissue regeneration is a fundamental biological process that preserves organ integrity and homeostasis throughout life. In many adult tissues, regenerative responses depend on the activation of resident stem and progenitor cells, dynamic interactions with the extracellular matrix (ECM), and tightly regulated growth factor signaling pathways [1–3]. Disruption of resident stem cells impairs tissue repair, as shown by the genetic ablation of satellite cells in the skeletal muscle [4, 5]. In parallel, ECM components directly or indirectly bind to growth factors, thereby shaping the spatial and temporal dynamics of their signaling during tissue regeneration [2, 6–8]. Within this framework, regeneration has been largely conceptualized as a growth factor-driven process, in which signals such as fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) orchestrate cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation [9]. FGFs play essential roles in tissue growth and repair, whereas TGF-β signaling is a key regulator of tissue remodeling and fibrosis [10–12]. Because growth factor signaling can be directly manipulated by exogenous administration, inhibition, or genetic modulation, it serves as a practical experimental approach for investigating regenerative responses in various tissues and model systems. Consequently, the identification of “regenerative factors” has led to numerous therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing tissue repair through the delivery, activation, or modulation of growth factors and/or stem cells in experimental models and clinical settings [13–19].

Although growth factor-based models have exerted significant conceptual influence and have been extensively investigated in preclinical studies, regenerative outcomes decline with age in various tissues and organisms [20]. Aging is consistently associated with delayed wound healing, impaired tissue repair, and progressive fibrotic remodeling in multiple organs, including the liver, muscle, skin, vessels, small intestine, lungs, and heart, which are conserved hallmarks of organismal aging [21, 22]. Among the hallmarks of aging, regenerative decline is frequently associated with stem cell dysfunction and persistent inflammatory signaling, both of which collectively reshape the tissue environment in aged organisms. Importantly, these age-associated regenerative defects cannot be attributed to the absence of regenerative signals. Aged tissues frequently exhibit chronic activation of inflammatory pathways and increased secretion of growth factors and cytokines as part of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which includes factors classically implicated in tissue repair [23–26]. Moreover, the transient appearance of senescent-like cells can be beneficial during tissue regeneration [27–29]. Together, these findings reveal a recurring feature of aging, in which regenerative signals remain present, but tissues progressively lose the capacity to interpret and execute these cues as effective regenerative programs. This suggests that age-related regenerative decline reflects not a reduction in signal availability, but an alteration in tissue responsiveness and flexibility of tissue-state transitions.

This observation underscores the importance of considering the intrinsic tissue condition in which signals are interpreted. It is well established that identical extracellular signals can elicit distinct, and sometimes opposing, cellular responses depending on cellular context, developmental stage, and environmental conditions. Such context dependency has been widely documented across signaling pathways central to regeneration, including FGF, TGF-β, and inflammatory cytokine signaling [30–33]. Aged tissues undergo significant alterations in intracellular stress responses, ECM composition and mechanics, immune cell composition, and inflammatory signaling [21, 34, 35]. Under such conditions, growth factor signaling should not be considered solely in terms of signal presence or intensity; rather, the same cues may be interpreted in fundamentally different ways depending on the context. Consequently, “regenerative factors” may acquire qualitatively different biological effects in aged contexts, favoring maladaptive outcomes such as fibrosis, chronic inflammation, or failed repair, rather than effective regeneration.

These considerations necessitate a fundamental reconceptualization of how growth factor signaling is interpreted in the context of tissue regeneration and aging. In this review, we introduce the concept of “tissue state” to describe a tissue-wide functional mode in which incoming signals are interpreted and executed. We first revisit the classical growth factor-based views of FGF and TGF-β signaling as representative examples and examine how their biological outcomes are shaped by the pre-existing tissue state. We then discuss how aging progressively restricts the landscape of accessible tissue states. Finally, we consider the implications of this view for regenerative medicine, suggesting that effective therapeutic strategies may benefit from addressing not only signal availability but also the restoration of tissue responsiveness and state plasticity.

The concept of “tissue state”: from cellular plasticity to tissue-level functional modes

Biological responses within the same tissue are not fixed but vary dramatically depending on physiological conditions. This fundamental plasticity raises the question of what determines how tissues interpret and respond to the same molecular signals in different contexts. Skin wound healing provides a clear example of this phenomenon. After injury, the tissue enters a highly responsive mode characterized by overlapping and partially sequential phases, including hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, re-epithelialization, matrix synthesis, contraction, and remodeling [36–38]. In contrast, under homeostatic conditions, the same tissue often lacks the competence to actively engage in regenerative programs, whereas chronic inflammatory contexts tend to bias repair toward fibrotic stabilization rather than effective regeneration [39–41]. These differences do not reflect simple binary switches between the “active” and “inactive” states. Rather, they reflect the transitions between distinct functional modes that shape how tissues interpret and execute incoming signals. This view motivates the need to conceptualize tissues not as static entities but as dynamic systems capable of occupying and transitioning between multiple functional states [42, 43]—a concept we term “tissue state.”

In this review, we define the “tissue state” as a metastable, tissue-wide organizational mode that biases the system toward specific functional outputs, such as regenerative, adaptive, or fibrotic states. This concept is rooted in Waddington’s epigenetic landscape, in which cell fates are represented as attractor-like states that are stable over time but reversible under appropriate perturbations [44, 45]. In cell fate commitment, biological systems often occupy stable or semi-stable states rather than instantaneously switching between discrete states [46, 47]. Importantly, aging is associated with epigenetic dysregulation that progressively reshapes this landscape, leading to restricted transitions [48, 49]. Extending these principles beyond individual cells, we propose that tissue-level configurations, such as adaptive, regenerative, and fibrotic states, can be viewed as attractor-like states of the interacting multicellular network [50]. The tissue state is therefore not reducible to individual constituent cells but instead emerges as a collective property arising from cellular composition, intercellular interactions, extracellular matrix architecture, and inflammatory context [51, 52]. Consistent with this multi-scale perspective, experimental studies on skin repair have demonstrated that fibrotic or regenerative outcomes are strongly influenced by the composition of distinct fibroblast lineages, wound microenvironment, and inflammatory conditions, rather than being uniformly determined by growth factor signaling alone [41, 53–55]. Therefore, the concept of a metastable tissue state provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how identical signals can induce varying biological responses during tissue repair, depending on the pre-existing tissue context.

Tissue state is determined by the integration of multiple intrinsic and extrinsic tissue components, each contributing distinct yet interconnected regulatory dimensions. First, cellular composition critically influences tissue behavior. The abundance and functional state of resident stem/progenitor cells determine regenerative capacity, whereas the composition of immune cell subsets, particularly the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophage populations, shapes the direction of tissue responses [56–58]. Second, ECM provides structural support as well as mechanical and biochemical cues that control tissue responses. Changes in ECM stiffness and architecture have been shown to alter cell fate decisions and the activation state of stem/progenitor cells, with aged or fibrotic ECM constraining regenerative potential [59–61]. Third, the quality and duration of inflammation play a decisive role; while acute, transient inflammation can promote regeneration, chronic inflammatory states shift tissue responses toward fibrosis and stabilization [35, 62–65]. Furthermore, intracellular stress responses, including the integrated stress response (ISR), unfolded protein response (UPR), and persistent DNA damage signaling, modulate how cells interpret extracellular cues, often constraining proliferative and regenerative outputs under aged or stressed conditions [66–70]. These components do not act independently but mutually reinforce their effects. Collectively, these interconnected factors define how incoming signals, including growth factors, are decoded and executed, thereby shaping the functional tissue state and its regenerative potential [43].

Several interventional studies provide direct experimental support for the concept that the tissue state governs regenerative outcomes. At the level of ECM mechanics, the aged progenitor cell niche stiffens, and this change alone is sufficient to impair oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, whereas culturing aged cells on substrates that mimic the softness of young tissue rejuvenates them [71]. At the level of intracellular stress, age-related failure of autophagy drives muscle stem cells into senescence through loss of proteostasis and oxidative stress, and re-establishing autophagy reverses senescence and restores their regenerative function [72]. In each case, identical cells yielded divergent regenerative outcomes depending on the state of the surrounding tissue, supporting the tissue state as a determinant of how regenerative programs are executed.

Differences in tissue state fundamentally determine how incoming signals are interpreted and executed at the tissue level. This concept is not intended to replace established frameworks such as the stem cell niche and the tissue microenvironment, but rather to integrate and extend them by adding a dynamic, tissue-level perspective. The niche refers to a localized anatomical structure that supports stem cell maintenance [73, 74], and the microenvironment broadly describes the surrounding conditions of individual cells [75]. These concepts are fundamentally limited to spatial and compositional descriptions. In other words, while they identify which cells and signals are present and where they are located, they do not explain why the same set of components and signals can lead to different biological outcomes. This limitation is evident in aged tissues, where regenerative signals remain abundant yet fail to drive repair [76, 77]. The tissue state addresses this gap by describing not the structural substrate on which signals act, but the dynamic functional mode that determines how they are decoded and whether the tissue can transition among regenerative, adaptive, and fibrotic configurations. In this review, we use “plasticity” to refer to the overall capacity of a tissue to occupy and transition among multiple functional states, and “flexibility” to describe the ease or reversibility of individual state transitions. Aging profoundly alters both the accessibility and reversibility of these states, such that signals that promote regeneration in young tissues may instead drive fixed, fibrotic outcomes in aged contexts (Fig. 1).

Tissue-state-dependent interpretation of regenerative signaling. Tissues occupy multiple metastable states—regenerative, adaptive, and fibrotic—that constrain the interpretation of signaling inputs. Under homeostasis, both young and aged tissues reside in an adaptive state, but they differ in their overall capacity to access and transition between states. In young tissues (high plasticity), the adaptive state remains broadly and reversibly connected to the regenerative state; therefore, appropriate signals readily drive the adaptive-to-regenerative transition and effective repair. In aged tissues (low plasticity), this transition loses flexibility, and the regenerative state becomes less accessible, biasing responses toward the fibrotic state. Thus, regenerative signaling acts as a contextual input rather than an instructive command

Classical views on FGF and TGF-β signaling

FGF and TGF-β signaling are used here as representative examples for developing the tissue-state framework. This perspective is equally applicable to other repair-associated signals, including Wnt, BMP, and inflammatory cytokines [31, 33, 78]. We chose these two because they offer a particularly clear illustration of state-dependent signal interpretation. Both are well-established context-dependent signals tightly coupled to the components that define the tissue state, such as the ECM and intracellular stress responses [31, 32]. In the classical view, tissue repair is initiated and driven by the induction of these growth factors to activate resident stem and progenitor cells, and regenerative failure is attributed to their insufficient availability or impaired activation.

Fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling is classically characterized as a proregenerative pathway that promotes cell proliferation, survival, and tissue growth. FGFs have been established as mitogenic and morphogenetic signals acting through FGFR tyrosine kinases to activate MAPK, PI3K–AKT, and PLCγ pathways [10]. In zebrafish and amphibian models, FGF signaling is indispensable for regeneration of various organs [79–84]. In mammalian skeletal muscle, loss of the muscle-enriched ligand FGF6 resulted in delayed myofiber regeneration and increased fibrotic remodeling following injury [85]. In the skin, FGF7 has been shown to promote keratinocyte proliferation and re-epithelialization during wound healing [86]. Moreover, genetic disruption of FGFR signaling in epidermal keratinocytes has been shown to impair re-epithelialization and delay wound closure in vivo [87].

Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling is known as a key regulator of tissue remodeling through its effects on extracellular matrix production, immune modulation, and cell fate control [88, 89]. Similar to FGF signaling, TGF-β signaling is required during regeneration of various organs in zebrafish, Xenopus, and axolotl [90–96]. In wound repair, transient TGF-β activation contributes to wound closure by promoting fibroblast activation, matrix deposition, and epithelial migration [97–99]. In another study, keratinocyte-specific FOXO1 deletion resulted in reduced migration, reduced TGF-β1 expression, and increased apoptosis, leading to delayed wound closure, which was rescued by exogenous TGF-β1 [100].

Taken together, these studies support a model in which regeneration is driven by the activation of growth factor signaling pathways, with FGF and TGF-β acting as indispensable mediators of tissue repair. This view has provided a powerful conceptual and experimental framework, motivating numerous preclinical and clinical efforts aimed at enhancing regeneration through exogenous growth factor delivery or pathway modulation [18, 101–103]. However, activated FGF and TGF-β signaling does not guarantee successful regeneration. In aged skeletal muscle, FGF signaling remains active within the niche, but satellite cell behavior becomes dysregulated, and regenerative output is compromised [76, 104]. Likewise, sustained or excessive TGF-β signaling acts as a driver of fibrotic tissue formation rather than functional tissue restoration [105–107].

Tissue-state constraints on signal interpretation

These findings indicate that signal activation alone is insufficient to determine regenerative outcomes. We propose that these context-dependent effects are regulated by the pre-existing tissue state, such as cellular stress, ECM stiffness, and epigenetic modification, which, in turn, constrain the interpretation of signals at the tissue level. By considering growth factor signals not as simply promoters or inhibitors of regeneration but as inputs acting on different tissue states, we can better explain how identical signals can lead to varied outcomes (Fig. 2).

Tissue-state-dependent interpretation of FGF and TGF-β signaling. Identical signaling inputs (FGF, TGF-β, and other growth factors) induce distinct biological outcomes, depending on the pre-existing tissue state. In young, high-plasticity tissues, these inputs are interpreted as regenerative, driving cell proliferation and functional repair (regenerative output). In aged, low-plasticity tissues, the same inputs lead to growth restraint, fibrosis, and failed repair (maladaptive output). Thus, growth factor signals function as context-dependent inputs, and their biological outcomes are determined not only by ligand identity but also by the plasticity of recipient tissues

Activation of key stress pathways, such as the ISR and p53 signaling, diverts cells from proliferative and regenerative programs. ISR is mediated by the phosphorylation of eIF2α, which suppresses global protein synthesis while selectively inducing ATF4 [108]. ATF4-dependent transcriptional programs support pro-survival and homeostatic programs while chronic ISR leads to a pro-apoptotic program [108–110]. ISR activation induces cell cycle arrest through ATF4-dependent upregulation of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p21, which contributes to cell survival under cellular stress [111, 112]. In addition, ISR itself affects stem cell fate decisions, resulting in delayed hair regeneration but accelerated re-epithelialization [113]. In parallel with the ISR, p53 signaling functions as part of a stress-responsive checkpoint that constrains regenerative responses: p53 integrates DNA damage, oxidative stress, and metabolic stress signals to induce cell cycle arrest, senescence, or apoptosis, thereby prioritizing tissue integrity over proliferation and regeneration [114, 115]. During axolotl limb and zebrafish heart regeneration, p53 signaling is downregulated, thereby permitting cell cycle entry and regenerative proliferation [116, 117]. Similarly, HIF-2 suppresses p53 to enhance the stemness of human embryonic stem cells under hypoxic conditions [118]. Furthermore, p53 controls both the initiation and termination of the regeneration process. Its transient induction following radiation damage promotes stem cell revival, while under inflammatory conditions it prevents chronic inflammation and carcinogenesis through appropriate termination of the regeneration process [119, 120]. Mechanistically, p53 cooperates with SMAD pathways to enforce TGF-β-induced transcription while suppressing FGF expression [121, 122]. Taken together, sustained intracellular stress negatively affects the regenerative program.

The extracellular matrix (ECM) constitutes a critical component of the tissue state.

The mechanical properties of the ECM, including stiffness and fiber organization, regulate tissue-level responses through mechanotransduction pathways. Changes in matrix stiffness and cytoskeletal tension control YAP/TAZ nuclear localization and transcriptional activity, thereby influencing multicellular growth and cell fate decisions [34, 123–125]. The ECM also acts as a reservoir for latent TGF-β. Mechanical forces generated by myofibroblast contraction activate latent ECM-bound TGF-β through integrin-mediated force transmission, thereby increasing local TGF-β activity [126, 127]. Activated TGF-β further promotes ECM deposition and remodeling, establishing a positive feedback loop between matrix mechanics and profibrotic signaling [128]. Moreover, ECM properties persist as mechanical memories that constrain tissue responses. Matrix stiffness restricts the lineage commitment of mesenchymal stem cells and reduces their responsiveness to reprogramming by soluble factors [61]. Similarly, fibroblasts cultured on stiff matrices acquire a persistent mechanical memory and maintain their fibrotic properties even after being transferred to compliant environments [129]. In summary, these findings indicate that ECM remodeling actively encodes tissue-state information that leads to deviations from regenerative-permissive states.

Epigenetic regulation operates at a broader level within the cell than these stress- and matrix-dependent mechanisms, shaping the genome-wide gene regulatory network that determines which transcriptional programs remain accessible. Indeed, altered DNA methylation, histone modification, and chromatin organization are recognized as central features of age-associated functional decline [21, 130]. Genome-wide analyses have demonstrated widespread age-related changes in DNA methylation, indicating that epigenetic dysregulation is a common feature of aging [131]. In skeletal muscle stem cells, polycomb-mediated regulation via EZH2 is required to maintain self-renewal capacity and transcriptional identity [132]. Similarly, epigenomic profiling of young and aged hematopoietic stem cells has revealed coordinated changes in chromatin accessibility and histone marks during aging that reinforce self-renewal at the expense of differentiation flexibility [133], while proliferation-dependent remodeling of DNA methylation landscapes further contributes to functional decline [134]. In addition, effective regeneration requires transient epigenetic remodeling, allowing cells to temporarily access regenerative transcriptional programs. Dynamic chromatin modifications have been shown to be essential for enabling regenerative gene expression programs across regenerative systems, including neuronal injury, wound repair, and appendage regeneration [135–137]. In vivo studies in amphibians and zebrafish have further demonstrated that the disruption of histone deacetylation or demethylation impairs regeneration, highlighting the importance of regeneration-specific epigenetic regulation [138, 139].

In summary, intracellular stress responses, extracellular matrix remodeling, and epigenetic regulation constitute interdependent layers that control the tissue state and constrain regenerative transitions. Although growth factor signaling, such as FGF and TGF-β, remains active across diverse contexts, the capacity of tissues to interpret these signals in a regenerative manner is progressively restricted by the tissue state.

Aging and SASP: signals without flexibility

A paradoxical feature of aged tissues is that regenerative failure occurs despite the abundance of secreted factors. The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) is characterized by the robust secretion of diverse factors, including growth factors such as FGF and TGF-β, inflammatory cytokines, and extracellular matrix-modifying enzymes [23, 140, 141]. In aged skeletal muscle, senescent cells accumulate and produce a SASP enriched in TGF-β and inflammatory mediators; however, this signal-rich environment is associated with impaired satellite cell function and defective regeneration rather than effective repair [77]. Similarly, the secretome of senescent fibroblasts in the lung contains TGF-β, inflammatory cytokines, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which drive fibrotic remodeling rather than tissue repair [142]. These observations imply that the composition, magnitude, and persistence of the SASP shape its effects. A transient SASP can support repair, whereas its persistence in aged tissue is associated with impaired regeneration and fibrosis [25, 28, 29, 143]. This context dependence may be influenced by both signal abundance and the recipient tissue state.

Aging is accompanied by persistent activation of intracellular stress pathways that progressively stabilize protective transcriptional and epigenetic programs, thereby restricting the tissue-state transitions required for regeneration [144]. Importantly, these alterations do not render cells unresponsive; instead, they restrict the range of accessible tissue states, predisposing aged tissues to fixed, fibrotic configurations. Consequently, identical extracellular signals, such as FGF or TGF-β, can induce distinct transcriptional and cellular responses in young tissues compared to those in aged tissues [76, 145]. This is not because the signals themselves differ but because the tissue state in which they are received has fundamentally changed. From this perspective, aging represents a progressive restriction of accessible tissue states, a condition in which tissues remain reactive but are increasingly unable to transition toward regenerative conditions.

This progressive fixation reflects the establishment of self-reinforcing loops that actively stabilize the protective and fibrotic configurations. With aging, ECM stiffening, persistent TGF-β signaling, and chronic inflammation converge into mutually reinforcing circuits that encode a form of tissue-state memory, biasing subsequent cellular responses toward fibrotic stabilization, even in the absence of the original injury stimulus [146, 147]. Once tissues enter a protective or fibrotic state, the structural and signaling environments resist transition toward a regenerative state [129]. In this context, aging progressively limits access to the tissue state required for regeneration.

This framework provides a basis for reconceptualizing regenerative capacity not as a binary property (regeneration-competent or -incompetent) but as a continuous and graded capacity that reflects the flexibility of the tissue-state transitions. Notably, even in zebrafish and African turquoise killifish with exceptional regenerative potential, aged individuals show impaired regeneration of both the heart and caudal fin [148–150]. Regenerative decline with age appears to be a common feature across species, and differences in regenerative capacity may reflect the initial level of tissue-state plasticity and the rate at which it is eroded with age. This reconceptualization suggests that restoring tissue-state flexibility, rather than simply administering “regenerative” factors, may be the fundamental goal of regenerative medical interventions.

Implications for regenerative medicine

Regenerative medicine has long pursued strategies aimed at enhancing tissue repair through the exogenous delivery of growth factors, gene transfer, or stem cell transplantation [18, 101, 151]. These approaches share the common assumption that regenerative failure results from insufficient signal availability and that restoring the appropriate molecular cues is sufficient to reinitiate the repair program. Although such strategies have demonstrated proof of concept in various experimental systems, their translational success in aged tissues remains limited. When the tissue-state landscape is progressively fixed toward protective and fibrotic configurations, signal-based interventions alone are unlikely to redirect cellular behavior toward regeneration without first addressing the underlying state constraints.

From this perspective, the primary therapeutic target may need to shift from growth factors to the restoration of tissue capacity to interpret and respond to these signals. Accordingly, interventions aimed at relieving ECM stiffening, resolving chronic inflammation, or transiently attenuating stress-response pathways may serve as essential prerequisites to restore tissue-state flexibility before applying signal inputs (Fig. 3). For example, several niche-rejuvenation strategies have restored the regenerative capacity of aged skeletal muscle stem cells without altering the growth factor environment itself by using biomechanically optimized soft hydrogels that mimic the young muscle environment, by reducing the inflammatory secretome, or by temporarily inhibiting the stress-activated p38α/β MAPK pathway [77, 104, 152]. In particular, reducing the secretome associated with chronic inflammation not only alters the availability of signaling molecules but can also be viewed as the removal of a persistent input that stabilizes the condition. Since chronic inflammation is part of a self-amplifying circuit that locks aged tissues into a fibrotic state, alleviating it allows the tissue to shift toward a regenerative state.

Restoration of tissue-state flexibility as a therapeutic strategy. In aged tissues without intervention, the landscape is dominated by the fibrotic state, and regenerative signals fail to drive effective repair. Interventions targeting ECM remodeling, resolution of chronic inflammation, and attenuation of stress-response pathways can restore tissue-state flexibility, enabling the interpretation of regenerative signals and effective tissue repair

Partial cellular reprogramming reverses age-associated phenotypes and improves regenerative outcomes. Short-term cyclic expression of Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc (OSKM) improved muscle regeneration in aged mice [153]. Similarly, ectopic expression of OSK in mouse retinal ganglion cells restored the young DNA methylation patterns and promoted optic-nerve axon regeneration [154]. These interventions do not supply additional regenerative signals but instead re-expand the range of accessible tissue states through epigenetic remodeling.

Differences in regenerative capacity across species may be explained by how readily tissues can transition toward regenerative states in response to injury, rather than by signal availability [155]. In highly regenerative organisms such as zebrafish, regeneration-associated genes are maintained in a poised chromatin state prior to injury, enabling rapid and robust entry into regenerative programs upon tissue damage [139, 156]. Whether similar chromatin permissiveness is maintained in adult mammals remains an open question, but the range of accessible tissue states may be more constrained in species with limited regenerative capacity. The objective is to understand the principles by which highly regenerative organisms maintain broad accessibility to regenerative tissue states and to explore whether these conditions can be partially reconstituted in aged mammalian tissues.

Conclusions and future perspectives

In this review, we propose a conceptual framework that redefines regenerative failure not as a consequence of insufficient growth factor availability, but as a progressive loss of tissue-state transition flexibility. The paradox in aging tissues, in which effective repair cannot be initiated even in the presence of signals, can be explained by the progressive fixation of tissue states toward protective and fibrotic configurations that are no longer permissive for regenerative transitions. Thus, aging, regenerative failure, and fibrosis emerge as interconnected outcomes of a shared process.

Regenerative capacity is also reconceptualized as a continuous and graded quality that reflects the flexibility of tissue-state transitions. Even in organisms with exceptional regenerative potential, aging impairs regeneration, suggesting that differences in regenerative capacity across species reflect the initial threshold and maintenance of accessible tissue states rather than the nature of the regenerative signals themselves.

This reconceptualization has important implications for regenerative medicine and aging research. Effective therapeutic strategies may need to target not only signal availability but also the restoration of tissue-state flexibility by relieving ECM stiffening, resolving chronic inflammation, or transiently attenuating stress-response pathways before applying signal stimulation. Understanding how tissues maintain, lose, and potentially recover the capacity to transition among states represents both the central challenge and the defining opportunity at the intersection of aging biology and regenerative medicine.
