Nanoscale metal-organic frameworks as a versatile platform for synergistic combination tumor therapy
Authors: Mengru Cai, Yuqing Yang, Jiahui Kong, Yongqiang Zhang, Shiman Li, Huizhong Zhang, Xiaohan Xv, Jian Ni, Xingbin Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As the second most common cause of death globally, tumor significantly impacts human health and quality of life. Although monotherapy has achieved remarkable progress, its therapeutic effectiveness remains less than ideal. The current strategy is increasingly inclined towards a combination of different treatments. Combination therapy is conducive to rectifying the inherent flaws of monotherapy, surmounting multi-drug resistance, and attaining a synergistic effect. The advancement of nanotechnology offers an unparalleled opportunity for anti-tumor combination therapy. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) possess distinctive structural features, including adjustable pore sizes, diverse organic ligands and metal ion centers, as well as the modifiability of functional sites. These characteristics render the combined application of multiple therapeutic approaches feasible. This article provides a review of the research on MOFs-based nano drug delivery platform for anti-tumor combination therapy under physiological conditions or exogenous stimulation. The opportunities and challenges of MOFs entering the clinic for anti-tumor combination therapy are also discussed to provide reference for the further application of MOFs, and provide a novel idea for the research of MOFs-based drug delivery system.Graphical abstract

Introduction

With the advancement of science and technology, tumor treatment has exhibited a tendency towards diversification and individualization [1–3]. Owing to the complexity and heterogeneity of tumors, the mortality rate associated with tumors remains high, posing a substantial burden on global health. Current treatment modalities mainly encompass chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgical resection, and immunotherapy [4–7]. Poor drug stability, rapid biodegradation, low therapeutic specificity, and systemic side effects severely impact the clinical efficacy [8, 9]. To address these issues, the combination of two or more drugs has emerged as an anti-tumor treatment strategy with wide appeal [10]. The combination of different drugs overcomes shortcomings such as drug resistance resulting from a single target and toxicity caused by high-dose administration. It achieves efficient, multi-pathway, and multi-target anti-tumor therapy through complementary effects. For instance, the combination of photothermal agents and doxorubicin enhances the sensitivity of tumor cells to chemotherapy and attains a synergistic effect [11]. Inspired by this, a thorough analysis of the potential mechanisms of various drugs and a rational combination of different treatments will contribute to enhancing the efficacy of single therapies. However, due to the diverse solubility, molecular weight, and other physicochemical properties of different drugs, treating tumors simultaneously poses a significant challenge. The integration of drugs with distinct properties into a single system or platform has thus become a crucial challenge and a research hotspot in the current field.

Currently, the significant advancements in nanotechnology offer great opportunities for combined tumor therapy and precise drug delivery. Nano drug delivery systems enhance the distribution and metabolism of drugs in vivo and enable the integration of different drugs within a single system, thus improving anti-tumor effectiveness [12, 13]. A diverse range of nanomaterials, including cationic amphiphilic polymers, liposomes, poly(lactide-co-glycolide), mesoporous silica nanoparticles, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), and so on, have been developed for drug delivery purposes [14–20]. MOFs, a class of porous hybrid materials, have drawn significant attention, which are self-assembled from organic ligands with inorganic units (metal ions or ion clusters) [21]. The porous structure of MOFs renders them outstanding candidates for the delivery of multiple drugs. Metal and carbon-based nanomaterials exhibit high toxicity. Additionally, during practical applications, mesoporous silica nanoparticles may lead to hemolysis because of the interaction between silane alcohols and erythrocyte phospholipids. Compared with other porous nanoparticles, the multi-level structure of MOFs, including organic ligands, metal ions, porous structures, and surface conjugates, facilitates the implementation of anti-tumor combination therapy based on MOFs. Discussing MOFs-based anti-tumor combination therapy is conducive to fully leveraging the structural advantages of MOFs to enhance anti-tumor efficacy. Jiang et al. utilized the assembly of endogenous Zn2+ with L-cystine to fabricate a novel tumor-sensitive biological nanoparticle for DOX delivery [22].

To date, nanoscale MOFs have been comprehensively explored for biomedical applications, including drug delivery systems, biosensors, contrast agents, and the detection of tumor biomarkers [23–25]. Numerous reviews have been published regarding the composition, synthesis methods, and applications of MOFs in drug delivery and tumor biomarker detection [26–29]. The specific surface area and tunable chemical structure of MOFs play a crucial role in influencing biodistribution, targeting capabilities, and circulatory lifetime in vivo [30]. Different structures can be constructed simply by changing the building blocks, following the same topology. For instance, biomolecules, such as amino acids, peptides/proteins, nucleobases, and carbohydrates, can serve as ideal building blocks. Given their high specific surface area, large surface area to volume ratio, and tunable chemical structure, MOFs have drawn significant attention as a platform for anti-tumor combined therapy.

Herein, the application of MOFs in combination with various tumor treatment methods is comprehensively reviewed. Initially, the significance of combination therapy is expounded upon, encompassing the overcoming of the inherent limitations of single therapy, the avoidance of multidrug resistance, and the integration of non-overlapping toxicities to achieve a synergistic effect. Subsequently, MOFs-based combination therapy is categorized and deliberated based on the type of stimulation and the nature of drug molecules (Fig. 1). MOFs hold the potential to co-deliver various drug molecules and implement combination therapy. This approach can significantly enhance the efficacy of single therapy, reduce the incidence of drug resistance, and lower the likelihood of tumor recurrence. Although MOFs-based combination therapy offers evident advantages, several challenges remain. Finally, this paper discusses the challenges and future development directions of MOFs-based combination therapy platforms.

Synthesis, design, and structural properties of MOFs

Mastery of the design and coordination properties of MOFs is crucial for the development of advanced anti-tumor combination therapy nanoplatforms. Given their highly porous structure and customizable nature, MOFs have demonstrated great potential in integrating multiple therapeutic strategies. The composition and design of MOFs enable the customization of combination treatment strategies to enhance efficacy and achieve personalized medicine. Thus, it is essential to understand the engineering of the surface and porosity of MOFs to fully realize their potential for medical applications.

The selection of appropriate metal ions and ligands is the key to design MOFs for anti-tumor combination therapy. Metal nodes affect electronic properties and stability, ligands determine pore structure and function, and porosity determines the molecular weight of the guest molecule. Ligands with functional groups such as carboxyl groups enhance the active site and selectivity of drug-delivery systems. Common metal ions include copper and iron, which can exert therapeutic effects and induce chemodynamic therapy. Polycarboxylate cuboctahedral Rh(II), a metal–organic polyhedral supermolecule, is used to construct linked bimetallic MOFs such as secondary building units (SBU) [31]. The highly porous MOFs enables efficient drug delivery and provides a larger reaction surface area, demonstrating superior drug loading capacity and catalytic activity. For instance, HTMU-55 exhibits the morphology of micro nanorods and nanospheres due to hydrophobic fluorine groups, indicating significant catalytic activity [32].

The design of the MOFs involves the reactions of metal nodes and organic ligands. The precise control of MOFs synthesis is expected to lead to the development of more diversified and personalized medical materials. The synthesis methods include hydrothermal/solvothermal, electrochemical, microwave, spray drying, ion thermal synthesis, etc. Owing to the green synthesis process, zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) have been employed to deliver bioactive substances, including viruses, bacteria, yeast, and even mammalian cells. Besides, the introduction of functional groups can bring additional electrostatic interaction sites, which is conducive to the delivery of targeted ligands and biomolecules. Modification of ligands with fluorine, hydroxyl, sulfur, olefin, bromo, nitrogen, etc. can provide multifunctional MOFs suitable for personalized drug delivery applications.

The surface chemistry of MOFs plays a non-negligible role in their biological stability and interaction with biological systems, which makes them essential for achieving targeted and coordinated multi-drug delivery. MOFs are of great interest in drug delivery systems because they can withstand multiple surface modification protocols. This is achieved by attaching biomolecules and surface chemical engineering to introduce modulators. Therefore, MOFs have been widely recognized as efficient drug delivery systems. Besides, post-synthetic coordination using small molecule surface ligands offers the advantages of enhanced stability and dispersion while satisfying multi-drug delivery. In addition, the structure of MOFs allows the insertion of different functional materials to obtain precise and improved stability, multifunctional sites and synergistic anti-tumor effects [33]. For example, MOFs doped with graphene and gold nanoparticles could be used to coordinate multiple therapeutic strategies. Different nanomaterials have different physical and chemical properties and can be suitable for the adsorption of different guest molecules, while gold nanoparticles are incorporated into MOFs due to their photothermal conversion properties.

Briefly, incorporation of guest substances into MOFs includes guest encapsulation via active or passive adsorption, covalent bonding to enhance stability, and post-synthesis modification with functional groups as anchor points. The MOFs should be designed with structural diversity and adjustability to optimize performance. MOFs with different pore sizes and shapes are obtained to accommodate guest molecules. The stability and selectivity of the material are modulated by functionalization with different organic ligands. The tunability of MOFs also allows the incorporation of multiple functional groups in a single material, resulting in synergistic effects and improved performance in applications such as catalysis and gas storage, drug delivery.

Advantages and mechanisms of MOFs for drug co-delivery platform

Over a long period, significant advancements have been achieved in tumor therapy, and a variety of therapeutic approaches have been developed. For instance, chemotherapy (CMT) is primarily employed to inhibit tumor proliferation. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) has also demonstrated certain efficacy in the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer and esophageal cancer. Additionally, cancer starvation therapy inhibits tumor growth by cutting off the nutrient supply [34]. However, the effectiveness of single therapy in tumor treatment is limited. It is challenging to completely eliminate tumors and prevent tumor metastasis and recurrence [35, 36]. Regarding the advantages of combination therapy, integrating multiple treatment modalities into a nanoplatform is conducive to enhancing the therapeutic efficacy.

In recent years, MOFs have drawn significant attention in the biomedical field [37, 38]. As nanoscale materials, MOFs are passively accumulated at tumor sites via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [39]. The abundant active sites enable MOFs to be readily modified with active targeting ligands (such as folic acid, hyaluronic acid, peptides, small molecules, etc.) to achieve specific tumor targeting [40, 41]. The tumor-selective accumulation of MOFs renders them extensively utilized in the research of drug delivery systems. The diversity and tunability of metal nodes and organic ligands enable therapeutic metal nodes, photosensitizers, or biological macromolecules to serve as components of nanoscale MOFs. Moreover, the pores of MOFs facilitate drug delivery [42–44]. MOFs play a therapeutic role as anti-tumor drugs, and deliver drug molecules to achieve CMT, immunotherapy (IMT), etc [45]. Therefore, MOFs are considered to be a highly advantageous nano-platform for anti-tumor combination therapy, which can easily integrate multiple therapeutic modalities in a single nanoparticle to improve efficacy (Fig. 1).

MOFs are suitable for various tumor treatments, including CMT, radiotherapy (RT), photothermal therapy (PTT), photodynamic therapy (PDT), microwave-assisted therapy (MWT), sonodynamic therapy (SDT), and chemodynamic therapy (CDT) [46–49]. MOFs present an unparalleled opportunity for integrating multiple treatment modalities and have demonstrated unique advantages in anti-tumor combination therapy. The utilization of MOFs to concurrently deliver multiple therapeutic drugs not only enhances the efficacy of single therapy but also synergizes multiple therapies, thereby achieving a “1 + 1 > 2” therapeutic effect. This combination therapy also reduces the administered drug dosage and circumvents the toxic side effects associated with higher dosages. The implications of combination therapy will be elaborated upon.

Improving the inherent pitfalls of monotherapy

Combination therapy, where two or more treatments are combined synergistically, is anticipated to surmount the inherent limitations of single treatments and enhance anti-tumor effectiveness. Although single therapy has achieved significant progress in tumor treatment, it has certain flaws, which are the primary cause of tumor treatment failure. For instance, complete resection of tumor tissue is challenging to accomplish, resulting in tumor recurrence [50]. CMT is a highly classic and effective approach for tumor treatment. However, non-targeted cytotoxicity and drug resistance significantly impede its clinical application. RT, regarded as a non-invasive treatment strategy, can cause long-term toxicity and even permanent damage to the surrounding tissues. The insensitivity of hypoxic tumor cells to chemotherapeutic agents and ionizing radiation is the primary cause of treatment failure following CMT and RT [51]. The penetration depth of PDT is insufficient to eliminate deep tumor cells. CDT has a high pH requirement for tumor tissue, and a weakly acidic environment is inadequate to initiate the Fenton reaction. Overall, single-therapy approaches for treating tumors have inherent limitations, and an ideal treatment method has yet to be identified. By integrating different therapies into MOFs-based nanoplatforms, it is possible to combine their advantages and mitigate their disadvantages. Moreover, MOFs can be utilized to enhance the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby improving the anti-tumor efficacy.

Overcoming multidrug resistance (MDR)

The complexity of tumors, including genetic alterations, cellular abnormalities, and heterogeneity, renders them highly aggressive in growth within the body and challenging to control with single therapies. To enhance the success rate, repeated high-dose treatments are often necessary, which can lead to multidrug resistance (MDR). MDR has consistently been one of the primary causes of tumor treatment failure and ultimately results in tumor recurrence and metastasis. The overexpression of active efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp) is the main cause of MDR, which prevents drug entry, promotes drug efflux, changes drug targets, increases DNA repair, and leads to insufficient drug accumulation in tumors, thus affecting the therapeutic effect [52]. Currently, several strategies have been devised to overcome MDR. These strategies include regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) stress, downregulating drug resistance genes, and integrating multiple treatments. Among them, combination therapy targets multiple crucial pathways. Its efficacy is additive, enabling it to effectively overcome MDR, reduce the drug dosage, and avoid potential side effects. For instance, P-gp inhibitors combined with CMT have been clinically applied. Zolsuquinda, daunorubicin, and cytarabine are jointly used in patients with acute myeloid leukemia, demonstrating enhanced anti-tumor activity [53]. However, administering different drugs separately to overcome MDR has drawbacks, including operational challenges and poor patient compliance. Nanoplatforms based on MOFs can simultaneously deliver different drugs to overcome MDR and reduce the complexity of operation.

Multiple therapies synergistically amplify the anti-tumor effect

Integrating two or more distinct drugs within a single nanodrug delivery platform offers the benefit of accumulating non-overlapping pharmacological effects while reducing toxicity. The synergistic mechanism of combination therapy involves targeting different pathways, thereby leading to the accumulation of efficacy and the reduction of side effects. The agents chosen for combination therapy typically should have distinct mechanisms and targets. For instance, the combination of a DNA-damaging agent and an antimitogenic agent (such as doxorubicin combined with paclitaxel), which serves as the first-line clinical regimen [54]. Numerous similar reports demonstrate the use of different drugs to enhance efficacy and reduce toxicity through various mechanisms. For instance, combinations such as paclitaxel and cisplatin, or paclitaxel and gemcitabine have been employed [55]. An increasing number of studies indicate that combination therapy plays a crucial role in enhancing anti-tumor effectiveness.

Mechanisms of MOFs as a versatile platform for synergistic combination tumor therapy

MOFs-based combination therapy platforms have achieved higher efficacy and specificity in multi-drug delivery against tumors through their unique ability to adsorb and bind drug molecules. In addition, the customizable characteristics of MOFs can optimize the drug delivery performance of nanoplatforms, making them a promising tool for tumor diagnosis and treatment. The common anti-tumor mechanism of MOFs as a versatile platform involves multiple strategies to improve specificity and efficacy. Redox-active molecules immobilized within the MOFs structure break the redox balance and trigger an intracellular ROS storm. Various metal ions that are components of MOFs can specifically enter tumor tissues or cells and break cellular ion homeostasis, such as cuproptosis, ferroptosis, calcium overload and so on. The integration of signaling pathway amplification strategies, such as co-delivery of ferroptosis inducers with iron, can significantly improve the anti-tumor efficiency of MOFs-based platforms, which facilitates the further incorporation of innovative anti-tumor design strategies. In addition, antibodies, aptamers or other peptides conjugated to the surface of MOFs can selectively bind to target receptors and specifically recognize tumor cells to achieve targeted drug delivery. The ability of MOFs to deliver multiple drugs simultaneously can be improved by ligand selection, optimization of synthesis parameters, and growth of hybrid materials on the surface or within the MOFs. Moreover, drug release with different kinetic properties can be achieved by tuning host-guest interaction strength and flexible linkages in polymetallic MOFs. The drug delivery process is mainly affected by two key factors: pore size and host-guest interaction. These aspects are critical in controlling the segmented phase release of various drugs within MOFs, and can significantly affect their overall performance and efficiency. These parameters need to be better optimized to control the drug delivery behavior of MOFs, paving the way for the development of novel drug delivery systems. Thus, understanding the different needs of MOFs for drug delivery, physical and chemical properties can be tuned to enhance anti-tumor applications. However, different MOFs have different priorities for drug properties and allow specific active substances to be transported.

MOFs-based combined anti-tumor treatment strategy

As mentioned above, a single strategy to treat tumors often has its limitations, such as drug resistance. MOFs have great advantages as a nanoplatform for combined strategies. For MOFs with high specific surface area and porosity, it is easier to implement the combined strategy, resulting in higher tumor mortality. To achieve better therapeutic effects on tumors, multiple strategies have been integrated into a platform to achieve higher tumor cell death rate and reduce tumor resistance to treatment. MOFs based therapeutic agents employed for combined anti-tumor treatment strategy can be mainly divided into two categories depending on whether exogenous stimulation is required or not. The MOFs-based combination therapy was further classified according to the type of drug molecules and the required exogenous stimulation. MOFs-based small molecule drugs co-delivery strategy

Currently, CMT remains an irreplaceable clinical approach for tumor treatment. Given the non-selective tissue damage, rapid in vivo metabolism, limited tumor-site accessibility, adverse side effects, and tumor resistance associated with chemotherapeutic agents, combined CMT emerges as a promising treatment strategy. In recent years, the synergistic combination of various non-overlapping anti-tumor drugs has been implemented [56]. Anti-tumor drugs with complementary molecular mechanisms can reduce tumor resistance by targeting different molecular pathways, mitigate side effects, promote tumor cell apoptosis, slow down the tumor’s adaptive processes, and enhance the overall anti-tumor efficacy. For instance, cisplatin (CDDP) induces apoptosis in cancer cells by interacting with DNA and inhibiting its replication. Doxorubicin (DOX) inhibits DNA remodeling by intercalating into double-stranded DNA and suppressing TOP2 activity. These two agents exhibit complementary anti-tumor effects, which synergistically augment the anti-tumor efficacy [57]. Nevertheless, delivering different drugs in a conventional manner is challenging owing to the disparities in biodistribution and pharmacokinetics. The high porosity of MOFs allows for the encapsulation of various drugs. MOFs tackle the aforementioned challenges by concurrently delivering multiple drugs with different properties and physiological activities.

The active groups of MOFs enable their post-synthetic modification. This modification allows for the coupling of large-sized drug molecules on their surface without being restricted by pore size. Moreover, the ratio and release rate of different drugs can be tailored according to the therapeutic objective, thereby achieving responsive drug release and targeted drug delivery. Loading various drugs into MOFs through physical encapsulation or chemical coupling can decrease the frequency of drug administration, prevent premature drug leakage, enhance drug solubility, and elevate the drug concentration at the tumor site. Zhang et al. were the first to use ZIF-8 to co-deliver the P-gp inhibitor verapamil hydrochloride (VER) and doxorubicin (DOX) to overcome MDR [58]. In contrast to inorganic nanoparticles, the organic linkers of MOFs allow them to achieve new chemical functions through surface modifications. The aldehyde group of the imidazole-2-carboxy (ICA) ligand in ZIF-90 reacts covalently with the amino group of DOX, which effectively anchor DOX on the surface of ZIF-90, while embedding 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) in the pores of ZIF-90 [59]. The co-delivery of two chemotherapeutic drugs is accomplished through physical encapsulation and chemical coupling.

However, some MOFs-based drug delivery systems may suffer from physiological instability and other drawbacks. Therefore, integration with other biological materials (such as polydopamine, β-cyclodextrin, hydrogels, upconversion nanoparticles) building hybrid nanoparticles becomes an attractive strategy [60–63]. DOX (an anthracycline-based anti-tumor compound) and celecoxib (Cel, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug with anti-angiogenic activity) were incorporated into nanoparticles hybridized with IRMOF-3 and a thermosensitive hydrogel to achieve stable dual drug delivery [64]. The hybrid nanoparticles encapsulate various drugs at different sites and release them concurrently in a weakly acidic environment. Hu et al. encapsulated CDDP and DOX within CUDPOE NPs to develop a hybrid platform. This platform enables the simultaneous release of the two drugs, thereby enhancing the efficacy against drug-resistant tumors (Fig. 3) [65]. The modification of specific biomaterials on the surface of MOFs is beneficial to improve the distribution of drugs at the tumor site. Fluorouracil and carboplatin were loaded into MOF-808 and coated with a sugar copolymer on the surface, which enhanced tumor uptake of nanoparticles [66]. Currently, numerous clinical trials on the combination of CMT have been carried out. For example, there is a phase II study of nilotinib and 7+3 induction (daunorubicin 60 mg/m²) in combination with high-dose cytarabine consolidation therapy for acute myeloid leukemia expressing KIT [67]. However, drug co-delivery based on MOFs still has to overcome the following challenges. These include reducing drug leakage before reaching the tumor, more accurately verifying the differences in the ratio and release rate of different drugs, and also paying attention to the biological safety and in vivo biological processes of MOFs themselves.

The presence of pores in some MOFs, even after the encapsulation of a single drug, and the development of a one-pot synthesis method, which can be employed for the encapsulation of macromolecules, have paved the way for the co-encapsulation of multiple drugs.

Co-delivery of biomacromolecules and small molecule drugs/biomacromolecules based on MOFs

Besides enabling the co-delivery of various drugs, the large specific surface area, high porosity, and adjustable pore size of MOFs enable them to encapsulate biomolecules with a high drug loading capacity. Notably, encapsulating biomacromolecules within the cavities significantly reduces the structural changes of biomolecules, prevents their degradation, and increases their active sites. ZIF-8 was employed as a protective shell to encapsulate the peptides. As a result, the structure of the peptides could withstand high temperatures, protease action, and repeated freezing and thawing without being destroyed [68, 69]. Furthermore, certain biomolecules rely on metal cofactors to exert their effects, and the delivery of biomolecules based on MOFs holds great promise. Mn-MOFs were used to co-deliver DNAzyme and DOX. With the assistance of Mn²⁺, DNAzyme was activated, effectively silencing the survivin gene and addressing the issue of DOX resistance [70]. Although MOFs have demonstrated unparalleled advantages in the delivery of biological macromolecules, a single therapeutic agent alone is still insufficient to achieve adequate anti-tumor efficacy. Co-delivering biomacromolecules with small molecule drugs or other biomacromolecule drugs can enhance the biological activity of individual drugs and overcome the limitations of biomolecular therapy. At present, the commonly adopted strategy is to encapsulate enzymes, proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides, and small molecule drugs within a MOFs-based platform [71]. A typical instance is that Mu et al. employed a sacrifice template approach to fabricate a nanoplatform capable of co-delivering glucose oxidase (GOx), catalase (CAT), and DOX for the combined induction of tumor cell starvation and CMT (Fig. 4) [72]. Consequently, several promising therapeutic modalities have emerged, including starvation therapy, enzyme activation prodrug therapy, and gene therapy [73–76].

Co-delivery of small-molecule drugs with enzymes and peptides

Due to the biodegradability and membrane impermeability of enzymes, appropriate carriers are required to deliver enzymes to tumors and minimize their degradation during the delivery process. Marina et al. employed a low-temperature spray drying method to co-encapsulate collagenase (COL) and methotrexate (MTX) in MIL-100, thereby achieving protection for the drug [77]. Starvation therapy is currently a highly popular treatment approach. It encompasses three fundamental methods: inhibiting tumor angiogenesis, blocking tumor blood vessels, and cutting off the tumor’s nutrition and energy supply [78]. During the abnormal metabolic process of the tumor Warburg effect, the growth and proliferation of tumor cells are highly susceptible to the influence of nutrients and energy, such as glucose supply. GOx utilizes glucose and transforms it into H2O2 and gluconic acid. By doing so, it interrupts the glucose supply to tumors, a mechanism frequently employed in starvation therapy [79]. However, starvation alone struggles to eradicate tumors and may even trigger autophagy in tumor tissues [80]. Liu et al. co-encapsulated the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) and GOx within ZIFs. This approach ultimately achieved enhanced starvation therapy and simultaneously induced tumor immunotherapy [73]. The unique structure of MOFs facilitates the combined application of different therapies. GOx-induced starvation will lead to increased glycolysis and aggravate the acidic TME, which is conducive to the construction of pH-responsive drug delivery system. In this regard, ZIFs possess unique advantages in fabricating GOx-based drug delivery systems. These advantages include mild synthesis conditions, prevention of enzyme inactivation, and specific drug release under weakly acidic conditions. Ke et al. employed ZIF-8 to co-encapsulate GOx and DOX for synergistic starvation-chemotherapy [81].

The application of MOFs-based core-shell nanoparticles in biomacromolecule delivery is also worthy of attention. Liu et al. co-delivered GOx and DOX by synthesizing mesoporous polydopamine/ZIF-8 nanoplatform with core-shell structure for synergistic starvation/chemo- therapy, which improved tumor drug resistance [82]. However, GOX-mediated starvation therapy consumes O2, which further exacerbates the hypoxia of solid tumors, thus limiting its anti-tumor effectiveness. The hypoxia-activated anti-tumor prodrug tirapazamine (TPZ) and GOx were employed for synergistic treatment. ZIF-8 was utilized as the delivery vector. GOx depleted endogenous glucose to induce a hypoxic microenvironment, thereby activating TPZ [83]. Banoxantrone (AQ4N) is likewise a hypoxia-activating prodrug. Its combination with GOx significantly inhibits tumor growth [84]. Nevertheless, lactate serves as the main energy source to support tumor cell survival when glucose is in short supply. Thus, it antagonizes GOX-induced starvation therapy. Yu et al. used ZIF-8 for the co-delivery of α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamate (CHC) and GOx, while lactate and glucose were deprived, and showed a 2-fold enhanced anti-tumor effect [85]. Enzyme-activated prodrug therapy (EAPT) is an effective strategy for tumor treatment, which refers to the conversion of non-toxic prodrugs into highly toxic drugs under the action of enzymes. The different cavity sizes of the porous MOFs allowed the loading of horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) prodrug, which was activated by HRP [86].

Co-delivery of small-molecule drugs with nucleic acids

Activating or inhibiting tumor-related signaling pathways can contribute to the improvement of tumor metastasis and drug resistance. The co-delivery of nucleic acids and small molecule drugs represents a synergistic treatment approach that holds great promise. Nevertheless, the instability, negative charge, susceptibility to degradation, and poor targeting of nucleic acids limit their clinical applications. To address these issues, nucleic acids and small molecule drugs are encapsulated within nanoparticles for protection and site-specific release. There have already been several successful instances of MOFs-based co-delivery platforms for nucleic acids and small molecule drugs.

iRNA has been used to knock down the expression of key genes with the advantages of high knockout efficiency, significant specificity, and no immunogenicity. Therefore, siRNA plays an important role in inhibiting tumorigenesis, development and metastasis. He et al. used encapsulation and surface coordination methods to load a cisplatin prodrug and siRNA into UiO-Cis (University of Oslo, UiO) [87]. UiO-Cis protected siRNA from nuclease degradation, enhanced cellular uptake of siRNA, and promoted siRNA escape from endosomes to silence MDR genes, thereby improving the efficacy of cisplatin on drug-resistant ovarian cancer by an order of magnitude. Huang et al. developed AMOFs with nitrogen reductase response to deliver HIF-1α siRNA and chemotherapy drug DOX, which inhibited hypoxia-induced high expression of HIF-1α and P-gp, thereby significantly inhibiting DOX efflux and effectively breaking chemotherapy resistance [74]. Co-encapsulation of siRNA with various cofactors in MOFs effectively avoided enzymatic degradation and achieved a 27% gene knockout rate [88]. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have achieved remarkable success in anti-tumor therapy by inhibiting RNA translation and concurrently regulating multiple target genes. Viral delivery vectors can effectively deliver miRNA. However, their immunogenicity and mutagenic toxicity impede the practical application of miRNA. Thus, there is a need to develop novel non-viral vectors that possess good biocompatibility, high drug-loading capacity, and are suitable for macromolecular drug delivery.

In recent research, plasmids have also been incorporated into MOFs. This approach effectively alleviates the molecular weight constraint of the loaded DNA while maintaining the adsorption capacity for small molecules. Yang et al. utilized ZIF-8 to co-deliver the herpes simplex virus type I thymidine kinase plasmid and ganciclovir for lung cancer treatment, reducing the dosage by one-third [89].

Co-delivery of small-molecule drugs with bacteria

ZIF-8 has attracted extensive attention as a delivery vector for enzymes, peptides, and nucleic acids [90]. Currently, ZIF-8 has been used for the delivery of complex organisms, such as viruses, bacteria, yeast, and even mammalian cells [91]. Biomimetic mineralization bioengineering based on ZIF-8 can maintain the survival of the organism and may provide additional functions to the guest organism, with significant potential for biomedical applications. Yan et al. coated the surface of Escherichia coli (MG1655) with ZIF-8 to achieve the co-delivery of Ce6 and DOX, and the nanoparticles showed excellent tumor selectivity and anti-tumor properties [75].

Co-delivery of different biomacromolecules

Besides being combined with small molecule drugs, co-delivery among biological macromolecules can also be realized. A number of recent studies have centered on delivering tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) to enhance the uptake of antigen presenting cells (APCs). The concurrent delivery of ovalbumin (OVA) and cytosine-phosphate-guanine oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG ODNs) to APCs using MOFs elicited robust immune responses and memory responses [92]. MOFs can deliver a diverse array of proteins into cells, regardless of the protein’s size and molecular weight. Zhao et al. utilized ZIF-8 to co-deliver perforin and granzyme B, and CaCO3 was employed to mineralize the MOFs to prevent premature drug release [76]. New cell-mediated drug delivery systems have also drawn extensive attention owing to their long circulation lifespan and flexible mobility [93]. The MOFs-Janus cell architecture was devised to maintain the inherent binding capacity of cells to the microenvironment and release cytotoxic enzymes in an acidic environment. This was achieved by growing ZIF-8 on the surface of MDA-MB-231 cells while delivering the cytotoxic enzymes [94].

The incorporation of bioactive molecules into MOFs represents an innovative and highly valuable research area. Nevertheless, unlike the co-delivery of small molecule drugs, when delivering biomacromolecules, the mildness and environmental friendliness of the synthetic route need to be taken into account to minimize damage to biomolecules. It is a great challenge to obtain crystalline porous structures for the co-delivery of small molecules with biomacromolecules. Probably because of this, bioactive MOFs for combination therapy were developed, albeit in limited quantities. Most of these MOFs involve ZIF-8. New studies may be reported in the near future by pushing the limits of conventional MOFs synthesis methods in combination with the development of novel bioactive molecules.

MOFs-based small molecule and gas co-delivery strategy

Gas molecules, including NO, H2S, and CO, have drawn increasing attention for disease treatment owing to their potent physiological regulatory functions and specific molecular targets. As an endogenous gas, CO exhibits anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects on various tumor cells. Nevertheless, an excessive amount of CO in the body will competitively bind to hemoglobin, leading to insufficient O2-carrying capacity and exerting adverse effects on normal tissues and blood. There is an urgent need to design a targeted delivery system for CO with precise release. Yao et al. used MIL-100 to simultaneously deliver CO, DOX and carbon nanoparticles to promote the combined application of CMT, PTT, and gas therapy, which greatly enhanced the sensitivity of tumor cells to CMT [95]. Nitric oxide (NO) is a free radical that exists stably in both tumor and normal tissues. It plays a crucial role in the formation and development of tumors. NO not only induces apoptosis in various tumor cells by causing damage to mitochondria and DNA but also regulates the tumor immune microenvironment. However, as a gas molecule, NO has a short half-life in vivo. It is unstable and not convenient to be utilized in aqueous solution. The use of NO as a therapeutic agent has certain limitations. Thus, the use of NO donors for treatment is an excellent alternative. Du et al. employed glutathione (GSH)-sensitive MOFs to encapsulate the immunosuppressive enzyme indoleamine 2, 3-diO2ase (IDO) inhibitor BMS-986,205 and the nitric oxide (NO) donor S-nitrosothiol. This approach was used to synergistically modulate the immunosuppressive TME, leading to an increase in CD8 + T cells and a reduction in regulatory T (Treg) cells [96]. In addition, high levels of NO relax blood vessels, relieve tissue hypoxia, increase tumor sensitivity to various therapies, promote drug penetration, and produce highly reactive peroxynitrite (ONOO-) molecules that kill tumor cells, destroy intracellular messengers, and enhance cytotoxicity. The O2 deficiency in solid tumors has been widely acknowledged, and this characteristic significantly restricts the application of O2-dependent therapies like PDT. Yan et al. employed a one-pot method to simultaneously encapsulate the NO donor L-arginine (L-Arg) and the H2O2 precursor Vitamin K3 (VK3) into the pores of ZIF-8, achieving their co-delivery to tumor sites to address these issues (Fig. 5) [97]. In the acidic TME, the pH-sensitive ZIF-8 undergoes degradation, thereby releasing VK3 and L-Arg. Under the action of the NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase-1 (NQO1) enzyme, VK3 generates H2O2, which elevates the oxidative stress levels in the TME. Additionally, VK3 reacts with L-Arg to produce NO, thus enabling tumor oxidative stress-based gas therapy. MOFs possess the function of gas storage. The utilization of MOFs can address the challenges of inadequate gas delivery efficiency and concentration. Notably, as a delivery carrier for gas molecules, MOFs may face risks such as gas leakage. Therefore, its preparation process requires systematic investigation. If necessary, other materials can be incorporated to regulate the release of gas and mitigate the side effects on normal tissues.

Metal nodes-dependent combination anti-tumor therapeutic strategies

Metal ions, including Zn2+, Cu2+, Fe2+, Mn2+, etc., play a crucial role in the growth and development of organisms and are essential elements for the body. For instance, Zn2+ regulates energy metabolism and gene expression. Nevertheless, abnormal accumulation of metal ions may cause irreversible damage. The properties of metal ions have led to their extensive use in the treatment of various diseases. For example, calcium overload is frequently employed in anti-tumor therapy [98]. The metal nodes of MOFs can be used as therapeutic metals to play a role in tumor therapy. A typical strategy for metal nodes to exert therapeutic effects is Fenton reaction-based CDT. Inspired by the Fenton reaction and the Fenton-like reaction, CDT was first proposed by Wang et al. in 2016 and has been widely studied because it can induce the generation of ROS in situ and break the redox homeostasis in cells [99]. The Fenton reaction catalyzes the generation of the highly toxic hydroxyl radical (·OH) from endogenous hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), the most active ROS, which induces tumor cell death by irreversibly destroying DNA. Due to the overexpression of H2O2 in the TME, ·OH is produced only in the tumor area and causes little damage to normal cells, thus making CDT highly specific and less invasive. Zhuang et al. fabricated a Cu-based two-dimensional nanosheet, “PEG/Cu-BDC,” for CDT with dual responsiveness to GSH and H2O2 [100]. Owing to the impact of the TME, including low metal ion concentration, insufficient acidity, limited H2O2 content (ranging from 100 µM to 1 mM), and overexpression of GSH, the efficacy of CDT alone is not satisfactory. For example, iron-based or mangane-based Fenton reactions are only effective under strongly acidic conditions (pH < 4). Although the efficiency of Cu+-induced CDT is not affected by pH and the reaction rate is also 160 times that of Fe (II), the insufficient content of H2O2 and the overexpression of GSH still limit the application of Cu-based MOFs [101]. Therefore, it is an attractive strategy to combine different therapeutic agents with metal nodes, including small molecule drugs, biological macromolecules, and dual therapeutic metals. The combination of different therapeutic agents is beneficial to improve the defects of single therapy and enhance the effect of tumor treatment.

Co-delivery of metal nodes with small molecule drugs

The structure of MOFs enables them to integrate Fenton reagents (such as Fe and Cu) with functional ligands or other drugs. This integration facilitates multimodal combination therapy and enhances the efficacy of CDT. Table 1 presents recent strategies for synergistic anti-tumor therapy involving metal nodes and small molecule drugs. Hollow mesoporous silica was employed as the core, and Zn/Cu co-doped MOFs were utilized as the shell to deliver the chemotherapy drug cisplatin, thereby achieving the combined application of CMT and CDT [102]. The anti-tumor effect of certain drugs is closely associated with the assistance of metal ions. For instance, artesunate (AS) necessitates the involvement of Fe2+ to activate its anti-tumor activity. Consequently, MOFs hold great significance in the delivery of small molecule drugs. Ji et al. employed MOFs to encapsulate AS. Fe2+ reacted with AS, generating high concentrations of toxic ROS, which significantly enhanced the anti-tumor therapeutic effect of AS [103]. Additionally, various strategies, such as increasing intracellular acidity, augmenting H2O2 content, and decreasing H2O2 consumption, can be utilized to enhance CDT efficacy via MOFs-based nanoplatforms [104]. Sun et al. developed liposomes that simultaneously deliver the acid adjuvant (dichloroacetic acid, DCA, pKa 1.35) and the Fenton reagent MOFs-Fe2+, which regulate intracellular acidity by DCA and enhance anti-tumor effects by promoting the conversion of H2O2 to cytotoxic ·OH via Fe-MOFs [105]. Cui et al. coated CaO2 and DOX with Cu/ZIF-8, and CaO2 was used to provide intracellular H2O2 to enhance CDT efficacy [106].

Therapeutic metal-based tumor therapies typically rely on the oxidative damage induced by an elevation in ROS. Autophagy, a self-protective mechanism of tumors, can reduce the oxidative damage to cells. This significantly impacts the effectiveness of tumor therapy. Co-delivery of autophagy inhibitors and therapeutic metals is advantageous for blocking the oxidative stress-mediated tumor self-protection pathway and enhancing tumor-specific oxidative damage. Yang et al. combined NH2-MIL-88B (Fe) with the autophagy inhibitor CQ to treat human melanoma, which showed significant anti-tumor effects [107]. Moreover, multiple mechanisms of cell death based on therapeutic metals have attracted wide interest. For instance, ferroptosis, a newly identified mode of cell death, is characterized by the accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxide (LPO). Ferroptosis represents an effective strategy for reversing multidrug resistance. Nevertheless, the simultaneous delivery of iron, ferroptosis-inducing agents, and antineoplastic drugs still poses a significant challenge. The unique structural features of MOFs provide great convenience for the simultaneous delivery of iron, ferroptosis-inducing agents and antineoplastic drugs. Consequently, MOFs have shown great application prospects in the research of anti-tumor drug delivery systems. Artemisinin is a sesquiterpene lactone with a peroxide group. The iron-mediated cleavage of the endoperoxide bridge can significantly enhance the anti-tumor efficacy of ART [108]. Thus, the combined use of iron ions and ART is an appealing strategy.

Co-delivery of metal nodes with biomacromolecules

Biological macromolecules, such as nucleic acids and proteins, offer significant advantages in tumor therapy. The porous structure and active sites of MOFs facilitate the loading or incorporation of biological macromolecules [115]. Table 2 presents the anti-tumor treatment strategies involving the combination of therapeutic metals and biological macromolecules in recent years. One of the most extensively used examples is GOx. It consumes high levels of glucose in tumor tissues, thereby inducing starvation therapy. Simultaneously, it generates H₂O₂, which disrupts cellular redox homeostasis. However, the adaptive ability of tumors and the continuous supply of glucose in the blood circulation greatly limit the efficacy of GOx. Therefore, GOx is usually combined with CMT, gas therapy, and CDT. Compared with other exogenous treatment methods, CDT combined with starvation treatment does not require external sound, light, and other stimuli, and has fewer toxic and side effects. Qiao et al. used ZIF-8 to co-load GOx and hemoglobin [116]. In tumor tissues, GOx consumed glucose and released H2O2, while hemoglobin released Fe3+ and induced Fenton reaction, finally realizing the synergistic effect of CDT and starvation treatment.

MOFs possess significant advantages in nucleic acid delivery. They can effectively prevent the inactivation of nucleic acids in the circulation and enhance the delivery efficiency of nucleic acids. ZIF-8 has been utilized for the efficient delivery of miRNA (miR-34a-m). This application reduces miRNA degradation during in vivo circulation, enabling efficient synergistic gene therapy/CDT [117]. The therapeutic effect of nucleic acid requires the assistance or activation of metal ions. The delivery of nucleic acids based on MOFs can effectively solve the problem of co-delivery of nucleic acids and auxiliary therapeutic metal ions. Wu et al. designed a Zn2+-activated DNAzymes that specifically cleaves GLUT1 mRNA, which was encapsulated in ZIF-8 to achieve ion interference-induced starvation treatment [118]. Coordination of DNA with metal ions is another attractive strategy. The outstanding molecular recognition ability, precise targeting ability and programmability of DNA provide unprecedented convenience for the targeted delivery of drugs. Wang et al. used ZIF-8 to encapsulate Fe-DNA hydrogel and GOx, which improved the targeting ability of nanoparticles [119].

Furthermore, the hybridization of bacteria with MOFs to create bio-nano platforms represents another appealing strategy. This approach enhances the tumor targeting ability and therapeutic effectiveness of nanoparticles. In recent years, numerous studies have shifted their focus to bacteria and viruses that possess targeting or therapeutic properties. Facultatively anaerobic bacteria inherently exhibit a tropism towards external environmental signals (such as biomarkers, hypoxia, acidic conditions, and temperature). Consequently, they can spontaneously home to specific tumor regions and even penetrate deeply into tumors. For instance, high lactate levels in the TME frequently result in poor CMT efficacy. Wang et al. integrated Shewanella oneidensis MR-1 (SO) with MIL-101 through electrostatic interaction to prepare a self-driven bioreactor [120]. SO effectively depleted lactic acid in tumor tissue and transfered electrons to generate Fe2+, inducing the cleavage of MIL-101 and the release of DOX to achieve a cascade of CDT/CMT/metabolic therapy.

Due to the distinctive structure and properties of biomacromolecules, numerous challenges remain to be addressed in the development of MOFs-based biomacromolecule delivery systems. For instance, (1) it is essential to ensure the effective enrichment of the nanoplatform at the tumor site and maximize the targeting efficiency to minimize immune escape. (2) Polypeptides, proteases, and nucleic acids should not be destroyed or inactivated in the process of synthesis, storage, and circulation in vivo. (3) Biomacromolecules should be fully released at the tumor site to meet the therapeutic needs.

Triple anti-tumor therapy based on therapeutic metals, small molecule drugs and biological macromolecules

The integration of biomacromolecules and therapeutic metals has drawn widespread attention. However, their combined utilization is constrained by inadequate efficacy, autophagy, and energy supply. Notably, endogenous sequential reactions, known as biochemical cascades, typically take place within the cell. Single therapies can be devised based on a single response, while combination therapies can target a multistep induction cascade, where each step initiates a single therapy. Monotherapy can be developed based on a single response, and combination therapy can target a multistep cascade in which each step triggers a monotherapy. Cascase-based therapeutic strategies are designed to address the characteristics or defects of the TME. Small molecule drugs are further introduced on this basis to provide potent anti-tumor effects through a multistep cascade. Pan et al. coencapsulated disulfiram prodrug with GOx in MOF-199 to generate a highly cytotoxic Cu(CDTC)2 for CMT at the tumor site, and GOx catalyzed the generation of H2O2 to enhance Cu2+-mediated CDT [124]. Liu et al. used TA-Mn2+ MOFs to encapsulate autophagy inhibitor rapamycin and Beclin 1 inhibitor DNAzyme to amplify the inhibition of autophagy in tumor cells through CMT combined gene therapy, and MOFs shell effectively reduced the degradation of DNAzyme [125]. Zhou et al. developed a tumor-specific catalytic nanomedicine for enhanced tumor ablation accompanied with tumor vasculature disruption and angiogenesis inhibition through a cascade reaction with GOD modified on Fe-MOF coupled with anti-VEGFR2 (Fig. 6) [126]. The GOD enzyme catalyzed the intratumoral decomposition of glucose, thereby triggering tumor starvation. It also provided an ample amount of hydrogen peroxide as the substrate for the Fenton-like reaction catalyzed by Fe-MOFs. This reaction generated sufficient highly toxic hydroxyl radicals for enhanced chemodynamic therapy. Additionally, it immediately attacked the tumor vascular endothelial cells to disrupt the existing vasculature. Meanwhile, the anti-VEGFR2 antibody directed the nanohybrids to target blood vessels and blocked the VEGF-VEGFR2 connection to inhibit angiogenesis.

Synergistic anti-tumor strategies based on dual therapeutic metals

To further enhance the efficacy of metal nodes-induced CDT, bimetallic enhanced CDT can be achieved by incorporating different metals into MOFs. Ma et al. designed progressively targeted Cu/Mn bimetallic nanosheets using folic acid (FA) and triphenylphosphine (TPP) to enhance the efficacy of CDT [127]. Hypoxia in solid tumors typically serves as a crucial factor contributing to chemoradiotherapy resistance and tumor recurrence. Enhancing the TME through a multi-step cascade reaction is beneficial for improving the sensitivity of tumor cells to chemoradiotherapy and enhancing treatment efficacy. Due to the uncontrolled reaction of GOx with glucose in vivo and its low stability, which impede its practical application, designing bimetallic MOFs platforms with cascade reactions, such as GSH depletion, H2O2 generation, and glucose consumption, also presents certain advantages. In recent years, Au nanoparticles have emerged as a new type of particles with glucose oxidase and catalase mimetic activities. Au is frequently employed as a core or anchored to the MOFs to enhance CDT induced by therapeutic metals in MOFs. He et al. reported that DOX was encapsulated within pegylated porphyrin-MOFs-gold nanoparticles. These nanoparticles exhibited catalase activity and promoted O₂ production at the tumor site, thereby improving the efficacy of radiotherapy and chemotherapy [128].

The incorporation of metals and drugs into MOFs has attracted great interest, and a large number of works in this field have been reported. Most of these MOFs involve MIL-100, MIL-101, ZIF-8 and Cu-MOF. Considering the need for cellular ion homeostasis and the wide synthesis possibilities of MOFs, we believe that this metal + drug combination therapy may be extended to different porous and biocompatible MOFs.

Combined treatment strategies that rely on exogenous stimulation

Combined treatment strategies based on laser stimulation

Phototherapy, a novel minimally invasive treatment emerging in recent years, has garnered extensive attention owing to its selective destruction of tumors. Phototherapy, encompassing PTT and PDT, elicits therapeutic effects by irradiating external light to initiate a photoresponse. With a potent anti-tumor effect, phototherapy offers a new prospect for tumor treatment. However, the limited penetration of exogenous light, the self-quenching property, and the poor water solubility of photosensitizers severely restrict the clinical application of phototherapy. The emergence of MOFs offers an unprecedented opportunity for the delivery of photosensitizers. The structure of MOFs alleviates the self-quenching of photosensitizers, enhances the specific surface area of photosensitizers, and overcomes their delivery deficiencies in vivo. With the advancement of science and technology, an increasing number of studies have centered on phototherapy-based tumor combination therapy. Yao et al. designed and fabricated ultrasmall MOFs nanodots (MSPC) that encapsulate metabolism inhibitors and mitochondria-targeted photosensitizers for the synergistic PDT of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) (Fig. 7) [129]. The MSPC exhibited glutathione depletion and mitochondrial respiration suppression. The glutathione depletion combined with metabolism inhibitor increased oxidative stress, which improved the efficacy of mitochondria-targeted PDT.

Multiple treatment strategies based on PTT

The photosensitizer was subjected to photothermal conversion under exogenous light irradiation to achieve PTT. Nevertheless, the development of PTT is limited by exogenous light transmission, and tumor penetration depth greatly affects the clinical application of PTT. It is essential to accomplish anti-tumor therapy through the integration of various treatment approaches. Wang et al. successfully achieved a DOX loading of 27.5% by fabricating a CuS@Fe-MOF nanoplatform with a core-shell structure. Moreover, the tumor inhibition effect of the combination of PTT and CMT was significantly superior to that of PTT or CMT alone [132]. The synergistic treatment of CMT and PTT based on MOFs offers several advantages, including easy implementation, simple synthesis, controllable processes, and stable quality. The simultaneous delivery of photothermal agents and chemotherapeutic drugs using MOFs has led to more effective tumor treatment. For instance, gold nanoparticles and polydopamine exhibit high photothermal conversion efficiency. MOFs-based core-shell structures or hybrid materials can induce synergistic PTT/CMT therapy.

The nano-platform based on MOFs provides convenience for the combined application of PTT/CDT. The increase of temperature in PTT accelerates the Fenton reaction, and CDT overcomes the defect of insufficient light penetration of PTT. Liu et al. used MOF-199 to carry the photothermal agent metallooxonate to realize the photothermal conversion in vivo, while Cu2+ in MOF-199 induced CDT, and realized PTT/CDT synergistic treatment through simple material synthesis [130]. However, the heat shock response (HSR) of tumor cells induced by PTT and CDT could curtail treatment. Cheng et al. significantly improved the synergistic therapeutic effect of PTT/CDT by utilizing MOFs to simultaneously deliver the photothermal agent polypyrrole (PPy) and the HSR inhibitor cantharidin (CTD) [133]. The encapsulation of bioactive molecules (biological enzymes or RNA, etc.) in MOFs to exert therapeutic effects together with photothermal agents is also a combination therapeutic strategy. Zhang et al. employed ZIF-8 to co-load ICG, DOX, and Hsp-70 siRNA to improve the anti-tumor activity of PTT by inhibiting the expression of heat shock proteins [134].

Multiple treatment strategies based on PDT

During the process of PDT, photosensitizers transfer light energy to O2. This generates ROS within tumor cells, ultimately leading to cell damage. Whether the photosensitizer serves as an organic ligand of MOFs or is encapsulated within the voids of MOFs, it exhibits a monodisperse state. This state reduces self-quenching and the band gap of energy transition. Compared with other treatment methods, PDT features good tissue selectivity, minimal invasiveness, and high biosafety. However, the efficacy of PDT has been seriously underestimated due to the low O2 content in tumor tissue, excess GSH, and limited tissue penetration ability. The unique porous structure of MOFs provides unprecedented convenience for the application of PDT. Wan et al. achieved a synergistic effect of Fe2+-dihydroartemisinin-mediated CDT, Ca2+-dihydroartemisinin-mediated tumor therapy, and TCPP-mediated PDT by delivering dihydroartemisinin via CaCO3 mineralized Fe-TCPP [135].

In recent years, PDT has demonstrated promising applications in anti-tumor therapy. This is attributed to its low invasiveness and specific spatiotemporal selectivity. Photosensitizers are activated by light of specific wavelengths, with the aid of O₂, to release cytotoxic ROS. The concentration of O₂ plays a decisive role in the efficiency of PDT. However, the tumor hypoxic microenvironment significantly restricts the clinical application of PDT. Therefore, PDT-based nanoplatforms need to cooperate with O2 supply to improve anti-tumor efficacy. Zhao et al. coated Fe2O3 nanoparticles with Fe-TCPP [136]. Red blood cell membrane and aptamer AS1411 were used to modify nanoparticles to improve targeting ability. The nanoparticles are capable of achieving the integration of CDT and PDT. They can catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 to generate O2, thereby improving the hypoxic microenvironment of tumors and enhancing the efficacy of PDT. Fe/Mn/Cu-based MOFs mimic peroxidase and generate ·OH and O2 for CDT and alleviating hypoxia, respectively. When combined with PDT, Fe/Mn/Cu-based MOFs can significantly enhance the efficacy of tumor treatment [137]. Tumor hypoxia promotes tumor cell survival and metastasis, and PDT treatment aggravates tumor hypoxia. Therefore, inhibition of hypoxia signal transduction is also one of the strategies of PDT treatment. Cai et al. constructed the PCN-ACF-CpG@HA nanoplatform to block the hypoxic survival signal and inhibit HIF-1α expression by loading acriflavine, a hypoxia-inducible factor signaling inhibitor [138]. At the same time, CpG adjuvant initiated a powerful anti-tumor immune response to eliminate residual tumor cells following PDT.

Another strategy for enhancing the efficiency of PDT involves reducing intracellular O₂ consumption. Chen et al. fabricated a ZIF-8 nanoplatform to co-deliver the photosensitizer phycocyanin and papaverine [139]. As a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor, PPV decreased O₂ consumption within the tumor. The combination of phycocyanin and papaverine augmented the efficacy of PDT. Furthermore, interfering with lactate metabolism is also a promising therapeutic approach. Chen et al. encapsulated α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid within Zr(IV)-based porphyrin MOFs [140]. Following treatment, lactic acid-based aerobic respiration was shifted to anaerobic glycolysis, which decreased O2 consumption and increased the intratumoral O2 concentration. This, in turn, enhanced the efficacy of PDT under hypoxic conditions.

Most photosensitizers exert anti-tumor therapeutic effects by producing 1O2 through O2-dependent type II PDT. Previous studies have shown that the efficacy of type II PDT can be improved by improving the hypoxic environment. However, O2-independent PDT produces superoxide radical (O2·−) and ·OH to achieve anti-tumor effect. Shi et al. reported a TiO2 modified zirconyl-porphyrin MOFs [141]. After the laser irradiation, MOF@TiO2 generated 1O2 via an O2-dependent type II energy transfer mechanism. Under severe hypoxic conditions, the nanoparticles produce O2·− and ·OH by an O2-independent type I mechanism.

ROS serves as a crucial mediator of cellular oxidative stress, which is implicated in tumorigenesis and development. It is also a key substance for PDT to exert its therapeutic effects. Redox homeostasis in tumor cells represents a defense mechanism against ROS, significantly influencing the therapeutic efficacy of PDT. GSH, an important intracellular antioxidant, can counteract oxidative stress and maintain cellular redox homeostasis. Excessive GSH (up to 10 mM) in the TME significantly restricts the efficacy of PDT. Zou et al. synthesized Cu/ZIF-8 shell-coated lanthanide-doped nanoparticles [142]. These nanoparticles were loaded with the photosensitizer Rose Bengal. Under 980 nm laser irradiation, Cu²⁺ depleted GSH, thereby enhancing the ROS generation efficiency of PDT and CDT. At present, the strategy of combining GSH depletion with PDT has been widely used in anti-tumor research, and good therapeutic effects have been obtained [143]. In addition, the thioredoxin/thioredoxin reductase (Trx/TrxR) system is also one of the extremely important regulators responsible for intracellular redox homeostasis. Thiols and selenols are used to remove ROS and resist PDT and other ROS-related therapies [144]. Cheng et al. constructed PCN-based nanoplatforms to deliver the TrxR inhibitor piperine and perturb cellular redox homeostasis [145]. When compared to conventional PDT, the combination of PDT and TrxR inhibition resulted in approximately 1.6-fold increase in cellular ROS levels, approximately 2-fold increase in cytotoxicity, and approximately 3-fold increase in apoptosis/necrosis rates.

To improve the therapeutic effect of PDT, most studies have adopted strategies such as improving hypoxia and inhibiting GSH activity. However, the limited O2 carrying capacity of MOFs and the low efficiency of in situ O2 generation in vivo have become the main reasons that affect the efficacy of PDT. The most straightforward strategy is to encapsulate chemotherapeutic drugs and photosensitizers in the same nanoplatform to employ direct anti-tumor effects. Zhang et al. fabricated ZIF-8 nanoparticles that co-delivered gold nanoparticles and DOX. Compared with PDT or CMT alone, the combination of PDT and CMT led to complete tumor suppression [146]. The porous and malleable characteristics of MOFs enable the co-delivery of photosensitizers and chemotherapeutic agents, effectively overcoming the limitations of single therapy, and greatly expanding the flexibility of MOFs for biomedical applications. Notably, even the organic ligands of MOFs can serve as photosensitizers. As a result, the combined application of CMT and PDT can be achieved simply by encapsulating chemotherapy drugs. Due to its potent proangiogenic effects, PDT frequently results in tumor metastasis and recurrence. Zirconium porphyrin MOFs were employed to deliver the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor inhibitor apatinib. The combination of PDT and anti-angiogenesis drugs significantly enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy.

Among numerous strategies for integrating CMT with PDT, the approach of leveraging tumor hypoxia to activate CMT has drawn significant attention. A variety of drugs can be reduced to free radicals by reductase within the cell. In the presence of O2, these free radicals are rapidly eliminated through oxidation. However, under severely hypoxic conditions, they exhibit potent cytotoxicity. PDT consumes O2, further deteriorating the tumor hypoxic environment. This, in turn, activates the drugs, thereby achieving the therapeutic effect of synergistic CMT/PDT. Wang et al. employed zirconium MOFs as a vehicle to encapsulate the photosensitizing agent pyrophosphamide a and the hypoxia-sensitive drug 6-aminoflavone (AF) [147]. Following PDT treatment, the tumor cells were further exposed to hypoxia, and AF was activated to exert an anti-tumor effect. TPZ is also a classic hypoxia-activated prodrug, which generates toxic oxidative free radicals through a one-electron reduction reaction in hypoxic TME. Shao et al. prepared TPZ-loaded UCS, which combined with anti-programmed death ligand 1 (α-PD-L1) to achieve effective inhibition of proximal and distal tumors [148].

Tumor drug resistance is the main mechanism that impairs the efficacy of PDT, and remodeling the TME to mitigate multiple drug resistance is the preferred method to optimize PDT. However, the development of multifunctional nanoparticles usually requires complex design concepts and preparation processes, which increases the cost, makes it difficult to achieve industrial production, and the quality of the finished product is difficult to guarantee. The active site, tunable organic ligand, and large pore size of MOFs facilitate the synthesis of multifunctional nanoparticles, reducing the synthesis steps and improving the reproducibility [149]. Wang et al. loaded a platinum (IV) -diazo complex (Pt (IV)) in MOF-199 (Cu) and conjugated the photosensitizing agent TBD with polyethylene glycol to modify MOF-199. This led to Cu (II)-induced depletion of GSH, Pt (IV)-induced improvement of hypoxia, and release of the chemotherapeutic drug Pt (II). Ultimately, multiple strategies worked synergistically to enhance the efficacy of TBD-induced PDT [150]. Cai et al. synthesized the CuTz-1-O2@F127 nanoplatform to enhance the efficacy of PDT under 808 nm laser irradiation by promoting O2 delivery and GSH consumption [151].

Anti-tumor strategy of PTT combined with PDT

Studies have shown that photothermal effect enhances the efficacy of PDT by alleviating tumor hypoxia [152]. When tissues are heated, blood vessels dilate, blood flow increases, and O2 content in tissues rises accordingly. Therefore, PDT and PTT can act synergistically to eliminate tumor cells. Gold nanoparticles exhibit strong absorbance at 650 nm and mainly induce PTT treatment. MOFs-coated gold nanoparticles can be used in combination with PDT and PTT [153]. Li et al. prepared tetrcarboxyphenyl porphyrin nanosheets (Cu-TCPP), Cu2+-induced PTT showed a synergistic effect with TCPP-induced PDT [154]. Zhang et al. constructed zirconia iron-porphyrin MOFs nanoshuttle (Zr-FeP MOF) for the delivery of Hsp70 siRNA [155]. Upon near-infrared laser irradiation, Zr-FeP MOF produced both ·OH and 1O2 with a photothermal conversion efficiency reaching as high as 33.7%. Cheng et al. synthesized ICG@Mn/Cu/Zn-MOF@MnO2, and the nanoparticles demonstrated photothermal/fluorescence/magnetic resonance three-mode imaging guided synergistic PTT/PDT/CDT [131]. This “all-in-one” therapeutic approach, which combines multimodal imaging-guided PDT and PTT, has drawn significant attention. Most of these combinations can be achieved through methods such as doping with multiple metals, mixing organic ligands, or encapsulating multiple guest molecules. More significantly, this synergistic anti-tumor therapy enhances the generation of tumor-associated antigens and triggers an anti-tumor immune response.

Anti-tumor strategy of phototherapy combined with immunotherapy

With the progress of tumor immunology, scientists have gradually realized that inhibiting tumor cells alone is insufficient to achieve the optimal therapeutic outcome. A tumor is a complex system composed of tumor cells and microenvironment (including fibroblasts, blood vessels, cytokines, etc.). Immunotherapy can improve the TME by activating the immune system, thereby inhibiting the rapid division of tumor cells. PTT/PDT stimulates adaptive immune responses by releasing inflammatory mediators and cytokines into the TME. Therefore, the combination of PDT and IMT is also an attractive tumor treatment strategy. Zou et al. achieved the combined application of PTT and IMT by constructing carbonized MOFs nanoparticles coated with cluster-like substances [156]. Zhang et al. employed ZIF-8 to realize the spatiotemporal synergistic effect of photothermal agent (IR820), an immune adjuvant (imiquimod), and immunomodulator (1-methyl-D-tryptophan) [157]. Hyaluronic acid modified IR820@ZIF-8 achieved tumor targeting and PTT, and released tumor antigens. Mannan-modified (R837 + 1MT)@ZIF-8 was utilized to achieve targeted immune expansion of dendritic cells. The two targeted nanoparticles aggregated in various tissues in vivo to address the issues of inadequate immune response activation and immune evasion. Ultimately, they achieved synergistic PTT/immunotherapy. The combination of thermal ablation and IMT elicits a robust systemic anti-tumor immune response, thereby attaining highly effective anti-tumor outcomes. Zeng et al. synthesized a benzo-porphyrin MOFs to achieve low O2-dependent PDT. The nanoparticles, when combined with αPD-1 checkpoint blockade therapy, have demonstrated great potential for tumor treatment [158]. With the progress of research, it has been recognized that photoresponsive nanoplatforms capable of efficiently generating ROS hold great promise as anti-tumor immune triggers. These nanoplatforms can induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) through ROS, release tumor antigens, secrete damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate the immune system, and regulate the immunosuppressive TME [159].

Most photosensitizers polymerize easily in aqueous solution. Co-encapsulation of photosensitizers and drugs in MOFs can not only improve the stability of photosensitizers, but also provide an opportunity for combination therapy. In addition, adjuvant therapy with PTT or PDT helps to overcome multidrug resistance. ZIF-8, MIL-100, and other MOFs using photosensitizers (TCPP: tetrakis(4-carboxyphenyl)por phyrin) as organic ligands are widely used as platforms for combining PDT, PTT, and other therapies.

Combined treatment strategies based on acoustic stimulation

The application of PDT in the treatment of deep tumors, such as brain tumors, is limited by the poor tissue penetration depth of light. Recently, new non-light-mediated dynamic therapies have been proposed and used for anti-tumor applications. SDT refers to the use of ultrasound (US) stimulation to trigger sonosensitizers to mediate the generation of ROS, thereby inducing cell damage. Due to its excellent tissue permeability, US irradiation offers irreplaceable advantages in the treatment of deep tumors. It can achieve targeted, controllable, and non-invasive tumor elimination. SDT is also classified into O2-independent type I SDT and O2-dependent type II SDT. Cao et al. constructed a MOFs-derived sheet TiO2/C nanocomposite, which is resistant to hypoxia and stable to repeated ultrasonic irradiation, effectively inducing type I SDT and producing large amounts of ROS [160]. So far, due to the influence of the number of type I sonosensitizers, its application is still limited, and type II SDT is mainly used for anti-tumor treatment. The clinical limitations of type II SDT are very similar to those of PDT. Limited O2 content and excessive glutathione affect the application of SDT. The combined treatment strategies based on SDT include increasing the supply of O2, initiating hypoxia-activated SDT, down-regulating the expression of reducing agents such as GSH, and introducing chemotherapy drugs [161]. Hu et al. reported the construction of two-dimensional (2D) ZnAl-TCPP MOF nanosheets through a phase transformation strategy for highly efficient SPDT (Fig. 8) [162]. ZnAl-TCPP nanosheets exhibited excellent ROS-generating activity under both light and US irradiation, which was superior than either light or US irradiation alone, demonstrating the synergistic effect for SPDT. The ROS generation activity of ZnAl-TCPP was superior to that of TCPP (by 3.0 times). Under light and US irradiation, ZnAlTCPP-PEG induced strong oxidative stress, which caused significant damage to mitochondria and lysosomes. Consequently, this led to cell apoptosis and the inhibition of tumor growth.

The metal nodes of MOFs plays a therapeutic role. The use of MOFs to deliver sonosensitizers can usually be combined with CDT/SDT to improve the intensity of anti-tumor therapy. Some metal nodes can also improve hypoxic microenvironment, down-regulate GSH and induce immune response when combined with SDT, such as Mn-MOF, MOF-525, Gd-TCPP-NC and DHMS [165]. Sun et al. developed an O₂-independent drug delivery platform (Cu-MOF) that degrades in the hypoxic environment. This degradation releases Cu²⁺ and an azo initiator (AIPH), ultimately causing DNA damage and apoptosis of tumor cells [166]. Additionally, incorporating PTT into the nano-platform can raise the temperature of the tumor. This, in turn, accelerates the Fenton reaction rate and further enhances the combined treatment effect of CDT and SDT [167].

CMT remains unaffected by the TME. Moreover, combining CMT with SDT is also an approach to enhance the anti-tumor efficacy. Dong et al. utilized Fe-TCPP to deliver sulfoximine (BSO) and coated the surface of the nanoparticles with a CaCO3 shell to prevent drug leakage [168]. The combination of BSO, an inhibitor of GSH synthesis, and TCPP, which induces SDT, significantly enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of SDT. Pu et al. synthesized P/M@CasMTH1 nanoparticles to improve the effectiveness of SDT in combination with gene editing. They utilized the nanoparticle-anchored CRISPR-Cas9 system to disrupt the defenses of tumor cells, thereby leading to tumor apoptosis and suppression [169]. Ren et al. developed a nanotherapeutic platform, DOX@PCN-224/Pt, which integrates cascade, CMT, and SDT. This platform catalyzes the generation of O2 from H2O2 to alleviate hypoxia in the microenvironment and enhances the efficacy of SDT when combined with DOX [163].

In addition, SDT can enhance the efficacy of SDT and improve patient survival by inducing ICD and activating anti-tumor immune response when combined with IMT [170]. Zhan et al. developed an SDT-nanovaccine (immune adjuvant CpG) integration platform (cMnMOF@CM) that induces robust systemic immune responses and long-term immune memory to prevent tumor growth and recurrence [171]. Luo et al. used Zr-TCPP(TPP)/R837@M to achieve simultaneous delivery of sonosensitizer and toll-like receptor agonist R837, resulting in a powerful tumor combination therapy effect [164].

Radiation-based combination therapy

RT is a broad-spectrum anti-tumor treatment method that utilizes electrical irradiation to eliminate tumor cells [172]. Nevertheless, the efficacy of RT is constrained by the hypoxic microenvironment within the tumor. Moreover, due to drug resistance, the tumor recurrence rate following RT remains high. The employment of multifunctional RT-sensitizing nanoparticles represents an important approach to enhancing the anti-tumor effectiveness of RT. Du et al. developed D-arginine-loaded MOFs that produced nitric oxide, down-regulated HIF-1α, alleviated tumor hypoxia, and enhanced radiation-induced tumor ablation [173]. Employing MOFs to sensitize RT is another effective strategy, such as Hf-MOFs [174]. Chen et al. synthesized mangane-chelated Hf-MOFs, which catalyzed H2O2 to generate O2 to enhance radiotherapy, and hafnium as a high-Z element to further sensitize CMT. Effective tumor treatment could be achieved with a single X-ray irradiation after injection of the nanoparticles [175]. Bao et al. achieved the combined application of sensitizing PTT/RT using the NMOF of hafnium (Hf) cluster and manganese (III)-porphyrin [176]. Li et al. developed the TME activated high-Z element-containing nanoferroptosis inducers FBSP MOFs to sensitize RT (Fig. 9) [177]. GOx was incorporated in the MOFs, causing the depletion of NADPH to terminate the conversion from cystine to cysteine. The accumulation of cystine would synergistically lead to disulfide stress and trigger disulfidptosis, and the hydrogen peroxide produced during glucose oxidation could cascade-react with the Fenton reaction, therefore enhancing ferropotosis. The drug delivery system relying on MOFs can effectively relieve tumor hypoxia and holds great promise when combined with RT.

Microwave thermotherapy, a tumor thermal ablation therapy that relies on MW radiation, offers the advantages of minimal invasiveness and deep penetration. It has been extensively applied in the clinical treatment of tumors [179]. Li et al. fabricated MOFs composed of Bi-MN-porphyrins. These MOFs functioned as a microwave sensitizer to generate cytotoxic O2 and heat. Additionally, apatinib was loaded to downregulate the expression of VEGF, thus inhibiting tumor recurrence [178]. Wu et al. combined imidazole with Mn2+ for CDDP loading and realized a novel nanoengineering of mangane-based MOFs for magnetic resonance imaging monitoring, microwave sensitization, and glutathione response [180]. Zhao et al. synthesized EuMOF with multifunctional features such as integrated microwave sensitization, fluorescence imaging, and drug loading [181]. The challenge of completely ablating tumor cells results in a high recurrence rate, which severely restricts its clinical application. Consequently, microwave sensitization nanomaterials have been developed to enhance their efficacy, and MOFs-based nanoplatforms have demonstrated promising microwave sensitization properties.

Challenges and perspectives

With the increasing demand for tumor therapy, the development of advanced materials is of great significance. Triggering synergistic therapeutic effects within tumor tissues using MOFs-based nanoplatforms can improve anti-tumor efficacy without complex nanoformulation design, which has gained numerous achievable outcomes. Among the numerous nanomaterials evaluated for tumor therapy, MOFs-based nanoplatforms offer multiple advantages (flexible binding of metal nodes and organic ligands in MOFs), as well as tunable surface/pore properties (which can be further functionalized), thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy and leading to some novel activities, recently emerging as promising drugs. One of the main advantages is synergistic effect, where the overall therapeutic benefit of the combination formulation is greater than the sum of the therapeutic effects of the individual drugs. In addition, synergistic combinations can overcome the toxicity and other side effects associated with high doses of single agents by counteracting biological compensation, reducing the dose of each compound, or exploiting a context-specific multitarget mechanism. More importantly, acquired resistance induced by long-term repeated treatment with a single agent can be well reversed by MOFs-based combination therapy.

In this review, we have summarized the recent progress in the application of MOFs-based combination treatment strategy. Despite the unique properties and diverse therapeutic properties of MOFs-based nanoplatforms, their researches are still in their infancy and faces many challenges. To facilitate the application development of MOFs-based combination treatment strategies, the following challenges and barriers should be considered in future studies.

Systematic investigation of long-term biosafety

MOFs-mediated combination therapy has demonstrated unique advantages. However, there remains a shortage of clinical studies based on this therapy. Before entering clinical studies, MOFs nanoplatforms still encounter significant challenges, and a crucial issue is the discussion regarding their biosafety. Currently, more systematic preclinical investigations regarding the biosafety of MOFs are still required, particularly with respect to their accumulation in the liver and kidneys and long-term toxicity. Although the acute toxicity of MOFs has been extensively examined both in vitro and in vivo, there is limited published data on their long-term toxicity. The exploration of this issue will be crucial for the clinical application of MOFs. MOFs-based anti-tumor research is still in its early stages. At present, the anti-tumor combined therapy strategy based on MOFs is still far from the clinic. The efficacy and mechanism of MOFs in anti-tumor combination therapy and preclinical studies still need to be carefully evaluated. However, more preclinical application studies are needed before their market potential can be studied.

Another obstacle in the clinical application of MOFs is its pharmacokinetic study. The biodistribution, metabolism, and clearance process in vivo have become the key issues that need to be solved before the clinical application of MOFs. MOFs are metabolized and cleared by the kidney or liver. Nanoparticles with a diameter of less than 10 nm typically undergo rapid renal clearance, whereas those with a diameter greater than 200 nm require liver clearance or remain in the Kupffer cells of the liver for an extended period. The long-term retention of MOFs in the liver may lead to hepatotoxicity, which is a crucial issue that needs special attention from researchers. Moreover, there are certain unpredictable challenges when translating MOFs from laboratory research to clinical trials. Addressing these challenges could ultimately contribute to the advancement of MOFs-mediated anti-tumor combination therapy.

Careful design of MOFs-based combination treatment strategy

The well-controlled preparation of nanoplatforms largely determines their in vivo effects. There is an urgent need to further explore better synthesis strategies for MOFs-based nanodrug delivery systems with appropriate size, structure, and targeting properties, and these strategies must be simple, controllable, reproducible, and cost-effective before they can be used in clinical applications. Firstly, the interaction between MOFs-based nanoplatforms and the TME or cell surface should be fully considered and optimized to better exert the anti-tumor efficacy of nanoplatforms in vivo. Secondly, the synergistic treatment mechanism and potential side-effects should be fully evaluated before combination therapy to maximize the expected efficacy and reduce adverse effects.

At present, some MOFs-based nanoplatforms have been successfully proposed and shown satisfactory results, but the exploration in this field is still in its early stage. A large number of preclinical and clinical studies have shown that selectively combining some therapeutic strategies not only fails to produce synergistic anti-tumor effects, but also brings unexpected and inevitable adverse effects. Therefore, a rational combination of treatment modalities should be fully considered to synergization different anti-tumor mechanisms. More importantly, the sequence and interval of drug release should be accurately designed and controlled to maximize the therapeutic effect while reducing toxicity.

In-depth exploration of the potential anti-tumor mechanisms

At present, there are limited studies on the potential anti-tumor mechanisms based on MOFs or other inorganic-organic hybrid nanoplatforms. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the in vivo multi-omics data of different MOFs to fully understand their in vivo effects. In addition, numerous metal ions in the organism cooperate to participate in many important life processes. At present, the research hotspots of MOFs mainly focus on the drug delivery behavior, and there is a lack of research on the influence of each metal node on the therapeutic effect. Therefore, conducting in vivo metalomics studies based on MOFs, extending the study to the interaction between metal ions and the body, and exploring the metabolism and excretion process of metal ions in vivo will deepen our understanding of the complex role of MOFs. In addition, the therapeutic effect of MOFs in vivo is affected by many factors, including intrinsic properties (such as shape, size, composition, surface chemistry) and extrinsic factors (such as TME, external stimuli). MOFs have been regarded as a promising platform to synerge multiple treatment strategies. However, combinations of different treatment strategies may cause unexpected side effects in some cases. Therefore, the anti-tumor mechanism of MOFs-based nanoplatforms should be further explored to make each treatment strategy cooperate with each other.

Enhancing the therapeutic efficacy and tissue-specific targeting ability

In contrast to other nanoplatforms, most MOFs-based nanoplatforms have adjustable micropores and steric hindrance. Different drug encapsulation and personalized release can be achieved by adjusting the pore size and steric hindrance. In addition, the components of MOFs, such as metal nodes (Fe3+, Cu+, etc.) and organic ligands (TCPP, chlorin, DNA, peptides, etc.), can be used as therapeutic agents to exert anti-tumor effects. Compared with other nanomaterials, more attention should be paid to the therapeutic properties of MOFs in order to reduce the design and synthesis steps of redundant nano platforms, reduce the difficulty and cost of preparation, and improve the stability of synthesis. Most importantly, the various components of the MOFs platform can work together to enhance anti-tumor efficacy under a simple design concept. Therefore, researchers can efficiently encapsulate drugs and promote in vivo release by precisely controlling the structure and pore size of MOFs. Besides, some appropriate surface modifications are used to enhance the penetration and retention of the nano-platform in the lesion site and improve the anti-tumor efficiency. In addition, nonspecific leakage and non-targeting selectivity of drugs may lead to drug accumulation in normal tissues and cause side effects. Therefore, it is necessary to rationally design nano-platforms with environmental response and specific cell targeting, and control the drug release behavior.

Conclusion

In summary, the current study has demonstrated that MOFs have become a promising drug delivery platform for anti-tumor combination therapy. Due to the unique structural features (tunability of metal ions and organic ligands, high porosity, large specific surface area, surface modification, etc.), MOFs provide unprecedented opportunities for the integration and synergy of different therapeutic methods. Therefore, MOFs provide a paradigm for the transition from monotherapy to combination therapy, which is the latest trend in clinical oncology research. In this review, the advantages and significance of MOFs in combination therapy are emphasized, and the research progress of MOFs-mediated anti-tumor combination therapy strategy in recent years is reviewed. Anti-tumor combination therapy based on MOFs facilitates the achievement of stronger anti-tumor effects with lower injection doses. This approach helps avoid the side effects caused by high doses. The ideal drug delivery system is one that attains synergistic anti-tumor effects through combination therapy, where the effect of the combination therapy exceeds the sum of the individual treatments. Furthermore, given the relatively late start of MOFs in the biomedical domain, the majority of MOFs-based studies are currently limited to laboratory validation. In the future, more in-depth research is required to further promote its clinical translation. Considering the potential of MOFs in combination therapy, it is anticipated that MOFs will assume a significant role in the development of multifunctional personalized therapies in the future.
