Background

Mycotic aneurysms involving the transplant renal artery (TRA) represent a rare but life-threatening complication following kidney transplantation, with an incidence below 1% [1]. These aneurysms are frequently caused by fungal arteritis, which leads to arterial wall necrosis and subsequent aneurysm formation [2].

Despite advances in surgical technique and postoperative immunosuppression regimens, the insidious progression of fungal arteritis poses a diagnostic challenge, often masked by immunosuppressive therapy in the early post-transplant period. The management of TRA mycotic aneurysms typically involves graft nephrectomy, especially in haemodynamically unstable patients, although isolated reports of graft salvage using endovascular or hybrid techniques have emerged [3].

We present two cases of Candida tropicalis-associated mycotic aneurysms with rupture in cadaveric renal allografts, underscoring the importance of early recognition, timely intervention, and preventive strategies in high-risk settings.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 38-year-old hypertensive male with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) on maintenance haemodialysis (MHD) underwent a deceased-donor renal transplant. The donor was a 38-year-old male declared brain dead following a road traffic accident (RTA). Cold ischaemia time was 6.5 h. The graft’s single renal artery and vein were anastomosed to the external iliac artery and vein, respectively. Basiliximab was administered as induction therapy, followed by triple immunosuppression with tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisolone.

The initial postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged with a nadir serum creatinine of 1.0 mg/dl. On postoperative day (POD) 18, he presented to the emergency department with breathlessness, abdominal distension, and oliguria. On examination, he was pale, hypotensive, and had diffuse abdominal tenderness. Although initially unstable, the patient was transiently stabilised after resuscitation, permitting a rapid focused contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) to localize the bleeding source and facilitate immediate surgical planning. CECT revealed active contrast extravasation from the graft arterial anastomotic site (Fig. 1).

He was taken for emergency exploratory laparotomy, which revealed aneurysmal rupture of transplant renal artery and a non-viable graft. Graft nephrectomy was performed, and the external iliac artery, which appeared friable with patchy discolouration, was repaired using interrupted non-absorbable sutures after limited debridement. The external iliac vein was intact and did not require repair. Histopathology of the graft revealed fungal hyphae consistent with Candida tropicalis within the arterial wall (Fig. 2). Despite initiation of intravenous amphotericin B and supportive care, the patient succumbed to rebleeding on POD 28.

Case 2

A 51-year-old male with ESRD on MHD for four years underwent a deceased donor renal transplant. The donor was a 28-year-old male declared brain death following a RTA. The early postoperative period was complicated by delayed graft function (DGF) requiring two sessions of dialysis. The patient was discharged on POD 10 with a down-trending serum creatinine of 3.2 mg/dl. Two days later, he returned to the outpatient department with fever, lower abdominal pain, and postural hypotension. Laboratory workup showed a haemoglobin of 4.8 gm/dl and elevated leukocyte and neutrophil count. Blood culture was sent which later revealed candidemia (Candida tropicalis).

Doppler ultrasound showed absent renal perfusion with a peri-graft collection, which was confirmed on CECT. In view of haemodynamic instability, an emergency laparotomy was performed. Intraoperatively, an aneurysmal dilatation with rupture was noted at the arterial anastomosis, along with an in-situ thrombus (Fig. 3). Graft nephrectomy was performed, and the external iliac artery and vein were repaired with 5 − 0 Prolene sutures. The graft was sent for histopathology and arterial tissue for culture. Histology revealed fungal hyphae and yeast cells within the vessel wall and renal interstitium (Fig. 4), confirming fungal involvement of the TRA. The patient completed antifungal therapy and made a satisfactory recovery. At six months of follow-up after graft nephrectomy, he remains on maintenance haemodialysis, with no recurrence of infection or vascular complication.

Discussion and conclusions

Fungal arteritis following renal transplantation is a rare but life-threatening complication that can lead to aneurysmal dilatation and rupture of the transplant renal artery (TRA). The infection may originate from an infected donor kidney, infection involving the recipient iliac arteries, or contamination during organ procurement and preservation. In deceased donor transplants, contributing factors include prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay, intra-abdominal injuries, prolonged cold ischemia time, and delayed graft implantation—particularly in immunocompromised recipients such as those with diabetes. Among these, contamination of the preservation solution has emerged as a consistent source, with Candida species most frequently implicated [4]. In our series, retrospective review revealed Candida tropicalis growth in the preservation fluid, indicating donor-derived contamination as the most likely source of infection. Donor blood and urine cultures were negative for fungal growth, consistent with previous reports where preservation-fluid contamination occurred despite sterile donor cultures [2].

Clinically, mycotic aneurysms often present with nonspecific symptoms such as fever, malaise, or abdominal pain. However, their initial manifestation may be catastrophic haemorrhage due to rupture, as observed in both our patients. Bindi et al. noted that mycotic aneurysms can remain asymptomatic or mimic other post-transplant complications, making early diagnosis particularly difficult [5]. Immunosuppression may further obscure signs of sepsis or vascular infection. Therefore, clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion, especially in patients with unexplained hypotension, anaemia, or graft dysfunction.

Imaging plays a central role in diagnosis. Doppler ultrasound may show turbulent flow patterns (“yin-yang” sign), while contrast-enhanced CT or angiography confirms aneurysm morphology and extent [6]. Management of TRA mycotic aneurysms depends on hemodynamic status, graft viability, and extent of infection. In hemodynamically unstable patients or in cases with rupture, graft nephrectomy remains the most immediate and definitive treatment, as seen in both our patients [7]. The external iliac artery was repaired using interrupted non-absorbable sutures to minimize tension on the friable arterial wall; however, this approach carries a risk of postoperative bleeding, pseudoaneurysm formation, or late arterial blowout due to infection-related wall necrosis. Whenever feasible, wide debridement of infected tissue followed by tension-distributing repair techniques—such as short continuous fine sutures, pledgeted stitches, or autologous vein patch/interposition—provides greater durability [8].

Recent case reports highlight successful graft-preserving strategies in selected patients. Li et al. described a hybrid approach combining femoral–femoral bypass, stenting, and coil embolization to isolate the pseudoaneurysm while preserving graft function [9]. Similarly, Bindi et al. described open resection of the aneurysm with re-anastomosis to the internal iliac artery, resulting in long-term graft survival [5]. These approaches are suitable only for stable patients with localized disease, minimal systemic infection, and high potential for graft salvage.

Prevention remains critical. Albano et al. underscored the importance of maintaining asepsis during organ recovery and suggested that routine candida genotyping and early fluid culture surveillance can mitigate risk [2]. A systematic review found that high-risk preservation fluid cultures - including Candida, Staphylococcus aureus, and Gram-negative bacilli - carried an odds ratio (OR) of 19.9 for graft-site arteritis compared with negative or low-risk preservation cultures (Coagulase-negative Staphylococci and Corynebacterium). Fungal growth had the highest individual odds ratio (OR) of 71.00, highlighting its strong association with graft site arteritis [10]. Maintaining sterility during organ procurement and handling, using freshly prepared sterile preservation solutions, and performing routine on-table preservation fluid cultures may allow early recognition and management, potentially avoiding graft nephrectomy and improving long term outcome. In high-risk donors, particularly those with intra-abdominal trauma before brain death, routine peritoneal irrigation with diluted povidone-iodine, may have additional preventive value.

In summary, mycotic aneurysm of the transplant renal artery remains a rare but life-threatening vascular complication after renal transplantation. These cases underscore the importance of routine preservation-fluid cultures and maintaining a high index of suspicion for fungal arteritis in early postoperative vascular complications. Our experience has prompted the implementation of mandatory preservation-fluid screening, early multidisciplinary evaluation of unexplained perigraft collections or Doppler abnormalities, and structured vascular imaging follow-up. Early diagnosis and prompt surgical intervention remain lifesaving in unstable patients, whereas graft salvage may be attempted in carefully selected stable cases. Given the friability of infected iliac vessels, meticulous arterial reconstruction and continued imaging surveillance are crucial to prevent delayed haemorrhagic events.
