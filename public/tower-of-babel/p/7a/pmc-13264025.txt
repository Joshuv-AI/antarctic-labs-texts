A self-amplifying RNA vector based on rubella virus for mRNA therapeutics and vaccine applications
Authors: Miao Jing, Michelle Cheok Yien Law, Cao Dai Phung, Yu He, Yaw Bia Tan, Minh TN Le, Dahai Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignificant efforts have been made in the burgeoning field of RNA therapies since the COVID-19 pandemic. Self-amplifying RNA (saRNA)-based therapies are considered more promising than conventional messenger RNA due to their lower concentration requirements, long-lasting effects, and self-adjuvanticity. Here, we developed a novel saRNA vector, based on the rubella virus ([RuV], based on the RA27/3 vaccine strain) as an alternative virus-derived replicon that demonstrates effective expression in multiple cell lines and induces immune response. As it is derived from a commonly used vaccine, it potentially offers a better safety profile with reduced risk of adverse events compared with current alphavirus-derived saRNAs. We further optimized this RuV saRNA by screening the capsid region, the nonstructural polyprotein p200 codon, and the 3' untranslated region. This RuV saRNA has great potential to be used as a vaccine vector and in other applications in RNA therapeutics.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, rubella virus, self-amplifying RNA, mRNA therapeutics, vaccine

Introduction

RNA-based therapeutics have received tremendous attention following the approval of mRNA vaccines for COVID-19. Conventional non-replicating mRNA-based therapeutics are in vitro-transcribed RNAs encoding viral proteins, such as Moderna or BioNTech-Pfizer COVID-19 vaccine or therapeutic proteins, such as antibodies or immune modulators for immunotherapy.1,2,3 They may incorporate chemically modified nucleotides, sequence optimization, and different purification strategies to improve the efficiency of mRNA translation and RNA stability.4 However, a limitation of mRNA-based therapies is that the amount of antigen or antibody administered is proportional to the number of conventional mRNA transcripts successfully delivered. Therefore, achieving adequate expression for protection or immunomodulation may require large or repeated doses, resulting in increased risk of adverse effects.

Unlike mRNA-based therapies, self-amplifying RNAs (saRNAs) contain a replicon encoded by a virus, which contains a non-structural protein that forms RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), conferring saRNA with replicative ability and enabling the synthesis of multiple copies of saRNA from the originally delivered saRNA, and the structural proteins are replaced with the gene of interest (GOI).5,6 This allows to produce a significantly higher number of GOI copies compared with conventional mRNA. saRNAs have shown potential as vaccine vectors for infectious agents, including the causative virus of the COVID-19 pandemic (SARS-CoV-2), and some clinical trials for the influenza virus and rabies virus.7,8,9,10 Preclinical studies have shown that saRNA vaccines can induce equivalent or more potent immune responses at lower doses compared with those achieved by non-replicating mRNA vaccines because of the autoreplicative properties of the viral replicon backbone.6,7,11 Common viral replicons currently used include those derived from alphaviruses such as Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), Sindbis virus (SINV), and Semliki Forest virus (SFV).12,13,14,15 They also share favorable features of mRNA-based vaccines, including modular design and a good manufacturing practice-compliant cell-free synthesis method. Preliminary studies in vitro have shown that unmodified saRNAs outperform unmodified and modified standard mRNAs in antigen expression when delivered in lipid nanoparticle formulations.16

Despite the therapeutic potential of saRNAs, there are several hurdles to overcome. saRNAs are much larger than standard mRNAs, 9–12 kb in length, and can mimic viral infection.17 For instance, saRNA-transfected cells often induce interferon (IFN) responses because of the presence of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) intermediates, activating cellular antiviral defenses and leading to antigen-specific B and T cell responses.18,19 The innate immune response is activated by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect replicon-generated pathogen-associated molecular patterns on nucleic acids.20 In addition, PRR detection by dendritic cells (DCs) upon exposure to saRNAs leads to the activation of adaptive immune responses, potentially restricting saRNA replication and antigen expression.21 Thus, there is a need to design novel saRNAs capable of efficiently expressing heterologous antigens while having a better safety profile.

Rubella virus (RuV) was previously classified under the Togaviridae family due to similarities in genomic structure. However, it has now been reclassified into the Matonaviridae family, and it is the sole member of the Rubivirus genus.22 Ruhugu virus (RuhV) and Rustrela virus (RusV) were recently identified as its relatives.23 They are enveloped viruses with a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA genome. It is reported that the genome organizations of RuV, RuhV, and RusV are identical, consisting of 5′ UTR, a first big open reading frame (ORF), an intergenic region, a second big ORF, and 3′ UTR. The first ORF encodes the non-structural polyprotein p200, which undergoes autocleavage to generate the p150 and p90 proteins, while the second ORF encodes structural polyproteins capsid, E2, and E1. RuhV and RusV use bats and mice as reservoirs, respectively, while humans are the only host for RuV in nature.23 RuV is first described as the causative agent of rubella, a disease transmitted exclusively among humans via the respiratory route. The current rubella vaccine, based on the RuV RA27/3 strain, is highly effective. This vaccine is also part of routine childhood immunization schedules as the measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine (MMR).24 It generates long-term immunity after a single dose, effectively preventing clinical disease, and has a very low risk of adverse effects.

Here, we construct an RuV saRNA replicon and demonstrate that the vector can efficiently express the antigen of interest. Due to its derivation from a commonly used vaccine, it offers a better safety profile with reduced risk of adverse events compared with the current alphavirus-derived saRNAs. This novel RuV saRNA provides an alternative RNA vector for infectious disease vaccines and potentially cancer immunotherapy.

Results

Construction of a new rubella virus-based self-amplifying RNA

A new saRNA was developed based on the genome of the RuV RA27/3 strain, which was designated as RuV saRNA V0. The RuV saRNA V0 was engineered to encode the RuV replicase genes, p150 and p90, which are essential for self-replication. The full-length capsid, the first 10 amino acids of E2, and the last 173 amino acids of E1 are expressed under the control of a subgenomic promoter. A secreted Gaussia luciferase (Gluc), fused with a C-terminal 2A peptide, was inserted between the E2 and E1 regions to serve as a reporter for antigen production (Figures 1A and 1B). The conserved motif GDD1966-1968 in the p90 RdRp region contains the catalytic active sites; the replication-defective RNA with G1967A or G1968A cannot be rescued by supplementation of the p90 in trans.25 We also generated a catalytically inactive RdRp mutant, referred to as the RuV RdRp GAA mutant (Figure 1C). Gluc can be detected in BHK-21 cells transfected with RuV saRNA V0, but not in those transfected with the RuV RdRp GAA mutant (Figure 1D). This suggests that the detected Gluc expression results from saRNA self-replication rather than direct translation.

Codon optimization of the nonstructural polyprotein p200 region

The GC content of the RuV genome is approximately 70%, which is the highest among RNA viruses.23,26 The total length of the RuV saRNA V0 is over 8 kb, and the non-structural protein p200 region takes more than 75%. There are several repetitive regions within p200, with the GC content in some localized areas exceeding 90% (Figure S1A). To reduce the GC content, facilitate subcloning, and enhance expression, we performed codon optimization within p200, specifically from amino acid residues 219 to 236 and from residue 420 to the C-terminal end, as shown (Figure 2A). The GC ratio was reduced from 70% to 57% after optimization. Surprisingly, luciferase expression was reduced over all the time points observed (Figure 2B). This suggests that not only the p200 protein itself but also the RNA sequence or the secondary structure of p200 may play a critical role.

Investigating the role of the capsid in the RuV replicon

To minimize the total saRNA length, we deleted the entire capsid gene based on the p200 codon-optimized saRNA replicon, which caused luciferase activity to be abolished (Figures 2A and 2B). This suggests that capsid may play an important role in saRNA replication and translation. To further investigate which region of the capsid is necessary for the activity of the replicon, the structure of capsid was predicted using AlphaFold server described in Abramson J et al., 2024 (Figure 3A).27 Amino acid residues 1–34 were predicted as an N-terminal hydrophobic α-helix motif, which may act for membrane and p150 association, and amino acid residues 281–300 were predicted to contain the transmembrane region and signaling peptidase recognition site. Hence, saRNA replicons with various truncations in the capsid region were constructed based on the RuV saRNA V0 (Figure 3B). First, we deleted amino acid residues 35–270 of the capsid and constructed ΔC35-270 replicon, which resulted in impaired luciferase activity compared with RuV saRNA V0 (Figure 3C). In addition, the following ΔC35-270 replicon also showed decreased luciferase production (Figure 3C). Previous research showed that the first 88 amino acid residues of capsid were required for the enhancement of genomic RNA production by analyzing the amounts of genomic and subgenomic RNA on a northern blot.28 We subsequently constructed two other replicons with deletions between amino acid residues 89–270 and 113–270, which were referred to as ΔC89-270 and ΔC113-270, respectively. All the saRNAs with truncated capsids, regardless of length or region, showed lower luciferase production compared with RuV saRNA V0 (Figure 3C), which indicates that a full-length capsid is necessary for the saRNA replication and translation.

RuV saRNAs with various truncated E1

Previous research reported that defective-interfering RNAs of RuV generated during serial passage contain large internal deletions in the subgenomic RNA, while maintaining the 5′ end of the capsid gene and the 3′ end of the E1 gene.29,30 This suggests the 3′ end of the E1 gene is necessary for RuV genome replication. The E1 protein is adjacent to the 3′ UTR, and we hypothesized that the 3′ end of the E1 protein functions similarly to the 3′ UTR. Hence, we introduced a stop codon before Gluc, thereby only allowing E1 to function at the RNA level (Figure 4A). The secondary structure of the last 305 nucleotides (including the last 291 nucleotides of E1 and 59 nucleotides 3′ UTR) of the replicon was predicted using the method described in Bellaousov et al., 201331; a series of E1 truncated saRNAs containing incremental deletions of the 3′ end of the E1 gene were generated according to the location of the hairpin loop (Figure S2A). Among them, the 3′-250-nt (nucleotide) saRNA showed the highest Gluc activity, approximately 3-fold higher than that of the RuVsaRNA V0 (Figures 4A and 4B). Therefore, the 3′-250-nt saRNA is the best candidate after the optimization of the RuV genome.

5′ untranslated region and cap analog optimizations

The original first nucleotide of RuV 5′ UTR is cytosine; we added an additional guanosine upstream in the RuV 3′-250-nt replicon. Previous research showed that nucleotides from position +4 to +8 downstream of the transcription start site affect T7 promoter activity more than 5-fold; they also identified promoter variants that significantly enhance the transcriptional output and increase the yield of in vitro transcription reactions.32 Thus, we also changed the transcription start site by adding one guanosine, two guanosines and one adenosine (GGA), or GGAAATA in front of the 5′ UTR of the 3′-250-nt replicon and predicted the secondary structure of these new 5′ UTRs (Figures S3A and S3B). However, none of them performed better than the original 3′ 250-nt replicon (Figure 5A). Another feature we sought to optimize was the 5′ cap structure. The 5′ cap structure is an essential post-translational modification that plays many essential roles in RNA biogenesis, stability, and translation.33 Viruses possess distinct 5′ cap structures, such as cap-0 (m7GpppNp) and cap-1 (m7GpppNm2′-Op)34,35; RuV employs a cap-0 structure. Many viruses encode their own capping enzymes: alphaviruses use nonstructural protein 1 to synthesize the 5′ cap-0 structure of viral RNAs,36 whereas flaviviruses generate cap-1, which has been reported to evade antiviral responses and promote efficient replication.37 CleanCap AU and CleanCap AG were also tested in the RuV saRNA. It was observed that the RuV saRNA replicon with a CleanCap AG showed comparable strong luciferase activity as that with no modified cap, while the replicon with a CleanCap AU showed reduced but still acceptable luciferase activity up to 96 h post-transfection in BHK21 cells (Figure 5B). In Vero cells, the RuV saRNA replicon with no modified cap showed the strongest luciferase activity, while replicons with CleanCap AU and AG showed reduced activity (Figure 5C).

RuV saRNA induces the production of Gluc and pro-inflammatory cytokines in dendritic cells

The RuV 3′-250-nt Gluc saRNA (RuV 3′-250 saRNA) and VEEV-Gluc saRNA (VEEV saRNA) were tested in DC2.4 mouse DCs and HEK293T cells. Both saRNAs were formulated using two delivery systems: a commercially available lipid-based nanoparticle (in vivo-jetRNA, Polyplus) and red blood cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) (RBCEVs), which we have previously demonstrated to be efficient and safe carriers for nucleic acid delivery.38 As shown in Figure S5, both mRNA and RuV 3′-250 saRNA exhibited consistently high loading efficiency into RBCEVs (Figures S4A–S4C). The RNAs were then transfected into DC2.4 mouse DCs, where the luciferase activity was measured at 24 and 48 h post-transfection (Figure 6A). Both saRNAs demonstrated robust luciferase activity in DC2.4 cells after 24–48 h. There is no significant difference in levels between RuV- and VEEV-based saRNAs at 24 h using in vivo-jetRNA, although VEEV showed a higher activity than RuV at 48 h (Figures 6B and 6C). In contrast, the opposite trend was observed with the RBCEV-mediated delivery, wherein the VEEV saRNA exhibited higher activity than RuV 3′-250 saRNA at 24 h, where it became comparable to RuV at 48 h (Figures 6B and 6C). However, in HEK293T cells, the expression of VEEV saRNA is significantly higher than that of RuV 3′-250 saRNA at both 24 and 48 h regardless of the delivery method, suggesting that the expression levels of saRNAs differ in different cell lines (Figures S5A and S5B). Notably, in vivo-jetRNA delivery of saRNA caused significant cytotoxicity in DC2.4 cells (Figure S5C); therefore, RBCEVs were used for subsequent experiments.

Both saRNAs were also examined for their pro-inflammatory cytokine profiles in DCs. VEEV saRNA induced a significantly higher level of IFN-β compared to RuV 3′-250 saRNA (Figure 6D). Both RuV 3′-250 and VEEV saRNAs induced similar levels of IFN-α and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) (Figures 6E and 6F). Moreover, VEEV saRNA exhibited more cell death compared with cells transfected with RuV saRNA in both formulations upon examination by light microscopy at 48 h post-transfection (Figure S5C). These results suggest that RuV saRNA may have a cytokine response and a better safety profile compared with its VEEV counterpart.

RuV 3′-250 saRNA expression in vivo

The activity of both saRNAs, along with conventional Gluc mRNA, was tested in vivo. RuV 3′-250 saRNA showed significantly higher expression of the Gluc compared with VEEV saRNA and achieved slightly higher, although not statistically significant, expression than conventional Gluc mRNA (Figures 7A–7C). There were no changes in the body weight of immunized mice, indicating both saRNAs and mRNAs were well tolerated (Figure 7D).

We next analyzed the formation of naive (CD44−CD62L+), effector (CD44+CD62L−), and central (CD44+CD62L+) memory CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, which are crucial for the rapid clearance of previously encountered antigens, in the spleens of immunized mice. The naive and central memory CD8+ and CD4+ T cells did not show significant changes among these four groups (Figures S6A–S6C and S7A–S7C). Immunization with RuV 3′-250 saRNA induced the highest numbers of both effector memory CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in peripheral blood, with significantly greater levels compared with unloaded EVs and conventional mRNA (Figures 8A–8D).

To further elucidate the memory function of these effector memory T cells, isolated splenocytes were restimulated with Gluc protein or RuV-capsid protein, followed by analysis of intracellular Granzyme B and IFN-γ expression in CD8+ T cells. Splenocytes from all groups treated with conventional mRNA, VEEV saRNA, or RuV saRNA produced comparable levels of these cytokines upon Gluc protein restimulation, with significantly higher responses than the EV control group as shown (Figures 8E and 8F). In contrast, upon restimulation with RuV-capsid protein, only splenocytes from mice immunized with RuV saRNA exhibited elevated expression of Granzyme B and IFN-γ (Figures 8G, 8H, S8A, and S8B).

We also examined the humoral immune response after immunization by quantifying the levels of anti-Gluc IgG and anti-RuV-capsid IgG in the serum of immunized mice using ELISA at various time points post-immunization. The results showed that immunization with mRNA and VEEV saRNA induced comparable levels of anti-Gluc IgG, whereas RuV 3′-250 saRNA elicited the highest levels of this antibody, significantly surpassing those induced by conventional Gluc mRNA and VEEV saRNA (Figure 8I). In the case of anti-RuV-capsid IgG, only mice immunized with RuV 3′-250 saRNA developed detectable levels of this antibody (Figure 8J). Notably, the levels of both anti-Gluc and anti-RuV-capsid IgG continued to rise over time in the RuV 3′-250 saRNA-treated group, indicating that this saRNA is capable of inducing a durable and sustained humoral immune response.

Discussion

RNA vaccines achieved significant success in combating the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, reigniting the global interest in RNA-based therapeutics research. However, RNA therapies still face several challenges before they can be widely adopted as a universal treatment. To address these issues, numerous technologies have been developed, including but not limited to optimizing RNA cargo design and developing tissue-specific lipid carriers and in vivo delivery systems. Each component of RNA, such as the cap, 5′ UTR, ORF, 3′ UTR, and poly A tail, can be optimized to enhance target protein expression. In addition to mRNA, circular mRNA and saRNA are alternative methods to express a target protein; they can increase the total yield of protein delivered without increasing the dose unlike conventional mRNAs.6,11,39,40 Here, we describe a new saRNA vaccine vector based on the RuV 27/3 strain; the commonly used MMR vaccine is also derived from this strain. Current saRNA vectors are predominantly derived from alphaviruses, such as VEEV (utilized in ARCT-154), SINV, and SFV. Compared with current alphavirus-derived saRNAs, our saRNA vaccine vector may be safer and have fewer side effects.

However, a large proportion of the population have been vaccinated with the MMR vaccine. An important consideration for RuV-derived saRNA vaccines is the potential impact of pre-existing anti-rubella immunity, for example, from prior MMR vaccination. While the present study did not directly evaluate this effect, related work provides useful context. Fusciello et al. demonstrated that pre-vaccination with an approved MMR vaccine did not impair, and in some cases enhanced, antitumor immune responses in mice receiving an MMR-based cancer vaccine, suggesting that existing immunity does not necessarily hinder vaccine efficacy.41 In addition, studies with saRNA vaccines have shown that pre-existing immunity against replicase proteins can modulate subsequent saRNA expression or immune responses, although protective immunity can still be achieved in both mice and non-human primates.42 Together, these findings indicate that pre-existing rubella immunity may influence RuV saRNA performance, but the magnitude and nature of this effect remain to be determined. Future studies could address this question by evaluating RuV saRNA expression and antigen-specific immune responses in animal models with pre-existing rubella immunity or in ex vivo human systems using sera or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from MMR-vaccinated donors. Such studies would provide valuable insight for the development and deployment of RuV-based saRNA vaccines in populations with varying immunity backgrounds.

To enhance the target protein, we optimized the saRNA cargo design, including cap analog, transcription initiation site, polyprotein p200 gene, capsid ORF, and 3′ UTR optimization. For the transcription starting site, introducing a single guanosine upstream of a cytosine in the RuV saRNA resulted in higher Gluc expression compared with other modifications, such as GG, GGA, or GGAAATA (Figure 5A). In addition, CleanCap AG performs better than previous mRNA capping methods and offers improved stability, reduced immunogenicity, high capping efficiency and yields, making it a superior choice for mRNA research and therapeutics.43 Notably, the BNT162b2 vaccine from Pfizer-BioNTech employs the CleanCap AG co-transcriptional capping strategy.44 CleanCap AU is specifically designed to work better in saRNA, mimicking the natural structure of alphavirus RNA, therefore enabling efficient translation, higher capping efficiency, increased yields, and improved manufacturing.17 Previous research has shown that there was a significant increase in the expression of VEEV saRNA by incorporating CleanCap AU rather than Cap-0.4 However, in our experiments using both BHK-21 and Vero cells, RuV saRNA with cap-0 outperformed the CleanCap AU variant (Figures 5B and 5C), possibly due to differences in the first two 5′ UTR nucleotides—adenylate-thymine in VEEV compared with cytosine-adenylate in RuV.

The RuV p200 protein is analogous to the non-structural polyprotein in alphaviruses, which undergoes self-cleavage mediated by a papain-like protease domain to produce the multifunctional proteins p150 and p90.45 These two proteins form a replication complex that is crucial for both genomic and subgenomic RNA synthesis. The N terminal of p150 and the C terminal of p90 are critical for their interaction.46 However, the detailed mechanism of replication complex formation and the process by which the viral genome is synthesized within remains unclear. Given these uncertainties, we performed the codon optimization of the p200 region to reduce the GC content without altering the amino acid sequence. Two versions of codon-optimized p200 saRNAs were tested; one resulted in reduced Gluc expression, while the other showed almost no expression (Figure 2B and data not shown). The reduction in efficiency observed after codon optimization of p200 likely stems from the disruption of internal cis-acting RNA elements. In SINV and chikungunya virus (CHIKV), which are the bases for most saRNA systems, there are two conserved stem loops within the nsp1 coding region that are essential for the virus genome replication.47,48,49 Besides alphaviruses, the synonymous mutants in the RdRp region of poliovirus are defective in the process of viral replication and infectivity.50,51 Another research also reported that the N-terminal portion of nsp1 and the primary sequence of the 5' proximal 634 nucleotides are important for viral RNA replication in SARS-CoV-2.52 Our optimization likely altered these critical RNA secondary structures in the p200 region, which indicates that prioritizing tRNA frequency over the essential RNA-level regulatory signals may severely affect the self-amplification of saRNA.

For the saRNA vaccine vector, the GOI usually replaces the structural genes, allowing for high expression, while preventing the formation of infectious viral particles. We deleted large segments of the E2 and E1 proteins and inserted Gluc between the first 10 amino acid residues of E2 and the last part of E1. However, we found that the full length of the capsid is critical, as any truncation in the capsid protein showed significantly impaired expression (Figure 3C), which is consistent with previous research. Previously, a chimera RuV replicon was constructed by fusing two wild-type RuV strains and replacing the entire subgenomic gene with the GFP gene, by providing the capsid in cis; significantly enhanced GFP expression was observed.53,54,55,56 The capsid also performs the non-structural functions in addition to participating in virus assembly during viral infection.46 Previous studies reported that capsid can rescue the lethal mutations in the Q domain of p150, which is a proline- and arginine-rich domain.54,55,56 All these data suggest that the capsid plays an important role in the RuV replication process. The capsid protein is known to contribute to viral RNA replication by associating with cellular membranes and components of the replication complex, as well as by modulating host antiviral responses, thereby creating a more favorable intracellular environment for RNA synthesis.54,55,57,58 Importantly, although the capsid is expressed, the RuV saRNA construct lacks the structural glycoproteins E1 and E2 that are required for virion assembly and budding, making the formation of infectious particles highly unlikely. Furthermore, because the RNA is delivered as in vitro-transcribed saRNA rather than through viral infection, the likelihood of recombination events that could restore replication-competent virus is extremely low. Together, these considerations suggest that capsid expression in this system supports efficient replicon activity without posing a practical risk of particle formation or recombination, while potential immunogenicity of the expressed protein can be evaluated as part of downstream vaccine development.

Alphaviruses contain a conserved sequence about 19 nucleotides in length in their 3′ UTR, which is critical for genome replication.59,60 In contrast, RuV has a 59-nucleotide 3′ UTR, most of which is required for viral viability, as shown by site-directed mutagenesis—unlike the large 3′ UTR deletions tolerated in picornaviruses and alphaviruses.61,62,63 Although previous research also reported that several step loops exist in the 3′ terminal of the E1 coding region, their findings were limited. We introduced a stop codon upstream and demonstrated that the terminal region of E1 can function as a 3′ UTR. By predicting the secondary structure of E1 last 246 nucleotides, we found there are eight step loops in total: five of them located within E1 and the last three located in the original 59-nt 3′ UTR (Figure S2A). Retaining the last seven stem loops as the new 3′ UTR resulted in the best performance of RuV saRNA, RuV 3′-250 nt (Figure 4B).

We subsequently benchmarked the performance of RuV 3′-250 saRNA to VEEV saRNA in vitro, as it is the most commonly used vector commercially, although the VEEV saRNA activity is much higher than that of RuV 3′-250 saRNA in HEK293T cells and BHK-21 cells (Figures S5A and S5B and data not shown). However, in DC2.4 cells, the expression difference between the two is not significant (Figures 6B and 6C), which suggested that cell type greatly influences saRNA expression. The observed differences in RuV saRNA replication and innate immune activation across HEK293T, BHK-21, and DC2.4 cells are consistent with the known biology of these cell lines. BHK-21 cells are highly permissive for virus RNA replication and saRNA expression, due in part to it being an immune-incompetent cell line and the presence of abundant host factors that facilitate saRNA synthesis. HEK293T cells have limited basal expression of innate immune sensors; consistently, saRNA showed attenuated expression compared with BHK21 cells. In contrast, DC2.4 cells are DCs with robust antiviral sensing machinery, including RIG-I, MDA5, and PKR, which can detect replicating RNA and induce type I IFN responses; the saRNA has lower expression than in BHK21 and HEK293T cells. The interplay between saRNA activity and innate immune competence likely underlies the observed differences in replication efficiency and expression, highlighting the importance of cell type context when interpreting in vitro saRNA results.

Interestingly, RuV saRNA induced lower IFN-β expression and cytotoxicity compared with alphavirus-derived saRNA systems in DC2.4 cells. Innate immune recognition of replicating RNA is primarily mediated by cytosolic PRRs such as RIG-I, MDA5, and PKR, which detect viral RNA features including dsRNA intermediates generated during RNA replication. Previous studies have shown that RuV infection activates type I IFN responses predominantly through the MDA5-MAVS signaling pathway, as knockdown of MDA5 significantly reduces RuV-induced IFN-β expression and antiviral gene activation.64 The magnitude of innate immune activation is strongly influenced by the abundance and accessibility of these immunostimulatory RNA species. Positive-strand RNA viruses typically replicate within membrane-associated replication complexes that can partially shield dsRNA intermediates from host RNA sensors. Differences in the architecture or dynamics of these replication complexes may, therefore, affect the efficiency with which viral RNA is detected by RIG-I-like receptors. In addition, replication kinetics may also contribute to innate immune activation. Alphavirus replicons are characterized by rapid and robust RNA amplification,65 which can generate high levels of dsRNA intermediates and strongly activate IFN responses. In contrast, RuV replication has been reported to proceed with relatively slower kinetics,66 potentially limiting the accumulation of immunostimulatory RNA species and attenuating activation of MDA5- and PKR-mediated antiviral pathways. Together, these factors may contribute to the reduced IFN-β induction and improved cytotoxicity profile observed for RuV saRNA, supporting the potential of RuV-derived replicons as an alternative platform for saRNA vaccine development. Collectively, these findings suggest that RuV-derived replicons may represent a promising alternative saRNA platform with reduced innate immune activation and improved cellular tolerability compared with conventional alphavirus-based systems.

In this study, we utilized Gluc as a primary model to evaluate RNA delivery efficiency, antigen expression in vivo, and subsequent immune activation. Gluc offers distinct technical advantages, including its high sensitivity, approximately 100-fold greater than Renilla luciferase, and its natural secretion into the bloodstream, which allows for real-time, non-invasive, longitudinal monitoring of protein translation kinetics in vivo.67,68 Gluc reporter has also been used in saRNA or viral systems, which demonstrated its superiority to monitor saRNA in vitro or in vivo expression and viral replication, spread, or inhibition.9,69 However, it is important to acknowledge that Gluc is a marine-derived protein from Gaussia princeps and is inherently immunogenic in mammalian systems, capable of eliciting both B cell and T cell responses.70 A limitation of the present study is the relatively small number of animals used in each experimental group, which is appropriate for proof-of-concept evaluation but may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the in vivo results. Future studies with larger cohorts will be necessary to validate these findings and assess variability across populations.

RuV 3′-250 saRNA has a higher expression than both mRNA and VEEV saRNA and induces higher anti-Gluc antibody production in vivo (Figures 7A, 7B, and 8I). RuV 3′-250 saRNA also induces a similar amount of effector memory CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in peripheral blood cells as VEEV and a comparable amount of Granzyme B and IFN-γ expression in CD8+ T cells isolated from splenocytes upon Gluc protein restimulation (Figures 8A–8F). In our RuV saRNA, Gluc is expressed in-frame with the RuV capsid, elevated expressions of Granzyme B and IFN-γ were only shown in the splenocytes of the RuV saRNA immunized group, and anti-capsid antibody was also detected in the RuV saRNA injection group (Figures 8G, 8H, and 8J). Consequently, the immune profiles observed in Figure 8 likely reflect a combination of the delivery platform’s adjuvant properties and the foreign nature of the Gluc protein itself. While this confirms the platform’s capacity to successfully deliver RNA and trigger immune recognition of the encoded cargo, we recognize that the magnitude and specificity of these responses may vary when using clinically relevant human antigens. Future studies will transition to bona fide antigens, such as the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein, to further characterize the therapeutic potential and neutralizing antibody induction of this platform. Nevertheless, the current results provide a robust proof of concept for the translation and immunogenic potential of our RuV saRNA system.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and saRNA transfection

BHK-21 and Vero cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) media (Gibco); DC2.4 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 media (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37°C and with 5% CO2. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco). Before transfection, cells were seeded in various types of cell plates as required. When cells reached ∼70% confluency, an saRNA replicon was transfected into the cells using a TransIT-mRNA Transfection Kit (Mirus Bio, cat# MIR2250) as per manufacturer’s instructions.

Construction of the RuV series of saRNA

The RuV sequence was based on the RA27/3 vaccine strain (GenBank: L78917.1). The full-length sequence of the RA27/3 vaccine strain was divided into two parts by an internal EcoRV restriction enzyme site and obtained individually as synthetic DNAs and cloned into the pACYC177 vector (GenScript; other synthetic DNAs were also ordered from the same company). These two plasmids were named P1 and P2. P1 has a T7 promoter in front of 5′ UTR; P2 has a 55-nt poly A tail followed by the antisense strand ribozyme from hepatitis delta virus and EcoR1 restriction enzyme site. The plasmid contains the full-length sequence of the RA27/3 vaccine strain, named P3, which was obtained by In-Fusion Cloning kit (Takara Bio, # 638943). As per manufacturer’s instructions, 5 μL of In-Fusion product was transformed into DH5-alpha-competent E. coli cells (New England Biolabs), recovered in SOC media before being plated on ampicillin 100 μg/mL-containing agar plates, and incubated overnight at 37°C. Colonies were selected to grow in LB containing ampicillin 100 μg/mL. Plasmids were recovered with a miniprep kit (TIANGEN Biotech, #4992420), and successful constructs were identified via Sanger sequencing (BioBasic Asia).

RuV saRNA V0 was obtained based on the P3 plasmid by three-step cloning: first deleting the 11–282 amino acid residues of E2, second deleting 1–308 amino acid residues of E1, and third inserting the Gluc (cloned from trans-replicase CHIKV reporter plasmid CMV-firefly luciferase [Fluc]-Gluc from Prof. Andres Merits lab71) with 2A sequence between the E21-10 and E1309-481. The RdRp GAA mutant as well as the series of RuV capsid optimization saRNAs and RuV 3′ UTR optimization saRNAs were obtained by In-Fusion Cloning kit (Takara Bio, # 638943). Successful constructs were identified via Sanger sequencing (BioBasic Asia).

VEEV saRNA construct

The Gluc reporter was cloned into the vector T7-VEEV-GFP (Addgene #58977), which encodes a T7 promoter, 5′ UTR, followed by the VEEV replicon (nsP1-4) based on TC-83, which is a live attenuated vaccine strain of VEEV; the viral subgenomic promoter and GFP; the IRES and puromycin resistance gene (IRES-puro); 3′UTR; and poly A tail. The IRES-puro in front of 3′ UTR was deleted, the GFP gene encoded after the subgenomic promoter was replaced with Gluc via In-Fusion Cloning, and successful constructs were identified via Sanger sequencing and used to generate VEEV saRNA.

In vitro transcription

The linearized vector encoding the saRNA template was digested with a restriction enzyme, followed by agarose gel purification. In vitro transcription of DNA template was performed with the mMESSAGE mMACHINE T7 Transcription Kit (Invitrogen, cat# AM1344) as per manufacturer’s instructions in a 20-μL reaction. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C for 3 h. TurboDNAse was subsequently added and further incubated for 15 min to digest the DNA template. RNA was precipitated with LiCl and gently mixed before the reaction mixture was incubated at −20°C for 2 h or overnight. RNA was pelleted by centrifuging at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was discarded. 1 mL 80% ethanol was added to wash, followed by centrifuging at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Supernatant was removed, and the RNA pellet was dried in a biosafety cabinet for 20 min. 20 μL nuclease-free water was added to dissolve RNA, and the concentration was measured on a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific NanoDrop 2000/2000c Spectrophotometer). 500 ng of RNA was added to an equal volume of RNA loading buffer (Thermo Fisher #R0641) and incubated at 85°C for 5 min before being run on a 0.6% agarose gel to determine the length of the transcript.

For saRNAs that were transcribed with CleanCap AU or AG reagent, the plasmids were linearized in a similar manner as above. In vitro transcription of DNA template was performed with the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs #E2040) containing CleanCap AU or AG (TriLink Biotechnologies #N-7114 or N-7113) with a final concentration of 4 mM as per manufacturer’s instructions in a 40-μL reaction. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C for 3 h, and TurboDNAse was subsequently added and further incubated for 15 min to digest the DNA template. Downstream recovery of RNA by LiCl precipitation is described above.

Luciferase assay

After the saRNA replicon encoding Gluc was transfected into cells, 10 μL of supernatant was collected at various time points into a Corning 96-well microplate (cat# 3880); then, 50 μL QUANTI-LUC luciferase detection reagent containing coelenterazine substrate (Invivogen, cat# rep-qlc2) was gently added, and the luciferase activity was measured immediately by using a Biotek Synergy H1 plate reader.

Cytokine analysis

Total RNA in DC2.4 cells was extracted by TRIZOL digestion after the saRNA transfection. A LunaScript Multiplex One-Step RT-PCR kit (NEB #1555) was used to detect the specific cytokine expression.

In vivo immunization study in mice

The C57BL/6 mice were maintained at the animal facilities of the National University of Singapore and treated according to an approved protocol (protocol no.: R19-1195) granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

RuV 3′-250 saRNA (10 μg/mouse), VEEV saRNA (10 μg/mouse), and Gluc-mRNA (10 μg/mouse) were loaded onto RBCEVs; the RBCEVs alone were used as the control and then injected intramuscularly into 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice (n = 4 per group).

Luciferase expression was measured every 3 days by imaging the mice using an IVIS imaging instrument (PerkinElmer, USA). The mice were observed for 3 weeks following immunization. Coelenterazine-SOL substrate reagent (NanoLight Technologies, AZ, USA) was administered via intravenous injection at a dose of 100 μg per mouse before imaging. Bioluminescence signals were acquired immediately after substrate administration.

The level of anti-capsid and anti-luciferase antibodies in the serum of immunized mice was quantified using an ELISA assay. Briefly, a 96-well EIA/RIA plate (Sigma Aldrich, USA) was coated with 1 μg/mL capsid or luciferase protein and incubated at 4°C overnight. The plate was washed thrice with Tris-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween 20 (TBST) and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 2 h with shaking at 350 rpm. The serum samples were diluted in ELISA Assay Diluent 1× (BioLegend, USA) at a 1:200 dilution to minimize backgrounds caused by non-specific antibody binding. After the addition of the standard anti-capsid antibody (Abcam, ab34749), anti-Gluc antibody (Proteintech, 67293-1-PBS), and diluted serum samples, the plate was incubated for an hour with shaking, washed thrice with TBST before incubation for another hour with 100 μL of anti-mouse HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5,000 dilution) added to each well, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with shaking at 300 rpm. The plate was washed thrice with PBST, and 100 μL TMB was added and incubated for 10 min with shaking. Finally, 50 μL of 2N H2SO4 was added to each well, and the plate was subjected to a microplate reader to measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

Flow cytometric analysis

Total PBMCs from the blood of the treated mice were isolated using Ficoll Paque PLUS following the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, the obtained cells were incubated with anti-mouse CD16/32 antibodies (BioLegend, USA) for 10 min at room temperature before incubating with the following anti-mouse antibodies for 30 min at 4°C: CD3-PE (BiolLgend, cat. no.: 100206, 1:200 dilution), CD45-PE-Cy7 (BioLegend, cat. no.: 147704, 1:200 dilution), CD8-APC (BioLegend, cat. no.: 100712, 1:200 dilution), CD44-FITC (BD Pharmingen, cat. no.: 553133, 1:200 dilution), and CD62L-AF700 (BioLegend, cat. no.: 104426, 1:200 dilution). Dead cells were identified using SYTOX Blue Dead Cell Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were washed twice, suspended in FACS buffer, and analyzed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Splenocyte restimulation assay

Mouse spleens were mechanically ground by syringe plungers and passed through 70-μm cell strainers. After that, cells were treated with 1 mL RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend, USA) for 4 minutes at room temperature. The obtained splenocytes were cultured overnight in a 96-well U-bottom plate at a density of 2 × 106 cells/well in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, L-glutamine (800 mM), penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 mg/mL), 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol, and 20 ng/mL IL-2 (TargetMol, USA). The next day, capsid or luciferase (NanoLight, Cat#321-1) proteins were added to the cultured splenocytes at a concentration of 1 μg/mL in the presence of 1× Protein Transport Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 6 h of stimulation, the cells were washed and treated with anti-mouse CD16/32 antibodies (BioLegend, USA) for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were then incubated with the following anti-mouse antibodies for 30 min at 4°C: CD3 Brilliant Violet 421 (BioLegend, cat. no.: 100228, 1:200 dilution), CD4-APC/Fire 750 (BioLegend, cat. no.: 116019, 1:200 dilution), and CD8-FITC (BioLegend, cat. no.: 100706, 1:200 dilution). Cells were then fixed and permeabilized by using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol and were subjected to intracellular staining (30 min at 4°C) using the following anti-mouse antibodies: IFN-γ-APC (BioLegend, cat. no.: 505810, 1:200 dilution) and Granzyme B-PE (BioLegend, cat. no.: 372207, 1:200 dilution). The stained cells were washed twice, suspended in FACS buffer, and analyzed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA).

saRNA Loading onto RBCEVs

RBCEVs were purified following our established protocol.72 Since hemoglobin (Hb) is the most abundant component in RBCEVs, EV amounts were quantified by measuring the Hb concentration using NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

RBCEVs were loaded with saRNA at the ratio of 50 μg EVs:1 μg RNA by using REG1 reagent (Carmine Therapeutics, Singapore) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 1 μg saRNA was incubated with 7 μg REG-1 in Opti-MEM medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL for saRNA and 70 μg/mL for REG-1, respectively, for 10 min at room temperature. Afterward, 50 μg EVs at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in Opti-MEM medium were added to the mixture of REG-1 and saRNA at 37°C for 30 min with gentle rotation. Finally, the saRNA-loaded RBCEVs were washed with PBS by centrifuging at 21,000 × g for 30 min.

To quantify the loaded amount of saRNA onto EVs, 20 μg of loaded EVs were resuspended in 16 μL of 0.1% Triton X-(Sigma Aldrich, USA) in nuclease-free water and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Next, 4 μL of heparin sulfate solution (100 mg/mL, Sigma Aldrich, USA) was added to the lysed EVs, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h with shaking at 100 rpm. After incubation, 4 μL of 6× DNA loading dye (New England Biolabs, USA) was added to the mixture, which was then subjected to electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel at 100 volts. Agarose gels were finally imaged using a Gel Documentation system (Gel Doc XR+, Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA).
