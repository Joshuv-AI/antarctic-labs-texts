Comparison of the healing rate with meniscal repair concomitant with anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction and isolated meniscal repair based on magnetic resonance imaging signal intensity
Authors: Yongun Cho, Jin Hwan Ahn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to compare the healing rate of meniscal repair concomitant with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction and isolated meniscal repair based on Intrameniscal signal intensity (IMSI).MethodsA total of 368 patients who underwent meniscus repair between March 2011 and July 2017 by an experienced single surgeon were enrolled. 228 patients were divided into the meniscus repair concomitant with ACL reconstruction (group A, n = 171) and isolated meniscal repair group (group B, n = 57). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed preoperatively and 12 months postoperatively, measurements were conducted from the most prominent slice of meniscal tear (PSMT). IMSI of the PSMT was measured with the free line region-of-interest (ROI) tool in a picture archiving and communication system.ResultsExcept for time of accident, no significant differences were found in terms of age, body mass index, posterior slope angle, or varus angle between two groups. There were statistically significant differences of post-operative adjusted mean IMSI of patients between the two groups using ANCOVA. The corrected postoperative adjusted mean IMSI in the coronal view of group A was an estimated mean (SD) of 1.44(0.08), and in group B was an estimated mean (SD) of 2.55(0.15). All the P-values were less than 0.05, which was the same compared with MRI values for healed meniscus.ConclusionsIMSI is a simple and conventional parameter for the assessment of meniscal healing. The healing rate of meniscal repair concomitant with ACL reconstruction compared with isolated meniscal repair using IMSI was similar to that of MRI reading. Thus, concomitant ACL reconstruction significantly improves the healing process of meniscus.Level of evidence and study designCase control study.

Introduction

The meniscus acts as a spacer in the knee joint, to provide load transmission,1 shock absorption2 and joint stability.3,4 Trauma and degenerative changes are common causes of meniscal tears. Meniscectomy, meniscal repair and meniscal transplantation are surgical treatment options for the management of meniscal tears. Meniscal repair resulted in a higher reoperation rate than meniscectomy, but yielded better long-term outcomes.5 Therefore, the repair of amenable meniscal tears is key to prevent degenerative changes. Tear location, tear type, tear length, and patient age affect meniscal healing after repair.6,7

Concomitant reconstruction of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) has a positive effect on the healing process of meniscus.8,9 Sgaglione et al.10 suggested that associated ACL reconstruction or associated chondral procedure were prognostic factors for meniscus repair. However, other studies suggested that ACL reconstruction did not affect the healing rate of meniscus.11

Postoperative evaluation of meniscal repair is based on clinical assessment, second-look arthroscopy, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Because of the advantages of noninvasiveness, MRI has been used widely. Okazaki et al.12 measured intra-meniscal signal intensity (IMSI) of posterior horn of the medial meniscus after the root tear repair to assess meniscal compositional change.

The purpose of this study was to compare the healing rate of meniscal repair concomitant with ACL reconstruction and isolated meniscal repair based on IMSI. The hypothesis is that the healing rate of meniscal repair concomitant with ACL reconstruction group based on IMSI is higher than isolated meniscal repair group.

Materials and methods

Subjects

A total of 368 patients who underwent meniscus repair between March 2011 and July 2017 by an experienced single surgeon were enrolled. The inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) a meniscus tear diagnosed via preoperative MRI, (2) a 12-month minimum follow-up via postoperative MRI, and (3) age less than 40 years. The exclusion criteria were: (1) multiple ligamentous injuries including high-grade partial ruptures of the medial collateral ligament and posterolateral complex, (2) surgical history of the knee or other arthropathies, such as rheumatoid arthritis, and (3) cartilage lesions with modified Outerbridge grade ≥3. Based on these criteria, 228 patients were enrolled and divided into the meniscus repair concomitant with ACL reconstruction (group A, n = 171) and isolated meniscal repair group, consisting of patients who did not have an associated ACL tear. (group B, n = 57). This retrospective comparative study was performed after obtaining approval of the Institutional Review Board (approval No.2019-08-036-001). Signed informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Surgical procedure

A single surgeon performed all the surgical procedures via an arthroscopic-assisted technique. After diagnostic arthroscopy, all meniscal repairs were performed prior to ACL reconstruction to avoid poor arthroscopic visualization caused by scattering of bone debris and drainage of saline in the joint through the bone tunnel. All meniscus repairs were performed using an all-inside or outside-in technique with a spinal needle, depending on the location and shape of the tear site (Fig. 1). Following the meniscus repair, trephination was performed at the meniscal tear site with a spinal needle to stimulate a local biological response and promote healing. In group A, in order to obtain clear visualization to observe the posterolateral aspect of the ACL femoral footprint, the posterolateral portal was made using a trans-illumination technique through the interval between the ACL and lateral femoral condyle. The 70° arthroscope was then inserted through the PL portal and advanced to reach the posterior aspect of the intercondylar notch. After making a PL portal, semitendinosus and gracilis tendons (harvested using a tendon stripper) were prepared as four-strand double-looped hamstring autografts. For creation of the tibial tunnel, a guide pin was inserted from the proximal medial tibial condyle to the center of the ACL tibial insertion using an ACL tibial drill guide (Linvatec, Largo, FL, USA) with a 50°. A cannulated reamer was used to match the diameter of the graft.

The femoral tunnel was usually adjusted to 7–9 mm according to the graft diameter. Under visualization through the PL portal, the ACL guide was inserted in the AM portal and guide tip was located at a point of about 12 mm anterior potion from the posterior cartilage margin of the femur. The femoral tunnel was placed within the inner margin of the cartilage and reamed with FlipCutter (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA). After retro-reaming to a depth of 30 mm on the femoral tunnel, the FlipCutter was removed and the ACL graft was passed through the tunnel. The RetroButton (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) was flipped to establish femoral fixation. After the graft was secured on the femoral side, the tibial side was fixed with a bio-absorbable interference screw and tied to a 4.0 mm cancellous screw post.

Postoperative rehabilitation

Postoperative care consisted of non-weight-bearing exercises and full extension with a knee immobilizer brace. Limited weight-bearing and range of motion exercises were administered starting 2 weeks postoperatively regardless of type of tear, progressing to full-weight bearing by postoperative week 6. The range of motion was increased by 30° every week to 120°. Running, swimming, and cycling were initiated at 3 months. Unrestricted activity was permitted at 6 months for patients with isolated meniscal repair and at 9 months for patients with meniscal repair and concomitant ACL reconstruction.

Clinical assessment

Clinical and functional tests were administered before surgery and at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively. All patients adhered to this follow-up schedule, with less than 5% of patients having a follow-up visit within ±2 weeks of the scheduled time point. Baseline patient characteristics (age, sex, height, weight, duration of surgical procedure, and follow-up duration), preoperative clinical scores (IKDC score, Lysholm score), and final follow-up clinical scores were compared between group A and group B.

Imaging assessment

X-ray of lower extremity view, anterior-posterior and lateral view of a knee joint were performed preoperatively. First, using a knee joint lateral tibia view, the tibial slope was measured using the method based on the proximal tibial anatomical axis.13,14 The diaphyseal axis of the tibia was obtained from two points equidistant between the anterior and posterior borders of the tibia: one just below the anterior tibial tubercle and the other 10 cm distal to it. A reference line at the level of the femorotibial joint was drawn perpendicularly to the diaphyseal axis line. Another line was drawn using the highest two points of the anterior and posterior edges of the medial plateau. The angle between this line and the reference line is defined as the tibial slope.14 Second, varus angle was assessed by measuring hip-knee-ankle angle in a preoperative standing lower extremity view. The hip center was identified using a Mose circle.15 The center of the knee joint was identified as the apex of the intercondylar notch. The center of the talus determined the center of the ankle joint. The femoral mechanical axis was then defined as a line joining the center of the hip and the center of the knee, with the tibial mechanical axis being a line joining the center of the knee and the center of the ankle. The hip-knee-ankle ankle was defined as the angle between the femoral mechanical axis and the tibial mechanical axis.

MRI was performed preoperatively and 12 months postoperatively using a 3.0-T MRI scanner (Achieva, Philips, Best, The Netherlands) and an eight-element (SENSE) phased array, knee-joint-dedicated coil. After assessing all the T2 coronal fat-suppressed proton density-weighted image (TR/TE, 4400/30) slices for each knee, which was acquired with a slice thickness of 3.0 mm, a field of view (FOV) of 100 mm, and a matrix size of 320 × 320, measurements were conducted from the most prominent slice of meniscal tear (PSMT) by single orthopedic surgeon, in order to obtain a high positive predictive value. The PSMT was identified in either the anterior horn or the posterior horn. Intrameniscal signal intensity (IMSI) of the PSMT was measured with the free line region-of-interest (ROI) tool in a picture archiving and communication system (PI View STAR, version 5025; Infinitt, Seoul, South Korea). The ROI was marked by drawing a freehand line along the edge of the PSMT (Fig. 2). The image was then defined by delineating the size and the color intensity using the specialized software of the picture archiving and communication system. The color level indicating the mean value of signal intensity was obtained from the screen, and the value representing the maximum area at a particular cut was used for the analysis. A higher IMSI value, approaching the signal of an unhealed state, indicates poorer healing. A lower IMSI value, approaching the signal of an intact meniscus, indicates better healing. Because IMSI varied between the examinations due to inconsistent screen contrast, we decided to use the ratio of signal intensity of the PSMT to that of the means of two controls with intact middle segments of contralateral meniscus for standardization of the signal intensity. Similarly, T2 sagittal fat-suppressed proton density-weighted image (TR/TE, 4400/30) slices were used, and the measurements were obtained from the anterior and the posterior horns (Fig. 3). The signal intensity of the PSMT was compared with that of the two adjacent slices of the intact horn on the ipsilateral meniscus, which was on the opposite side of the tear (e.g., if the tear was in the posterior horn, the anterior horn was used as a reference). The ratio of signal intensity of the PSMT was calculated. This normalization method, employing an internal control within the same knee joint, was necessitated by the retrospective study design, wherein MRI was performed exclusively on the affected knee. While our specific method of defining the reference point differs from some previous studies, the principle of using a signal intensity ratio for quantitative analysis is a widely accepted method to correct for variations in MRI parameters. IMSI ratios were measured twice (preoperatively and at the final follow-up) in the sagittal view of anterior and posterior horn area and coronal view. Zones of meniscus were divided to red-red zone, red-white zone and white-white zone based on the blood supply. All values from images were measured by single orthopedic surgeon to minimize inter-observer reliability. All measurements of IMSI were repeated by single orthopedic surgeon at a two-week interval at both the preoperative and final follow-up assessments. The surgeon was blinded to the results of the initial measurement.Fig. 3T2 sagittal fat-suppressed proton density-weighted image showing measurement sites. Statistical analyses

All the values are expressed in terms of mean ± standard deviation, depending on the characteristics of the parameters. All data were subjected to statistical analyses using the Mann–Whitney U test, the Student t-test, or the chi-square test. The Pre- and post-IMSI values were assessed using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA). The intra-observer reliability of the IMSI measurement was evaluated with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) model using two-way and absolute agreement with the single measurement model. All values with a P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM CO., Armonk, New York, USA).

The mean IKDC score for Group A increased from 54.02 ± 6.30 preoperatively to 89.25 ± 5.12 postoperatively (P < 0.05). The mean Lysholm score similarly improved from 68.00 ± 8.50 to 95.00 ± 4.30 (P < 0.05). A correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between the postoperative IMSI and clinical outcomes. A statistically significant negative correlation was found between the postoperative IMSI in the sagittal anterior view and postoperative IKDC score (r = −0.17, P < 0.05) and Lysholm score (r = −0.18, P < 0.05) in Group A. No other significant correlations were observed in other views or in Group B.

Discussion

Our goal was to determine the superiority of concomitant meniscal repair with ACL reconstruction compared with meniscal repair alone for the treatment of meniscal tears using signal intensity of MRI. Many studies revealed that the signal abnormality at the repair site may persist despite a healed meniscus in standard MRI.16,17 Okazaki et al.12 evaluated the result of pullout repair in the medial meniscus using MRI signal intensity. Song et al.18 reported that 3D MRI combined with signal-to-noise ratio may improve the reliability of evaluation of the repaired meniscus.

Several studies have compared the results of meniscal repair alone with those of meniscal repair concomitant with ACL reconstruction. A few studies reported that repair combined with ACL reconstruction enhanced the healing percentage compared with an isolated suture.19,20 The hemorrhage generated during the ACL reconstruction, rich in pluripotent stem cells, platelets and growth factors21,22 may affect the healing outcome.

Toman et al.23 reported the overall clinical success rate of meniscal repair ranged from 70 % to 95 %. Ana et al.24 reported superior clinical results in meniscus repair concomitant with ACL reconstruction but their study did not involve objective measurements such as second-look arthroscopy or postoperative MRI. Most of the previous studies evaluated surgical outcomes based on clinical symptoms. Meniscus healing rate are generally lower than the clinical success rate. Ahn et al.6 reviewed meniscal repairs among 140 patients undergoing simultaneous arthroscopic ACL reconstruction, and found that 84.3 % of meniscal repairs were completely healed at the time of second-look arthroscopy. However, the clinical success rate was 96.4 %, because most patients in the incompletely healed group manifested no clinical symptoms associated with residual meniscal tears.

The two approaches to determine healing after meniscus repair include postoperative MRI and second-look arthroscopy. The second-look arthroscopy facilitates visual assessment of the condition of meniscus and evaluation of the meniscus for tears undetectable on MRI. However, it is an invasive and expensive technique, requiring anesthesia.

Many studies have confirmed the limitation of conventional MRI for the evaluation of healing status of the meniscus after short-term repair.25, 26, 27 However, Hantes et al.28 reported the evaluation of healing process of meniscus via indirect MRI at 3, 6 and 12 months after the operation. In each of the 20 patients who participated in this study, hypersignal was detected at three months postoperatively. A significant decrease in this hypersignal occurred from three to 12 months, suggesting that the meniscal healing lasted more than 12 months. Miao et al.29 evaluated the MRI signal characteristics and MRI diagnostic accuracy using second-look arthroscopy to confirm complete healing of repaired meniscus. A T2 hypersignal was found in 63 % of the 38 cases at a mean follow-up of 16 months. MRI diagnostic accuracy was correlated positively with the follow-up time. Farley et al.30 compared arthrography and MRI in the evaluation of meniscal healing after repair. T2 fat-saturated MRI sequences showed a sensitivity of 60 % and a specificity of 90 % for meniscal healing. Arthrography was associated with sensitivity and specificity of 90 %. A decrease in IMSI over time reflects improved tissue organization and has been correlated with findings from second-look arthroscopy.26

In conclusion, postoperative MRI can be used to confirm the healing status of meniscus non-invasively. However, further comparative studies are needed to assess the accuracy of arthro-MRI for the management of repaired meniscus.27 In order to rule out the ambiguity of MRI and limitations associated with subjective evaluation of the observer, we used the objective IMSI method to indicate the status of postoperative meniscus.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of this study is associated with an inherent selection bias. Second, compared with group A, the group B enrolled fewer patients and only identified meniscus healing occurring within 12 months, suggesting the need for studies comparing meniscal healing 12 months after surgery. Third, type of tear and surgical methods are not consistent because suture technique was used based on the tear condition. However, a few reports suggest that the suture method is not related to healing rate.31 Fourth, a large number of meniscal tears are traumatic, and we failed to determine whether or not isolated meniscal tears were triggered by trauma. Finally, as a retrospective study, a formal power analysis was not performed to determine the required sample size. Therefore, the heterogeneous sample sizes between the two groups are a limitation. However, we believe that the total sample size of 228 patients was sufficient to detect statistically significant differences in our primary outcomes, which was confirmed by a post-hoc power analysis that revealed a power of 92.9 % for the sagittal anterior horn.

Conclusions

IMSI is a simple and conventional parameter for the assessment of meniscal healing. The healing rate of meniscal repair concomitant with ACL reconstruction compared with isolated meniscal repair using IMSI was similar to that of MRI reading. Thus, our findings indicate that concomitant ACL reconstruction significantly improves the healing process of meniscus.
