Investigative reporting on factor VIII whereabouts
Authors: Kathleen P. Pratt
Published: 2026

Despite being a long-studied and essential blood coagulation cofactor, factor VIII (FVIII) has been notoriously difficult to pin down, that is, to localize in cells and tissues, using conventional methods, such as immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence staining. This is primarily because of its low expression levels in both tissues and plasma. In this issue of Blood Advances, Severeid et al1 explored FVIII expression patterns using a novel, transgenic, hemophilia A (HA) F8 promoter-reporter mouse model that expresses the fluorescent protein mScarlet under control of the endogenous F8 promoter.

Hepatocytes, hepatic endothelial cells (ECs), and hepatic immune cells were isolated from these mice, and mScarlet fluorescence was detected only in the hepatic ECs using flow cytometry. The fluorescent signal was not sufficiently strong to localize mScarlet expression in liver sections, but, fortunately, an anti-mScarlet antibody produced more robust staining, showing FVIII expression in livers from mScarlet mice but not from negative control C57Bl/6 HA mice. Costaining with anti-CD31 and anti–von Willebrand factor (VWF) antibodies confirmed FVIII localization to liver sinusoidal ECs (LSECs) with brighter FVIII staining closer to the central veins of hepatic lobules (acinar zones 2 and 3). These results were consistent with definitive studies in which EC-directed knockout of F8 or of the LMAN1 gene that encodes an essential FVIII transporter protein produced mice with severe HA.2,3

Cryosections of healthy human liver were then stained for FVIII and VWF, which showed a similar acinar zonal pattern for both FVIII and VWF expression. Thus, a novel finding in the study is visualization of a zonation pattern, which indicates differential FVIII expression not only in distinct subsets of ECs, as has been reported elsewhere,4,5 but also according to anatomic location within liver lobules. Additional murine tissues were then examined for mScarlet expression by immunofluorescent staining and/or flow cytometry. Significant mScarlet expression was detected only in renal ECs, albeit at much lower levels than in the LSECs. Although staining did not reveal a zonal distribution, cells that coexpressed a marker for renal peritubular ECs were negative for mScarlet fluorescence, indicating that only specific EC subsets express FVIII in the kidney.

The investigators subsequently analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data that were collected as part of a separate study of immunodeficient, humanized mice that were infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV) and then underwent transplantation with primary human hepatocytes. Livers from both infected and uninfected mice were perfused and subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing, followed by analysis of the data from the human liver cells in these samples. Although most of these human cells were identified as hepatocytes, as expected, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and hierarchical clustering revealed a small but distinct cluster that was annotated as human ECs, indicating that human ECs were also reconstituted in these mice. F8 transcripts were found only in this small cluster, providing further confirmatory evidence that F8 is expressed in human ECs with no detectable expression in human hepatocytes.

Adding further rigor to their localization of mScarlet expression in specific liver zones of the transgenic mice, mScarlet expression levels were measured by flow cytometry in CD32bhi LSECs from reporter mice, which also localize to zones 2+3, and compared it with the levels in CD32b−/low LSECs. MScarlet expression was higher in the CD32bhi cells. The F8 mRNA transcript levels were subsequently measured in CD32bhi and CD32b−/low LSECs from wild-type C57BL/6 mice, and higher F8 expression in the CD32bhi LSECs was consistent with the mScarlet reporter mouse results.

Multiple studies have shown that FVIII is expressed in and/or secreted from human ECs isolated from distinct vascular beds, including the lungs, although the liver is acknowledged as the primary organ that produces FVIII.6, 7, 8 A 1971 report confirmed extrahepatic FVIII expression in dogs, because transplantation of an HA liver into a non-HA dog did not cause severe HA.9 Interestingly, the present study detected mScarlet only in the liver and some renal ECs in the reporter mice. Several reports have shown that transplantation of a non-HA kidney into a patient with severe HA does not correct HA. Thus, the mScarlet staining in murine renal ECs may reflect a mouse-human species difference, or the renal FVIII expression level may be too low to affect overall hemostasis. Additional species differences were seen. Staining of human liver cryosections revealed zonal gradations in both FVIII and VWF, whereas mice showed more uniform VWF staining in LSECs, and the central vein walls in human liver did not show staining, indicating FVIII expression, unlike the positive mScarlet staining observed in central vein ECs in liver tissues from the reporter mice.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis of healthy and diseased human liver tissues was subsequently carried out, and disease-associated zonal variations were seen in the levels of transcripts encoding CD32b and CD34 but not CD31; the transcriptomics panel did not include measurement of F8 levels, but because FVIII co-localizes with CD32b, this suggested that a similar pattern might be seen in FVIII expression. Indeed, immunofluorescence staining of human liver cryosections revealed stronger FVIII expression in zones 2+3 of the diseased liver sections when compared with the same zones in healthy liver; this was consistent with the fact the FVIII is an acute phase protein.

As the authors point out, the acinar zonal variation in the FVIII levels in LSECs may be related to differences in oxygen pressure, blood flow/shear stress, or nutrient content of the blood that enters the lobule in zone 1 vs the blood that exits through the central vein in zone 3. Recent elegant studies have revealed distinct properties of LSECs in these zones, including variations in the expression of various proteins, suggesting that they fulfill specific roles in processes that occur at the blood/LSEC interface.9, 10, 11 This zonal variation in FVIII expression has now been visualized in murine,1 canine,12 and human1 LSECs. Some variability in staining patterns remains, which may reflect genuine differences between tissues or species or, in some cases, may be a consequence of technical issues, including (imperfect) specificity of the primary or secondary antibodies.12 Future studies can bring further clarity by analyzing additional images from healthy and diseased/inflamed tissues using validated antibodies and reagents and by confirming the results using different antibodies and/or methods. Blood coagulation needs to occur exactly when/where it is needed; future research can further explore the roles of distinct anatomic sites of FVIII production/secretion in both normal and perturbed hemostasis.
