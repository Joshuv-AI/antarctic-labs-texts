Subcutaneous abscess caused by Chondrostereum purpureum, an unusual infection from plant to human: The first case report in a transplant recipient
Authors: Piriya Kijacharoenchai, Arsa Thammahong, Natavudh Townamchai, Jakapat Vanichanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFilamentous basidiomycetes have been recognized as emerging causes of human infection. Schizophyllum commune is the most frequently reported, whereas infections caused by other species are exceedingly rare. We report a case of subcutaneous abscess caused by Chondrostereum purpureum in a kidney transplant recipient. Definitive identification was achieved by sequencing the internal transcribed spacer and the D1/D2 regions. The patient was successfully treated with the surgical procedure and oral posaconazole. This report emphasizes the potential pathogenicity of plant fungi and the importance of molecular tools for identification.Keywords: Subcutaneous abscess, Chondrotereum purpureum, Filamentous basidiomycetes, Molecular identification

Introduction

Basidiomycota is a major phylum with the second-highest number of species in the Kingdom of Fungi. Well-known human pathogens in this phylum include basidiomycete yeasts, such as Cryptococcus, while filamentous basidiomycetes (FBM) are commonly identified as environmental fungi [1]. The pathogenic role of FBM in humans has increasingly been recognized. The spectrum of disease ranges from allergic disorders to severe invasive infections, especially in immunocompromised patients. The majority of infections were caused by Schizophyllum commune, followed by Hormographiella aspergillata and Ceriporia lacerata [2].

Chondrostereum purpureum is a well-known plant pathogen causing silver leaf disease in trees and shrubs. Its spores spread through airborne dispersal, landing on freshly exposed wood wounds, particularly during the wet season [3]. While this fungus commonly affects plants, infection in humans is exceedingly rare, with only one case previously reported, causing paratracheal abscess in an immunocompetent individual [4]. Here, we report the first case of subcutaneous abscess due to C. purpureum in a post–kidney transplant patient.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old Thai female patient presented to the outpatient department with a progressively enlarging mass on the right shoulder for two months. The patient also complained of low-grade fever and mild tenderness around an affected area. She had received a 1-week course of amoxicillin/clavulanic acid from her primary care physician for empirical therapy without improvement. She had a history of end-stage renal disease due to pre-eclampsia and underwent deceased donor kidney transplantation one year earlier. Her immunosuppressive regimen included tacrolimus 2 mg/day, mycophenolate mofetil 2000 mg/day, and prednisolone 5 mg/day. She also took acyclovir and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole as antimicrobial prophylaxis. The patient denies trauma or injury around the right shoulder area. She was a housewife with limited exposure to soil or plants.

On examination, her body temperature was 38.2 °C. A 4 × 10 cm warm, tender, fluctuant subcutaneous mass was noted on her right shoulder (Fig. 1). The other physical examination was unremarkable. Needle aspiration yielded 10 ml of pale green pus. Gram and GMS stains revealed branching septate hyphae consistent with filamentous mold. After one week, greyish velvety colonies grew on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) without reverse pigmentation (Fig. 2). Lactophenol cotton blue staining demonstrated non-sporulating hyaline septate hyphae with clamp connections (Fig. 3).

Subsequently, sequencing of the ITS and D1/D2 regions identified the organism as Chondrostereum purpureum. The patient also underwent incision and drainage, and posaconazole was started as definitive therapy. Anti-fungal treatment was discontinued after a full 6-month course. The patient was well and showed no evidence of recurrence at 1-year follow-up.

Discussion

Filamentous fungi in the phylum Ascomycota are well known as the primary pathogens causing disease in humans. On the other hand, FBM are identified as environmental fungi abundantly found in soil, wood, or decaying material. Until recently, FBM infections have gained increasing recognition. According to the literature review, 218 cases of human infection have been reported worldwide. Schizophyllum commune was the most common causative pathogen, accounting for more than 50% of cases [2]. The respiratory system was commonly involved, which causes a broad spectrum of disease ranging from allergic disorders such as allergic fungal sinusitis or allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis to fungal sinusitis or pneumonia. However, retrieving FBM from respiratory specimens should be interpreted with caution, as environmental contamination is possible [5].

Besides respiratory infections, disseminated and skin and soft tissue infections have also been reported. Similar to our case, the pathogenic role of FBM was less skeptical as the specimens were obtained from sterile sites and diagnosis was confirmed by biopsy [2]. Potential pathogenesis of skin and soft tissue infection due to FBM included direct inoculation in immunocompetent patients and disseminated disease in immunocompromised hosts, especially transplant recipients [2,6,7].

Among pathogenic FBM, C. purpureum has been rarely reported as a cause of human disease. Only one confirmed case of an immunocompetent individual with paratracheal abscess has been reported to date [4]. Interestingly, the reported patient worked as a plant mycologist and had a substantial opportunity to be exposed to a high inoculum of C. purpureum during his career. However, our patient had no significant contact history. This fact underscores the potential for C. purpureum to assume a more pathogenic role in humans, even without clear exposure. The cross-kingdom may be potentiated by several factors, including global warming, changing ecosystems, urbanization, and the increase in immunosuppressed patients [3].

Invasive fungal disease is an essential infectious complication in solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients, as it significantly impacts 1-year survival in this population. Candida (53%) was the most common causative agent, followed by Aspergillus (19%) and Cryptococcus (8%), according to data from the Transplant-Associated Infection Surveillance Network (TRANSNET) [8]. Interestingly, unspecified mold was reported at up to 2%, indicating a burden of rare mold, including FBM, in this patient population. Conventional identification from the colony is difficult due to the lack of specific morphological features. Clamp connections in microscopic findings, as observed in our case, are considered characteristic of FBM and have been reported in more than 70% of common species [9]. Currently, sequence analysis of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and D1/D2 ribosomal regions appears to be an attractive molecular identification method for FBM and should be performed in suspicious cases [10,11].

Another challenging aspect of infection caused by FBM is anti-fungal therapy, since an established break point or epidemiological cutoff value is not available. Anti-fungal susceptibility test demonstrated low MIC90 of amphotericin B, itraconazole, voriconazole, and posaconazole against almost all common species [2]. These anti-fungal agents can be considered as a therapeutic option for invasive infection due to FBS. Unfortunately, the in vitro susceptibility of C. purpureum has never been tested and reported in the literature. Based on a single case report, voriconazole was used for treatment, with a favorable outcome [4]. Similarly, our patient was successfully treated with posaconazole. Therefore, using triazole anti-fungal agents with activity against filamentous mold, including itraconazole, voriconazole, and posaconazole, is reasonable.

In conclusion, we reported the second case of human infection with C. purpureum, the first in a transplant recipient. The case emphasizes the importance of emerging infections due to FBM, especially among those with immunocompromised conditions. Identification can be difficult; molecular analysis is helpful and should be considered.
