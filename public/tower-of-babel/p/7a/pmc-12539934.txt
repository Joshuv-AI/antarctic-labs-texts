Retinal Microvascular Correlates of Cerebral Small Vessel Disease in Older Age
Authors: Louis Arnould, Aïcha Soumaré, Timothy D'Aoust, Sara Cristina Lima Rebouças, Constance Bordes, Fabrice Crivello, Jean-François Korobelnik, Michel Paques, Muralidharan Sargurupremraj, Aniket Mishra, Yi-Cheng Zhu, Cécile Delcourt, Catherine Helmer, Anthony Khawaja, Pirro Hysi, Christine Binquet, Catherine Creuzot-Garcher, Stéphanie Debette
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesCerebral small vessel disease (cSVD), characterized by pathologic changes in the structure and function of small brain vessels, is detectable on brain MRI in the absence of clinical symptoms. However, imaging cerebral small vessels themselves in vivo remains costly and challenging. There is growing interest in investigating whether retinal microvascular imaging features could be proxies for changes in the brain microvasculature. Using a multipronged approach, we explored the relation of retinal microvascular characteristics with MRI markers of cSVD (MRI-cSVD).MethodsFirst, we explored this relationship in older community persons from the population-based 3C-Dijon cohort. MRI-cSVD was assessed on a 1.5-Tesla MRI at baseline, comprising white matter hyperintensity volume (WMHV), lacunes, and a composite extreme cSVD phenotype (WMHV extreme distributions and presence/absence of lacunes). At 10-year follow-up, participants underwent measurements of retinal microvascular features on fundus using the Singapore “I” Vessel Assessment software. To support 3C-Dijon findings, we conducted a comprehensive literature review up to July 2024 from PubMed/EMBASE and used 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) leveraging large-scale genome-wide association studies, to assess causality and directionality.ResultsIn 670 3C-Dijon participants (median age 70.7, 65.7% women), multivariable analyses (adjusted for age, sex, axial length, and cardiovascular risk factors) showed a significant association of lower arteriolar fractal dimension (FDa) with extreme cSVD (odds ratio [OR] 1.68, 95% CI 1.20–2.34) after multiple testing correction (p < 0.0042), and at p < 0.05, associations of lower FDa and smaller arteriolar caliber with larger WMHV (β = 0.0534 [95% CI 0.0075–0.0569] and 0.0519 [95% CI 0.0045–0.0993]), and of greater venular tortuosity (TORTv) with lacunes (OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.05–2.00). Lower FDa was also associated with poorer executive function. The systematic review of the literature identified 12 studies (N = 7,796) that showed mostly consistent direction of effects for FDa (5/6 studies), TORTv (4/6), and arteriolar caliber (10/11), although statistical significance was observed in 5 individual studies only. Two-sample MR based on large genome-wide association studies (N = 5,292–52,798) showed evidence for a potentially causal association of greater TORTv with extreme cSVD and larger WMHV (p = 0.0017 and 0.049), with no evidence for reverse causation.DiscussionWe provide multimodal evidence that geometric characteristics of the retinal microvasculature are associated with increased burden of MRI-cSVD and possibly worse executive function.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD) is a leading cause of stroke and dementia.1 Covert cSVD is commonly detected on brain imaging in the general population with increasing age, in the absence of clinical manifestations, and is associated with a considerably increased risk of stroke and dementia,1 making it a major target for prevention.2 cSVD arises from alterations of the structure and/or function of small brain vessels.1 However, the specific nature of these alterations and how they lead to cSVD consequences on the brain parenchyma, such as white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), lacunes, and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), remain poorly understood partly because small brain vessels are challenging to visualize in vivo. Several pathologic processes may coexist (arteriolosclerosis, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, atheroma, etc.), and various pathophysiologic mechanisms are likely to contribute at different stages of the disease process, such as blood-brain barrier dysfunction, endothelial and smooth muscle cell dysfunction, inflammation, or altered hemodynamics.e1-e3 High blood pressure is the strongest known risk factor for cSVD, a condition that is also highly heritable with numerous genetic susceptibility factors identified. Currently, cSVD diagnosis relies on the detection of its consequences on the brain parenchyma on MRI (WMH, lacunes, and CMB),3 thus capturing already advanced disease.

There is growing interest in exploring the potential of retinal microvascular imaging features to serve as a low-cost, noninvasive, easy access surrogate marker for changes in the brain microvasculature, given shared embryonic origins, morphologic, and physiologic properties of both microvascular systems.4 Recent reviews reported inconsistent associations between fundus-based retinal microvascular alterations and MRI markers of cSVD (MRI-cSVD).5-7 Most studies focused on individual MRI-cSVD features (e.g., WMH, lacunes, and CMBs), although these often co-occur. Previous studies also focused primarily on qualitative retinal features or vessel calibers,8-13 with limited data on quantitative geometric parameters such as retinal vessel tortuosity (TORT) or fractal dimension (FD),8,9,12 which have been associated with stroke and dementia.4,14 Moreover, the causal nature of associations between retinal microvascular features (as proxies for cerebral microvascular changes) and MRI-cSVD remains unexplored.

In the present work, we first examined the relation of fundus-based quantitative retinal microvascular markers, including geometric features, with lacunes, WMH volume (WMHV), and a composite extreme MRI-cSVD phenotype (extreme cSVD), in the population-based 3C-Dijon cohort. We recently described that the extreme cSVD phenotype enhanced power to detect genetic risk variants for cSVD15,16 and was strongly associated with increased risk of stroke and dementia,16,17 but its relation with retinal features is unexplored. We assessed the relation of cSVD-associated retinal microvascular features with concurrent cognitive performance. Second, for retinal parameters associated with MRI-cSVD in 3C-Dijon, to place our results into context, we conducted a comprehensive review of published studies exploring these relation. Third, for a triangulation of evidence and to explore the potentially causal nature and directionality of observed associations, we conducted a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) experiment leveraging large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWASs) of retinal microvascular parameters and MRI-cSV.

Methods

Observational Study

Study Population

Data were derived from the 3C-Dijon study, a French population-based cohort of 4,931 noninstitutionalized individuals aged 65 years or older randomly selected from the city of Dijon's electoral rolls (1999–2001, baseline).18 Participants aged younger than 80 years (n = 2,763) were invited to a brain MRI substudy (1999–2000); 2,285 agreed but 1,924 scans were performed because of funding restrictions.18 Baseline examinations consisted of face-to-face interviews, physical and cognitive examinations, and fasting blood sampling. Over 12 years, 6 follow-up visits occurred.

Of the 2,283 participants re-examined at the fifth (10-year) follow-up of 3C-Dijon, a subsample of 1,663 was invited to participate in the Montrachet ancillary study, designed to explore the relation of age-related eye diseases with neurologic/heart diseases in the elderly. Montrachet consisted in a comprehensive eye examination (including examinations such as fundus photography) conducted at the Ophthalmology Department of Dijon University Hospital. Invitations were extended in priority to participants with a prior brain MRI from the 3C-Dijon baseline imaging substudy (n = 1,163), and then to a random sample of 500 individuals taken from the 1,120 remaining participants without MRI. Of the 1,663 individuals invited, 1,153 agreed to participate (n = 915 with MRI).

MRI Acquisition at Baseline and MRI-cSVD Phenotypes

Brain MRI acquisitions were performed at baseline on a 1.5-T Magnetom scanner (Siemens, München, Germany) using standardized 3-dimensional high-resolution T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and proton density-weighted sequences, using a validated fully automated software to detect and quantify WMH19 (eMethods 1). An experienced reader, blind to clinical data, visually assessed the presence of lacunes on the aforementioned sequences (axial, coronal, and sagittal planes). Brain infarcts were defined as focal lesions ≥3 mm with CSF-like signals on all sequences; following Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging,3 lacunes of presumed vascular origin (hereafter referred to lacunes) were defined as brain infarcts of 3–15 mm in diameter located in basal ganglia, caudate nucleus, brainstem, white matter (WM), thalamus, or internal capsule. We excluded participants with large (>15 mm), cortical or cerebellar infarcts from analyses of lacunes (eMethods 1). The composite extreme cSVD phenotype was defined, as previously described, in the full 3C-Dijon brain MRI substudy,16,17 contrasting participants with extreme distributions of WMHV residuals (adjusted for age, sex, and WM mask volume) and presence/absence of lacunes: 71 and 113 participants had extensive and minimal extreme cSVD, respectively (eMethods 1).

Retinal Microvascular Assessment

Methods to assess the retinal microvasculature in the Montrachet substudy were described previously and detailed in eMethods 2.20 Briefly, we acquired 45-degree color retinal photographs, centered on the optic disc, for both eyes using a fundus camera (Topcon, Tokyo, Japan) after pupil dilation with tropicamide 0.5% (Thea, Clermont-Ferrand, France). We sent fundus photographs anonymously to Yamagata University reading center (Japan), and a single trained grader extracted retinal vessel characteristics with the Singapore “I” Vessel Assessment (SIVA) software.21 We retained only 1 eye for analysis: (1) fundus photograph of the right eye for participants born in even-numbered years and left eye otherwise; (2) functional eye for single-eye participants; and (3) in case of uninterpretable images of 1 eye, the other was used. We focused on the 8 most relevant retinal microvascular features in arterioles and venules: FD (zone C), caliber (zones B, C), and curvature TORT (zone C). Axial length was also measured.

Covariate Measurements, Cognitive Function, and Clinical Event Assessments

eMethods 3 described covariate assessments, and eMethods 4 detailed cognitive function and clinical events evaluations.

Statistical Analyses

To account for differences in head size and T2 image acquisition field of view, we used the ratio of WMHV over WM mask volume, followed by natural logarithmic transformation to remove skewness (eMethods 5). Retinal microvascular characteristics were used as z-scores obtained through standardization for normally distributed variables (calibers) or inverse normal transformation for other variables (FD, TORT). Cognitive scores were standardized and normalized through inverse normal transformation.

To explore cross-sectional associations of retinal features with MRI-cSVD, we used linear regression for WMHV or logistic regression for lacunes and extreme cSVD. Underlying model assumptions were checked (and met) through various tests and graphs (eMethods 5). Models were adjusted for age at MRI, sex, and axial length (model 1) and then further adjusted for 3C-Dijon baseline vascular risk factors (body mass index [BMI], smoking, history of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, and model 2). We prioritized baseline vascular risk factors in primary models to ensure temporally coinciding measurements with MRI-cSVD. We checked the linearity of associations using restricted cubic splines with 5 knots (placed at 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th). To assess the robustness of results, we performed sensitivity analyses. First, we further adjusted model 2 for vascular risk factors at 10-year follow-up. We addressed multicollinearity between age at MRI and 10 years (scaled generalized variance inflation factor [squared scaled GVIF >10]), and between BMI at baseline and 10 years (squared scaled GVIF >5) by conducting Principal Component Analyses on each pair of variables and including the resulting first component in the model. Second, we excluded participants with incident (interim) dementia and stroke between 3C-Dijon baseline MRI and Montrachet baseline. Finally, we assessed the variance explained by retinal features and their importance for the models' predictive accuracy (eMethods 5).

To examine the relation of retinal microvascular features associated with MRI-cSVD (p < 0.05) with measures of cognitive performance, we used linear regression, adjusting for age, sex, and axial length (model 1); additionally educational achievement (model 2); and 10-year follow-up vascular risk factors (model 3).

We applied Bonferroni correction for multiple testing for both analyses, after determining the number of independent tests using the Pearson correlation matrix between the retinal features and MRI-cSVD markers or cognitive tests, using matSpDlite.22 This yielded a predetermined 2-tailed type 1 error rate (α) of 0.0042 for analyses relating retinal and MRI-cSVD phenotypes and 0.0034 for analyses relating retinal and cognitive phenotypes (eMethods 5). Nominal significance was defined as 0.0042 < p ≤ 0.05 and 0.0034 < p ≤ 0.05, respectively. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC) and R (version 3.5.1).23

Systematic Review of Published Observational Studies

This work is part of a larger systematic review conducted under the Population, Exposure, Outcome (PEO) framework that aimed at exploring the association of retinal microvascular features (Exposures/risk factors) assessed through fundus photography or advanced retinal imaging (optical coherence tomography angiography, adaptive optics) with MRI-cSVD and diffusion-based MRI markers of WM microstructure (Outcomes) (PROSPERO CRD42022327471). We included English language human case-control, cross-sectional, and cohort studies with ≥20 participants published from PubMed/Embase inception to July 2024 (or identified from relevant literature), performed in participants of any age recruited from the general population (community-based setting or participants described as “healthy”) or from high-risk groups (Population). Details on the methodology (PEO definitions, inclusion/exclusion criteria, search strategy, dual screening of title/abstract and full papers' review, data collection, and study selection) are reported in eMethods 6. Owing to limitations preventing us from conducting meta-analyses, we used vote counting based on direction of effects (regardless of statistical significance) to synthetize cross-sectional associations (eMethods 6), with results summarized in effect direction plots.24 We used the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies to assess quality and risk of bias,25,26 presented on traffic light plots.e4 We assessed the risk of bias because of missing results arising from selective nonreporting (eMethod 6).

MR Analyses

Data Sources

MR uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) for an exposure of interest to provide evidence for a putative causal relation with an outcome. We leveraged the latest, largest European-ancestry GWAS summary statistics to construct genetic instruments for retinal microvascular features (FD [N = 38,932], TORTv [N = 52,798], and arteriolar width [N = 52,798])27,28 and MRI-cSVD (WMHV [N = 21,647] and extreme cSVD [N = 5,292]) phenotypes (eMethods 7, eTable 1). In corresponding GWAS, FD, curvature TORT, and widths (referred hereafter as calibers) were extracted from the Quantitative Analysis of Retinal Vessel Topology and Size (QUARTZ) fully automated retinal image analysis system29 (eMethods 7).

Two-Sample MR Analyses

Associations between retinal microvascular features and MRI-cSVD markers that were nominally significant in our observational study were examined with bidirectional 2-sample MR to investigate potential causality and directionality of effects. Genetic IVs for MR analyses were selected by identifying single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) showing (1) significant association with the exposure (p < 5 × 10−8), (2) an r2 measure of linkage disequilibrium <0.01 (within 10,000 kb window) between each other, and (3) availability in outcome GWAS data. We selected variants with strong association with the exposure (mean F-statistic >10). For exposures with multiple IVs, we used the inverse-variance–weighted (IVW) method.30 To detect the presence of pleiotropy, we used both the Cochran Q statistic and the MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier global test (p < 0.05 was considered significant).31,32 Multiple MR sensitivity analyses were used to assess IV assumptions and validate our findings (Figure 1A, eMethods 7). Significant MR analyses were followed up with the Steiger test to further confirm directionality of the association.33 For exposures with a single SNP IV, we computed MR estimates using the Wald ratio, following up significant results by colocalization analyses34 to rule out pleiotropic effects. MR analyses were performed with the TwoSampleMR package,35 in R software version 4.0.0.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The Ethics Committees of the University Hospital of Kremlin-Bicêtre and Dijon University Hospital approved the 3C and Montrachet study protocols, which adhere to the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Data Availability

Anonymized data supporting the findings of this study are available on reasonable request from the 3C Scientific Committee (E3C.CoordinatingCenter@u-bordeaux.fr). Raw data are not publicly available due to the European application of the General Data Protection Regulation and because participants did not provide consent for their data to be shared that way.

Results

Observational Study

Of 1,153 Montrachet participants, 837 had both an MRI with measures of at least one of the MRI-cSVD markers and fundus-based retinal microvascular data (Figure 2). After additional exclusions (eye diseases affecting the interpretation of retinal images, dementia, or stroke pre-MRI), 670 participants were included in association analyses (666 with lacune assessment and 647 with WMHV measurements, Figure 2). Compared with excluded Montrachet participants with useable MRI and fundus measures (N = 167), those included were significantly younger, more often women, and less frequently exhibited baseline cardiovascular disease. They also had significantly smaller WMHV, larger arteriolar and venular calibers, larger FD, and better performances in processing speed and verbal fluency (eTable 2). Comparisons with all Montrachet participants not included (N = 483) were substantially similar (eTable 2). Table 1 presents characteristics of the study sample overall and by cSVD burden. Participants (65.7% women) had a median age (Q1, Q3) at the time of 3C-Dijon MRI and at the time of fundus photographs of 70.7 (68.4, 73.9) years and 79.2 (76.8, 82.6) years, respectively (Table 1). Comparisons according to WMHV level or lacunes status are shown in eTable 3. Using the composite extreme cSVD phenotype, we found that lower arteriolar FD (FDa) was associated with extensive MRI-cSVD after accounting for multiple testing (p < 0.0042) and adjustment for age at MRI, sex, axial length, and baseline vascular risk factors (odds ratio [OR] 1.68 [95% CI 1.20–2.34] per SD decrease, Table 2). In addition, lower FDa (β = 0.0534 [95% CI 0.0075–0.0569] per SD decrease, p = 0.02) and smaller arteriolar caliber in zone B (β = 0.0519 [95% CI 0.0045–0.0993] per SD decrease, p = 0.03) were nominally associated with larger WMHV (Table 2). Greater TORTv was associated with lacunes at nominal significance (OR 1.45 [95% CI 1.05–2.00] per SD increase, Table 2). In sensitivity analyses adjusted for 10-year vascular risk factors (Table 3), associations were substantially unchanged. Rerunning analyses after excluding participants with interim stroke or dementia (between MRI and fundus) also yielded similar results (Table 3).

Systematic Review of Published Observational Studies

From our systematic review, we identified 11 papers exploring the cross-sectional associations of fundus-based TORTv, arteriolar caliber, or FDa with MRI-cSVD markers8-13,36-40 (eTables 6 and 7). These represented 12 studies (9 cohorts, 1 cross-sectional, and 2 case-control studies) from 6 countries and analyzed 7,796 participants from community-based, high-risk, and mixed populations (mean age: 45.6–78.3 years). The heterogeneous reporting of results precluded a meta-analysis.

Overall, 11 studies, 6 in the general population (N = 6,841),9-13,39 4 in high-risk (N = 658),8,12,37,38 and 1 in mixed (N = 55)36 populations explored the relation between arteriolar caliber and MRI-cSVD. All but 1 small study (high-risk population, N = 91)37 reported associations between smaller arteriolar caliber and larger WMH burden,8,9,11-13,36 lacunes,8,10,13 or composite cSVD burden.12,38,39 Statistical significance was observed in 3 larger population-based cohort studies9,12,13 and 1 high-risk cross-sectional study (eTable 6).38 Vote counting results are summarized in the effect direction plot (Figure 3).

Based on the IVW method, we found evidence that genetically determined higher TORTv is associated with larger WMHV (p = 0.049) and risk of extreme cSVD (p = 0.035, Table 4, Figure 1B). Discussion

Using a multipronged approach, we explored associations between fundus-based retinal microvascular features and MRI-cSVD. Among 670 older participants from the 3C-Dijon population-based cohort, lower FDa was associated significantly with extensive MRI-cSVD, independently of axial length and vascular risk factors, and, at nominal significance, with worse executive function. In addition, smaller arteriolar caliber, lower FDa, and greater TORTv were nominally associated with larger WMHV and presence of lacunes. A systematic review of the literature identified 12 studies (7,796 participants) exploring the relation between fundus-based retinal microvascular features and MRI-cSVD. The vast majority of these showed consistent direction of effects for associations of lower FDa, smaller arteriolar caliber, and greater TORTv with MRI-cSVD, further supported by vote counting, although individually statistical significance was limited to a small subset of cohorts and risk of bias was high. Finally, using bidirectional 2-sample MR (leveraging GWAS meta-analysis from independent datasets), we found genetically predicted greater TORTv to be significantly associated with extensive cSVD and larger WMHV.

The strongest finding from our observational study in 3C-Dijon was the significant association of lower FDa with extensive MRI-cSVD, corroborating results from the similarly sized population-based LBC1936 study. In this work, lower FDa was significantly associated with greater cSVD burden, defined as a latent factor derived from visual Fazekas scores for periventricular and deep WMH, lacunes, microbleeds, and basal ganglia perivascular spaces, adjusted for vascular risk factors.12 WMH burden was the strongest contributor of this composite cSVD trait, consistent with our nominal association between lower FDa and larger WMHV. Our results suggest that combining 2 common manifestations of cSVD (lacunes and WMH volume) with a simple MRI-derived extreme phenotype approach might improve power to detect associations through better contrast between individuals with minimal and extensive MRI-cSVD and might also be easily transposable into clinical settings. This extreme phenotype was previously shown to be associated with a 2- to 2.5-fold increased risk of stroke and dementia, with evidence for a causal relation.16,17 Of interest, we additionally found a nominally significant association between lower FDa and poorer TMT-B performance, consistent with the dominating impairment of executive function in cSVD.41 These results reinforce the clinical relevance of FD as a potential cSVD marker.

To contextualize our results, we synthetized the existing literature on the relation of fundus-based retinal microvascular markers and MRI-cSVD through a comprehensive overview conducted as part of a larger systematic review. Although the heterogeneity and scarcity of available information precluded a formal meta-analysis, and overall risk of bias was high, inspection of effect direction plots showed consistent harmful effects of smaller arteriolar caliber, lower FDa, and greater TORTv on MRI-cSVD overall. These associations were observed among most individual studies, with the exception of 1 small population-based study (N = 242)40 and 2 small studies in high-risk populations of 91 patients with Alzheimer disease (AD) or vascular cognitive impairment,37 and 155 patients with recent clinical lacunar or mild cortical ischemic stroke,12 respectively, all nonsignificant.

Leveraging large published GWAS meta-analyses in independent cohorts (N = 5,292 to 52,798 participants), bidirectional 2-sample MR provided evidence for a putative causal relation between greater TORTv and MRI-cSVD (extreme cSVD and WMHV). Sensitivity analyses ruled out directional pleiotropy or reverse causation. These results support the nominal association found between TORTv and lacunes in our observational analysis, as well as converging evidence from the literature.8 Significant associations of greater TORTv with higher risk of clinical stroke and/or large MRI-defined brain infarcts,9 and vascular or mixed dementia were also observed.42 Although there was no significant association of genetically determined FD with MRI-cSVD, it should be noted that different quantification methods were used 3C-Dijon (SIVA) and in UK Biobank (QUARTZ) from which the genetic instruments were derived. SIVA calculated arteriolar and venular FD, with results in 3C-Dijon significant only for arteriolar FD, while QUARTZ provided a single FD value for all retinal vessels, which may have hampered our ability to detect significant associations using MR for this trait.

Mechanisms underlying the reported associations between retinal microvascular features and MRI-cSVD markers remain speculative. These associations could be driven by common risk factors, such as hypertension or diabetes1,43; however, this is unlikely to be the only explanation because our results remained unchanged or were reinforced after adjustment for vascular risk factors. A reduced FDa, suggesting a less complex and sparser (thus less efficient) arteriolar network, may lead to hypoperfusion and higher risk of vascular damage due to an impairment of the microcirculation.44 Whether observed associations reflect similar reduction of the branching complexity, shape and caliber of small brain vessels remains to be determined. The mechanisms underlying the relation between TORTv and MRI-cSVD are also hypothetical. Tortuosity was shown to increase with age in the brain vasculature. 7T brain MRI studies have suggested that venular tortuosities could be among the earliest markers of cSVD and are associated with APOE4 carrier status,45 and that higher TORT of deep cerebral medullary veins is associated with early AD.46 Preclinical studies also suggested that venular pathologies may be an underestimated source of microinfarcts and perfusion vulnerability.e5

We acknowledge limitations. First, the 3C-Dijon cohort comprises well-functioning volunteers not perfectly representative of the general population which limits the generalizability of our results, as in most population-based cohort studies. Second, compared with participants excluded because of features compromising the assessment of retinal and brain imaging markers, the study sample had less cardiovascular disease, MRI-cSVD, and retinal microvascular changes, which may have resulted in an underestimation of associations between retinal microvascular features and MRI-cSVD. In addition, we cannot rule out unmeasured confounding and potential survival bias because only 3C-Dijon participants who survived up to 10 years and were preferentially selected based on prior brain MRI availability were asked to join the Montrachet study. As a result, the study sample may not fully represent either the full 3C-Dijon cohort re-examined at 10 years or the sample of participants recruited in Montrachet, limiting its overall representativeness and generalizability. Third, the use of WM mask for WMHV instead of total intracranial volume (TIV) may introduce some degree of confounding from WM atrophy. However, this choice was a methodologic trade-off, driven by more robustly and precise estimates than TIV in 3C-Dijon. Fourth, among our results, only the association of FDa with MRI-cSVD remained statistically significant after correction for multiple testing. Nevertheless, the consistency of nominally significant findings with both prior observational research (systematic review: association of smaller arteriolar caliber and greater TORTv with MRI-cSVD) and our MR experiment (association of greater TORTv with MRI-cSVD) supports that these results may be real, warranting further exploration. Fifth, we prioritized WMH and lacunes as MRI-cSVD phenotypes because they were the basis of the composite extreme cSVD phenotype we recently developed, and CMBs were not measured in 3C-Dijon due to lack of T2* sequences. Future studies incorporating other MRI-cSVD markers, such as CMBs and perivascular spaces, are warranted. Sixth, the vote counting approach does not account for the magnitude or precision of effect estimates, or study weight, and does not yield a pooled effect estimate of associations across studies.e6 However, it is considered superior to a narrative approach and allowed us to assess consistency of association patterns across studies, highlighting general trends and areas of agreement. Our work emphasizes the need for transparent reporting, standardization of retinal and MRI-cSVD measurements across studies, and harmonization of statistical approaches to facilitate formal meta-analyses. Seventh, another limitation of results in the 3C-Dijon study is the 10-year gap between MRI at baseline and later retinal imaging, preventing us from establishing the directionality of observed associations. Although the MR experiment offers complementary insights into directionality and causality through the use of genetic instruments, our observational design in 3C-Dijon does not allow firm conclusions about temporal precedence. However, given their greater accessibility, we remain interested in retinal microvascular features as a surrogate marker for cSVD, regardless of the directionality and temporality of associations.

Finally, our analyses were restricted to participants of European ancestry, limiting generalizability to non-European populations and warranting further cross-ancestry research.

In conclusion, using a multimodal approach, we provide converging, robust evidence for an association of retinal microvascular features, especially lower FDa and greater TORTv, with MRI-cSVD. These results support the potential of fundus-based retinal geometric parameters as promising noninvasive and cost-efficient imaging biomarkers to characterize MRI-cSVD and stratify cSVD risk. Although more advanced technologies such as optical-coherence tomography angiography or adaptive optics could refine retinal markers in future studies and provide precious additional information on underlying mechanisms, fundus imaging remains essential due to its accessibility, affordability, and widespread use in various health care settings and populations, particularly in underserved communities. Further research would benefit from synchronous retinal and brain imaging in different age groups and diverse populations to enhance generalizability, and from measures of longitudinal change to assess their potential use as surrogate outcomes.
