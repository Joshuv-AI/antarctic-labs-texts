Impact of Grid Size on Dose Calculations for Cranial Tumors
Authors: Nayron Medeiros Soares, Maria José Almada, José Alejandro Rojas López, Carlos Daniel Venencia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The purpose of this study was to evaluate the impact of calculation grid size on dose calculations for multiple cranial tumor types using Monte Carlo (MC) calculation, comparing different grid settings.Methods:This cross-sectional study optimized 110 treatment plans using Elements Cranial and included arteriovenous malformation (AVM), glioblastoma (GBM), meningioma (MGM), brain metastasis (BM), pituitary adenoma (PA), and vestibular schwannoma (VS). Dose coverage at D98%, D95%, and D2%, along with mean dose, were assessed, and other relevant planning and anatomical parameters were analyzed.Results:GBM had the largest gross tumor volume and planning target volume (PTV) volumes, while VS had the smallest volumes and the highest PTV–bone intersection. For MC, grid size variations (1%/1 mm vs. 2%2 mm) did not significantly affect AVM, GBM, or MGM. However, statistically significant dose reductions were observed for BM, PA, and VS. When adjusted for PTV volume and bone intersection, median dose values decreased with the 2%/2 mm grid compared to the 1%/1 mm grid: D98% (25.60 Gy vs. 25.27 Gy, adjusted P = 0.026), D95% (27.05 Gy vs. 26.14 Gy, adjusted P = 0.005), and D2% (30.48 Gy vs. 30.00 Gy, adjusted P < 0.001). Application of the 2%/2 mm grid resulted in increased conformity index (CI) and gradient index (GI) values, with the effect being more pronounced in smaller or anatomically irregular targets, such as VS and PA.Conclusions:Grid size impacted dose calculations for small or irregular targets such as BM, PA, and VS. The 2%/2 mm grid led to significant dose reductions and increased CI and GI values.

INTRODUCTION

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is a precisely targeted, noninvasive radiation therapy designed to treat tumors that are challenging to remove surgically, vascular malformations, as well as certain functional brain disorders.[123] This highly conformal approach ensures that the target receives a high therapeutic dose while minimizing exposure to surrounding healthy brain tissue. The steep radiation dose fall-off at the tumor margins effectively spares normal tissue, reducing toxicity and side effects while maintaining treatment safety.[4] This therapeutic precision in radiotherapy is underpinned by technological advancements in imaging, immobilization, and, critically, dose calculation algorithms.[56]

Achieving optimal treatment outcomes relies on sophisticated treatment planning systems (TPSs) that employ advanced dose calculation algorithms, such as pencil beam (PB) and Monte Carlo (MC) simulation. One key limitation of the PB algorithm is its reduced accuracy in estimating dose distributions near interfaces involving air cavities or heterogeneous tissues. Additionally, the PB algorithm may not be sufficiently accurate to predict dose distributions for small fields with a loss of lateral charged particle equilibrium.[7] In contrast, MC algorithms are considered the gold standard for dose calculation in radiotherapy due to their ability to model complex tissue heterogeneities and scattering phenomena accurately.[89] However, the computational efficiency and accuracy of MC-based calculations can inherently be influenced by grid resolution settings.[10]

The choice of grid size can significantly impact dose distribution estimations, particularly for small targets where minor variations may lead to clinically relevant differences in dose deposition.[11] Despite the growing adoption of MC algorithms in clinical practice, a comprehensive analysis of calculation grid size effects on dose accuracy in SRS treatment planning remains underexplored. This study aims to evaluate the impact of different grid resolutions in MC-based dose calculation accuracy in SRS treatment planning for a heterogeneous group of cranial pathologies. Our findings provide valuable insights into the optimization of MC grid configurations for cranial radiosurgery, offering practical recommendations for improving the accuracy and reliability of dose calculations.

METHODS

Study design

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.[12] A total of 110 volumetric modulated arc therapy treatment plans were included in the dataset, comprising 10 plans for arteriovenous malformation (AVM), 20 for glioblastoma (GBM), 20 for meningioma (MGM), 20 for brain metastases (BMs), 20 for pituitary adenoma (PA), and 20 for vestibular schwannoma (VS). All plans were initially optimized using the PB algorithm and subsequently recalculated using the MC algorithm within the SRS workflow of the Cranial SRS Elements TPS, version 4.0 (Brainlab AG, München, Germany),[13] in order to standardize the optimization process and minimize potential sources of bias.

The MC algorithm was used as the dose calculation engine to investigate the impact of grid size resolution on dosimetric parameters, rather than as a primary variable for comparison with the PB algorithm. The PB algorithm was employed exclusively during plan optimization due to its computational efficiency, while MC recalculation was performed to obtain more accurate dose estimations, particularly in small fields and heterogeneous tissues. Two MC grid configurations (1%/1 mm and 2%/2 mm) were applied to all plans to evaluate the influence of grid resolution on dose distribution and plan quality metrics.

Plan quality metrics

The study analyzed the characteristics of the gross tumor volume (GTV) and planning target volume (PTV) coverage, including their volumes of the structures, PTV margin, and the intersection of PTV with bone structures, total monitor units (MUs), inverse Paddick conformity index (CI), gradient index (GI), GTV coverage at D98%, D95%, and D2%, along with mean dose. Additionally, dose metrics for organs at risk (OARs) such as the cochlea (ipsilateral), brainstem, chiasm, optic nerves, and optic tracts were assessed [Table 1]. Grid sizes were configured to 1 mm for the PB algorithm and 1%/1 mm and 2%/2 mm for the MC algorithm, ensuring optimal resolution and accuracy in dose calculations. All plans were normalized to deliver 95% of the prescribed dose to the GTV.

Treatment protocols

Treatment protocols varied according to pathology. AVM was treated with single-fraction SRS at a dose of 21 Gy, with a PTV margin of 1 mm around the CTV. GBM treatment included a 10-fraction regimen, with CTV 52 Gy and PTV 43 Gy, with a PTV margin of 3 mm around the CTV. MGM was treated with five-fraction SRS delivering 30 Gy, with a PTV margin of 1 mm around the CTV. BMs were treated with single-fraction SRS at 21 Gy, with a PTV margin of 1 mm around the CTV. PA (nonsecreting) was treated with five fractions totaling 27 Gy, with GTV defined without margin. VS, a single-fraction SRS with 12 Gy, was used, applying a 1 mm PTV margin around the CTV.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive data were presented as the median, minimum, and maximum values, along with a 95% confidence interval. To compare the 1%/1 mm and 2%/2 mm grids for the MC algorithm at the D98%, D95%, and D2% dose levels of the GTV, the Mann–Whitney test was applied. Additionally, rank analysis of covariance was performed to assess the impact of grid configuration on dose parameters while adjusting for potential confounding variables. Given that the presence of high-density tissues such as bone within or adjacent to the PTV can significantly affect dose deposition, particularly in MC simulations due to steep density gradients and complex electron transport phenomena, the degree of PTV–bone intersection was quantified and included in the model. Along with PTV volume, which is known to influence spatial resolution and dosimetric accuracy, both variables were incorporated as covariates to account for their impact on dose distribution and optimization complexity. The interaction between grid configuration and these covariates was also tested to evaluate potential modifying effects. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) v. 24 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the impact of grid size on MC-based dose calculations in SRS for a heterogeneous group of cranial pathologies. Our results demonstrate that although grid size variation from 1%/1 mm to 2%/2 mm did not lead to clinically significant differences in dose distribution for large-volume lesions such as GBM and MGM, it had a measurable and statistically significant impact on dose metrics in smaller targets, including BM, PA, and VS. These findings underscore that while the overall dose distribution remains stable across grid settings for large-volume tumors, grid resolution becomes a critical factor for accurate dose modeling in small and anatomically complex targets.

Impact of tumor size and dose grid resolution on dosimetric accuracy

The most relevant findings indicate that smaller lesions are more sensitive to changes in dose grid resolution. For example, in VS and PA, median differences in GTV D98% and D95% between the two MC grid configurations reached statistical significance (P < 0.05), suggesting that the 2%/2 mm grid may underrepresent actual dose coverage in small and complex anatomical regions. This is consistent with earlier simulation-based studies reporting that voxel size plays a crucial role in dose estimation accuracy, particularly in stereotactic techniques involving high-dose gradients and small targets.[11] In contrast, larger targets such as GBM showed remarkable stability across grid configurations, with negligible variation in key dosimetric parameters. This observation supports the notion that volumetric averaging effects introduced by larger grid sizes are more impactful in smaller targets due to reduced sampling density and steeper dose gradients at the tumor periphery. This behavior is expected due to the spatial averaging effect inherent in larger voxel sizes, which may fail to accurately capture sharp dose gradients at the interface between tumor and normal tissue. Compared with larger grid sizes, the smaller calculation grid size increases the mean dose to PTV and decreases the mean dose to OARs.[19]

Tumor volume and bone intersection as modulators of grid impact

Our ANCOVA analysis confirmed that the influence of grid size on dose metrics is modulated by both the target volume and the intersection with bony structures. Small volumes are inherently more sensitive to grid resolution due to reduced spatial sampling, and bone interfaces further complicate dose estimation due to density heterogeneity and electron transport modeling. In particular, VS lesions, with median PTV volumes below 1 cc and high bone intersection percentages, exhibited the greatest discrepancies between grid settings. This confirms that grid size plays a disproportionately important role in radiosurgical plans involving small targets adjacent to high-density tissue combinations frequently encountered in skull base lesions, where MC calculations have proven superior to analytical PB algorithms in complex heterogeneous environments.[2021]

Conformity and gradient indices

Both the CI and the GI varied with changes in grid resolution, showing higher values in the 2%/2 mm grid plans, especially in small lesions. This finding suggests that a finer grid provides a more accurate depiction of dose fall-off, crucial for sparing adjacent critical structures in radiosurgical contexts, and also yields a lower CI, indicating a dose distribution that better conforms to the size and shape of the target volume. These results demonstrate that both the CI and GI are particularly sensitive to voxel resolution in SRS planning. Variations in dose voxel size produced greater deviations in plan quality metrics compared to changes in statistical uncertainty, particularly for stereotactic radiotherapy plans where small targets and steep dose gradients demand high spatial resolution for accurate dosimetric representation.[11] Furthermore, studies of targets treated with SRS found that the CI increased significantly for lesions smaller than 1 cm3, indicating that the accuracy of conformity metrics is particularly sensitive to lesion size and the spatial resolution used in planning.[22] Additionally, it has been shown that small changes in absolute volume can result in large relative differences in conformity metrics for small targets, further supporting the increased sensitivity of these indices to spatial resolution effects.[2223]

Clinical implications for planning practice

The clinical implication of these findings is clear: grid size selection must be adapted to tumor characteristics, particularly volume and proximity to OARs. For small-volume targets, fine grid resolution (1%/1 mm) is not merely a technical preference but a clinical necessity to ensure accurate dose deposition and avoid undercoverage or unintended OAR exposure. In lesions such as PA and MGM, proximity to the optic apparatus and brainstem makes precise dose modeling critical. Even small underestimations in maximum dose could result in exceeding established toxicity thresholds. Likewise, in VS cases, accurate cochlear dose estimation is essential for hearing preservation, an objective potentially compromised by coarser grids. Our results support the adoption of finer grid settings in small or anatomically complex lesions, particularly when planning with MC algorithms known for their sensitivity to spatial resolution.

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study include the inclusion of a diverse range of cranial pathologies, which enhances the applicability of the findings across a broad spectrum of radiosurgical indications. This heterogeneity enables meaningful comparisons of grid-size effects across varying anatomical and dosimetric conditions. In addition, the incorporation of clinically relevant covariates, such as PTV volume and PTV–bone intersection, strengthens the interpretation of the dosimetric results.

However, limitations must be acknowledged. Although the total dataset comprised 110 treatment plans, the number of cases per pathology was relatively limited, particularly for AVMs. This study also focused on two anatomical covariates, PTV volume and PTV–bone intersection, as primary modifiers of dose variability. While both are highly relevant, the inclusion of additional anatomical and planning-related variables could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing sensitivity to grid resolution. Finally, the analysis was conducted using a single TPS (Elements Cranial, Brainlab), which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other platforms.

CONCLUSION

This study comprehensively assessed the impact of MC grid size resolution on dose calculations for cranial tumors treated with SRS. Our results indicate that, for large-volume lesions such as GBM and MGM, dose coverage and conformity metrics remained largely stable between the 1%/1 mm and 2%/2 mm MC grid configurations. In contrast, smaller targets, especially BM, PA, and VS, exhibited statistically significant reductions in D98%, D95%, and D2% when calculated with the 2%/2 mm grid. These findings suggest that spatial resolution becomes a critical factor in accurately modeling dose distributions for small or anatomically complex lesions, where the impact of grid coarseness on steep dose gradients is more pronounced. Moreover, the use of finer grids (1%/1 mm) demonstrated a tendency toward better preservation of CI and GI values, enabling a steeper dose fall-off and potentially enhancing OAR sparing, particularly for structures such as the cochlea, brainstem, and optic pathways. These dosimetric trends underscore the clinical importance of tailoring grid resolution according to the size and location of the target, rather than applying a uniform calculation setting across all cases. After adjusting for covariates such as PTV volume and PTV–bone intersection, the statistical significance of grid size differences was reinforced, highlighting the need for context-aware optimization strategies in treatment planning. While in MC, the computational demands of finer grids may be higher, their use appears justified in cases where high precision is paramount for both tumor control and toxicity reduction.
