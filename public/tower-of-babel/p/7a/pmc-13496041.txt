Ratiometric Mycotoxin Detection in Living Plants With Dual‐Emissive Nanosensors
Authors: Yuliang Li, Di Shen, Ziyang Huang, Suppanat Puangpathumanond, Calvin Thenarianto, Yue Zhao, Jolly Madathiparambil Saju, Rajani Sarojam, Dongmei Zhou, Tedrick Thomas Salim Lew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly diagnosis of fungal infections in crops is essential for mitigating yield losses, yet most detection strategies rely on destructive assays that are incompatible with in situ monitoring. Among major fungal pathogens, Fusarium species pose a significant threat to global agriculture and produce the phytotoxin fusaric acid (FA), a key biomarker of early infection. Here, we develop a non‐destructive, ratiometric optical nanosensor that integrates aggregation‐induced emissive carbon dots within a zeolitic imidazolate framework (CD@ZIF‐8) for selective FA detection in living plants. By rationally tuning the balance between dispersed and aggregated CDs within the ZIF‐8 framework, we generated a dual‐emissive nanosensor with distinct blue and red fluorescent signals that enable self‐referencing readouts. Upon exposure to FA, interactions with CD@ZIF‐8 quench the blue emission while leaving the red aggregation‐induced emission stable, thereby affording highly selective ratiometric sensing. Integrating CD@ZIF‐8 into plant‐compatible microneedle patches enables minimally invasive sampling of plant interstitial fluid, allowing FA detection in Fusarium‐infected crops before visible symptoms and discrimination from abiotic and bacterial stresses. This work establishes an integrated, self‐referenced nanosensing platform for non‐destructive plant diagnostics, laying the foundation for early disease surveillance in precision and climate‐resilient agriculture.Keywords: fungi infection, microneedles, optical nanosensors, plant health, plant sensors

Introduction

Fungal and oomycete pathogens are responsible for many of the world's most destructive crop diseases, causing an estimated 10–20% reduction in global crop yields each year [1]. Among these pathogens, Fusarium species are particularly devastating, infecting a wide range of economically important crops [2, 3]. A key feature of Fusarium infections is the secretion of fusaric acid (FA), a phytotoxin that induces wilting, yellowing, and stunted growth symptoms in plants [4, 5], while also compromising crop quality and safety [6, 7]. As FA accumulates before visible symptoms appear, it serves as a sensitive biomarker for early detection of Fusarium infection. Rapid and accurate detection of FA is therefore crucial for effectively managing Fusarium infections and minimizing their impact on crop losses [8, 9]. However, mycotoxin detection in plants still relies on chromatography‐ and mass spectrometry‐based strategies [10], which are labor‐intensive, destructive, and incompatible with in situ crop monitoring. Consequently, there is an urgent need for simple, non‐destructive sensing approaches capable of detecting FA directly in living plants.

Carbon dots (CD) have emerged as promising optical nanomaterials in the field of biosensing due to their bright fluorescence, tunable surface chemistry, and biocompatibility [11, 12]. CD can be synthesized from a wide array of precursors through various methods, including solvothermal and hydrothermal techniques [13, 14], providing design handles to control particle size, surface functionality, and optical properties [15, 16, 17]. Recent studies have shown that CD can also be engineered to display increased fluorescence upon aggregation, a phenomenon known as aggregation‐induced emission (AIE), in contrast to conventional CD which typically shows fluorescence quenching when aggregated [18]. AIE‐active CDs provide new optical sensing modalities for the detection of various organic molecules and ions [18, 19], but most CD‐based nanosensors suffer from poor selectivity and instability in complex biological environments [16, 20]. Encapsulation within porous metal‐organic frameworks (MOFs) offers an attractive strategy to protect CDs from environmental fluctuations and enhance selective interactions with target analytes [21, 22]. Notably, the mycotoxin FA is a strong metal chelator [23], suggesting it could interact with metal ion nodes of MOFs to modulate the fluorescence of CD‐MOF nanocomposites.

In this study, we engineer a non‐destructive, ratiometric optical nanosensor for in situ FA detection in living plants by rationally tuning the population of dispersed and aggregated AIE‐CD within zeolitic imidazole framework‐8 (ZIF‐8) nanoarchitectures (herein denoted as CD@ZIF‐8). This nanosensor design produces two distinct emission peaks at 450 and 610 nm, enabling self‐referenced signal readouts. FA selectively quenches the blue emission while leaving the red AIE signal stable, allowing robust ratiometric quantification with a single nanosensor platform. By integrating CD@ZIF‐8 into plant‐compatible microneedle patches, we demonstrate in situ, non‐destructive monitoring of FA in Fusarium‐infected plants prior to visible symptom development. This dual‐emissive nanosensor strategy provides a practical platform for early detection of fungal infections in crops and expands the toolkit for precision plant diagnostics in climate‐resilient agriculture.

Results

Synthesis and Characterization of CD@ZIF‐8 Nanosensor

AIE‐exhibiting CD were synthesized via a one‐pot solvothermal method using melamine (MA) and dithiobenzoic acid (DTSA) in acetic acid solution. DTSA was chosen as the AIE‐CD precursor as it possesses a symmetrical molecular structure which can exhibit intramolecular rotation around the disulfide bond, leading to AIE property (Figure 1a) [18]. Melamine was used as a precursor and nitrogen dopant to generate a new energy state in CD. The as‐synthesized CD exhibited blue emission around 450 nm in ethanol as dispersed nanoparticles (Figure 1b). This blue emission can be attributed to the dominance of the graphitic core of CD. In water, which serves as a poor solvent, these CDs aggregate owing to hydrophobic interactions, and the ensuing π–π stacking among their conjugated domains quenches the intrinsic blue emission while simultaneously activating an aggregation‐induced emission pathway that generates red fluorescence centered at around 610 nm (Figure 1b). This optical property can be attributed to the restriction of the intramolecular rotation around the disulfide bond, resulting in red AIE, while the formation of J‐aggregates led to aggregation‐caused quenching (ACQ) of the blue emission [18]. Given these emission features, AIE‐CD displays unique properties for optical sensing as the ratio of the blue and red emitters, corresponding to the dispersed and aggregated CD, respectively, can be tuned by the microenvironment around the nanoparticles. AIE‐CD showed a narrow particle size distribution with an average diameter of 2.58 nm (Figure S1). Additionally, the CD also displayed well‐resolved lattice fringes with an interlamellar spacing of 0.21 nm, corresponding to the (100) facet of graphene (Figure 1c).

After successful synthesis of AIE‐CD, we encapsulated them within the core of a porous framework to protect CD fluorescence from modulation by non‐specific interactions with plant biomolecules (Figure 1a). ZIF‐8 was chosen as the porous framework to host the CD because it can decompose into soluble zinc ions (Zn2+) in the plant microenvironment in the long term, which are inherently biocompatible as essential plant micronutrients [24]. Moreover, we hypothesized that CD@ZIF‐8 would selectively interact with FA, as the Zn2+ centers in ZIF‐8 may act as coordination sites for the pyridine nitrogen and oxygen functional groups of FA. For this encapsulation, we explored an in situ formation of ZIF‐8 cage around CD by adding the precursors of ZIF‐8 into an aqueous dispersion of CD. Interestingly, Zn2+ addition to aggregated CD in aqueous solution progressively suppressed the AIE emission at 610 nm, while simultaneously enhancing the blue emission at 450 nm associated with dispersed CD (Figure 1d). Zeta potential of CD became less negative as more Zn2+ was introduced, indicating a strong electrostatic attraction between Zn2+ and CD (Figure 1e; Figure S2). Adjusting the Zn2+ to CD ratio precisely from 0 to 13 led to an approximately 7‐fold decrease in the hydrodynamic diameter of the colloidal system, consistent with the transition from an aggregated to a dispersed state (Figure 1f). This Zn2+‐induced decrease in particle size is accompanied by an increase in the ratio of the fluorescence intensity at 450 nm to that of 610 nm (I450/I610) (Figure 1f). Notably, conventional strategies for regulating dispersion‐aggregation state of CDs rely on organic solvent/water transitions, in which the organic solvent could induce cytotoxicity and severely restrict in vivo applications on live plants. In contrast, the proposed organic solvent‐free strategy indicated that Zn2+ could effectively help disperse the aggregated, hydrophobic CD in water, providing a biologically‐compatible route to rationally tune the population of blue and red emitters to construct a ratiometric nanoprobe with two distinct emission profiles.

After confirming the role of Zn2+ in facilitating the dispersion of CD, we proceeded with the encapsulation of CD within ZIF‐8 nanoframework. We chose a CD: Zn2+ ratio of 5:1 for CD@ZIF‐8 synthesis as this ratio afforded clearly visible blue and red emission peaks at 450 and 610 nm, respectively (Figure 1d). To spatially position CD within the core of ZIF‐8, CD was first dispersed in an aqueous solution and then mixed with Zn2+ ions, followed by the sequential addition of HMIM and the surfactant cetrimonium bromide (CTAB) for in situ formation of CD@ZIF‐8. The resulting hybrid CD@ZIF‐8 retained its cubic morphology similar to pristine ZIF‐8, indicating that the incorporation of CD did not disrupt the ZIF‐8 structure (Figure 1g; Figure S3). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging revealed that CD was spatially distributed within the core of ZIF‐8 matrix (Figure 1g), in agreement with previous findings that CTAB helped to precisely guide the spatial organization of nanoparticles within MOF core [25]. Energy‐dispersive X‐ray (EDX) spectroscopy confirmed that the characteristic sulfur element of CD was distributed within the core of MOF framework (Figure 1h). CTAB‐guided nanoassembly effect was further verified by replacing CD with gold nanoparticles to provide a higher contrast under electron microscopy, confirming nanoparticle encapsulation within the core of ZIF‐8 (Figure S4). X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis also showed the presence of sulfur element in CD@ZIF‐8 (Figure 1i), indicating the successful integration of CD within ZIF‐8. Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum of CD@ZIF‐8 displayed characteristic peaks from both ZIF‐8 and CD. Notably, CD exhibited a peak at 490 cm− 1 corresponding to the S–S bond (Figure S5), which is a key component responsible for the AIE properties of CD [18]. After CD encapsulation into the ZIF‐8 framework, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) peaks of ZIF‐8 were retained, confirming the preservation of its crystalline structure (Figure 1j; Figure S6). Compared to pristine ZIF‐8, the incorporation of amorphous CDs in CD@ZIF‐8 led to slight peak broadening and a decrease in peak intensity as observed by XRD. Collectively, these results validate the successful formation of the CD@ZIF‐8 composite while maintaining the framework integrity of ZIF‐8.

In terms of optical properties, CD@ZIF‐8 exhibits dual emission centers with fluorescent peaks at 450 nm and 610 nm (Figure 1b, bottom). This can be attributed to the partial dispersion of CD by Zn2+ ions, resulting in blue emission, while the remaining aggregated CD within ZIF‐8 generated AIE‐induced red emission. This also confirms that Zn2+:CD ratio of 5:1 is sufficient to induce in situ formation of CD@ZIF‐8 while preserving the dual emission properties of the nanocomposites. Upon UV light excitation, aqueous dispersions of CD and Zn2+ exhibit a slightly pink emission profile which is visibly different from pure CD in ethanol or water (Figure S7). CD displayed two main absorption peaks at 280 and 365 nm, attributed to the π–π* transitions of C═C within the CD core [18], while ZIF‐8 exhibited characteristic absorption around 215 nm (Figure 1k). All characteristic absorption peaks of both components were present in CD@ZIF‐8, confirming the successful integration of CD and ZIF‐8 through the in situ encapsulation strategy.

FA Induces Ratiometric Fluorescence Quenching of CD@ZIF‐8

We next investigated the interaction between FA, as a biomarker of Fusarium fungal infection in plants, and the nanosensor CD@ZIF‐8. Interestingly, we found that only the blue emission of CD@ZIF‐8 at 450 nm (I450) is quenched upon the introduction of FA, while red emission at 610 nm (I610) remained nearly constant (Figure 2a). The FA‐induced decrease of I450 relative to I610 followed a dose‐dependent profile with rapid quenching within the first 10 mins (Figure 2b). Using these two distinct emission peaks with contrasting responses to FA, a ratiometric sensing of FA (I450/I610) was enabled with a single nanosensor platform. This is in contrast to other ratiometric sensors previously used in plant studies, which relied on employing two different types of nanosensors introduced at separate locations in plant tissues to enable ratiometric sensing readout [26, 27, 28]. CD@ZIF‐8 nanosensor also showed excellent stability and comparable ratiometric sensing performance in a plant microenvironment‐mimicking buffer (Figure S8). More importantly, the optical nanoprobe showed excellent selectivity to FA compared to other potentially interfering metabolites in the plant cellular environment (Figure 2c). Upon FA addition, the sensor showed approximately 80% quenching in terms of I450/I610 ratio, in contrast to negligible fluorescence changes (<20%) induced by endogenous plant molecules and hormones.

To investigate the sensor's mechanism of interaction with FA, nanosensor's responses to structural analogs of FA were analyzed (Figure S9). While picolinic acid and 2,5‐pyridinedicarboxylic acid could elicit quenching in I450/I610 ratio of CD@ZIF‐8 at a similar magnitude to FA, they exist at a much lower concentration in plants compared to FA and, therefore, will not compromise the practical selectivity of the sensor in plants [29, 30]. Our results revealed that the pyridine carboxylic acid structure is critical for the observed fluorescence quenching, likely due to its ability to chelate Zn2+ ions within the ZIF‐8 framework. Morphological characterization with SEM showed that FA induced visible surface defects on CD@ZIF‐8 (Figure S10), corroborating its metal chelating effect on structural disruption of CD@ZIF‐8 framework. Similar to FA, the introduction of EDTA, a known metal chelator, elicited significant quenching of the blue emission of CD@ZIF‐8 while the red emission remained relatively unaffected (Figure S11). These results suggested that FA could chelate Zn2+ ions within CD@ZIF‐8, leading to the structural disruption of CD@ZIF‐8. DFT calculations supported this hypothesis by showing that nitrogen and oxygen atoms, which exhibit the lowest electronegativity and mean local ionization energy values, are the most reactive sites (Figure 2d; Figure S12) [31]. This implies that FA interacts with ZIF‐8 predominantly at these positions, leading to structural changes in the MOF that promote access of FA to interact with the encapsulated fluorescent CD. Such interactions further manifest in FA‐specific modulation of CD@ZIF‐8's fluorescence properties.

To elucidate the interactions between FA and CD after etching the ZIF‐8 framework through metal chelation, the hydrodynamic diameter of CD was characterized in the presence of Zn2+ ions and FA. While there was a significant decrease in the hydrodynamic size of CD from ∼700 to 100 nm after Zn2+ addition, such a dramatic decrease was only partially reversed (from 100 to ∼200 nm) upon the introduction of FA (Figure 2e). This suggests that FA could not completely remove electrostatically‐bound Zn2+ from CD surface, which would otherwise have resulted in a recovery of CD hydrodynamic size closer to 700 nm. Furthermore, the absorption spectrum of the CD‐FA system showed disappearance of 250 nm and 300 nm peaks present in CD, indicating the formation of a ground‐state complex (Figure S13). To investigate the quenching mechanism, fluorescence decay curves of CD with Zn2+ and CD‐FA were fitted to a biexponential equation, with the average fluorescence lifetime calculated as ∑biτi products (Figure 2f; Table S1). For CD, the decay components were τ1 = 0.34 ns (53.2%) and τ2 = 5.08 ns (46.8%), yielding an average lifetime of 2.56 ns. After adding FA, the decay components shifted slightly to τ1 = 0.32 ns (59.7%) and τ2 = 5.68 ns (40.2%), with an average lifetime of 2.49 ns. The minimal change in the average lifetime suggests a static quenching mechanism due to ground‐state complex formation between CD and FA [32, 33, 34].

To further investigate the distinct responses of the two emission centers, fluorescence intensity changes of the blue‐emissive dispersed CDs and AIE‐active aggregated CDs were independently monitored in the presence of FA. For dispersed CDs, the blue emission at 450 nm decreased markedly over time following FA addition (Figure S14a). Together with the negligible change in fluorescence lifetime (Figure 2f) and the evidence of ground‐state complex formation from absorption spectral analysis (Figure S13), this behavior indicates a static quenching mechanism arising from the formation of non‐emissive CD–FA complexes. In contrast, the red emission centered at 610 nm for aggregated CDs remained unchanged under identical treatment (Figure S14b), suggesting that the AIE pathway is inherently insensitive to FA interaction. These results demonstrate that FA interacted with Zn2+‐dispersed CD through static quenching, resulting in an increase in the hydrodynamic diameter of CD and quenching of CD's blue emission which stems from its carbonized core. This static quenching interaction between FA and CD has a minimal effect on the energy level of AIE attributed to the restricted intramolecular rotation around the S─S bond of CD, causing negligible changes in the red emission. Taken together, our findings suggest that the interactions between FA and CD@ZIF‐8 initially involve chelation of Zn2 + ions in the CD@ZIF‐8 framework by FA, leading to the partial disruption of the ZIF‐8 structure and enhanced exposure of the encapsulated CDs. Subsequently, the aromatic ring of FA mediates π‐π stacking interactions with the benzene rings of the CDs, causing FA‐dependent quenching of the blue emission from the graphitic core of the CDs while the red AIE emission remains unaffected (Figure 3). The red AIE emission could therefore be used as the reference signal invariant to FA, and the blue graphitic core emission could serve as the active signal that is highly sensitive to FA. By leveraging dual emission centers with such distinct responses to FA, the CD@ZIF‐8 nanoprobe enables a ratiometric sensing of FA.

Integrating CD@ZIF‐8 With Microneedles for FA Detection

We next sought to develop a wearable platform integrated with CD@ZIF‐8 for in situ and non‐destructive detection of FA within living plant tissues. Microneedles (MN) present a minimally invasive interface to access the plant interstitial fluid containing FA. CD@ZIF‐8‐embedded MN were fabricated using a micro‐molding technique (Figure 4a). MN composed of PVA and CD@ZIF‐8 was used for interfacing with plant tissues to passively extract plant sap and detect FA in extracted plant biofluids. MN exhibited a pyramidal shape, with a height of approximately 210 µm and a width of 100 µm (Figure 4b, top). Nanosensors‐embedded MN displayed strong fluorescence in both the blue and red channels (Figure 4b, bottom). Additionally, SEM images indicated a rougher surface of the sensor‐loaded MN compared to pristine MN (Figure S15). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) confirmed the uniform distribution of fluorescence within each MN tip (Figure S16), further verifying the successful incorporation of the nanosensors within the MN. To evaluate the mechanical strength of MN for penetrating plant leaves, we analyzed the force‐displacement profiles of MN, both with and without CD@ZIF‐8 loading. As shown in Figure 4c, MN tolerated forces exceeding 0.10 N/MN, indicating sufficient strength for effective penetration of plant leaves without structural failure [35, 36]. After the insertion of MN into plant leaves, the resulting puncture sites were monitored over seven days. Plant transpiration rate remained stable from day 1 to day 7, with no significant differences observed among these time points (Figure 4d). This indicates that the MN treatment did not compromise leaf physiological function. In contrast, the SDS‐treated group exhibited a significantly lower transpiration rate, reflecting impaired plant health. These results collectively suggest that MN insertion causes minimal disruption to plant tissues while preserving normal leaf physiological activity.

In addition to mechanical and biological performance, the stability of the CD@ZIF‐8‐integrated MN was evaluated under both ambient and hydrated conditions (Figure S17). After one week of storage in ambient air, the MN retained strong blue and red fluorescence signals, with negligible change in the fluorescence intensity ratio (Figure S17a,c), indicating minimal signal drift during storage. Similarly, following immersion in water for 3 h to mimic the hydrated PVA microneedle environment, the fluorescence signals and ratio remained unchanged without statistically significant variation (Figure S17b,c). Furthermore, CD@ZIF‐8 particles extracted after both treatments preserved their morphology, and SEM analysis confirmed that the ZIF‐8 framework remained structurally intact with no observable degradation (Figure S17d,e). These results demonstrate that the nanosensor maintains structural integrity and sensing performance under relevant storage and operating conditions.

Detection of FA in Plant Matrices and Living Plants With CD@ZIF‐8‐Integrated MN

After verifying the mechanical properties and minimally invasive nature of nanosensor‐loaded MN, we investigated the sensing response of these MN toward FA. When treated with artificial plant sap containing FA, CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN exhibited a rapid ratiometric response within 5 min, with a significant decrease in fluorescence at 450 nm while the 610 nm emission remained stable (Figure 4e,f). Over 60 min, this quenching response intensified with increasing FA concentrations, showing a clear concentration‐dependent trend (Figure S18). Notably, ratiometric response in the range of 0‐0.5 ppm of FA showed a clear concentration‐dependent response at low FA levels, further confirming the applicability of the sensor in the low‐concentration regime (Figure S19).

We constructed a remote imaging setup for standoff monitoring of microneedle‐sensor fluorescence in real time (Figure S20). This system enabled non‐contact detection of fluorescence changes in a cuvette, confirming FA sensing by CD@ZIF‐8, with decreased fluorescence at 450 nm upon FA addition while 610 nm intensity remained stable (Figure S21 and Video S1 and S2). To evaluate sensor performance in realistic plant backgrounds, we next examined its response in leaf extracts from multiple species, including maize, cucumber, pak choi, and wheat. In untreated extracts, the fluorescence ratio (I450/I610) remained stable over time, indicating minimal interference from intrinsic biochemical and physicochemical components (Figure S22a,b). In contrast, FA‐spiked extracts from all plant species induced a pronounced, time‐dependent decrease in the ratiometric signal. These consistent results across diverse plant matrices indicate that the fluorescence response is predominantly driven by FA, with negligible contributions from variations in pH, ionic composition, or endogenous biomolecules. We further assessed potential interference from representative metabolites and organic acids commonly present in plant tissues, including salicylic acid, ferulic acid, p‐coumaric acid, citric acid, and malic acid. These compounds caused negligible changes in fluorescence ratio, whereas FA triggered a clear and distinct response (Figure S22c). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN exhibit high selectivity toward FA in complex plant environments.

To rigorously validate that the ratiometric fluorescence signal quantitatively reflects FA levels in infected plants, we performed parallel FA measurements using an orthogonal analytical technique. Microneedle‐based fluorescence sensing and high‐performance liquid chromatography‐mass spectroscopy (HPLC–MS) were conducted on the same cucumber (Cucumis sativus) plants infected with Fusarium oxysporum, a major fungal pathogen responsible for significant crop losses worldwide [2, 37]. Visible leaf yellowing was observed, suggesting advanced infection and substantial FA accumulation (Figure 5a), thereby providing a suitable disease model for cross‐method validation. HPLC‐MS confirmed FA identity by a dominant peak at m/z = 180.10 (Figure S23a), corresponding to the [M+H]+ ion, and calibration with FA standards showed excellent linearity (R2 = 0.99; Figure S23b). In parallel, CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN exhibited a concentration‐dependent decrease in the I450/I610 ratio when calibrated in healthy plant leaf extracts spiked with FA (Figure S24), providing a quantitative basis for interpreting nanosensor readouts in plant tissues. Using these orthogonal approaches, FA concentrations in stems and leaves were quantified and found to be in close agreement between nanosensor readouts and HPLC‐MS measurements (Figure 5b,c). Specifically, in stems, both methods yielded FA concentrations of approximately 4–6 ppm, while in leaves, values ranged from approximately 9–13 ppm. This strong concordance confirms that nanosensor readout quantitatively tracks FA levels in complex plant tissues, instead of arising from nonspecific quenching caused by other infection‐induced biochemical changes.

Having established quantitative accuracy in a late‐stage infection model, we next evaluated whether the platform could detect FA at earlier stages of disease progression. Pak choi (Brassica rapa) plants infected with F. oxysporum were used as a model system. Notably, after three weeks of growth in F. oxysporum–contaminated soil, infected plants remained visually indistinguishable from healthy controls (Figure 5d), highlighting the challenge of early diagnosis. Nevertheless, leaf extracts from infected plants induced a significant ∼75% decrease in the ratiometric signal, whereas extracts from healthy plants produced negligible changes (Figure S25). These results demonstrate that CD@ZIF‐8‐interfaced microneedles can detect FA in infected plants prior to visible symptom onset. To further evaluate in situ applicability without extraction for early infection diagnosis, CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN were directly applied to the base stems of both infected and healthy B. rapa plants (Figure 5e). The blue CD@ZIF‐8 emission was selectively quenched in infected plants, while the red emission remained stable in both the healthy and pathogen‐infected groups (Figure 5f). Quantitatively, the I450/I610 ratio of the nanosensors was ∼30% lower in Fusarium‐infected plants. Importantly, this clear signal contrast was obtained despite the absence of visible phenotypic differences, confirming the capability of CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN for early and non‐destructive diagnosis of fungal infection in living plants. We further assessed the specificity of sensor‐microneedle fluorescence responses under various abiotic stress conditions, such as high heat, salt, and UV stress, as well as pathogenic bacterial (Xanthomonas campestris) infection. The ratiometric signal showed minimal variation under these conditions and was clearly distinguishable from the substantial decrease induced by Fusarium infection (Figure 5g; Figure S26), demonstrating CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN selectively detect FA and discriminate fungal infection from other stresses.

Biocompatibility Assessment of CD@ZIF‐8‐Loaded MN

To study the biocompatibility of our sensing platform on living plants, we compared the chlorophyll content of living plant leaves untreated and treated with microneedles containing CD@ZIF‐8. The surfactant sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was used as a negative control as it can permeabilize the cell membrane, leading to cell death [38]. No significant difference was found between the microneedle‐treated and control groups, while the SDS‐treated group displayed markedly reduced chlorophyll content from the other two groups, indicative of leaf senescence (Figure 6a). We further employed propidium iodide (PI) staining to assess microneedle biocompatibility at the cellular level, as PI will fluorescently stain the nuclei of dead cells but not intact cells. PI staining showed negligible nuclear staining in the microneedle‐treated and untreated control groups (Figure 6b). In contrast, the SDS‐treated group exhibited extensive cell death (Figure 6c). We further examined whether the application of the sensing microneedle platform compromised the photosynthetic efficiency of living plants as another metric of biocompatibility. The microneedle‐treated group displayed similar or slightly higher values of the maximum quantum efficiency of Photosystem II (Fv/Fm), quantum yield of Photosystem II (ΦPSII), and non‐photochemical quenching (NPQ) compared to the control group, whereas the SDS‐treated group exhibited significantly lower photosynthetic efficiency (Figure 6d). These data suggest that CD@ZIF‐8 microneedle treatment did not disrupt the photosynthetic ability of living plant leaves, unlike SDS which induced a significant reduction in photosynthetic efficiency crucial for plant growth. Additionally, OJIP transient patterns of both the microneedle‐treated group and the control group displayed typical profiles of healthy plants, indicating normal functioning of the electron transport chain involved in photosynthesis (Figure 6e). These findings suggest that microneedle insertion into plant leaves had minimal effect on the photosynthetic capacity of the treated plants, providing evidence that the CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN exhibit excellent biocompatibility in living plants.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a novel ratiometric fluorescence sensor based on CD@ZIF‐8 optical nanoprobes for the selective detection of FA in plant samples. These nanosensors were successfully integrated into microneedle patches, allowing for a minimally invasive, rapid, and robust platform for in situ FA detection in plant tissues. The dual emission of CD@ZIF‐8, with fluorescence peaks at 450 and 610 nm, enabled ratiometric detection through FA‐dependent quenching in the blue emission while maintaining an FA‐invariant red reference signal, providing a self‐referencing optical readout. Unlike previous plant diagnostic strategies that require multiple nanosensors to obtain two emission channels, our dual‐emissive AIE‐active CD@ZIF‐8 nanoprobes achieve ratiometric sensing using a single probe, eliminating complications such as differential subcellular localization, inconsistent illumination, and sensor‐to‐sensor variability and thereby improving the practicality of plant diagnostics. The nanoprobes also demonstrated excellent selectivity against plant endogenous metabolites. Complemented with DFT calculations, we identified the key role of the pyridine carboxylic acid structure of FA in chelating Zn2+ ions and explained the distinct responses of blue and red emission of CD@ZIF‐8 toward FA. We further integrated CD@ZIF‐8 nanoprobes within biocompatible microneedles and demonstrated non‐destructive sensing in living plants infected with a Fusarium fungal pathogen, confirming its utility for biotic stress diagnostics in agriculture. Photosynthetic efficiency and cellular viability measurements verified the biocompatible nature of the CD@ZIF‐8 microneedles for plant biotechnology applications. These results highlight the sensor's potential for practical applications in monitoring FA both in extracted plant samples and directly within living plants.

Compared with previously reported FA detection methods (Table S2), including chromatography‐based assays and other reported optical sensing strategies that still rely on sample extraction prior to analysis, the proposed nanosensor‐microneedle platform enables direct, minimally invasive, and in situ detection in living plant tissues. Importantly, the low detection threshold of the sensor (∼0.1 ppm), together with its well‐resolved response in the low‐concentration range, indicates that its dynamic range is suitable for the biologically relevant concentration window of early Fusarium infection. This interpretation is further supported by the successful discrimination of infected plants at an asymptomatic stage, well before the onset of visible phenotypic changes (Figure 5). Taken together, these results demonstrate that the MN architecture enables a robust and reliable signal for FA detection while offering clear practical advantages over existing destructive analytical approaches.

This study showcases the potential for the CD@ZIF‐8‐based sensor in agricultural diagnostics, but further exploration is needed. Specifically, the long‐term stability of both the fluorescence properties and the microneedle platform under greenhouse or field conditions is critical. Understanding how these variables impact sensor functionality is essential for ensuring reliable and repeatable results over time. Additionally, scaling up the manufacturing process and ensuring the reproducibility of microneedle arrays for large‐scale agricultural applications remains a challenge. Nonetheless, emerging 3D printing and mold‐based fabrication strategies offer promising routes toward scalable and reproducible microneedle production [39, 40]. Another avenue for future research is combining the CD@ZIF‐8 sensor with real‐time monitoring systems using wireless technology or portable devices. This could provide agricultural users with actionable insights into crop health, allowing for timely interventions and improved pest management strategies. By addressing challenges related to environmental stability, sensor scalability, and field testing, the integrated nanosensor‐microneedle biosensing platform could play a pivotal role in advancing sustainable agriculture and precision farming.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Consumables

Zinc acetate hexahydrate (Zn(OAc)2·6H2O), hexadecyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB), tween 20, 2‐methylimidazole (HMIM), melamine (MA), dithiosalicylic acid (DTSA), acetic acid, fusaric acid (FA), nicotinic acid, picolinic acid, 2,5‐pyridinedicarboxylic acid and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, Mw ∼145,000) was purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (Singapore). All reagents were of analytical grade and used directly without further purification. Deionized water was produced through a Millipore water purification system (Milli‐Q, Millipore) and used throughout the study. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) female molds of microneedle patches (ST‐10×10‐H300B100P500) were purchased from Micropoint Technologies Pte., Ltd. (Singapore).

Synthesis of ZIF‐8

A mixture of Zn(OAc)2·6H2O (2.7 mmol) and HMIM (27.2 mmol) was dissolved in CTAB solution (10 mL, 0.55 mM). ZIF‐8 precipitated from the solution after undisturbed incubation at 25°C for 2 h. ZIF‐8 was washed with methanol (3 × 5 mL) and dried in air (30 min).

Synthesis of CD

CD was synthesized with slight modifications from previous studies [18]. Briefly, MA (201.6 mg) and DTSA (544 mg) were dissolved into acetic acid (40 mL). The solution was transferred into a Teflon reactor (80 mL) and kept at 180 °C for 10 h. After reaction, the solution was poured into 1 L of boiled water to form CD powder. The precipitated CD powder was washed by water and collected through vacuum filtration.

Synthesis of Carbon Dots@ZIF‐8 (CD@ZIF‐8)

The prepared CD (20 mg) was dispersed in 5 mL of water. Then, 5 mL of Zn(OAc)2 solution (0, 20, 40 or 60 mg/L) was added into the CD dispersion and stirred for 1 h. Subsequently, HMIM (4 M, 5 mL) and CTAB (0.55 mm, 0.5 mL) were introduced and left undisturbed to form CD@ZIF‐8 precipitation at 25°C for 2 h. Finally, CD@ZIF‐8 was washed with methanol (3 × 5 mL) and dried in air for 30 min.

Preparation of Microneedle Patch

To prepare a CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded microneedle patch, 10 mg of CD@ZIF‐8 was first dispersed in 1 g of PVA solution (10 wt.%) as the precursor. 100 mg of precursor was cast on a microneedle mold. The precursor‐mold system was subsequently kept under 0.1 bar for 15 min followed by 12‐h dehydration in a desiccator. Finally, CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN patch was peeled from the mold. Blank MN patch was prepared by excluding CD@ZIF‐8 from the PVA precursor.

Fluorescence Analysis

Fluorescence intensities of solution samples were determined using a BioTek Synergy H1 hybrid microplate reader. CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN samples were imaged by a Nikon Ti2U fluorescence microscope with DAPI and Texas Red dichroic filters (Chroma). Fiji ImageJ was used for false‐color processing and fluorescence quantification.

Detection of FA in Fusarium‐Infected Plants

Unless otherwise specified, the detection of FA in plants using MN was performed on whole, intact plants. CD@ZIF‐8‐loaded MN patch was applied directly to the plant stem. After absorbing plant sap for 5 min, the MN patch was removed, and in‐situ FA detection was conducted using a fluorescence microscope. For artificial plant sap samples or plant extract samples, the liquid sample was dropped onto the surface of the MN patch. After 5 min, the MN patch was observed under a fluorescence microscope. The composition of the artificial plant sap was shown in Table S3. For cuvette samples, we used a detector equipped with 425 and 500 nm long‐pass filters to monitor the emission fluorescence from the cuvette at a distance of 1 meter under constant excitation, recording fluorescence changes over 5 min.

In Vitro Screening of Sensor Selectivity

To demonstrate the specificity of the nanosensor (CD@ZIF‐8) for the detection of FA in plants, we selected endogenous plant metabolites, including glucose (2.5 mm), sucrose (66 mM), L‐cysteine (41 µµ), glycine (67 µµ), tryptophan (24 µµ), gibberellic acid (2.8 mm), and indoleacetic acid (57 µµ) [41]. In addition, several FA analogues (at 10 ppm) were chosen, including picolinic acid, nicotinic acid, isonicotinic acid, and 2,5‐pyridinedicarboxylic acid.

Sample Characterizations

Sample morphology was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM‐2010) and high‐resolution TEM (HRTEM, JEM‐2100F). UV‐Vis absorption spectra of the powder samples were collected using a Shimadzu UV‐1800 ultraviolet‐visible spectrophotometer. PL spectra were measured using a Hitachi FL7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer instrument apparatus. Time‐resolved fluorescence decay traces were recorded with Fluorolog‐3 system (Horiba Jobin Yvon, France) by using an excitation wavelength of 365 nm and an emission wavelength of 450 nm. IBH DAS 6.0 software was used to measure lifetime decay data using the iterative deconvolution method. The decay values were fitted with bi‐exponential function. Crystallographic information of samples was established by X‐ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source. The chemical bonds with characteristic infrared absorption bands were characterized by a FTIR spectrometer (attenuated total reflection mode, Bruker Vertex 70). X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS‐His, Kratos Analytical) was used to analyze the surface composition of the samples. XPS spectra were calibrated with respect to C 1s at a binding energy of 284.5 eV. Zeta potential was measured by Zetasizer (ZSU5700, Malvern Panalytical). Chlorophyll fluorescence imaging was performed using Closed FluorCam 1300 (Photon System Instruments, Czech Republic). OJIP transients were measured using FluorPen instrument (Z991, USA) after 20‐min dark adaptation.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.), and n represents biological replicates or independent nanoparticle sample replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using OriginPro 2021. Comparisons among three or more groups were evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was defined as ns (p > 0.05), * (p < 0.05), ** (p < 0.01), and *** (p < 0.001) with a 95% confidence interval.

Details on sample extraction and FA quantification with HPLC‐MS, DFT calculations, biocompatibility, mechanical testing, and stress response evaluations are provided in the Supplementary Information.
