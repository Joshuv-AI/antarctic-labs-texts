Addressing binge eating in people with type 2 diabetes: Co-creating and feasibility testing dialogue tools based on a design thinking approach
Authors: Pil Lindgreen, Loa Clausen, Helle Nergaard Grønbæk, Charlotte Humble Andersen, Khalida Ismail, Bryan Cleal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBinge eating disorder may affect one in four individuals with type 2 diabetes, potentially leading to negative health outcomes. However, binge eating often goes unaddressed in diabetes care due to the absence of screening tools and the reluctance of patients to seek help due to shame and guilt.ObjectiveThe study aim was to develop acceptable and feasible diabetes-specific dialogue tools to facilitate discussions about binge eating between patients with type 2 diabetes and clinicians, serving as a first step toward the detection and management of potential binge eating in this population.DesignThe development and evaluation of the dialogue tools were conducted according to the Medical Research Council’s complex interventions framework.SettingIndividuals with type 2 diabetes and clinicians were recruited from a specialized regional diabetes hospital during March to September 2023. Researchers were recruited from the same hospital and relevant research networks.ParticipantsThe co-creation process consisted of 11 workshops and 16 individual feedback meetings with people with type 2 diabetes (n = 21), clinicians (n = 16), and researchers (n = 8). Three clinicians feasibility-tested the dialogue tools (n = 24).MethodsApplying a design thinking approach, the dialogue tools were co-created with individuals with type 2 diabetes, diabetes clinicians, and researchers with diabetes and/or eating disorder expertise. The dialogue tools were feasibility tested consecutively in routine diabetes consultations.ResultsThe study generated three dialogue tools aiming to facilitate discussions among clinicians and patients with type 2 diabetes about the patients’ i) daily thoughts about food and drink, and whether they find these burdensome, ii) eating and drinking patterns, and iii) overall eating habits. Each dialogue tool included three parts; in part one, potential eating challenges in everyday life with diabetes are described, and the purpose of the exercise embedded in the tool is described. In part two, the patient actively carries out the described exercise, and in part three, the patient and clinician discuss follow-up questions. Parts two and three were designed to help patients recognize and reflect on their eating behaviors. Potential binge eating behaviors were identified in 25 % of the consultations, while other eating challenges that could impact diabetes management were commonly discussed in the remaining consultations.ConclusionsThe dialogue tools may be effective in addressing not only binge eating but also other significant eating challenges in individuals with type 2 diabetes during routine diabetes consultations. Further research is encouraged to evaluate the tools in different settings.

Background

By 2045, it is estimated that 783 million people worldwide will be living with diabetes, an increase of nearly 250 million relative to the current level (Sun et al., 2022). Besides being socioeconomically burdensome, due to loss of productivity and health expenses (Sortsø et al., 2018), living with type 2 diabetes is often experienced as onerous, due to the numerous self-management tasks involved to achieve recommended glycemic levels (Polonsky et al., 1995). Such tasks include blood glucose monitoring, medication administration, and paying attention to dietary intake and weight (Davies et al., 2022). Many people with type 2 diabetes also experience marked emotional distress due to persistent concerns about diabetes complications (e.g., neuropathy and nephropathy), untimely death (Polonsky et al., 1995), and the stigmatizing belief held by others, including clinicians, that type 2 diabetes is self-inflicted as a result of poor lifestyle choices (Salvia et al., 2023; Bennett and Puhl, 2023). Such negative influences on the physical and mental well-being of people with type 2 diabetes may be exacerbated by co-existing binge eating (Salvia et al., 2022). Binge eating is defined as recurrent episodes of eating a large amount of food within a discrete period of time while experiencing a loss of control and subsequent emotional distress, such as feelings of guilt, shame, and self-disgust (World Health Organization, 2023). At least one binge eating episode a week over a period of three months or longer is typically applied as the diagnostic threshold for binge eating disorder (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). The etiology of binge eating is not yet fully understood, but is widely recognized as being multifactorial, including biomedical (e.g., genetics and medication), psychosocial (e.g., personality traits and childhood trauma), and environmental factors (e.g., food availability) (Rienecke et al., 2022; Harris et al., 2021).

Prior research has reported binge eating disorder to be up to 10 times as prevalent in people with type 2 diabetes than in the general population (1.4–25.6 % vs. 2.3–3.5 %, respectively) (Abbott et al., 2018; Chevinsky et al., 2020), while the prevalence of subthreshold binge eating disorder is unknown. Systematic reviews have documented that binge eating often correlates with obesity and elevated long-term glycemic levels (HbA1c) (Harris et al., 2021; Abbott et al., 2018; García-Mayor and García-Soidán, 2017), both of which may elevate the risk of diabetes-related complications and overall mortality (Davies et al., 2022). In addition, controlled studies have repeatedly demonstrated correlations between binge eating and significantly reduced psychosocial well-being, including body image concerns, depression, anxiety, social phobia, and suicidality (i.e., suicidal thoughts and/or suicide attempts) (Araujo et al., 2010; Baek et al., 2018; Olgiati et al., 2022; Thompson et al., 2022). Finally, if left unaddressed, subthreshold eating disorders may progress into full-syndrome eating disorders (Koreshe et al., 2023).

Co-existing type 2 diabetes and binge eating may negatively reinforce each other, thus making individuals feel “trapped in a vicious cycle” (Salvia et al., 2022;Lindgreen et al., 2024a). Insulin injections, for example, can sometimes induce cravings that trigger binge eating, subsequently leading to elevated glycemic levels that need to be managed by additional insulin (Salvia et al., 2023; Salvia et al., 2022; Lindgreen et al., 2024a). Likewise, people with type 2 diabetes may experience heightened guilt when engaging in binge eating, as they worry about its impact on glycemic levels. Some individuals cope with this emotional distress by resorting to further binge eating which, in turn, results in additional emotional turmoil (Salvia et al., 2023, Salvia et al., 2022; Lindgreen et al., 2024a). Thus, binge eating may have adverse effects on the well-being and diabetes management of people with type 2 diabetes, and seen in this light, how it is or is not addressed routinely in diabetes treatment warrants further attention (Chevinsky et al., 2020). Although the vast majority of people with type 2 diabetes and eating disorders typically have had the eating disorder prior to their type 2 diabetes diagnosis (Herpertz et al., 1998), research indicates that diabetes treatment may introduce or intensify binge eating behaviors due to the extensive focus on diet, exercise, and body weight (Lindgreen et al., 2024a; Ritholz et al., 2022). For instance, feelings of guilt may arise in relation to unsuccessful attempts to achieve recommended levels of weight loss, which can, ultimately, provoke binge eating as a coping mechanism (Salvia et al., 2022; Lindgreen et al., 2024a). Still, binge eating detection rates are, for various reasons, estimated to be poor (Chevinsky et al., 2020). On the clinician side, this may result from a lack of awareness of the issue, the absence of appropriate tools to address the topic, or concerns about overstepping personal boundaries if inquiring about sensitive information like binge eating (Chevinsky et al., 2020; Chao et al., 2019). On the patient side, poor detection rates may, in part, be explained by feelings of guilt and shame and concerns about being humiliated and judged (Salvia et al., 2022; Lindgreen et al., 2024a; Kornstein et al., 2016). Various screening tools for binge eating disorder exist (House et al., 2022), but as these measures are generic, they do not address binge eating in people with type 2 diabetes specifically. In addition, existing screening tools aim to detect binge eating disorder as opposed to facilitate a constructive dialogue in-clinic about binge eating behaviors. The aim of this study was, therefore, to co-develop and test relevant and feasible dialogue tools addressing binge eating in people with type 2 diabetes during routine type 2 diabetes consultation.

Methods

Design thinking: reflexivity and theoretical framework

A design thinking approach was used to develop relevant and feasible dialogue tools in collaboration with end-users and stakeholders. Following a needs assessment, in which the perspectives and needs of stakeholders are explored, co-creation ensured user relevance through iterative prototype development and testing, culminating in feasibility testing to assess applicability in real-world contexts (Brown and Wyatt, 2010).

In design thinking, the researchers are actively involved when generating and analyzing data during the needs assessment and the co-creation process (Brown and Wyatt, 2010). To safeguard that findings and prototypes are not merely a reflection of predefined expectations, researchers are required to remain reflexive by constantly seeking to recognize and challenge their own biases about the topic (Malterud, 2001; Thorne, 2016). In this study, the researchers’ multidisciplinary clinical and academic qualifications within nursing, medicine, psychiatry, health psychology, nutrition, public health, and medical anthropology facilitated reflexivity nuanced discussions of preliminary findings and their own positioning within the research field.

In line with the biopsychosocial understanding of the etiology of binge eating (Rienecke et al., 2022; Harris et al., 2021) and the multidisciplinary backgrounds of the researchers, the primary theoretical framework employed in this study reflected Engel's biopsychosocial perspective (Engel, 1977). In practice, this theoretical lens guided the design thinking process by ensuring that biopsychosocial aspects of living with co-existing type 2 diabetes and binge eating were investigated.

The design thinking process of this study consisted of two phases: 1) a development phase which included a needs assessment and a co-creation process, and 2) a feasibility testing phase in which the co-developed dialogue tools were tested in-clinic and adjusted further (Fig. 1). Development

Needs assessment: The specific purpose of the needs assessment was to investigate the biopsychosocial support needs of people with type 2 diabetes and binge eating to generate knowledge informing the co-creation process (Brown and Wyatt, 2010). The needs assessment was carried out in two previous studies conducted in 2022–2023 and published as open access in 2024 (Lindgreen et al., 2024a; Lindgreen et al., 2024b), where the methods and findings are described in more detail. Below, the these are presented briefly to provide context.

First, we attained an overview of the literature to gain insights into existing evidence concerning the experiences, needs, and preferences of people with type 2 diabetes and binge eating. Major databases (including Pubmed, Cinahl, Embase, and PsycInfo) was searched using relevant search terms (including “type 2 diabetes”, “feeding and eating disorders”, and “disordered eating”). Next, individual in-depth semi-structured interviews with people with type 2 diabetes and varying degrees of binge eating (N = 20) were conducted in one study to explore their everyday life experiences and treatment needs (Lindgreen et al., 2024a). The interview guide addressed significant topics and knowledge gaps about type 2 diabetes and binge eating identified in the literature search (Lindgreen et al., 2024a). In an additional study, eight months of online observation in a type 2 diabetes-specific Facebook group with 6500 members was carried out to investigate topics related to binge eating that were discussed among the group members (Lindgreen et al., 2024b). The themes and subthemes of the studies were derived based on the qualitative methodological approach of Interpretive Description (Thorne, 2016).

The two needs assessment studies identified that people with type 2 diabetes and binge eating often experience immense emotional distress due to feelings of shame and guilt. The study participants described their reflections on how type 2 diabetes and binge eating constituted a vicious circle where worries about being at an increased risk of developing diabetes complications due to prior binge eating could, in fact, result in further binge eating performed as a way of coping with the emotional distress. In addition, the participants reported a need to speak up about binge eating to normalize it and to be able to seek help and support to manage it, precisely because it is associated with such marked feelings of guilt and shame. The findings were integrated in the dialogue tool prototypes that were developed, tested, and altered in the co-creation process.

Co-creation process: Five co-creation workshops were carried out in March-September 2023, with people with type 2 diabetes and current or prior binge eating (n = 18) alongside six workshops with clinicians working in type 2 diabetes care (n = 16) (Fig. 1). The workshops were supplemented by consecutive individual feedback sessions with people with type 2 diabetes and current or prior binge eating (n = 3) who were unable or reluctant to attend a workshop. Additionally, individual feedback sessions were held with researchers (n = 8) providing feedback based on their expertise in diabetes and eating disorder treatment and research.

The mean age of the participants with type 2 diabetes was 65.3 years. Their mean diabetes duration was 16.2 years, and 38.1 % were female. The clinicians and researchers’ mean age was 55.5 years, and 83.3 % were female. Of the sample, 37.5 % had a PhD degree; among the 16 clinicians, the mean number of years working in clinical diabetes care was 18.2. Participating in several workshops or feedback sessions was encouraged to ensure that some participants reviewed the alterations made to the initial prototypes. Six people with type 2 diabetes and binge eating and five clinicians participated in two workshops each. Five researchers participated in two individual feedback sessions each.

The workshops aimed to co-develop dialogue tools aligned with the biopsychosocial support needs identified in the preceding needs assessment. To enhance ideation, creativity, discussions, and interaction among the workshop participants, different prompts were utilized (Brown and Wyatt, 2010). Prompts included hypothetical case stories, quotes from the needs assessment interviews as well as questionnaire items and exercises from existing screening measures and dialogue tools related to type 2 diabetes or binge eating. Examples include selected parts of the Problem Areas In Diabetes (PAID) scale (Polonsky et al., 1995), the REDUCE intervention (Stenov et al., 2024), the Empowerment, Management, and Adherence (EMMA) dialogue tools (Varming et al., 2015), the Disordered Eating Problem Survey Revised (Markowitz et al., 2010) (DEPS-R), and the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q) (Lichtenstein et al., 2021). Prototypes of dialogue tools co-created in one workshop were presented, tested, and discussed in the following workshop and adjusted according to the workshop participants’ feedback Fig. 2 provides an overview of how workshop feedback was integrated in the development of the dialogue tools during the co-creation process.Fig. 2Illustration of the co-creation process.Fig. 2

The workshops were audio recorded and tangible output (e.g., drawings and post-it notes) was documented in written summaries and photographs. These summaries were analyzed using systematic text condensation to generate themes that depicted topics of importance to the workshop participants (Malterud, 2012). To validate these topics, the workshop audio recordings were revisited repeatedly. Furthermore, all versions of the dialogue tools were tracked to document the changes made throughout the co-creation process.

Workshop participants with type 2 diabetes and binge eating were asked to fill in a survey to collect sociodemographic and health-related data. To avoid the survey affecting the topics discussed in the workshops, it was filled in at the end of each workshop. The survey included the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q); a 28-item questionnaire measuring the range and severity of eating disorder symptoms within the previous 28 days (Carey et al., 2019). The EDE-Q includes four subscales (Restraint, Eating Concern, Shape Concern, and Weight Concern), each measured on a 7-point Likert scale, a total score (i.e., the mean across the subscales), and binging and purging frequencies (Carey et al., 2019). The survey also comprised the World Health Organization-Five Well-Being Index (WHO-5); a 5-item scale assessing mental well-being within the previous 14 days using a 6-point Likert scale. The WHO-5 generates a score between 0 and 100 (from the worst to the best possible mental well-being) with a cut-off of 50 or less often used to screen for depression (Topp et al., 2015). The WHO-5 and the EDE-Q are translated into Danish (Lichtenstein et al., 2021; Topp et al., 2015). Based on the findings of a systematic literature review including studies applying the Danish WHO-5, the instrument is considered a valid overall well-being assessment tool with a pooled sensitivity of 0.86 and specificity of 0.81 (Topp et al., 2015). Although the original factor structure of the EDE-Q was not replicated, a study demonstrated that the Danish version had a good construct validity, with Pearson’s correlations ranging from 0.35 to 0.77 when compared to other validated eating disorder screening tools, supporting its utility in detecting eating disorders (Lichtenstein et al., 2021). In our study, descriptive statistics were calculated using RStudio version 4.3.0.

Program theory: According to the Medical Research Council’s framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions, an intervention should rest on a program theory in which the following is described: i) the desired outcomes of the intervention, ii) the mechanisms of the intervention that will expectedly lead to the desired outcomes, and iii) contextual factors that affect how the intervention is delivered (Skivington et al., 2021). The desired outcome of applying the dialogue tools was defined as people with type 2 diabetes feeling comfortable discussing any eating concerns or challenges with their clinician. Based on the findings from the needs assessment and some of the core mechanisms embedded in the prompts applied in the workshops (Stenov et al., 2024; Varming et al., 2015), we expected the following mechanisms to be useful to include in the dialogue tools: i) the normalization of binge eating and other eating challenges in everyday life with diabetes, and ii) aided reflection of one’s relationship with food. Based on feedback from the clinician workshops, expected contexts of importance were that clinicians felt confident that they had enough time available, and the skills needed to apply the tools. Therefore, the dialogue tools were only tested in annual status consultations with a duration of 60 min, and the clinicians received thorough oral and written instructions about how to apply the tools in consultations.

Feasibility testing

The feasibility testing took place in September-December 2023 to assess the real-world feasibility and acceptability of the co-developed dialogue tools, guided by the Medical Research Council’s framework (Skivington et al., 2021). The objectives were to examine: i) mechanisms influencing tool effectiveness, (ii) contextual factors affecting use, iii) practical outcomes, and iiii) needed adjustments (Skivington et al., 2021).

Consultations were observed by the first author or two trained assistants (BSc in Exercise and Sport Science; and Social Anthropology) using a semi-structured observation guide (Supplementary material 1). Field notes were compared to identify mechanisms, outcomes, contextual factors, and areas for tools modification (Skivington et al., 2021).

After each observed consultation, both the participants and clinicians completed a brief satisfaction rating scale (adapted from prior instruments with response options from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree” plus “not relevant”) (Stenov et al., 2021). Sociodemographic and health data (for instance, occupation, HbA1c, and diabetes duration) were collected from participants with type 2 diabetes. Descriptive statistics were computed using RStudio version 4.3.0.

Setting

The study took place at Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen, a specialized regional diabetes hospital serving >12,000 people with diabetes, including >4300 adults with type 2 diabetes. Patients are referred by general practitioners for complications or comorbidities. The hospital includes a research department and a department for diabetes education and knowledge dissemination.

Recruitment

Needs assessment

In the interview study, participants were recruited through invitations distributed via a local volunteer user panel affiliated with Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen (70 %), the Danish Diabetes Association (15 %), and a Facebook post in a type 2 diabetes-specific group describing the needs assessment study (15 %). Participants self-reported binge eating disorder; formal diagnoses were not required, consistent with the exploratory design. Symptom severity was assessed using the EDE-Q (Lindgreen et al., 2024a). In the Facebook study, data were limited to publicly available discussions on co-existing type 2 diabetes and binge eating, and users were not directly contacted (Lindgreen et al., 2024b).

Co-creation

People with type 2 diabetes and self-reported binge eating were recruited via invitations sent to the aforementioned volunteer user panel . Inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years, type 2 diabetes for at least one year, varying degrees of prior or current binge eating, and fluency in Danish. All interested participants were contacted by telephone to ensure that the inclusion criteria were met, which was the case for all. To accommodate those unwilling to attend workshops due to topic sensitivity, individual feedback sessions were offered. Travel expenses were reimbursed.

Clinicians from the diabetes clinic and researchers with relevant expertise were invited by work email. Clinicians participated outside regular hours and were reimbursed for their time.

Feasibility testing

For feasibility testing, clinicians invited people with type 2 diabetes scheduled for an annual status consultation, ensuring a diabetes duration of ≥1 year and sufficient time to use the dialogue tools. Participants were not required to have current or prior binge eating, as the tools were designed to assess its presence.

Clinicians for this phase were recruited from the clinician workshop if interested in conducting feasibility tests. They received oral and written instructions on inviting eligible participants and applying the tools during consultations.

Three clinicians (one clinical dietitian, two diabetes nurse specialists) and 24 people with type 2 diabetes participated. Each of the three tools was tested in eight consultations. The mean age of participants with type 2 diabetes was 70.4 years; 33.3 % were female; mean diabetes duration was 18.0 years. Clinicians had a mean age of 49.7 years, all were female, and had an average of 15.7 years’ experience in diabetes care. Of eligible participants, four declined, giving a response rate of 83.0 %. Sample sizes

In the interview study, the sample size was considered sufficient when no new themes emerged, while in the Facebook study it was determined by the number of group members discussing co-existing type 2 diabetes and binge eating (Thorne, 2016).

In the co-creation process, all clinicians who wished to participate were included, and no researchers declined to contribute, yielding a practically determined sample size (Brown and Wyatt, 2010). The sample of people with type 2 diabetes was diverse in gender, age, and diabetes duration, and was therefore considered both purposively and practically appropriate (Thorne, 2016).

For the feasibility phase, sample size was pragmatically defined by the number of tests that clinical management deemed feasible.

Ethical considerations

All participants received oral and written information about the aim and methods of the study and the right to withdraw at any time. If agreeing to be included in the study, the participants signed an informed consent form. People with type 2 diabetes included in the feasibility testing were invited to participate by their clinician without the presence of the observer in the consultation room.

After pseudonymization, all digital data material was stored on encrypted drives with limited access, 24-hour logging, and backup (Harris et al., 2019). However, as only three clinicians participated in the feasibility test, they were made aware that, although pseudonymization would be attempted, it could not be guaranteed. Physical data material (i.e., signed consent forms and filled-in questionnaires) was kept behind two locks until it was added to the online study database and could then be disposed of.

The study was registered with the Danish Data Protection Agency (case no: P-2022-27) and conducted according to current legislation (Danish Ministry of Health, 2022; Danish Ministry of Health, 2020). No ethical approval was needed and an exemption letter was obtained (case no.: H-20082723) (Danish Ministry of Health, 2020).

Results

Dialogue tools developed in the co-creation process

The co-creation process in which the initial prototypes were developed and adapted into dialogue tools that were later feasibility tested is illustrated in Fig. 2. The co-creation process amounted to three dialogue tools entitled “My thoughts about food and drink in everyday life” (Tool A), “My daily eating and drinking pattern” (Tool B), and “My eating habits” (Tool C). Each tool includes an instruction sheet which is primarily for the clinician to read out loud, and an exercise sheet on which people with type 2 diabetes can write or draw to complete the exercise. Each tool includes three parts; Part one states that challenges with food and drink are common among people with diabetes and briefly summarizes the purpose of the tool. In part two, the exercise is described and completed. Part three is a follow-up section that includes questions for the clinician and individual with type 2 diabetes to discuss and reflect on. Each tool is described in more detail below.

Tool A aims to support the individual with type 2 diabetes in assessing whether they experience the amount of time and energy they spend thinking about food and drink as appropriate rather than burdensome. First, the individual with type 2 diabetes fills in an empty circle to illustrate how much time and energy they spend thinking about food and drink. Then, the individual with type 2 diabetes fills in another empty circle to show how much (or little) time and energy they would prefer these thoughts to consume. The degree of difference between these circles can be taken as an indication of how dissatisfied the individual with type 2 diabetes is with the time and energy they spend thinking about food and drink and, by implication, the scale of the challenge they feel that they are confronting with regard to changing this. The questions in the follow-up section have the aim of making the individual with type 2 diabetes think about i) if some of the thoughts are particularly distressing and why, and ii) if the individual can envision strategies to make these thoughts less pertinent.

In Tool B, the purpose is for the individual with type 2 diabetes to reflect on and illustrate when and how often during a typical day (24 h) they consume something (food or drink). The individual with type 2 diabetes writes an “F” for food and a “D” for drink next to the circle corresponding to the time of day when eating and drinking occur. The follow-up questions aim to make the individual reflect on i) if and when during the day or night eating and drinking pose challenges, ii) ideas to change undesired eating and drinking patterns, and iii) people who can support the individual if wanting to make such changes.

The objective of Tool C is for the individual with type 2 diabetes to think about their eating habits, and whether they are content with these. The individual reads five quotes (e.g., “I’ve experienced losing control over how much I ate”) and then assesses the degree to which each of the quotes fits their own eating habits. The follow-up questions in part three aim to make the individual consider if they, based on their completion of the exercise, i) want to change any eating habits, ii) have ideas for ways to change their eating habits, and iii) have suggestions for people in their social network who can provide support if deciding to make such changes. If wanting to change several habits, the individual is encouraged to select one to focus on initially.

Feasibility testing

Significant mechanisms and contexts

As expected, mechanisms leading to the desired outcome, namely, that people with type 2 diabetes were comfortable discussing any eating concerns, appeared to be i) the normalization of eating challenges in everyday life with type 2 diabetes, and ii) the process of reflecting on one’s relationship with food and drink. The validity of these mechanisms was supported by the feasibility testing data. For instance, that eating challenges were normalized when using the dialogue tools was exemplified by people with type 2 diabetes making statements such as “I am relieved that I am not the only one struggling” (participant 12) or “Since you [clinician] are bringing it up, I can’t be the only one” (participant 4). Similarly, several people with type 2 diabetes would make comments like “I’ve never thought about it [my eating pattern] like this before” (participant 15) or “I can’t believe I didn’t realize sooner that I would over-eat at night because I skipped lunch” (participant 8), suggesting that the dialogue tools made them reflect on their relationship with food and drink. Such comments were typically made when the individual with type 2 diabetes carried out the exercise and discussed the follow-up questions with their clinician. Additionally, the patient responses to the brief assessment scale indicated that the dialogue tools normalized and helped them reflect on any eating challenges. For instance, 62.5 % (Tool A and Tool C) and 50.0 % (Tool B) agreed or strongly agreed that the dialogue tool in question helped them “[…] understand that it is normal to experience food and drink as challenging […]” (Supplementary Material 2). When asked if the dialogue tool “[…] helped me reflect on my own relationship with food and drink […]”, 37.5 % (Tool A and Tool C) and 50.0 % (Tool B) agreed or strongly agreed, while 37.5 % (Tool A and Tool B) and 50.0 % (Tool C) agreed or strongly agreed that the dialogue tool "[…] helped me reflect on how I can manage challenges with food and drink”.

Besides the expected contexts of importance (namely, clinicians having enough time and the skills needed to test the tools) the absence of severe stressors in people with type 2 diabetes (e.g., other serious diagnoses or the recent loss of a spouse) was detected as a crucial context. For example, several of the individuals with type 2 diabetes declining to participate in the study had recently been diagnosed with cancer or other severe illness or had a partner that was critically ill. This indicates that the dialogue tools should not be prioritized in consultations with patients enduring such marked life stressors.

Outcomes of the dialogue tools

Our observations of the feasibility testing provided insights into the acceptability of the dialogue tools in routine type 2 diabetes consultations. In 25 % of the observed consultations, the tools initiated a dialogue in which the person with type 2 diabetes brought up, for instance, “[...] eating way more than planned although not feeling hungry” (participant 23), “[...] feeling like losing control when eating at night” (participant 5), and “[...] being concerned all the time about eating too much” (participant 19). Although there was nothing to indicate binge eating behavior in the other 75 % of consultations, the dialogue tools consistently enabled patient-clinician conversations about eating and drinking patterns, habits, and challenges relevant to type 2 diabetes management. For example, Tool B revealed in one participant that he typically had two glasses of milk before bedtime every night because it helped him feel calm and fall asleep. Until the clinician explained it to him, he had not considered that drinking milk likely made him tired by causing hyperglycemia, which would show in his blood glucose test the next morning. Other approaches to improve sleeping hygiene without causing elevated blood glucose levels were then discussed.

Another observation of interest was that Tool A often highlighted the positive significance of food and drink to people with type 2 diabetes rather than highlighting burdensome thoughts about food and drink. For instance, some people with type 2 diabetes practiced cooking as a hobby by experimenting with low-carb versions of common recipes or low-fat meal preparation alternatives like air frying. Others viewed their meals as something to look forward to during the day, a socially enjoyable event, or a way of treating oneself. Such responses provided the clinicians with insights into the perceptions of food and meal situations, in general, in people with type 2 diabetes. These insights could guide future treatment efforts, for example, by indicating areas where patients may be highly motivated to make changes benefitting their diabetes management (e.g., by trying out new diabetes-friendly recipes).

Finalizing the dialogue tools

We adjusted the tools slightly based on the insights gathered from observing the consultations in which the dialogue tools were tested (Fig. 3). In Tool A, filling in the empty circles to illustrate the amount of time and energy occupied by thoughts about food and drink was too abstract an exercise for most of the participants, which became apparent from the observational data. Thus, we replaced the circles with measuring cups which the participants considered easier to fill in. We altered Tool B by adding a slice named “lunch/midday” to the pie chart depicting 24 h of eating and drinking patterns, as the participants repeatedly expressed the opinion that it was missing. Finally, in Tool C, we changed one quote exemplifying an eating habit from a negative wording (“I have not been able to tell when I was hungry or full”) to a positive wording (“I have been […]”) to make the statement clearer. Acceptability of the dialogue tools

Data from the brief rating scales demonstrated that the participating patients and clinicians were generally satisfied with the dialogue tools. However, Tool C received the most positive ratings with all patients finding the exercise easy to execute and the majority finding it relevant to their everyday life with diabetes (87.5 %) and helpful in normalizing challenges with food and drink when living with diabetes (62.5 %). Similarly, the clinicians found Tool C useful in all tests for initiating discussions with patients about food and drink as well as easy to understand and apply. Following all tests, the clinicians stated that they would use Tool C in future consultations and would also recommend other clinicians to use it in type 2 diabetes care. Except for one clinician’s test rating of Tool C, none of the participating patients or clinicians stated that the tools disrupted the consultation, and only one patient found a tool (Tool C) leading them to feeling overwhelmed by having diabetes. All results from the satisfaction rating scale are listed in Supplementary Material 2.

Discussion

In this study, we co-developed and feasibility tested three dialogue tools aiming to facilitate discussions about binge eating between people with type 2 diabetes and clinicians. Such conversations may be the first step toward detecting and managing binge eating or other disordered eating behaviors in people with type 2 diabetes. Currently, this seemingly large population is, for the most part, not getting the treatment and support that it needs, often because they are not identified in the clinic (Abbott et al., 2018; Chevinsky et al., 2020). The dialogue tools can help counteract this if implemented successfully in routine diabetes care.

As described, 25 % of the individuals with type 2 diabetes participating in the feasibility testing of the dialogue tools in this study exhibited signs indicative of binge eating. This finding is in line with other studies detecting binge eating disorder when screening using validated questionnaires (Abbott et al., 2018; Chevinsky et al., 2020). Although the remaining 75 % of the study participants did not express having any binge eating behaviors, other eating challenges that may potentially affect their diabetes management were regularly discussed. This indicates that, although the dialogue tools are not diagnostic, using them in consultations may be an effective, structured way of addressing and managing not only binge eating behaviors, but also other forms of disordered eating or eating challenges affecting everyday life of people with type 2 diabetes. Addressing these eating challenges can also act as a preventive measure for individuals with type 2 diabetes who are at risk of developing full-syndrome binge eating disorder or other eating disorders. Early detection and intervention are crucial, as they can help prevent subclinical eating disorders from progressing into clinical diagnoses (Koreshe et al., 2023).

Our study findings emphasize the need for clinicians in type 2 diabetes care to receive clear written and oral instructions on using the dialogue tools to ensure that they feel confident in adopting them. This aligns with other research emphasizing the importance of step-by-step guidelines for clinicians on how to apply novel tools. Providing such guidance ensures that clinicians not only develop the necessary skills but also feel confident in their ability to apply the tools effectively (Stenov et al., 2024). Therefore, clinician self-efficacy should be a key consideration if planning the implementation of the dialogue tools developed in this study.

In this study, clinicians who participated in the feasibility testing of the dialogue tools were recruited from prior co-design workshops. At the start of each workshop, the prevalence, challenges, and consequences of co-occurring type 2 diabetes and binge eating were presented to underscore the purpose of the workshop. The clinicians’ participation in the workshops contributed to the successful feasibility testing of the tools by emphasizing their relevance and importance, thereby motivating clinicians to apply them. Similarly, other studies highlight the need for initial training sessions or workshops to take place when new tools or interventions are introduced in clinical care. It is also important to acknowledge that some clinicians may feel overburdened and thus unmotivated to adopt additional tools during routine consultations (Stenov et al., 2024; Fisher et al., 2017). However, recognizing how identifying binge eating can improve diabetes management and broader health outcomes, clinicians may come to view the tools as beneficial rather than burdensome. Thus, to promote a sense of ownership and purpose, future implementation of the dialogue tools will benefit from the provision of a thorough introduction to clinicians about their aims, namely, to facilitate discussions, normalize experiences, and initiate the management of binge eating or other eating challenges in people with type 2 diabetes. Additionally, emphasizing the rationale behind co-developing and integrating the dialogue tools into diabetes care, such as the prevalence and potential adverse effects of co-existing type 2 diabetes and binge eating, will further support their adoption by clinicians in type 2 diabetes care.

Future perspectives: In recent years, digital healthcare services have become increasingly common, aiming to increase patient adherence, overcome geographical barriers, and optimize resource use (Yeung et al., 2023). We therefore recommend future research explores how the dialogue tools can be digitized, possibly with the inclusion of interactive features that may increase patient engagement and satisfaction with the tools (Palacios et al., 2024).

As most people with type 2 diabetes are treated in primary care by general practitioners (Andersen et al., 2024), we recommend that the dialogue tools are tested in general practitioner consultations. If feasible in such settings, the dialogue tools may aid the detection and subsequent management of binge eating and other eating challenges in a vast number of people with type 2 diabetes.

Limitations

The generalizability of the findings from this study is limited by the fact that the dialogue tools were feasibility tested at a specialized diabetes hospital to which people with type 2 diabetes are only referred if they have diabetes complications and/or comorbidities. However, almost half of the people with type 2 diabetes participating in the co-creation process received diabetes treatment elsewhere than Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen, which may increase the likelihood of the developed dialogue tools being useful in other settings. We therefore recommend that future studies test the tools in other settings to learn if and how other mechanisms and contexts are of importance elsewhere when applying the tools (Skivington et al., 2021). While the optimal number of test consultations remains debatable (Skivington et al., 2021), additional testing would provide further data to support or challenge the findings of this study. Although the feasibility testing offered valuable insights into the usability and acceptance of the dialogue tools among patients and clinicians, further testing would be beneficial, given that the tools were evaluated in only 24 consultations.

Conclusions

While up to 25 % of individuals with type 2 diabetes may have binge eating disorder, it is often overlooked in routine diabetes care due to the absence of diabetes-specific detection tools. Additionally, individuals with both conditions may avoid seeking help due to marked emotional distress, including feelings of shame and guilt. In this study, we co-developed and feasibility tested three dialogue tools aimed at facilitating discussions about binge eating between individuals with type 2 diabetes and clinicians during diabetes consultations. These tools were designed to normalize the co-existence of type 2 diabetes and binge eating, while encouraging patients to reflect on their eating behaviors. The dialogue tools may be an effective approach for detecting and initiating the management of binge eating and other unfavourable eating behaviours in this population. Yet, successful implementation in clinical practice requires that clinicians have access to adequate training and feel confident in applying the tools. Further research is recommended to validate the current findings and assess the tools’ effectiveness in diverse healthcare settings.
