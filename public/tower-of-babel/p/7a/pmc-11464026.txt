Acquired cholesteatoma
Authors: Daniel Newsted, Munir Demir Bajin, Peng You
Published: 2024

Cholesteatoma is the presence of keratinizing squamous epithelium in the middle ear or mastoid space

Cholesteatoma is not a neoplasm and does not contain cholesterol. Eustachian tube dysfunction and chronic otitis media cause keratin accumulation (acquired cholesteatoma), which then causes local destruction to the middle ear structures and skull base. The incidence is about 10 cases per 100 000.1

Persistent otorrhea should raise suspicion of acquired cholesteatoma

Foul-smelling otorrhea and hearing loss are common presentations of cholesteatoma.2 This diagnosis should be considered with recurrent or persistent otorrhea for more than 2 weeks. Additional symptoms of facial nerve weakness, tinnitus, or vertigo suggest extensive disease and should prompt urgent referral to an otolaryngologist and computed tomography scan (lower cost and better availability than magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) to assess bony erosion and disease progression.3

Diagnosis requires a thorough otoscopic examination

The superior aspect of the tympanic membrane, pars flaccida, is where cholesteatoma is most commonly found and warrants special attention during otoscopy (Figure 1).4 Otorrhea or granulation tissue can be treated with combination steroid and antibiotic drops, twice daily for 7 days, followed by a reassessment in 2 weeks.4 If cholesteatoma is suspected, prompt referral to an otolaryngologist is necessary.

Treatment is surgical but recurrence rates are high

Cholesteatoma must be surgically removed. Rates of recurrence approach 40%, with symptoms often returning years after the initial surgery.1,5 Recurrent disease presents similarly to primary disease and, if suspected, the patient’s otolaryngologist should be notified.

Regular follow-up is important to assess for recurrence

Regular examinations under otomicroscopy are required to assess for new perforations, retractions, or new masses in the context of altered anatomy postoperatively. Patients are usually followed up every 6–12 months by their otolaryngologist; however, some patients require more frequent reassessment and débridement. Disease recurrence can be monitored using diffusion-weighted MRI.3
