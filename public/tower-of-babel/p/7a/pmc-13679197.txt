Healthcare professionals and relatives in shaping the preferred place of end-of-life care and death for patients with advanced cancer: the WhereToCare qualitative study
Authors: Amandine Ferreux, Léon Breuillot, Florent Schepens, Florence Mathieu-Nicot, Mathilde Giffard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough most people in France wish to die at home, the majority of deaths still occur in hospitals. The choices of end-of-life care for patients with advanced cancer and how these preferences are shaped by interactions with relatives and healthcare professionals, remains poorly understood. This study aimed to explore how patients with advanced cancer develop and adjust these preferences, and to examine the roles of relatives, general practitioners, and oncologists in this process.MethodsWe conducted a single-centre longitudinal qualitative study at a French university hospital involving adults with advanced, incurable solid cancers. Participants were invited to take part in up to three semi-structured interviews conducted approximately one month apart. Interviews explored illness trajectory, preferences for place of end-of-life care and death, and discussions with relatives and healthcare professionals. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsEleven patients with metastatic solid tumours receiving care at a French university hospital completed at least two interviews between January and November 2023. Findings showed that although relatives were a key source of emotional support, patients deliberately limited their involvement in end-of-life discussions to protect them, resulting in relational distancing. A second layer of isolation concerned general practitioners, who were rarely consulted and not perceived as relevant interlocutors for end-of-life issues due to limited involvement in cancer care. In contrast, oncologists were identified as the main medical references, but primarily as technical experts focused on disease management rather than as professionals to address existential or end-of-life concerns. Consequently, patients often lacked a clearly identified professional with whom to discuss end-of-life preferences, reflecting communicative and decisional isolation. The longitudinal design further showed that these preferences were not fixed but evolved over time, often without a stable interlocutor to support reflection and discussion. When available, palliative care teams, or participation in the research interviews themselves, partially mitigated this gap by offering a space for reflection.ConclusionEnd-of-life preferences among patients with advanced cancer are strongly shaped by concerns for relatives, which may lead to limitations in discussions and contributes to a sense of isolation, with no clearly identified professional responsible for addressing these issues. Background

In France, as in many countries, there is a marked discrepancy between people’s preferred and actual place of death. The French population’s preference of dying at home has been consistently documented. In a 2010 survey, 81% of respondents expressed the wish to “spend their final moments at home” [1]. This finding was confirmed by a 2022 national survey, in which 60% of participants reported wanting to die at home, and an additional 22% indicated that they would do so only if adequate support were available, representing a total of 82% of respondents [2]. This finding underscored the importance of anticipating end-of-life care trajectories, considering both patient perceptions and the conditions required to make these wishes feasible.

Despite this strong and stable preference, most deaths in France still occur in institutional settings. In 2019, 53% of deaths took place in hospitals and 12% in nursing homes, while home deaths accounted for only about a quarter of cases [3]. Similar discrepancies have been reported internationally: although most people in good health express a wish to die at home, death at home remains the exception rather than the norm [4].

Previous studies have identified multiple factors associated with the place of death, including the availability of home-based care, disease trajectory, socioeconomic characteristics, and the involvement of relatives or primary care physicians. The presence of a general practitioner (GP) and early involvement of palliative care teams have been shown to increase the likelihood of dying at home [5, 6]. However, several studies have highlighted the limited occurrence of end-of-life discussions between patients and healthcare professionals. In France, for instance, Pennec et al. reported that in 30% of cases, the patient’s preferred place of death was not known by the attending physician [7]. When such preferences are discussed, they are not always recorded in the medical file, leading to possible omission of these preferences when forming a care plan.

In addition, few studies have examined the evolution of patients’ preferences over time. The available evidence suggests that these wishes may change as the disease progresses or as physical, psychosocial, or familial circumstances evolve [8, 9]. However, the underlying reasons for such changes (and the mechanisms by which patients construct and revisit their end-of-life choices) remain poorly understood.

For patients’ end-of-life wishes to be heard and respected, they must be expressed, shared and discussed. Yet, it remains unclear with whom these discussions should take place and who is best positioned to support patients in developing an end-of-life care plan, relatives, oncologists, or GPs. The present study aimed to understand how patients with advanced cancer construct and revise their preferred place of end-of-life care and death over time through interactions with relatives and healthcare professionals. Specifically, this article focuses on the role played by relatives and healthcare professionals (particularly GPs and oncologists) in shaping these evolving preferences.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a single-centre longitudinal qualitative study using semi-structured interviews with patients with cancer receiving care at the university hospital of Besançon in France. We used thematic analysis to explore decision-making regarding the place of end-of-life care and death. Study reporting adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ).

Participants

In order for patients to be eligible for inclusion, they had to meet the following criteria: diagnosis of advanced-stage solid tumor, treatment at the university hospital of Besançon, and fluency in French. Both men and women were included provided they were over the age of 18.

Patients were also required to have an incurable disease, with prognosis previously provided by the referring physician and clearly understood by the patient.

Participants were excluded if fewer than two interviews could be conducted or if the patient or their relatives refused an audio recording of the interviews.

Recruitment

Patients were recruited in collaboration with physicians from the palliative care unit and the oncology department at the university hospital of Besançon. These physicians were familiar with potential participants, enabling them to identify patients who met the inclusion criteria and to inform them that they might be contacted by the research team, should they provide verbal consent.

Subsequently, the investigator contacted each patient to explain the study objectives and procedures, and to provide the Information and Non-Opposition Form, in accordance with French regulations. Participants were free to withdraw at any time. A patient was considered included after the first interview, and their data were deemed usable once the second interview had been completed.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with the included patients, who were invited to participate in three sessions approximately one month apart, in a location of their choice.

The first interview began with a sociodemographic questionnaire. Both the remainder of the first interview and the follow-up interviews used open-ended questions covering the main study themes: medical history and its progression, preferred places of end-of-life care and death, and any related discussions with relatives or healthcare professionals (the interview guide is available in the supplementary file).

Data collection was conducted concurrently with analysis, allowing the interview guide to be adapted as the study progressed. For example, an item addressing the experience as a family caregiver for a deceased relative was added.

All interviews were audio-recorded, made anonymous, and fully transcribed prior to analysis. Each participant and each interview were assigned a unique anonymization code.

Data analysis

Study themes were not predefined but emerged from the data. Consistent with the exploratory design and without reliance on a predefined theoretical framework, the analysis followed an inductive thematic analysis approach. Coding was performed by the two interviewers, who first coded the data independently and then compared and discussed their coding at each stage during regular meetings. Codes were iteratively organised into categories and broader themes through an ongoing process of discussion and refinement. Two cases were fully double-coded, and the resulting coding structures were compared to ensure consistency. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, leading to refinement of the thematic framework. The research team confirmed the inter-coder agreement and validated the thematic structure. Decisions regarding the cessation of data collection were made once data and thematic saturation were reached.

Research team

The research team consisted of three women and two men. The two interviewers were A. F., a resident in general medicine, and L. B., a psychologist and clinical research officer. Dr. M. G. was the principal investigator of the study and is a physician in the mobile palliative care team at the university hospital of Besançon. Dr. F. M-N. was a psychologist in the same team and is also a researcher in psychology. Pr. F. S. is a sociologist.

Results

Sample characteristics

Out of twenty patients approached, seventeen were included in the study. We were able to complete the entire study with eleven participants (see Fig. 1).

Participants flow chart

The interviews took place between January and November 2023. Semi-structured interviews were spaced at least four weeks apart and mostly conducted at patients’ homes. They lasted an average of 45 min, ranging from 13 min to 1 h and 51 min. For three of the eleven participants, only two interviews could be conducted.

Of the eleven participants, four were men. Participants were between 48 and 78 years old at the time of inclusion, with a median age of 53 years. All had metastatic solid cancers, with disease durations ranging from 11 months to 11 years (median: 40 months). Breast cancer was the most represented oncological condition in the sample.

Among the eleven participants, eight died between inclusion and the writing of this article. One death occurred at home, as wished by the patient; six occurred in hospital settings, including four in the palliative care unit of the university hospital of Besançon. Relatives as a source of support in care and decision-making

During the interviews, participants emphasized the essential role played by relatives in coping with the illness. Participants explained that they needed their support, because of the vulnerability that comes with the disease and the awareness of being in an end-of-life situation. Although they do not directly take part in the decision-making process, relatives support patients in decisions concerning the initiation or continuation of oncological treatments.

“As I say, if you don’t have the support of your loved ones, it’s much harder to get through.” (04-10-1, about the important family support she received during her illness.)

Even though families are more or less informed about end-of-life decisions

However, regarding end-of-life issues, participants involved their relatives very little in the reflection process. Some participants mentioned that they had already communicated certain wishes, such as the preferred place of death, but only to inform in a manner that removed relatives from the deliberation process. Communication on this topic was often limited or even deliberately avoided, with the intention of protecting loved ones. Once the information was given, patients rarely revisited the topic.

“No, I don’t talk about it anymore, it’s done. Once I’ve said it, I’ve said it. No, I’m not going to stir that up again, what for? It’s no use reopening the wound.” (04-06-1, about informing his six children of the progression of his cancer and his end-of-life wishes).

In order to protect them

Participants described the daily impact of the disease not only on themselves but also on their relatives.

“I know it’s not easy for him either — being the spouse of a patient is not easy every day, let’s be honest.” (04-11-3, about his husband and the difficulties within their relationship, partly caused by the illness).

Consequently, the progression of the disease and thoughts about end-of-life concerned them not only for their own sake but also because of the potential burden this could place on their relatives.

Thus, decisions were often made with consideration for relatives’ experiences.

This concern was particularly highlighted when discussing the hospital as a potential place of death. Three main arguments emerged to justify the preference for an institutional setting for end-of-life care and death.

Firstly, participants wished to spare their relatives the burden associated with caregiving that accompanied worsening symptoms and loss of autonomy. Therefore they perceived hospitalization as a way to relieve their family of this strain.

“I don’t want to impose, to say ‘I’ve chosen to die at home,’ if it’s not comfortable for my husband either — I don’t want to impose that on him.” (04-16-2, regarding the possibility of imposing her end-of-life choices on her husband).

Moreover, some participants doubted their relatives’ ability to provide adequate end-of-life care at home, which made the hospital setting preferable:

“Yes, because when I’m not doing well, how will he (her husband) react? For him and for me, maybe a bit selfishly, I wonder how he’ll manage. That’s one of the reasons — not the only one — why I prefer to die in hospital.” (04-12-3, regarding her wishes concerning her preferred place of death in a hospital).

Secondly, participants expressed concern about the aesthetic aspect of dying. For some, dying at home was not an option because the last days of life involve significant physical and psychological deterioration (cognitive, emotional, and consciousness changes).

“If it really gets worse… if I deteriorate too much, I don’t want my children to go through that… hum… That would be the limit, if I see that I’ve become useless, a wreck… Yes, I’d rather be in the hospital so that my children don’t witness the decline.” (02-01-1, describing her preference for hospital care in case of severe decline to avoid exposing her children to the dying process).

At the same time, this concern also reflects a wish to preserve self-esteem and the final image left to loved ones:

Finally, the third argument was the protection of the home environment. For several participants, the home should not be “contaminated” by death; it should remain a space for life for those who continue living there. Dying at home could compromise this symbolic meaning of home:

“I recall someone I knew who felt the same way and who said to her children: ‘No, this is a place of life, of joy, not of suffering and death,’ referring to her home. I feel the same way.” (04-12-2, regarding her wishes concerning her preferred place of death in a hospital).

Participants distinguished clearly between the GP and the oncologist

When asked about the role of physicians in their care, participants drew a clear distinction between the specialist and the GP. The latter was consulted only secondarily and rarely about cancer-related issues:

“When you’re followed in the hospital, wrongly I think, you tend to see your GP much less.” (04-11-2, answering the question ‘Is your GP aware of changes in treatment and the evolution of your health condition?’).

The GP was consulted only for issues unrelated to cancer or for administrative purposes. Overall, patients did not perceive their GP as essential to their cancer management:

“LB: And is your GP still involved in all this? – Patient: Well, I talk to her on the phone from time to time, but for now, I don’t really need her. Well, yes, I’ll see her tomorrow for some paperwork, but that’s all.” (02-01-1).

In contrast, for almost all participants, the oncologist was the key point of contact — the one who understood the disease and could potentially cure it. Participants generally felt well supported by their oncologist, whom they saw regularly:

“Especially since it’s been ten years now that I’ve been going to oncology. So yes, that’s the service that knows me best.” (04-17-3, regarding the role of her oncologist).

It was also the oncologist or oncology service that they contacted in case of problems or emergencies:

“Who am I going to call if things go wrong? During my treatments, I’ve already called them in the evening and on weekends — it’s great. They immediately fax a prescription to my pharmacy if needed — it’s really great.” (04-16-2, regarding the role of her oncologist).

Whose opinion on illness and treatment matters to them

Although secondary, the GP was expected to stay informed about the course of the disease and ongoing treatments. These key updates were shared either during unrelated consultations or through hospital correspondence:

“That’s why I haven’t seen my GP lately. But he knows everything — they send him all the reports, blood tests, everything. He follows from afar. I haven’t seen him.” (04-10-3, answering the question ‘Is your GP aware of changes in treatment and the evolution of your health condition?’).

This link with the GP was maintained because they remained the local physician, someone the participants hoped to rely on later:

“AF: So your GP is still in the loop? – Patient: Yes, yes. And I think that’s important, because when things go bad, I know he’ll be there. […] I think the relationship with the GP remains important.” (04-11-3).

Sometimes, the GP could contribute to treatment decisions, but it was ultimately the oncologist’s opinion that weighed most in choices regarding treatment and overall management. What patients sought was the oncologist’s expertise:

“I prefer to talk to her because I’m expecting a more medical, expert opinion.” (01-05-1, regarding his exchanges with the oncologist rather than with his general practitioner).

But whom they rarely consult about end-of-life discussions

When it came to end-of-life reflections or discussing their feelings and experiences, neither the GP nor the oncologist was perceived as a resource person. When decisions were made, they were made by the patients themselves, who then informed the doctors of their wishes. Once informed, patients expected their doctors to ensure that these wishes were respected:

“That’s it, I’ve already told the doctors at the hospital. (…) I told my GP as well.” (04-06-1, after she described to the researcher her wishes regarding the place of care and the individuals she had informed).

Despite recognizing the value of a third party to discuss their wishes

However, such discussions did not always take place. When they hadn’t, participants identified other professionals as potential resources to help them reflect on their wishes — particularly concerning the place of end-of-life and death.

“I don’t know if I should see the psychologist or the palliative care team to work this through, to get it off my chest, to have one less weight to carry.” (04-11-2, mentioning her difficulty in making decisions regarding her end-of-life wishes).

Indeed, participants who had met with palliative care professionals during their follow-up reported having been able to discuss their overall feelings and approach end-of-life topics with them. Finally, it appeared that successive interviews within the study may have helped participants clarify their wishes, expectations, and limits. Even when no formal decisions were made, these discussions encouraged reflection and supported their personal journey:

“Afterwards, I really appreciated your visits, because it was a kind of motivation, a framework that pushed me to think more deeply.” (04-12-3, mentioning her need for external support in navigating end-of-life issues).

Discussion

Main findings

A key theme across the interviews is the participants’ desire to protect their loved ones, which strongly shapes their decisions. Preferences regarding place of death are largely framed by concerns about relatives’ experiences and the potential burden imposed by illness and the end-of-life period. When participants feel they may become a burden or anticipate increasing caregiving demands, they tend to prefer hospital-based end-of-life care to spare their families (see Fig. 2). These findings suggest that preferences regarding place of death are shaped in relation to interactions with relatives and healthcare professionals, rather than being fixed individual preferences.

Thematic map of the findings

This aligns with the literature: both patients and healthcare professionals consider relatives’ experience when defining end-of-life care plans [10, 11]. Cancer patients often avoid talking about end-of-life issues with their relatives to protect them, both to avoid distress and to protect their relationship [12]. Patients even consider that not being a burden to their family constitutes a criterion for a “good death” [13]. This protective dynamic reflects a relational process in which patients anticipate the emotional and practical responses of their relatives, and adjust their own preferences accordingly.

Findings regarding healthcare professionals mirror existing literature. GPs remain largely peripheral to oncology care. They tend to “lose sight” of patients followed in oncology [14] or are consulted for issues unrelated to cancer management, such as routine health concerns or the psychosocial follow-up of the patient and relatives [15]. Patients preferentially turn to oncologists, perceived as the appropriate experts for cancer-related concerns. In a study conducted between 2018 and 2019, Tomasso et al. reported that only 46% of unplanned oncology inpatients had consulted their GP for the symptom that led to their unplanned admission to the cancer centre [16]. Similarly, Bungener et al. reported that GPs remained “medically active” for only about one in two oncology patients [17]. GPs tend to regain a more central role as the disease progresses and care becomes increasingly comfort-oriented, when cure is no longer the goal [18]. Nevertheless, they remain only marginally involved in end-of-life discussions [19].

Oncologists occupy a central role in cancer care, sought for their expertise in treatment decisions and overall management. The rise of advance care planning in oncology has positioned them as key actors in comprehensive patient care. The goal is to anticipate the course of the disease and its symptoms (whether related to the disease itself or to its treatments) as well as to support discussions about end-of-life care [20, 21].

However, unlike treatment-related issues, which are routinely discussed with the medical team, end-of-life discussions rarely occur with the healthcare professionals who follow the patient. In this study, patients who had expressed their wishes had seldom discussed them with their oncologist. Bernard et al. explored why such discussions rarely occurred, with patients citing fear of affecting their long-standing relationship with this emotionally involved physician [12]. Conversely, it seems that healthcare professionals feel more comfortable discussing end-of-life wishes when they are their patients [22].

Patients are therefore left alone to grapple with their doubts, questions, and choices. This could be explained by the taboo surrounding end-of-life care and death in our society. Indeed, the literature confirms that patients expect physicians to initiate end-of-life discussions [12, 23], while physicians expect patients to raise the topic first [24, 25]. From the physician’s perspective, choosing the “right moment” is crucial to avoid appearing abrupt when addressing such sensitive issues. Consequently, no one dares to broach this essential subject. Yet, the literature also shows that discussing end-of-life matters does not increase symptoms of depression or anxiety. In fact, it can even bring relief once patients have clarified and shared their wishes. Wright et al. demonstrated that, beyond the benefits of easing the burden of medical management at the end of life, such discussions may also be associated with better acceptance of the terminal phase of illness [19].

This may explain why patients seek external support in a context where end-of-life preferences are shaped and negotiated across fragmented interactions with relatives and healthcare professionals. A third party could play a key role in listening to, and supporting reflection. This third party could be a healthcare professional or a loved one, who would only need to initiate the discussion concerning end-of-life issues.

Beyond confirming findings from previous studies, this work adds a longitudinal perspective showing how end-of-life preferences are not fixed but progressively shaped, adjusted, and sometimes constrained over time through limited and fragmented interactions. It also highlights a form of relational and communicative isolation, in which no single actor consistently supports patients in articulating and revisiting their preferences. Finally, our findings underline the potential role of a third party not only as provider of support, but also as facilitator of reflection within these interactional gaps.

Strengths and weaknesses

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a single tertiary care centre, reflecting a specific organisational culture and model of collaboration, which may limit its generalisability. The findings rely solely on patients’ perspectives; including family caregivers and/or referring oncologists could provide a more comprehensive understanding of end-of-life decision-making. Finally, the follow-up period may have been too short to capture the longitudinal evolution of patients’ experiences and attitudes toward end-of-life care.

Nevertheless, the study also has notable methodological strengths. The design involved repeated interviews with the same participants over several weeks, allowing for a more dynamic understanding of their experiences and likely enhancing the depth and richness of the data on this sensitive and complex topic.

Conclusion

In this longitudinal qualitative study, discussions about end-of-life preferences were infrequent, and patients often appeared to develop these reflections in relative isolation through limited interactions with others. Limited communication with relatives was largely driven by a desire to protect them from emotional distress and burden. Within the healthcare system, oncologists were primarily perceived as technical experts focused on cancer treatment, while general practitioners were mainly consulted for non-cancer-related issues or administrative matters, resulting in limited interactions regarding end-of-life concerns. These patterns reflect how end-of-life preferences are shaped through fragmented interactions across personal and medical contexts. As a result, neither group was consistently involved in end-of-life discussions, reflecting current relational dynamics and professional role expectations rather than an inherent inability to address such issues.

In this context, patients frequently expressed the need for a supportive third party to help them reflect on, articulate, and revisit their end-of-life preferences over time. This need emerges within an interactional context where discussions are not consistently shared across a single relational space. The growing integration of early palliative care into oncology supports a more structured implementation of palliative care principles within cancer care pathways, with an emphasis on creating spaces for dialogue rather than focusing solely on end-of-life transitions. Facilitation of discussions about patient preferences may benefit from a broader interprofessional approach involving nurses, psychologists, social workers, and palliative care teams. Overall, strengthening the relational and organisational conditions that enable ongoing conversations about end-of-life preferences appears essential.

Under French legal framework, written consent is not required for this qualitative study. According to Article L.1122-1-1 of the French Public Health Code, research involving humans requires free, informed, and express consent, but the form of that consent (written or verbal) may vary depending on the type and risk of the study. In non-interventional qualitative studies such as WhereToCare, verbal informed consent is permissible, provided that participants are properly informed and their agreement is documented. In this study, participation was based on verbal agreement from all participants, and any refusal to participate or to use data was documented through a signed opt-out form.
