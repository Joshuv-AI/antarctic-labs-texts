Mechanobiology of hypertrophic chondrocyte mineralization: Biomechanical regulation and therapeutic implications in skeletal disorders
Authors: Yuhao Liu, Guangyu Xu, Yi Shen, Shaotian Fu, An Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChondrocyte proliferation, differentiation and hypertrophic mineralization are central events that guide subsequent vascular invasion and bone replacement processes. This process is controlled by a sophisticated molecular network including transcription factors such as SOX9 and Runx2, as well as key signaling pathways such as Bone Morphogenetic Protein (BMP), Indian Hedgehog (Ihh) and Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF). Dysregulation of this network due to genetic mutations or metabolic deficiencies can disrupt mineralization, which underpins aberrant skeletal biomechanics properties. Therefore, understanding the physiological and pathological mechanisms governing chondrocyte proliferation, differentiation and mineralization holds significant potential for developing novel therapeutic strategies for disorders with compromised skeletal biomechanics.Keywords: Chondrocyte, Hypertrophic mineralization, Endochondral ossification, Biomechanics

Introduction

Bone formation begins with the aggregation of mesenchymal cells to form condensates.1,2 Mesenchymal condensates differentiate into chondrocytes that undergo proliferation and hypertrophy.3 Hypertrophic chondrocytes increase their volume by 10-20 times and send signals to induce blood vessel invasion.4 The invasion of blood vessels and osteoclasts remodels the hypertrophic cartilage, followed by osteoblastic ossification to form a new bone. The transition from cartilage to bone is critical in bone biomechanics. A complex molecular network regulates chondrocyte differentiation and mineralization. In this review, we summarize the current understanding of the key transcriptional factors, signaling pathways, the extracellular matrix, and the mineralization process. In addition, we summarize the related pathologies and diseases to emphasize the importance of chondrocyte differentiation, mineralization and bone formation in bone biomechanics.

Transcriptional regulation of chondrocyte differentiation

The master transcription factor SOX9

SOX9 is a master transcription factor that initiates chondrogenic differentiation in MSCs. It activates cartilage-specific extracellular matrix genes, such as Col2a1, Col11a2 and aggrecan, and suppresses Runx2 activity to prevent premature hypertrophy.5,6 SOX9 functions synergistically with the co-factors SOX5 and SOX6 to ensure proper chondrocyte differentiation7, 8, 9. Although SOX5 and SOX6 lack intrinsic transcriptional activation domains, they are essential co-factors of SOX9. By forming transcriptional complexes with SOX9, they significantly potentiate its activity on downstream target genes, including Col2a1 and Aggrecan. Smits et al. demonstrated that in SOX5/SOX6 double-knockout mice, although SOX9 expression and the early stages of chondrogenesis are initiated, the absence of SOX5/SOX6-mediated cooperative enhancement leads to the failure of efficient extracellular matrix production by chondrocytes. This results in severe cartilage underdevelopment and structural fragility.10 (Fig. 1).

Transcriptional regulation of chondrocyte differentiation and hypertrophy.

The homeobox transcription factor Nkx3.2

The homeobox transcription factor Nkx3.2 (also known as Bapx1) plays an essential role in endochondral ossification during axial skeleton development. Genetic evidence from Nkx3.2 null mice demonstrates that loss of Nkx3.2 function leads to severe vertebral agenesis and profound skeletal malformations, which result from a failure of chondrogenic makers such as Col2a1, SOX9 and Ihh. These findings underscore the indispensable role of Nkx3.2 in regulating the progression from prechondrogenic mesenchymal cells to functional chondrocytes during axial skeletogenesis.11 Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling initiates somitic chondrogenesis by inducing the expression of the transcriptional repressor Nkx3.2 and the HMG-box transcription factor SOX9. Once established, Nkx3.2 and SOX9 engage in a positive autoregulatory loop in which each factor promotes the expression of the other in the presence of BMP signals, thereby maintaining the chondrogenic program independent of continued signaling. Mechanistically, Nkx3.2 represses the synthesis of an unknown inhibitor of SOX9 expression, thereby derepressing SOX9. This indirect mode of regulation allows Nkx3.2 to sustain SOX9 expression and activity, which in turn drives the expression of cartilage matrix genes, such as aggrecan, epiphycan and collagen IX. Importantly, while Nkx3.2 can only promote chondrogenesis in the paraxial mesoderm, SOX9 is sufficient to induce de novo cartilage formation in non-chondrogenic tissues.12 Nkx3.2 also suppresses Runx2 expression to inhibit premature chondrocyte hypertrophy.13 Thus, by promoting SOX9 expression and inhibiting Runx2, Nkx3.2 helps maintain chondrocyte stability and prevents premature transition towards hypertrophic pathways.14 (Fig. 1).

The master transcription factor for chondrocyte hypertrophy Runx2

In the growth plate, chondrocytes in the proliferation zone cease to divide and differentiate into hypertrophic chondrocytes. During this transition, the expression of SOX9 is downregulated to relieve its suppression of Runx2 and allow the hypertrophy program to proceed at full capacity.15,16 The expression and activity of Runx2, a master transcription factor for chondrocyte hypertrophy, are modulated by the complex interplay of various signaling pathways. Ihh directly promotes Runx2 transcription, whereas BMPs upregulate the Ihh signal, thereby indirectly supporting Runx2-driven maturation. Wnt/β-catenin signaling is critically required downstream of Runx2 for chondrocyte survival and osteoblast differentiation. However, parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP), activated downstream of Ihh, exerts a dominant negative effect by counteracting Runx2 function, creating a negative feedback loop that controls the pace of hypertrophy.17,18 The transcriptional activity of Runx2 is also enhanced by MEF2C in growth plate chondrocytes.8 Driven by these factors, chondrocytes undergo a dramatic increase in size, simultaneously degrading and remodeling their original matrix to produce a new matrix characterized by an abundance of type X collagen. MMP13 and ADAMTS-4 play major roles in this remodeling process.19 ADAMTS-4 is a key enzyme responsible for aggrecan degradation and exhibits high activity in the hypertrophic zone.20 The initiation of abundant type X collagen secretion serves as the most specific marker of hypertrophic chondrocytes.21 (Fig. 1).

Key signaling pathways during chondrocyte differentiation

The Bone Morphogenetic Proteins

Mineralization of hypertrophic chondrocytes requires tight molecular control, including different signaling pathways and various transcriptional regulation. Bone Morphogenetic Proteins (BMPs) play a crucial role in the core signaling network. BMPs do not directly deposit calcium phosphate crystals.22 BMP2, which is highly expressed in the hypertrophic zone23 can transduce signals via the Smad-dependent and Smad-independent pathways during mineralization.24 Upon receptor binding, Smad1/5/8 are phosphorylated and translocated to the nucleus to cooperate with transcription factors such as Runx2 to upregulate the expression of mineralization-related genes, including Col10a1 and ALPL. BMP2 also upregulates key signaling molecules, including Ihh, to regulate hypertrophic chondrocyte differentiation.23,25 BMP6 is another highly expressed gene in hypertrophic chondrocytes, working synergistically with BMP2 to promote chondrocyte differentiation and mineralization.26 In addition, BMP4 is involved in the initiation of chondrogenesis. BMP7 also promotes osteoblast differentiation following mineralization. In addition, GDF5 regulates joint development and cartilage matrix synthesis.27,28 The BMP family also includes inhibitory factors, such as BMP3, which counteracts excessive ossification and maintains mineralization homeostasis. BMP3 binds to Activin Receptor Type IIB (ActRIIB) and recruits BMPRII to form a complex. This complex does not activate the Smad1/5/8 pathway but activates the inhibitory Smad2/3 signaling cascade to play a crucial negative regulatory role in maintaining bone homeostasis.29,30 In summary, hypertrophic chondrocyte mineralization relies on the sophisticated regulation of the BMP network. Its members exhibit functional redundancy, spatiotemporally ordered expression, and mutual antagonism to ensure the maintenance of homeostasis (Fig. 2).

Key signaling pathways during chondrocyte differentiation.

The Indian Hedgehog (Ihh) signaling pathway

The Indian Hedgehog (Ihh) signaling pathway, along with Gli and Runx2 transcription factors, forms a precise regulatory hierarchy that collectively directs the differentiation of chondrocytes31, 32, 33. The process begins with Ihh secretion from chondrocytes in the proliferative zone of the growth plate. Ihh helps maintain homeostasis in the proliferative zone by regulating the PTHrP signaling pathway to prevent chondrocyte hypertrophy.31 In regions adjacent to the hypertrophic zone, chondrocytes are exposed to high Ihh concentrations, which promotes their entry into the hypertrophic differentiation program by suppressing the transcriptional repressor activity of Gli3. The activated Gli1/2 proteins translocate into the nucleus to bind specific promoter regions of target genes, such as Runx2, to regulate hypertrophic chondrocyte differentiation. Runx2 forms a positive feedback loop by enhancing Ihh expression to amplify differentiation signals. Additionally, the Hedgehog pathway upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression to facilitate vascular recruitment into the mineralization zone31,33, 34, 35 (Fig. 2).

The fibroblast growth factors

The fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling pathway plays a critical role in chondrocyte regulation. During endochondral ossification, multiple FGFs and FGF receptors (FGFRs) exhibit distinct spatial and temporal expression patterns in the growth plate. FGFR1 expression persists throughout all stages of chondrocyte differentiation, whereas FGFR3 expression is restricted to the resting and proliferating zones. All four FGF receptors (FGFR1-FGFR4) can upregulate the expression of SOX9.36 Among these, the function of FGFR3 has been the most extensively studied. Activating mutations in FGFR3 in mice lead to a marked reduction in chondrocyte proliferation. This is accompanied by a significant shortening of both the proliferative and hypertrophic zones, resulting in shorter and less organized chondrocyte columns.37 Furthermore, FGFR3 signaling negatively regulates Ihh expression. Ihh is upregulated in FGFR3-deficient mice, indicating that FGF signaling acts as an upstream inhibitor of Ihh to modulate chondrocyte hypertrophy.37,38 (Fig. 2).

Extracellular matrix mineralization

Extracellular matrix metabolism

At the matrix metabolism level, type II collagen secreted by proliferating chondrocytes is progressively replaced by type X collagen, produced by hypertrophic chondrocytes. Type II and type X collagens play distinct biomechanical roles.39 Type II collagen contains a region consisting of three polypeptide chains (α-chains) wound into a triple-helix structure. The triple helix structure provides the extracellular matrix with important shear and tensile properties that help stabilize the matrix. Proteoglycans occupy the interfibrillar space of the chondrocyte extracellular matrix, which is critical for its ability to resist compressive loads.40 Furthermore, bound water with proteoglycans results in low matrix permeability, which maintains a stable mechanical microenvironment.41 In contrast, type X collagen has shorter molecules that do not form fibrils but assemble into fine pericellular filaments or supramolecular hexagonal lattices. It contributes to the compartmentalization of matrix components within the hypertrophic zone, ensuring that matrix vesicles and proteoglycans are retained in the appropriate locations to provide an environment conducive to mineralization. Moreover, type X collagen interacts directly with vesicles and activates Ca2+ influx, thereby regulating the mineralization process. Deficiency of type X collagen leads to an abnormal distribution of matrix vesicles and proteoglycans, resulting in altered trabecular bone architecture and mineralization patterns.42 Type X collagen possesses specialized domains that must be proteolytically removed to facilitate subsequent cell-mediated mineralization, suggesting its role as a transient modulator rather than a permanent structural scaffold.43

Matrix vesicles in mineralization

Matrix vesicles are key functional carriers that initiate mineralization in hypertrophic chondrocytes. They mainly originate from multivesicular bodies (MVBs),44 which utilize molecular machinery, such as the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT), to sort and package intraluminal vesicles enriched with key mineralization-related proteins and lipids44, 45, 46. This process intersects with the cellular autophagy system. For example, microautophagy is involved in sorting pre-formed calcium phosphate crystal nuclei or specific enzymes into multivesicular bodies to ensure the precise loading of mineralization raw materials47, 48, 49. The membranes of these matrix vesicle bodies are rich in mineralization-related proteins including annexins, tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP/ALPL), ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1) and the phosphate transporter-1 (Pit-1). Annexin 5 and 6 can bind to phosphatidylserine in the presence of calcium ions to form calcium channels and thus to allow Ca2+ to enter and facilitate the detachment of the vesicle from the membrane50, 51, 52. Pit-1 transports free phosphate into matrix vesicles.53 ENPP1 hydrolyzes ATP to release pyrophosphate (PPi). TNAP/ALPL then hydrolyzes PPi into inorganic phosphate (Pi), thereby removing the inhibition of PPi and supplying substrate for mineralization.54,55 Calcium ions and inorganic phosphate continuously flow into the vesicle lumen, where they condense to form an amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) precursor, which further undergoes a spontaneous phase transformation.56 The first crystalline phase to form is octacalcium phosphate (OCP), which appears as plate-like or needle-like crystals.57,58 Octacalcium phosphate further hydrolyzes in solution, absorbing calcium ions and releasing hydrogen ions to transform its structure towards hydroxyapatite. During this conversion process, a type of hydroxyapatite with a calcium content lower than the ideal stoichiometry is formed, known as calcium-deficient hydroxyapatite (CDHA).59,60 Calcium-deficient hydroxyapatite continues to acquire calcium and phosphate ions to repair defects in its crystal structure. The Ca/P ratio eventually reaches the ideal value of 1.67, forming mature hydroxyapatite crystals with a typical hexagonal crystal structure.61,62 (Fig. 3).

As these molecules are enriched in membrane microdomains, these regions begin to bulge outward from the cell surface. The neck of the bud constricts and seals to detach from the cell membrane of hypertrophic chondrocytes63, 64, 65, 66.

The size of matrix vesicles typically ranges from 100 to 300 nm64, which allows them to diffuse within the extracellular matrix and interact with collagen fibrils at specific sites, thereby regulating the spatial distribution of mineralization.67 It should be emphasized that matrix vesicle-mediated mineralization is not the only pathway for endochondral ossification. Existing evidence confirms that acellular processes can also drive mineralization, and collagen plays a pivotal role in mineral formation.42 Type X collagen has specific molecular surfaces that serve as nucleation sites for hydroxyapatite crystal deposition.42

The process of releasing crystals into the matrix described above is more consistent with dystrophic mineralization. For physiological ECM mineralization, the polymer-induced liquid-precursor (PILP) theory proposed by L. Gower provides a more comprehensive explanation: In the PILP process, anionic polypeptides are placed in solution with supersaturated levels of calcium and phosphate. The acidic polypeptide sequesters ions to induce nanodroplets of liquid-like amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) precursor. These liquid-like precursors infiltrate collagen fibrils and then undergoes a phase transition to form mineral crystals, which are then deposited on the collagen scaffold.58,68

Although the mineralized cartilage matrix has a certain hardness, it remains as cartilage. Its mechanical strength, toughness, and fatigue resistance are far inferior to those of bone.68 Moreover, cartilage tissue is avascular. Its nutrition relies on diffusion from surrounding tissues. This supply method limits the thickness and repair capacity of the cartilage.69,70 This necessitates the replacement of soft cartilage with a hard bone tissue.

Mineralization by hypertrophic chondrocytes and the biomechanics of the extracellular matrix

During endochondral ossification, the mineralization of the cartilage matrix surrounding late hypertrophic chondrocytes is initiated by the release of membrane-bound matrix vesicles that serve as nucleation sites for hydroxyapatite deposition. This mineralized cartilage matrix is thought to provide a temporary scaffold that supports the subsequent deposition of bone by osteoblasts, as evidenced by studies in which reduced matrix mineralization led to decreased bone volume in the primary spongiosa.9 Cartilage matrix mineralization confers significant biomechanical stability to the growth plate. Radhakrishnan et al. have shown that the elastic modulus of the extracellular matrix increases substantially from the non-mineralized reserve zone to the mineralizing zone, reflecting enhanced capacity to sustain compressive mechanical stresses.71 Furthermore, the interface between the growth plate and metaphysis exhibits a gradual increase in tissue modulus, enabling efficient load redistribution and providing mechanical protection to the underlying cartilage structure.72 This mineralization-driven stiffening is closely regulated by the local mechanical environment and serves as a critical preparatory step for the subsequent replacement of cartilage with bone.73 The mineralized cartilage matrix serves as a source of signaling molecules that coordinate the invasion of the ossification front. VEGF is synthesized by hypertrophic chondrocytes precisely before the onset of cartilage neovascularization.74 This chondrocyte-derived VEGF drives endothelial cell migration and invasion through activation of VEGFR2 activation, establishing a paracrine angiogenic loop that guides blood vessel invasion into the hypertrophic cartilage.74 Following vascular invasion, osteoclasts assist in the removal of the cartilage matrix, while differentiating osteoblasts utilize the remnants of the cartilage matrix as a scaffold for authentic bone matrix.9 This coordinated process ensures the orderly replacement of cartilage with bone during endochondral ossification. Therefore, mineralization mediated by hypertrophic chondrocytes is a critical step in the transition from a plastic cartilage to load-bearing bone tissue, serving as an early event that contributes to substantial improvement in biomechanical performance.

This imbalance in mineralization regulation is closely associated with various pathologies. In osteoarthritis, pathological mineralization in the deep layers of articular cartilage (associated with a hypertrophic chondrocyte phenotype) reduces cartilage elasticity, increases brittleness, and accelerates wear and fracture under stress.75,76 Additionally, certain genetic bone diseases caused by ALPL dysfunction, such as hypophosphatasia, severely compromise skeletal biomechanical integrity due to inherent defects in the mineralization process, leading to deformities or increased fracture susceptibility.

In summary, the impact of hypertrophic chondrocyte mineralization on skeletal biomechanics is dependent on the context. Its timely, moderate, and localized occurrence is fundamental for acquiring the initial strength and stability. Conversely, dysregulation of this process is a key pathological factor leading to increased bone brittleness and deteriorated mechanical performance. Therefore, the fine-tuned regulation of mineralization is central to maintaining the healthy mechanical function of bone.

Mineralization-related diseases and advanced therapies

Achondroplasia

Autosomal dominant mutations in fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) cause achondroplasia (Ach), the most common form of dwarfism in humans (Table 1).77,78 Compared to wild-type mice, the resting zone was significantly expanded in FGFR3936/936 mice, whereas the proliferative and hypertrophic zones were markedly reduced and disorganized.79 FGFR3 missense mutations can lead to ligand-independent activation or dimerization of FGFR3, which can subsequently induce Stat1, Stat5a, Stat5b nuclear translocation to upregulate the expression of cell cycle inhibitors P16 and P19.79,80 In addition, FGFR3 mutation downregulates the expression of Ihh and the PTHrP receptor, causing arrest in chondrocyte differentiation.79

Pseudoachondroplasia

Pseudoachondroplasia (PSACH) is a rare skeletal dysplasia primarily caused by mutations in the cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) gene (Table 1). It is mainly characterized by disproportionate short stature.81 COMP is a pentameric glycoprotein that interacts with collagens I, II, and IX in a zinc-dependent manner via its C-terminal globular domain, thereby contributing to the stability of the collagen fibrillar network82, 83, 84. In addition, COMP interacts with aggrecan through its signature domain and glycosaminoglycan side chains and with fibronectin via its C-terminal globular domain, supporting its role as a key matrix-organizing protein in the cartilage extracellular matrix.85,86 COMP contains seven type 3 calcium-binding repeats. Calcium binding induces a conformational shift from an extended to a more compact structure, as mutant COMP (D469del) exhibits an extended conformation compared to wild-type. This calcium-dependent conformation is critical for COMP interactions with ECM proteins, such as aggrecan, and for chondrocyte attachment, thereby modulating protein affinity.87 Mutant COMP fails to be secreted properly from chondrocytes and accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where it co-aggregates with binding partners such as type IX collagen and matrilin-3. This accumulation induces severe ER stress, ultimately triggering premature apoptosis of chondrocytes.88 Moreover, the absence of functional COMP in the ECM disrupts its structure and function, thereby compromising the chondrocyte microenvironment. The combined effect of accelerated chondrocyte death and defective ECM leads to severe impairment of skeletal growth and deformity.89

Hypophosphatasia

Hypophosphatasia (HPP) is a rare inherited metabolic bone disorder caused by mutations in the ALPL gene, leading to a deficiency in tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP). This impairs the hydrolysis of inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi), resulting in defective mineralization of bones and teeth (Table 1).90 Extracellular inorganic pyrophosphate is a potent inhibitor of mineralization as it binds to calcium ions and competitively inhibits the formation and growth of hydroxyapatite crystals.91 ALPL catalyzes the degradation of inorganic pyrophosphate. In addition to hydrolyzing PPi to generate phosphate (Pi), members of the alkaline phosphatase family and related ectoenzymes, such as PC-1, can cleave other phosphorus-containing substrates in the bone microenvironment, including nucleoside di- and monophosphates. The resulting Pi, together with calcium ions, contributes to the local supersaturation necessary for hydroxyapatite crystal formation, which occurs in close association with matrix vesicles and collagen fibrils.92

X-linked hypophosphatemia

X-linked hypophosphatemia is caused by a dominant mutation in the PHEX gene on the X chromosome (Table 1). Osteopontin(OPN) has been identified as a substrate of PHEX in mineralizing tissues, as PHEX can cleave the OPN ASARM peptide in vitro. The OPN ASARM peptide is a phosphorylated acidic motif derived from osteopontin, a member of the SIBLING family of mineral-regulating proteins. Phosphorylation is critical for its function; phosphorylated forms bind strongly to hydroxyapatite and potently inhibit bone matrix mineralization, whereas non-phosphorylated forms lack this activity. PHEX directly cleaves the phosphorylated OPN ASARM peptide at specific amide linkages, reversing its inhibition of mineralization. PHEX mutations impede the effective degradation of this substrate.93,94 Concurrently, PHEX deficiency leads to the overproduction of fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) by osteocytes, resulting in significantly elevated blood FGF23 levels.94 Excess FGF23 binds to its receptor and the co-receptor α-Klotho. This drastically reduces phosphate reabsorption. It also inhibits 1α-hydroxylase (responsible for activating vitamin D) while promoting 24-hydroxylase (which degrades active vitamin D) to decrease the level of active vitamin D. These two effects synergistically cause persistent hypophosphatemia, leading to impaired skeletal mineralization.95,96

Therapeutic implications and druggable targets

Understanding of the molecular and biomechanical mechanisms of hypertrophic chondrocyte mineralization has laid the foundation for developing targeted therapies for skeletal disorders. Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) has achieved remarkable results in treating genetic diseases caused by enzyme deficiemcies. For example, asfotase alfa, a recombinant human tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP), has been approved for treating hypophosphatasia.90 Clinical studies have demonstrated that asfotase alfa treatment markedly improves survival and respiratory outcomes in infants with life-threatening hypophosphatasia. In a pivotal trial, 1-year survival rate among treated patients was 95%, compared to only 42% in historical controls. Furthermore, ventilator-free survival was achieved in 84–100% of patients, with many weaning off mechanical ventilation as early as 48 weeks after the initiation of treatment. Long-term follow-up (up to 7 years) has shown sustained improvements in skeletal mineralization, motor function, and quality of life, with most patients maintaining ambulation and no longer requiring respiratory support.97,98 For X-linked hypophosphatemia, Burosumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting FGF23, was developed to improve bone mineralization.99 For achondroplasia caused by gain-of-function mutation of FGFR3, vosoritide (a C-type natriuretic peptide analogue) can antagonize FGFR3 signaling to promote chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation, thereby improving endochondral bone growth.77 In addition, small molecule inhibitors selectively targeting BMP6 (and potentially BMP2) signaling, such as cryptotanshinone, are being developed for the treatment of ectopic mineralization diseases such as fibrodysplasia ossificans progressive (FOP).100

Gene therapy also shows significant potential. Preclinical studies have shown that CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing has been successfully used to correct collagen mutations in osteoblasts from osteogenesis imperfecta101 and to downregulate FGFR3 expression in chondrocytes in achondroplasia.102 Additionally, gene delivery systems, including adeno-associated virus (AAV) and non-viral mRNA vectors, can be used to overexpress key regulatory factors, such as Runx2 and VEGF, promoting bone repair and regeneration in bone defects and fracture healing.103 Therefore, advanced therapeutic strategies are promising for the treatment of these diseases.

Conclusion

Hypertrophic chondrocytes mineralization is a complex and precise process regulated by the interaction between molecular signals and biomechanical factors. It not only provides the initial mechanical support for skeletal development but also participates in bone tissue repair and regeneration. Dysregulation of this process leads to various skeletal disorders characterized by abnormal biomechanical properties. The integration of molecular biology, biomechanics, and clinical medicine has promoted the development of therapeutic strategies such as enzyme replacement therapy, targeted drug therapy, and gene therapy. However, many questions remain unanswered in this field, and future research is needed to further explore different physiological and pathological mechanisms. This will not only deepen our understanding of bone biology but also provide new ways for the treatment of skeletal disorders in the future.
