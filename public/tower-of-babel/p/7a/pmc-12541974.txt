Risk factors for experiencing substance use-related employment stigma among emergency department patients at high risk of opioid overdose
Authors: Ralph Welwean, Laura Chambers, Brandon Marshall, Francesca Beaudoin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople who use drugs report hesitance to seek employment because of stigma around drug use, which other forms of stigma may compound. We evaluated risk factors for substance use-related employment stigma among emergency department (ED) patients who use drugs.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study among ED patients at high risk of opioid overdose in Rhode Island. The outcomes were three self-reported measures of substance use-related employment stigma. Multivariable log-binomial regression was used to estimate the association between participant characteristics and each outcome.ResultsAmong 648 participants, 25.8% reported they had been turned down for a job due to current/past drug use, 40.8% disagreed that most employers will hire someone treated for drug use if qualified, and 77.7% agreed that most employers will pass over applicants treated for drug use in favor of others. Females reported they had been turned down for a job due to drug use less often than males (adjusted prevalence ratio [PR] 0.72, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.53–0.98). Persons with a history of homelessness (aPR 1.82, 95% CI 1.24–2.66) and addiction treatment (aPR 1.95, 95% CI 1.22–3.12) more often reported having ever been turned down for a job due to drug use. Race/ethnicity was not associated with substance use-related employment stigma.DiscussionPerceived substance use-related employment stigma was common among ED patients who use drugs, and men and those with a history of homelessness or addiction treatment may be particularly affected. Employers can diminish the harms of stigmatization by acknowledging those who struggle with addiction and changing hiring practices to reduce stigma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12954-025-01289-7.Keywords: Stigma, Hiring practices, Substance use disorder, People who use drugs, Addiction treatment

Introduction

People who use drugs (PWUD) report reluctance to seek employment because of stigma related to past or current drug use [5, 7], despite personal and economic benefits [5, 21]. Stigma is a set of negative beliefs that a group of people have about others due to their circumstances or personal characteristics; these beliefs are often deeply discrediting, unfair, and convey devalued stereotypes [2, 25]. In contrast, discrimination is an action taken when these stigmatizing attitudes or beliefs lead to unfair treatment, exclusion, or denial of rights and opportunities. Thus, stigma may lead to discrimination, but not always. This study focuses on evaluating perceptions of substance use-related stigma in the workplace setting among emergency department patients.

Many PWUD also occupy other stigmatized statuses, such as those related to race/ethnicity, gender, age, education, employment, homelessness, incarceration history, and addiction treatment history. Multiple overlapping stigmas can compound (i.e., intersectional stigma) and contribute to PWUD feeling ostracized from everyday life activities and fearful that they will not be accepted or treated fairly when seeking employment [13, 22, 23, 25]. Intersectional stigma can increase PWUD vulnerability to experiencing drug-related emergency department (ED) visits [4, 7]. EDs are often the main location where PWUD access health care [18] and so are an important setting for linkage to addiction treatment and other services.

However, little is known about perceived substance use-related stigma in employer hiring practices (i.e., when seeking employment) among ED patients who use drugs. Most prior studies focused on employers’ opinions about employee drug use, the ability of employees in recovery to maintain employment, and employee reluctance to seek addiction treatment due to fear of retaliation if their supervisors learn they are in recovery [3, 5–8, 10, 11, 14, 17, 21]. Employers have reported low willingness to hire people previously in addiction treatment [5, 21], often based on false assumptions that they would be less productive and prone to relapse [3, 5]. To better understand the role of intersectional stigma in creating barriers to employment, we evaluated risk factors for perceived substance use-related employment stigma in employer hiring practices among ED patients at high risk of opioid overdose. We hypothesized that participants identifying as a racial/ethnic minority or female would more often report substance use-related employment stigma, consistent with the prior literature [9, 15]; Weller [24].

Methods

Study design and sample

This was a cross-sectional, secondary analysis of baseline data from the Navigator Trial (NCT03684681), a randomized controlled trial of an ED behavioral intervention delivered by a certified peer recovery support specialist compared to one delivered by an ED licensed clinical social worker for ED patients at high risk of opioid overdose (described previously [4, 12]). Participants were randomly assigned 1:1 to an intervention arm using permuted block sizes and stratification by study site, age, and sex. Briefly, adults attending two Rhode Island EDs from November 2017 to May 2021 were eligible if they were being treated for an opioid overdose or opioid use disorder or had an opioid overdose within the past year. Participants completed a baseline survey and received $40 for their time enrolling. The trial was approved by the study site’s institutional review board. Participants provided informed consent.

Measures

Outcomes

The study outcomes were participant responses to three questions from the Navigator Trial baseline survey regarding substance use-related employment stigma, including whether: most employers will hire someone who has been treated for substance use if he or she is qualified for the job (strongly disagree/disagree versus strongly agree/agree, outcome 1), most employers will pass over the application of someone who has been treated for substance use in favor of another applicant (strongly disagree/disagree versus strongly agree/agree, outcome 2), and have you ever been turned down for a job because of current or past drug use (yes vs. no, outcome 3). Outcomes 1 and 2 were individual items from the Perceived Stigma of Substance Abuse Scale, a validated instrument [19]. We dichotomized the 4-item Likert scale responses for this analysis due to our interest in factors associated with any level of agreement and to facilitate direct estimation of prevalence ratios, which are more interpretable for public health and policy audiences than odds ratios when the outcomes are common.

Independent variables

The independent variables of interest were selected based on their hypothesized influence on perceptions of substance use-related employment stigma. They included: (1) race/ethnicity, (2) biological sex at birth, (3) age, (4) highest level of education, (5) employment status, (6) lifetime history of homelessness, (7) life time history of detention or incarceration in an adult jail, and (8) lifetime history of alcohol or drug treatment.

Statistical analyses

Bivariate log-binomial regression was used to estimate the association between each independent variable and each study outcome. Subsequently, a multivariable log-binomial regression model was constructed for each outcome, including race/ethnicity, sex assigned at birth, and any other independent variables associated with the outcome in bivariate analyses. Log-binomial regression was used due to the high prevalence of the outcomes (> 10%). Race/ethnicity and sex assigned at birth were automatically included in the multivariable models due to the hypothesized associations between these characteristics and the outcomes. Finally, to explore potential variation in the risk factors for substance use-related employment based on current employment status, we conducted a sensitivity analysis stratified by employment status.

For each study outcome, analyses excluded participants with unknown/missing data on that outcome. Characteristics of participants with and without missing data for each outcome were compared using chi-square and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4.

Results

Sample characteristics

In total, 648 ED patients at high risk of opioid overdose enrolled in the trial (Table 1). Of those participants, 83 (12.8%) were eligible for the trial due to a prior overdose in the past 12 months, while the remaining 565 (87.2%) were eligible due to opioid-related treatment in the ED, including overdose and OUD complications. Overall, 420 (64.8%) participants were White (non-Hispanic), 38 (5.9%) were African American/Black (non-Hispanic), 107 (16.5%) were Hispanic (any race), and 50 (7.7%) were Other (non-Hispanic) race/ethnicity. Most participants were male (n = 439, 67.8%), and mean age was 36.9 years. Additionally, 35.7% (n = 231) reported having completed high school or obtained a GED as their highest level of education, while 27.9% (n = 181) were currently employed. Most participants had been homeless (n = 446, 68.8%), detained or incarcerated in an adult jail (n = 444, 68.5%), and in alcohol or drug treatment (n = 497, 76.7%) in their lifetime.

Participants with missing substance use-related employment stigma outcome data were excluded from analyses for that specific outcome. Of 648 total participants, 119 (18.4%) were missing information on whether most employers will hire someone who has been treated for substance use if he or she is qualified for the job (Outcome 1), 120 (18.5%) were missing information on whether most employers will pass over the application of someone who has been treated for substance use in favor of another (Outcome 2), and 31 (4.8%) were missing information on whether they had ever been turned down for a job because of current or past drug use (Outcome 3). Participants with and without data for each outcome had some differences (Supplemental Tables S1-S3). Notably, participants without outcome data were more likely than those with outcome data to have missing data for other measures.

Substance use-related employment stigma

Overall, 216 of 529 participants with outcome 1 data (40.8%) strongly disagreed/disagreed that most employers will hire someone treated for substance use if qualified for the job. Additionally, 410 of 528 participants with outcome 2 data (77.7%) strongly agreed/agreed that most employers will pass over applicants treated for substance use in favor of others. Finally, 159 of 617 participants with outcome 3 data (25.8%) reported having ever been turned down for a job because of current/past drug use.

In adjusted models, race/ethnicity and sex were not associated with the three substance use-related employment stigma outcomes, with one exception (Table 2). Females reported having ever been turned down for a job because of current/past drug use less often than males (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] 0.72, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.53–0.98; p-value = 0.0344). Compared to participants who had not completed high school, those who had completed some college or trade school more often strongly agreed/agreed that most employers will pass over applicants treated for substance use in favor of others (aPR 1.14, 95% CI 1.02–1.26; p-value = 0.0177). Additionally, participants who had ever been homeless (aPR 1.82, 95% CI 1.24–2.66; p-value = 0.0021) and ever been in alcohol or drug treatment (aPR 1.95, 95% CI 1.22–3.12; p-value = 0.0055) more often reported having ever been turned down for a job because of current/past drug use (Table 2).

In sensitivity analyses stratified by employment status, some findings differed (Supplemental Tables S4–S5). Among unemployed participants, the results were similar to the primary analysis, except that sex was not significantly associated with having been turned down for a job due to current/past drug use. Among employed participants, only one characteristic was significantly associated with any study outcome: compared to White (non-Hispanic) participants, those reporting “Other” (non-Hispanic) race/ethnicity (includes American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander, multiple, and self-defined race) more often strongly disagreed/disagreed that most employers will hire someone treated for drug use if qualified.

Discussion

Among ED patients at high risk of opioid overdose, the prevalence of perceived substance use-related employment stigma in employer hiring practices was high, particularly beliefs that most employers will not hire someone treated for substance use if qualified (40.8%) and will pass over applicants treated for substance use in favor of others (77.7%). Additionally, 25.8% of participants reported having been turned down for a job due to current/past drug use. These findings are consistent with prior studies suggesting that employers are reluctant to hire people with a history of drug use or addiction treatment [5, 8].

In the primary analysis, there was no evidence of an association between race/ethnicity and the three substance use-related employment stigma outcomes, consistent with a previous study that found no racial/ethnic disparities in employment in the year following addiction treatment admission [1]. However, among employed participants, those of Other (non-Hispanic) race/ethnicity were more likely than White (non-Hispanic) participants to believe that most employers will not hire someone treated for substance use if qualified. It may be the case that people who experience a rejection are more likely to attribute it to their drug use rather than their race/ethnicity. Although sex was not associated with beliefs about employer hiring behaviors, in the primary analysis, males more often reported having been turned down for a job because of current/past substance use. This association was not observed in analyses stratified by employment status, although that may reflect reduced study power. Males may be more likely to anticipate substance use-related stigma when they apply for jobs and, thus, more often attribute job rejection to drug use [16]. Employers may also be more likely to turn down males for jobs because they view drug use history (and its possible influence on job performance) differently for males and females. For example, males are more likely to work in physically demanding jobs, and employers may view males with a history of extensive drug use as less capable of performing such jobs effectively.

Some other participant characteristics were associated with perceived substance use-related employment stigma. Compared to participants who had not completed high school, those who had completed some college or trade school more often believed that employers are likely to pass over applicants treated for substance use in favor of others. The jobs available to those who have not completed high school may have more accommodating hiring practices if applicants can meet the physical demands of the job [20]. Additionally, participants who had ever been homeless and ever received addiction treatment were more likely to have been turned down for a job because of current/past drug use. Efforts to address existing substance use-related employment stigma are needed because employment can help individuals gain economic mobility, obtain stable housing, and maintain recovery [3, 5, 7]. Although these associations were not observed among employed participants, this may reflect reduced study power in this small subgroup.

This study had limitations. First, the study sample included ED patients enrolled in a randomized trial of an ED behavioral intervention in Rhode Island [4, 12], potentially limiting generalizability to other settings. Second, the small sample size and limited racial/ethnic diversity of the sample may have limited our power to detect racial/ethnic disparities. It is possible that the association between race/ethnicity and each outcome could vary depending on the participant’s sex and/or treatment history. Similarly, the association between sex and each outcome could vary depending on treatment history. Unfortunately, our power to test for interactions is very limited based on our sample size. Third, the survey only captured substance use-related employment stigma in the context of the hiring process. Some subgroups may be more or less likely to experience other unmeasured forms of substance use-related employment stigma. Fourth, participants’ recent substance use patterns and eligibility for substance use disorder treatment may have influenced their perceptions of substance use-related employment stigma, but this information was not available in the analysis dataset. Finally, because study staff prioritized the participants’ clinical care in the ED over study survey completion, many participants (5–19%) had missing data on stigma measures and were excluded from the respective analyses. Missing data may have biased study findings if there were important differences between participants with versus without missing data, although our comparisons did not suggest major differences.

Despite these limitations, this study identified key subgroups of ED patients at high risk of opioid overdose who more often reported substance use-related employment stigma in employer hiring practices, including males, those of Other (non-Hispanic) race/ethnicity, and those who had completed some college or trade school, ever been homeless, and ever received addiction treatment. Employers (e.g., leadership, human resources professionals) can use these findings to improve employee attitudes towards applicants who use or have used drugs, inform changes to hiring practices that reduce stigma, and create a more inclusive work environment. Additionally, ED providers can consider patients’ experiences with substance use-related employment stigma when identifying their health and social needs in the ED.
