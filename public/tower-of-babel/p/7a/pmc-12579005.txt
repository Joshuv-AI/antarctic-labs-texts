Irisin inhibits dopaminergic neuron lactate metabolism and repairs mitochondrial function to alleviate Parkinson’s disease by activating SIRT1 signaling pathway
Authors: Lijun Cai, Yin Liu, Shuang Tang, Song Deng, Li Zhang, Xin Liao, Bei Zhang, Bing Han, Rujia Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIrisin, an exercise-induced myokine with promising therapeutic potential, exhibits neuroprotective effects in Parkinson’s disease (PD). However, its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study investigated the role of irisin in an MPTP-induced mouse model of PD. Irisin treatment improved motor function, as evidenced by enhanced performance on the rotarod test, and promoted dopaminergic neuron survival, demonstrated by increased TH-positive cell counts and reduced α-synuclein accumulation. Additionally, irisin inhibited lactate metabolism in the substantia nigra by decreasing lactate and pyruvate levels and downregulating key glycolytic enzymes. Mitochondrial function was restored through reductions in oxidative stress markers and improvements in ATP synthesis and mitochondrial morphology. Furthermore, irisin activated the SIRT1 signaling pathway, leading to the deacetylation of HIF-1α and PGC-1α, which suppressed apoptosis and enhanced cell viability in MPP + -treated SH-SY5Y cells. Irisin also attenuated neuroinflammation by reducing microglial activation and protecting neurons from microglia-induced apoptosis. These findings demonstrate that irisin confers neuroprotection in PD through multiple mechanisms, including the regulation of motor function, dopaminergic neuron survival, mitochondrial function, and neuroinflammation. Activation of the SIRT1 pathway appears to be a core therapeutic target for these effects of irisin. Subject terms: Neuroscience, Neurological disorders

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder globally, with its incidence steadily increasing as the global population ages1. The pathophysiology of PD is characterized by α-synuclein accumulation and the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra2,3. PD risk factors include aging, genetic mutations, and environmental toxins4.

Evidence underscores the role of abnormal lactate metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction in PD pathogenesis. For example, lactate concentrations are elevated in the striatum of PD animal models and levodopa-induced dyskinesia models5,6. The expression of HK2 and LDHA and the lactate levels were markedly increased in the SNpc of MPTP-treated mice and in MPP + -treated SH-SY5Y cells. Exogenous lactate treatment led to the apoptosis of SH-SY5Y cells7. Moreover, stereotaxic lactate injection into the third ventricle induces PD-like phenotypes, including motor impairments, in murine models8. These findings suggest that elevated lactate levels are linked to PD pathophysiology. Mitochondria, as the primary energy source, are critical in processes like oxidative phosphorylation, lipid metabolism, and cell death. Dysregulated mitochondrial function, including increased reactive oxygen species production, disrupted intracellular calcium signaling, and impaired ATP synthesis, contributes to PD pathogenesis9–11.

Current therapeutic strategies for PD, including dopaminergic pharmacotherapy and surgical interventions like deep brain stimulation, focus on symptom alleviation. However, their long-term efficacy remains limited, with a lack of disease-modifying effects12. In contrast, exercise has become recognized as an effective intervention for managing neurodegenerative diseases, including PD, and is now routinely endorsed in clinical settings13. Recently, irisin, a muscle-derived protein produced during physical activity, has garnered attention for its potential therapeutic role.

Irisin is produced by cleaving the C-terminal domain of the precursor protein FNDC514. Once secreted into the circulatory system, irisin binds to the αVβ5 integrin receptor on cells, exerting diverse physiological effects. Previous studies show that irisin can mitigate α-synuclein-induced neurodegeneration in the preformed fibril (PFF) mouse model of sporadic PD15. Notably, serum levels of irisin and α-synuclein are negatively correlated, as determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)16. Additionally, positron emission tomography (PET) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) reveal that irisin enhances DOPA uptake in the striatum of PD patients. Recent research suggests that irisin exerts a neuroprotective effect by attenuating mitochondrial dysfunction in PD17. Despite these findings, the precise mechanism of the role of irisin in PD pathophysiology remains unclear, warranting further investigation.

In PD models, no studies to date have explored the potential of irisin to modulate lactate metabolism within dopaminergic neurons. Intriguingly, recent investigations have demonstrated that irisin upregulates the expression of TFAM (mitochondrial transcription factor A) through the activation of the SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling pathway, thereby enhancing mitochondrial metabolism and mitigating microglial activation18. Simultaneously, another PD-related study has revealed that Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a NAD + -dependent protein deacetylase, plays a critical role in maintaining lactate homeostasis in the brain by inhibiting PKM2 through deacetylation8. These observations suggest that irisin may potentially modulate lactate metabolism and mitochondrial function in dopaminergic neurons via the SIRT1 pathway.

In this study, we examined the effects of exogenous irisin on motor function and dopaminergic neurodegeneration in MPTP-treated mice. We also showed that irisin mitigates mitochondrial damage by downregulating lactate metabolism via the SIRT1 pathway. These findings support the therapeutic potential of irisin in PD and provide new insights into its mechanisms of action.

Materials and methods

Animal model and tissue preparation

Male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Company. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the ethics committee for experimental animal use at The Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (No.2200545). Male mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 20–30 g, 6–10 per group) received daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of MPTP (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, M0896; 30 mg/kg body weight), irisin (R&D, China, 8880-IR; 200 μg/kg body weight), or vehicle treatment. A detailed treatment regimen is shown in Fig. 1A. Following behavioral assessments and euthanasia, brain tissues were promptly harvested, dehydrated using a sucrose gradient, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde before being transferred to optimal cutting temperature compound for long-term storage at −80 °C.

Pole test and rotarod test

Behavioral tests were conducted 10 days following the final MPTP injection. Mice were trained on both the rotarod and pole tests 2 days prior to the assessments. In the rotarod test, the apparatus was set to a speed of 20 RPM. Mice exhibiting impaired motor coordination typically fell off quickly, and the endurance time for each animal was recorded. For the pole test, a vertical pole (50 cm in length and 1 cm in diameter) with a cork placed at the top was used. Mice were positioned on the cork, and the time taken for each mouse to descend from the cork to the bottom of the pole was measured.

Cell culture and drug treatment

SH-SY5Y cells were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Primary microglia and astrocytes were isolated from postnatal day 1 SD rat pups. Briefly, the meninges-free cortices were collected and digested with trypsin. The resulting cell suspension was cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced every 2 days. After 10–12 days, the cells were subjected to shaking at 200 rpm for 2 h. Microglia were harvested from the floating cells after centrifugation. For astrocytes, the cells were shaken at 250 rpm for 12–16 h, and the remaining attached cells were collected as astrocytes.

1-Methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+, Sigma-Aldrich, USA, D048) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, USA, D2650) to a final concentration of 0.01%. Nicotinamide (NAM), a known inhibitor of SIRT1, and SR-18292, a selective inhibitor of PGC-1α activity. Cells were treated with MPP+/Irisin/Niacinamide/SR-18292 at the indicated concentrations and time points. The treatment conditions were as follows: MPP+ (1 μM, 24 h), irisin (200 ng/mL, 24 h), NAM (1 μM, 24 h), and SR-18292 (10 μM, 24 h). After being digested and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), SH-SY5Y or microglia cells were further analyzed.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) at 4 °C and sectioned at 12-μm thickness using a vibratome. The sections were blocked with QuickBlock buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. P0260) for 1 h at room temperature. Following three washes in PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20), the sections were incubated with an anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (anti-TH) antibody, followed by a biotin-conjugated secondary antibody (Zsbio, Beijing, China; Cat. No. SP-9000) and ABC reagents (Zsbio, Beijing, China; Cat. No. PK-4001). Color development was performed using the Fast DAB Peroxidase Substrate (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. D0426). For immunofluorescence, 12-μm-thick serial brain sections were used. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-Nissl, anti-TH, anti-Iba-1, anti-GFAP, anti-SIRT1, anti-LDHA, and anti-UCP2. The sections were then washed three times with PBST and incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Following the removal of the secondary antibodies and additional washes, the sections were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager M2). The total number of TH-positive cells was quantified using an unbiased stereology method as described previously. Iba-1- and GFAP-positive cells were analyzed using ImageJ software. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) morphological analysis, freshly collected cell pellets were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in FBS at 4 °C for 12 h. After three washes with phosphate buffer, the samples were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series at 4 °C. The dehydrated samples were then embedded in epoxy resin and polymerized at 60 °C for 2 h. Ultrathin sections (70-nm thick) were mounted onto copper grids and examined using a Hitachi electron microscope (Japan).

Western blot analysis

Cell lysates were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, IN, USA). Western blot analysis was performed to detect target proteins, using the appropriate primary antibodies followed by probing with corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated to the appropriate labels. The reactive bands were visualized using ECL Plus reagents (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and the relative intensities of the bands were quantified with ImageJ 1.46r software. The blots were incubated with the following primary antibodies: GAPDH (1:10,000, Proteintech, 60,004–1-Ig), HK2 (1:1000, Abcam, ab209847), TH (1:5000, Abcam, ab137869), LDHA (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 3582S), Bax (1:1000, Abcam, ab32503), Bcl2 (1:1000, Abcam, ab32124), AMPKα1/2 (H − 300) (1:500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-25,792), phospho-AMPKα1/2 (Thr 183/172) (1:500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-101,630), phospho-Akt (Thr308) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4056S), Akt (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 9272S), phospho-mTOR (Ser2448) (1:1,000, Cell Signaling Technology, 5336S), mTOR (7C10) (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 2983S), α-Synuclein (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4179S), BDNF (1:1000, Abcam, ab108319), PGC-1α (1:100, Abcam, ab191838), PDK1 (1:2000, Abcam, ab202468), PKM2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4053), GPX4 (1:1000, Abcam, ab125066), UCP2 (1:1000, Abcam, 89326), ATP5A (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 18023), UQCRC2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, 99258), MTCO1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab14705), SIRT1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab110304), HIF-1α (1:1000, Abcam, ab308433), NRF1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab175932), and NRF2 (1:1000, Abcam, ab207223).

Quantitative PCR

RNA was extracted from brain or cell samples using TRIzol reagent, and its concentration was determined using a Nanodrop 2000 UV–vis spectrophotometer. Two micrograms of RNA from each sample were reverse transcribed using MuLV reverse transcriptase and oligo(dT) primers. SYBR Green Premix was employed for real-time PCR amplification. The amplification conditions were set as follows: 95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, for a total of 40 cycles. The mRNA expression levels of all target genes were normalized to GAPDH using the 2-ΔΔCt method19. The qPCR primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

ATP assay

SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in 12-well plates and treated with the indicated supplements for the specified duration. After incubation, the medium was removed, and 100 μL of cell lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to lyse the cells. The culture plate was gently pipetted or shaken to ensure thorough contact between the lysate and the cells. Following lysis, the cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. To measure ATP levels, 100 μL of ATP detection working solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 5 min to consume background ATP, thereby reducing interference. After this incubation, 20 μL of the sample or standard was added to each well, and the relative luminescence unit (RLU) value was measured using an ELX800 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) after at least 2 s.

Pyruvate and lactate assay

Pyruvate and lactate levels were measured using a pyruvate assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. MAK071) and a lactate assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. MAK064), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Pyruvate kinase activity and LDH assay

Pyruvate kinase activity and lactate dehydrogenase activity were measured using a pyruvate kinase activity assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. MAK072) and a lactate dehydrogenase activity assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. MAK464), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell transfection

A plasmid for HIF-1α overexpression was constructed by cloning human HIF-1α cDNA into the pcDNA3.1 plasmid (designated pcDNA-HIF-1α), with the empty pcDNA3.1 plasmid serving as the vector control. Transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) by the manufacturer’s protocol.

4-HNE, MDA, GSH, and GSSG assay

Midbrain tissues from mice with PBS perfusion were homogenized in ice-cold lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, and a protease inhibitor mixture. The homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. 4-HNE levels were measured using a commercial assay kit (Sangon Biotech, Cat. No. D751041), following the manufacturer’s instructions. MDA, GSH, and GSSG levels were quantified in the supernatant using respective assay kits20. MDA determination was based on the reaction between MDA and thiobarbituric acid (TBA), producing a red product with an absorption maximum of 535 nm. GSH was measured by its reaction with the chromogenic substrate DTNB, resulting in a yellow TNB product with an absorption maximum of 412 nm. Absorbance at 535 and 412 nm was measured for MDA and GSH, respectively. For GSSG determination, GSH was first removed from the sample, and GSSG was subsequently reduced to GSH by glutathione reductase. The absorption maximum at 412 nm was recorded for GSSG using the DTNB reaction.

SOD activity

SOD activity was measured using a commercial kit (DOJINDO, China, S311). For the assay, 20 μL of standards, ultra-pure water, or sample solution were added to the standard, control, or sample wells, respectively. Then, 200 μL of WST working solution was added to each well, followed by 20 μL of enzyme working solution. The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and SOD activity was determined using a calibration curve.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in a 96-well plate, and 10 μL of CCK-8 working solution was added to each well. After incubating for 2 h in a cell incubator, the absorbance at 405 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Synergy H1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise specified. Group comparisons were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For non-parametric data, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used. Significance was set at p < 0.05. To ensure adequate statistical power, a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1) based on previously observed effect sizes from pilot studies and similar experimental models in the literature. Based on an expected medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.4), α = 0.05, and desired power = 0.8, a minimum of n = 6–8 animals per group was determined to be sufficient to detect significant differences in key neurochemical and behavioral endpoints. Where applicable, sample sizes were increased beyond the calculated number to account for potential variability in response to MPTP administration or surgical procedures. All experiments were independently replicated at least three times, and efforts were made to randomize treatment groups and blind experimenters to group allocation during data collection and analysis.

Results

Irisin improves motor function and dopaminergic neurodegeneration in MPTP-treated mice

To investigate the role of irisin in PD, we utilized MPTP-treated mice to establish a PD model that replicates motor dysfunction and pathological damage. Following MPTP treatment, irisin was administered to examine its effects on motor function and neural damage in the mice (Fig. 1A). The results revealed that, compared to the MPTP-treated group alone, irisin significantly reduced the total time in the rod test and increased the residence time on the rotating rod, suggesting that irisin improves MPTP-induced motor balance dysfunction (Fig. 1B, C). In conjunction with the behavioral tests, we assessed the number of dopaminergic neurons (TH) in the substantia nigra pars compacta and striatum of the midbrain using immunofluorescence. Irisin treatment significantly enhanced the number of TH-positive cells (Fig. 1D–G) and mitigated MPTP-induced damage to striatal TH-positive fibers. Moreover, irisin treatment reduced MPTP-induced α-synuclein (α-Syn) protein levels in the midbrain, while increasing the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) proteins (Fig. 1H, I). Immunohistochemical analysis corroborated these findings (Fig. 1J–L). These results suggest that irisin exerts a neuroprotective effect in PD mice, significantly enhancing motor function by promoting the survival and function of dopaminergic neurons.

Irisin inhibits lactate metabolism in MPTP-treated mice

Previous studies have demonstrated a significant increase in lactate metabolism in MPTP-induced PD mouse models8. This study aims to further elucidate the relationship between lactate metabolism and PD and to investigate the potential regulatory effects of irisin on lactate metabolism. We analyzed glycolysis and lactate synthesis in the substantia nigra tissue of MPTP-treated mice and observed a significant increase in lactate and pyruvate levels (Fig. 2A, C), accompanied by enhanced enzyme activity of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and pyruvate kinase (PK) (Fig. 2B, D). However, irisin intervention significantly reduced the synthesis of lactate and pyruvate. To further investigate the effects of irisin on lactate synthesis, we assessed the expression levels of several key enzymes involved in the process. The results demonstrated a significant downregulation of HK2, LDHA, pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) (Fig. 2E, F). Next, we examined the localization of LDHA in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) using immunofluorescence co-staining and conducted colocalization analysis with markers for dopaminergic neurons, microglia, and astrocytes. LDHA was predominantly expressed in dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 2G–I). Furthermore, irisin treatment reduced LDHA expression in dopaminergic neurons compared to MPTP-treated mice (Fig. 2H). These findings suggest that irisin alleviates MPTP-induced lactate metabolism in dopaminergic neurons, providing potential insights into its neuroprotective effects in PD.

Irisin restores mitochondrial dysfunction in MPTP-treated mice

Mitochondrial dysfunction has been confirmed to be one of the key factors in the development of PD[9]. To investigate the effects of irisin on oxidative stress and mitochondrial function under MPTP treatment, we first measured the levels of lipid peroxidation products, malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) (Fig. 3A, B). The results demonstrated that irisin treatment significantly reduced the levels of both products. Furthermore, irisin effectively restored ATP synthesis, which was reduced by MPTP treatment, to near-normal levels (Fig. 3C). Compared with the MPTP-treated group, irisin restored the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), which had been impaired by MPTP (Fig. 3D). Additionally, irisin significantly increased the glutathione (GSH) level, enhanced the GSH-to-GSSG ratio and decreased the oxidized glutathione (GSSG) content (Fig. 3E–G). Assessment of mitochondrial quantity using cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COX IV) indicated that irisin markedly improved mitochondrial integrity in dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 3H, I). Immunofluorescence co-staining revealed that uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) was localized in dopaminergic neurons. Compared with the MPTP group, irisin treatment significantly increased the colocalization intensity of UCP2 within dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 3J, K). Additionally, irisin treatment upregulated both the transcription and protein expression levels of UCP2. The transcription and expression of the antioxidant protein glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), as well as mitochondrial function-associated proteins ATP synthase subunit alpha (ATP5A), ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase core protein 2 (UQCRC2), and cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (MTCO1), were also restored following irisin treatment (Fig. 3L, M). In summary, irisin enhances the antioxidant capacity of dopaminergic neurons and ameliorates mitochondrial dysfunction induced by MPTP treatment.

Irisin activates the SIRT1 signaling pathway to inhibit MPP+-induced SH-SY5Y apoptosis

A substantial body of research has confirmed that SIRT1 plays a pivotal role in regulating lactate metabolism and various biological processes involved in mitochondrial antioxidant stress3,8. First, we investigated the expression changes of histone deacetylase SIRT1 in the substantia nigra of MPTP-induced PD mice. Immunohistochemistry revealed that irisin significantly increased SIRT1 expression compared with MPTP treatment alone (Fig. 4A, B). Immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated that MPTP treatment markedly reduced the fluorescence intensity of SIRT1 and its colocalization with tyrosine hydroxylase-positive (TH+) cells. However, irisin not only enhanced SIRT1 fluorescence intensity but also increased its colocalization with TH+ cells (Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, quantitative protein analysis confirmed significant upregulation of SIRT1 expression upon irisin treatment (Fig. 4C). In the MPP + -induced PD cell model (SH-SY5Y), SIRT1 expression was similarly upregulated (Fig. 4D, E). Additionally, irisin downregulated the downstream target protein hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) and upregulated peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α). However, these effects were reversed following the addition of the SIRT1 inhibitor NAM. To further evaluate irisin’s effect on apoptosis, we analyzed the expression of pro-apoptotic protein Bax and anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. Irisin treatment significantly decreased the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, while NAM treatment markedly increased it (Fig. 4F). TUNEL analysis revealed that irisin reduced MPP+-induced apoptosis, an effect reversed by NAM (Fig. 4G, H). Moreover, cell viability assays corroborated the flow cytometry results, showing that irisin alleviated MPP + -induced apoptosis and enhanced cell viability, effects that were negated by NAM (Fig. 4I). To verify whether the impact of Irisin on lactate metabolism was indeed achieved via the SIRT1 signaling pathway, we measured the mRNA expression levels as well as the activities of key enzymes closely related to lactate metabolism such as LDHA and HK2. Additionally, we assessed the levels of lactate and pyruvate and the activities of LDH and PK to comprehensively understand the specific impacts of Irisin on lactate metabolism under conditions of SIRT1 knockdown (Fig. 4J–L). Collectively, these findings suggest that irisin mitigates apoptosis and promotes cell viability by upregulating SIRT1 expression and modulating downstream targets, including HIF-1α and PGC-1α.

Irisin activates SIRT1 to deacetylate HIF-1α, reducing lactate metabolism and cell apoptosis

To explore how irisin regulates HIF-1α through SIRT1, we used co-immunoprecipitation to isolate HIF-1α and measured its acetylation status. The results demonstrated that irisin significantly reduced the acetylation of HIF-1α, while the addition of the SIRT1 inhibitor NAM reversed this deacetylation effect (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, key indicators of glycolysis and lactate metabolism were examined in SH-SY5Y cells under various treatment conditions. Irisin was found to decrease lactate and pyruvate levels and inhibit LDH activity (Fig. 5B–D). However, overexpression of HIF-1α markedly attenuated these effects of irisin. Further transcriptional analysis revealed that irisin significantly downregulated the mRNA and protein expression levels of lactate metabolism-related proteins, including LDHA, PKM2, and HK2 (Fig. 5E, F). Conversely, HIF-1α overexpression significantly upregulated the expression of these proteins. TUNEL analysis further showed that HIF-1α overexpression substantially promoted apoptosis in SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 5G, H). Additionally, irisin treatment inhibited the p-AMPK/AMPK signaling pathway while simultaneously activating the p-AKT/AKT and p-mTOR/mTOR signaling pathways (Fig. 5I–K). Collectively, these findings suggest that irisin exerts neuroprotective effects by inhibiting the function of HIF-1α through SIRT1-mediated deacetylation. This mechanism suppresses lactate metabolism and mitigates neuronal apoptosis, highlighting the potential therapeutic value of irisin in neurodegenerative diseases.

Irisin activates SIRT1 to deacetylate PGC-1α and improve mitochondrial function

To further investigate the biological effects of irisin in regulating PGC-1α through SIRT1, we employed co-immunoprecipitation to purify the SIRT1 downstream target protein, PGC-1α and assessed its acetylation levels. The results demonstrated that irisin significantly reduced the acetylation level of PGC-1α, and this deacetylation effect was reversed upon the addition of the SIRT1 inhibitor NAM (Fig. 6A). Consistent with in vivo experiments, irisin significantly reduced the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products, MDA and 4-HNE, in MPP + -induced SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 6B, C), and restored ATP synthesis levels (Fig. 6D). However, these effects were abolished upon the addition of the PGC-1α inhibitor SR-18292.To further investigate the role of irisin in mitochondrial function recovery, we used transmission electron microscopy to observe mitochondrial morphology under different treatment conditions. Under normal conditions, mitochondria exhibit an interconnected tubular network structure, a dense matrix, and ordered cristae (Fig. 6E–I). MPP+ treatment caused mitochondrial fragmentation, manifesting as short, independent tubular or swollen forms, accompanied by matrix rarefaction and cristae fragmentation. Irisin significantly ameliorated the abnormal morphology of these mitochondria, and the addition of SR-18292 restored mitochondrial morphology to a state similar to that observed in the MPP+ group. Protein level analysis demonstrated that irisin treatment significantly restored the expression of PGC-1α downstream target proteins, including UCP2, NRF1, and NRF2 (Fig. 6J, K). Furthermore, assessment of Bax and Bcl2 expression, along with the Bax/Bcl2 ratio, indicated that inhibition of PGC-1α by SR-18292 could reverse the anti-apoptotic effect of irisin on SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 6L). Specifically, we measured oxygen consumption rate (OCR) to assess mitochondrial respiration, including basal respiration, ATP production, and maximal respiratory capacity. ECAR was used to evaluate glycolytic activity. Our results show that Irisin significantly improves mitochondrial function and modulates metabolic reprogramming under pathological conditions (Fig. 6M–O). These findings collectively suggest that irisin enhances mitochondrial function and mitigates apoptosis via SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of PGC-1α.

Irisin reduces microglia-induced inflammatory responses

To investigate whether irisin mitigates microglial activation in MPTP-treated mice, we observed that microglia in the midbrain of MPTP-treated mice exhibited augmented cell size, along with increased branching and more complex morphological features compared to control mice (Fig. 7A, B). Quantitative analysis revealed a heightened density of Iba-1 immunostaining and an elevated number of Iba-1+ microglia in MPTP-treated mice (Fig. 7C–E), indicating robust microglial activation. In contrast, the morphology of microglia in irisin-treated MPTP mice resembled that of control mice, suggesting a reversal of activation. Furthermore, MPTP-treated mice exhibited significantly upregulated expression levels of iNOS, TNFα, and IL-1β compared to controls, whereas irisin treatment notably attenuated the expression of these pro-inflammatory markers (Fig. 7F–H). To further elucidate whether microglial activation contributes to neuronal apoptosis, we exposed SH-SY5Y neuronal cells to a conditioned medium derived from microglia treated with MPP+ or irisin (Fig. 7I). The results demonstrated that conditioned medium from MPP+-treated microglia substantially induced neuronal apoptosis and impaired cellular viability, whereas conditioned medium from irisin-treated microglia significantly reduced neuronal apoptosis and enhanced cell survival (Fig. 7J–L). We had isolated primary microglia and treated them with MPP+ alone or in combination with Irisin. After the treatment, we collected the microglia-conditioned media and performed cytokine profiling or ELISA assays to measure the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and iNOS. Just like in animal models, irisin treatment had notably attenuated the expression of these pro-inflammatory markers (Fig. 7M–O). These findings suggest that irisin may confer neuroprotection by modulating microglial activation and alleviating neuroinflammation (Fig. 7P). Discussion

In this study, we found that irisin can significantly alleviate dopaminergic neuronal damage in the MPTP- or MPP + -induced PD model. The underlying mechanism may involve irisin’s inhibition of lactate metabolism, improvement of mitochondrial function, promotion of ATP synthesis, and reduction of oxidative stress. Irisin also protects dopaminergic neurons through the SIRT1/HIF-1α/PGC-1α pathway. Overall, irisin holds promise as a potential therapeutic for PD.

We used MPTP to create a PD model in mice, a widely accepted method for modeling PD. The metabolite of MPTP, MPP + , induces mitochondrial dysfunction, increases oxidative stress, and leads to neuronal death, exhibiting PD-like pathological characteristics21. Irisin, a hormone secreted by muscle and adipose tissue, plays a critical role in regulating fat metabolism and converting white adipose tissue to brown adipose tissue22,23. Recently, irisin has attracted significant attention for its potential role in PD16,24, although the precise molecular mechanisms remain unclear. Our study found that irisin improved MPTP-induced motor deficits in PD mice. We also observed dopaminergic damage in the substantia nigra pars compacta and striatum, two key regions of the midbrain25. Analysis showed that irisin inhibited α-synuclein aggregation and enhanced the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1α (PGC-1α). Abnormal α-synuclein aggregation forms Lewy bodies, contributing to cell apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, which are hallmarks of PD26. Kam et al.15 suggested that irisin reduces the accumulation of pathological α-synuclein by enhancing its degradation through endolysosomal pathways. BDNF, a member of the neurotrophic factor family, plays a pivotal role in cell differentiation and synaptic plasticity, essential for central nervous system function27,28. In PD models, BDNF promotes the survival of dopaminergic neurons, enhances dopaminergic neurotransmission, and improves motor performance29. Furthermore, PGC-1α, a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, protects against PD symptoms30, as PGC-1α knockout mice are more susceptible to MPTP-induced damage31. Irisin has been shown to alleviate PD symptoms by increasing the expression of neuroprotective factors such as BDNF and PGC-1α17. These findings suggest that irisin may be a promising therapeutic strategy for PD and warrant further investigation.

While our data demonstrate that irisin reduces α-synuclein accumulation in the substantia nigra, the exact mechanisms underlying this effect remain to be fully elucidated. Emerging evidence suggests that irisin may influence protein clearance pathways such as autophagy and the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS). Notably, a study showed that irisin enhances autophagic flux in neuronal cells, leading to improved clearance of misfolded proteins32. In addition, Huo CJ et al. (2020) reported that irisin activates the Nrf2 pathway, which is known to regulate proteasomal function and oxidative stress responses, potentially contributing to reduced protein aggregation33. Given the central role of α-synuclein pathology in Parkinson’s disease, future studies should investigate whether irisin modulates autophagy or UPS activity in vivo, and whether these effects contribute directly to its neuroprotective properties.

While our study demonstrates that exogenous irisin administration confers neuroprotection in an MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease model, it is important to consider how the pharmacological doses used compare to physiological levels achieved through exercise. In both humans and rodents, acute bouts of endurance exercise typically elevate circulating irisin levels by 2–3-fold, with reported concentrations ranging from ~3 to 10 ng/mL in serum34. In contrast, the dose used in our study (10 µg/kg/day) likely results in supraphysiological concentrations during the treatment period, which may be necessary to achieve therapeutic effects in a disease state where endogenous irisin production or signaling could be impaired. This discrepancy highlights a key consideration for translational applications: while voluntary exercise naturally boosts irisin and may offer neuroprotective benefits, patients with neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s often experience mobility limitations that restrict sustained physical activity35. Therefore, pharmacological agents that mimic or enhance irisin signaling—such as stable irisin analogs or small-molecule agonists—may offer a viable alternative for maintaining therapeutic levels without relying solely on physical exercise. Future studies should aim to determine whether lower, more physiologically relevant doses of irisin or its mimetics can still confer neuroprotection, potentially offering safer and more sustainable long-term treatment options for patients.

The brain predominantly relies on glucose for ATP generation, and disruptions in energy metabolism pathways—such as glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, or oxidative phosphorylation—can lead to neuronal dysfunction and degeneration, contributing to PD36. Abnormal lactate metabolism is closely associated with PD progression7. For example, upregulation of HK2 expression promotes lactate production and induces dopaminergic neuron apoptosis. We measured pyruvate and lactate levels in the substantia nigra of PD mice to assess the effect of irisin on lactate metabolism. The results showed that irisin significantly reduced the levels of both metabolites and downregulated the expression of genes involved in glycolysis and lactate synthesis. Irisin also inhibited the expression of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), which impairs ATP synthesis by blocking pyruvate conversion into the TCA cycle. Our data suggest that irisin reduces lactate synthesis and promotes pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle, alleviating energy deficits caused by insufficient ATP synthesis. Immunofluorescence showed that LDHA co-localized primarily with dopaminergic neurons in the MPTP-treated PD model, not with astrocytes or microglia. Another study also reported that HK2 co-localizes with dopaminergic neurons7. These findings emphasize the critical role of irisin in correcting neuronal lactate metabolism abnormalities. Mitochondrial dysfunction is another hallmark of PD. Studies have shown that irisin improves mitochondrial function by inhibiting oxidative stress, repairing the antioxidant system, and increasing ATP synthesis capacity17,37. Consistent with previous studies, our results show that irisin reduces lipid peroxidation products (e.g., MDA and 4-HNE), restores SOD activity, and improves the GSH/GSSG antioxidant system. Additionally, irisin increased mitochondrial numbers and normalized the expression of ATP synthesis-related genes. Notably, lipid peroxidation products are markers of ferroptosis38. Recent studies suggest that microglial CR3 induces neuronal ferroptosis through ROS generated by NOX219, but whether irisin regulates ferroptosis via microglia remains to be explored. Furthermore, we found that UCP2 expression was suppressed in PD mice. Unlike UCP1, UCP2 is widely studied in the central nervous system and is known to attenuate ROS release under pathological conditions39. For instance, UCP2 knockout mice exhibit an inverse correlation between mitochondrial ROS production and mitochondrial numbers in dopaminergic neurons. Furthermore, UCP2 deficiency increases the vulnerability of dopaminergic neurons to MPTP, whereas UCP2 overexpression mitigates MPTP-induced melanogenesis and neuronal loss40. Our experiments demonstrated that irisin upregulated UCP2 expression, with strong colocalization to neuronal cells. In summary, irisin protects against PD pathology by normalizing neuronal lactate metabolism and reducing mitochondrial oxidative damage.

Recent studies have shown that SIRT1 exerts a neuroprotective effect in MPP+-treated dopaminergic neurons, primarily through deacetylation of PKM2, which improves brain lactate homeostasis and upregulates PGC-1α expression, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis in MPP+-treated cells8,41. To determine whether irisin ameliorates neuronal damage via the SIRT1 pathway, we assessed SIRT1 expression. The results showed that SIRT1 levels were significantly reduced in both MPTP-induced animal and MPP+-induced cell models, but were restored after irisin treatment. Further studies showed that irisin modulates the HIF-1α and PGC-1α pathways through SIRT1, regulating lactate metabolism and mitochondrial function repair. Under MPP+ treatment, HIF-1α expression was elevated, and PGC-1α expression was reduced, consistent with ref. 17. In contrast, irisin treatment decreased HIF-1α levels and increased PGC-1α expression. The effects of irisin were reversed by SIRT1 inhibitors, suggesting that irisin’s neuroprotective effect is mediated through the SIRT1 pathway. In conclusion, irisin likely repairs neuronal damage through the SIRT1 pathway.

In ovarian cancer, SIRT1 inhibits the transcriptional activity of HIF-1α through deacetylation, thereby preventing the mesenchymal transformation of ovarian cancer cells42. To verify this mechanism, we examined the acetylation status of HIF-1α. Our results showed that irisin enhanced SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of HIF-1α, inhibiting its function. However, this effect was reversed by the SIRT1 inhibitor NAM. Further investigation revealed that irisin, through SIRT1 regulation, reduced lactate synthesis and apoptosis by inhibiting lactate metabolism-related genes. Overexpression of HIF-1α increased lactate synthesis and attenuated the effect of irisin. Consistent with our findings, other studies have also demonstrated that SIRT1 regulates lactate metabolism by inhibiting it through deacetylation8. Our findings suggest that irisin may exert its effects by regulating the AMPK/Akt/mTOR pathway, although the precise mechanism requires further investigation.

Previous studies have shown that SIRT1 enhances PGC-1α expression and promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, with PGC-1α knockdown nearly abolishing SIRT1’s neuroprotective effect in SH-SY5Y cells41. To investigate whether irisin acts through this pathway, we examined the acetylation of PGC-1α. Our results showed that irisin enhanced SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of PGC-1α, improving its function, an effect attenuated by NAM inhibitors. Irisin, via SIRT1, reduced oxidative stress and repaired mitochondrial function in neuronal cells. These effects were reversed by the PGC-1α inhibitor SR-18292. Additionally, irisin upregulated NRF1 and NRF2, key regulators of mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidant defense, both of which are regulated by PGC-1α. We hypothesize that irisin reduces lactate synthesis by inhibiting the HIF-1α/LDHA pathway via SIRT1-mediated deacetylation and repairs mitochondrial function through the PGC-1α/UCP2 pathway, ultimately improving neuronal damage. Studies have shown that excessive lactate activates microglia, leading to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which exacerbate neuronal damage7,8.

To investigate whether irisin mitigates neuronal damage induced by microglial activation, we utilized immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence techniques. Our results demonstrated that irisin significantly inhibited microglial activation and reduced the secretion of inflammatory mediators in the substantia nigra of PD mice. Furthermore, conditioned medium experiments revealed that irisin markedly decreased the microglia-induced neuronal apoptosis rate and enhanced neuronal cell viability. Based on these findings, we propose that irisin exerts neuroprotective effects by attenuating the secretion of inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species by microglia.

While our study demonstrates that irisin exerts neuroprotective effects on dopaminergic neurons in an MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease mouse model, it is important to consider potential off-target effects associated with systemic administration. Intraperitoneal delivery may lead to widespread distribution of irisin, potentially affecting non-dopaminergic cells and tissues. Our findings suggest that irisin activates the SIRT1 signaling pathway, which is not exclusive to dopaminergic neurons and may also modulate glial function, as evidenced by reduced microglial activation. These observations indicate that while irisin benefits dopaminergic neurons—by improving motor function, reducing α-synuclein accumulation, and regulating lactate metabolism—its actions are not entirely cell-type-specific. To enhance therapeutic precision and minimize unintended effects, future studies should explore targeted delivery methods or utilize cell-specific genetic tools to dissect irisin’s mechanisms of action more accurately.

While our current study provides significant insights into the neuroprotective role of Irisin through its modulation of microglia-induced neurotoxicity, it is important to acknowledge that exploring the involvement of astrocytes and the broader neuroimmune response remains beyond the scope of this manuscript. Astrocytes are key players in maintaining brain homeostasis and can become reactive in response to neuronal damage, contributing to both protective and detrimental effects in Parkinson’s disease (PD). Although our findings primarily focus on the anti-inflammatory impact of Irisin on microglia and dopaminergic neurons, we recognize the potential significance of astrocyte modulation in Irisin’s protective mechanisms.

Future research should investigate how Irisin affects astrocytic activation, particularly concerning markers such as GFAP, IL-6, and CCL2. Co-culture systems involving neurons, microglia, and astrocytes could elucidate the complex interactions within the neuroimmune network and provide deeper insights into the multifaceted roles of Irisin. Additionally, global analyses using cytokine arrays or RNA sequencing may reveal other immune-related pathways influenced by Irisin, enhancing our understanding of its comprehensive neuroprotective profile.

Despite the limitations of our current study in fully addressing these aspects, we have included a discussion highlighting the potential involvement of astrocytes and the broader neuroimmune environment. This sets the stage for future investigations aimed at uncovering the complete spectrum of Irisin’s therapeutic potential in PD, ultimately leading to more effective treatment strategies.

Although our study demonstrates promising neuroprotective effects of irisin in an MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease model using male mice, it is essential to consider the potential impact of sex differences. Future studies should include female animals to fully elucidate whether the beneficial effects of irisin are consistent across both sexes and to better understand the underlying mechanisms involved.

While our study demonstrates the neuroprotective effects of irisin in an acute MPTP-induced model of Parkinson’s disease, we acknowledge that all findings are based on short-term exposure. But long-term viability and safety are critical considerations for any potential therapy targeting chronic neurodegenerative diseases. Existing studies on chronic irisin administration in metabolic and cardiovascular models have reported sustained benefits without significant toxicity, suggesting that irisin may be a safe therapeutic candidate. However, long-term data in neurodegenerative contexts remain limited. Future studies using chronic or aged animal models will be essential to evaluate prolonged efficacy, dose tolerance, and systemic safety, including potential effects on organ function and histopathology. Regarding body weight, we observed no significant differences between control and irisin-treated groups, consistent with previous reports indicating that irisin does not markedly affect body weight despite its metabolic actions. Nevertheless, monitoring physiological parameters over extended treatment periods remains crucial. In conclusion, while our current results are short-term, existing evidence supports irisin’s potential as a well-tolerated therapy, warranting further investigation into its long-term application in neurodegenerative disease models.

Although our study provides substantial evidence suggesting the potential neuroprotective effects of Irisin on Parkinson’s disease (PD) and preliminarily explores its mechanism of action through the activation of the SIRT1 signaling pathway, there are still certain limitations to our research. Particularly, we have not thoroughly investigated the direct validation of SIRT1’s role in vivo. While our in vitro experiments and cell studies provide robust support that Irisin influences lactate metabolism and mitochondrial function by modulating the SIRT1/HIF-1α/PGC-1α axis, data from further validation using specific inhibitors such as EX-527 in vivo are still lacking. Moreover, future research plans include employing conditional knockout mouse models to more comprehensively and deeply assess the neuroprotective effects mediated by SIRT1. This will help to more accurately understand the mechanisms of action of Irisin within biological systems.

In summary, our findings indicate that irisin enhances brain lactate homeostasis and mitochondrial function via SIRT1, thereby alleviating PD symptoms. Specifically, irisin exerts neuroprotective effects by inhibiting HIF-1α and activating PGC-1α through deacetylation. Additionally, irisin reduces microglia-induced neuroinflammation, further safeguarding neuronal cells. While this study demonstrates that irisin confers protective effects through multiple pathways in PD animal models, whether exogenous use of irisin has the same effect on PD patients and its mechanism of action needs further exploration.
