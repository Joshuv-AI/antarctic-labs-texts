Exosome-functionalized alginate/gelatin composite scaffolds: synergistic enhancement of osteogenic differentiation for bone tissue engineering
Authors: Mohammadali Meskaraf-Asadabadi, Mohammad Rasool Khazaei, Mahsa Amiri, Zeinab Ghesmati, Elham Ghanbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the osteogenic potential of exosome (Exo)-loaded alginate/gelatin (OA/Gel) macromolecular composite scaffolds for human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs). OA/Gel scaffolds were fabricated and crosslinked with EDC, followed by the incorporation of Exo at concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 µL. The scaffolds were characterized in terms of morphology, porosity, swelling behavior, mechanical properties, and cell adhesion. Osteogenic differentiation was assessed through alkaline phosphatase activity, Alizarin Red staining, and RT-PCR for Runt-related transcription factor 2, alkaline phosphatase, and bone gamma-carboxyglutamic acid-containing protein expression. Scanning electron microscopy analysis revealed interconnected pores predominantly within the range of 100–200 μm, with OA/Gel scaffolds demonstrating reduced pore size and enhanced mechanical strength compared to single-component scaffolds. The OA/Gel macromolecular scaffold exhibited optimal pore architecture (~ 180 μm), superior cell adhesion and proliferation, and significantly upregulated osteogenic markers at days 7 and 21. These findings suggest that Exo-loaded OA/Gel scaffolds, particularly at a concentration of 2.0 µL Exo, provide a favorable three-dimensional microenvironment for hASC osteogenic differentiation, highlighting their potential as a promising biomaterials for bone tissue engineering applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06945-7.Keywords: Alginate, Gelatin, Stem cell, Exosomes, Bone, Tissue engineering

Introduction

Bone defect reconstruction poses a significant challenge in the management of traumatic and orthopedic conditions, owing to the inherent limitations of bone tissue regeneration. These defects can substantially impair daily functioning and quality of life. Autologous bone grafting is considered the clinical gold standard; however, its application is constrained by limited donor availability, high costs, and potential donor-site morbidities, including pain, infection, and hemorrhage. These limitations have spurred increasing interest in the development of alternative bone substitutes. In this context, biomaterials have emerged as promising candidates, offering a diverse range of natural and synthetic options [1–3].

Advances in biomaterials have facilitated the development of a wide array of bone substitutes. Biologically derived materials, including demineralized bone matrix, platelet-rich plasma, bone morphogenetic proteins, hydroxyapatite, and coral, generally exhibit low immunogenicity and acceptable structural properties. However, their limited angiogenic capacity and insufficient osteoinductive potential can restrict their application in the treatment of large bone defects. Conversely, synthetic substitutes, such as calcium sulfate, calcium phosphate cements, beta-tricalcium phosphate composites, bioactive glasses, and polymer-based scaffolds, offer advantages such as controlled bioresorption, biocompatibility, and cost-effective production. Nevertheless, their clinical performance is often limited by inadequate osteoinduction and insufficient vascularization [4].

Among the biomaterials explored for scaffold fabrication, gelatin (Gel) and alginate (Alg) have garnered significant interest. Gelatin possesses Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) sequences, analogous to those present in the extracellular matrix (ECM), that facilitate cell adhesion and proliferation, rendering it a promising material for bone tissue engineering applications [5–7].

Exosomes (Exo) are nanoscale extracellular vesicles (50–150 nm) that play a critical role in intercellular communication through the transfer of bioactive molecules. A growing body of evidence indicates that Exo can effectively promote the osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). Furthermore, their inherent biocompatibility, stability, and targeting capabilities position them as promising candidates for regenerative applications [8, 9].

Incorporating Exo into three-dimensional scaffolds represents an emerging strategy for enhancing bone regeneration. However, the integration of Exo within Alg/Gel-based scaffold systems and their combined effects on osteogenic differentiation remain insufficiently explored. Thus, the present study aims to develop and evaluate Exo-loaded Alg/Gel composite scaffolds and to investigate their in vitro osteogenic potential using human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells.

Materials and methods

Isolation and characterization of Exo

The human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs) used in this study were obtained from a previously approved research project (Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences; Approval No. IR.TBZMED.REC.1400.376) [10]. At the time of original collection, informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. For the current study, the cells were retrieved from the laboratory’s cell bank. All experimental protocols were conducted in compliance with the ethical guidelines of the institutional review board and were further approved by the ethics committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.KUMS.MED.REC.1403.200).

hASCs were obtained from our previous research. Passage four hASCs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, the cells were washed and transitioned to low-glucose DMEM supplemented with KnockOut™ Serum Replacement (Gibco). Conditioned medium (CM) was collected every 48 h, centrifuged to eliminate cells and debris, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane.

Exo were isolated from filtered conditioned medium (CM) using the AnaCell Exo Kit (AnaCell, Tehran, Iran). The CM was mixed with reagent A in a 5:1 ratio, incubated at 4 °C for 12 h, and subsequently centrifuged. The resulting pellet was resuspended in reagent B and stored at − 80 °C. Protein concentration was determined in triplicate using a BCA assay kit (Parstous, Iran).

Characterization of the Exo included dynamic light scattering (DLS), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and Western blot analysis for CD9, CD63, and TSG101. Particle size distribution was measured using a Zetasizer Nano-ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). For FE-SEM (MIRA3, TESCAN, Czech Republic), 10 µL of the Exo suspension was placed on a glass slide, air-dried for four hours, dehydrated, fixed, gold-sputtered, and imaged at 25.0 kV. TEM (Carl Zeiss, EM-900, Germany) was conducted on phosphotungstic acid-stained grids. Western blotting utilized primary HRP-conjugated antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and secondary anti-rabbit IgG-HRP antibodies.

Preparation of OA/Gel scaffolds

Oxidized Alg (OA) was synthesized and subsequently utilized to produce hydrogels blended with Gle. The preparation of OA involved the oxidation of sodium periodate. Specifically, 2.01 g of sodium Alg was dissolved in 225 mL of deionized water (DW). A solution of 1.16 g of sodium periodate (NaIO4) in 30 mL of DW was added, and the mixture was agitated in the dark for 24 h. To terminate the oxidation process, 0.1 mL of ethylene glycol was introduced. After 30 min, 7 g of sodium chloride was added to neutralize the remaining sodium periodate, and the solution was subsequently precipitated using ethanol. This neutralization and precipitation procedure was performed three times. The resulting OA solution was dialyzed and then lyophilized for future applications.

The hydrogel generation process commenced by mixing 5 mL of a 6% (w/v) solution of OA with 5 mL of a 15% (w/v) solution of Gel at room temperature. A solution of 0.12 g of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and 0.05 g was added for crosslinking. Under the current ambient conditions, gelation occurred almost immediately, within approximately 4–5s, while complete gelation was achieved after 1 min. The hydrogels were subsequently stored at 20 °C and freeze-dried at − 80 °C for 48 h [11].

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, USA) was utilized to examine the surface morphology and internal porous structure of the lyophilized scaffolds at an accelerating voltage of 1.5 kV. Scaffold samples (1 × 1 × 1 cm3) were sectioned and sputter-coated with gold prior to imaging to evaluate pore architecture.

For quantitative analysis, three SEM images were acquired per sample. Image-based evaluations, including pore size analysis, were performed by two independent investigators blinded to the sample groups. Pore size distribution was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) by analyzing at least five randomly selected fields per sample, with three independent measurements per field. The average pore diameter was then calculated.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The characteristic peaks of the scaffolds were analyzed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Equinox 55, Bruker Co., United States), with wavenumbers ranging from 400 to 4000 cm−1.

The scaffolds (n = 3) were initially weighed and recorded as W0. Evaluation of compressive strength

Compression tests were conducted to evaluate the mechanical properties using a universal testing machine (Instron Ltd., High Wycombe, UK) equipped with a 20 kN load cell. Scaffolds (n = 3) measuring 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm were subjected to compression at a rate of 1 mm/min until a strain level of 70% was reached. The stress-strain (σ − ε) curves of the scaffolds were recorded, and the compressive modulus was determined from the slope of the initial linear region.

Degradation rate measurement

The degradation rate of the scaffolds was evaluated by monitoring weight loss over time while incubated in a PBS solution at 37 °C. Scaffolds (n = 3) were cultured in the PBS solution for 1, 7, 14, and 21 days. At each designated time point, the scaffolds were collected, washed three times with water, freeze-dried, and weighed at predetermined intervals. Scaffold sterilization

The scaffolds (five per group) underwent disinfection using 70% ethanol. They were placed in sterile petri dishes and processed within a laminar flow bench to ensure sterility. Subsequently, the scaffolds were exposed to UV radiation for 20 min on each side. The scaffolds were categorized into five groups: Ale/Gel and Ale/Gel/Exo, with Exo administered at concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 µg [13]. Scaffolds measuring 5 x 5 x 5 mm3 were placed in 12-well plates at a seeding density of 6 × 104 hASCs per scaffold per well. After 24 h of adhesion, the medium was replaced with osteogenic differentiation media (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution, 10mM β-glycerophosphate, 100nM dexamethasone, and 50µM L-ascorbic acid), incorporating Exo concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 µg/ml. The cells seeded on the scaffolds were cultured in this medium for three weeks to promote osteogenic differentiation.

MTT assay

The cytotoxicity of the scaffolds was assessed using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl) 2,5-diphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT), sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Human osteoblast-like (Saos-2) cells were utilized for the cytotoxicity evaluation. A total of 10,000 cells were seeded onto each scaffold (1cm × 1cm × 1cm) within the wells of a 96-well plate and were subsequently cultured in an incubator for 72 h. Following the removal of the treated supernatant, 200 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added and incubated for 1 h and 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After removing the MTT solution, 200 µL of DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was introduced, and the mixture was agitated for 10 min. Cytotoxicity was quantified by measuring absorbance at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Osteogenic differentiation assay

Alizarin red staining

All scaffolds were incubated in a 4% formaldehyde solution and subsequently stained with a 40 mM Alizarin Red S (ARS) solution, agitating gently for 30 min before washing with distilled water. Control wells containing undifferentiated scaffolds were maintained in culture media devoid of osteogenic supplements, with media replenished every three days. After 21 days, semi-quantitative assessment of osteogenic differentiation was conducted using the Alizarin Red S assay. Prior to scraping to collect cells and mineral deposits, scaffolds were treated with a 10% acetic acid solution. Each sample was incubated for 10 min in a water bath at 85 °C, followed by a 5 min cooling period on ice before centrifugation. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm in triplicate using an ELISA reader [14].

Alkaline phosphatase assesses

After inoculating human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs) onto scaffolds, the activity of alkaline phosphatase (ALP), a marker specifically expressed in osteoblasts, was evaluated using the ALP kit (Parsazmoon, Iran) to confirm differentiation into osteocytes. Following the removal of the culture medium after 21 days, the extract solution was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and mixed with the substrate solution according to the kit’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 405 nm.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR was employed to evaluate the expression of osteogenic and osteogenic-related genes, including BGLAP, Runx2, and ALP, in cells adhered to various scaffolds. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Japan) and stored at − 80 °C until needed. The concentration of the extracted RNA was determined at 260 nm using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with nuclease-free water as the blank control. cDNA synthesis was carried out using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was conducted on the synthesized cDNA utilizing Power SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), in conjunction with SYBR Green primers and TaqMan™ Gene Expression assays. The relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, employing GAPDH as the housekeeping gene. The necessary primer sequences are provided in Table 1.

FT-IR spectra of Alg, OA, and scaffolds

FT-IR spectra confirmed the specific functional groups present in the resultant OA. As shown in Fig. 2A, both Alg and OA exhibited broad hydroxyl stretching vibration absorption peaks within the 4000 to 3000 cm−1 range. Alg displayed prominent characteristic peaks at 2924.09, 1626.99, and 1425.40 cm−1, attributable to the C-H stretching vibration of the polysaccharide (PS) structure, as well as the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of −COO−. Additionally, the absorption peak at 1031.92 cm−1 corresponds to the C−O stretching vibration within the PS skeleton. In contrast, OA exhibited a new characteristic peak at 1739.79 cm−1, which is associated with the vibrational absorption of the C=O bond in the aldehyde group. This peak was weak, indicating the presence of hemiacetal formation of the free aldehyde groups.

FTIR spectra of alginate, oxidized alginate (A), and the OA, Gel and OA/Gel scaffolds (B)

Additionally, the hydroxyl stretching vibration absorption peak at 3437 cm−1 in the Alg spectrum shifted to 3427.51 cm−1 in the OA spectrum, indicating a reduction in the number of −OH groups in Alg at 3277.06 cm−1. These findings show that adjacent hydroxyl groups in Alg were oxidized to aldehyde groups (CHO-) by NaIO4. Furthermore, OA exhibited FT-IR spectroscopy results indicating that the periodate ion can cleave the C2–C3 linkage of Alg through the oxidation reaction, forming a dialdehyde [11, 15].

The FT-IR spectra of the OA/Gel scaffolds display characteristic peak bands at 1627.92 cm−1, which indicate the presence of C=N stretching vibrational modes associated with Schiff base reactions. The bands observed at 1035.77 cm−1 and 1085.92 cm−1 correspond to the C–O–C and C–O vibrational modes in the guluronic and mannuronic groups. Additionally, the bands at 1635 cm−1 and 1384 cm−1 are associated with the carboxylic acid units of Alg. A peak at 3425.58 cm−1 is attributed to –OH and –NH stretching modes (Fig. 2B).

Cross-sectional FE-SEM images of OA, Gel, and OA/Gel scaffolds were obtained to analyze the microstructures of the lyophilized scaffolds (Fig. 3). The Gel scaffolds exhibited a porous structure with an average pore size of 166 ± 31 μm (Fig. 3C), and the cells on these scaffolds were observed to have an elongated shape (Fig. 3D). OA/Gel hydrogels demonstrate a porous structure with an average pore size of 180 ± 22 μm and an interconnected architecture. Additionally, the pore walls are thicker than those found in both gelatin and OA scaffolds (Fig. 3E). After seven days of cell seeding on scaffolds, the cells exhibit an elongated shape, indicating strong adhesion between the cells and the scaffolds (Fig. 3F).

Discussion

This study examines the effects of Exo incorporation into OA/Gel scaffolds on the osteogenic differentiation ofhASCs). It evaluates essential scaffold characteristics and osteogenic markers, including mechanical properties, pore size, swelling behavior, cell adhesion, and the expression of key osteogenic genes such as Runx2, ALP, and BGLAP. This research adds to the existing evidence supporting the potential of Exo-loaded biomaterial scaffolds for bone regeneration, with a specific focus on OA/Gel-based hydrogels as platforms for the sustained delivery of bioactive cues to guide stem cell differentiation [16–18]. The discussion situates these findings within recent literature, addresses the relationship between scaffold design and Exo biology in promoting osteogenic differentiation, and highlights the broader implications for bone tissue engineering.

The introduction of Exo-functionalized scaffolds in tissue engineering represents a significant paradigm shift, transitioning from traditional cell-seeded constructs to advanced, next-generation platforms. These cell-free systems utilize the regenerative potential of bioactive vesicles [17, 19, 20]. Exo, particularly those derived from MSCs, have emerged as crucial mediators of cell-cell communication, capable of transferring a diverse array of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids to recipient cells, thereby orchestrating processes such as proliferation, angiogenesis, and lineage-specific differentiation [17, 20] incorporation of Exo into OA/Gel scaffolds enhances their inherent bioactivity, while the hydrogel matrix provides a hydrated, three-dimensional microenvironment that closely resembles the native extracellular matrix, thereby facilitating cell migration and tissue integration [21, 22].

Recent reviews underscore the advantages of Exo-laden hydrogels compared to traditional cell-based therapies, citing enhanced safety, diminished immunogenicity, and streamlined regulatory pathways [23]. Exo provide stable paracrine signaling with sustained release capabilities when incorporated into hydrogels, effectively addressing the challenges of rapid clearance and poor retention associated with direct Exo therapies [24, 25]. In the current study, OA/Gel scaffolds functionalized with Exo facilitated a microenvironment conducive to hASC adhesion and osteogenic differentiation. These findings corroborate and extend the existing consensus in bone tissue engineering [6, 26, 27]. The convergence of biomimicry and bioactivity is increasingly recognized as essential for effective bone regeneration strategies [20].

Optimal mechanical strength and elasticity are critical requirements for bone substitute scaffolds, ensuring that the construct can withstand physiological loads and promote tissue integration without premature collapse or inadequate support [28, 29]. In the present OA/Gel scaffold, Exo did not compromise the scaffold’s compressive modulus; however, they may confer additional modulation of viscoelastic properties through indirect effects, such as influencing cell-secreted matrix remodeling. Literature indicates that the compressive modulus of OA/Gel hydrogels can be fine-tuned by adjusting the alginate-to-gelatin ratio or crosslinking conditions, typically ranging from 10 kPa to several tens of kPa—aligning with the lower spectrum of cancellous bone and optimizing the mechanotransduction necessary for osteogenic commitment [6, 26, 29].

Importantly, the mechanical microenvironment significantly influences stem cell lineage commitment. Substrates with higher stiffness generally promote osteogenic differentiation, mediated by mechanosensitive pathways and gene expression (e.g., through focal adhesion kinase signaling, YAP/TAZ nuclear translocation, and the upregulation of osteogenic markers [30]. A recent study demonstrated that increasing the stiffness of OA/Gel hydrogels from approximately 10 to 55 kPa enhanced the expression of osteogenic genes in dental pulp stem cells while supporting overall cell adhesion and proliferation [26]. The current findings, where OA/Gel/Exo scaffolds facilitated robust osteogenic differentiation, align with models suggesting that an appropriately stiff yet compliant scaffold is vital for mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) engraftment and differentiation.

Moreover, the incorporation of nanoscale fillers (e.g., hydroxyapatite, silica nanoparticles) has been shown to further enhance the mechanical properties, mineralization potential, and bioactivity of OA/Gel hydrogels, indicating that a multifactorial approach may be advantageous for optimizing constructs for clinical translation [27, 31, 32]. While Exo are unlikely to directly contribute to bulk mechanical strength, their regenerative signals can stimulate endogenous matrix production, thereby improving mechanical properties during in vivo remodeling [25].

Pore architecture specifically, size, distribution, and interconnectivity, plays a crucial role in the performance of scaffolds for bone regeneration, affecting key processes such as cell migration, vascularization, nutrient diffusion, and tissue formation [6]. In alignment with previous research, the OA/Gel scaffolds in this study were designed to exhibit pore sizes within the optimal range for bone tissue engineering (typically 150–500 μm), as larger pores facilitate osteogenic differentiation and host cell infiltration [33].

Foundational studies utilizing various OA/Gel compositions have shown that larger pore sizes (~ 600 μm) enhance stem cell adhesion, proliferation, and the upregulation of osteogenic markers such as ALP and BGLAP, compared to scaffolds with smaller pores. This is likely attributed to the enhanced spatial environment and improved oxygenation [6]. Additionally, investigations involving 3D-printed or freeze-dried OA/Gel constructs have confirmed that an interconnected, open-pore network accelerates the ingrowth of new bone tissue in vivo [28]. The current findings, wherein Exo-loaded OA/Gel scaffolds demonstrated increased osteogenic gene expression and matrix deposition, underscore the importance of pore optimization in conjunction with the integration of Exo cues. Furthermore, the combination of Exo and architecturally optimized scaffolds ensures the delivery of potent regenerative signals alongside the necessary physical context for cellular interpretation, thereby enhancing the efficacy of tissue engineering constructs [34].

The swelling equilibrium, water retention, and degradation kinetics of OA/Gel hydrogels are critical for modulating the cellular microenvironment throughout bone regeneration [35]. Swelling facilitates nutrient influx and metabolic waste efflux while enabling three-dimensional cell infiltration within the scaffold. However, excessive swelling can compromise mechanical integrity, whereas insufficient swelling may hinder transport and cellular activity [36]. The swelling behavior of the OA/Gel system can be tailored by adjusting the alginate and gelatin content, the ratio of crosslinking agents (e.g., CaCl2 concentration), and the incorporation of enzymatically inducible crosslinks [37]. Achieving the optimal swelling ratio requires careful balance; recent studies have indicated that OA/Gel hydrogels exhibiting swelling ratios of 20–35% water absorption maintain dimensional stability while still allowing adequate mass transport and cell viability [26, 35].

Furthermore, the degradation rate of the scaffold should ideally be calibrated to align with the rate of new bone formation, thereby facilitating construct replacement as endogenous mineralized matrix is deposited. Controlled degradation in OA/Gel hydrogels can be achieved by modulating crosslink density, with more stable ionic or enzymatic crosslinks providing slower degradation rates and increased persistence in vivo factors that are crucial for cell-scaffold interactions during bone formation [29]. The results of the present study, which indicate that OA/Gel scaffolds maintain favorable swelling and retention profiles, support existing evidence that dynamic, hydrated matrices enhance osteogenic differentiation while allowing for the gradual release of bioactive vesicles over time [38]. Such timed Exo delivery is essential for replicating the spatiotemporal progression of natural bone healing.

Successful osteogenesis in engineered constructs relies on initial cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. The OA/Gel matrix inherently offers multiple cell-interactive domains: alginate provides carboxyl groups for ionic crosslinking, while gelatin (denatured collagen) presents integrin-binding motifs that emulate the native bone extracellular matrix (ECM), facilitating strong attachment and spreading of stem cells [21].

Functionalization of scaffolds with Exo further enhances cell adhesion and intercellular communication. Exo can transfer membrane proteins, signaling RNAs, and bioactive factors that modulate the expression of adhesion molecules in recipient hASCs, enhance ECM production, and activate various signaling pathways (e.g., focal adhesion kinase, integrin, MAPK pathways) involved in cell attachment and survival [17].

A growing body of literature supports the notion that Exo-functionalized hydrogels promote higher levels of cellular adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, and organized migration compared to non-functionalized counterparts, partly due to Exo mimicking niche-specific cues from the cellular microenvironment [19]. For instance, Lee et al. [39] demonstrated that Exo-enriched gelatin/hydroxyapatite scaffolds facilitated increased attachment and mineralization of hASCs for bone tissue engineering. In the current study, robust hASC attachment observed on OA/Gel/Exo scaffolds highlights the ability of such constructs to replicate the cellular niche and primes subsequent osteogenic differentiation, consistent with the established understanding that engineered enhancements of cell-matrix and cell-Exo interactions are pivotal drivers of regenerative success [18, 21].

Runx2 is recognized as the principal transcription factor governing osteoblastic commitment and differentiation from MSCs, initiating the transcriptional cascade that culminates in bone matrix production [40–42]. The elevation of Runx2 in hASCs following seeding on Exo-functionalized OA/Gel scaffolds, as observed in this study, indicates an early commitment to the osteogenic lineage.

Recent genomic and expression profiling confirm that Runx2 occupancy precedes the upregulation of target genes such as ALP and BGLAP during the sequential stages of osteoblastogenesis [40, 41]. Notably, Exo delivery systems can enhance Runx2 transcription or stabilize its expression in MSCs by transferring regulatory microRNAs and transcriptional activators, thereby improving the efficiency and speed of osteogenic differentiation [16, 17]. Furthermore, Exo cargo, including microRNAs such as miR-21, miR-218, and lncRNAs, has been shown to upregulate Runx2 by targeting its upstream repressors, thereby facilitating a pro-osteogenic microenvironment [19, 23]. The increased Runx2 expression in the Exo-loaded scaffold group in this study thus provides mechanistic validation for the osteoinductive function of Exo supplementation.

ALP activity serves as a crucial early marker of the mineralization phase in osteoblast differentiation, catalyzing the hydrolysis of phosphate esters and promoting hydroxyapatite deposition in the matrix [40]. Compared to controls, the enhanced ALP activity in hASCs seeded onto OA/Gel/Exo scaffolds aligns with existing literature, demonstrating that hydrogels incorporating Exo significantly enhance early osteogenic signaling and matrix vesicle formation [20, 34]. Current evidence also links ALP expression to scaffold composition, as ionically crosslinked OA/Gel scaffold inherently provide calcium substrates, further supporting matrix mineralization in conjunction with Exo signaling [6, 28].

BGLAP is a late-stage, non-collagenous bone matrix protein that is tightly regulated by Runx2 and is exclusively expressed in mature osteoblasts, indicating complete differentiation and matrix mineralization [40, 42]. The upregulation of BGLAP in hASCs within Exo-functionalized OA/Gel scaffolds suggests the induction of terminal differentiation and indicates the functional competence of the formed tissue for bone regeneration applications [17, 20]. These findings are consistent with previous studies involving 3D-printed OA/Gel constructs and underscore the utility of Exo delivery in completing the differentiation sequence [27]. Collectively, the results of this study demonstrate that Exo play a role at multiple stages of osteogenesis, modulating both the initiation (Runx2, ALP) and terminal (BGLAP) phases, while OA/Gel scaffolds provide the necessary context and support for these processes.

Limitations and future directions

While the study provides compelling evidence regarding the efficacy of Exo-loaded OA/Gel scaffolds, several limitations warrant acknowledgement. First, although current findings indicate favorable osteogenic outcomes, further investigation is required to evaluate long-term integration, vascularization, and biomechanical functionality in clinically relevant models. Additionally, the precise optimization of Exo concentration, scaffold architecture, and degradation-controlled release kinetics remains an area for further research, with batch-to-batch consistency and scalability representing significant translational considerations [25].

Controlled Exo engineering, including the utilization of preconditioned or genetically modified parental MSCs to augment osteoinductive potential, represents a promising yet underexplored strategy. Furthermore, a comprehensive in vivo evaluation of the immunological and angiogenic responses elicited by Exo-laden OA/Gel scaffolds will be essential to predict host interactions and ensure clinical safety and efficacy [43].

Conclusion and outlook

The current findings, in conjunction with recent literature, confirm that Exo-incorporated OA/Gel scaffolds represent a mechanistically coherent and promising approach for bone regeneration. The OA/Gel hydrogel, characterized by customizable mechanical properties, swelling and degradation profiles, and favorable pore architecture, serves as an effective delivery vehicle for Exo, facilitating controlled and sustained bioactivity while creating a microenvironment conducive to hASC adhesion and osteogenic differentiation. This synergy is evidenced by the upregulation of critical osteogenic genes (Runx2, ALP, BGLAP) and the formation of functional bone matrix. The integration of Exo bioengineering with advanced hydrogel design and rigorous preclinical-to-clinical pipelines is positioned to facilitate the translation of these constructs into routine clinical practice for the repair of critical-sized bone defects and beyond. Ongoing multidisciplinary collaboration is essential to address the remaining challenges in manufacturing, standardization, and regulatory approval, ensuring that the potential of Exo-functionalized OA/Gel scaffolds is fully realized in the field of regenerative medicine. Despite the promising findings, this study has certain limitations. The results are based solely on in vitro experiments, which may not fully replicate the complex physiological environment of bone tissue in vivo. Therefore, further in vivo studies are required to validate the therapeutic efficacy and clinical applicability of the Exo-loaded scaffolds.
