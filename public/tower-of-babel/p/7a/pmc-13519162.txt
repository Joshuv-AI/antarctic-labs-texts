Difficult conversations in bladder cancer survivorship: Integrating mental health and intimacy into routine urologic care
Authors: Armine K Smith, Daniela Wittmann, Krisztina Emodi, Gail Dykstra, Brian Billings, Mary Sullivan, Mary W Dunn, Glenn J Treisman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBladder cancer survivorship extends beyond oncologic control and includes significant psychological distress, demoralization, sexual dysfunction, and identity disruption. Despite their clinical relevance, structured approaches to difficult conversations in bladder cancer care remain inconsistent.ObjectiveTo develop interdisciplinary consensus recommendations for integrating structured communication regarding mental health and intimacy into routine bladder cancer care.MethodsA multidisciplinary breakout session was convened during the 2025 Bladder Cancer Advocacy Network (BCAN) Think Tank. As practice-changing perioperative and metastatic therapies extend survival for a growing population of bladder cancer survivors, structured communication about mental health and intimacy becomes increasingly central to comprehensive care. Introduction

Bladder cancer remains one of the most psychologically burdensome urologic malignancies.1–4 Radical cystectomy, intravesical therapy, and systemic treatments are associated with profound effects on continence, body image, sexual function, identity, and existential outlook.5,6 While oncologic surveillance is well-described in contemporary guidelines, structured communication addressing psychological and sexual health is not consistently integrated into routine practice.

Patients frequently report unmet needs related to mood changes, intimacy disruption, and relational strain. 7 Despite the prevalence of these concerns, many patients do not initiate these discussions unless directly invited. Women in particular are even less likely to have their needs adequately addressed in traditionally male-centered urologic environments.8,9

Recognizing this gap, the Bladder Cancer Advocacy Network (BCAN) convened a multidisciplinary Think Tank session focused specifically on difficult conversations in bladder cancer survivorship. The objective was to develop consensus-based, implementable recommendations to standardize communication practices.

Methods

A multidisciplinary breakout session, entitled “Difficult Conversations: Practitioners & Conversations with Patients on Depression, Intimacy, Mental and Sexual Health,” was convened at the 2025 BCAN Think Tank. The session was co-chaired by a urologic oncologist, a nurse practitioner, and a BCAN patient advocate. It was attended by a multidisciplinary group of participants representing urology, medical oncology, psychiatry, sexual medicine, advanced practice providers, nursing, social work, and patient advocacy.

The session followed a structured format. Designated discussion leaders, drawn from urologic oncology, psychiatry, behavioral and sexual health, and patient advocacy, first delivered brief didactic presentations summarizing current evidence and clinical experience within each predefined domain. Each presentation was followed by a moderated, open discussion in which attendees were invited to respond to guiding prompts, share practice-based perspectives, and identify areas of agreement and divergence. Preliminary points of consensus were recorded in real time by the co-chairs and subsequently refined through iterative follow-up email correspondence among the discussion leaders and co-chairs until the wording of each statement was mutually endorsed. Formal Delphi methodology and quantitative voting thresholds were not employed.

Six domains were predefined by the co-chairs in advance of the session and structured both the discussion and the resulting consensus statements: (1) normalization of psychosocial and intimacy-related conversations; (2) routine screening and structured assessment; (3) psychological distress, including the distinction between depression and demoralization; (4) sexual dysfunction and intimacy; (5) multidisciplinary referral pathways and advocacy integration; and (6) team-based survivorship care.

Normalization and iterative dialogue

Normalization emerged as a foundational principle. Patients frequently interpret silence as an indication that emotional or intimacy-related concerns are inappropriate. Participants agreed that clinicians should proactively introduce these topics using routine framing language and revisit them across survivorship phases, recognizing that readiness to engage may evolve over time.

Structured patient-reported outcome measures can serve as potential facilitators of longitudinal dialogue by providing consistent, disease-specific anchors for discussion.

Screening and structured assessment

Routine screening was endorsed as a pragmatic entry point into difficult conversations. Recommended tools include brief validated instruments assessing mood, demoralization, and sexual function. Recommended instruments and their key characteristics are summarized in Table 1. For depressive symptoms, the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) 10 and the Zung Self Rating Depression Scale (SDS) were discussed, with acknowledgment that measures validated in medically ill populations may better account for somatic symptom overlap common in oncology. 11 The Demoralization Scale II (DS-II) was identified as a complementary tool to capture existential distress. 12

For sexual function, the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) 13 and Sexual Health Inventory for Men (SHIM) 14 were recommended when appropriate, although both may be less informative in sexually inactive patients and should therefore be interpreted in the context of sexual activity status rather than in isolation. PROMIS sexual function and satisfaction measures may be more adaptable in this setting because they include interest and satisfaction domains, but they also require contextual interpretation. 15 PROMIS may therefore be particularly useful when a broader, sex-specific patient-reported outcome is desired, whereas FSFI and SHIM remain useful for more focused clinical screening.

For broader survivorship assessment, the Bladder Cancer Index (BCI) and FACT-Bl instruments were endorsed across disease stages, while EORTC QLQ-NMIBC24 and EORTC QLQ-BLM30 are stage-specific modules for non-muscle-invasive and muscle-invasive disease, respectively, and FACT-Bl-Cys was highlighted for its inclusion of diversion-specific and cystectomy-related domains. 16

Screening scores should prompt further assessment, shared decision-making, and referral rather than serve as diagnostic endpoints.

Psychological distress and demoralization

Bladder cancer is associated with substantial psychological burden across disease stages, from diagnosis through surveillance and recurrence. A central area of agreement was the need to distinguish major depressive disorder from demoralization. Although overlapping in presentation, demoralization is characterized by hopelessness, loss of meaning, and existential distress without necessarily meeting criteria for major depression. 17 Failure to differentiate these constructs may result in pharmacologic treatment when counseling-based or meaning-centered interventions are more appropriate.

Clinicians should be trained to recognize both syndromes, and structured screening should be used to support, rather than replace, clinical dialogue.

Sexual dysfunction and intimacy

Sexual dysfunction was identified as a nearly universal yet persistently overlooked consequence of bladder cancer treatment. Men commonly experience erectile and orgasmic dysfunction, while women report changes in arousal, lubrication, dyspareunia, and body image. Both sexes describe relational strain and disruption of sexual identity and self-image. Women remain particularly underserved in counseling and survivorship pathways. Sexual health discussions should therefore begin preoperatively and continue longitudinally rather than relying on patient-initiated disclosure.

Because sexual health is closely linked to broader treatment-related quality of life, bladder cancer–specific patient-reported outcome measures were also recognized as valuable adjuncts to survivorship assessment (Table 1).

Multidisciplinary referral pathways and advocacy integration

Participants agreed that survivorship care must extend beyond the urologist through structured, early referral pathways. Core collaborators include psychiatry, psychology, sexual medicine, pelvic floor physical therapy, gynecology, social work, and primary care.

Importantly, patient advocates were recognized as integral components of survivorship infrastructure rather than peripheral support resources. Peer-based programs, including Survivor to Survivor, were endorsed as structured referral options that may reduce isolation, facilitate disclosure of sensitive concerns, and enhance engagement with care. Proactive introduction of advocacy resources across survivorship phases was recommended.

Team-based care

Responsibility for difficult conversations should not rest solely with the physician. Survivorship communication is inherently multidisciplinary and should be embedded within team-based workflows. Nurses and advanced practice providers often identify psychosocial distress and intimacy concerns early, given their longitudinal patient contact. Structured training in communication skills and psychosocial assessment was endorsed to ensure consistent identification and response. Integrating patient advocates within these workflows reinforces the legitimacy of psychosocial and relational health as expected components of bladder cancer care. These coordinated roles operate longitudinally across survivorship phases, as illustrated in the proposed ladder framework (Figure 1).

Discussion

This BCAN Think Tank consensus represents a structured effort to formalize communication practices within bladder cancer survivorship. While advances in surgical technique, systemic therapy, and surveillance imaging have significantly evolved, structured communication frameworks have lagged behind. Unlike well-defined oncologic surveillance pathways, communication practices surrounding psychological and sexual health remain variable and often dependent on individual clinician comfort and training.

The clinical urgency of this work is amplified by a rapidly evolving treatment landscape. Practice-changing phase 3 trials have recently redefined therapy across disease states. In the perioperative setting, the NIAGARA trial demonstrated that adding perioperative durvalumab to neoadjuvant chemotherapy significantly improved event-free and overall survival in muscle-invasive bladder cancer. 18 In the metastatic setting, the EV-302 trial established enfortumab vedotin plus pembrolizumab as a new standard of care, approximately doubling median overall survival compared with platinum-based chemotherapy. 19 As more patients live longer with bladder cancer across all disease states, the population navigating extended survivorship and the cumulative physical, psychological, and relational sequelae of these therapies will expand substantially. In the view of the panel, this makes structured, consensus-based approaches to psychosocial and sexual health communication not a peripheral concern but an increasingly central component of comprehensive bladder cancer care, and underscores the need for consensus guidance of the kind proposed here.

Unlike oncologic surveillance, which can be guided by stage-specific recurrence risk, communication surrounding psychological and sexual health is inherently relational and dynamic. Nevertheless, our consensus demonstrates that structured, implementable strategies can be developed. The proposed survivorship ladder framework (Figure 1) operationalizes the six consensus domains across the bladder cancer care continuum, from diagnosis through palliation, providing clinicians with a concrete, phase-specific scaffold for when and how to initiate difficult conversations. This framework is intended as the central, practice-facing deliverable of the consensus.

The integration of routine normalization language, brief screening tools, multidisciplinary referral pathways, and team-based training provides a feasible model for incorporation into contemporary practice. Importantly, these recommendations do not require substantial resource expansion but rather systematic incorporation of practices that are currently applied inconsistently.

Our consensus aligns with broader evidence demonstrating that integration of patient-reported outcome measures in oncology improves symptom control, quality of life, and, in a landmark randomized trial of routine electronic symptom monitoring during chemotherapy, overall survival. 20 However, survivorship-specific communication in bladder cancer has received comparatively limited formal attention.

Limitations of this consensus include the absence of formal Delphi methodology and quantitative voting thresholds. While agreement was achieved through structured discussion and collective endorsement, future work should incorporate formal consensus metrics and external validation. Additionally, while multidisciplinary, certain stakeholders including geriatricians and rehabilitation specialists were underrepresented.

Implementation science approaches will be necessary to evaluate the real-world impact of structured communication frameworks on patient-reported outcomes, healthcare utilization, and clinician satisfaction.21,22

Conclusion

Bladder cancer survivorship requires more than oncologic surveillance. Psychological distress, demoralization, and sexual dysfunction are intrinsic components of the patient experience.

The BCAN Think Tank consensus provides a structured, implementable framework to integrate difficult conversations into routine care. Prospective evaluation of these recommendations will be critical to determining their impact on patient outcomes and quality of life.
