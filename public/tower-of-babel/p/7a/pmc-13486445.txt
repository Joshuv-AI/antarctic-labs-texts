A machine learning-guided epithelial plasticity score refines prognostication and immune-context stratification in muscle-invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Difei Yu, Yu Zhang, Jiarun Tang, Jing Qing, Ke Hu, Jiamo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMuscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) remains biologically heterogeneous, and clinicopathological variables do not fully capture epithelial-state remodeling linked to progression and treatment response. We developed an epithelial plasticity-related risk score (EPRS) and evaluated its prognostic, biological, and immunological relevance.MethodsUsing 368 primary TCGA-MIBC cases, we built a basal-luminal plasticity axis and integrated differential expression, correlation with the continuous plasticity index, and survival association to define 331 candidate genes. Consensus clustering, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, and 10 modeling strategies were used for subtype discovery and score development. EPRS was externally validated in GSE32894 and GSE13507 and further characterized by enrichment analysis, immune deconvolution, single-cell RNA sequencing, immunotherapy-related analyses, and nomogram construction.ResultsTwo stable plasticity-associated subtypes showed distinct overall survival. WGCNA identified opposing lipid-metabolic and extracellular-matrix/injury-response programs. Among portable models, the Ridge-based EPRS showed the most stable cross-cohort performance and significantly stratified survival in TCGA-MIBC, GSE32894, GSE13507, and the pooled meta cohort. High-EPRS tumors were enriched for extracellular-matrix remodeling, inflammatory signaling, stromal expansion, and an immune-evasive microenvironment despite increased checkpoint-related expression. Single-cell analysis linked EPRS to a continuous malignant epithelial-state landscape. An EPRS-based nomogram improved individualized survival estimation.ConclusionsEPRS captures a clinically relevant epithelial plasticity axis in MIBC and links poor outcome to stromal-immune remodeling and malignant epithelial-state organization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05555-3.Keywords: Muscle-invasive bladder cancer, Epithelial plasticity, Machine learning, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Bladder cancer is one of the most frequently diagnosed malignancies of the urinary tract, and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) accounts for a substantial proportion of bladder cancer-related deaths because of its strong propensity for local invasion, nodal dissemination, and distant relapse [1]. Although clinicopathological staging remains the foundation of routine management, patients with apparently comparable disease burden often experience markedly different outcomes, indicating that conventional stratification does not fully capture the biological heterogeneity of MIBC [2]. Over the past decade, transcriptomic profiling has considerably refined our understanding of this disease. Early studies identified basal and luminal MIBC subtypes with distinct differentiation programs and therapeutic sensitivities [3], and subsequent integrative analyses further extended these observations into broader molecular taxonomies that include luminal, basal or squamous, stroma-rich, and neuroendocrine-like classes [4–5]. More recently, expression-based subtypes have also been associated with pathologic response to neoadjuvant immune checkpoint blockade [6]. Nevertheless, even these molecular frameworks remain insufficient to fully describe the dynamic state transitions that accompany tumor progression and therapeutic adaptation.

In parallel with advances in molecular classification, immunotherapy has expanded the therapeutic landscape of urothelial carcinoma. Anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 strategies can produce durable responses in a subset of patients with advanced disease, underscoring the clinical importance of the tumor immune microenvironment [7–8]. However, treatment benefit remains highly variable, and many tumors with apparent immune activity still fail to derive meaningful clinical response. Previous studies have shown that EMT- and stroma-related transcriptional programs are associated with resistance to PD-1 blockade [9], whereas TGF-beta-driven stromal remodeling can promote T-cell exclusion and attenuate the efficacy of PD-L1 inhibition [10]. These findings suggest that the biological state of the tumor epithelium and the surrounding immune or stromal context are tightly interconnected, and that biomarkers integrating both dimensions may have greater value than checkpoint expression alone.

Epithelial plasticity represents one such candidate dimension. Rather than reflecting a fixed molecular subtype label, epithelial plasticity describes the capacity of malignant urothelial cells to shift across lineage-biased transcriptional states, including basal-like, luminal-like, and more dedifferentiated or mesenchymal-associated phenotypes. This process is increasingly recognized as a key feature of bladder cancer evolution. Single-cell analyses of recurrent and metastatic bladder cancer have revealed coordinated programs of stemness maintenance, EMT-like remodeling, and microenvironmental reorganization [11–12], whereas urothelial-to-neural transdifferentiation has been implicated in progression to small-cell bladder cancer [13]. On one hand, such plasticity may facilitate invasion, adaptation, and therapeutic resistance; on the other hand, it may encode clinically meaningful differences in differentiation state, stromal interaction, and immune responsiveness. Focusing on epithelial plasticity may therefore provide a useful framework for understanding the biological and clinical diversity of MIBC.

We therefore constructed an epithelial plasticity-related framework in MIBC and derived a prognostic score from it. Starting from a state-defining epithelial marker panel and a continuous plasticity axis, we combined phenotype-guided gene selection, consensus clustering, WGCNA, and machine-learning model selection to construct the epithelial plasticity-related risk score (EPRS). We then examined its associations with clinicopathological features, transcriptional programs, immune contexture, single-cell epithelial-state organization, immunotherapy-related phenotypes, and individualized survival prediction. Methods

Study design and public datasets

Clinical and transcriptomic data for TCGA-BLCA were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons [2]. External survival-validation cohorts GSE32894 and GSE13507, the single-cell dataset GSE135337 [11], and the urothelial carcinoma immunotherapy cohort GSE176307 [14] were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus. The IMvigor210 cohort was obtained from the public IMvigor210CoreBiologies resource [7]. For GEO cohorts, processed expression matrices and matched phenotype tables were used directly. When multiple probes mapped to the same gene symbol, probes without valid symbols were removed and the remaining probes were collapsed by the median expression value. Survival time was analyzed in the units provided by each repository, with TCGA reported in days and the principal GEO validation cohorts in months.

TCGA-MIBC cohort establishment and outcome definition

TCGA-BLCA RNA-seq data and matched clinical annotations were retrieved from the Genomic Data Commons. Among 431 transcriptomic profiles, 412 primary tumors and 19 solid-tissue normal samples were available. Only primary tumors were retained. Expression data were converted to a gene-level log2(TPM + 1) matrix after removal of non-gene features and collapsing duplicated gene symbols. Cases were restricted to pathological T2-T4b disease with valid overall-survival annotations, yielding a final TCGA-MIBC cohort of 368 patients.

Overall survival (OS) was the primary endpoint and was defined from diagnosis to death or last follow-up. Patients alive at last follow-up were censored.

Construction of the epithelial plasticity framework

To establish an epithelial plasticity framework, a state-defining marker panel was curated from basal-luminal classification studies and the consensus molecular classification of bladder cancer [3–5]. Basal-related genes included KRT5, KRT6A, KRT14, CD44, and EGFR. Luminal-related genes included GATA3, FOXA1, PPARG, KRT20, UPK1A, UPK2, and UPK3A. Additional squamous or transition-associated markers included TP63, DSG3, SFN, ELF3, ERBB2, GRHL3, CLDN4, and EPCAM.

Basal and luminal scores were calculated as the mean log2(TPM + 1) expression of the corresponding seed genes, and the plasticity index was defined as basal score minus luminal score. This basal-minus-luminal formulation was chosen for biological interpretability and lineage directionality, and all seed genes were equally weighted to avoid optimizing the phenotype definition against outcome. Tumors were dichotomized at the median plasticity index for the initial phenotype comparison. Sensitivity analyses evaluated z-score-based, canonical-marker, and leave-one-marker-out formulations by testing their Spearman correlation with the original plasticity index, Spearman correlation with the final EPRS, and Cox association with overall survival.

Stepwise expansion and shrinking of epithelial plasticity-related candidate genes

Candidate genes were refined in three steps. Differential expression between plasticity-high and plasticity-low tumors was performed with limma [15] using |log2 fold change| > 0.5 and adjusted P < 0.05. Genes were then screened for association with the continuous plasticity index by Spearman correlation, retaining those with |rho| > 0.4 and false-discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01. Prognostic filtering was performed by univariable Cox regression, retaining genes with FDR < 0.05. Multiple-testing correction used the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. The final 331-gene candidate set was defined as the overlap of prognosis-supported genes and strict correlation-supported genes within the phenotype-linked expression pool. Because this sequential filtering was performed within TCGA-MIBC, downstream performance was evaluated primarily by fixed-coefficient external projection and continuous-score validation rather than by internal training performance alone.

Consensus clustering and WGCNA

Unsupervised consensus clustering was performed using a 90-gene state-defining panel composed of the curated seed genes together with the 80 genes showing the strongest absolute correlations with the plasticity index. Consensus clustering was implemented with ConsensusClusterPlus [16] using hierarchical clustering, Pearson distance, maxK = 6, reps = 1000, pItem = 0.8, and pFeature = 1. The optimal number of clusters was determined by the consensus matrix, cumulative distribution function curve, delta area plot, PAC score, sample distribution, and survival discrimination.

WGCNA was performed using the top 8,000 most variable genes in the TCGA-MIBC cohort [17]. The soft-thresholding power was selected with pickSoftThreshold, and co-expression modules were constructed with blockwiseModules using minModuleSize = 30, mergeCutHeight = 0.15, and reassignThreshold = 0. Module eigengenes were correlated with overall survival status, age, sex, pathological stage, and consensus-cluster assignment to identify plasticity-associated modules.

Model development, external validation, and comparison with published signatures

The 331 candidate genes were entered into ten modeling strategies, including LASSO, Elastic Net, Ridge, Stepwise Cox, StepCox + Ridge, CoxBoost, random survival forest, LASSO + StepCox, ElasticNet + StepCox, and StepCox + ElasticNet. Penalized Cox models were fitted with cv.glmnet under 10-fold cross-validation using lambda.min, with alpha = 1 for LASSO, alpha = 0.5 for Elastic Net, and alpha = 0 for Ridge. Stepwise Cox modeling started from the top 50 univariable Cox genes and used bidirectional selection. StepCox + Ridge and StepCox + ElasticNet were then fitted on the StepCox-retained genes. CoxBoost was tuned with optimCoxBoostPenalty (minstepno = 50, maxstepno = 200, start.penalty = 9 * number of events) and 10-fold cv.CoxBoost (type = “verweij”), and random survival forest was fitted with ntree = 500. Model performance was assessed with Harrell’s concordance index, bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, and paired bootstrap C-index differences where applicable. Because external projection required a fixed coefficient vector, model selection focused on portable models that could be transferred without refitting. The Ridge model was retained as the final EPRS model.

Feature matching and external projection were performed without refitting the model. For each cohort, the fixed coefficient vector was used to calculate an EPRS value for every patient. When complete feature transfer was not possible because of platform or annotation differences, only genes shared between the final model and the target expression matrix were retained for score projection; for the final Ridge model, 216 of the 331 genes were available in GSE13507 and 211 were available in GSE32894. Score direction was harmonized so that larger values indicated higher risk. Because partial feature transfer and platform differences altered the absolute score scale across cohorts, the primary validation used continuous EPRS in Cox models, while cohort-specific median dichotomization was used only for Kaplan-Meier visualization and descriptive group comparisons. As sensitivity analyses, a TCGA-derived cutoff was also tested in external cohorts both on the raw projected scale and after within-cohort standardization. For pooled external analyses, cohort-specific scores were standardized within cohort before merging GSE32894 and GSE13507. Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed at 1, 3, and 5 years in each cohort. The final Ridge coefficient vector and projection information are provided in Supplementary Table S25.

EPRS was compared with six representative transferable bladder cancer prognostic signatures whose published definitions could be reconstructed across the same four cohorts: Wang_8gene, Zhang_MIBC_3gene, TAM_6gene, Jin_MIBC_immune, Jiang_hypoxia_4gene, and TGFb_5gene. Hazard ratios, C-index values, and 3-year AUCs were compared across the same four cohorts.

Clinicopathological association analyses

The clinical relevance of EPRS was evaluated in TCGA-MIBC. For subgroup analyses, age was dichotomized at 60 years, pathological stage was grouped as stage II versus stage III-IV, and nodal status was grouped as N0 versus N+. Differences in EPRS between clinicopathological categories were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Within each subgroup, patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups using the training-cohort median EPRS value, and Kaplan-Meier analysis with Cox regression was used to estimate survival differences, hazard ratios, and 95% confidence intervals.

Functional enrichment analysis

Differential expression analysis between the high- and low-risk groups in the 368-case TCGA-MIBC cohort was performed with limma [15] using the log2(TPM + 1) expression matrix. Significant differentially expressed genes were defined by P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed with clusterProfiler [18]. GSEA [19] was conducted on the full ranked gene list ordered by limma-derived log2 fold change. Hallmark pathway activity was quantified by GSVA [20] using the MSigDB Hallmark collection, and differential GSVA scores between the two EPRS groups were evaluated with limma [15].

Immune landscape analysis

Global stromal and immune abundance was estimated using ESTIMATE [21]. Cell-abundance patterns were further assessed with EPIC [22], quanTIseq [23], and xCell [24]. Representative immunoregulatory molecules were organized into four display categories: antigen presentation, checkpoint or exhaustion, inflamed chemokine or cytotoxicity, and stromal-immunosuppressive remodeling. ssGSEA [25] was used to estimate immune-cell subsets and immune-pathway activity in the high- and low-risk groups.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from GSE135337, comprising seven bladder cancer samples and one matched adjacent sample (36,619 raw cells), were processed using a Seurat-based workflow [26]. Seurat objects were initialized with min.cells = 3 and min.features = 200. Cells with fewer than 500 detected genes, more than 6,000 detected genes, or mitochondrial transcript proportions of 10% or higher were removed. Potential doublets were identified with scDblFinder on a per-sample basis before merging. Data were then log-normalized, highly variable genes were selected, principal-component analysis was performed, and sample-level batch effects were harmonized with Harmony [27] using sample_id as the integration variable. Graph-based clustering and UMAP visualization were generated on the corrected embeddings using 30 dimensions and a clustering resolution of 0.4. Major cellular compartments were annotated by canonical markers and cluster-level transcriptional features.

Tumor epithelial cells were extracted for focused analysis. Epithelial clusters were organized into four higher-order meta-subpopulations (G1-G4) on the basis of cluster-level transcriptional features, including basal differentiation, luminal differentiation, plasticity, and phenotype composition. Each sample was also assigned a dominant epithelial type according to the relative predominance of basal and luminal programs, yielding basal-like and luminal-like categories for downstream mapping.

Scissor was used to connect TCGA-MIBC bulk transcriptomes and the corresponding binary EPRS phenotype with the malignant epithelial compartment [28]. A binary model (family = “binomial”) was fitted with alpha = 0.05 and cutoff = 0.2 to identify phenotype-associated epithelial cells. For computational efficiency, Scissor was first applied to a sample-balanced epithelial subset of 200 cells (115 low-risk-like, 84 high-risk-like, and one background cell), and labels were then propagated to the remaining epithelial cells by nearest-neighbor transfer in PCA space with k = 15. Repeated hold-out validation of this projection procedure yielded a mean macro-F1 of approximately 0.645.

Per-cell basal, luminal, and plasticity program scores were generated with AddModuleScore using ENSEMBL-mapped epithelial program gene sets. The epithelial state space was defined by basal-luminal balance and plasticity program score. Copy-number variation was inferred from raw epithelial counts using inferCNV with T/NK cells and endothelial cells as reference populations [29]. inferCNV was run with cutoff = 0.1, cluster_by_groups = TRUE, denoise = TRUE, HMM = FALSE, num_threads = 1, and write_expr_matrix = TRUE. Pseudotemporal progression was reconstructed with Monocle3 after preprocess_cds(num_dim = 50, method = “PCA”), cluster_cells(reduction_method = “UMAP”), and learn_graph(use_partition = TRUE) [30]. Root cells were selected from the low-risk, luminal-biased, and low-plasticity epithelial state before order_cells. Differentiation potential was estimated with CytoTRACE2 [31] using raw epithelial counts with species = “human”, batch_size = 10,000, and parallelize_models = FALSE.

Immunotherapy-related analyses and external immunotherapy cohorts

Immunotherapy-related analyses were performed in two stages. In the TCGA-MIBC cohort, checkpoint-related genes were screened by comparing expression between the predefined EPRS groups while considering expression abundance, prevalence across samples, and survival-stratifying capacity. Five representative genes were retained for the main analyses: PVR, NT5E, IDO1, CD274, and HAVCR2. For each gene, patients were dichotomized by the cohort median expression level and then divided into four subgroups based on combined EPRS status and gene-expression level for survival analysis.

The immunophenoscore framework [32] was applied to gene-by-sample TCGA-MIBC expression matrices to derive composite and class-level component scores. Group-wise comparisons were made between the low- and high-EPRS groups, and Spearman analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between the continuous EPRS value and IPS-related summary categories. In parallel, the official TIDE framework was run through its TIDEpy implementation [33] to calculate total TIDE score, dysfunction score, exclusion score, CTL score, and predicted responder status.

External validation was performed in two disease-matched public urothelial carcinoma immunotherapy cohorts, IMvigor210 and GSE176307. IMvigor210 represents metastatic urothelial carcinoma treated with atezolizumab, and GSE176307 represents a real-world metastatic urothelial carcinoma immune checkpoint blockade cohort. The fixed EPRS model was projected into each external cohort without retraining. Because annotation and platform differences prevented complete feature transfer, only genes shared between the final model and each external expression matrix were retained for score calculation. Scores were dichotomized at the cohort-specific median. Treatment response was analyzed as responder versus non-responder according to cohort-specific annotation.

Nomogram construction and evaluation

A prognostic nomogram was developed in the TCGA-MIBC cohort. Candidate variables included EPRS and available clinicopathological factors, including age group, sex, pathological stage, T stage, N stage, grade, and M stage. Univariate Cox regression was first performed for OS. Because of structural overlap among overall stage, T stage, and N stage and because of missingness in nodal and metastatic annotations, the final multivariable model was constructed using EPRS, age group, sex, pathological stage, and grade in patients with complete data for all included predictors. Complete-case filtering excluded two patients because of missing covariate data, leaving 366 patients for multivariable modeling and nomogram construction. In this section, age was coded as older than 65 years versus 65 years or younger.

Nomogram performance was evaluated by time-dependent ROC analysis [34] at 1, 3, and 5 years (365, 1095, and 1825 days), dynamic AUC analysis comparing the nomogram with EPRS and clinicopathological variables during follow-up, bootstrap-corrected calibration analysis using 200 resamples with 60 patients per calibration group, and decision curve analysis [35]. The 3-year DCA was selected for the main text because it retained a more stable effective sample size and provided the most interpretable threshold range.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1, and penalized Cox modeling used glmnet version 4.1–10. Group comparisons were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two-group continuous comparisons unless otherwise specified, and categorical response frequencies in the immunotherapy cohorts were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman analysis. Survival outcomes were analyzed with Kaplan-Meier curves, log-rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards models, with results reported as hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Proportional-hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals for the principal Cox models and adjusted Cox models. Model performance was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and time-dependent ROC curves. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was used for high-dimensional differential-expression, correlation-screening, and univariable-survival analyses as described above. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless an FDR threshold was explicitly specified.

Results

Development of an epithelial plasticity-centered framework in TCGA-MIBC

Among 431 TCGA-BLCA transcriptomic profiles initially retrieved from GDC, 412 primary tumors and 19 adjacent normal tissues were identified. After retaining only primary tumors, restricting the cohort to pathological T2-T4b disease, and excluding cases with invalid survival annotations, 368 patients with MIBC were retained for analysis. Based on a curated epithelial state-defining panel comprising basal, luminal, and squamous/transition-associated markers, we quantified basal and luminal programs and defined a plasticity index as basal score minus luminal score. This continuous epithelial-plasticity axis was then used as the common framework for phenotype stratification, candidate-gene expansion, subtype discovery, and downstream model development.

Three complementary evidence layers were integrated to extend the initial framework beyond the seed markers. Differential expression between plasticity-high and plasticity-low tumors identified 4,612 genes, correlation analysis with the continuous plasticity index identified 6,542 genes, and univariable Cox regression identified 713 prognosis-associated genes. Their stepwise integration yielded 331 epithelial plasticity-related candidate genes that were carried forward into subtype discovery, network analysis, and model development (Supplementary Table S1).

Consensus clustering and WGCNA identify plasticity-associated transcriptional programs

Using a 90-gene state-defining panel composed of curated seed genes together with the 80 genes showing the strongest absolute correlations with the plasticity index, consensus clustering showed that the K = 2 solution yielded the clearest consensus structure and the most balanced sample allocation, whereas higher K values produced small and unstable minor clusters. CDF curves, delta area plots, and PAC scores all supported the two-cluster solution, with PAC values of 0.1989, 0.2039, and 0.2261 for K = 2, K = 3, and K = 4, respectively. The 368 MIBC cases were therefore classified into two epithelial plasticity-related consensus subtypes containing 158 and 210 tumors, respectively (Fig. 2A-C; Supplementary Figure S1A-F; Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed significant overall survival separation between these subtypes (P = 0.024; Fig. 2D; Supplementary Figure S1G-I), indicating that the clustering framework was not only stable but also clinically informative.

To further define the co-expression architecture underlying epithelial plasticity, WGCNA was performed using the top 8,000 most variable genes. Soft-threshold analysis identified power = 3 as the first value achieving approximate scale-free topology (Fig. 2E). Multiple co-expression modules were identified (Fig. 2F), and module-trait analysis revealed several modules significantly associated with both clinical variables and epithelial plasticity-related features. Among these, the brown module showed the strongest positive correlation with consensus-cluster assignment (r = 0.832), whereas the red module showed an opposite pattern and aligned with epithelial-plasticity features in the extended trait matrix (Fig. 2G; Supplementary Figure S1J; Supplementary Table S4).

Functional analysis further clarified the biological content of these representative modules. The brown module was mainly enriched in lipid and fatty-acid metabolic processes, whereas the red module was enriched in cell-substrate adhesion, cell-matrix adhesion, wound healing, epithelial proliferation, and related injury-response programs (Fig. 2H; Supplementary Table S5). The corresponding brown-module genes, red-module genes, and their union are summarized in Supplementary Tables S6-S8. When the union of representative module genes was intersected with prognosis-associated genes, 84 network-supported prognostic genes were retained from an overlap between 1,139 module genes and 713 prognosis-associated genes (Fig. 2I; Supplementary Table S9). This 84-gene overlap was used as a network-grounded biological anchor, whereas model training retained the broader 331-gene candidate space to preserve a richer transferable feature pool for downstream cross-cohort selection. These data indicated that epithelial plasticity in MIBC was organized around opposing metabolic and ECM/injury-response transcriptional programs.

Cross-cohort model selection defines EPRS as the final portable score

Ten machine-learning and survival modeling strategies were trained using the 331 candidate genes. Although random survival forest showed the highest apparent training performance in the initial screen, it was not retained as the final score because it did not provide a fixed coefficient vector for direct external projection without refitting. Among the portable candidates displayed in the principal four-cohort comparison, the Ridge model achieved the highest mean C-index across TCGA-MIBC, GSE32894, GSE13507, and the pooled external meta cohort (0.685), exceeding ElasticNet (0.644), LASSO (0.641), CoxBoost (0.639), StepCox + Ridge (0.619), and StepCox + ElasticNet (0.614; Fig. 3A). The broader external benchmarking summary in Supplementary Table S10 preserved the same rank ordering. Bootstrap C-index analyses supported a training-versus-transferability tradeoff: Ridge was not the strongest apparent training model in TCGA-MIBC (Ridge C-index = 0.659, 95% CI 0.615–0.703), but it performed more strongly in external cohorts, including GSE32894 (0.810, 95% CI 0.730–0.878) and GSE13507 (0.604, 95% CI 0.533–0.673). We therefore defined the fixed Ridge-based score as EPRS for downstream analyses because of its cross-cohort stability and direct coefficient-based portability (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S6D; Supplementary Tables S10 and S28A, B).

EPRS significantly stratified overall survival in the TCGA-MIBC training cohort, in which median-based grouping generated balanced low- and high-risk strata (184 vs. 184; log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3B). The score retained prognostic separation in GSE32894 (112 vs. 112; log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3C) and GSE13507 (83 vs. 82; log-rank P = 0.019; Fig. 3D). When the two external cohorts were pooled after score-direction harmonization, EPRS continued to separate survival outcomes in the meta cohort (Fig. 3E), supporting external reproducibility beyond any single dataset.

When evaluated as a continuous score, EPRS was significantly associated with overall survival in TCGA-MIBC (HR = 1.86 per SD increase, 95% CI 1.55–2.23, P < 0.001), GSE32894 (HR = 2.22, 95% CI 1.67–2.96, P < 0.001), GSE13507 (HR = 1.30, 95% CI 1.02–1.65, P = 0.031), and the pooled principal external meta cohort comprising GSE32894 and GSE13507 (HR = 1.62, 95% CI 1.30–2.03, P < 0.001). A raw TCGA-derived cutoff did not transfer directly to external microarray cohorts because the projected score scale collapsed groups, whereas a standardized TCGA-derived cutoff retained evaluable groups and preserved survival separation in TCGA-MIBC, GSE32894, and GSE13507. These findings support continuous-score use and indicate that any clinically deployable cutoff would require platform harmonization and assay locking (Supplementary Fig. S6A, C; Supplementary Table S22).

Time-dependent ROC analysis showed that discrimination was strongest in the short- to intermediate-term window and became more variable at 5 years across datasets. In TCGA-MIBC, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year AUCs were 0.825, 0.682, and 0.480, respectively (Fig. 3F), indicating attenuation of long-term performance in the training cohort. In GSE32894, the corresponding AUCs were 0.849, 0.857, and 0.871 (Fig. 3G), whereas in GSE13507 they were 0.738, 0.660, and 0.601 (Fig. 3H). The pooled meta cohort achieved AUCs of 0.768, 0.728, and 0.681 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 3I). These results support cross-cohort portability of the fixed EPRS model, while indicating that long-term discrimination remained cohort dependent.

EPRS outperforms representative published prognostic signatures

EPRS was compared with six representative transferable bladder cancer prognostic signatures whose published definitions could be reconstructed across the same four cohorts. In the hazard ratio comparison, EPRS remained significantly associated with overall survival in all cohorts, whereas the retained comparator models showed less consistent significance patterns (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Table S11). When discrimination was evaluated with C-index, EPRS ranked highest in TCGA-MIBC, GSE32894, GSE13507, and the pooled meta cohort, indicating that its advantage was preserved in both training and external validation settings (Fig. 4B).

Direct head-to-head comparison with the Wang_8gene model further illustrated this advantage. EPRS achieved C-indices of 0.659, 0.810, 0.604, and 0.666 in TCGA-MIBC, GSE32894, GSE13507, and the pooled meta cohort, respectively, whereas the corresponding C-indices for Wang_8gene were 0.604, 0.614, 0.469, and 0.552 (Fig. 4C). A similar pattern was observed for 3-year AUC, with EPRS ranking highest across all four cohorts (Fig. 4D). These comparisons support EPRS as a robust and externally portable transcriptomic score rather than a model whose apparent performance is confined to the training set.

Clinical correlates of EPRS in TCGA-MIBC

EPRS was then evaluated against clinicopathological variables in the TCGA-MIBC cohort. For the subgroup display used in Fig. 5, age was dichotomized at 60 years to match the distribution shown in the figure, whereas the nomogram section used the age coding available for multivariable clinical modeling. EPRS differed significantly according to age (P = 0.007), sex (P = 0.043), and pathological stage (P < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between nodal-status groups (P = 0.123). Older patients, female patients, and those with stage III-IV disease showed higher EPRS values than their respective counterparts (Fig. 5A-D).

E–L Kaplan-Meier survival curves comparing high- and low-EPRS groups within the age younger than 60 years (E), age 60 years or older (F), female (G), male (H), stage II (I), stage III–IV (J), N0 (K), and N+ (L) subgroups. We then assessed whether EPRS retained prognostic value within clinically relevant subgroups. In patients younger than 60 years, the high-EPRS group showed significantly worse survival than the low-EPRS group (HR = 2.74, 95% CI 1.15–6.52, P = 0.018; Fig. 5E), and a similar result was observed in patients aged 60 years or older (HR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.38–2.74, P < 0.001; Fig. 5F). When stratified by sex, EPRS remained significantly associated with overall survival in male patients (HR = 2.48, 95% CI 1.70–3.62, P < 0.001; Fig. 5H), whereas separation did not reach significance in female patients (HR = 1.54, 95% CI 0.85–2.78, P = 0.150; Fig. 5G). Across pathological-stage subgroups, EPRS retained prognostic value in both stage II disease (HR = 2.79, 95% CI 1.29–6.03, P = 0.007; Fig. 5I) and stage III-IV disease (HR = 1.83, 95% CI 1.29–2.60, P < 0.001; Fig. 5J). Similarly, significant risk stratification was observed in both N0 patients (HR = 2.68, 95% CI 1.64–4.37, P < 0.001; Fig. 5K) and N+ patients (HR = 1.58, 95% CI 1.00-2.51, P = 0.047; Fig. 5L). In supplementary comparison, EPRS also showed stronger discriminatory ability than the examined clinicopathological variables (Supplementary Table S12).

To formally test subgroup heterogeneity, Cox models were fitted with the continuous EPRS term, each clinicopathological variable, and the corresponding interaction term. Formal interaction testing showed no significant interaction for age (interaction HR = 1.19, 95% CI 0.33–4.33, P = 0.789), sex (interaction HR = 1.57, 95% CI 0.59–4.17, P = 0.366), or pathological stage (interaction HR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.27–2.60, P = 0.757), whereas nodal status showed only borderline heterogeneity (interaction HR = 0.40, 95% CI 0.15–1.08, P = 0.072; Supplementary Table S13). These results support broad clinical portability of EPRS across standard clinicopathological strata, while indicating that the weaker separation in selected subgroups should be interpreted cautiously rather than as firm evidence of subgroup-specific score behavior.

EPRS was also compared with TCGA mRNA subtype labels. The final analysis set contained basal/squamous (n = 135), luminal (n = 24), luminal-infiltrated (n = 74), luminal-papillary (n = 118), and neuronal (n = 17) cases. EPRS differed markedly across subtypes (Kruskal-Wallis P < 0.001), with higher median values in basal/squamous and neuronal tumors and lower values in luminal-papillary tumors. Importantly, continuous EPRS remained associated with overall survival after adjustment for mRNA subtype alone (HR = 3.33 per SD, 95% CI 2.48–4.47, P < 0.001) and after combined adjustment for age, sex, pathological stage, grade, and mRNA subtype (HR = 3.14, 95% CI 2.35–4.19, P < 0.001). These findings indicate that EPRS is biologically aligned with established basal-luminal subtype structure, but is not merely a categorical subtype surrogate. Instead, EPRS provides a continuous risk gradient that remains prognostically informative after adjustment for mRNA subtype and clinicopathological variables.

Functional enrichment reveals ECM-remodeling and inflammatory programs in high-EPRS tumors

Transcriptomic differences between high- and low-risk tumors were analyzed by GO, GSEA, KEGG, and GSVA (Fig. 6A-E). GO enrichment was dominated by extracellular matrix organization, extracellular structure organization, and external encapsulating structure organization, with additional enrichment of immunoglobulin-mediated immune processes. Cellular-component and molecular-function terms were centered on extracellular matrix, collagen trimer, extracellular matrix structural constituents, heparin binding, and collagen binding (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Table S14).

Functional enrichment analysis of EPRS risk groups. A GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes between the high- and low-EPRS groups, displayed separately for biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. B GSEA-enriched pathways in the high-EPRS group. C GSEA-enriched pathways in the low-EPRS group. D KEGG-enriched pathways of differentially expressed genes between the high- and low-EPRS groups. E Differences in hallmark pathway activity scores, calculated by GSVA, between the high- and low-EPRS groups

GSEA revealed clear pathway divergence between the two risk strata. High-EPRS tumors were enriched for ECM proteoglycans, integrin cell-surface interactions, assembly of collagen fibrils and other multimeric structures, collagen formation, and interferon signaling (Fig. 6B; Supplementary Table S15). By contrast, low-EPRS tumors were enriched for fatty-acid metabolism and translation-related pathways, including eukaryotic translation elongation, peptide-chain elongation, paracetamol ADME, and viral mRNA translation (Fig. 6C; Supplementary Table S15).

KEGG analysis and GSVA converged on ECM-receptor interaction, complement or coagulation, cytokine-related signaling, EMT, hypoxia, IL6-JAK-STAT3, and TNFA-NFKB as prominent features of the high-EPRS group (Fig. 6D, E; Supplementary Tables S16 and S17). Notably, these enrichment patterns were concordant with the earlier WGCNA results, in which the representative modules separated lipid/metabolic programs from wound-healing and ECM-related states. Together, these analyses indicate that increasing EPRS tracks a shift from metabolic or homeostatic programs toward ECM-remodeling and inflammatory signaling.

EPRS is associated with an inflamed but stromally constrained immune microenvironment

Immune-state analyses integrated ESTIMATE-derived stromal and immune metrics with cell-abundance estimates from EPIC, quanTIseq, and xCell. High-EPRS tumors showed higher stromal and immune content together with lower tumor purity, with broader enrichment of endothelial, B-cell, macrophage or monocyte, dendritic-cell, neutrophil, and regulatory T-cell-related signals rather than an immune-desert pattern (Fig. 7A, C).

Immune landscape associated with EPRS in MIBC. A Integrated heatmap showing ESTIMATE-derived global stromal and immune metrics together with selected cell-abundance estimates from EPIC, quanTIseq, and xCell across low- and high-EPRS tumors. B Differential expression heatmap of representative immunoregulatory genes grouped into antigen presentation, checkpoint or exhaustion, inflamed chemokine or cytotoxicity, and stromal-immunosuppressive remodeling categories. C Comparison of estimate score, stromal score, immune score, and tumor purity between the low- and high-EPRS groups. D ssGSEA comparison of immune-cell population scores between the two EPRS groups. E ssGSEA comparison of immune-pathway activity scores between the two EPRS groups. In box plots, the center line indicates the median and the box denotes the interquartile range. Full quantitative summaries of the displayed immune comparisons, including effect sizes and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P values, are provided in Supplementary Table S29

Representative immunoregulatory genes were then examined across four categories: antigen presentation, checkpoint or exhaustion, inflamed chemokine or cytotoxicity, and stromal-immunosuppressive remodeling. High-EPRS tumors expressed higher levels of antigen-presentation genes (TAP1, TAP2, B2M, HLA class I), inhibitory checkpoints (PDCD1LG2, HAVCR2, CTLA4, PDCD1, CD274, IDO1), cytotoxic or inflamed mediators (PRF1, GZMB, CCL5, CXCL9-CXCL11, IFNG), and stromal-immunoregulatory factors (CSF1R, CXCL12, MMP9, CCL2, IL6, TGFB1) (Fig. 7B).

ESTIMATE confirmed that the high-EPRS group had significantly higher estimate, stromal, and immune scores and lower tumor purity (Fig. 7C). Consistent with these observations, ssGSEA showed enrichment of multiple immune-cell populations and stronger antigen-presentation, checkpoint, cytolytic, interferon-response, inflammatory-promoting, and stromal-remodeling programs in high-EPRS tumors (Fig. 7D, E). Quantitative summaries of the displayed immune comparisons, including high-EPRS versus low-EPRS effect sizes and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P values, are provided in Supplementary Table S29. Together, these data describe an inflamed yet stromally expanded and immunoregulatory microenvironment associated with high EPRS.

To address whether EPRS was reducible to stromal admixture or tumor purity, we fitted additional Cox models using standardized continuous EPRS with pathological stage and ESTIMATE-derived tissue-composition metrics. EPRS remained significantly associated with overall survival after adjustment for stage plus stromal score (HR = 1.85, 95% CI 1.53–2.23, P < 0.001), stage plus immune score (HR = 2.00, 95% CI 1.66–2.42, P < 0.001), or stage plus tumor purity (HR = 1.93, 95% CI 1.60–2.32, P < 0.001). The association also remained significant in clinicopathological models including age, sex, stage, grade, and stromal/immune or purity adjustment. Schoenfeld residual testing did not detect EPRS-specific proportional-hazards violations in these models. These analyses indicate that EPRS is not explained solely by ESTIMATE-derived stromal or purity variation, although its interpretation remains tissue-context dependent (Supplementary Fig. S6B; Supplementary Table S23).

Single-cell analysis links EPRS-related phenotypes to malignant epithelial-state organization

Single-cell analysis of GSE135337 resolved a structured MIBC ecosystem comprising seven major cellular compartments. UMAP projection showed that tumor epithelial cells represented the dominant population, whereas fibroblasts, myeloid cells, T/NK cells, plasma cells, endothelial cells, and a pericyte-like population occupied smaller but clearly separable territories (Fig. 8A). Marker-gene profiling supported these annotations: EPCAM, KRT8, and KRT19 marked tumor epithelial cells; PECAM1, VWF, and KDR marked endothelial cells; COL1A1, DCN, and LUM marked fibroblasts; CD3D, NKG7, and TRBC1 marked T/NK cells; LST1, TYROBP, and FCER1G marked myeloid cells; JCHAIN, MZB1, and IGKC marked plasma cells; and RGS5, MCAM, and ACTA2 supported the pericyte-like compartment (Fig. 8B; Supplementary Figures S2A-D and S3A, B).

Dot size indicates the proportion of cells expressing each marker, and color intensity indicates average expression. C, UMAP maps of the malignant epithelial compartment colored by higher-order epithelial meta-subpopulation (CellType), epithelial type (Type), and Scissor-derived phenotype. D, Alluvial plot showing relationships among epithelial meta-subpopulation, epithelial type, and Scissor-derived phenotype. The left panel shows all epithelial clusters, and the right panels show representative cluster-level distributions. After restriction to the malignant epithelial compartment, epithelial cells were organized into four higher-order meta-subpopulations (G1-G4) that occupied distinct but partially overlapping territories on the epithelial manifold (Fig. 8C). Luminal-like cells predominated along the continuous main epithelial backbone, whereas basal-like cells were concentrated in the isolated epithelial island and adjacent restricted regions. Scissor-derived phenotype-associated cells were distributed non-uniformly across this manifold, and the alluvial plot showed that meta-subpopulation, epithelial type, and Scissor-derived phenotype were related but not reducible to a single categorical boundary (Fig. 8C, D; Supplementary Figure S4A-D).

Projection of malignant epithelial cells into a two-dimensional state space defined by basal-luminal balance and plasticity program score revealed a continuous but highly structured landscape (Fig. 8E). Cluster 1 was mainly distributed in a luminal-biased and relatively low-plasticity region, whereas cluster 2 extended further toward the basal-dominant and higher-plasticity domain. Clusters 0 and 3 occupied broader intermediate territories rather than behaving as fully polarized endpoints. inferCNV provided orthogonal support for the malignant identity of the epithelial compartment by showing broad and recurrent chromosomal alterations relative to reference populations (Fig. 8F). Complementary Monocle3 and CytoTRACE2 analyses supported state continuity but did not collapse this epithelial manifold into a single monotonic trajectory that fully recapitulated the binary risk-state overlay (Fig. 8G, H). Together, these analyses indicate that the principal single-cell signal captured by EPRS lies in structured epithelial-state organization rather than in a simple linear progression model.

EPRS is linked to immunotherapy-associated biology

Representative checkpoint-related genes were first examined in the TCGA-MIBC cohort. PVR, NT5E, IDO1, CD274, and HAVCR2 were all expressed at higher levels in the high-EPRS group than in the low-EPRS group (Fig. 9A; Supplementary Table S18). We then asked whether combining checkpoint-related expression with EPRS would further refine survival stratification. For each of the five genes, patients were divided into four subgroups according to EPRS status and gene expression level. All five genes yielded significant four-group survival separation (all log-rank P < 0.0001; Fig. 9B-F), with the low-risk/high-expression subgroup showing the most favorable survival pattern and the high-risk/high-expression subgroup showing the poorest outcome.

IPS-based analyses indicated selective remodeling rather than uniformly favorable immunogenicity. Overall IPS, raw IPS, checkpoint-related, and SC-related components were lower in the high-EPRS group, whereas EC- and MHC-related components were higher (Fig. 9G). Correlation analysis similarly showed positive associations of the continuous EPRS value with MHC- and EC-related features but negative associations with checkpoint-related and composite IPS-related categories (Fig. 9H; Supplementary Table S19). TIDE analysis further indicated a more immune-evasive phenotype in high-EPRS tumors: total TIDE, dysfunction, exclusion, and CTL scores were all higher in the high-risk group, whereas the predicted responder fraction was markedly lower (Fig. 9I, J; Supplementary Table S19). Thus, higher checkpoint-related expression in the adverse-risk group did not translate into a more treatment-favorable immune context.

The fixed EPRS model was then projected into two disease-matched urothelial carcinoma immunotherapy cohorts, for which endpoint harmonization is summarized in Supplementary Figure S5 and Supplementary Table S19. In IMvigor210, the high-EPRS group showed a numerically lower objective response rate than the low-EPRS group, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.198; Fig. 9K). In GSE176307, neither survival separation (P = 0.62; Fig. 9L) nor objective response rate (P = 0.591; Fig. 9M) differed significantly between risk groups, although both trended in the same direction. These external data do not establish predictive utility, and the immunotherapy analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Nevertheless, they are consistent with EPRS capturing immunoregulatory biology in urothelial carcinoma.

Construction and validation of an EPRS-based clinicomolecular nomogram

An integrated nomogram was constructed in the TCGA-MIBC cohort. In univariate Cox analysis, EPRS showed a strong association with overall survival, and several clinicopathological variables, including age group, pathological stage, T stage, and N stage, were also significantly related to outcome (Fig. 10A; Supplementary Table S20). Missing-data assessment showed no missing values for OS time, OS status, EPRS, stromal score, immune score, or tumor purity; age and pathological stage each had one missing value, grade had two missing values, and N stage had 24 missing values. Given overlap among overall stage, T stage, and N stage, together with missingness in nodal and metastatic annotations, the final multivariable model incorporated EPRS, age group, sex, pathological stage, and grade in patients with complete data.

Construction and performance evaluation of an EPRS-based nomogram. A Univariate Cox regression analysis of EPRS and clinicopathological variables for overall survival. B Multivariable Cox regression analysis of EPRS and clinicopathological variables for overall survival. C Nomogram constructed from the final multivariable Cox model for predicting 1, 3, and 5-year overall survival probabilities. D Time-dependent ROC curves of the nomogram at 1, 3, and 5 years. E Dynamic AUC comparison of the nomogram, EPRS, and individual clinicopathological variables during follow-up. F Calibration curves of the nomogram at 1, 3, and 5 years. The dashed line indicates ideal calibration, and the solid curves represent agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. G Three-year decision curve analysis comparing the net clinical benefit of the nomogram, EPRS, and individual clinicopathological variables

After multivariable adjustment, EPRS remained independently associated with poor overall survival (HR = 4.840, 95% CI 2.984–7.849, P < 0.001). Age > 65 years also retained independent significance (HR = 1.909, 95% CI 1.359–2.682, P < 0.001), and stage IV disease remained significantly associated with inferior survival relative to stage II (HR = 2.491, 95% CI 1.591–3.898, P < 0.001), whereas sex, stage III disease, and grade were not independently significant in the final model (Fig. 10B; Supplementary Table S21). Based on these predictors, a nomogram integrating EPRS, age group, sex, pathological stage, and grade was established to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival probabilities (Fig. 10C). Within the nomogram, EPRS contributed the largest proportion of total points, followed by pathological stage and age group.

Time-dependent ROC analysis showed moderate but consistent nomogram performance, with AUCs of 0.760, 0.761, and 0.764 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 10D). Dynamic AUC analysis showed a modest but persistent advantage of the nomogram over EPRS alone, with a mean absolute AUC gain of 0.061 across follow-up, and over individual clinicopathological variables (Fig. 10E). Calibration curves showed reasonable agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 10F). Decision curve analysis further showed that the nomogram delivered the highest net clinical benefit among the displayed predictive models across threshold probabilities from 0.05 to 0.74 and outperformed EPRS alone from approximately 0.13 to 0.74 in the 3-year setting (Fig. 10G). Incorporation of clinicopathological variables with epithelial plasticity-derived risk information therefore modestly improved individualized prognostic estimation in MIBC.

Discussion

MIBC remains clinically heterogeneous, and conventional clinicopathological variables do not fully capture that heterogeneity [36]. To address this gap, we built an epithelial plasticity-centered framework and derived EPRS from a 331-gene phenotype-guided candidate space. EPRS stratified survival in TCGA-MIBC and external cohorts, retained prognostic value when evaluated continuously, remained informative after adjustment for stromal score, immune score, tumor purity, clinicopathological variables, and TCGA mRNA subtype, and outperformed several representative published signatures in cross-cohort analyses. These results place epithelial plasticity as a measurable bulk transcriptomic axis in MIBC rather than a purely descriptive feature.

This pattern fits current views of bladder cancer biology. Basal-luminal and consensus molecular classifications have clarified MIBC taxonomy [3, 5], but discrete subtype labels do not fully capture state transitions. Lineage plasticity is increasingly linked to dedifferentiation, adaptation, and therapeutic resistance across epithelial cancers [37]. In our study, the high-risk state was enriched for ECM remodeling, wound-healing, inflammatory, and mesenchymal-associated programs, whereas the low-risk state retained stronger lipid and metabolic features. Direct comparison with TCGA mRNA subtype labels showed expected enrichment of high EPRS in basal/squamous tumors, but EPRS remained prognostic after subtype adjustment. This supports a graded basal-luminal/plasticity continuum that overlaps with, but is not identical to, established subtype categories, and it argues against interpreting EPRS as only a relabeled subtype assignment.

WGCNA and enrichment analyses reinforced this interpretation. One representative module was dominated by lipid and fatty-acid metabolism, whereas the opposing module emphasized epithelial proliferation, matrix interaction, and injury-response programs. GO, GSEA, KEGG, and GSVA converged on the same shift, with high-EPRS tumors enriched for collagen organization, ECM-receptor interaction, complement or coagulation, hypoxia, IL6-JAK-STAT3, and TNFA-NFKB signaling. Recurrent or aggressive bladder cancer has been linked to EMT-like remodeling and stemness maintenance [11–12]. Fibroblast-driven microenvironmental remodeling further supports this transition in advanced disease [38]. At the same time, luminal tumors can remain dependent on PPAR-gamma-linked lipid programs [39]. Preclinical studies also implicate SCD1, FASN, and glutamine metabolism as therapeutic vulnerabilities in bladder cancer [40–42]. The combined evidence places EPRS along a transition from a more differentiated metabolic state to a matrix-rich and inflammatory state that may carry distinct therapeutic liabilities.

The immune findings provide a second layer of interpretation. High-EPRS tumors were not immune deserts; they showed higher immune and stromal scores, broader infiltration signatures, stronger antigen-presentation signals, and widespread upregulation of inhibitory checkpoints and inflammatory mediators. Higher expression of TAP1, TAP2, B2M, and HLA class I genes together with stronger MHC-related IPS components indicates that antigen presentation is not simply lost in this state. Instead, partial antigen visibility coexists with stromal and myeloid suppressive programs. This helps explain why high-EPRS tumors can appear inflamed while still showing higher TIDE, dysfunction, and exclusion scores and lower predicted responder fractions. In urothelial carcinoma, EMT- and stroma-related programs have been associated with resistance to PD-1 blockade [9]. TGF-beta-associated ECM remodeling and fibroblast-linked exclusion provide a mechanistic framework for this pattern [10, 43]. FGFR3-associated immune suppression and myeloid-cell-driven stromal restraint further reinforce immune escape [14, 44]. Mass-cytometry studies likewise highlight suppressive immune ecosystems in bladder cancer [45–46], and bulk/single-cell integrative analyses reach a similar conclusion in immunotherapy-treated cohorts [47–48]. EPRS therefore appears to mark an inflamed but constrained immune state rather than an immunologically favorable one.

The single-cell data gave cellular resolution to the bulk-derived EPRS signal. Malignant epithelial cells occupied a continuous manifold shaped by lineage bias and plasticity rather than discrete basal and luminal endpoints. Scissor-based integration showed non-random distribution of phenotype-associated epithelial states across this manifold, while the basal-luminal/plasticity map provided the clearest summary of state organization. Monocle3 and CytoTRACE2 were informative as trajectory descriptors, but they did not collapse the epithelial landscape into a single linear progression. This is consistent with single-cell studies showing extensive intratumoral diversification and adaptive cell-state programs in bladder cancer [11–12]. Related work on urothelial-to-neural transdifferentiation and immunotherapy-linked integrative analyses further supports this plastic landscape [13, 49].

Two translational issues emerge from these analyses. First, Ridge was selected because it transferred more reproducibly across cohorts than models with higher apparent training performance but less stable external behavior. The current 331-gene coefficient vector is suitable for transcriptome-level or targeted high-plex expression platforms, but real-world implementation would require assay locking, platform harmonization, coefficient locking, prospective validation, and potentially model simplification into a smaller clinically feasible panel. Second, high EPRS was accompanied by recurrent upregulation of CD274, HAVCR2, NT5E, IDO1, TGFB1, CSF1R, and CXCL12. These findings suggest stromal, myeloid, or adenosine-related mechanisms that could be tested in future combination strategies, but the present data do not establish treatment-predictive utility [8, 10]. Emerging work on myeloid perturbation and CAF-derived CXCL12 signaling provides additional biological context for this hypothesis [44, 50–51].

The study has several limitations. This was a retrospective analysis of public datasets with heterogeneous platforms and incomplete clinical annotations. Although sensitivity analyses showed that alternative plasticity-index formulations were concordant with EPRS, the epithelial state space and candidate-gene filtering still depended on a curated marker panel and TCGA-based discovery pipeline. Sequential differential-expression, correlation, and survival filtering within the training cohort may introduce optimism; therefore, the external continuous-score validation and fixed-coefficient projection should be interpreted as mitigation rather than elimination of overfitting risk. Bulk RNA-seq from MIBC also contains non-tumor components, including stromal, smooth-muscle, and residual urothelial signals; because high EPRS tracks ECM expansion and stromal enrichment, some fraction of the score may reflect tissue admixture rather than malignant-cell plasticity alone [52]. Stromal, immune, tumor-purity, and subtype-adjusted analyses reduce this concern but do not replace spatial, histological, or purified-cell validation. The single-cell analysis was based on a limited number of samples, and Scissor label propagation showed only moderate classification performance (macro-F1 approximately 0.645); classification uncertainty may therefore influence the apparent boundaries between epithelial-state groups. These findings should therefore be regarded as supportive localization rather than definitive cellular validation. External immunotherapy validation was limited and statistically non-significant, and no perturbational, organoid, or in vivo/in vitro experiments were available to test causality. Prospective validation and assay harmonization will therefore be needed before EPRS can be considered for clinical deployment.

Taken as a whole, the analyses define EPRS as a bulk-transcriptomic readout of an epithelial-plasticity state in which matrix remodeling and immunoregulatory programs accompany adverse outcome in MIBC.

Conclusions

EPRS stratifies prognosis in MIBC and maps high-risk tumors to a matrix-rich, immune-regulated epithelial state. The score should next be tested prospectively and in biomarker-enriched treatment cohorts before clinical use.
