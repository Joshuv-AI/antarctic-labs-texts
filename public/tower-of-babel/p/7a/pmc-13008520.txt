Over-the-Counter Loperamide Overdose Resulting in Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Margaux Everingham, Ashley C Bolte, Elliott Goldberg, Avery Michienzi, Lindsay AL Bazydlo, Patrick L Stafford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOver-the-counter loperamide at high doses can lead to QT prolongation and cardiac arrest.Case SummaryWe present a case of a woman with cardiac arrest due to ventricular tachycardia storm. Overdrive pacing, antiarrhythmic medications, and deep sedation were used to manage the recurrent ventricular tachycardia. Untargeted urine testing helped determine the etiology of the arrest may be loperamide overdose, and this was later confirmed on patient interview and targeted testing. The patient was discharged without the need for an implanted defibrillator.DiscussionMultiple over-the-counter medications, such as loperamide and diphenhydramine, can result in QT prolongation at high doses and therefore can be a cause of arrhythmia and cardiac arrest.Take-Home MessagesIn cases of cardiac arrest in patients where overdose is suspected, untargeted urine testing through mass spectrometry may be helpful in determining the etiology to guide therapies and prevent unnecessary medical interventions.Key words: electrophysiology, loperamide overdose, overdrive pacing, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia storm

History of Presentation

A 47-year-old female presented as a transfer to a tertiary care center intensive care unit (ICU) for ventricular tachycardia (VT) storm. Emergency medical services initially responded to a fall at home. While with emergency medical service, the patient experienced cardiac arrest requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation and defibrillation. She was initially taken to a local community hospital where she had recurrent polymorphic VT and was admitted to their ICU. She was started on lidocaine and amiodarone infusions and intravenous magnesium sulfate. She continued to have runs of unstable polymorphic VT with her baseline rhythm reported as sinus tachycardia around 120 beats per minute with frequent ventricular extrasystoles. Due to her VT storm, she was intubated and sedated with propofol. She underwent cardiac catheterization and was found to have normal coronary arteries (Figure 1). During transit to the tertiary care center and upon arrival to the ICU, the patient required multiple further rounds of cardiopulmonary resuscitation and defibrillations.

•Despite potential for abuse and danger of QT prolongation and cardiac arrest at higher doses (>200 mg), loperamide remains available over-the-counter and can be purchased online in large quantities.

Cardiac Catheterization Revealed Normal Coronary Arteries

Ischemic heart disease was deemed an unlikely source of the ventricular tachycardia given normal coronary arteries seen on cardiac catheterization at the outside facility.

Past Medical History

Her past medical history includes anemia, hypothyroidism, chronic pain, gastric bypass surgery, and medication misuse with recent hospitalization for diphenhydramine overdose.

Of note, she presented to the emergency department on the day prior to her cardiac arrest with myalgias, anorexia, dyspnea, and chest pain. She had a CT scan of her abdomen and pelvis, which demonstrated nonspecific pancolitis and possible focal ileus of the transverse colon. Her electrocardiogram (ECG) showed PR and QT prolongation, as well as a new left bundle branch block compared to prior (Figure 2). High-sensitivity troponin was 2.7 ng/L, and BNP was 194 pg/mL (normal <73 pg/mL). She declined admission due to childcare responsibilities and was discharged home.

Differential Diagnosis

Given her presentation, history, and testing performed prior to transfer, acute coronary syndrome, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, toxic ingestion, and genetic and acquired channelopathies were considered. Her prolonged QT compared to a previously normal baseline, as well as her history of substance misuse, made an acquired long QT syndrome more likely. Her angiographically normal coronary arteries on cardiac catheterization made ischemic heart disease unlikely.

Investigations

The patient arrived intubated and sedated. Physical exam was notable for equal pupils at 3 mm bilaterally with minimal reactivity to light, moist mucous membranes, and active bowel sounds. She did not have clonus and was not rigid, and patellar reflexes were absent.

Basic labs on arrival were relatively unremarkable, including a potassium of 3.9 mmol/L, magnesium at 3.9 mg/dL, and mild microcytic anemia with a hemoglobin of 11.6 g/dL. High-sensitivity troponin was elevated at 35.5 ng/L compared to the normal level the day prior and peaked at 85.0 ng/L early the next morning. Thyroid-stimulating hormone was unremarkable at 2.19 mIU/L. BNP was further elevated compared to the prior day at 923 pg/mL. Bicarbonate, anion gap, and lactic acid were all within normal ranges.

An ECG during a period of sinus rhythm demonstrated marked PR, QRS, and QT prolongation with ventricular bigeminy (Figure 3). Transthoracic echocardiogram was obtained that showed a severely reduced ejection fraction to 15% to 20%, as compared to a prior normal echocardiogram.

Given her prior history of medication misuse, the toxicology team was consulted. A urine sample was sent for untargeted testing using mass spectrometry, which eventually revealed the presence of both diphenhydramine and loperamide (Figure 4).

Management

Upon arrival to our ICU, advanced cardiac life support was continued due to frequent episodes of pulseless polymorphic VT. Due to QT prolongation and suspicion for a toxic overdose, amiodarone was stopped on arrival given its QT prolonging effects, whereas lidocaine and IV magnesium were continued. The patient was deeply sedated using propofol, midazolam, and fentanyl. Due to the initial concern for diphenhydramine overdose, she was also started on a bicarbonate drip. A temporary transvenous pacemaker was placed bedside, and overdrive pacing was used to suppress further episodes of VT (Figure 5).7,8 Her VT subsided, and deep sedation was maintained for several days. Daily ECGs and continuous cardiac telemetry were notable for gradual normalization of the QT interval and narrowing of the QRS complex.

Electrocardiogram After Overdrive Pacing Was Accomplished

Further episodes of ventricular tachycardia were suppressed.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was obtained, which showed atypical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with asymmetric basal to mid-anterior and anteroseptal hypertrophy without late gadolinium enhancement or evidence of obstruction (Figure 6). Genetic testing revealed 2 variants of uncertain significance: ANK2 and TMEM43, but no specific genetic mutations associated with QT prolongation.

Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) Did Not Reveal an Etiology for Ventricular Tachycardia

The MRI showed atypical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy but no inflammation or scar to explain nidus for ventricular tachycardia.

Subsequent targeted send-out blood levels showed undetectable diphenhydramine levels, an elevated loperamide level of 6.8 ng/mL (reporting limit is 5 ng/mL), and an elevated desmethylloperamide level of 99 mg/mL, which is an inactivated metabolite of loperamide (reporting limit is 5 ng/mL).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was successfully extubated 3 days after arrival and was neurologically intact despite over 15 cardiac arrests requiring defibrillations. When awake and oriented, she confirmed that she had been taking 100 pills (five 2-mg pills every hour) of loperamide daily for 8 days prior to presentation due to diarrhea and reported dumping syndrome in the setting of prior gastric bypass. She reported purchasing these medications on the internet. She was unaware of the risks associated with increasing amounts of loperamide.

Her temporary pacemaker was removed once her ECG intervals normalized. On repeat transthoracic echocardiogram, there was total normalization of the left ventricular ejection fraction, and she was discharged without guideline directed medical therapy.

Discussion

In this case, the patient recovered from multiple cardiac arrests in the setting of VT storm due to loperamide overdose. Multiple etiologies of VT storm were investigated in this case, including genetic testing, coronary angiography, and cardiac MRI, eliminating myocardial ischemia, myocarditis, known genetic condition, or nonischemic cardiomyopathy as causes.

Ultimately, the untargeted testing of the patient's urine and resultant mass spectrometry results shaped the outcome of this case. At our hospital, the clinical toxicology lab has the ability to analyze urine samples using liquid chromatography-quadruple time of flight mass spectrometry. An untargeted approach is used to identify substances based on mass accuracy and fragmentation patterns matched against a library of known compounds. The results of the untargeted testing were available within 1 day of the patient's presentation, compared to the targeted confirmatory testing which returned multiple weeks later. While these results are not quantitative, they were rapidly available to guide clinical decision-making on implantable cardioverter defibrillator placement.

Loperamide in high doses inhibits fast sodium and delayed rectifier potassium channels. Thereby it produces QRS widening, QT prolongation, and ventricular arrhythmias. The recommended maximum daily dose of loperamide is 8 mg over-the-counter and 16 mg by prescription. Approximately 40% of the drug is absorbed into the bloodstream after oral administration, and then it is metabolized to the inactive byproducts (including desmethylloperamide), which are eliminated through the urine and stool. Therapeutic concentrations in the blood and plasma are up to 3 ng/mL. Our patient's loperamide and desmethylloperamide levels of 6.8 ng/mL and 99 ng/mL, respectively, are far above what would be expected from therapeutic use.1 A 2- to 4-mg dose of loperamide will cause a serum peak loperamide level of 0.24 to 0.62 ng/mL at 5 to 6 hours after administration and a desmethylloperamide level of 13 ng/mL at around 11 hours after administration.2

Loperamide is also a mu-opioid receptor agonist. At high doses (100-200 mg), the drug can penetrate the central nervous system and produce euphoric effects.3 From 2010 to 2015, the National Poison Center Data System saw a 91% increase in reported loperamide exposures, with 15 recorded deaths.4 All cases with cardiac toxicity occurred at doses >200 mg. This led to a change in regulations in 2019 that limited the over-the-counter purchase of loperamide to 24 2 mg tablets per blister-pack.4 However, larger quantities of loperamide can still be purchased in bottles on the internet.

This patient had also been taking diphenhydramine. Similar to loperamide, diphenhydramine inhibits fast sodium channels and delayed rectifier potassium channels, which amplifies dispersion of ventricular repolarization and QT prolongation.5 However, since our patient was seen the previous day without signs of antimuscarinic toxidrome such as mydriasis, anhidrosis, or urinary retention,6 loperamide was suspected as the causative agent. It would be unusual to develop cardiac diphenhydramine toxicity without overt evidence of an antimuscarinic toxidrome or central nervous system depression. Diphenhydramine levels sent the day after presentation were undetectable.

Conclusions

This case of cardiac arrest and VT storm due to loperamide overdose demonstrates the continued danger of certain over-the-counter drugs despite attempts at regulation. Even when toxic ingestion is suspected, other etiologies of prolonged QT should be considered and ruled out including acute coronary syndrome, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, and genetic long QT syndrome. Although not widely available, untargeted urine studies can be used to narrow the differential in cases of suspected toxic ingestion and provide guidance for management decisions.
