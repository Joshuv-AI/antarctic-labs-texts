Comparison of 3D model–based and slide-based teaching for afferent and efferent spinal cord pathways: a pre-test/post-test study
Authors: Ayşe Ömerli̇, Hatice Kübra Aslantaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding the organization of afferent and efferent spinal cord pathways can be challenging for students due to their complex spatial relationships. This study aimed to develop a three-dimensional (3D) educational model demonstrating these pathways and to compare student learning outcomes between model-based instruction and conventional slide-based teaching.MethodsA pre-test/post-test educational study was conducted with second-year dental students (n = 60). Transverse sections of the C4 and T7 vertebrae were designed and 3D-printed at four times their anatomical size. A spinal cord model made of modeling clay was placed within the vertebral canal, and afferent and efferent pathways were represented using color-coded cables. Students completed a 15-item multiple-choice pre-test assessing baseline knowledge of spinal cord pathways and were allocated into two groups. Group 1 (n = 30) received conventional slide-based instruction, while Group 2 (n = 30) was taught using the 3D model. After the instructional sessions, students completed the same test as a post-test. Within-group and between-group comparisons were analyzed using paired-sample and independent-sample t-tests.ResultsBoth instructional approaches resulted in significant improvements in post-test scores. In the slide-based instruction group, mean scores increased from 5.00 ± 1.98 to 8.47 ± 2.54 (p < 0.001). In the 3D model group, mean scores increased from 4.77 ± 2.39 to 10.27 ± 2.29 (p < 0.001). The mean improvement was significantly greater in the 3D model group (5.50 ± 3.07) compared with the slide-based group (3.47 ± 2.70) (p = 0.007). The effect size for the difference between groups was moderate to large (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.70).ConclusionBoth instructional methods improved short-term knowledge of spinal cord pathways; however, model-based instruction was associated with greater learning gains. These findings suggest that three-dimensional models may serve as a useful supplementary tool in neuroanatomy education. Further studies using validated assessment tools and evaluating long-term knowledge retention are needed to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09398-y.

Introduction

Understanding the organization of afferent and efferent pathways within the spinal cord is fundamental for interpreting neurological function and clinical disorders. However, these neural pathways involve complex three-dimensional spatial relationships that are often difficult for students to visualize using traditional teaching materials [1].

Conventional anatomy teaching frequently relies on lectures and two-dimensional images, which may limit students’ spatial understanding of neural pathways. Although theoretical descriptions of spinal cord structures and pathways are well established, students often struggle to understand the spatial organization of these tracts within the vertebral canal [2, 3].

Three-dimensional (3D) educational tools have increasingly been introduced in anatomy education to facilitate visualization of complex anatomical structures and to improve spatial comprehension. Physical models allow learners to observe anatomical relationships from multiple perspectives and may support the integration of theoretical knowledge with structural understanding [1].

Despite these educational approaches, representing spinal cord pathways—particularly the relationships between afferent and efferent tracts—remains challenging using conventional models and two-dimensional images [3–5]. Therefore, innovative teaching tools may help improve students’ comprehension of these neural pathways.

The aim of the present study was to develop enlarged three-dimensional vertebral sections demonstrating afferent and efferent spinal cord pathways and to compare the effectiveness of model-based instruction with traditional slide-based teaching using a pre-test/post-test design. We hypothesized that instruction using the 3D model would result in greater improvement in student learning outcomes compared with conventional theoretical instruction.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This educational study employed a pre-test/post-test comparative design to evaluate the effectiveness of a three-dimensional (3D) educational model in teaching afferent and efferent spinal cord pathways. The study was conducted in the Anatomy Laboratory of the Faculty of Dentistry at Nuh Naci Yazgan University.

The project was supported by The Scientific and Technological Research Council of Türkiye (TÜBİTAK) 2209-A University Students Research Projects Support Program (Project No: TBTK-0120-1978).

Preparation of anatomical materials

A three-dimensional educational model representing spinal cord pathways was developed for instructional use. The model consisted of vertebral sections enlarged fourfold relative to their natural anatomical size in order to enhance visualization of internal structures.

Digital models of the C4 and T7 vertebrae were created using ZBrush software based on computed tomography (CT) references. The 3D-printed models of the C4 and T7 vertebrae are shown in Figs. 1 and 2. The models were subsequently produced using a Flament-brand 3D printer with Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene (ABS) filament. ABS material was selected due to its durability and its compatibility with acetone vapor smoothing, which allowed refinement of the printed surfaces.

Implementation of the course

The course was implemented with second-year students (n = 60) enrolled in the Faculty of Dentistry at Nuh Naci Yazgan University.

Before the instructional intervention, all participants completed a 15-item multiple-choice pre-test designed to assess baseline knowledge of afferent and efferent spinal cord pathways.

Following the pre-test, students were allocated into two equal groups (Group 1, n = 30; Group 2, n = 30). The allocation was based on existing classroom organization rather than formal randomization.

Group 1 received conventional slide-based instruction that included diagrams and theoretical explanations of afferent and efferent pathways and their associated sensory and motor functions. Group 2 received instruction using the 3D-printed vertebral model, which allowed students to directly visualize the spinal pathways and interact with the model during the teaching session.

Both groups were taught by the same instructor to ensure instructional consistency. The duration of the teaching session and the learning objectives were standardized across groups. The only difference between groups was the instructional modality (traditional slide-based teaching versus 3D model-based teaching).

After completion of the instructional sessions, both groups completed a post-test identical to the pre-test.

Assessment tool

Student knowledge of afferent and efferent spinal cord pathways was assessed using a questionnaire consisting of 15 multiple-choice questions.

The questions were developed based on neuroanatomy course learning objectives and standard anatomical references related to spinal cord pathways. Each correct response was assigned one point, resulting in a total possible score ranging from 0 to 15.

The same questionnaire was administered both before instruction (pre-test) and immediately after the teaching session (post-test) in order to evaluate short-term knowledge acquisition.

The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and was reviewed by two anatomy faculty members to ensure content relevance and alignment with the course learning objectives. However, it was not previously validated through formal psychometric testing. Therefore, potential limitations related to instrument validation are acknowledged.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the TURCOSA cloud (Turcosa Analytics Ltd. Co., Turkey) statistical software (https://turcosa.com.tr/). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Within-group comparisons between pre-test and post-test scores were analyzed using paired-sample t-tests. Between-group comparisons of score improvements (post-test minus pre-test) were evaluated using independent-sample t-tests.

Results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to evaluate the magnitude of the observed differences. In addition, 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for the mean differences between groups.

Potential confounding variables such as prior exposure to neuroanatomy content were minimized by including only students without previous formal instruction on spinal cord pathways.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 60 second-year dental students completed both the pre-test and post-test assessments. Thirty students were included in the slide-based instruction group and thirty students in the 3D model-based instruction group.

Pre-test scores

The mean pre-test score was 5.00 ± 1.98 in the slide-based instruction group and 4.77 ± 2.39 in the 3D model group.

Post-test scores

The mean post-test score was 8.47 ± 2.54 in the slide-based instruction group and 10.27 ± 2.29 in the 3D model group.

Within-group changes (post-test minus pre-test)

In the slide-based instruction group, the mean improvement was 3.47 ± 2.70 (paired-sample t-test, p < 0.001; 95% CI: 2.46 to 4.48).

In the 3D model group, the mean improvement was 5.50 ± 3.07 (paired-sample t-test, p < 0.001; 95% CI: 4.35 to 6.65).

Between-group comparison of improvement

The mean difference in improvement between the 3D model group and the slide-based group was 2.03 (independent-sample t-test, p = 0.007; 95% CI: 0.54 to 3.53). The effect size for the between-group difference in improvement was Cohen’s d ≈ 0.70.

Assumption checks

Normality of the improvement scores was supported by the Shapiro–Wilk test in both groups (p > 0.05). Therefore, parametric tests were considered appropriate for the analysis.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the effectiveness of a three-dimensional educational model in teaching afferent and efferent spinal cord pathways using a pre-test/post-test design. Both instructional approaches resulted in improvements in post-test scores; however, students who received instruction using the 3D model demonstrated greater short-term score improvements compared with those who received slide-based instruction.

These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that three-dimensional models can improve the visualization of complex anatomical structures and support spatial learning in anatomy education [6, 7]. Similar improvements in spatial understanding have been reported in studies evaluating the use of 3D printed models in medical and anatomy education [6–9]. One possible explanation for the observed improvement is that three-dimensional models facilitate spatial cognition by transforming abstract anatomical descriptions into tangible visual representations. Physical models allow students to examine structures from multiple perspectives and interact directly with anatomical components, which may enhance comprehension of spatial relationships [8, 9].

Neuroanatomical pathways are particularly difficult for students to conceptualize because they involve multidirectional spatial relationships that are not easily represented using two-dimensional images [10].

However, the findings of the present study should be interpreted with caution. The study assessed only short-term learning outcomes measured immediately after the instructional intervention. Therefore, the results do not provide evidence regarding long-term knowledge retention, which is an important component of meaningful learning [11].

Another limitation is related to the assessment tool. The questionnaire used in this study was developed specifically for the purposes of this research and did not undergo formal psychometric validation. Although the questions were based on course learning objectives, the lack of a validated instrument may affect the reliability of the measurement [12].

The novelty effect should also be considered. Students exposed to new educational technologies or instructional tools may demonstrate improved engagement simply because the learning method differs from traditional approaches [13]. This increased engagement may partially contribute to short-term performance improvements.

Instructor-related influences represent another potential source of bias. Although the same instructor delivered both teaching sessions and the duration and learning objectives were standardized, subtle differences in teaching style or classroom interaction may influence student learning outcomes [14].

Furthermore, students were allocated into groups based on existing classroom organization rather than formal randomization. This approach may introduce potential group differences that were not fully controlled.

Despite these limitations, the findings suggest that the integration of three-dimensional physical models may serve as a useful supplementary visualization tool in neuroanatomy education. From an educational perspective, low-cost physical models such as the one developed in this study may provide an accessible tool for improving spatial understanding of complex neuroanatomical pathways, particularly in institutions with limited access to cadaveric resources. Future studies involving larger student cohorts, validated assessment instruments, and long-term retention assessments would help clarify the broader educational implications of such models.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted with a single cohort of students from one institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, only short-term learning outcomes were assessed, and long-term knowledge retention was not evaluated.

Third, the assessment questionnaire used in this study was developed specifically for the research and did not undergo formal psychometric validation, which may influence measurement reliability.

In addition, students were allocated into groups based on existing classroom organization rather than formal randomization. Although the same instructor delivered both instructional sessions and teaching duration was standardized, potential instructor-related influences cannot be completely excluded.

Another possible limitation is the novelty effect, as students exposed to a new instructional tool such as a three-dimensional model may demonstrate increased engagement compared with conventional teaching methods.

Additionally, the 3D model included a simplified representation of spinal pathways, which may not fully capture the full anatomical complexity of the spinal cord.

Another limitation of this study is that the internal consistency of the questionnaire could not be assessed, as item-level response data were not retained and only total test scores were available for analysis.

Conclusion

The present study evaluated the use of a three-dimensional educational model for teaching afferent and efferent spinal cord pathways using a pre-test/post-test design. The results indicated that students who received instruction with the 3D model demonstrated greater short-term improvements in test scores compared with those who received conventional slide-based instruction.

However, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. The study assessed immediate learning outcomes only and did not evaluate long-term knowledge retention. In addition, the assessment instrument used in this study was developed specifically for this research and did not undergo formal psychometric validation.

Future studies should investigate the long-term educational impact of three-dimensional anatomical models using delayed post-tests and validated assessment tools. Multi-center studies involving larger student cohorts may also help clarify the generalizability of these findings and further evaluate the role of 3D models in neuroanatomy education.
