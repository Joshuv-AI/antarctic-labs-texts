Exploration-based feedback for BCI training: a case study with an adolescent with paraplegia
Authors: Nicolas Ivanov, Tom Chau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeBrain–computer interface (BCI) inefficiency limits clinical utilization of BCIs, as many users struggle to produce consistent machine-recognizable electroencephalography (EEG) patterns for reliable control. While training can improve BCI performance for most users, the required duration and intensity may hinder BCI accessibility. Prior BCI and motor learning research suggests that feedback enabling efficient exploration of different task strategies may enhance training.MethodsAn eight-session BCI training case study was completed with an adolescent participant with paraplegia. To support training, a novel feedback system that visualized EEG signal pattern states identified via K-means clustering within the EEG covariance space. Unlike common classifier feedback, this interface presented the EEG signal patterns produced throughout each trial, allowing the participant to explore strategies that yielded task-specific pattern states.ResultsThe participant was initially a low-performing user and showed little progress across the first five sessions. After transitioning to a simplified feedback mode emphasizing deviation from resting state patterns in the sixth session, the participant displayed significant improvement in task-related physiological signal discriminability. Post-training analysis, however, revealed that this improvement was partially attributable to electromyography (EMG) activity from cranial muscles.ConclusionAlthough the observed gains were not solely attributable to neuro-cortical signal modulation, the case study highlights the potential of simplified feedback to support task exploration in low-performing users and presents potential implications for hybrid EEG-EMG BCIs for relevant clinical populations.

Background

Electroencephalography (EEG)-based brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) hold promise as assistive technologies for individuals with severe motor disabilities [1, 2]. However, clinical adoption has been limited because many users fail to produce machine-discriminable EEG signals [3–5]. It is estimated that up to 50% of individuals experience “BCI inefficiency,” where the computer decoder cannot reliably distinguish between the user’s mental control tasks [3, 4, 6].

Many studies have found that users can often improve their ability to induce targeted neural activity and produce more easily discriminable signals with training [7–14]. Simultaneously, however, there have been several cases where limited progress has been achieved even after extended training [15–17]. Because BCI training can be mentally exhausting and poor performance and high effort often lead to assistive device abandonment [18], there has been considerable motivation to develop more effective user-training methods [5, 9, 19, 20].

Early BCIs relied on operant conditioning, requiring users to discover mental strategies that elicited target neural responses through an exploratory trail-and-error process [21–23]. Machine learning-based approaches later enabled significantly faster calibration by automatically identifying user-specific EEG patterns [24, 25], and classifier-based feedback became the dominant user training paradigm. However, for low-performing users, classifier feedback often appears random because the models are conditioned on poorly discriminable data [26, 27], thereby limiting the instructional value of the feedback [28, 29].

Recent work has revisited exploratory, operant-style feedback methods that guide users toward distinct and stable EEG patterns rather than solely providing task recognition-based feedback. For example, training feedback provided as real-time cortical maps [30] and low-dimensional visualization of task distributions [17] have been shown to improve performance in initially low-performing users. Moreover, [31] found participants achieved significant performance improvements when receiving feedback from classifiers initialized using high-performance user data.

A shared goal among these approaches is to support efficient exploration of mental strategies during early learning. Contrary to feedback from poorly-conditioned classifiers which can incorrectly connote a degree of “correctness” for each trial, these approaches convey when users produce similar patterns for multiple tasks (i.e., similar heatmaps, data point projections, or stable classifier outputs). Consequently, for initially low performance users, these feedback methods provide trial-wise feedback that can indicate when a user has achieved EEG signal patterns that differ from patterns that they previously generated. This type of exploratory learning supported by action-perception feedback loops is believed to play an important role in skill learning [32, 33].

With the aim of enabling efficient exploration of mental task strategies during early stages of learning, we developed a novel training protocol and feedback system. This approach, summarized in Fig. 1, builds on unsupervised EEG clustering techniques [34, 35] that define a discrete state space of K+1 EEG pattern states: K states derived from prior data and an additional state representing statistically dissimilar patterns. As the segmentation is derived using unsupervised methods, the model does not attempt to identify associations between the states and any single task. Therefore, states in this model have the flexibility to be associated with multiple tasks or to be task-specific. Similarly, some tasks may commonly yield multiple pattern states [34–36].

This model flexibility provides agency for the user to explore task execution approaches and form the associations between their intrinsic senses related to task execution and the EEG pattern state outcome. More concretely, during training, short EEG epochs are classified into one of the K+1 pattern states, and users receive feedback indicating the current state. Unlike classifier feedback, this approach does not signal task correctness but instead encourages users to discover strategies that yield stable, task-consistent, and mutually distinct EEG patterns. Unlike the exploratory feedback approaches discussed above, this approach removes the requirement for users to generate an a priori defined signal response via preselected tasks. As an initial evaluation of the system, we recruited an adolescent with paraplegia who initially exhibited low BCI performance (i.e., someone likely to experience BCI inefficiency) to complete the training protocol. Herein, we describe the participant’s outcomes and experience across training sessions.

Methods

Participant

The participant was a 17-year-old right-handed male who sustained a T6 level traumatic spinal cord injury, classified as ASIA grade B (incomplete; sensory but not motor function preserved below the lesion) [39], 19 weeks prior to the beginning of the study. He had no prior neurodevelopmental diagnoses, used a manual wheelchair, and regularly attended physiotherapy and occupational therapy sessions during the study. His only previous BCI experience was a recreational activity not involving mental-task control.

EEG equipment and data

EEG was recorded using a R-Net electrode system and LiveAmp amplifier (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). Data were analyzed from the following 18 electrode positions placed according to the international 10–10 system: Fz, FC1, FC2, T7, C3, Cz, C4, T8, CP5, CP1, CP2, CP6, P3, Pz, P4, O1, Oz, and O2. Ground and reference electrodes were placed at FPz and FCz, respectively. Data were sampled at 500 Hz.

Sessional overview

The participant completed 8 sessions (1 strictly for data collection, 7 for user training) over 18 days. Each session took place on a unique day and was approximately 2 h in length, including EEG setup time. Sessions were scheduled at times selected by the participant to adhere to his availability.

Prior to beginning the training, the researchers explained the goal of the sessions (i.e., to identify and practice mental task approaches that produce discriminable EEG signals) to the participant. Additionally, the participant was instructed not to move or intentionally activate any muscles while performing the mental tasks. During EEG setup, he was shown his EEG recordings and asked to perform ocular movements and clenching of his jaw to visualize how the EEG is impacted by the electromyography (EMG) signals produced by these movements. Trials where movements were observed were excluded from analysis. After an observed movement, the researchers paused the session and reminded the participant not to move.

Session 1

The purpose of session 1 was to collect EEG data for generating the initial EEG pattern state space and for selecting the mental control tasks to be used throughout the training. No feedback was provided during this session.

The participant completed 192 mental imagery task trials across four 48-trial blocks. Session 1 mental imagery tasks

Before session 2, task discriminability metrics [26] were computed for different combinations of two active mental imagery tasks and mental rest (MR). All data were normalized using the covariance rebiasing technique (see “Covariance re-centering” section) to limit the impact of inter-block signal nonstationarities on the metrics. These results were reviewed with the participant at the beginning of session 2. The participant was then asked to consider both the discriminability results and his task preferences to select two mental tasks to use for the training sessions. The participant initially selected right hand motor imagery (RMI) and spatial navigation (SN). After session 3, however, he reported difficulty performing the SN task consistently and replaced it with the categorical word generation (CWG) task.

Structured practice blocks

Following the baseline recording, the participant completed two structured practice and four free practice blocks. During the structured practice blocks the participant was cued to begin a mental imagery task at a specific time and then received feedback from the computer during the trial. These blocks consisted of 36 trials (12 trials for each of the MR and two active mental imagery tasks) following the timing structure shown in Fig. 2. Task sequences within each block were randomized subject to the constraint that every consecutive triplet (trials 1–3, 4–6, etc.) included one instance of each task.

Throughout these trials, the participant received feedback that displayed the temporal evolution of his observed EEG pattern states (Fig. 1c; see “Online signal processing” section for signal processing and pattern state details). The pattern state information was conveyed through both a visual interface, depicted in Fig. 4, and vibrotactile feedback. The visual interface consisted of K+1 light bulbs, each one representing one the K+1 pattern states, whose brightness increased according to the number of within-trial temporal epochs in which that state was observed [34–36]. At the start of each trial, all bulbs were off. Within trials, each trial epoch was assigned to a pattern state and the corresponding light bulb would increase in brightness. A light bulb would reach maximum brightness only if every epoch within a trial was assigned to the cognate state. The brightness level of each light would not decrease until all lights were reset at the beginning of the next trial. Simultaneously, the participant also received tactile feedback from K+1 ≤ 5 vibration motors secured to the tips of the fingers of both hands. Like the light bulbs, each motor-finger pairing represented a different pattern state. During a trial, the motor representing the pattern state of the most recent epoch would vibrate; all other motors would remain off. The vibration frequency of the motors was not within the range of sensorimotor activity (8–30 Hz) [40, 41] and therefore not expected to induce EEG activity that would confound pattern state classification [42].

Before the first structured practice block, the participant was instructed on how to interpret the feedback and viewed a video illustrating the visual feedback. He was then asked to verify his understanding of the feedback.

At the conclusion of each structured practice block, summative performance metrics—run-wise classification accuracy and classDistinct [26]—were computed and reviewed with the participant. These summative metrics complemented the pattern state-based feedback by indicating the extent to which EEG signals from different tasks could be distinguished. Following suggested guidelines for effective BCI feedback [20, 28], this review contextualized the trial-wise feedback within the broader training goal of improving task classification accuracy and highlighted the gap between current and target performance.

Free practice blocks

The 4-minute free practice blocks were implemented to follow recommendations from reviews and critical analyses of BCI training schemes [5, 20, 28]. Specifically, these blocks were self-paced (i.e., the participant dictated the start and duration of practice periods) and the participant selected the subset of tasks to practice. Additionally, the participant could select between two feedback modes: (1) synchronous mode where trials were cued and feedback presented as in the structured practice blocks or (2) asynchronous mode where no cues were presented and the visual (light bulb) and tactile (motor) feedback continuously indicated the current EEG pattern state (updated every 0.5 s). The asynchronous mode provided opportunity for the participant to attempt different task strategies and observe the associated pattern state outcomes without rigid timing constraints. The participant was allowed to alter the practice task subset or feedback mode at any time throughout the block. For example, during a single free practice block the participant could practice right hand motor imagery in asynchronous mode for 1.5 min, take a 30 s break, and finally practice all three tasks in synchronous mode for 2 min.

At the beginning of each of the structured and free practice blocks, the participant completed 5 trials of mental rest without receiving any feedback. These trials were used to calibrate the rebias transformation as described within the “Covariance re-centering” section.

No feedback and BrainDriver blocks

Following the structured and free practice blocks, the participant completed a synchronous block of trials where he received no feedback. These blocks consisted of the same number of trials (36) and timing structure as the structured practice blocks. These blocks were not intended to provide instructive training but rather to assess whether the participant’s performance was dependent upon the feedback.

Finally, the sessions concluded with a second baseline measurement and the participant playing the BCI Cybathlon competition BrainDriver game.1 Within the proposed training protocol, the game provided both a motivation goal for the participant and, similarly to the no feedback blocks, an opportunity to assess performance in the absence of the primary training feedback. Because we trained the participant with only three tasks, we removed all “headlight” sections from the race tracks so the participant only needed to produce three commands: (i) straight/neutral (MR), (ii) right turn (RMI), and (iii) left turn (SN/CWG). The participant completed 1 race in each of sessions 2 and 3 and 2 races in session 4. Due to scheduling constraints, the participant did not complete any races in session 5.

Sessions 6–8

Sessions 6–8 followed the same block structure as sessions 2–5 (Fig. 3); however, a modification was made to the pattern state-based feedback presented during the structured and free practice blocks. The modification was made following analysis of the performance metrics from sessions 2–5. The participant was experiencing BCI inefficiency (low task discriminability, chance-level classification accuracy) and had demonstrated no significant performance improvement through sessions 2–5. Analysis of his performance self-assessments (“User experience questionnaires” section) indicated that the multi-pattern state feedback was challenging to interpret because no clear task-pattern state associations emerged. Indeed, reviewing measures of task discriminability and the ratio of inter-task to intra-task variance, it appeared likely that the participant was not consistently producing any consciously controlled task-related EEG modulations.

Therefore, the pattern state model was changed to comprise of only one state (i.e., K=1) that represented the resting state EEG activity that the participant produced during the preceding sessions. Correspondingly, feedback was simplified to two light bulbs and two motors: one light bulb-motor pair activated when EEG patterns resembled those from prior sessions, and the other when they differed significantly (signal processing details in the “Single pattern state deviation” section).

To improve the interpretability of the feedback, visual feedback was made continuous by scaling bulb brightness according to EEG similarity with the resting-state template (Fig. 5; brightness level calculation details in the “Single pattern state deviation” section). Unlike the multi-state version, brightness was updated using only the most recent epoch. Vibrotactile feedback remained binary (ON/OFF).

All other procedures matched those of sessions 2–5. Artifact rejection

Each re-centered epoch covariance matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{C}_e^\prime $$\end{document} was subjected to a Riemannian potato anomaly detector [47] with z-score threshold set to 3.5. The model for each session was initialized using re-centered epoch covariance matrices from previous sessions. The selected threshold exceeded the typical single session values of 2.5-−3.0 [47, 48] because inter-session EEG nonstationarities resulted in baseline differences between the current and previous session. If a trial epoch was deemed to be an outlier, it was not used to update the feedback nor was it included in any rebias transformation updates.

BrainDriver classifier

The minimum Riemannian distance to the mean with geodesic filtering (FgMDM) [49] method was used to classify tasks during the BrainDriver game. As depicted in Fig. 6, the classifications were made using covariance matrices as inputs; therefore, all preprocessing steps (blue blocks in Fig. 6) were identical to other feedback blocks. The classifier was initialized using trials from the structured practice and no feedback blocks from the current session.

User experience questionnaires

To assess the participant’s interpretation of the feedback, he completed a performance self-assessment following the structured and free practice blocks. The questionnaire asked him to self-assess within the following categories: (i) discriminability of EEG signals for each mental task, (ii) consistency of EEG signals for each mental task, and (iii) predicted classification accuracy if the session’s trials had been subjected to a classifier.

To assess the participant’s motivation he completed the BCI variant of the Questionnaire on Current Motivation (QCM-BCI) [50, 51] at the beginning of each session. Finally, to assess the participant’s experience performing the training and using the feedback interfaces, he completed the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) [52] and a Subjective Experience questionnaire which asked him to rate how difficult, tiring, and enjoyable the session was on a five-point Likert scale at the end of each session. At the end of the final session, the participant was also invited to provide feedback about his experience throughout the entire study.

Post-training analysis

All post-training analyses were conducted on full 7 s trials from the structured practice, no feedback, and synchronous portions of free practice blocks. Trials from consecutive free practice blocks (i.e., blocks 3–4 and 5–6) were grouped together for block-wise analyses.

Preprocessing and artifact rejection

Unless otherwise specified, all trials were zero-phase bandpass filtered between 8 and 30 Hz using a fourth order Butterworth filter prior to analysis. Then all trials were subjected to the four-step artifact rejection procedure outlined below. Note that the second structured practice block of session 2 was excluded from analysis due to the plurality of trials rejected.

First, trials where the researchers observed participant movements during the sessions were excluded. Second, any bandpass filtered trials containing absolute voltage amplitudes exceeding 70 µV were removed. Third, trials with excessive EMG artifacts were removed according to [53]. All raw trials were filtered using a 1–120 Hz bandpass and 60 Hz notch filter. Then, a first-degree polynomial was fit to the log-log power spectral density (PSD) vs. frequency plot between 7 and 45 Hz. Any trial where the slope of the estimated polynomial exceeded a threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{th}=0.32$$\end{document} were excluded. The value for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _{th}$$\end{document} was derived as the slope value representing a 3 decibel power increase between 7 Hz (where EMG contamination is expected to be less prominent) and 45 Hz (where EMG would be expected to dominate EEG). The remaining trials in each block were assessed with a Riemannian potato anomaly detector (z-score threshold = 2.5) to remove anomalous trials [47, 48].

After excluding rejected trials, covariance matrices for each trial were computed and re-centered according to (1) using the mean of all trial covariance matrices within the same block.

Longitudinal task discriminability

To evaluate the participant’s performance throughout the sessions, a z-score-based variant of the classDistinct metric [26] was computed for each block. First, the classDistinct metric for a block was computed using the true task labels. Then, a null distribution was estimated by re-computing the metric using 1000 random permutations of the labels. A z-score was computed for each block by subtracting the mean of the null distribution from the true observed classDistinct and scaling by the standard deviation of the null distribution. This modification facilitated comparison between blocks with differing numbers of trials (i.e., structured vs. free practice blocks) and reduced the metric’s sensitivity to outlier trials. Only free practice blocks where the participant completed synchronous practice of all three tasks were included in this analysis.

Longitudinal performance changes were also assessed by computing 10-times repeated three-fold cross-validation accuracy with a FgMDM classifier for each session. A null distribution of chance level accuracies was derived by repeating the 10-times repeated three-fold cross-validation process with 1000 random permutations of the task labels. Significant (p<0.05) sessional accuracies were identified by comparing against the critical values of this null distribution. Note that during the cross-validation accuracy analysis, the covariance re-centering transformations were computed using data from the training fold only and applied to the data within the held out fold.

Participant experience

Figure 14 depicts the results of the QCM-BCI, NASA-TLX, and Subjective Experience surveys. QCM-BCI results were consistent throughout the sessions and indicated favorable motivation scores for BCI training [50] with high interest in the training, high confidence that he would be successful in learning how to use the BCI, strong belief that the training would be challenging, and moderate-to-low fear of incompetence.

Notably, the NASA-TLX frustration and performance (lower performance scores indicate better performance) scores peaked within sessions 3–5 when the participant was struggling to make significant improvement. Scores for both of these scales decreased in session 6–8 when the participant began achieving more favorable feedback results with the single-pattern state system. Following the feedback change, there was a slight increase in the mental and temporal demand reported by the participant; however, both of these decreased by session 8. The initial increase may have been a response to having to perform the tasks while adapting to and learning a new feedback interface.

The decrease in difficulty and fatigue aligns with the downward trends in mental and temporal demand in NASA-TLX responses for sessions 6–8. Discussion

Analysis of both online feedback and offline post-training task discriminability-based measures suggested that the participant achieved increases in the machine-discernibility of physiological signals associated with active mental tasks versus their resting-state baseline over the course of the training sessions, particularly in sessions 6–8. Post-training analysis suggested that a prominent component differentiating mental task trials from resting state were EMG signals originating from low intensity activation of scalp muscles. Consequently, while conclusions regarding the effectiveness of the training protocol and feedback for training purely EEG-based BCI control cannot be drawn from the results of this study, the feedback did appear to support the development of multi-model physiological control signals. Implications of these observations and relevant lessons drawn from this case for EEG- and hybrid-BCI training are discussed below.

Merits of single-state feedback

The increase in online feedback and longitudinal signal discriminability results beginning in session 6 when the single-state feedback was introduced indicated that this feedback provided a platform for the participant to explore and identify task strategies that yielded machine-discriminable signals. Although the offline analysis revealed that the signals within sessions 6–8 were of mixed neuro-cortical and cranial-muscular origin, the participant appeared to ably utilize the feedback to improve during these sessions.

While the observed increases in task discriminability coincided with the change to single-state feedback in session 6, the structure of this case study (i.e., changes were introduced in response to case study particpant difficuly, not within a controlled experiment) prevents exclusive attributation of performance improvements to feedback modality change. Indeed, effects such as accumulated practice and task habituation cannot be controlled for in this case study and it is unclear to what degree such factors contributed to the progression of the task discriminability. Furthermore, changes observed in the user’s experience (fatigue, cognitive demand) captured via the Subjective Experience survey and NASA-TLX cannot be causally attributed to the introduction of the single-state feedback system.

Nonetheless, the observations indicate that single-state feedback may be effective in early stages of training where users are exploring different task approaches and benefit from indication when signals deviate from baseline activity. Indeed, the increased discrimination between the MR and the active tasks (Fig. 10b) supports that the feedback reliably communicated when the user’s neurophysiological activity deviated from or resembled the MR baseline and the user was able to leverage that information to improve the discriminability between MR and the two active tasks.

The identification of task strategies that yield EEG signals that significantly differ from a users’ basal state is a significant challenge for training users with initially low performance [4, 34, 58]. This study’s longitudinal task-discriminability observations and the participant’s favorable subjective experience during sessions 6–8 indicate that the proposed training and feedback approach warrant further exploration. Given the influence of EMG activity in this study, it would be prescient to directly measure cranial muscle activity in future research to determine the extent to which EEG signal changes reflect muscle rather than cortical activity and to provide this information as an additional layer of user feedback.

Challenges with multi-pattern state feedback

Meaningful task discriminability improvements were not observed during sessions 2–5 when the participant received the multi-pattern state-based feedback. Further investigation would be required to more rigorously assess the efficacy of the multi-modal feedback for initially low-performance users. Nevertheless, the observations from this study provide valuable insights for future evaluations of this and similar feedback systems.

As discussed in the brief review of other feedback approaches for BCIs [17, 30, 31], the theoretical value of such feedback approaches is to provide a stable representation that yields similar outcomes when users apply the same mental task approach. This stability provides the foundation for users to learn associations between the observed feedback outcome and their task execution approach. Indeed, a primary design objective for this feedback methodology was to provide this stable representation and indicate to the user when they were producing similar EEG patterns for multiple tasks. However, the combination of minute inter-state differences and the high frequency of intra-trial state transitions (Fig. 7) likely obscured this information from the user.

During sessions 2–5, inter-state boundaries likely failed to reflect signal states that the user could consciously modulate between. Consequently, although most trials consisted of signals with similar patterns, representing the trials as sequences of multiple discrete states likely misleadingly implied the presence of significant inter- and intra-trial signal variations. Similarly to how poorly-conditioned classifiers produce feedback that users can struggle to interpret because associations between action and classifier output are challenging to identify [28, 29], the results of this study suggest that discrete pattern state transitions that represent minute signal changes may be equally challenging for users to interpret.

Furthermore, the high frequency of intra-trial state transitions may have falsely conveyed an ability to consciously modulate EEG signals. To the participant, this may have created the illusion of conscious actions yielding meaningful modulations in the EEG signals, when, in reality, inter-state differences were relatively minute and transitions were prone to occur in response to spurious signal activity. Even in cases where the multi-state feedback could have plausibly been reflecting an expected neurophysiological response (i.e., RMI task in Fig. 7, the shift from S3 to S2 possibly reflecting a event related desynchronization followed by a synchronization), the pattern sequence was likely obscured by these states occurring with similar frequency during the other tasks.

These observations suggest that deployment of multi-state feedback for individuals experiencing BCI inefficiency should be deferred until a user demonstrates the ability to consistently produce consciously-induced signals that deviate from baseline activity (see next section). Note that deferring use of multi-state feedback until consistent deviation from baseline can be achieved would not necessitate that the states within the model be uniquely associated with a single task (as is typical with classifier feedback). Multi-state models would retain the flexibility to identify and communicate patterns that are produced during multiple tasks.

Another related challenge with multi-state feedback exposed during this case were unstable variations in the pattern states between sessions. Because the participant did not produce discriminable EEG patterns during the early sessions, outlier patterns were more likely to be identified during the K-means clustering process. However, these outlier patterns were unlikely to be repeated during subsequent sessions (e.g., S1 in Fig. 7), reducing the number of states available to represent meaningful differences within typical EEG activity. For users experience BCI inefficiency, risk of these outlier states emerging during K-means analysis is heightened due to the lack of robust inter-task signal differences. This further supports deferring multi-state feedback until conscious modulation abilities are demonstrated. Additionally, while the risk of outlier states is less prevalent for higher-performance users, identification of outlier states remains possible. Therefore, methods to discern the physiological origins of identified pattern states and prune such outlier states from the model should be developed in future work.

Combined, the lack of identifiable associations between states and task actions and the high frequency of state transitions likely contributed to the heightened frustration, performance, fatigue, and difficulty ratings reported by the user in the NASA-TLX and Subjective Experience surveys during sessions 3–5. The increase in these factors combined with the high cognitive load required to try and identify potentially non-existent task-state associations with the multi-state feedback model could reduce the likelihood of achieving BCI control [59–62] and increase the likelihood of device abandonment [18, 63]. Ultimately, the combination of these observations suggest that multi-state feedback may not be an optimal feedback modality for initial training for users experiencing BCI inefficiency. Instead, an incremental training and feedback approach that builds towards multi-state feedback would be more appropriate.

Progressive feedback: single- to multi-pattern state

In future implementations of the proposed training protocol for users with initially low BCI performance, a more effective training strategy may be to begin with single-state feedback before transitioning to multi-state feedback. Once the user demonstrates an ability to produce signals that deviate from their baseline activity with single-state feedback, the transition to multi-state feedback could be made to enable identification of strategies that yield different patterns for each mental task. Such an approach could mitigate against the challenges and risks identified with the multi-state feedback discussed in the previous section. Similarly, transitioning to the multi-state feedback once the user demonstrates ability to reliably deviate from baseline would overcome the primary limitation of the single-state feedback: positive reinforcement was provided whenever the participant produced signals that deviated from baseline activity, regardless of whether they aligned with any task-specific pattern template. While this may be appropriate in early stages of learning, it provides limited opportunity for the user to receive feedback on the discriminability between active tasks. In This study, this limitation likely contributed to the lower classifier discrimination observed between the RMI and CWG tasks compared to their individual discrimination versus MR (Fig. 10b). Progressing to multi-state feedback would add degrees of freedom for the feedback to indicate when active tasks produced similar signals.

Spontaneous emergence of multimodal neurophysiological control

As presented in the “Evidence of implicit learning of multimodal physiological control” section, EMG signals from subtle cranial muscle activations appeared to contribute meaningfully to the participant’s ability to produce signals deviating from baseline activity. Prior studies investigating EEG activity before and after neuromuscular paralysis have demonstrated significant presence of EMG in EEG frequencies exceeding 20 Hz in relaxed states due to tonic muscle activation [64–66]. These EMG signals become more prominent during scalp and jaw muscle contractions [53] that are common responses to increased concentration or performance of a challenging task [67, 68]. A likely explanation for the performance changes observed in this study, therefore, are that the participant increased his state of focus and concentration during the active mental imagery tasks in later sessions. Consequently, he inadvertently introduced EMG resulting from subtle muscle activations associated with increased focus and concentration. With the improvement indicated by the online feedback, the participant likely conflated the increased concentration, and its associated muscle activity, with improved mental task performance further reinforcing concentration strategies that induce muscle tension.

While the EMG activity aided the participant in controlling the BCI and appeared to increase his overall experience and enjoyment of the training, there are potential limitations to EMG-based control schemes. McFarland et al. [68] observed that individuals relying on EMG activity for BCI control during early training sessions struggled to maintain control in later sessions, potentially because they were unaware of their initial reliance on EMG. As exemplified by the oscillating levels of EMG present between sessions 6–8 in this study, it is likely that the participant was similarly not aware of the influence of EMG activations and not consciously attempting to modulate EMG activity to achieve control. It is unclear, therefore, whether the EMG activations that the participant successfully utilized within session 8 represent a learned activation that would be consistently repeated in future sessions. Additionally, while EMG alone can be reliably used to produce discriminable binary states, it may prove more challenging for discrimination in cases with more than two classes. Finally, EMG would not a viable control modality for individuals with muscle disorders, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis or dyskinetic cerebral palsy; two populations of individuals who could benefit from BCIs [53].

Implications for hybrid BCI approaches

For individuals with the ability to consciously control their cranial muscles and who display low task-discriminability of EEG signals, a hybrid EEG-EMG approach may be a viable strategy to enable reliable activations and keep users engaged during longer-term training. Indeed, previous BCI training studies have found that users with initially low performance (i.e., near-chance level classification accuracy) can struggle to significantly improve their performance with practice [3, 6]. Sustained low performance combined with the mentally taxing experience of BCI training would likely lead to disinterest and abandonment of the technology [18], particularly within pediatric populations [63]. The increased frustration and perceived training difficulty reported by the participant in session 5 of this study may have reflected early indications of waning interest due to the low performance and lack of clear progression.

In such cases, it may be beneficial for the user to utilize cranial EMG activations as an alternative, secondary control modality to revert to when the user cannot reliably produce discriminable EEG activations [69–71]. For example, if utilizing gamified training feedback (e.g., BrainDriver) users could revert to an EMG activation if their initial attempt at EEG-based control is unsuccessful. Such an approach would permit users to continue practicing EEG-based control while still enjoying and actively engaging in the game experience. Similar hybrid approaches combining mental imagery and other modalities like steady-state visually evoked potentials have enhanced BCI accuracies while users develop mental imagery proficiency [72]. Other approaches have combined EEG and EMG from muscles in the extremities [73, 74] to improve reliability. The multi-pattern feedback presented herein could naturally be extended for such a hybrid approach as different states could be dedicated to EEG or EMG activity.

A hybrid EEG and cranial EMG approach would also be appropriate for some clinical BCI-use cases such as switch-based play and therapeutic recreation [75] where it is mostly irrelevant whether the activation is EEG- or EMG-based as long as the user can reliably produce a distinct signal. As users in these cases may utilize BCIs infrequently, they would be less likely to pursue long-term training programs to develop robust EEG-based control. Therefore, it would be imperative to quickly identify techniques that result in recorded signals that deviate from baseline. This study’s results suggest that the single-pattern state feedback could effectively provide such training.

Conclusion

This case study presented a novel mental imagery BCI user training protocol rooted in exploratory, action-perception learning and presented the experience of an adolescent with paraplegia. Initially, the participant struggled to produce task-specific signals that could be discerned above chance levels. While our initial feedback system was designed to try and address this training challenge, results within initial sessions indicated that the feedback may have been overly complex for the user and did not support his training. In response, the feedback was simplified to support the participant identifying task approaches to produce signals that deviated from baseline activity. Online results and post-training analysis indicated that the participant learned a multimodal strategy (EEG and EMG) to produce signals that deviated from baseline and that could be used to achieve above-chance-level BCI control. The observations from the case provide useful implications for further refinement and study of similar training approaches and for the development of hybrid BCIs.
