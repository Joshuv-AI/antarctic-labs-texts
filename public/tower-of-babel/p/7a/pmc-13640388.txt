Explainable early prediction of short-term severe deterioration in gallstone-related biliary disease using MIMIC-IV, eICU, and local real-world data
Authors: Qianli Chen, Xiaoxiong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with gallstone-related biliary disease may deteriorate rapidly before definitive source control. This study aimed to develop an interpretable 0-24-hour prediction model for short-term severe deterioration in gallstone-related biliary disease and validate it across eICU-CRD and a local real-world cohort.MethodsMIMIC-IV was used for model development, eICU-CRD for primary external validation, and local clinical data for secondary validation. Eligible patients had gallstone disease, cholecystitis, cholangitis, or biliary obstruction. The outcome was a composite of in-hospital or ICU death, vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy, or prolonged ICU stay. Feature selection, imputation, scaling, model selection, and threshold determination were restricted to MIMIC-IV development data. Performance was assessed using AUROC, AUPRC, Brier score, calibration, decision curves, threshold consequences, subgroup analyses, bootstrap risk uncertainty, and SHAP explanations.ResultsThe study included 2818 MIMIC-IV stays, 1492 eICU-CRD stays, and 115 local patients, with event rates of 58.4%, 65.7%, and 52.2%, respectively. The locked elastic net model retained 12 routine early predictors. AUROC was 0.70 in MIMIC-IV internal validation, 0.64 in eICU-CRD external validation, and 0.63 in local validation. Calibration, net benefit, threshold consequences, and SHAP analyses supported use as a bedside risk-support tool rather than a replacement for clinical judgment.ConclusionsA routine early model captured multi-system deterioration signals in gallstone-related biliary disease and showed transportable, moderate discrimination across external cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05080-1.Keywords: Gallstone-related biliary disease, Clinical deterioration, Prediction model, External validation, Explainable artificial intelligence

Introduction

Acute gallstone-related biliary disease is common, but the clinical danger is concentrated in a smaller group of patients who deteriorate early. At the bedside, the difficult question is not merely whether a patient has cholelithiasis, cholecystitis, cholangitis, or biliary obstruction; it is whether that patient will soon require vasopressors, invasive respiratory support, renal replacement therapy, prolonged intensive care, or will die before source control and organ support can reverse the trajectory. Contemporary reviews and the Tokyo Guidelines emphasize early recognition, infection control, and timely biliary drainage or surgery, yet they also acknowledge that deterioration is shaped by age, comorbidity, organ dysfunction, and local resource pathways [1–3].

Recent studies have made important progress in risk stratification for acute cholangitis and acute cholecystitis. Admission-based mortality scores, machine-learning models for cholelithiasis-induced acute cholangitis, and preoperative scores for severe acute cholecystitis show that routinely available variables contain prognostic information [4–6]. Surgical studies also show that early cholecystectomy risk is clinically heterogeneous, with morbidity and mortality depending on patient condition and disease complexity rather than on diagnosis labels alone [7]. These findings support a pragmatic clinical premise: an early prediction tool should help identify patients whose physiology has already begun to depart from a stable biliary disease course.

However, several gaps remain clinically important. First, many studies focus on a single diagnosis, a single center, or a single endpoint such as mortality, whereas ICU clinicians often face overlapping biliary phenotypes and treatment-linked deterioration outcomes. Second, markers such as procalcitonin, anion gap, lactate, and kidney injury have been associated with severity or adverse outcomes, but they are rarely integrated with vital signs, hepatobiliary injury, coagulation, and electrolyte information in a cross-database validation framework [8–10]. Third, model papers often emphasize discrimination alone, although clinical use requires calibration, threshold consequences, net benefit, and transparent explanations. Fourth, tools developed in one hospital system may not transport well to another because patient selection, timing of drainage, ICU admission practices, and treatment thresholds differ.

We therefore designed this study from a clinical decision perspective. We used MIMIC-IV as the development database and validated the locked model in eICU-CRD and a local real-world cohort [11, 12]. Predictors were restricted to the first 24 h after ICU admission so that the model would reflect early bedside information rather than downstream treatment consequences. Reporting and risk-of-bias assessment were aligned with TRIPOD + AI and PROBAST + AI principles [13, 14]. Our objective was to develop and externally validate an interpretable early prediction model for short-term severe deterioration in gallstone-related biliary disease and to evaluate whether its predictions were clinically usable through calibration, decision curves, threshold consequences, subgroup analyses, individual uncertainty, and SHAP-based explanations.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective multicohort prediction-model study used MIMIC-IV version 3.1 for model development, eICU-CRD for primary cross-database external validation, and a local clinical dataset for secondary real-world validation. MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD contain de-identified data available to approved credentialed users. The local dataset was analyzed as a retrospective de-identified clinical dataset and was interpreted as a secondary feasibility validation cohort because of its small sample size. All feature selection, imputation, scaling, hyperparameter selection, and threshold determination were performed using MIMIC-IV development data only. eICU-CRD and the local cohort were used only after the model was locked. Reporting followed TRIPOD + AI, and model-bias considerations were checked against PROBAST + AI principles [13, 14].

Participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD are de-identified public critical care databases available to credentialed users after completion of required data-use training and approval. The local retrospective dataset was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital, and all local data were analyzed after removal of sensitive identifying information. The requirement for informed consent was waived because this study used retrospective de-identified data. The study population consisted of ICU patients with gallstone-related biliary disease. In MIMIC-IV, 94,458 ICU stays were screened. Eligible admissions were identified using ICD-10 codes K80, K81, K83.0, and K83.1, and ICD-9 codes 574 and 576.1, capturing gallstone disease, cholecystitis, cholangitis, and biliary obstruction. This yielded 3,331 biliary disease-related ICU stays. Repeated ICU stays within the same hospital admission were excluded (n = 513), leaving 2,818 first ICU stays for analysis. In eICU-CRD, 200,859 unit stays were screened using structured diagnosis and admission diagnosis text. Search terms included gallstone, cholelithiasis, choledocholithiasis, cholecystitis, cholangitis, biliary obstruction, common bile duct stone, and bile duct stone. This identified 1,625 biliary disease-related unit stays. Repeated unit stays within the same health-system stay were excluded (n = 133), leaving 1,492 first unit stays for external validation. The local standardized validation dataset contained 115 records; no patients were excluded for missing outcome status. The participant flow and validation design are shown in (Fig. 1).

Outcome

The primary outcome was short-term severe clinical deterioration. It was defined as a composite of in-hospital death, ICU death, vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation or intubation-related invasive respiratory support, renal replacement therapy/CRRT, or ICU length of stay longer than 3 days. Outcome components were not used as predictors. Component distributions were additionally summarized for MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and the local validation cohort to clarify how much of the composite endpoint was driven by death, organ support, renal replacement therapy, and prolonged ICU stay.

Candidate predictors and time window

Predictors were restricted to baseline variables and measurements obtained from 0 to 24 h after ICU admission. Candidate domains included demographics, biliary diagnosis flags, malignancy, vital signs, acid-base and perfusion markers, hepatobiliary laboratory tests, renal function and electrolytes, coagulation markers, and inflammatory markers. Repeated measurements were summarized as first, minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation values when available. Variables with likely information leakage were retained only for outcome construction or descriptive reporting and were excluded from model development.

Statistical analysis, model development, and validation

MIMIC-IV was split into a 70% development cohort and a 30% internal validation cohort using stratified sampling by outcome. Baseline characteristics were summarized as median [25th percentile, 75th percentile] or n (%), compared across cohorts, and accompanied by standardized mean differences. Missingness was evaluated with variable-level rates and pattern plots. Locked-feature missingness across cohorts was summarized separately in Supplementary Table S1. Candidate predictors were retained when missingness was no more than 80% in MIMIC-IV, no more than 85% in eICU-CRD, no more than 95% in the local cohort, and when the variable had more than one non-missing value in MIMIC-IV. Median imputation and standardization parameters were estimated in the MIMIC-IV development cohort and then applied unchanged to MIMIC internal validation, eICU-CRD, and local validation data.

Comparator models included traditional logistic regression, ridge logistic regression, LASSO logistic regression, elastic net logistic regression, random forest, extra trees, gradient boosting, support vector machine, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost when available. These models were fitted in the MIMIC development cohort and compared in the MIMIC internal validation cohort only. The final model was selected after this internal comparison using discrimination, calibration, parsimony, coefficient transparency, and clinical deployability. External cohorts were not used for model selection. The locked threshold was chosen in the MIMIC development cohort by the Youden index and then applied unchanged to MIMIC internal validation, eICU-CRD, and the local validation cohort.

Model performance was evaluated using AUROC with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals, AUPRC, Brier score, calibration intercept, calibration slope, calibration curves, decision curve analysis, clinical impact curves, and threshold tables reporting sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, false positives, and missed events. Net benefit was calculated as TP/N - FP/N x threshold/(1 - threshold). Numerical decision-curve estimates were reported at selected thresholds in Supplementary Table S2. Additional threshold sensitivity analyses identified the threshold with maximum net benefit within the 0.30–0.70 range and the highest thresholds achieving at least 80% and 90% sensitivity in each validation cohort (Supplementary Table S3).

Results

Study population and cohort drift

Baseline characteristics across development and validation cohorts

Outcome components and cross-cohort comparability

Additional cohort-definition and diagnostic-composition variables are summarized in Supplementary Table S4. Cholangitis was recorded in 38.9% of MIMIC-IV stays, 52.0% of eICU-CRD stays, and 43.5% of local validation patients. Malignancy was recorded in 29.1% of MIMIC-IV stays, 5.0% of eICU-CRD stays, and 20.9% of local validation patients. The supplied local table also recorded biliary obstruction (20/115, 17.4%), cholecystitis (26/115, 22.6%), and gallstone diagnosis (49/115, 42.6%).

Outcome-component distributions are shown in Supplementary Table S5. In MIMIC-IV overall, severe deterioration occurred in 1646 of 2818 stays (58.4%); among patients with severe deterioration, the most frequent components were vasopressor use (1025/1646, 62.3%), prolonged ICU stay (972/1646, 59.1%), mechanical ventilation or intubation (794/1646, 48.2%), in-hospital death (445/1646, 27.0%), ICU death (274/1646, 16.6%), and renal replacement therapy/CRRT (140/1646, 8.5%). In eICU-CRD, severe deterioration occurred in 980 of 1492 stays (65.7%); mechanical ventilation or intubation (764/980, 78.0%), prolonged ICU stay (452/980, 46.1%), vasopressor use (299/980, 30.5%), in-hospital death (159/980, 16.2%), ICU death (92/980, 9.4%), and renal replacement therapy/CRRT (90/980, 9.2%) contributed to the composite outcome. Local validation had 60 events among 115 patients (52.2%); among these events, mechanical ventilation or intubation (42/60, 70.0%), ICU length of stay > 3 days (38/60, 63.3%), vasopressor use (35/60, 58.3%), renal replacement therapy/CRRT (6/60, 10.0%), in-hospital death (0/60, 0.0%), and ICU death (0/60, 0.0%) contributed to the composite outcome.

Event-related clinical differences

In the MIMIC development cohort, patients with severe deterioration had more abnormal early physiologic and biochemical profiles than patients without severe deterioration (Table 2). Representative differences included lower first bicarbonate (21.00 [18.00, 24.00] vs. 23.00 [20.00, 25.00], P < 0.001), higher first creatinine (1.20 [0.80, 2.00] vs. 1.00 [0.70, 1.40], P < 0.001), higher first lactate (2.10 [1.40, 3.40] vs. 1.70 [1.20, 2.40], P < 0.001), lower first platelet count (169.00 [110.00, 247.00] vs. 179.00 [128.75, 250.00], P < 0.01), and higher respiratory rate (20.00 [16.00, 24.00] vs. 19.00 [16.00, 23.00], P < 0.01). These differences were consistent with a clinical pattern of systemic stress, impaired perfusion, renal dysfunction, and hematologic disturbance rather than isolated cholestasis alone.

Feature selection and conventional regression

The model-development workflow retained 12 early predictors after elastic net selection, bootstrap stability assessment, permutation importance, feature-level de-duplication, and correlation pruning: mean sodium, maximum lactate, minimum platelet count, maximum creatinine, mean pulse oximetry oxygen saturation, mean heart rate, minimum ALT, maximum respiratory rate, minimum AST, minimum blood urea nitrogen, age, and first INR. Feature selection paths and importance results are shown in Fig. 2, and the correlation clustering and feature-count AUROC plateau are shown in Supplementary Figure S2. In conventional multivariable logistic regression, higher maximum lactate (OR 1.81, 95% CI 1.50–2.18, P < 0.001), higher minimum AST (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.23–2.52, P < 0.01), higher minimum blood urea nitrogen (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.18–1.61, P < 0.001), higher mean heart rate (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.16–1.45, P < 0.001), and higher maximum respiratory rate (OR 1.25, 95% CI 1.12–1.39, P < 0.001) were associated with severe deterioration. Lower platelet count (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.79–0.96, P < 0.01) and lower minimum ALT (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.48–0.81, P < 0.001) were also retained in the multivariable model. Collinearity, conventional multivariable regression, and stability/importance details are reported in Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Table S7, and Supplementary Table S8, respectively.

Model performance and clinical utility

Figure 3 displays the five highest-AUROC machine-learning models: CatBoost, random forest, XGBoost, extra trees, and gradient boosting, with AUROC values of 0.73, 0.73, 0.73, 0.72, and 0.72, respectively. Supplementary Table S9 compares all candidate models during internal validation. Elastic net logistic regression, which was also included in the candidate-model comparison, achieved AUROC 0.70, AUPRC 0.78, Brier score 0.22, and calibration slope 0.95. Because the AUROC gain of the more complex machine-learning models was small and the study aimed to produce a transparent bedside tool, elastic net logistic regression was selected as the final locked model after internal comparison and before any eICU-CRD or local validation. The five machine-learning models in Fig. 3 were therefore retained as benchmark comparators and were not refitted, reselected, or tuned in external cohorts.

At the locked threshold of 0.45, sensitivity/specificity were 0.66/0.63 in MIMIC internal validation, 0.61/0.57 in eICU external validation, and 0.53/0.67 in local validation. At the locked threshold, model net benefit was 0.259 in MIMIC internal validation, 0.280 in eICU-CRD, and 0.149 locally, corresponding to positive net benefit versus treat-none in all validation cohorts. Relative to treat-all, the locked threshold produced small positive differences in MIMIC internal validation and local validation, and a negative difference in eICU-CRD, where the event rate was higher. Discussion

In this study, we developed and externally validated an interpretable early risk model for short-term severe clinical deterioration among ICU patients with gallstone-related biliary disease. Using routinely available variables from the first 24 h of ICU admission, the locked model showed reproducible discrimination across MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and the local validation cohort, while retaining a compact structure suitable for clinical interpretation. These findings suggest that early physiological, inflammatory, hepatic, renal, and coagulation-related information can provide useful risk stratification for identifying patients at higher risk of organ support requirement or prolonged intensive care. The model is best interpreted as an adjunctive risk-estimation tool that complements established severity assessment and clinical judgment.

The clinical meaning of the retained predictors is important. Lactate, bicarbonate-related variables in descriptive analyses, respiratory rate, heart rate, oxygen saturation, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, platelet count, INR, sodium, and transaminase summaries do not describe one organ in isolation. Together, they describe perfusion stress, acid-base disruption, respiratory compensation, kidney injury, coagulation or hematologic disturbance, and hepatobiliary injury. This pattern is consistent with recent cholangitis studies showing prognostic value for inflammatory, nutritional, acid-base, lactate, platelet, and renal markers [15–21]. It also helps explain why bilirubin or transaminases alone are insufficient as a bedside deterioration tool: patients deteriorate when biliary obstruction or infection becomes systemic, not simply when a hepatobiliary marker is abnormal.

Our findings align with recent work showing that admission variables can predict mortality or severe disease in acute cholangitis, but this study extends that literature in several clinically relevant ways. Recent models have focused on severe acute cholangitis occurrence, mortality, or cholelithiasis-induced acute cholangitis outcomes [4, 5, 15]. Other studies have refined TG18 application or evaluated novel ratios such as NLR, BUN-to-albumin, RDW-to-albumin, or neutrophil-to-albumin markers [17, 20–25]. Those efforts are valuable, but they often leave the clinician with either a single-marker signal or a model developed in a narrow population. By contrast, the present analysis intentionally used a broader gallstone-related biliary disease population, a fixed early ICU window, cross-database validation, and a composite endpoint reflecting escalation of organ support.

The decrease in AUROC during external testing is clinically and methodologically interpretable. MIMIC-IV, eICU-CRD, and the local cohort differed in diagnostic ascertainment, malignancy prevalence, cholangitis prevalence, ICU admission context, and observable outcome components. The component analysis showed that the MIMIC-IV composite was commonly driven by vasopressor use and prolonged ICU stay, whereas the eICU-CRD composite had a larger contribution from mechanical ventilation or intubation. In the supplied local component table, the composite was mainly driven by mechanical ventilation or intubation, prolonged ICU stay, and vasopressor use, with no recorded in-hospital or ICU deaths. These differences are compatible with variation in ICU admission thresholds, organ-support practices, and source-control pathways. Therefore, the external AUROC decrease should be read as a transportability signal rather than as simple model failure. It also explains why calibration, net benefit, threshold consequences, and gray-zone uncertainty are as important as AUROC for deciding how the model should be used.

For acute cholecystitis and adjacent gallstone-related disease, the broader literature similarly supports a need for early risk tools that complement surgical judgment. Recent studies have developed preoperative scores for severe acute cholecystitis, predicted morbidity and mortality after early cholecystectomy, examined relapse after non-operative management, and modeled subtotal cholecystectomy or complicated cholecystitis risk [6, 7, 26–30]. Explainable machine-learning models for gangrenous cholecystitis and models predicting ERCP after emergency laparoscopic cholecystectomy demonstrate growing interest in deployable, interpretable tools for biliary surgical decision-making [31–33]. Our study differs by centering on short-term critical deterioration rather than operative difficulty alone, which is the decision clinicians face when deciding who needs closer monitoring, urgent senior review, or earlier ICU-level intervention.

The clinical use case is therefore a risk-supported workflow. A high predicted risk should prompt active review for reversible drivers: inadequate source control, evolving sepsis, hypoperfusion, kidney injury, respiratory compromise, coagulopathy, or electrolyte derangement. It may support earlier repeat lactate, renal function, coagulation, and oxygenation assessment; escalation of monitoring; multidisciplinary discussion with endoscopy, interventional radiology, and surgery; and lower thresholds for ICU-level review. A low predicted risk should not delay antibiotics, biliary drainage, surgery, or other standard care when conventional indications are present. The bootstrap uncertainty analysis is particularly relevant because 26.1–32.2% of validation patients had confidence intervals crossing the locked threshold. For these gray-zone patients, the appropriate response is repeat clinical assessment, short-interval laboratory review, and senior decision-making rather than automatic escalation or automatic reassurance.

The study also has limitations. First, MIMIC-IV cases were identified by ICD codes, whereas eICU-CRD cases were identified through diagnosis text and the local cohort used a standardized local definition; this may introduce cohort-definition differences. Second, the outcome included treatment-based components such as vasopressor use, ventilation, CRRT, and prolonged ICU stay. These components are clinically meaningful but are partly practice-dependent, and the component distribution differed between MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD. Third, biliary drainage timing, surgical strategy, TG18 severity grading, and full SOFA could not be reconstructed consistently across all three harmonized datasets, so qSOFA/SIRS proxies and core biomarkers were used as comparators. Fourth, the local validation cohort was small (n = 115, 60 events) and is best interpreted as secondary feasibility validation with wide uncertainty rather than as a definitive external validation. Fifth, the analysis was retrospective; prospective workflow testing and impact studies are required before deployment. Recent artificial intelligence applications in gallstone pancreatitis and cholelithiasis imaging underscore the broader movement toward data-driven biliary care, but also the need for transparent validation before clinical adoption [34, 35].

Conclusions

An interpretable 0-24-hour model using routine clinical variables was developed in MIMIC-IV and externally validated in eICU-CRD and a local real-world cohort for short-term severe deterioration in gallstone-related biliary disease. The model captures a clinically plausible multi-system deterioration pattern involving perfusion, renal function, platelets/coagulation, electrolytes, oxygenation, and vital-sign instability. Its role is best framed as an early warning and risk-communication aid that complements guideline-based diagnosis, source-control decisions, and bedside clinical judgment.
