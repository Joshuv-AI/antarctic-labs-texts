Psychometric Performance of Children With Amblyopia During a Tablet-Based Adaptive Visual Acuity Assessment
Authors: Jan Skerswetat, Zain Ahmed, Nikhil Chaudhry, Peter J. Bex, Emily Wiecek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeVisual acuity is a key metric for the diagnosis of amblyopia. Here, we introduce a self-administered near visual acuity (VA) test using the Angular Indication Measurement paradigm (AIM-VA) and evaluate it in children with and without amblyopia.MethodsThirty-five children (age range, 5–16 years; 20 males) with (n = 22) and without (n = 13) amblyopia monocularly completed the AIM-VA twice within the same day and a Snellen-VA test. The AIM-VA presented three 4 × 4 charts of Landolt-C optotypes that adaptively varied in size and had randomized gap orientations. Orientation report errors as a function of optotype size were fit with a one-parameter (VA) model or a three-parameter (VA, slope, and response error) model. Bland–Altman analysis assessed test–retest repeatability and intertest agreement with Snellen-VA. Linear correlations and leave-one-out analyses explored relationships between psychometric parameters across groups and amblyopia subtypes.ResultsInterocular acuity difference (IOD) was significantly larger in children with amblyopia than in controls for both AIM-VA and Snellen-VA. The one-parameter AIM-VA yielded repeatability comparable to previous reports, particularly in the amblyopic group, and showed no significant test–retest bias (P > 0.05). However, there was a systematic mean bias of lower logMAR estimates relative to Snellen-VA in the amblyopic group (P = 0.002), likely reflecting the difference in testing distance and task. Exploratory analysis revealed that shallower psychometric slopes were consistently associated with larger noise values across groups, with stronger parameter covariance in refractive than strabismic amblyopia.ConclusionsAIM-VA successfully detected amblyopic near-vision IOD. The one-parameter AIM-VA model showed no retest bias and comparable variance in this pediatric population. Exploratory psychometric parameters showed promise as additional indicators of functional deficits, with potential phenotypical differences between amblyopia subtypes warranting further investigation. AIM-VA is an adaptive, self-administered task that may be an effective tool for managing amblyopia.

Amblyopia is a neurodevelopmental condition that occurs in early childhood and is estimated to have a worldwide prevalence of 1% to 4%.1 Characterized by reduced visual acuity (VA) in one or both eyes due to abnormal visual development, amblyopia is the leading cause of unilateral vision loss in children and can result in permanent impairment if untreated.1 Amblyopia has detrimental impacts on VA, contrast sensitivity, and visuomotor control2,3 and often leads to impaired binocular vision, including reduced stereopsis and fusion, and increased interocular suppression.4 Moreover, amblyopia significantly affects quality of life, with studies showing that children with untreated amblyopia experience slower reading speeds, reduced academic performance, and lower self-esteem due to visual and psychosocial challenges.5,6 These deficits can persist into adulthood, affecting educational attainment and occupational opportunities, underscoring the need for early vision screening and accurate VA assessment to mitigate long-term adverse consequences.7

Amblyopia is typically defined by a best-corrected interocular VA difference of 0.20 logMAR (two lines in an Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study chart), and diagnosis and assessment of treatment response in children heavily rely on measurements of VA.8 However, VA measurements can be challenging in children, given limited attention span, difficulty understanding instructions, unfamiliarity with optotype identities, short time windows for testing, and inconsistent cooperation. This can affect test reliability as children may favor their better-seeing eye or guess responses.9,10 While the Snellen chart is a standard tool for older patients, it is often impractical for young children, leading clinicians to employ age-appropriate alternatives like the HOTV subset, Lea symbols, Auckland optotypes, or Teller acuity cards, which use matching or preferential looking methods to suit pediatric needs.11 Many tests lack interactive, age-appropriate elements, resulting in boredom or confusion that can reduce engagement and compromise accuracy. Measurements can vary across sessions or examiners due to fatigue, learning effects, or inconsistent methods.12 Tests using letters or culturally specific symbols may not suit patients from different cultural backgrounds, risking misinterpretation or exclusion.

Recently, a new generalizable method for assessing visual function, called the Angular Indication Measurement (AIM) method, has been published and applied to distance13 and near14 VA in healthy adults. The method is computer-based, response-adaptive, and self-administered. AIM-VA uses orientation judgments (e.g., the orientation of the gap of Landolt C-optotypes) and fits the resulting response error data (the difference between the true and reported target orientation) as a function of stimulus level (e.g., Landolt-C size) with a three-parameter psychometric function. In addition to the standard psychophysical parameters of threshold (e.g., VA) and slope, AIM generates further information about performance, specifically the error bias profile (e.g., systematic orientation-dependent effects that could arise from the oblique effect or astigmatism) and minimum report error or noise parameter (i.e., accuracy for easily detected suprathreshold stimuli that could arise from sources of noise, e.g., visual distortion, hand tremor, tear film inconsistencies, or neural spiking variations). A previous study using AIM-VA measured at a 4-m distance in healthy young adults used spherical and cylindrical lenses to induce blur and astigmatism, respectively, and showed that both threshold and noise parameters were significantly larger with blur than without blur.13 The same study also showed that systematic report errors can be detected when astigmatism is induced via cylindrical lenses. Evidence of differences in psychometric performance between amblyopic and nonamblyopic eyes exists.15 Thus, these parameters may provide additional information about interocular psychometric differences, which could benefit amblyopia screening and intervention monitoring.

The current study has four aims. Specifically, we first introduce the AIM-VA near test using a tablet device in children with and without amblyopia. Then, we investigated the repeatability of AIM-VA in children with and without amblyopia. Next, we measured interocular acuity difference with AIM-VA and tested its agreement with a reference distance Snellen visual acuity test. Finally, we evaluated the relationship among all psychometric outcome parameters—namely, threshold, slope, noise, and error bias—and across both groups.

Methods

Participants were recruited from the Boston Children's Hospital Department of Ophthalmology during regularly scheduled clinic visits. The Institutional Review Boards of Northeastern University and Boston Children's Hospital approved the study, which was in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, 1975. Upon completing the experiment, participants received compensation in the form of a $20 Amazon gift card for their time. Prior to the main experiment, a pilot was conducted, which we report in the Appendix.

Participants

In the main experiment, 35 patients aged 6 to 18 years were categorized as having refractive (n = 9), strabismic (n = 10), or mixed (n = 3) amblyopia or typically sighted control participants (n = 13) by a certified ophthalmic technician, orthoptist, optometrist, or ophthalmologist. Entering VA was not always best corrected in both cohorts, but Snellen and AIM-VA were tested using the same refractive error when applicable. All children with amblyopia had a documented clinical diagnosis of amblyopia by either a pediatric optometrist or ophthalmologist. Control participants had at least 0.10 logMAR visual acuity in each eye, with no history of amblyopia or strabismus. Participants with other ocular comorbidities or developmental conditions that could confound the interpretation of the results were excluded. The experiment was conducted preceding dilation.

Equipment

The AIM-VA method has been described previously in detail13 and validated in healthy adults using the same hardware and near testing distance.14 The software was deployed on a Surface Pro 8 Tablet (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) positioned 40 cm from the participant. Stimuli were generated using MATLAB (R2021a; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) software in combination with the Psychophysics Toolbox.16,17 The tablet was gamma-corrected using the datacolor Spyder X (Switzerland, Lawrenceville, NJ, USA) photometer. The screen resolution was 2736 × 1824 pixels, and the width of the tablet screen was 26 cm, which subtended 36° and provided a resolution of 76 pixels/degree, with each pixel subtending 0.79 arcmin.

Optotypes and Psychometric Function

C optotypes were each presented within a surrounding indication ring (diameter, Ø: 5°) with an intercell gap of 1°. Three charts were presented, based on agreement data in our previous studies of refractive error13 and a requirement to minimize the test duration. The initial chart was a 4 × 4 grid of C optotypes ranging in size from −0.05 to 1.00 logMAR and 14 equal log-stepped optotypes between (see Fig. 1A). The task stopped automatically after completing the third chart. Stimuli of different sizes were randomized in terms of their location within each chart and their gap orientation (360°). Each subsequent adaptive chart then contained optotypes ranging ±2 standard deviations around the estimate of VA from the fit to data from all previous charts.

Schematic of the AIM paradigm. (A) Each AIM chart contained 16 stimuli embedded in cells whose circular surrounds served as indication rings. After indicating the orientation of the C target in each cell with unlimited time and adjustments, a green “Next” button appeared on the right-hand side, and participants could click it to proceed to the next chart. After each chart, auditory motivational feedback was given based on the performance. The stimuli on subsequent charts were adaptively changed based on performance across all previous charts. (B) Example outcome including angular error reports (blue dots) for each stimulus size (x-axis). A psychometric function (beige line) and its 95% shaded confidence intervals generated three parameters: acuity, minimum report error, and slope. The inset depicts the angular error principal, whereby the absolute difference between true gap orientation and indicated gap orientation determined the angular error.

Participants indicated the perceived orientation of each stimulus by tapping on the surrounding response ring (Fig. 1A). Participants were encouraged to guess if they were unsure of the orientation and could repeatedly adjust their response by clicking again on the response ring. When the participant was satisfied with their selection, they were instructed to proceed to the next chart by tapping the “Next” button (this only appeared after responses for all 16 stimuli had been recorded). The response error for each optotype was calculated as the difference between target orientation and indicated orientation in degrees (see Fig. 1B). At the end of the final chart, a cumulative Gaussian psychometric function was fit to the error report data as a function of optotype size from all charts, defined as (1)τθs=θi+θguess-θi0.5-0.5*erfs-τ2gwhere θguess is orientation error for a guess response (the absolute orientation error of random responses for C stimuli is uniformly distributed with a mean of 90°), s is signal intensity (here stimulus size), τ is threshold, θi is internal angular uncertainty (noise—the angular report error for the most visible stimulus), and g is the slope.

The function was fit to trial-by-trial absolute angular error data using nonlinear least squares estimation (MATLAB's fittype and fit functions). Two versions of the fit were applied: (1) an unconstrained model, in which all three parameters (τ, g, θi) were free to vary for each participant, with g initialized at 0.5. With three parameters, confidence intervals for the estimate of the acuity parameter, τ, increased with uncertainty in the other two parameters (g, θi) because of parameter covariance. Since Snellen VA has only one parameter, we also used (2) a constrained model, in which g and θi were fixed at 0.07 and 7°, respectively (the mean values across all participants), and only τ was estimated freely. The constrained model was used for intertest agreement comparisons with Snellen VA to reduce differences in the number of reduced degrees of freedom between tests, thereby minimizing variability due to this difference when comparing fundamentally different test formats. The unconstrained model was used for repeatability analysis and exploratory psychometric parameter investigations.

Procedure

Before beginning the experiment, a research assistant thoroughly explained the procedure and implications of the study to participants and their families to ensure informed consent. A preliminary examination by an ophthalmologist, orthoptist, optometrist, or ophthalmic technician was performed, including distance monocular Snellen VA. For AIM testing, participants were seated in a dark clinical examination room to minimize ambient light interference. The order of eye testing was randomized across participants. The nontested eye was occluded using an eye patch to ensure monocular viewing.

Analysis

We used customized MATLAB software (version R2023a) for data analysis. Bland–Altman analysis was performed to calculate and visualize the coefficient of repeatability, limits of agreement, and mean and proportional biases.18

Limits of agreement were calculated by using (2)LoA=1.96×σw,where σw is the within-subject standard deviation (i.e., the standard deviation of test A – test B or near-distance differences).

The coefficient of repeatability (CoR) was calculated using (3)CoR=LoA×2.

Confidence intervals for Bland–Altman analysis were calculated using the t-distribution to account for sample size uncertainty. The 95% confidence interval for the mean bias was computed as bias ± t₀.₀₂₅(n – 1) × SEM, where SEM is the standard error of the mean difference (SD/√n), and the 95% confidence intervals for the limits of agreement were similarly calculated as LoA ± t₀.₀₂₅(n – 1) × SEM, using the tinv function to obtain critical t-values at α = 0.025 for n – 1 degrees of freedom.

MATLAB's corrplot function was used to generate linear models between outcomes, resulting in Pearson's r and the t-test to investigate whether the correlations between thresholds, slopes, noise, and error bias were significantly different from chance. We also conducted a leave-one-out control analysis in which one value was systematically removed, then fitted again, and repeated for all values. The means and standard deviations of resulting r values were also reported.

We excluded three participants for incomplete data. Of the remaining 32 participants, two contained a trial that was not different from random responses, which were also excluded from the analysis. Two children with bilateral amblyopia were also excluded. The remaining data for 28 participants (18 amblyopes, 10 controls) were used for the analysis (Table 1).

Results

The mean total testing duration was 147 ± 49 seconds (32% completed in under 2 minutes, 78% in under 3 minutes), and the mean chart duration was 46 ± 20 seconds. This demonstrates that AIM-VA can be administered within a reasonable time (under 3 minutes). AIM-VA detected larger interocular acuity differences due to amblyopia compared to children with normal vision (Table 2). Additionally, we report slope and noise fit parameters, as well as the mean error bias.

Repeatability of AIM-VA

Table 3 summarizes the test–retest Bland–Altman analysis of the AIM for both VA and interocular difference (IOD) estimates, comparing one- and three- parameter model fits across control and amblyopic groups, as well as pooled across both groups (see also Figs. A1A, A1B). Overall, repeatability across all groups, fitting approaches, and pooled analyses resulted in no statistically significant mean test–retest bias observed in any condition (all P > 0.05), indicating that AIM estimates were not systematically shifted between sessions. Proportional bias was likewise nonsignificant across all conditions (all P > 0.05), suggesting that repeatability did not vary as a function of performance level in either group or when data were considered across participants.

For VA, the one-parameter fit (Table 3 AIM*) yielded narrower limits of agreement and smaller coefficients of repeatability than the three-parameter fit (AIM**) across all analyses. This advantage was modest in controls (LoA: ±0.19 vs. ±0.19 logMAR; CoR: 0.26 vs. 0.27 logMAR) but more pronounced in the amblyopic group (LoA: ±0.22 vs. ±0.35 logMAR; CoR: 0.31 vs. 0.49 logMAR). The pooled analysis mirrored this pattern, with the one-parameter fit again outperforming the three-parameter fit (LoA: ±0.21 vs. ±0.31 logMAR; CoR: 0.30 vs. 0.43 logMAR), confirming that the benefit of parameter constraining generalizes across the full sample. This pattern suggests that constraining model parameters meaningfully improves the precision of VA estimates, particularly in the amblyopic group, where psychometric functions may be less well defined.

A similar pattern was observed for IOD estimates. The one-parameter fit produced tighter repeatability than the three-parameter fit in controls (CoR: 0.31 vs. 0.34 logMAR), the amblyopic group (CoR: 0.42 vs. 0.68 logMAR), and the pooled sample (CoR: 0.40 vs. 0.59 logMAR). The one-parameter fit showed notably wider limits of agreement in the amblyopic group (LoA: ±0.48 logMAR) than the three-parameter fit (LoA: ±0.30 logMAR), a difference that was also evident in the pooled analysis (LoA: ±0.42 vs. ±0.29 logMAR). The substantially larger variability of unconstrained IOD estimates in the amblyopic group likely reflects the increased instability of fitting additional free parameters when psychometric data are noisier or more asymmetric between eyes.

Intertest Agreement

Table 4 summarizes the Bland–Altman intertest agreement analysis between Snellen and AIM (one-parameter, first-run) estimates of visual acuity and interocular difference across control and amblyopic groups, as well as pooled across both groups (see also Fig. 3).

No statistically significant mean bias was observed in controls (mean bias: −0.06 logMAR, P = 0.08), the amblyopic group (mean bias: 0.04 logMAR, P = 0.41), or the pooled sample (mean bias: 0.01 logMAR, P = 0.84). Proportional bias was likewise nonsignificant across all three analyses (P = 0.63, P = 0.77, and P = 0.23, respectively), indicating that intermethod agreement for IOD did not vary systematically as a function of IOD magnitude in either group. The limits of agreement were wider in the amblyopic group (±0.36 logMAR) compared to controls (±0.18 logMAR), with the pooled sample yielding intermediate limits (±0.32 logMAR), consistent with the greater variability in IOD estimates expected in a clinically heterogeneous population.

For VA, a more complex picture emerged. In controls, no significant mean or proportional bias was observed (mean bias: 0.04 logMAR, P = 0.20; proportional bias P = 0.20), and the limits of agreement were moderate (±0.27 logMAR). In the amblyopic group, however, a statistically significant mean bias was found (mean bias: 0.14 logMAR, P = 0.002), indicating that Snellen systematically yielded higher VA estimates than AIM in this group. This pattern was also reflected in the pooled analysis, which revealed a significant mean bias of 0.11 logMAR (P < 0.001), confirming that the systematic offset between methods generalizes beyond the amblyopic subgroup alone when data are considered across participants. Proportional bias was nonsignificant across all three analyses (controls, P = 0.20; amblyopia, P = 0.28; pooled, P = 0.22), suggesting that the systematic offset between Snellen and AIM was relatively consistent across the range of acuity values rather than being driven by performance at any particular level. The limits of agreement were wider in the amblyopic group (±0.47 logMAR) and pooled sample (±0.42 logMAR) compared to controls (±0.27 logMAR), reflecting greater intermethod variability when acuity losses are present.

Exploratory Investigation of Relationships Between Visual Acuity Performance Parameters

By correlating the psychometric parameters with those of another for each eye condition, we explored their interdependency for both groups, as previous research in adults suggested that psychometric performance may differ in children with amblyopia compared to normally sighted observers.15 We examined how threshold, slope, noise, and the report error bias correlated for each eye and group (Table 5). There were negative correlations between slope and noise parameters across all eye conditions. Here, slope is representative of how visual performance changes as a function of optotype size, and noise represents the accuracy for easily detected supra-threshold stimuli. We also analyzed strabismic and refractive subtypes and show that refractive amblyopia showed stronger correlations between VA and slope and noise compared to strabismic subtypes. The current exploratory study encourages larger cohort research, investigating these potential phenotypical differences between amblyopia subtypes further.

To assess whether interparameter relationships differed significantly between groups and eye conditions, we compared correlation coefficients across subgroups using the leave-one-out analysis to evaluate stability (Table 5). Several patterns emerged. The negative slope–noise correlation was present across all groups (amblyopic eyes [AEs]: r = −0.53; fellow eyes [FEs]: r = −0.10; controls: r = −0.33; strabismic: r = −0.69; refractive: r = −0.46), although it was notably stronger in the strabismic amblyopia subgroup, suggesting that the trade-off between psychometric steepness and supra-threshold accuracy may be more pronounced in this group. The threshold–noise correlation was positive in amblyopic and fellow eyes (AEs: r = 0.47; FEs: r = 0.33) but negative in controls (r = −0.47), pointing to a qualitative difference in how acuity loss and supra-threshold performance covary in amblyopic versus normally sighted children. Refractive amblyopia showed stronger threshold–slope (r = −0.64) and threshold–noise (r = 0.56) correlations compared to the strabismic subgroup (r = −0.38 and r = 0.15, respectively), indicating that in refractive amblyopia, VA loss is more tightly coupled to both the rate of perceptual improvement across optotype sizes and the accuracy for clearly visible stimuli. The error bias parameter showed weak and inconsistent correlations across most groups, except in the refractive amblyopia subgroup, where a moderate positive correlation with noise was observed (r = 0.65), warranting further investigation.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the interparameter relationships are not uniform across groups and are unlikely to be explained solely by model-fitting constraints. Rather, they may reflect genuine phenotypical differences in visual processing deficits between amblyopia subtypes.

Discussion

The current study deployed the adaptive and generalizable AIM paradigm to measure VA in children with and without amblyopia. Its self-administered, active gap orientation judgment task, in combination with child-friendly feedback, enables researchers and clinicians to estimate VA while also gathering additional psychometric information that may be useful in characterizing visual deficits in amblyopia.

AIM-VA's Repeatability in Children

Previous studies in healthy adults validated AIM-VA at a 4-m distance viewing using a wireless computer mouse as an indication device13 and at 40 cm near viewing using the same touch-based indication via tablet used here.14 These studies found that AIM-VA shows no significant test–retest bias and repeatability variance less than 0.20 logMAR. Here, we report AIM-VA's repeatability in children with and without visual impairment.

Prior studies that evaluated visual acuity tests in children with and without amblyopia showed these tests have a small bias, with limits of agreement ranging from ±0.12 to ±0.27 logMAR.10,19–22 Repeatability measured in the current study was broadly comparable between controls and amblyopic participants for the one-parameter fit across both VA and IOD, whereas the three-parameter fit showed a more pronounced reduction in repeatability in the amblyopic group relative to controls (Table 3; Fig. 2). This dissociation was corroborated by the pooled analysis, where constrained fit repeatability remained stable while unconstrained fit variability was intermediate between the two groups. These findings collectively support the use of the constrained one-parameter model when reliable, repeatable estimates are the primary goal, particularly in clinical populations where measurement precision is critical.

These results demonstrate that AIM-VA yields repeatable visual acuity estimates suitable for both research and clinical applications in pediatric and amblyopic populations. The lack of systematic bias and the consistency of results support the robustness of the method for repeated testing and longitudinal follow-up, both of which are paramount in the management and treatment of amblyopia. Furthermore, there is potential to improve repeatability with increased testing time, as increasing the number of adaptive charts has been shown to improve the repeatability of AIM-VA in adults.13 Future research may investigate the trade-off between maximizing repeatability while maintaining an appropriate and child-friendly testing duration.

Intertest Agreement

Several previous studies compared visual acuity tests in children (Table 6). A systematic review found that few digital VA tests demonstrated accuracy comparable to clinical assessment, while most other self-administered tests exhibited inferior accuracy.23 The intertest agreement findings of the current study suggest that AIM and Snellen provide comparable IOD estimates across groups, but two measures diverge systematically for VA, particularly in the amblyopic group and when data are pooled across participants (Table 4, Fig. 4). Snellen consistently returned higher VA estimates than AIM, an offset that likely reflects a combination of methodological and procedural differences between the two approaches. Notably, Snellen testing was conducted at a distance while AIM was administered at near, and differences in acuity between viewing distances are well documented, particularly in children with amblyopia. In addition, Snellen testing permits contextual cues and crowding configurations that may differ from the AIM paradigm, and children with amblyopia are known to be particularly susceptible to these procedural influences. These results highlight the importance of method and viewing distance consistency when monitoring VA longitudinally in amblyopic children. The limits of agreement for controls (±0.27 logMAR) were comparable to those reported in previous intertest agreements (Table 6). However, they were wider in the amblyopic group (±0.47 logMAR) and pooled across groups (±0.42 logMAR).

The systematic positive mean bias observed for VA in the amblyopic group, with Snellen returning higher acuity estimates than AIM-VA, is unlikely to be explained by viewing distance alone. A PEDIG study24 directly comparing monocular near and distance VA in children with moderate amblyopia found no systematic difference between the two testing distances, concluding that individual near-distance discrepancies are more likely attributable to test–retest variability than a genuine viewing distance effect. Instead, the observed bias more plausibly reflects the combined influence of several methodological differences between the two tests. First, Snellen uses letter optotypes, which engage recognition acuity and allow observers to draw on cognitive facilitation, including familiarity with letter shapes and linguistic context, to reconstruct degraded stimuli. AIM-VA, by contrast, uses C optotypes, which require resolution acuity based solely on localizing a gap orientation, without equivalent cognitive scaffolding. Letter-based charts have been shown to systematically overestimate VA relative to Landolt C by approximately 0.04 to 0.06 logMAR in normally sighted adults, with evidence that this discrepancy widens under conditions of visual impairment. Second, the crowding characteristics of the two tests differ meaningfully. Snellen charts present multiple letters per line with scaled interoptotype spacing, while AIM-VA presents Landolt-C optotypes in a 4 × 4 chart with adaptive optotype sizes, at fixed spacing. Given that crowding is disproportionately elevated in the central vision of children with strabismic amblyopia and contributes independently to letter chart performance, differences in the spatial arrangement and flanker proximity of the two tests are likely to introduce additional intermethod variability. Third, unlike the fixed-scale Snellen chart, AIM-VA employs an adaptive procedure that adjusts optotype sizes between charts based on the child's responses, providing a more precise psychometric estimate of threshold rather than the coarser line-by-line scoring of conventional charts. This adaptivity may result in more sensitive and arguably more accurate threshold estimates. Taken together, these differences in optotype type, cognitive demands, crowding configuration, and measurement precision collectively account for the systematic offset between the two methods.

AIM-VA's Additional Outcome Measures

A pioneering study15 on psychometrics of VA in adults showed shallower slopes in the amblyopic than nonamblyopic eye. Here, we expand upon this finding to explore how psychometric parameters differ in children with and without amblyopia. In an exploratory analysis, we found negative associations between slope and noise (also known as lapse values)29 in children with and without amblyopia (Table 5). This indicates that steep slopes, corresponding to a narrower range of performance variation, are associated with higher noise levels. In a recent study in healthy young adults, AIM-VA's noise was significantly elevated when inducing spherical and cyclical blur, demonstrating the vulnerability of this additional parameter to decreased visibility.13 We also found stronger associations between VA and slope, as well as noise, for refractive compared to strabismic subtypes. The negative correlations between slope and noise were consistent across all eye conditions and participant groups, including controls, suggesting this relationship is a stable feature of the data rather than an artifact of fitting in any particular subgroup (Table 5). Furthermore, the parameter relationships differed meaningfully between groups in ways that would not be expected if correlations were driven solely by model parameterization. Future research may investigate these subtypes and their psychometric performance parameters with adequately powered groups.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

We acknowledge a number of study limitations that invite future research. The current test assessed distance and near visual acuities during the intertest agreement experiment, which compared orientation judgments of C optotypes with letter identification tasks. Variations in accommodative states and residual refractive error may have contributed to the variability observed during the experiments. Our inclusion criteria for children with amblyopia required only a clinically documented diagnosis of amblyopia, rather than the more typical requirement of a two-line-or-greater interocular difference in visual acuity. This approach allowed the inclusion of children with bilateral amblyopia, as well as those undergoing treatment for amblyopia, who may no longer meet strict acuity-based criteria at the time of testing. Future studies evaluating the role of AIM-VA in the diagnosis and management of amblyopia would benefit from applying more conventional interocular difference criteria. Furthermore, future clinical trials will require larger sample sizes to compare different subtypes of amblyopia and different treatment modalities to confirm the utility of these additional outcomes with regard to amblyopia screening and treatment monitoring.

Conclusions

The self-administered Angular Indication Measurement or AIM technique was used to measure the psychometric performance during a visual acuity task and near distance in children with and without amblyopia and successfully detected deficits in amblyopic children. Moreover, AIM-VA showed intertest agreement in both amblyopic and typically sighted children, comparable to reports from previous studies investigating agreement between acuity tests in amblyopic children. Both groups showed no significant test–retest bias and variance comparable with previous reports. AIM's response-adaptive and personalized psychometric function generates additional outcome values, including the slope, minimum report error, and report error bias. Exploratory analysis of all psychometric parameters revealed significant negative correlations between slopes and noise parameter, indicating that the range in which stimulus detectability changes narrows with increasing noise. AIM-VA has the potential to be used for pediatric testing of visual acuity. Future research may investigate larger amblyopic subgroups before and during treatment to further explore whether additional parameter changes may improve the test's ability to identify amblyopic eyes.
