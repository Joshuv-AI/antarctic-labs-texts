Strengthening primary care facility management: a systematic review of management interventions, implementation strategies, and outcomes in six sub-Saharan African countries
Authors: Ermyas Birru, Sandra Urusaro, Aneth Dinis, Stélio Tembe, Teresa Jewell, Steve Gloyd, Kenneth Sherr, Lisa R Hirschhorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough primary health care (PHC) is central to health system performance, effective facility-level management is a critical determinant of service quality, resource use, and improved health outcomes. We conducted a systematic review to synthesize evidence on interventions, implementation strategies, and outcomes aimed at strengthening PHC facility management in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).MethodsWe conducted a systematic review using thematic synthesis. Eight electronic databases were searched for peer-reviewed studies and grey literature published between 2012 and 2022, focusing on interventions targeting PHC facility management in SSA. Implementation strategies were mapped to the thematic cluster areas of the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) framework. Implementation outcomes were classified using Proctor’s Implementation Outcome Framework, and management effectiveness domains were identified through thematic analysis.ResultsOf 3,752 studies screened, nine met the inclusion criteria. These studies represented six SSA countries: Botswana, Ethiopia, Kenya, South Africa, Tanzania, and Zambia. Four categories of intervention were identified: (1) training and capacity building programs, (2) peer-to-peer learning, (3) audit and feedback-based quality management systems, and (4) supportive supervision. These interventions were delivered through multiple implementation strategies, including training and education, coaching and mentoring, learning collaboratives, interactive continuous learning, and routine data monitoring. Acceptability was the most consistently reported implementation outcome. Improvements in management effectiveness were identified across four domains: financial management, organizational climate and culture, resource management and utilization, and human resource management. Five studies also reported intermediate service-level outcomes including improvements in maternal and child health indicators.ConclusionInterventions aimed at strengthening PHC facility management in SSA primarily emphasize skill development, collaboration, and ongoing managerial support, typically delivered through multiple strategies. However, few studies have evaluated these interventions using rigorous experimental or pragmatic study designs. More robust implementation research is needed to identify effective strategies for strengthening PHC facility management and supporting the consistent implementation of national PHC guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03485-1.Keywords: Primary care, Leadership, Primary care facility management, Systematic review, Implementation science, Sub Saharan Africa

Background

Strong primary healthcare (PHC) is essential to achieving the health-related Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by 2030, which aim to reduce preventable child and maternal deaths, control epidemics, and promote mental well-being for all [1]. As defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), primary health care refers to a whole-of-society approach to health that includes health promotion, disease prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliative care delivered at the first level of the health system, serving as the main point of entry for individuals and communities into the broader health system [2, 3]. Universal health coverage (UHC) is a key SDG target that highlights the need for everyone to have access to necessary health services without financial hardship [4]. A robust PHC system is essential for achieving this goal, as up to 90% of essential health services required for UHC can be provided in the PHC sites [5–7]. Throughout this review, the terms ‘primary health care’ and ‘primary care’ are used interchangeably to refer to this first-level care system and the facilities and personnel responsible for its delivery.

Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) comprises 48 countries situated south of the Sahara Desert, with a combined population of approximately 1.2 billion people [8]. SSA carries a disproportionate share of the global burden of disease, accounting for the majority of preventable maternal and child deaths worldwide, as well as the highest burden of infectious diseases including HIV, tuberculosis, and malaria [9]. Strengthening PHC delivery in this region is therefore critical not only for improving individual health outcomes but for achieving broader health system resilience and equity. Although PHC is central to health system performance, effective facility-level management is a critical determinant of service quality, resource use, and patient experience in PHC settings [10]. Despite Sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries’ efforts to expand government-run PHC services for better coverage, studies highlight quality issues due to poor service delivery [11, 12]. Primary care managers are key actors in ensuring quality by managing daily operations and implementing national PHC guidelines effectively [13].

Primary care facility managers face a complex mix of challenges that prevent effective service delivery, including weak infrastructure, limited human resources, and shortage of medical supplies [14, 15]. Healthcare workers often lack adequate training and support when they are promoted to managerial roles, further impeding the development of strong health care systems [16–19]. The consequences of weak management capacity at the facility level are tangible and well-documented. Studies have shown that facilities with stronger management practices demonstrate better adherence to clinical guidelines, improved supply chain management, and higher staff retention, all of which directly shape service delivery quality [20, 21] and, in turn, may influence patient care and potentially patient outcomes. Yet despite this evidence, investments in PHC manager training and support remain chronically underfunded relative to clinical training programs.

This lack of focus on management capacity and effectiveness is further highlighted by the limited efforts to measure and improve management effectiveness in SSA [22–25]. Despite available tools and evidence from both high-income countries (HICs) and some emerging examples in SSA, there remains a gap in establishing consistent and generalizable tools for measuring and evidence for strategies to strengthen management effectiveness in SSA designed to improve PHC delivery. It is worth noting that there is no established core set of indicators to measure leadership and management in global health, particularly in PHC management [24, 26]. The Global Reference List of 100 Core Health Indicators is a standardized set of indicators developed by the global health community to provide concise information on health status, trends, and responses at national and global levels [26]. It supports countries and partners in monitoring health priorities by using harmonized indicators across four domains: health status, risk factors, service coverage, and health systems. Only one indicator addresses leadership and management within the Health System Domain under the Governance subdomain, focusing solely on the existence of a national health sector policy, strategy, or plan, with no indicators specific to health facility management [27].

The absence of standardized measures for assessing management practice and effectiveness in SSA creates a major barrier to improving the quality of primary care delivery. Implementation science, a field focused on studying methods to improve the uptake of evidence-based practices into routine healthcare settings, could be used to bridge this gap [28, 29]. Synthesizing this evidence offers decision-makers, policymakers, and researchers a consolidated view of what has worked and in what contexts, supporting more informed selection of strategies and further investigation into their feasibility and sustainability.

To ensure this review addresses a true gap in the synthesized literature, we conducted a preliminary search for existing systematic reviews on PHC management interventions in SSA. No existing systematic reviews were identified that synthesized evidence on implementation strategies and outcomes specifically targeting PHC managers in SSA at the time of this study. While reviews examining healthcare management more broadly exist, these have predominantly focused on secondary -level hospital settings or high-income country contexts and do not address the unique implementation challenges and health system structures present in SSA primary care settings.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a systematic review using thematic synthesis, following the methodology described by Thomas and Harden, 2008; which provides a structured approach to synthesizing findings across qualitative and descriptive studies [30]. Where quantitative data were reported, these were descriptive in nature and were incorporated to supplement and contextualize qualitative findings. We registered the systematic review protocol in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO # CRD42023445520) and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [31]. The completed PRISMA checklist is provided in Additional file 1: Table A.

Search strategy

We searched the following databases: PubMed, Embase (Elsevier), Global Index Medicus, CINAHL (EBSCO), Web of Science Core Collection (SCI-Expanded, SSCI, A&HCI, ESCI), Cochrane Library (Wiley, CENTRAL), SciELO Citation Index (Clarivate), and WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS). The search covered January 1, 2012 – December 31, 2022. The search strategy focused on the concepts: primary health care, interventions, strategies, leaders/managers, management effectiveness outcome (skills/capacities gained) and SSA (see Additional file 2).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included peer-reviewed articles and grey literature with a specific emphasis on interventions and strategies aimed at improving primary healthcare facility management in SSA, published between January 2012 and December 2022. The 10-year timeframe was selected to capture evidence generated following the publication of Proctor et al.‘s Implementation Outcome Framework in 2011 [32], which provided a standardized language for classifying and reporting implementation outcomes in health settings. Studies were included only if they reported both an intervention or strategy and at least one implementation or management effectiveness outcome, as our aim was to capture a comprehensive picture of what was implemented, how it was delivered, and what outcomes were reported. Studies were excluded if they did not report an intervention or strategy, reported outcomes unrelated to management effectiveness, focused on the wrong population, or if the full text was unavailable. To maintain conciseness in the main text, full criteria with justification for each are available in Additional File 1: Table B.

Study selection and data extraction

Our team used Covidence [33] to conduct abstract and full text screening. Both stages employed a double-blind approach, in which each article was independently assessed by two reviewers at a time (EB, SU, AD and ST) without knowledge of each other’s decisions. Discrepancies at each stage were resolved by a third reviewer (other than the two who reviewed), with weekly team meetings held throughout the process to monitor progress and reach consensus on borderline cases. During the abstract screening, reviewers assessed whether articles reported an intervention, implementation strategy, and management effectiveness outcome. Articles meeting these initial criteria proceeded to full-text review, where reviewers assessed whether the intervention and outcomes were situated in a PHC facility setting in SSA and met our inclusion criteria of reporting a comprehensive picture of intervention, delivery strategy, and outcome. Articles meeting full-text inclusion criteria were then advanced to data extraction.

Data synthesis

We conducted a qualitative thematic synthesis following Thomas and Harden [30], to examine trends across the nine included studies. This approach was selected because the available literature on PHC management interventions in SSA is predominantly qualitative or descriptive, and thematic synthesis allows for rigorous identification of patterns and themes across heterogeneous study designs. Given the limited number of studies, findings should be interpreted as preliminary thematic patterns rather than saturated themes. Thematic coding was conducted independently by two reviewers (EB and SU) using Excel; disagreements were resolved by ST and AU. Potential conflicts in coding of interventions, strategies, and outcomes were discussed through weekly team meetings among EB, SU, ST, and LH until consensus was reached. Implementation strategies were mapped to the cluster areas of the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) framework [34, 35], which provided a standardized typology for grouping and describing delivery strategies. Implementation outcomes were classified using Proctor’s Implementation Outcome Framework [32], which had a structured categorization of implementation outcomes. Management effectiveness areas were identified through thematic categorization.

Quality assessment

To assess the quality of the included studies, we used the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist, adapting it to our research question as needed [36]. Due to the limited number of studies, none were excluded on the basis of quality assessment. However, the outcomes of our quality assessment were reported.

Results

The search strategy yielded 3752 studies. After removal of 1,568 duplicates, we screened 2,184 titles and abstracts, with 38 proceeding to full-text review. Nine studies met final inclusion criteria, all of which were peer-reviewed articles. Of the nine databases, two (IRIS and Africa Journal online) returned no relevant records. Exclusion reasons for the 29 full-text articles are detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), including studies that did not report an intervention or strategy (n = 17), reported outcomes unrelated to management effectiveness (n = 5), had no management effectiveness outcomes (n = 6), involved the wrong population (n = 6), or had unavailable full text (n = 1).

Overview of selected studies

The nine case studies were conducted across six SSA countries: Ethiopia (n = 3) [37–39], South Africa (n = 2) [40, 41], and one each in Botswana [42], Kenya [43], Tanzania [44], and Zambia [45] (Table 1). Study designs included quantitative (n = 4), mixed methods (n = 3), and qualitative approaches (n = 2), with durations ranging from 11 months to four years. Interventions targeted various levels of the health system: facility, sub-district, and district and focused on different groups of PHC managers, including facility managers, unit managers, and district health managers responsible for overseeing service delivery and staff performance. The interventions to improve management effectiveness differed in duration and structure, a one-year certificate program featuring mobile health communication platforms in Zambia [45], and multi-year action learning cycles in South Africa [40]. Overview of interventions for enhancing management

The interventions in the studies can be broadly categorized into four main types: training and capacity building programs [37–39, 45], peer-to-peer learning [16, 42], integrated quality improvement system using audit and feedback of management practices [43], and supportive supervision [44] (see Table 2). The development of these interventions varied across contexts. For example, in Zambia, a formative assessment conducted by a national university informed intervention development, implemented in partnership with development partners at the request of the Ministry of Health [45]. In Kenya, interventions were derived from the national strategy on the quality model for quality management and an integrated primary health care quality measurement electronic application from Germany, adapted with support from foreign development partners and the Kenyan Ministry of Health’s Department of Standards and Regulatory Services [43]. In South Africa, the two included studies were part of a collaborative work involving health managers, researchers, and local health sector policymakers through service-research partnerships and participatory intervention design efforts [40, 41]. In Ethiopia, three studies utilized the same intervention; a locally adopted version of the USAID’s In-service leadership, management, and governance syllabus for training health professionals to master practices and competencies for managers and governance developed in 2016, with variations in the target group and level of the health system represented. These interventions involved leadership development manuals with in-service training for healthcare managers using training guidelines developed by the Federal Ministry of Health and development partners [37–39].

Reported outcomes

Implementation outcomes

Implementation outcome classification below reflects retrospective mapping of study-reported findings to Proctor’s framework by the review team; no included study used this framework prospectively. For implementation outcomes, acceptability emerged as a consistent outcome across all studies In addition, six studies reported adaptation, appropriateness, and feasibility. Only one study reported scale-up of an intervention to a national level [45], with Ministry of Health adopting intervention and implementation strategies.

Management effectiveness

The key domains of management effectiveness that emerged in the included studies are: improved financial management, enhanced organizational climate and culture, improved resource management and utilization, and improved human resource management (see Table 4). Improved financial management is demonstrated through increased health facility-level budget utilization and improved resource mobilization. Enhanced organizational climate and culture measured by favorable work climate scores and the development of a collaborative environment, promoting communication, ownership, motivation, innovation, and problem-solving capacity. Improved resource management and utilization facilitated through enhanced data quality, strategic planning, and overall improvement in information utilization culture. Improved human resource management are presented through reported staff willingness to work together and engage in on-the-job training among facility managers.

Management effectiveness outcomes

Service and clinical improvements

Quantitative outcomes

Four studies reported improvement in service provision and clinical indicators [37, 38, 43, 45].

Clinically, Argaw found maternal and child health performance scores significantly improved in facilities receiving locally developed leadership, management and government intervention package and delivery strategy (t = 5.43, p < 0.001) [37]. Bayou reported increases in institutional delivery rates (40% to 80%) and antenatal care retention (46% to 82%) improvements [38] among those receiving management intervention package. Foster reported improvement in HIV-exposed infant testing (60% to 83%), ANC coverage (35% to 62%), and fully immunized children (6% to 80%) among those receiving competency-based blended learning program for leadership and management practice.

At the service level, Bayou reported improvements in facility data quality (60% to 80%) and budget utilization (60% to 99%) [38]. Marx reported average change improvements in waiting times (15.78%), supplies (26.17%), and drugs (12.78%) [43].

Qualitative outcomes

At the service level, Foster noted shorter wait times, improved patient satisfaction, and increased community outreach [45]. Bayou highlighted improved staff communication, motivation, and problem-solving capacity [38].

Contextual factors enhancing implementation strategy uptake

The implementation strategies were facilitated by contextual factors characterized by stakeholder engagement from the outset, involving collaborative intervention design and solution prioritization with local policymakers, community leaders, and health facilities. Studies emphasized a step-by-step process, including adequate onboarding, workshops, and continuous learning as formal training methods, supplemented by targeted mentorship from existing district or provincial experts to improve health facility management. This participatory approach formed the basis for strengthening management effectiveness, with primary healthcare playing a central role in each country’s health system, supported by health governing institutions.

Limitations recognized by the studies included a lack of baseline information, non-blinding of interventions to data collectors, the challenge of conclusively determining factors influencing improvement, absence of control groups in some studies, potential bias in supervisions made by project staff, and challenges in maintaining energy around local area groups. Several studies recommended long-term monitoring of intervention effects, emphasizing the need for sustained evaluation beyond the study’s immediate timeframe. Additionally, economic evaluation information in included studies, including cost-related details, was absent in all studies.

Quality appraisal

Utilizing the JBI-derived quality appraisal tool and following a two-person quality appraisal, the studies’ quality percentages ranged from a low of 46% to a high of 100% on a 16-item quality appraisal questionnaire, where 100% means high quality (see Additional file 3: Table F).

Discussion

This systematic review offers a nuanced understanding of various published interventions, targeted areas, strategies, methodological approaches, and outcomes aimed at improving primary healthcare management. We found that there are limited reports on the implementation of existing interventions to improve selected areas of primary healthcare management, with only nine studies available. This scarcity of evidence is itself a significant finding, reflecting a broader pattern observed in the health workforce development literature, where investment in management training has consistently lagged investment in clinical skills development in low- and middle-income country contexts [16].

The interventions identified in this review primarily focused on skill development, collaboration, and ongoing managerial support. This is consistent with findings reported elsewhere in the broader PHC quality-improvement literature, which suggests multicomponent interventions may be more effective than single-strategy approaches [46]. Although bundling of strategies was observed, the limited information available on how effectively these strategies worked individually or in combination remains a gap, which could be further tested using Hybrid type 2 or type 3 studies [47].

The limited reporting of service-level and clinical outcomes across included studies reflects a well-documented challenge in management effectiveness research, namely establishing a clear causal pathway between management practice improvement and patient-level outcomes [48, 49]. While direct impact assessment on population health outcomes is limited, some studies included in this review reported improvements in service-level and clinic-level outcomes. However, there needs to be rigorous controlled or pragmatic trial assessing such causal pathway.

We found variability in measurement tools used to capture implementation and effectiveness outcomes. This is not unique to SSA, as the broader PHC quality improvement literature has similarly struggled with outcome measurement standardization, making cross-study comparisons difficult [50]. The development and validation of context-appropriate management effectiveness measures for PHC settings in SSA represents an important priority for future research.

It is important to note that the nine studies included in this review represent only six of the 48 countries that comprise SSA, namely Ethiopia, South Africa, Botswana, Kenya, Tanzania, and Zambia. The generalizability of these findings to other SSA countries should therefore be considered with caution. However, given the shared health system challenges, disease burden, and resource constraints across many SSA settings, the intervention approaches and implementation strategies identified in this review may offer useful insights for PHC management improvement efforts across the broader region.

Limitations

This review may have missed relevant interventions despite thorough search and inclusion criteria. Grey literature was identified primarily via databases, not hand-searching, all nine included studies were ultimately peer-reviewed publications; the review may therefore be subject to publication bias, as program-level or unpublished implementation experiences that did not result in a peer-reviewed publication would not have been captured. The definition of PHC management varies across contexts, and study heterogeneity limits comparability. Authorship positionality may influence framing, though most authors are SSA-based with local expertise. Consistent with our approach of not excluding studies based on quality score given the limited evidence base, it is worth noting that methodological quality varied considerably across included studies (46% to 100%), which should be considered when interpreting the overall strength of the findings. The classification of strategies and outcomes according to the ERIC and Proctor frameworks involved a degree of interpretive judgment by the review team, particularly where studies did not explicitly use these frameworks themselves. While disagreements were resolved through team discussion and consensus, some subjectivity in this mapping process cannot be ruled out.

Recommendations

Considering management as a core component of the health system’s leadership and governance building block, we learned that there is a lack of studies showing interventions and strategies aimed at enhancing the capacity of primary health care managers. Therefore, there is a need for more reports on demonstrating effective interventions for PHC managers in SSA and their implementation outcome and how it affects service-level and clinical outcomes. Implementation research can help accelerate the uptake and integration of effective intervention to improve PHC management. Given the shortages of infrastructure, equipment, and necessities, studies will need to examine how these factors influence the quality of health facilities and health facility management. Future studies or policy consideration could apply interventions and strategies identified in this review to enhance primary health care management effectiveness through various engagement processes identified in this review.

Additionally, it is essential to clearly distinguish the role of PHC managers in different SSA settings through a broader landscape analysis to design specific tools and interventions for each [24]. We believe the distinction between the two concepts – leadership and management -- could be difficult to discern in some settings where PHC managers have authority to influence strategic planning and making fundamental changes in policies and guidelines. The studies emphasize the importance of validation, consistency, and addressing gaps such as the absence of control groups, potential bias, and challenges in maintaining energy in local groups, especially when using participatory or co-creating designs. Implementing interventions with an implementation science approach, utilizing research frameworks, models, and theories, is necessary for a deeper understanding of implementation processes, outcomes, and effectiveness on health outcomes. Additionally, there is a need for better measures to understand what interventions specifically target in terms of management, aiming not only for improved management outcomes but also sustained improvements in population health.

Conclusion

This review highlights the emerging but limited evidence base on interventions aimed at strengthening PHC facility management in six SSA countries. The available evidence suggests that management capacity strengthening efforts hold promise for improving PHC service delivery, though this relationship has not been directly demonstrated by the studies included in this review. Effective management is essential for translating policy and system investments into improved service delivery. While evidence remains limited, the findings support multi-component, participatory approaches, and provide a foundation for future implementation research to strengthen PHC and support universal health coverage. Future research should prioritize pragmatic implementation studies, particularly Hybrid type 2 and type 3 designs, to simultaneously test effectiveness and implementation outcomes and generate rigorous, context-sensitive evidence to inform policy and support scale-up across the region.
