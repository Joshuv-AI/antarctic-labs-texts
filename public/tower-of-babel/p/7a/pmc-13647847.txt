Acute myeloid leukemia following treatment of malignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney: a case report
Authors: Majid Vafaie, Elahe Jafari, Manoochehr Mahram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMalignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney (MRTK) is a rare and highly aggressive pediatric malignancy with a poor prognosis. We report a rare case of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) developing after successful treatment of MRTK in a child and discuss its clinical course and possible therapy-related etiology.Case presentationWe successfully treated a 12-year-old Iranian girl of Turkish ethnicity with stage III or higher MRTK in Qazvin, Iran, using surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Approximately 7 months after completion of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and 16 months after the initial diagnosis of MRTK, the patient developed acute myeloid leukemia.ConclusionThis case highlights the development of acute myeloid leukemia after treatment for malignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney in a pediatric patient. Long-term follow-up is essential for the early detection of secondary malignancies.

Introduction

Malignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney (MRTK) is a rare and highly aggressive malignancy in pediatric oncology with tendency to metastasize to the lungs and brain [1]. This tumor has poor prognosis with current therapeutic protocols [2].MRT of the kidney usually affects children less than 2 years of age [3].

Epidemiologically, it contains 1.8% of all pediatric renal tumors in the National Wilms’ Tumor Study [4]. Although no standard treatment is defined for this malignancy, patients are treated according to institutional preferences. MRTK is an extremely aggressive neoplasm with a short survival time [5]. According to this research, the overall 3-year survival rate in patients with MRTK ranges from 12% to 38.4%, with higher rate of survival in ages older than 24 months [6, 7].

In this study, we present a rare case of therapy-related acute myeloid leukemia occurring after successful treatment and complete remission of MRTK, highlighting the clinical implications and potential risk factors associated with this rare sequence.

Case presentation

A 12-year-old girl of Turkish ethnicity was admitted to Qods Children’s Hospital, Qazvin, Iran, on November 23, 2021 with chief complaint of abdominal pain from one month earlier and right foot pain of one-week duration. The parents reported weight loss and decreased appetite during recent weeks and nausea without vomiting in recent days in the girl. At physical examination, pallor was obvious and a large abdominal mass in right flank was palpable. The liver and spleen could not be examined well because of the large mass mentioned. At the time of admission, the patient’s weight and height were 44 kg and 158 cm, respectively and vital signs were: Temperature was 37 °C, pulse rate was 140 beats per minute., Blood Pressure: 112/75 mm Hg and O2 saturation: 97%.

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated severe anemia (Hb 7.3 g/dL, Hct 26.8%), leukocytosis (WBC 17,700/mm³; neutrophils 87%), and thrombocytosis (410,800/mm³). Inflammatory markers were significantly elevated (ESR 66 mm/h, CRP 133.8 mg/L). Serum electrolytes, renal and hepatic function tests were within normal ranges. Urinalysis revealed gross hematuria (> 100 RBC/HPF) with mild proteinuria.

In addition, bone marrow aspiration was also done, which showed normal trilineage haemopoiesis.

Sonography of the abdomen and pelvis showed a large mass with vascularity, extending from right upper to the right lower quadrants with a size of 194 × 94 mm. The mass appeared to arise from the right kidney and demonstrated extension into the right hepatic lobe, with the hepatic component measuring approximately 109 × 56 mm.

Chest X-ray and echocardiography showed normal results. Abdominal CT scan with contrast showed a huge heterogeneous mass with necrotic changes, mainly retroperitoneal with pressure on the right kidney and pancreas, and shifting aorta to the left side. It did not show any obvious invasion of the tumor into the aorta or inferior vena cava. The span of the spleen was 109 mm and the psoas muscle margins were not clearly observed (Fig. 1).

After obtaining the above-mentioned evidence, the patient was referred to a pediatric surgeon, and on November 28, 2021, a surgical biopsy of the tumor was performed under general anesthesia. The surgeon reported necrosis and massive hemorrhage inside the tumor; he had to suture the margins following hemostasis. Following surgery, a decrease in hemoglobin level was observed, so a transfusion of packed cells was done. The report of the pathologist revealed a diagnosis compatible with a malignant small round cell tumor and with extensive necrosis.

Based on the available clinical, radiological, and pathological findings, the tumor was considered at least Stage III and was managed as an unfavorable-histology renal tumor. Because of the huge size of the mass and its highly hemorrhagic property, the mass was considered inoperable at this stage; therefore, chemotherapy protocol including Vincristine, Adriamycin (doxorubicin), Cyclophosphamide, and Etoposide was implemented. The cumulative doses administered during treatment were 16,896 mg for cyclophosphamide, 2,400 mg for etoposide, and 250 mg for doxorubicin.

After six cycles of chemotherapy, significant tumor shrinkage was documented on follow-up ultrasonography and CT imaging. Consequently, the patient was referred again for surgical resection, and on January 15, 2022, she underwent exploratory laparotomy with attempted complete tumor excision. The surgeon reported a huge mass extending from diaphragm to the pelvis, containing inferior vena cava and with adhesion to it and to iliac vessels and with hemorrhagic property; that rather than total excision of the tumor, right nephrectomy, adrenalectomy and regional lymphadenectomy were also done (Fig. 2). Histopathological examination of the nephrectomy specimen confirmed malignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney (MRTK), Stage III.

After all therapeutic performances, the patient was discharged with good condition, with continuing chemotherapy for next six months and subsequently, a course of irradiation, including twenty sessions to the tumor bed at a dose of 1,080 cGy [8].

In March 2023, approximately 16 months after the initial diagnosis of MRTK and about 7 months after completion of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, the patient was readmitted because of severe pallor, malaise, and vomiting. Complete blood count revealed WBC 8,700/mm³ with 90% atypical cells, hemoglobin 8 g/dL, and platelet count 36,000/mm³.

Peripheral blood smear and bone marrow aspiration were obtained (Fig. 3), which showed the diagnosis of Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML). Furthermore, Bone marrow examination revealed acute myeloid leukemia, and flow cytometric immunophenotyping confirmed AML-M1. HLA-DR expression was negative. Detailed immunophenotypic marker analysis and cytogenetic/molecular studies, including karyotyping and FISH analyses, were not available in our center at the time of diagnosis (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Despite the poor prognostic and aggressive properties of MRTK and the low rate of cure [6, 9, 10], we achieved successful treatment after surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, as mentioned above. Although having hypercalcemia is expected in cases with MRTK, we did not find it in our patient. The reason for hypercalcemia in MRTK is hypersecretion of parathormone by tumor cells.

There are various viewpoints about the therapeutic protocols for MRTK. Based on the National Wilms’ Tumor Studies (NWTS) protocol, MRTK is treated by an enlarged nephrectomy, followed by chemotherapy (including carboplatin, cisplatin, and cyclophosphamide) for 24 weeks and radiotherapy. The SIOP (International Society of Pediatric Oncology) treatment consists of first chemotherapy followed by surgery, then postoperative chemotherapy (including etoposide, carboplatin, ifosfamide, and epirubicin) for 34 weeks and radiotherapy postoperatively [11].

Our management strategy was largely consistent with the SIOP approach and achieved satisfactory control of the primary tumor, enabling successful surgical resection following neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Nevertheless, the patient developed secondary AML-M1 approximately 16 months after the diagnosis of malignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney and seven months after completion of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, representing a rare and serious late complication.

Although the occurrence of AML could be coincidental, the temporal association with prior chemotherapy and radiotherapy raises the possibility of a therapy-related etiology. However, cytogenetic and molecular analyses required for definitive classification were not available in this patient. Several factors have been proposed to contribute to second malignancies; however, in our case, no specific risk factor beyond prior chemotherapy and radiotherapy could be identified. The risk of second malignancies is reported to be 3–10 times higher than in the general population [12].

Regarding a multi-center cohort study in Taiwan, among patients who developed a second malignancy, AML was the most frequent second cancer, followed by ALL, CNS tumors and sarcomas. Although the patients of the study of Taiwan were diagnosed with a second cancer at any time after primary cancer diagnosis, a large proportion of the second malignancies were diagnosed at early onset stages. They pointed to some reports by others, that believed secondary hematological malignancies (mainly ALL and AML) had a shorter time interval from the primary diagnosis to developing a second cancer [13]. The results of the study by Moser et al. in Germany which assessed second malignancies after treatment of childhood non-Hodgkin lymphoma revealed that solid malignancies were the most frequent second malignancies followed by acute myeloid leukemia (AML)/myelodysplastic syndrome and second lymphoid malignancy [14].

According to previous studies, some chemotherapy drugs, principally alkylating agents, increase the risk of leukemia and there is strong evidence that etoposide can cause malignancy [12, 15]. The patient had received cyclophosphamide and etoposide, both of which have been associated with an increased risk of secondary leukemia. Nevertheless, in the absence of cytogenetic and molecular studies, therapy-related AML could not be definitively confirmed and remains a presumptive diagnosis based on clinical correlation.

Radiotherapy is known as an important risk factor for forming second cancers, although its probability in adulthood is said to be less than 10% [12, 16]. AML and myelodysplastic syndrome are known as more common second malignancies in individuals exposed to radiation for a primary cancer [17].

Our case was limited by the lack of cytogenetic and molecular analyses, including karyotyping and FISH studies, as well as the inability to evaluate genetic susceptibility. Therefore, the classification of AML as therapy-related remains presumptive and is based primarily on temporal association with prior treatment rather than definitive molecular evidence.

Conclusion

This case highlights the occurrence of acute myeloid leukemia following multimodal treatment for malignant rhabdoid tumor of the kidney in a pediatric patient. Although a therapy-related etiology is suspected based on the temporal association with previous treatment, definitive confirmation was not possible because cytogenetic and molecular analyses were unavailable. This case underscores the importance of careful long-term follow-up for pediatric cancer survivors to facilitate early detection of secondary malignancies.
