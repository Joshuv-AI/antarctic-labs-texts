Associations of Haller index and manual depth measurement with clinical findings and pulmonary function in children with pectus excavatum: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Begüm Yörük, Yasemin Mocan Çaglar, Özge Ülgen, Sinem Can Oksay, Gülay Bilgin, Deniz Mavi Tortop, Saniye Girit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPectus excavatum (PE) is the most common congenital chest wall deformity. Its severity is usually assessed by chest computed tomography (CT) based indices such as the Haller index (HI) and correction index (CI). However, evidence supporting non-imaging anthropometric methods is limited. Manual depth measurement (MDM) is a simple, noninvasive bedside method that may offer clinical value.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study included children diagnosed with pectus excavatum and followed at a tertiary center between 2017 and 2024. Demographics, pulmonary function tests (PFTs), symptoms, comorbidities, and echocardiographic data were analyzed. HI and CI were measured using chest CT scans. MDM values recorded during follow-up were retrieved from physical exam notes.ResultsA total of 127 patients were included, with a median age of 7.25 years (IQR, 7.05). Median HI was 2.70 (IQR, 0.56), median MDM was 2.00 cm (IQR, 1.35), and median CI was 16% (IQR, 6.7). MDM was significantly correlated with HI (p = 0.012). ROC analysis showed that MDM moderately but significantly discriminated severe deformity defined as HI> 2.5, with an optimal cut-off value of 1.75 cm. Respiratory symptoms and comorbidities were present in 39.4% and 38.6% of patients, respectively. Pulmonary function impairment was observed in 19% of patients, predominantly with a restrictive pattern. HI> 2.5 was associated with reduced forced vital capacity (FVC) and total lung capacity (TLC), whereas the association between MDM> 1.75 cm and TLC <80% weakened after adjustment for age.ConclusionMDM is a practical bedside measurement that correlates with HI and involves no radiation exposure. An MDM threshold of 1.75 cm was associated with HI> 2.5 in the CT-evaluated subgroup and may support conservative follow-up when interpreted together with clinical and functional findings. Background

Pectus excavatum (PE) is the most common congenital malformation of the chest wall [1]. Surgical intervention is required for severe deformities. Accurate anthropometric measurement parameters are essential for clinical follow-up to assess progression in children without severe deformities. Quantitatively evaluating the extent of sternal depression in a clinical situation without imaging is helpful for both patient and clinician.

Currently, numerous anthropometric indices (AIs) are employed to assess the severity of PE. The haller index (HI) and the correction index (CI) are the most commonly recognized and reliable metrics. They are used to determine whether patients need surgery [2, 3]. External depth measurements, including three-dimensional scanners, caliper measurements, and manual depth measurement (MDM), are procedures that do not expose children to radiation [4–7].

For pediatric pulmonologists, the association of these AIs with clinical findings and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) is crucial for patient follow-up. A review of the literature on the impact of HI on PFTs reveals a correlation between HI and the severity of deformity, thus establishing HI as a metric for assessing the adverse effects of PE on lung capacity [8–10].

The symptoms experienced by children with PE include dyspnoea, chest pain, and rapid exhaustion during physical exercise. Research suggests a potential association between AIs and these symptoms, with some studies indicating that patients with elevated HI and CI may experience more severe symptoms [11, 12]. It is worth noting that many studies have examined age’s impact on PFTs and the correlation between HI and restrictive lung disease in children with PE [13–15].

A chest CT scan is required to assess HI and CI. In recent years, a few studies have assessed the compatibility of MDM, an external depth measurement approach that does not necessitate imaging, with HI and CI [7].

The primary outcome of the study was the correlation between MDM and the HI in patients with available chest CT data. Secondary outcomes included the discriminatory ability of MDM to identify patients with HI > 2.5, the associations between HI and MDM thresholds and PFT parameters, and the relationships of AIs with symptoms, chest CT findings, and comorbidities.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was retrospective and cross-sectional. Patients aged 3 months to 18 years, with a diagnosis of PE, had not undergone surgery for PE, who were followed up at our tertiary care hospital between 2017 and 2024, were included. Patients were divided into two age groups: ≤11 years and > 11 years. Patients with irregular follow-up, cerebral palsy or asymmetric deformities were excluded (Fig. 1).

Study flow diagram

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Data from patient files and electronic medical records included demographics, imaging, PFTs, and MDMs. Symptoms assessed included cough, exercise intolerance, chest discomfort, and wheezing. Not noted as a symptom, aesthetic anxiety was commonplace in all patients. Comorbidities included asthma, scoliosis, skeletal or connective tissue abnormalities and chest wall–related disorders. In this study, the diagnosis of PE was clinically established through physical examination in all patients; the HI, however, could only be calculated in patients for whom chest CT data were available.

Anthropometric indices

Three methods, including HI, CI, and MDM, were applied. A pediatric pulmonologist performed all measurements.

Haller index

The measurement is obtained from the CT scan. HI was calculated by dividing the widest transverse diameter of the chest on axial chest CT images by the shortest anteroposterior distance between the posterior surface of the sternum and the anterior surface of the vertebral body [8]. Patients with HI > 2.5 were considered to have severe PE deformity.

Correction index

CI is calculated from chest CT images by measuring the distance from the anterior surface of spine to the most depressed point of the sternum ‘posterior surface of the sternum and the anterior surface of the spine.

A: maximum distance between the anterior surface of the spine and the anterior chest wall B: the total distance from the anterior surface of the spine to the outermost point of the chest wall. The formula used is [(B-A) /B]X100.

Manual depth measurement

MDM was measured as the external head-of-bed measurement of the most prominent pectus depression on the anterior chest wall. The measurement was taken from the deepest point of the depression on the sternum. A rigid, flat reference was placed horizontally along the edges of the anterior chest wall adjacent to the deformity, and the perpendicular distance from this horizontal plane to the deepest point of the depression was measured in centimetres using a ruler [6] (Fig. 2). MDM was used to assess the external depth of the pectus depression, not to measure the entire anteroposterior diameter of the chest. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the utilisation of a ruler was deemed the optimal approach due to its integration within a standard outpatient examination protocol. This method was selected to ensure simplicity, cost-effectiveness, radiation-free operation, and practicality in the context of bedside assessments during conservative follow-up or vacuum bell treatment [7]. For each patient, the MDM value recorded at the first clinical visit was used for analysis. All MDM measurements were performed during routine outpatient follow-up by the same pediatric pulmonologist.

Schematic illustration of manual depth measurement

A rigid, flat reference is placed horizontally across the anterior chest wall at the level of the adjacent anterior chest wall margins. Manual depth measurement is defined as the perpendicular distance from this horizontal plane to the deepest point of the anterior chest wall depression

Pulmonary function tests

PFTs were assessed by measuring spirometric parameters and lung volumes in patients older than 5 years. All measures were conducted by a specialised pulmonary laboratory technician utilizing a Minibox+ (PulmOne Advanced Medical Devices, Ra’ananna, Israel). The results were acquired following the conventional methodologies outlined by European Respiratory Society (ERS) and American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines [16, 17]. The following spirometry parameters were assessed: forced expiratory volume in first second (FEV1), forced vital capacity (FVC), FEV1/FVC ratio, total lung capacity (TLC), RV. All tests were conducted during asymptomatic periods, and the results were expressed as percentages of the predicted normal values, which were automatically calculated. Values above 80% were considered within the normal range. Additionally, relevant prediction equations were used to calculate the lower limits of normal (LLN), defined as values above the 5th percentile (z> -1.64) [18]. For patients who underwent multiple spirometry evaluations within the same time frame, the average value for each parameter was used. An obstructive pattern was defined as an FEV1/FVC ratio below 80% or LLN, along with an FEV1 below 80% or LLN. A restrictive pattern was identified when the FEV1/FVC ratio was at or above 80% or LLN, but FVC was below 80% or LLN.

Radiological evaluation and chest CT scans

Due to the potential risks of radiation exposure to the paediatric population, it is not standard practice to perform chest CT scans on all patients. Chest CT was performed exclusively in patients deemed to be clinically necessary, i.e. when the deformity was more pronounced, respiratory symptoms were present, comorbidities were present, pathological findings were detected on physical examination, or additional anatomical evaluation was required. In patients with available chest CT data, HI was calculated from the chest CT examination performed as part of the initial diagnostic evaluation following the first clinical visit. The interval between MDM assessment and chest CT examination was less than one month in these patients. Consequently, analyses involving HI and CI were conducted exclusively on patients with available chest CT data.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Chicago, USA), with significance set at p < 0.05. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using visual methods, including histograms and Q–Q plots, together with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on these assessments, continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test when expected cell counts were adequate or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were < 5. Correlations between the HI, MDM, CI, and age were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis.

HI > 2.5 was considered the threshold for severe deformity. The discriminatory power of MDM at this threshold was evaluated using ROC analysis, and the optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden index. The resulting MDM cut-off value was used to assess associations with PFT parameters. In logistic regression analysis, the association between.

HI > 2.5 and MDM > 1.75 cm with FEV1, FVC, and TLC < 80% was examined. Considering the potential influence of age on MDM values, the relationship between MDM > 1.75 cm and PFT parameters was further evaluated using age-adjusted models.

Sample size was calculated using G*Power for the correlation analysis between MDM and HI. Based on a previously reported correlation coefficient of r = 0.50 [6], a minimum of 32 patients was required for α = 0.05 and 85% power.

Results

The study included 127 patients, with a median age of 7.25 years (IQR, 7.05), of whom 73.2% (n = 93) were male. Patients were categorized into two age groups, ≤ 11 years and > 11 years, and most patients were aged ≤ 11 years (70.9%, n = 90). The median values for HI, MDM, and CI in all patients were 2.70 (IQR, 0.56), 2.00 cm (IQR, 1.35) and 16% (IQR, 6.7) respectively. At least one comorbidity was seen in 38.6% (n = 49) of the patients. The predominant comorbidities were asthma (16.5%) and scoliosis (7.9%). The prevalence of comorbidity varied by age category, with scoliosis being the most prevalent at 18.9% among patients older than 11 years. At least one respiratory symptom (cough, exertional dyspnea, chest discomfort, or wheeze) was recorded in 39.4% (n = 50) of the participants in the study (Table 1). Chest CT was conducted on 32 individuals, revealing abnormal findings in 40.6% (n = 13). The predominant pathological signs were atelectasis (22.2%) in children under 11 years and deviation of the heart and major vascular structures, along with cardiac compression, in individuals over 11 years (21.4%). Following cardiological assessment, 96 patients (75.5%) underwent echocardiography, which revealed abnormal findings in 17.7% (n = 17). Valve abnormality was the most common pathological finding across all age groups (Table 1). Spirometry results were assessed in 76 individuals (59.8%), revealing respiratory impairment in 19% (n = 14). In patients aged 11 years or younger (n = 41), the prevalence of pathology was 12.2% (n = 5), all of which were classified as a restrictive pattern. In patients older than 11 years (n = 35), the prevalence of pulmonary dysfunction was 25.7% (n = 9); a restrictive pattern was observed in 20% and an obstructive pattern in 5.7% (Table 1).

In the logistic regression analysis, an HI > 2.5 was significantly associated with FVC < 80% and TLC < 80%. In patients with HI > 2.5, the likelihood of FVC < 80% was 8 times higher, and the likelihood of TLC < 80% was 26 times higher (OR = 26.00; 95% CI: 1.12–604.49; p = 0.042). MDM > 1.75 cm was associated with TLC < 80% in univariate analysis; however, this association lost its significance after adjustment for age. No statistically significant associations were observed for the CI in relation to any of the PFTs (Table 4). MDM was observed to be elevated in patients with comorbidities (p = 0.013) and in individuals with pathological results on chest CT, the HI value exhibited a slight increase, nearing the threshold of significance (p = 0.07). However, it remained statistically insignificant (Table 5). The FEV1 z score was considerably reduced in individuals with comorbidities (p = 0.024). The prevalence of normal pulmonary function patterns was 67.9% in the cohort with comorbidities and 89.1% in the cohort without comorbidities (p = 0.041). The FEV1/FVC z score was significantly lower in symptomatic individuals than in asymptomatic ones (p = 0.035), with 76.5% exhibiting a normal respiratory pattern compared to 85.0% of asymptomatic patients (p = 0.02) (Table 6). Detailed parameter level results are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Symptoms were noted in 67.3% of individuals with comorbidities, whereas in 21.8% of those without comorbidities (p < 0.001). The prevalence of cough was 45.5% among patients with comorbidity, whereas it was merely 5.9% among those without comorbidity. The prevalence of cough was determined to be notably high (70.6%) and statistically significant (p = 0.017) among participants with asthma. Symptoms like cough, wheeze, and exertional dyspnea were frequently observed in people with chest wall involvement and skeletal system defects. Dyspnea on exertion was noted in 62.5% of patients with scoliosis.

Discussion

This study evaluated the relationship between AIs and PFTs and clinical findings in children with PE. A significant positive correlation was found between HI and MDM, suggesting that MDM may serve as a radiation-free parameter associated with CT-based anatomical severity and may be useful for outpatient follow-up without requiring repeated imaging. The MDM cutoff value determined for this purpose was 1.75 cm. Abnormalities in PFT were observed in approximately one-fifth of the patients, with a predominant restrictive pattern. Reduced FVC and TLC were more pronounced in patients with HI > 2.5. Although MDM > 1.75 cm showed a possible relationship with reduced TLC, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because MDM also increased with age. Abnormal PFT results were observed more frequently in symptomatic patients and those with comorbidities.

The severity of PE may be measured using multiple imaging methods or external thoracic measurements. However, it is most commonly measured using HI and CI. These two measurements provide a chest measurement based on cross-sectional imaging, including mainly chest CT scanning, increasing concerns about patient safety (exposure to radiation) and cost [19]. External depth measurements, such as three-dimensional scanners, caliper measurements, and MDM, are methods that do not expose children to radiation [4, 5]. External HI calculations performed by 3D scanners showed a weak correlation with actual HI, and their high cost was considered another disadvantage [20]. Another external measurement method is using a caliper to measure the external thorax size. Studies have suggested that this method helps monitor the defect in time, but cross-sectional imaging is still a more standard method while planning surgical correction [21]. The aim of this study was not to propose a method to replace CT; rather, it was to evaluate the relationship between MDM and HI, to determine whether MDM serves as a useful bedside measurement for identifying patients with an HI > 2.5 during clinical follow-up, and to assess the relationship between these thresholds and PFT parameters.

In recent years, limited studies have been conducted on external thoracic measurements used to determine PE severity [6, 7, 21]. The modified percent depth method developed by Snyder et al. enables a more practical measurement compared to the HI to evaluate PE severity, and it was emphasized that this method could reduce the need for routine imaging [22]. In a study performed by Jacobson et al., the median physician-estimated depth (PED) was found to be 2.5 cm. A positive correlation was observed between HI and PED. It was observed that Body Mass Index (BMI) affects PED, and it was shown that a PED value of ≥ 2.0 cm is a more precise and efficient screening tool, especially for slender patients. In conclusion, it was emphasised that the depth measurements taken by physicians during the examination could be used for preoperative evaluation and analysis while exposing patients to lower radiation levels [6]. In our study as well, MDM showed a significant association with HI and was associated with HI > 2.5 in the CT-evaluated subgroup. However, MDM should be regarded as a complementary bedside measurement that objectively documents the depth of external deformity during outpatient follow-up, rather than a tool for surgical decision-making or determining the need for CT imaging. This application may be particularly significant in children undergoing conservative management or receiving vacuum bell treatment. Indeed, in recent studies of the vacuum bell, the vacuum depth of external deformity, also known as the funnel depth, has been utilised as a practical longitudinal follow-up parameter. This has enabled the monitoring of morphological response during non-surgical treatment [23]. Consequently, MDM may facilitate the documentation of alterations in the visible depth of the deformity during conservative follow-up or vacuum bell therapy. However, MDM should not be interpreted as a standalone criterion for surgical referral.

In an article in which patients who had undergone surgical repair were compared with entirely healthy individuals, it was reported that external measurement with a caliper expressed as “modified percent depth” showed a slightly better performance compared to HI and CI, and it was emphasized that more research was needed for this to be used as a diagnostic test [21]. In addition, we included all PE patients in our study, including those with mild and severe cases, and examined a broader age range. We demonstrated that MDM is consistent with HI and identified a correlation between MDM and increasing age. These findings suggest that after PE develops in childhood, the deformity may progress with age and the symptoms may become more pronounced. This suggests that the deformity may become more evident during childhood and adolescence. However, since MDM is an external measurement, it may be influenced by BMI, subcutaneous tissue thickness, and chest wall structure. Therefore, MDM should be interpreted not as a replacement for HI, but rather as a radiation-free, complementary bedside measurement for clinical follow-up.

In individuals diagnosed with PE, impairment in PFTs may be observed due to sternum compression and reduced thoracic volume due to chest deformity, thereby inhibiting normal lung growth [13, 14, 24]. Both static and exercise PFTs have helped demonstrate the physiologic effect of the defect on the patient. Research on this subject indicates that the restrictive pattern is predominant and is characterized by exercise intolerance and chest pain during exercise [8, 14, 24, 25]. However, it can be stated that tests based on spirometry and lung volume measurement are more reliable, as these findings may also be associated with cardiovascular dysfunction [24, 25]. To date, many studies have evaluated the relationship between AIs and PFTs, particularly through the HI, and have reported a tendency for pulmonary function to decline as the severity of PE increases [14, 25]. Lawson et al.demonstrated that high HI values are particularly associated with restrictive physiology and increase the likelihood of reduced FVC and FEV1 [14]. In our study, PFT abnormalities were detected in approximately one-fifth of the patients, and a restrictive pattern was predominant. This rate was higher in patients over 11 years of age; this finding may be related to the progression of deformity with age, increased exertional dyspnoea, and accompanying conditions such as scoliosis and connective tissue anomalies. HI > 2.5 was associated with reduced FVC and TLC.

One of the main findings regarding MDM was that a threshold of 1.75 cm was associated with HI > 2.5 in the CT-evaluated subgroup. This result suggests that MDM not only measures the visible depth of the deformity during routine clinical examinations but can also provide a practical warning regarding which patients require closer attention. Particularly in children with an MDM value above 1.75 cm, it should be kept in mind that the deformity may have more pronounced anatomical deformity; the need for further evaluation should be reconsidered in conjunction with symptoms, comorbidities, increasing age, and PFT findings. The finding that MDM is associated with reduced TLC in univariate analysis also suggests that this measurement may accompany a decrease in thoracic volume in some patients. However, the weakening of this association after age adjustment indicates that MDM is not a standalone measure for determining functional impairment; it should be interpreted in conjunction with age, symptoms, comorbidities, and, when necessary, PFT/CT findings during the clinical decision-making process. This approach is consistent with previous pediatric surgical data showing that PE management is influenced by both anatomical and clinical factors. Jukić et al. reported that indications for Nuss repair included psychological and psychosocial problems, respiratory disorders, and cardiorespiratory disorders, and that higher HI values and higher BMI were associated with lower postoperative satisfaction [26]. These findings support the need to interpret anatomical measurements within a broader clinical framework rather than as isolated decision-making tools.

In patients with PE, common symptoms such as exercise intolerance, chest pain and cough and comorbidities such as scoliosis, skeletal system/connective tissue disorder and asthma are important in clinical progression [13, 27]. Knowing the effects of these clinical parameters on PFT is essential in terms of disease management. A correlation between clinical findings and PFT could not be demonstrated in a study in which 103 children with PE were evaluated. PFT impairment was found in 46% of the patients (42% obstructive, 5% restrictive). Although the percentage of comorbidity of asthma was 21% in the obstructive pathology group, this is far from explaining this finding. The authors mentioned the possibility of overreporting or underreporting because the diagnosis of asthma was based on previous history rather than objective criteria [13]. In our study, comorbidities such as asthma, scoliosis, and connective tissue disorders were found in approximately 38% of the subjects. Patients reporting symptoms suggestive of asthma constituted 16% of the group. This value is similar to the general prevalence of asthma in our community (12.4%) and the prevalence of asthma in the Caucasian population (17%) [28, 29]. In addition, it should be kept in mind that our evaluation may be subjective because the diagnosis of asthma was based on responses given to questions related to the history of wheezing, coughing on exertion, or use of bronchodilators rather than on objective criteria.

Chest CT examinations have demonstrated that pathological findings are observed more commonly with increasing age [12]. In the literature, it has been reported that the effects of PE on the heart and great vessels increase with age [30]. In our study, cardiac compression was observed in 12% (n = 4) of our patients who underwent chest CT. We observed that findings such as cardiac compression and significant vessel deviation were more common in our patients aged over 11 years. In parallel, we observed that valve anomaly was the most common pathological finding on echocardiographic examination in all age groups.

It has been reported that respiratory problems are common, besides spine disorders, such as scoliosis, in patients with PE [12]. In addition, it has been shown that increased age is associated with a higher risk of scoliosis [31]. These data indicate common comorbidities observed in PE patients. Scoliosis may increase the severity of thoracic deformity, which may increase negative effects on pulmonary functions. We found that 51.4% of the symptomatic patients were over 11 years old, and symptoms such as dyspnea on exertion, chest pain, and cough were more common in this group. In addition, we found that reductions in PFT values (especially TLC and FVC) were significant in symptomatic patients. Similarly, Lawson et al. and Malek et al. (2006) reported a marked reduction in pulmonary functions and a decrease in exercise tolerance in symptomatic PE patients [14, 24]. These findings show that PE may influence physical performance negatively in advanced ages.

One of the study’s strengths is its evaluation of the relationship between MDM—a radiation-free, bedside-friendly measurement in children with PE and CT-based HI. Furthermore, the ROC analysis examining the value of MDM in distinguishing severe deformities defined by an HI > 2.5 provides a practical threshold for how this measurement can be used in clinical follow-up. The study’s consideration of AIs not only in relation to anatomical severity but also in conjunction with PFT, symptoms, and comorbidities provides a comprehensive perspective on pediatric PE assessment.

Study limitations

The main limitations of this study include its retrospective and single-centre design, the lack of chest CT and PFT data for all patients, and the fact that the ROC analysis was performed on a limited number of patients. Chest CT was available only in a subset of patients because imaging was performed according to clinical indication rather than a standardized imaging protocol. This may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of the ROC analysis, and HI/CI-related comparisons, and the proposed MDM threshold of 1.75 cm. Therefore, this threshold should be interpreted as an exploratory cut-off derived from a clinically selected CT subgroup rather than as a universally applicable threshold. In addition, logistic regression analyses were performed in small subgroups, which resulted in wide confidence intervals and limited precision of the effect estimates. Therefore, these estimates should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Multiple statistical comparisons were performed; therefore, the possibility of type I error cannot be excluded. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied due to the exploratory nature of the study. An additional limitation is that interobserver and intraobserver reliability analyses for MDM could not be performed because the measurements were obtained retrospectively from routine outpatient records. Therefore, the reproducibility of MDM could not be evaluated in the present study. Another limitation is that MDM was performed using a ruler rather than a caliper, which may have introduced measurement variability and limited the precision of external depth assessment. However, this approach reflects routine outpatient practice and the intended use of MDM as a simple, low-cost, radiation-free, and easily repeatable bedside measurement. Age is an important confounding factor that could influence both MDM and PFT results. Indeed, the association between MDM and low TLC weakened after adjustment for age. Since MDM cannot reliably distinguish clinically significant deformity from abnormal PFT findings, this measurement should be interpreted as a complementary tool in clinical follow-up rather than a substitute for chest CT or PFT.

Another limitation is that the present study was not designed to evaluate longitudinal changes in MDM during conservative follow-up or vacuum bell therapy. Nevertheless, MDM has clinical value as a simple, radiation-free, and repeatable bedside measurement for objectively documenting the deformity of external depth during outpatient follow-up. In children managed conservatively or treated with vacuum bell therapy, repeated MDM measurements may help clinicians monitor whether the visible deformity is progressing, remaining stable, or improving over time.

Conclusion

MDM is a practical bedside measurement method for patients who do not require surgical treatment; it does not involve radiation exposure and can be easily repeated during outpatient follow-up. In this study, an MDM threshold of 1.75 cm was associated with HI > 2.5 in the CT-evaluated subgroup. However, this threshold was derived from a limited and clinically selected group of patients with available chest CT data and should therefore be interpreted with caution. In this context, MDM can contribute to monitoring patients below the surgical threshold and/or evaluating the response to non-surgical treatments such as vacuum bell, and to objectively monitoring whether the depth of the deformity increases during follow-up. For patients with low MDM values and no symptoms or comorbidities, routine clinical follow-up may be sufficient. In children with MDM values above 1.75 cm, PFT may be considered. However, the decision to perform chest CT should not be based on the MDM threshold alone, but should be guided by symptoms, comorbidities, physical examination findings, and clinical judgment. Prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate the clinical utility of the 1.75 cm threshold.
