Development of a quality indicator set for day hospices in Germany – a multi-stage, quality-assured review of German guidelines and quality indicator sets
Authors: Ulrich Kaiser, Ursula Vehling-Kaiser, Ana Hoffmann, Felix Kaiser, Michael Rechenmacher, Eleène Kaiser, Romina Chalupper, Moritz Fiedler, Florian Kaiser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDay hospices are an emerging component of outpatient palliative care in Germany. However, a standardized quality management system utilizing defined quality indicators has not yet been implemented. This study aimed to develop a new set of quality indicators for day hospices.MethodsThrough a multi-phase, quality-assured review, we screened validated quality indicators from German-language S3 guidelines and established quality indicator sets, including the Quality Indicator System for Outpatient Care (QISA) and Outpatient Quality Indicators and Key Figures (AQUIK) based on defined criteria for their relevance to day hospices. Selected indicators were analyzed by a validated and operationalized evaluation tool (QUALIFY) for their applicability to day hospices by an expert panel and subsequently finalized through a nominal group process.ResultsA total of 19 quality indicators, suitable for quality assurance of day hospices were identified from 482 potential quality indicators drawn from 29 S3 guidelines and 11 quality indicator sets. Of these, 13 indicators pertained to process quality, while 6 addressed structural quality. Indicators for the outcome quality were either not available or not suitable for a day hospice. The 463 not selected indicators were excluded mainly due to possible mismanagement or lack of reference to the goals and tasks of a day hospice.ConclusionsThe newly developed quality indicator set provides a robust framework for assessing and comparing the quality of care in German day hospices. Nevertheless, ongoing refinement and practical evaluation are necessary to ensure its relevance and effectiveness in routine clinical settings.Trial registrationThe study was registered as part of the IMPULS-Study in the German Register of Clinical Studies (DRKS-ID: DRKS00031613; Date of registration: 04 April 2023) and the Display portal of the Center for Clinical Studies of the University Hospital Regensburg (Z-2022-1734-6; Date of registration: 01 October 2022).

Background

In Germany, the main aim of palliative care is to maintain and improve the quality of life of patients (and their relatives) with an advanced, life-limiting illness [1]. Palliative care can be provided in both, inpatient (palliative care units, hospices) and outpatient (outreach palliative care services for the home) settings, depending on need [1]. Day hospices are a relatively new type of outpatient palliative care facility in Germany, currently available in only a limited number of locations. Their primary goal is to enhance the independence and quality of life for individuals with advanced, life-limiting illnesses. This is achieved through interventions such as “social interactions (...), rehabilitation, physiotherapy, ergotherapy, artistic therapies (...) and/or medical care to control symptoms” [1]. Ensuring palliative nursing care and counteracting social isolation are particularly important for patients [2, 3]. Additionally, day hospices aim to ease the burden on family caregivers, thereby enabling continued care for patients at home [4–6]. Especially the daily respite care provided by a day hospice is particularly important for relatives [2, 3]. Patients typically attend day hospices on weekdays during standard working hours and return to their home environment afterward [3]. Research on the effectiveness of day hospices has reported high levels of patient satisfaction [4, 7]. However, earlier quantitative studies—characterized by varying methodologies and difference in day hospice structures [1]—have produced inconsistent findings regarding their impact on quality of life [8, 9]. Despite these mixed results, day hospices are anticipated to positively contribute to the German healthcare system [3]. Initial theoretical structural recommendations have already been published, proposing standardized criteria to guide the future establishment of day hospices in Germany [10].

Given that day hospices are a recent addition to the German healthcare system, specific measures for quality assurance and improvement have not yet been implemented. Establishing defined quality criteria and target values is essential for delivering high-quality medical and nursing care. These criteria provide a framework for evaluating specific aspects of care and identifying areas for improvement, particularly in newly developed care structures [11, 12]. Quality indicators are particularly well-suited for this purpose, as they facilitate the differentiation between high- and low-quality. However, the global parameter “quality of a facility” is not measured directly as an individual single value. Instead, individual aspects are examined using various quality indicators relating to structures/organisations, processes/interventions and results of a facility as auxiliary parameters in the sense of a multidimensional approach. They do not directly measure quality but indirectly by comparing numerical ratios. This approach determines the degree to which specific target values are achieved [11, 12]. By utilizing multiple quality indicators, various dimensions of a facility—such as structures, processes, or outcomes—can be evaluated [11, 12]. For quality indicators to effectively assess care quality and identify potential improvements, they must meet specific criteria. These include the ability to collect data simply and cost-effectively, minimize subjective influence, and ensure the indicators are meaningful, relevant to the institution being evaluated, and reflective of healthcare quality. Furthermore, they should also measure those aspects that are particularly relevant for patients in the context of the analyzed institution. In Germany, the “QUALIFY” assessment tool is available to support this process. QUALIFY was developed by the German Federal Agency for Quality Assurance as a validated and operationalized evaluation tool for the structured, standardized and quality-assured evaluation of quality indicators. It comprises a set of clearly defined evaluation criteria divided into three categories: relevance, scientific validity and practicability [11–13].

The IMPULS study, a quality-assured investigation into day hospices (DRKS-ID DRKS00031613, Z-2022-1734-6 [14]), aims among others to identify potential quality indicators for monitoring and ensuring the quality of care in day hospices. These indicators may support the future development of day hospices and contribute to the growing importance of quality management in this field.

Methods

The development of a quality indicator set for day hospices followed the German Guideline Program in Oncology for creating guideline-based quality indicators [15] and the Program for National Care Guidelines–Quality Indicators [11].

Selected guidelines and quality indicator sets

To identify potentially relevant quality indicators [16] for day hospices, three German national platforms for validated quality indicators and with the best references to relevant topics for a day hospice like ambulant medical organisation/structure, palliative care and chronic or malignant diseases were used: the German-language S3 guidelines from the national guideline registry of the Association of Scientific Medical Societies in Germany (AWMF) [17], along with the German-language quality indicator sets from the National Association of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (KBV) [18]: Outpatient Quality Indicators and Key Figures (AQUIK) and from the General Local Health Insurance Fund (AOK) [19]: Quality Indicator System for Outpatient Care (QISA). The S3 guidelines, developed by relevant professional societies, represent the highest methodological and qualitative state of the art standards for various diseases in Germany. The AWMF coordinates “the development of medical guidelines for diagnostics and therapy by medical societies and publishes them through the AWMF Guidelines Register” [20]. The KBV represents the interests of medical specialists in a practice and in outpatient care in Germany. AQUIK consists of 65 quality indicators for outpatient care, addressing a range of medical conditions and medical office management. It is intended as a tool for quality management control [18]. AOK is one of the largest health insurance companies in Germany. QISA offers a set of quality indicators to assess outpatient medical care, organized into 14 volumes, each addressing different clinical scenarios, healthcare models, and cross-sectional topics [19]. International quality indicators were excluded from the selection process due to different healthcare systems and the resulting challenges of adapting them to the German healthcare system without significant modification [11]. However, they were used as comparative or reference indicators in the discussion.

Stage 1: Selection of potentially relevant guidelines and quality indicators sets

All S3 guidelines and all quality indicator sets/volumes from AQUIK, and QISA volumes were independently reviewed for their relevance for a day hospice by the authors KU, VKU, and KF (all three are specialists in palliative medicine with several years of experience in the care of patients in a day hospice). The screening criteria by keywords for Stage 1 are outlined in Table 1. In a first joint meeting during Stage 1, the three authors discussed their findings and collectively decided on the acceptance or rejection of potential guidelines or quality indicator sets/volumes. To facilitate structured decision-making within the research team, the authors employed the nominal group process described by Dunham [21]. This process involves pooling analyses to gather and present the views and judgments of as many participants as possible in a clear and objective manner [21]. Stage 2: Selection of potentially relevant quality indicators

All quality indicators from the guidelines and quality indicator sets/volumes selected in Stage 1 were independently screened by the three authors KU, VKU and KF as potential indicators for day hospices. Relevant screening criteria can be found in Table 1. In a second joint meeting during Stage 2, the authors discuss their results and consolidated a final selection of potentially relevant quality indicators for a day hospice using a further nominal group process according to Dunham [21]. In a third stage, all final selected potentially relevant indicators were then sent to a panel of experts for further evaluation (Fig. 1).

Stage 3: Selection of the final quality indicator set

For further evaluation, an initial kick-off meeting was held with the expert committee. Prerequisites for participation as an expert were a specialist qualification in palliative medicine and/or quality management as well as several years of professional experience in palliative medicine and in hospices/day hospices mainly in Germany. Experts from various specialist disciplines were recruited (palliative care physicians, palliative care nurses and quality managers) in order to enable the widest possible range of different perspectives. Particular attention was paid to a balanced mix of participants with university and non-university experience, especially among the palliative care physicians. The final expert committee consisted of four palliative care physicians, two palliative care nurses, and two quality managers from the palliative care sector. The methodology [11, 15, 21], the existing German-language quality indicator sets/guidelines [17–19], and the planned steps were outlined here. Specifically, the committee examined the criteria for identifying and evaluating potential quality indicators for day hospices:

Additionally, assessment criteria such as measurability, potential for improvement, clarity, and data collection effort were considered [15].

Based on these principles, the experts conducted a written evaluation of the selected quality indicators using a standardized evaluation form based on QUALIFY [13]. The evaluation included five criteria from the areas of relevance, scientific foundation, and practicability (identifying improvement potential, clear definition, describing an aspect that can be influenced; risk of misdirection; justifiable data collection effort). Additional comments were allowed on the focus of the quality indicators, external influenceability, and implementation barriers [13, 15]. Each criterion was rated with a Yes/No response. Following the established guidelines, an indicator was considered accepted if ≥ 75% of the participating experts agreed on all criteria [15]. In a joint meeting, the expert group discussed the quality indicators using a nominal group process [21]. Based on these evaluations, the indicators were reevaluated if necessary, and a final set of quality indicators for day hospices was established. When indicators from different guidelines or sets overlapped, only one version was included. The selection prioritized the S3 guidelines as the highest quality guideline and the indicators best suited for day hospices.

Validation and modification

Since the study was based exclusively on already validated quality indicators from guidelines or already tested quality indicator sets, no further separate validation of the selected indicators was carried out. Recommendations for possible modifications to enhance applicability to day hospices were also discussed and approved by consensus among the expert panel.

Ethical aspects

As part of the IMPULS-study [14], there is an ethics approval by the ethics committee of the University of Regensburg (Ethics Committee Number: 22–3012-101). As the researchers for the selection process were not themselves part of the study and no personal data was collected from the researcher, no separate consent forms were obtained from them.

Results

Selection of potentially relevant guidelines/quality indicator sets

In the initial screening, a total of 213 S3 guidelines, 1 AQUIK volume/quality indicator set, and 14 QISA volumes/quality indicator sets were reviewed. From these, 29 S3 guidelines (n = 262 quality indicators), 1 AQUIK volume/quality indicator set (n = 65 quality indicators), and 10 QISA volumes/quality indicator sets (n = 155 quality indicators) were deemed potentially relevant for day hospices.

Of the 17 preselected quality indicators rejected from the S3 guidelines, three related to the quality of outcomes (reduction of pain/respiratory distress, use of laxatives for opioids; reasons for rejection: A, B), ten related to process quality (in particular, special counselling/support by specialists, e.g. stoma therapist, psycho-oncologist, social worker; reasons for rejection: A, C, D, G) and four related to structural quality (structural requirements, irrelevant for a day hospice; reasons for rejection: A, B, D, E).

One preselected quality indicator related to structural quality from AQUIK was rejected due to duplication (G).

Of the 38 preselected and rejected quality indicators from QISA, none related to outcome quality, 28 to process quality (medical and structural care of chronic diseases, hospital admissions, optimization and control of medication/side effects, definition of therapy goals, education on medical therapies; reasons for rejection: A, B, C, E, G), and 10 to structural quality (management of quality circle work, surveys among doctors, further training of doctors, coordination of care; reasons for rejection: D, E, F, G).

By consensus during the detailed evaluation, a final set of 19 indicators (S3 guidelines, n = 7; AQUIK, n = 3; QISA, n = 9) was chosen (Fig. 1; Tables 2 and 3). Summarizing, 13 quality indicators are related to process quality, and 6 quality indicators are related to structural quality (Table 3). The process quality indicators focus on areas such as patient care, co-care by relatives, psychosocial support, symptoms tracking, and the collection of clinical, medical, and psychosocial data/complaints, as well as the organization within the day hospice. The structural quality indicators cover topics such as staff training, networking, and satisfaction. Furthermore, many of the quality indicators could not be directly applied to day hospices. In more detail, of the 7 indicators in the S3 guideline, 3 came from the palliative medicine guideline, 1 from the gastric cancer guideline and 3 from the psycho-oncology guideline. Of these, 6 indicators could be assigned to process quality. The focus was on clinical and psycho-oncological symptom assessment, including nutritional status and the nursing and psycho-oncological measures carried out. 1 indicator was assigned to structural quality with a focus on the specialized training of day hospice nursing staff. Except the indicator “Symptom recording using MIDOS or IPOS”, 6 indicators required modification to adapt them to the special structures of a day hospice.

All 3 indicators from AQUIK were assigned to process quality and modifications were necessary for all of them. They related to the handling of critical events, the regular review of emergency medication and the medication plans of the day hospice guests.

Of the 9 indicators from QISA, 3 were from volume F1 “Palliative care in general practice”, 5 from volume B “General Indicators” and 1 from volume F2 “Multimorbidity”. All indicators required modifications. The indicators from volumes F1 and F2 were all assigned to process quality. They related to the documentation of a contact person, the evaluation of quality of life and the implementation of supportive/counseling discussions on the palliative situation for patients and relatives. The indicators from volume B were assigned to structural quality. They focused on participation in regional palliative care quality circles, the information provided and the satisfaction of patients and referring physicians with the day hospice.

Discussion

As day hospices are still a relatively new palliative care model in Germany, there are currently few scientifically grounded recommendations for their implementation and design [10].

The goal of this work was to develop a set of quality indicators for day hospices, drawing on validated quality indicators from guidelines for chronic or malignant diseases and quality indicator sets for outpatient care. A total of 482 quality indicators from 40 guidelines/quality indicator sets were analyzed, leading to the development of a final set of 19 quality indicators for day hospices.

In the analyzed guidelines and quality indicator sets for both malignant and chronic, non-malignant diseases that often lead to death, several potential quality indicators were identified. However, only a few were deemed suitable for palliative care/day hospices in the final evaluation. This is mainly due to the fact that the majority of the analyzed quality indicators focus on the diagnosis and treatment of diseases and/or the necessary medical and disease-specific structures and less or not on palliative care aspects [15, 16]. Specifically, only one indicator each from the multimorbidity set and the S3 guideline on gastric cancer were included in the final quality indicator set. Most of the selected indicators came from guidelines/indicator sets focused on (oncological) palliative medicine/psycho-oncology or on organizational/structural topics. This pattern is also evident in the international literature [16] and reflects, on one hand, the growing distinction of palliative medicine and psycho-oncology as separate specialties. However, it highlights the ongoing need for continuous education and interdisciplinary collaboration between palliative medicine and the specialties of hematology/oncology, particularly for chronic non-malignant diseases [22]. This is important because, currently, day hospices, like the rest of the palliative care system, primarily treat patients with advanced malignant diseases [23, 24], although they are technically open to all patients, including those with advanced non-malignant diseases. This should be considered in the future development of quality indicators for day hospices.

Most of the quality indicators in the final set were highly suitable for palliative medicine but could not be directly applied to the specific needs of day hospices without modifications. This misalignment is why a significant number of quality indicators, primarily from palliative medicine guideline/indicator sets, were not included in the final set for day hospices. Day hospices are primarily focused on palliative nursing care with an emphasis on social interaction and quality of life [1, 10]. Physician involvement is not mandatory in day hospices and is only an additional option. Furthermore, day hospices provide outpatient care, while many of the quality indicators mentioned earlier require physician involvement and/or an inpatient stay to be fully applicable. Although day hospices can carry out physician orders on the nursing side, the resulting benefits cannot be solely attributed to the day hospice setting. But since high-class quality indicators should measure dimensions that can be directly influenced by the analyzed facility, many of the (palliative) quality indicators are not suitable for evaluating day hospices [25]. However, given their specialized palliative training, nursing staff at day hospices are highly capable of recognizing issues or crises in patients and, if necessary, their relatives and can recommend or organize further actions. By adapting the indicators to account for these aspects, many quality indicators could be made suitable for use in day hospices. Due to the complex and sometimes different forms of care in palliative medicine, it is considered sensible to adapt quality indicators to the requirements of the respective form of care [25, 26]. The new set of quality indicators for day hospices is based on already validated quality indicators. However, most of these indicators had to be modified by the expert committee to meet the special requirements of a day hospice. These modifications have not yet been empirically validated in a day hospice as part of this study.

Quality indicators are intended to specifically evaluate and monitor the quality of care provided by medical institutions. A comparison of the new quality indicator set with the contractual requirements for day hospices in Germany (the contract cannot be published due to data protection laws; see also Sect. 113, Social Security Code (SGB) XI [27]) revealed that most of the required objectives and quality assurance criteria could be met. The primary mission of day hospices is to provide palliative nursing care to guests of a day hospice, including social, therapeutic, and spiritual services, as well as end of life and bereavement support for both patients and their families. The main goal is to enhance quality of life [1, 4–6]. These central tasks of day hospices, in particular the quality of life and the relief of relatives, are not measured as direct quality indicators. The collection and evaluation of this data as part of quality management may be impracticable for day hospices due to a very high/too high effort. But specifically, the quality indicators in areas such as patient care, co-care with relatives, psychosocial support, and the recording of symptoms and clinical, medical, and psychosocial data/complaints can cover these objectives very well in the sense of an indirect measurement. These are core topics that also play an important role in international quality indicator sets on palliative care [25, 28, 29]. The new set of quality indicators also covers important internal and external structural criteria, such as the integration of day hospices into a regional (hospice and palliative) network, as well as regular case discussions and ongoing training [27]. Furthermore, the documentation and quality system in day hospices should remain within a reasonable and justifiable scope [27]. The quality indicator set, consisting of 19 individual indicators, aligns with previous German-language indicator sets on specialized topics [30, 31], particularly since some indicators only need to be collected once or a few times per year. However, certain contractually defined quality requirements for day hospices are not fully covered by the new set. These include specific care measures, the evaluation of special support services (e.g., music therapy, breathing therapy, volunteer helpers), and day-structuring activities (e.g., shared meals, walks). This omission can be attributed to the unique and new nature of the “day hospice” care model, which has not yet been fully addressed in existing guidelines and quality indicators. It should also be considered that in day hospices, the special support and day-structuring measures are usually handled very individually and patient-centered. Too strict standardization carries the risk of undermining the idea of personalized hospice-care. In principle, future quality indicators for these topics should be more flexible and more general than too specific. Although the development of quality indicators in palliative care is in a constant state of flux [32], it is important to consider that the quality requirements remain within a reasonable and practicable framework. Many day hospices are small and have only a few employees. Extending the documentation requirements entails the risk of excessive demands and a lack of implementation. This must be taken into account when adapting the current quality indicator set, which will likely need further updates in the future.

Due to structural differences—for example, in Austria, where fixed physician coverage in day hospices is mandatory [33]—a comparison with international quality indicators is limited [11]. However, there is some overlap, particularly in the goals of day hospices, such as enhancing quality of life and social integration, as well as providing care with an emphasis on palliative nursing care [33]. A comparison with the new quality indicator set could offer an initial indication of its quality. International collections of quality indicators mainly address areas such as psychosocial care for patients and their families, palliative nursing care measures, and general aspects like the documentation of contacts/emergency contacts or networking [25, 28, 29, 32–36]. The quality indicator set developed in this study covers these topics broadly, though, as mentioned earlier, some subaspects are not included in the new set. This includes, in particular, the specific services provided by a day hospice, such as the daily activities offered, the outcomes of palliative nursing/medical measures including symptom management, social integration efforts, and general aspects like documentation/determination of therapy preferences at the end of life, as well as care planning. Indicators that evaluate the activities offered and social integration measures will likely play a significant role in the quality management of day hospices in the future. However, other aspects, such as medical planning and determining the medical treatment of symptoms, and the determination of therapy preferences, fall outside the scope of day hospices in Germany or cannot be fulfilled due to the non-mandatory presence of physicians. Taking this into account, the new set of quality indicators primarily focuses on process quality, with a lesser emphasis on structure quality. There are no indicators for quality of outcomes. This may be because day hospices are typically integrated into an interdisciplinary palliative care network [37], making it difficult to attribute expected outcomes solely to the effect of the day hospice. The international literature also categorizes the measurement of outcomes of a day hospice, particularly on the basis of patient-reported outcomes, as difficult [23] and localized them more in the area of process or structural quality indicators [25]. An optimal tool does not yet exist, and previous studies have not yet been clearly successful or conclusive [9, 23]. The quality of outcomes in a day hospice is certainly multidimensional and includes social, psychological, and interpersonal aspects and not only medical objectives [8, 23]. A potential recording within the framework of a quality management system as well as the differentiation from external influences could be associated with a disproportionate amount of effort, which could make possible development and establishment more difficult [25]. This is also reflected in the low number of indicators on quality of outcome in the guidelines/indicator sets on palliative care analyzed [1, 30]. Therefore, this does not necessarily capture the full qualitative value of day hospices.

In the future, the new quality indicator set could be a further decision-making aid for health insurance companies in the context of quality assessments and approval procedures for day hospices. The set could also be used in certification procedures in accordance with a standardized quality management system and could serve as an orientation aid for providers when founding and setting up new day hospices. Even if there is currently no nationwide establishment of day hospices in Germany, the new indicator set could enable patients and their families to compare day hospices in the future - in particular the quality of structures and processes. The extent to which outcome-focused measures will improve this comparability remains unclear, especially in view of the difficulty of recording and evaluating the quality of outcomes and the interdisciplinary and sometimes individualized working methods of day hospices.

Limitations

A significant portion of the identified quality indicators could not be directly applied due to the unique structure and objectives of day hospices, requiring necessary modifications by experts. The final set of adapted quality indicators has not been undergone specific validity testing in day hospices due to these modifications. This may limit the applicability and may require further research. However, they are based on recommendations from an interdisciplinary expert committee and still reflect the core principles of the original, validated quality indicators. While the new quality indicator set covers a wide range of quality criteria outlined in the care contract, some required aspects (both nationally and internationally) are not included. This should be considered when evaluating the relevance of the indicator set. International quality indicators were not examined for the final quality indicator set. This was due to the different structures of the international healthcare systems and the resulting problems of transferability to the German healthcare system. Nevertheless, certain relevant aspects may not have been taken into account. Furthermore, physicians, nurses, and quality managers were involved in the development of the final quality indicator set, but no patients or guests of the day hospice and no relatives. They were not included, firstly because of the development and evaluation process of the quality indicator set extended over many months and the life expectancy of the patients concerned was usually shorter. Secondly, the development of the quality indicators took place at a very early stage of the IMPULS study [14]. At this time, the final structures of the day hospice under investigation had not yet been conclusively established. Therefore, the experience and knowledge of patients and relatives with day hospice structures had to be considered limited at this time. This represented a further exclusion criterion. The ‘consumer’ perspective is therefore missing and should be taken into account in the future development of the indicator set. Finally, the paper describes the work of a small number of German experts. Whether a larger number of - possibly international - experts could have identified further important quality aspects for a day hospice cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the newly developed set of 19 quality indicators for German day hospices represents the first compilation of indicators for the assessment and monitoring of day hospices within Germany in a verified and comparable manner. As recommendations for practice and research the new indicator set can already be used to assess the quality of day hospices and allows individual day hospices to be compared with one another. It can also provide guidance for funding organisations when reviewing the relevant basic requirements and quality of a day hospice. Particularly in the start-up phase of new day hospices, the indicator set can provide assistance in establishing the necessary structures. However, this set requires further research, clinical evaluation and the development of additional quality indicators to more fully and accurately address the specific needs of day hospices.
