Transtibial Technique for Medial Meniscus Root Repair and Centralization
Authors: Quang Ton Quyen Nguyen, Quang Huy Nguyen, Duc Trung Do, Viet Nam Nguyen, Duc Ha Hoang, Kenneth D. Montgomerry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent evidence suggests that meniscotibial ligament disruption with progressive extrusion often precedes medial meniscus posterior root tears, clarifying the limited efficacy of isolated root repair. Accordingly, combined root repair and centralization yield more favorable biomechanical restoration and clinical outcomes. This technical note describes an arthroscopic root repair reinforced by dual centralization to enhance stability and recovery.

Triple‐tunnel transtibial technique for medial meniscus posterior root repair with and centralization (left knee). The patient was positioned supine with a tourniquet applied above the knee. The left leg was secured in approximately 20°‐30° of knee flexion with valgus force using a Trimano arm to optimize medial compartment exposure. Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals were established, and diagnostic arthroscopy confirmed a medial meniscus posterior root tear with extrusion. With the knee maintained in valgus stress, a controlled medial collateral ligament release was performed using the pie‐crust technique. Under anterolateral portal visualization, three tibial tunnel entry points were marked: (1) at the native posterior root footprint, (2) adjacent to the medial collateral ligament for meniscal body centralization, and (3) at the midpoint between these two sites. Through a 3‐cm oblique anterolateral tibial incision, three 4.5‐mm transtibial tunnels were sequentially drilled to the three previously marked intra‐articular target points using an anterior cruciate ligament drill guide, with divergent trajectories converging at the anterolateral tibial cortex. Two No. 2 Ultrabraid sutures were passed through the meniscal root and shuttled through the posterior root tunnel. Meniscal body centralization and submeniscal capsular sutures, capturing the meniscotibial ligament, were placed using an outside‐in technique with an 18‐gauge needle. An additional set of centralization sutures was placed between the posterior root and meniscal body. All sutures were passed through their corresponding transtibial tunnels and secured over a cortical fixation device on the anterolateral tibial cortex. Sequential tensioning reduced the posterior root and partially corrected meniscal extrusion. Final arthroscopic inspection confirmed stable fixation and satisfactory meniscal reduction. Nonoperative treatment or meniscectomy has consistently been associated with suboptimal outcomes, most notably early osteoarthritis, leading to a paradigm shift toward meniscal preservation through repair. In medial meniscus posterior root tears, repair has shown clear advantages in joint preservation and cost‐effectiveness over meniscectomy or conservative care, with long‐term data further highlight the unfavorable prognosis of nonoperative treatment. 1 , 2 A study by Krych et al. reshaped our understanding of meniscal root tears, showing that degeneration of the meniscotibial ligament initiates instability, progressive extrusion, and eventual root failure. 3 This explains why isolated root repair is often less effective than combined repair with centralization in preventing progressive meniscal extrusion. 4 Multiple centralization techniques have been proposed, generally categorized into transtibial–based or anchor‐based fixation. Biomechanical evidence regarding their effectiveness in restoring meniscal extrusion remains conflicting. 5 , 6 This article describes a surgical technique combining posterior root repair with two transtibial centralization points to improve anatomic reduction of the extruded meniscus and lower the risk of progressive extrusion.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Selection

Our indication for root repair with centralization includes patients with medial meniscal root tears and extrusion, neutral alignment or less than 5° of varus, and osteoarthritis no greater than Kellgren–Lawrence grade 3 (Video 1).

Position and Landmark

The patient is positioned supine and a tourniquet applied above the knee. A post is placed at the proximal thigh for stabilization, and a Trimano arm is attached at the corresponding thigh level to help securing the leg in 20°‐30° extension with valgus force (Figure 1).

The leg was held at 20° flexion with valgus force using a Trimano arm (left leg, supine position, view from the side and above, (A) before and (B) after surgical draping).

Arthroscopic Inspection and Medial Compartment Release

Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals are established, followed by diagnostic arthroscopy to confirm meniscal root tear and extrusion.

With the knee stabilized in approximately 20° to 30° of flexion and valgus strain via a Trimano arm, a controlled release of the medial collateral ligament (using the “pie‐crust” technique) was carried out to widen the medial compartment and improve visualization.

A curette was then used to mark the planned tibial tunnel sites: one at the native root footprint, a second adjacent to the medial collateral ligament, and a third at the midpoint between these two positions (Figure 2).

Tibial tunnel sites were marked: (1) at the native root footprint, (2) adjacent to the medial collateral ligament, and (3) at the midpoint between the two previous tunnels (left knee, supine position, view from anterolateral portal with the knee flexed at 20°, (A) root footprint, (B) posterior to MCL, and (C) midpoint between (A,B)).

Transtibial Tunnels Preparation

A 3‐cm oblique skin incision was made from Gerdy's tubercle toward the tibial crest to expose the anterolateral surface of the tibial plateau. Through this incision, an anterior cruciate ligament drill guide (Acufex director drill guide; Smith & Nephew) was introduced and directed to the meniscal root footprint to create the first transtibial tunnel. Two additional tunnels were then established in a similar fashion in the previous mark points. Each tunnel was reamed to 4.5 mm in diameter, and all three tunnels were oriented in divergent trajectories but converge at the anterolateral tibial plateau (Figure 3).

Locations of three tibial tunnels: (A) at the meniscal root footprint; (B) adjacent to the medial collateral ligament; (C) midway between (A,B). White dot: medial posterior meniscus root; yellow arrow: tunnel adjacent to the MCL for the meniscal body centralization (left knee, supine position, view from anterolateral portal with the knee flexed at 20°).

Meniscal Root and Centralization Suturing

Two No. 2 Ultrabraid sutures (Smith & Nephew) were first passed through the meniscal root using a FirstPass Mini suture passer (Smith & Nephew). These could be configured as two simple loop stitches or as one simple loop combined with a horizontal mattress stitch to enhance fixation strength. Both sutures were then shuttled through the prepared tibial root tunnel.

Centralization of the meniscal body was performed using an outside‐in technique with an 18‐gauge needle. The first limb of the Ultrabraid suture was advanced from outside to inside along the inferior meniscal surface and retrieved through the anteromedial portal. The needle was then withdrawn slightly beyond the capsule, redirected superiorly, and reinserted parallel along the superior surface to exit again at the inferior surface. The second limb was retrieved through the same portal, creating a loop that captured the meniscus, capsule, and part of the meniscotibial ligament (Figure 4).

(A) Posterior root sutures, (B) centralization suture (yellow arrow) and submeniscal capsular suture around the meniscotibial ligament (white arrow), (C) centralization suture of the meniscal body retrieved through the anteromedial portal (blue arrow); additional second centralization sutures between the body and posterior root (white dot) passed through the transtibial tunnel (left knee, supine position, view from anterolateral portal with the knee flexed at 20°).

A submeniscal capsular loop suture was subsequently placed around the meniscotibial ligament using the same technique, with an 18‐gauge needle and a No. 2 Ultrabraid suture (Figure 4). An additional second set of centralization and capsular loop sutures were placed midway between the root and body sutures. All sutures were finally passed through their respective transtibial tunnels (Figure 4).

Meniscal Reduction and Fixation

After being shuttled through the transtibial tunnels to the anterolateral tibial cortex, the sutures were loaded through a cortical fixation device (UltraButton; Smith & Nephew). They were then tensioned and secured over the button, reducing the meniscal root and part of the meniscal extrusion to its native position, and achieving stable fixation. A fibrin clot or platelet‐rich fibrin could be applied at the repair site to enhance biological healing (Figure 5).

(A) Sutures loaded over an UltraButton, (B) stability of the repair checked after fixation, and (C) fibrin clot applied at the meniscal root repair site (left knee, supine position, view from anterolateral portal with the knee flexed at 20°).

Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation consists of early quadriceps activation with the knee braced in extension. Range of motion is limited to 0°‐90° for the first 4 weeks, with toe‐touch weight‐bearing only. Progression to full weight‐bearing and unrestricted motion is allowed after 4 weeks. Stationary cycling is introduced once 90° flexion is achieved, light jogging at 2 months, and deep flexion or high‐impact activities after 3 months.

DISCUSSION

Medial meniscus posterior root tears occur most frequently in aged population, predominantly in women, with reported prevalence of 21% among meniscal tears requiring surgery. 7 However, diagnosis is not always straightforward, as the presenting pain is often attributed to osteoarthritis, suggesting that the true incidence may be underestimated. 8 Meniscal root repair has shown the capacity to attenuate osteoarthritic progression and lowering the long‐term likelihood of requiring total knee arthroplasty. 1 , 9

As the pathogenesis of medial meniscus posterior root tears has become clearer, treatment strategies have shifted from isolated root repair toward meniscal centralization to reduce failure rates. 3 Nevertheless, clinical data indicate that meniscal extrusion often continues after centralization, although at a slower rate than with isolated root repair. 4 This may be explained by the fact that a single centralization point only prevents medial displacement, whereas hoop stresses continue to drive radial extrusion, with the posterior horns bearing approximately 50% of the load in extension and nearly 90% in flexion. 8

On this basis, we describe a technique combining posterior root repair with two transtibial centralization points: one adjacent to the medial collateral ligament and another at the meniscal body–posterior horn junction. This supplementary fixation provides a more anatomic reduction of the extruded meniscus (Figure 6), protects the root repair by redistributing forces, and does not significantly alter meniscal kinematics, as the posterior horn of the medial meniscus exhibits minimal motion during flexion–extension. 10

Indeed, the use of additional tibial tunnels and multiple intra‐articular sutures increases surgical complexity, raises the risk of suture entanglement, and may prolong operative time, representing inherent limitations of this technique. Moreover, a persistently narrow joint space despite adequate release may increase the risk of iatrogenic cartilage or meniscal injury during tibial guide insertion and suboptimal tibial tunnel positioning; in such situations, an accessory submeniscal portal facilitates more accurate tunnel drilling while minimizing inadvertent cartilage damage.

In addition, the use of multiple sutures during the procedure increases the risk of suture misidentification and entanglement. To address this issue, a practical strategy is to drill all three tibial tunnels before meniscal suturing and to shuttle each suture immediately into its corresponding tunnel once it has been passed through the intended meniscal portion. Similarly, insufficient spacing between tibial tunnel entry points predisposes to tunnel convergence and cortical collapse, underscoring the importance of an adequate skin incision and sufficient exposure of the anterolateral tibial cortex.

Accordingly, a streamlined surgical workflow was developed to minimize redundant maneuvers and ensure smooth and timely execution.
