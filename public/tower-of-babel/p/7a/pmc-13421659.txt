Effects of exergames on depression, anxiety, and sleep in adolescents with subthreshold depression: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Guofeng Liu, Yang Jing, Jinjian Xie, Fei Xie, Dan Chen, Junping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Subthreshold depression (SD) is highly prevalent among adolescents and increases the risk of developing major depressive disorder. Exergaming, an interactive form of digital physical activity, may enhance engagement and improve adherence compared with traditional exercise, making it a promising approach for adolescent mental-health promotion. This randomized controlled trial investigated the effects of an eight-week exergame intervention on depressive symptoms, anxiety, and sleep quality among adolescents with SD. A total of 51 students were randomly assigned to an exergame group (n = 26) or a control group (n = 25). Participants in the exergame group completed moderate-intensity exergaming for 40 min, two to three times per week, while the control group continued routine school activities. Mental-health outcomes were assessed before and after the intervention using validated self-report questionnaires, including the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) for subjective sleep quality. After the 8-week intervention, the exergame group showed significantly greater improvements than the control group in depressive symptoms, anxiety, and sleep quality (all p < 0.05). These findings indicate that exergames are an engaging, feasible, and effective school-based strategy for improving mental health in adolescents with SD. As a low-threshold and scalable digital exercise modality, exergaming may offer meaningful benefits for early intervention and mental-health promotion in youth populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54710-x.

Introduction

Adolescent mental health problems have become one of the most pressing public health challenges worldwide1,2. Among these issues, subthreshold depression (SD) is particularly prevalent and is characterized by insidious onset, low detection rates, prolonged symptom duration, and a substantially elevated risk of progression to major depressive disorder (MDD). SD adversely affects adolescents’ emotional regulation, social functioning, and academic performance3–5. A survey of 12,395 adolescents from 11 European countries reported that 29.2% exhibited subthreshold depressive symptoms, and these individuals had markedly higher risks of developing depression and suicidal behaviors in adulthood compared with their healthy peers6. Evidence further indicates that adolescents with SD are approximately 5.7 times more likely to develop MDD within one year than those without such symptoms7. Therefore, early identification and intervention for SD has become a critical component of adolescent depression prevention systems.

Similar to clinically diagnosed depression, early detection and timely intervention can substantially improve the quality of life of individuals with SD. Previous studies have consistently shown that preventive interventions for adolescents with SD can effectively reduce the risk of subsequent progression to MDD8–12. Clarke et al. conducted a randomized intervention and long-term follow-up among adolescents with SD. During the 15-month follow-up period, the incidence of MDD was 9.3% in the intervention group, compared with 28.8% in the control group9. Another study has shown that implementing mental health interventions in school settings can effectively reduce mental health problems among adolescents13. These findings underscore the importance of early, school-based intervention strategies for adolescents with SD, which are essential for youth mental health education and public health systems.

Exergames, a form of digital physical activity integrating motion-sensing technologies and computer vision into immersive virtual gaming environments, enable users to engage in physical movement within interactive gameplay settings14. With features such as enjoyment, interactivity, and exercise engagement, exergames may help overcome the monotony of traditional exercise and increase enjoyment, a key factor influencing exercise adherence. Consequently, they have increasingly been adopted as an innovative intervention method for depressive conditions. Existing evidence suggests that exergames can alleviate depressive symptoms in adults, older adults, and university students15–18. However, research specifically targeting adolescents with subthreshold depression (SD) remains notably limited. This trial is among the first randomized controlled studies focusing on adolescents with SD—a population that is both highly vulnerable and substantially understudied.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of a school-based exergame intervention on depressive symptoms, anxiety, and sleep quality among adolescents with SD. The findings are expected to offer methodological insight for SD-specific interventions and contribute empirical evidence to support school-based mental-health promotion and early prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design

This study was a single-center, assessor-blinded, two-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT) designed to evaluate the effectiveness of a digital exergame-based physical activity intervention on psychological health among adolescents with subthreshold depression (SD). The study protocol was developed in accordance with the SPIRIT 2025 guidelines to ensure methodological rigor and reproducibility. The trial followed a superiority framework.

This experiment was conducted at the senior high school division of Shandong Labor Vocational and Technical College from 13 February 2025 to 16 May 2025 (the first four weeks were devoted to participant recruitment, followed by the intervention phase, excluding one week of holiday). The semi-closed campus environment provided highly consistent daily schedules among students, thereby minimizing external social and environmental influences and enhancing experimental control.

A total of 51 adolescents who met the SD screening criteria were enrolled and randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to the Exergame group or the Control group. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent researcher using a computer-based algorithm, and allocation concealment was ensured through sealed, opaque envelopes. Outcome assessments were performed by trained assessors who were blinded to group assignment and were not involved in the intervention procedures.

The intervention lasted for 8 weeks. Participants assigned to the Exergame group received a moderate-intensity exergaming intervention delivered through the Nintendo Wii platform (40 min per session, 2–3 sessions per week). Participants in the control group did not receive any additional exercise intervention from the research team. They continued their regular school routines, including attending classes, self-study periods, and scheduled rest, following the school’s standardized daily timetable. The primary outcome was depressive symptoms assessed using the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). Secondary outcomes included anxiety assessed by the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and sleep quality assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). All outcomes were assessed at baseline and after the intervention.

No patients or members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this trial. One prespecified secondary outcome, the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS), was not collected during the assessment phase, representing a protocol deviation from the original study plan. The decision to omit this outcome was made prior to baseline data collection due to concerns regarding participant burden and potential fatigue associated with completing multiple questionnaires. This modification was applied uniformly to all participants and therefore did not affect the collection or analysis of the primary outcomes. Participants

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Subthreshold depression (SD) currently lacks a universally accepted diagnostic criterion. It is generally characterized by the presence of clinically significant depressive symptoms that do not meet the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder (MDD), typically involving two to four depressive symptoms. To improve the accuracy of participant identification, this study adopted a two-stage screening strategy to identify adolescents with subthreshold depression.

In the first stage, the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) was administered to all Grade 2023 senior high school students at a secondary school in Shandong Province for preliminary screening. Students with SDS scores below the conventional cutoff for clinically significant depression (< 53) were invited for further structured assessment. In this study, SDS < 53 was used as a preliminary screening condition rather than a standalone criterion for subthreshold depression19,20.

In the second stage, two trained evaluators independently conducted structured assessments using the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24)21 among those who passed the initial screening. The 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) is a clinician-rated depression scale administered by trained professionals to assess the severity of depressive symptoms. The total score ranges from 0 to 76, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. Generally, scores of ≤ 7 indicate no depression, 8–20 indicate possible depressive symptoms, 21–34 indicate mild to moderate depression, and ≥ 35 indicate severe depression22.

Re-assessment proceeded in descending order based on SDS scores. Students with HAMD-24 scores between 8 and 20, and presenting at least two core depressive symptoms (e.g., markedly depressed mood, loss of interest), were identified as meeting the criteria for SD and were included as the target study population.

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: aged 12–18 years; had an SDS score < 53; had a HAMD-24 score of 8–20 with ≥ 2 core depressive symptoms; were physically healthy with no contraindications to exercise; and provided written informed consent jointly with a legal guardian.

Participants were excluded if they were younger than 12 or older than 18 years; had intellectual disability or physical limitations (e.g., severe motor, visual, or hearing impairment), asthma, or cardiovascular disease; regularly engaged in structured physical activity within the past six months (defined as moderate-to-vigorous physical activity ≥ 2 times per week for at least 30 min per session); had been diagnosed with another psychiatric disorder; or reported suicidal ideation or behavior.

Recruitment

Recruitment was conducted using convenience sampling between 13/02/2025 and 14/03/2025 in a boarding senior high school in Shandong Province. The school operates under a relatively closed and standardized management system, with students sharing highly consistent daily routines and lifestyle patterns, thereby minimizing environmental confounding and enhancing internal validity.

Initial screening was based on the school’s routine, anonymous mental health assessment (SDS), administered each semester by the school psychological center. Upon voluntary authorization from students and their guardians, the research team obtained de-identified screening data. Only students whose SDS scores fell within the predefined range were contacted individually. The secondary assessment (HAMD-24) was conducted by trained assessors in a private setting to avoid potential stigmatization or psychological distress.

Before randomization, all potential participants completed a cardiopulmonary screening test to determine their eligibility and safety for the eight-week intervention. The stair-climbing test was used as a simple screening method to assess cardiopulmonary tolerance. Participants were instructed to climb three flights of stairs (approximately 9–11 m vertical height) at a moderate pace comparable to normal walking speed. If no symptoms such as chest discomfort, shortness of breath, or excessive dyspnea occurred after completing the task, the participant was considered to have adequate cardiopulmonary tolerance. If the completion time exceeded 90 s or if any adverse symptoms occurred, the result was considered indicative of insufficient cardiopulmonary tolerance or an invalid test, and such participants were excluded from the study. Although the stair-climbing test is not an internationally standardized assessment tool for cardiopulmonary function in adolescents, it is considered a feasible and safe screening method in school settings and has been used in several studies for preliminary assessment of cardiopulmonary tolerance23,24.

Participants meeting all eligibility criteria were enrolled after written informed consent was obtained from both the student and their guardian. All participants and guardians were informed in detail about the study aims, procedures, potential risks, and expected benefits. Participation was entirely voluntary and withdrawal was permitted at any time. All data were anonymized, securely stored, and not disclosed to teachers or any third parties.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was based on the expected between-group difference in the primary outcome (SDS score). Using G*Power 3.1.9.7 with the “two-independent-means comparison (two-tailed)” model—statistically equivalent to ANCOVA with baseline adjustment—the significance level was set at α = 0.05 and the statistical power at 1 − β = 0.80. Based on prior literature and pilot data, the expected effect size was set at Cohen’s d = 0.80 (moderate-to-large effect). The calculation showed that a minimum of 21 participants per group was required.

To strengthen the robustness of the study, approximately 30 students per group (about 60 in total) were targeted prior to safety screening. In practice, 60 students passed the SDS preliminary screening and proceeded to secondary assessment. Nine students were excluded due to failure to meet cardiopulmonary safety criteria. Ultimately, 51 participants were enrolled (26 in the exergame group and 25 in the control group), meeting and exceeding the minimum required sample size.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence. An independent researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment, assessment, or intervention delivery generated the allocation sequence using SPSS 26.0. Participants were randomly assigned to either the exergame group (n = 26) or the control group (n = 25) in a 1:1 ratio. To ensure allocation concealment, all randomization codes were placed into sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE). After participants completed the baseline assessment, another staff member who was not involved in the intervention opened the envelopes sequentially to determine group allocation. Neither the research team nor the outcome assessors had prior knowledge or ability to predict the allocation sequence.

Given the behavioral nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and intervention instructors was not feasible. However, assessor blinding was rigorously maintained to minimize measurement bias. All psychological assessments (SDS, SAS, PSQI) were conducted by independent evaluators who were blinded to group assignment and followed standardized assessment procedures. Throughout data entry and statistical analysis, group labels were anonymized using coded identifiers (e.g., Group A, Group B), ensuring that analysts remained blinded until all analyses were completed. No incidents of unblinding occurred during the study.

Intervention protocols

Exergame group

The Nintendo exergaming system was used as the exercise intervention platform. Based on findings from preliminary pilot testing, four games capable of eliciting moderate-intensity physical activity were selected: Aerobic Step from Wii Fit Plus, Tennis and Boxing from Wii Sports, Fitness Boxing, and Just Dance. Exercise intensity was prescribed according to the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription, 11th Edition (2021) 25, with moderate intensity defined as 64–76% of maximum heart rate (HRmax), corresponding to a target training heart rate of 129–153 beats/min.

Each training session consisted of 5–10 min of warm-up, 30 min of exergaming, and 5 min of cool-down, totaling approximately 40 min. The intervention schedule was as follows: two sessions per week during Weeks 1–2, followed by three sessions per week during Weeks 3–8, resulting in 22 sessions in total. Participants with an attendance rate of ≥ 80% were considered to have valid intervention data.

All sessions were supervised by two qualified instructors to ensure participant safety, training adherence, and correct execution of movements. Participants wore a wrist-worn heart rate monitor (Redmi Band 3, Xiaomi, China) during each session. Heart rate was monitored in real time to ensure that exercise intensity remained within the prescribed moderate-intensity range (64–76% HRmax). The intervention followed a standardized protocol including fixed session duration, predefined game selection, and consistent training frequency to maintain intervention fidelity across sessions.

Control group

Participants in the control group did not engage in any form of structured exercise intervention. They continued their regular school routines, including attending classes, self-study periods, and scheduled rest, following the school’s standardized daily timetable. No additional exercise sessions or psychological training organized by the research team were provided to this group.

Similar to the exergame group, control group participants completed psychological assessments at the same time points, including baseline (pre-intervention) and post-intervention evaluations of SDS, SAS, and PSQI.

Outcome measures and assessment timeline

Primary outcome

Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS): The primary outcome was depressive symptoms assessed using the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), developed by Zung in 196526. The SDS evaluates individuals’ subjective experience of depressive symptoms over the past week, covering emotional, somatic, and psychological domains. The total score of the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) is calculated as the sum of the scores of the 20 items. A standard score of 53 is used as the cut-off point: a score below 53 indicates normal, 53–62 indicates mild depression, 63–72 indicates moderate depression, and above 72 indicates severe depression. The SDS is one of the most widely used self-report instruments for screening depressive states in both clinical and research settings26.

Secondary outcomes

Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS): Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), also developed by Zung27. The SAS measures individuals’ subjective experience of anxiety symptoms during the past week. The Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) consists of 20 items rated on a four-point Likert scale. A standard score of 50 is used as the cut-off point: a score below 50 indicates normal, 50–59 indicates mild anxiety, 60–69 indicates moderate anxiety, and above 69 indicates severe anxiety27. It is widely used in both clinical practice and research settings as a validated instrument for evaluating anxiety symptoms28.Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI): Sleep quality was evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), developed by Buysse et al. in 198929. The PSQI assesses overall sleep quality and sleep disturbances over the past month. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a widely used self-report instrument for assessing sleep quality. It consists of 19 self-rated items that generate seven component scores, including subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3, yielding a global score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. In Chinese populations, a cut-off score of 7 is commonly used: a global score ≤ 7 indicates good sleep quality, whereas a score > 7 indicates poor sleep quality. In the present study, the PSQI global score was used as the primary indicator of sleep quality for statistical analysis. Previous studies have demonstrated that the PSQI has good reliability and validity in Chinese populations30.

Data management and statistical analysis

All data were managed following standardized procedures to ensure accuracy, confidentiality, and reproducibility. Two researchers independently entered all questionnaire scores and demographic information, and discrepancies were resolved through cross-checking. After verification, the final dataset was stored on an encrypted hard drive accessible only to the research team.

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to analysis, the normality of continuous variables was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD).

Baseline characteristics between groups were compared using independent-sample t-tests to assess group comparability prior to the intervention. Within-group pre–post differences were assessed using paired-sample t-tests to describe changes within each group. Between-group intervention effects were analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with baseline scores included as covariates to control for baseline differences. In randomized controlled trials, ANCOVA with baseline adjustment is commonly used because it allows comparison of post-intervention outcomes while accounting for baseline variability between groups. The assumption of homogeneity of regression slopes (group × baseline interaction) was tested before conducting ANCOVA, and all models met this requirement. Adjusted post-intervention means and their 95% confidence intervals were reported, and Cohen’s d was calculated as the effect size (0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively).

As there were no dropouts or missing data in this trial, the results of intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses were identical. Therefore, complete-case data were used for reporting. Sensitivity analyses were performed using unadjusted change scores (Δ = post-intervention − baseline) to compare between-group differences, and the results were consistent with the ANCOVA findings, indicating robust conclusions. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at α = 0.05.

Data availability

Due to ethical restrictions and the requirement to protect the confidentiality of minor participants, individual-level data from this trial cannot be publicly shared. De-identified aggregated data, data dictionaries, and analysis code may be made available by the corresponding author upon reasonable academic request. All data-sharing procedures comply with the ethical requirements approved by the Ethics Committee of Capital University of Physical Education and Sports (Approval No. 2024A125).

Adverse events monitoring

Prior to the start of the intervention, all participants underwent cardiopulmonary function testing to evaluate exercise tolerance and exclude individuals unsuitable for physical activity, thereby minimizing potential exercise-related risks at the outset. Throughout the intervention period, all exergaming sessions were supervised by trained instructors to ensure that exercise intensity remained within safe limits and to allow real-time monitoring of participants’ physical responses.

If a participant experienced discomfort during training—such as dizziness, palpitations, chest tightness, nausea, dyspnea, unusual fatigue, or muscle pain—the session was immediately paused. Instructors conducted an initial assessment and, if necessary, accompanied the participant to the school medical office for further evaluation and management. All such incidents were documented as adverse events, including the time of occurrence, symptoms, management procedures, and recovery status.

In cases of severe adverse events (SAEs)—such as acute exercise-induced injury, syncope, or any condition potentially threatening life—the research team followed a pre-established emergency response protocol to ensure immediate and appropriate safety measures. Additionally, any SAE would be reported to the ethics committee within 24 h.

No severe adverse events occurred during the study. All participants completed the intervention safely, indicating that the exergaming-based exercise protocol demonstrated a favorable safety profile among adolescents.

Ethics and informed consent

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Capital University of Physical Education and Sports (Approval No. 2024A125) and was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2400092742; URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html) on 22/11/2024. At the time of manuscript submission, the trial results had not yet been publicly posted on the registry.

Before participation, all adolescents and their legal guardians were fully informed of the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks, and written informed consent was obtained from both parties. All personal data collected were kept strictly confidential and used solely for scientific analysis and reporting purposes. The entire study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring full protection of participants’ rights, privacy, and welfare throughout the research process.

Results

During the mid-intervention SDS safety monitoring (Week 4), no participants showed a clinically significant worsening of depressive symptoms or met criteria requiring psychological referral. Therefore, all participants remained eligible and completed the intervention.

Baseline comparison between groups

At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the exergame group and the control group in Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), or Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores (all p > 0.05). Specifically, SDS scores were 50.29 ± 1.38 in the exergame group and 50.12 ± 1.36 in the control group (t = 0.45, p = 0.66); SAS scores were 41.38 ± 5.55 and 41.45 ± 6.59, respectively (t = − 0.04, p = 0.97); and PSQI scores were 9.0 ± 2.2 and 8.9 ± 2.3, respectively (t = 0.16, p = 0.87). These findings indicate good baseline equivalence between the two groups across all psychological indicators, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent analyses of intervention effects (see Table 1).

Based on Table 2, the two groups showed comparable SDS scores at baseline (exergame group: 50.29 ± 1.38; control group: 50.12 ± 1.36), with no significant difference, indicating good baseline equivalence in depressive symptoms. After the 8-week intervention, the SDS score in the exergame group decreased markedly to 47.92 ± 1.31, whereas the control group remained essentially unchanged (50.01 ± 1.40). Regarding within-group changes, the exergame group demonstrated a mean reduction of 2.37 ± 2.11 points, compared with only 0.11 ± 1.77 points in the control group, suggesting a substantially greater improvement in depressive symptoms among participants receiving the exergame intervention. To further compare post-intervention outcomes while accounting for baseline differences, ANCOVA was performed with baseline SDS as a covariate. The results revealed a significant main effect of group, F (1, 49) = 18.23, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.27, indicating a strong intervention effect of the intervention in reducing depressive symptoms (partial η² ≥ 0.14 considered a large effect). ANCOVA-adjusted means showed post-intervention SDS scores of 47.90 ± 0.25 for the exergame group and 50.11 ± 0.25 for the control group. The adjusted between-group difference was 2.21 points (95% CI: 1.18–3.25), further confirming the statistical significance of the intervention effect, with the confidence interval entirely favoring improvement in the exergame group. Moreover, the between-group effect size calculated from the change scores (Δ) yielded Cohen’s d = 1.16, indicating a large effect (d ≥ 0.8). This result demonstrates that the exergame intervention not only produced statistically significant improvements compared with the control condition but also yielded a substantial and practically meaningful reduction in depressive symptoms.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that an exergame-based exercise intervention significantly improved depressive symptoms, anxiety levels, and sleep quality among adolescents with subthreshold depression. After adjusting for baseline differences using ANCOVA, the exergame group demonstrated significantly lower post-intervention SDS, SAS, and PSQI scores compared with the control group, with adjusted between-group differences of 2.21, 6.10, and 1.90 points, respectively (all p < 0.01). The corresponding effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 1.06–1.16) ranged from medium to large, highlighting the clinical relevance of the improvements. In contrast, the control group exhibited no meaningful changes across outcomes. These results are consistent with previous studies by Rosenberg18 and Hsin-Yen Yen16 in older adults with depression, as well as Xu Wenge17 in college students, collectively suggesting that exergaming, as an interactive digital exercise modality, effectively reduces negative emotions and enhances psychological well-being across age groups and varying levels of psychological vulnerability.

Exergaming integrates physical activity, entertainment, and human–computer interaction, enabling real-time motor-driven control of virtual environments and creating an immersive “gamified exercise” experience. The mechanisms through which exergames alleviate depressive symptoms appear to involve both psychological and physiological pathways. Psychologically, moderate-intensity exergame activity promotes the release of neurotransmitters such as endorphins, dopamine, and serotonin, activating reward circuits that elicit pleasure and reduce stress31,32. Game features—such as real-time scoring, progressive challenges, and visual feedback—enhance self-efficacy and foster positive affect. In addition, virtual interaction can strengthen social engagement and emotional connectedness, reducing loneliness and maladaptive self-focus while enhancing perceived social support.

Physiologically, the exercise intensity elicited by exergames is comparable to traditional aerobic exercise, which may downregulate hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity, reduce cortisol levels, and mitigate stress responses33. Exergaming has also been shown to increase brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), supporting neuroplasticity and prefrontal cortical regulation of emotion34. Furthermore, cognitive–motor tasks within exergames—such as coordination, judgment, and response inhibition—activate prefrontal and parietal cortical regions, contributing to improvements in cognitive flexibility and executive function35,36. Together, these psychological and physiological mechanisms may explain the superior effects of exergaming on emotional recovery and cognitive–affective regulation.

In addition to its therapeutic effects, exergaming offers notable advantages over traditional aerobic exercise, particularly in terms of enjoyment and intrinsic motivation. These characteristics are associated with higher adherence, which is a critical predictor of long-term intervention success, as indicated by prior research (e.g., Helgadóttir37; Patrick38). Such features make exergaming especially suitable for adolescents with depressive tendencies, who often struggle with engagement in conventional exercise programs.

Furthermore, exergames are easy to implement, well accepted by youth, and require minimal space or equipment, making them highly feasible for school-based applications. For adolescents who may avoid traditional psychological counseling due to stigma or discomfort, exergaming provides a low-threat, low-stigma alternative that supports emotional regulation and social–emotional engagement in a non-clinical environment. Integrating exergames into school physical education and mental health programs may help establish a routine mechanism for emotional support and contribute to the digitalization and diversification of school-based mental health promotion. Moreover, in out-of-school contexts, such as home or community settings, exergaming also demonstrates high feasibility and strong potential for broader dissemination39. Its flexibility and engaging nature enable it to serve as an effective complement to traditional forms of exercise, particularly in enhancing adolescents’ participation and adherence in unstructured environments40. Therefore, exergaming shows good real-world generalizability and translational potential; however, factors such as equipment accessibility, implementation guidance, and intervention design should be carefully considered in practical applications.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and drawn from a single geographic region, which may limit generalizability. Second, the intervention duration was relatively short, preventing assessment of long-term maintenance effects. Third, although validated questionnaires were used to measure emotional and sleep outcomes, objective physiological indicators (e.g., EEG, heart rate variability, or sleep monitoring) were not included; future research should incorporate multimodal measures to enhance objectivity. In addition, some potentially relevant baseline characteristics (e.g., BMI, baseline physical activity level, and prior gaming familiarity) were not systematically collected, which may limit the comprehensiveness of baseline comparability assessment between groups. Finally, the present study did not differentiate between types of exergames (e.g., competitive, rhythm-based, or dancing games); future research could employ multi-arm designs to identify optimal exergame formats for mental health enhancement.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a digital exercise intervention based on exergaming can significantly improve the mental health status of adolescents with subthreshold depression. The 8-week exergame training produced statistically and clinically meaningful improvements in depressive symptoms, anxiety levels, and sleep quality, with effect sizes ranging from medium to large and clearly superior to those of the control condition. As an interactive, engaging, and exercise-integrated digital modality, exergaming facilitates emotional regulation, reduces stress responses, and enhances psychological resilience, while offering high feasibility, acceptability, and strong potential for school-based implementation.

Overall, this study provides empirical support for the use of exergaming as a “digital exercise therapy” for adolescent mental-health promotion. Future research with larger samples, longer intervention periods, and multidimensional assessment approaches is needed to further clarify the underlying mechanisms, identify optimal target populations, and refine intervention protocols, thereby promoting the standardized and systematic integration of exergaming into school mental-health education systems.
