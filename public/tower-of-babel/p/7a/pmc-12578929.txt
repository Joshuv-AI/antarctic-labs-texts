TET1 modulates trophoblast function by regulation of ODC1 in preeclampsia
Authors: Chao Fan, Yan Kang, Zhenya Fang, Lixia Liu, Chunying Wang, Man Zhao, Li Xiao, Changqing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreeclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy-specific disease that seriously endangers the health of the pregnant woman and the fetus. DNA hydroxymethylation, an epigenetic modification, plays crucial roles in the development of PE. However, the molecular mechanism remains to be elucidated. TET1 is a DNA demethylase that regulates placental trophoblast function. Herein, we investigated the role of TET1 in PE occurrence. Firstly, we confirmed that the 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) levels were reduced in placental tissues from human PE patients and a mouse PE model, while TET1 expression was upregulated. In an oxidative stress model of HTR8/SVneo cells, knockdown of TET1 partially restored the impaired cell invasion and migration caused by H2O2. To elucidate the mechanism, bioinformatics analysis of TET1-knockdown RNA sequencing and ChIP-seq data identified ornithine decarboxylase (ODC1), a rate-limiting enzyme in the polyamine biosynthesis, as a potential target. Similarly, knockdown of ODC1 alleviated the H2O2-induced dysfunction in HTR8/SVneo cells. Moreover, the CUT&RUN and dual-luciferase assays revealed that TET1 regulates the expression of ODC1 by affecting the methylation level of its promoter. Collectively, these results demonstrate that TET1-mediated DNA hydroxymethylation is associated with the occurrence of PE. Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a serious pregnancy disorder that affects about 2–8% of pregnancies worldwide and is characterized by new-onset of hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks’ gestation. PE can progress rapidly to serious complications and even death in the mother and fetus1,2. The only available cure for PE patients is delivery of the placenta, which poses significant risks to both maternal and fetal health. PE is classified into two distinct subtypes: early-onset and late-onset PE. Early-onset PE is primarily driven by placental insufficiency, whereas late-onset PE results from the interplay between physiological placental aging and maternal genetic predispositions to certain diseases3. Growing evidence suggests that genetic, immune, and environmental factors could be involved in the development of PE, but the pathogenesis remains unclear4–7. Recent findings note that DNA methylation and hydroxymethylation, the epigenetic modifications, contribute to PE by regulating gene expression linked to placental dysfunction8,9. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying DNA methylation may identify new novel therapeutic targets and inform early intervention strategies for PE.

DNA methylation is a widespread epigenetic mechanism that plays a critical role in gene regulation. Dysregulation of DNA methylation could alter the expression of key genes implicated in PE10. It has been shown that DNA methylation patterns are altered in specific regions of the placental epigenome in PE patients11–13. The ten-eleven translocation methylcytosine dioxygenase 1 (TET1), an important DNA demethylase converting 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5-hmC), regulates the methylation levels at gene promoters and transcription start sites14. The function of TET1 has been most fully investigated in tumors, where it acts as both a suppressor and oncogenic factor in tumor development15–18. While it is a tumor suppressor under normoxia, TET1 promotes oncogenesis of thyroid cancer in hypoxic condition through a non-enzymatic interaction with the hypoxia-inducible factor alpha (HIF1α)19. Additionally, TET1 exerts an important influence on early embryonic development20. Deletion of paternal TET1 disrupts imprinted gene expression, leading to placental and fetal developmental defects, and even lethality21. In the choriocarcinoma cell line JEG3, knockdown of TET1 reduced the expression of HIF1α by hypermethylation of its promoter, impairing cell migration and invasive functions during placentation22. Nonetheless, the function of TET1 under different PE pathogenesis conditions still needs to be further explored.

Polyamines regulate the proliferation and invasion of trophoblast cells during implantation and are essential for embryonic development and placentation23. At 34 weeks of gestation, the incidence of PE is positively corelated with polyamine metabolism24. Elevated levels of the polyamine metabolite N1, N12-diacetylspermine (DiAcSpm) in plasma is associated with risk of PE development25. Ornithine decarboxylase (ODC1), a pivotal enzyme in the polyamine synthesis pathway, is essential for maintaining intracellular polyamine homeostasis. Accumulating evidence supports the involvement of ODC1 in PE genesis and embryonic development. Fatkhudinov et al. reported significant upregulation of ODC1 in a subset of CD14 + + monocytes from the peripheral blood of pregnant women experiencing late-onset PE21, suggesting its potential role in the development of PE. Studies in mouse models further showed that dysregulated of ODC1 impairs proliferation and invasion of trophoblast cells, as well as failure to form the placental labyrinthine and junctional zones26. Although ODC1 has been implicated in the occurrence of PE, its precise role in this condition requires further investigation.

Although both TET1 dysfunction and ODC1 overexpression have been implicated in the pathogenesis of PE, the direct mechanistic link between these two pathways remains to be elucidated. The objective of this study was to ascertain whether TET1 functions as an epigenetic regulator of ODC1, thereby integrating epigenetic and metabolic dysregulation in trophoblast cells during the course of PE. In this study, we investigated the role of the DNA hydroxymethylase TET1 in the development of PE. Our findings indicate that TET1 contributes to PE by impairing the invasion and migration functions of trophoblasts. Further analysis revealed that TET1-mediated DNA hydroxymethylation influences trophoblast function by modulating both the promoter activity and expression of ODC1. These findings identify TET1 as a potential therapeutic target for preventing PE.

Materials and methods

Samples collection

The clinical specimens were obtained from Shandong Provincial Maternal and Child Healthcare Hospital. The study upheld the subjects’ right to privacy, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Maternal and Child Healthcare Hospital Affiliated to Qingdao University (NO.2024-095) and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Placental samples were obtained from patients (n = 12) with severe early-onset PE (26–32 years old) who exhibited diastolic blood pressures ≥ 110 mmHg, systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 160 mmHg and proteinuria > 300 mg/day (Supplementary Table 1). Healthy pregnant women within the same age range served as normal controls (n = 12). Isolated placental tissues were immediately rinsed with PBS to eliminate residual blood. A portion of each sample was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for future use, while the remaining tissue was processed for the extraction of protein, DNA, and RNA.

Cell culture

The human placental trophoblast cell HTR8/SVneo purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, CRL-3271) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Invitrogen, USA), 1% sodium pyruvate (Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Once cells reached 80% confluence, the cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with 200 µM H2O2 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h to induce trophoblastic oxidative stress.

Cell transfection

HTR8/SVneo cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 4 × 10⁵ cells per well. Upon reaching 70% confluence, 40 pmol siRNA and 8 µL of Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX transfection reagent were mixed in Opti-MEM™ medium (Invitrogen, USA) and added into each well. After 6 h post transfection, the medium was replaced with complete medium. After 12 h, H2O2 was added into the medium to a final concentration of 200 µM, followed by incubation for another 24 h. Finally, the cells were collected for protein or RNA extraction, Transwell or Wound healing assays. siTET1 (5′-GAUGAGUAUUGGUCAGACATT-3′) and siODC1 (5′- GCGUCUAUGGAUCAUUUAATT-3′) were used for knocking down, while siNC (5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACAUTT-3′) served as a negative control.

Cell counting Kit‑8 (CCK8) assay

HTR8/SVneo Cells (1.0 × 105 cells/well) were cultured in 96-well plates until 80% confluence. Then, the cells were treated with 200 µM H2O2 for 24 h. After washing with PBS, cell culture medium was replaced with 110 µL of CCK-8 working solution and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm (OD450) using a microplate reader (Norgen, Canada).

Global DNA methylation level profiling

Genomic DNA of the placenta tissue or trophoblast cells was isolated using the FastPure Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, China). The global DNA methylation levels were detected by MethylFlash™ Global DNA Methylation (5-mC) ELISA Easy Kit (Colorimetric) (Epigentek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-Time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the following procedure: Placental tissue or trophoblast cells were homogenized in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Then, 200 µL of chloroform was added, and the mixture was vigorously vortexed before being centrifuged. Subsequently, 500 µL of the aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol to precipitate the RNA. After centrifugation, the resulting pellet was washed once with ice-cold 75% ethanol and air-dried. Finally, the RNA was dissolved in DEPC-treated water. The reverse transcription of RNA into the first strand cDNA was accomplished using HiScript®III RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China). qPCR was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) in the LightCycler 480 system (Roche, USA). The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used for RT-qPCR were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Total proteins of the placental tissues or trophoblast cells were extracted with cold RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) with 1 mM PMSF (phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride, Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was determined by a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 12% or 7.5% SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred onto 0.22 μm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Sigma, USA). Subsequently, the PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing three times, an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:3000, G1213, Servicebio, China) was applied for 1 h incubation. The protein bands were visualized using the ECL chemiluminescent reagent and Amersham Imager 600 system (Amersham, USA) and analyzed with ImageJ software (ImageJ 1.54 m). The following primary antibodies were used in this study: anti-TET1 (1:2000, A1506, ABclonal, China), anti-ODC1 (1:2000, A1948, ABclonal, China), anti-β-ACTIN (1:2000; GB11001, ServiceBio, China).

Wound-healing assay

Following siRNA transfection and H₂O₂ treatment as described above, HTR8/SVneo cells (4 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates. Once cells reached 100% confluence, wounds were inflicted using a 200 µL micropipette tip. And then the cells were washed twice with PBS carefully and cultured in fresh medium containing 2% FBS for 24 h. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h of cell culture. The wound area was quantified using ImageJ software and the migration index was calculated as follows: Migration index = (Wound area at 0 h – Wound area at 24 h)/Wound area at 0 h.

Transwell invasion assay

60 µL of Matrigel matrix (1 mg/mL, ABM, China) was added in the Transwell inserts (Biofil, China) and solidified at 37 °C for 1 h. 1.0 × 105 of HTR8/SVneo cells with siRNA transfection and H₂O₂ treatment were seeded into the upper chamber in 150 µL of serum-free medium. The lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of culture medium containing 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, the cells invaded on the bottom of the insert were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with crystal violet (1%) for 30 min. Finally, the cells were observed under a microscope (Nikon SMZ25, Japan) and the number of invaded cells was analyzed using ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing

RNA extracted from trophoblast cells was submitted to Hangzhou Kaitai Biotechnology Co. Ltd. for sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform in PE150 mode. Clean reads used for subsequent analysis were obtained after filtering raw data, assessing sequencing error rates, and evaluating GC content distribution. Transcript assembly, quantification, and differential expression analysis were performed using StringTie software. For multiple testing correction during RNA-seq analysis, the Benjamini–Hochberg method was applied. Differentially expressed genes were identified with edgeR software using the thresholds of |log2FC|>1 and padj < 0.05. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the given differential genes was performed using clusterProfiler software.

CUT&RUN and the dual-luciferase assay

The CUT&RUN assay kit (HD102-01, Vazyme, China) was used to analyze the enrichment of TET1 in ODC1 promoter region following manufacturer’s instructions. HTR8/SVneo cells were harvested at 48 h after transfection with plasmid pCDNA-TET1 (OETET1) or the empty vector control pCDNA-NC (OENC), respectively. The harvested cells were incubated with ConA beads for 10 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with 1 µg of TET1 antibody at 4 °C. A parallel negative control was prepared using 1 µg of normal IgG antibody. After incubation with pG-MNase, genomic DNA was digested and purified. The enriched DNA fragments were amplified using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, and relative enrichment was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Similarly, 5-mC enrichment in the ODC1 promoter was detected using a 5-mC antibody (Epigentek, USA) following the same kit protocol.

HEK-293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216) were used for dual-luciferase reporter assays to evaluate promoter activity. The cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well. Upon reaching 70% confluence, plasmids were transfected into cells with Lipofectamine™ 3000 and P3000™ (Invitrogen, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The plasmid pRL-TK was purchased from Jinan Boshang Biotechnology Co. Ltd., pGL3-P4512, pGL3-P4513, pGL3-NC and pCDNA-TET1 were constructed via homologous recombination. At 36 h post-transfection, dual-luciferase activity was measured using a microplate reader and defined as the ratio of firefly luciferase to Renilla luciferase activity (Vazyme, China). Primer sequences used for qPCR and molecular cloning are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

PE mouse model establishment

The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital (NO.2024-067). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines, the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020), as well as relevant local legislation and institutional requirements. ICR pregnant mice purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. were divided into two groups (n = 6 per group): a control group and an L-NAME group (MedChemExpress, USA). The presence of a vaginal plug was designated as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). From E8.5 to E17.5, mice in the L-NAME group received daily intraperitoneal injections of 125 mg/kg.d.bw (mice weight ranges from approximately 30 g to 45 g from early to late pregnancy) L-NAME, while the control was treated with an equivalent volume of PBS. Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% pentobarbital (60 mg/kg) and received cervical dislocation at E18.5, after which the fetuses and placentas were collected and weighed.

Statistical analyses

All experiments were performed independently at least three times and analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad, USA). Statistical significance was assessed by Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, with p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

DNA methylation level was reduced and TET1 expression was increased in PE placental tissues

To analyze the DNA methylation levels in placental tissues from PE patients (PE) and normal controls (Nor), global 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) levels were measured in genomic DNA. The 5-mC level in PE placental tissues was significantly lower compared to the normal group (Fig. 1A), suggesting a potential role of DNA methylation in PE pathogenesis. Subsequently, RT-qPCR was performed to determine the transcript levels of key DNA methylation-related enzymes. The results showed that TET1, a DNA demethylase, was the most markedly upregulated transcript in PE placental tissues (Fig. 1B). Western blot analysis further confirmed a significant increase in TET1 protein expression in the PE group (Fig. 1C), further supporting the involvement of TET1-mediated DNA methylation in the development of PE.

The protein levels of TET1 in PE placental tissues (C) and HTR8/SVneo cells treated with 200 µM H2O2 (F). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001

To investigate the role of TET1 in PE, a H2O2-induced oxidative stress model of HTR8/SVneo cells was established. Notably, the global DNA methylation level of HTR8/SVneo cells was markedly decreased with H2O2 treatment (Fig. 1D). Consistent with the expression in PE placental tissues, TET1 mRNA exhibited the most pronounced upregulation among the DNA methylation-related enzymes (Fig. 1E). This was corroborated by a substantial increase in TET1 protein levels (Fig. 1F).

Downregulation of TET1 alleviates the functional impairment of HTR8/SVneo cells induced by H2O2

To explore the function of TET1 in PE, we knocked down TET1 expression in HTR8/SVneo cells using siRNA. Successful knockdown was confirmed by Western blotting, which showed a significant reduction in TET1 protein levels (Fig. 2A). As expected, inhibition of TET1 activity resulted in an increase of the DNA methylation level (Fig. 2B). Western blotting showed that the expression of TET1 was elevated in response to H2O2 treatment, while it was reduced in TET1-knockdown cells under the same oxidative stress conditions (Fig. 2C). We assessed the proliferative ability of HTR8/SVneo cells using the CCK-8 assay, which revealed that the cell proliferative capacity was significantly diminished in the presence of H2O2 but was restored upon TET1 knockdown (Fig. 2D). The wound healing assay indicated that H₂O₂ markedly reduced cell migration compared to the control group, whereas TET1 knockdown prior to H₂O₂ exposure considerably enhanced migratory ability (Fig. 2E). Consistent with these findings, Transwell invasion assays showed that H₂O₂ significantly suppressed cell invasion, and this effect was markedly rescued by TET1 inhibition (Fig. 2F).

ODC1 is a downstream target of TET1 in HTR8/SVneo cells

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms through which TET1 regulates trophoblast cell function, total RNA was extracted from siTET1- and siNC-transfected trophoblast cells for RNA sequencing (Fig. 3A). Compared to the control group, 709 genes were downregulated and 629 genes were upregulated upon TET1 knockdown (Fig. 3B). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that TET1 silencing significantly affected several biological processes in trophoblast cells, including angiogenesis, cell junction assembly, cell adhesion, and DNA binding (Fig. 3C). Next, we retrieved the CHIP-seq data of TET1 in HUES8 cells (ID: 82036) from the Cistrome DB database27, and compared it with our RNA-seq results to identify potential direct target genes. A total of 11 common genes were identified in both datasets. Among these, ODC1, an ornithine decarboxylase, was selected for the following analysis as it exhibited the lowest expression level and higher ChIP-seq score (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Table 3). Subsequent Western blot analysis that ODC1 expression was significantly downregulated following TET1 knockdown, suggesting its role as a potential downstream target of TET1 in trophoblast cells (Fig. 3E).

Notably, the H2O2-induced upregulation of ODC1 expression was markedly reduced when ODC1 was knocked down in HTR8/SVneo cells (Fig. 4C).

Additionally, ODC1 also affected the function of trophoblast cells. Wound-healing and Transwell invasion assays demonstrated that H₂O₂ treatment significantly impaired the migratory and invasive capacities of HTR8/SVneo cells. These effects were partially reversed upon ODC1 knockdown (Fig. 4D-E). These findings suggest that ODC1 contributes to the pathogenesis of PE and may play a role in TET1-mediated dysfunction of trophoblast cells.

TET1 binds to the promoter of ODC1 to enhance its expression

Based on the above results, we further investigated the mechanism by which TET1 regulates ODC1. The co-expression patterns of TET1 and ODC1 in the placenta and trophoblast were analyzed using the ChIPBase database28, which revealed a strong positive correlation between their expression levels (Fig. 5A-B). This was consistent with our experimental results, supporting a potential regulatory relationship. Analysis of the Cistrome DB database (ID: 82036) identified two prominent peaks, peak4512 and peak4513, within the promoter region of the ODC1 gene in human embryonic stem cells (HUES8) (Fig. 5C). To explore the combination between TET1 and the ODC1 promoter, we characterized six target sites located on peak4512 and peak4513 using CUT&RUN method under TET1 overexpression conditions. While the negative control group (OENC) showed significant TET1 enrichment only at sites 2 and 6, TET1 overexpression (OETET1) led to substantial enrichment across all six target sites located across peak4512 and peak4513 (Fig. 5C-D). To assess the effect on promoter methylation, we enriched the fragments at the ODC1 promoter site 4 using a 5-mC antibody and found that TET1 overexpression significantly diminished fragments enrichment at this locus, thereby demonstrating a reduction in promoter 5-mC levels (Fig. 5E).

To further validate these findings, we conducted dual-luciferase reporter assays utilizing peak4512 and peak4513 as promoters. The results showed that peak4512 exhibited promoter activity and luciferase expression driven by peak4512 was enhanced under TET1 overexpression (Fig. 5F). Similar results were observed for peak4513 (Fig. 5G). These findings suggest that both peak4512 and peak4513 function as active promoter elements of ODC1 and that TET1 enhances their activity through direct binding and promoter demethylation, providing additional evidence that TET1 regulates ODC1 expression through direct binding to its promoter.

Increased TET1 and ODC1 expression levels in the placental tissues of PE mice

As a specific eNOS inhibitor, L-NAME can successfully induce key manifestations of PE in mice, including abnormalities in fetal and placental development30. Therefore, we established a PE mouse model by administering L-NAME from E8.5 to E17.5 (Fig. 6A). Compared with controls, the L-NAME group exhibited significantly reduced fetal and placental weights, along with increased rates of embryonic resorption (Fig. 6B-C). Consistent with the above findings, 5-mC levels were decreased in the placentas of L-NAME group (Fig. 6D). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Tet1 and Odc1 expression levels were significantly upregulated in the L-NAME-treated placentas (Fig. 6E). Similarly, Western blotting confirmed elevated protein expression of Tet1 and Odc1 in the L-NAME group compared to controls (Fig. 6F). Altogether, these results suggest that dysregulated DNA methylation contributes to PE pathogenesis in mice, with Tet1 and Odc1 playing important roles in this process.

Discussion

PE, a pregnancy-related disease with unclear pathogenesis, is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates for both mothers and neonates worldwide. Recent studies have shown that DNA methylation and hydroxymethylation, key epigenetic modifications, play a significant role in the development of PE31,32. In 2023, De Borre et al. identified DNA methylation differences in plasma from control and PE pregnancies, establishing cell-free DNA methylation profiling for presymptomatic PE risk assessment33. Vasconcelos et al. pointed out that abnormal placental development may result from the dynamic dysregulation of 5-hmC and 5-mC levels34. Nevertheless, the precise regulatory mechanisms linking these epigenetic changes to PE remain not fully elucidated. In a systematic review, Cirkovic et al. found significant differences in DNA methylation levels between the placental tissues from PE patients and normal pregnancies, with the majority of studies (14 out of 19) indicating hypomethylation in PE placentas35. Further investigations showed that TET enzymes regulate trophoblast cell differentiation, migration, and invasion of placental, underscoring their importance in PE pathophysiology22,36. Impaired trophoblast invasion and migration leading to aberrant placental development are recognized as major contributors to PE37. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed significantly reduced global DNA methylation (5-mC) in placental tissues from PE patients compared to normal controls, suggesting that DNA hypomethylation may contribute to PE pathogenesis. We also found increased mRNA and protein levels of TET1, indicating a potential role for TET1 in modulating trophoblast cell invasion and migration in PE.

TET1 acts as a key DNA demethylase that converts 5-mC to 5-hmC, thereby initiating the process of DNA demethylation. While the role of TET1 in preeclampsia (PE) remains poorly characterized, we investigated its function under oxidative stress conditions mimicking the PE placental environment. In HTR8/SVneo cells treated with 200 µM H₂O₂, TET1 expression was upregulated, accompanied by a decrease in global 5-mC levels. Knocking down of TET1 led to a dramatic increase of the 5-mC levels in HTR8/SVneo cells, supporting its role as an active demethylase in trophoblasts. Compared to the siNC group, the proliferation capacity of HTR8/SVneo cells was significantly reduced by H2O2 treatment, aligning with the results of previous studies38. Furthermore, while the invasion and migration abilities of trophoblast cells were significantly diminished under H2O2 induction, knockdown of TET1 partially restored these functions. It indicates that the upregulated expression of TET1 inhibits the proliferative, invasive, and migratory capacities of HTR8/SVneo cells, which is consistent with its function in cervical cancer cells39.

As a DNA demethylase, TET1 binds to CpG-rich region of the genome and activates gene transcription by hindering the catalytic activity of DNA methyltransferases40. In JEG3 cell line, another type of trophoblast, TET1 was shown to regulate the expression of HIF1α through modulation of DNA methylation, thereby influencing cell invasion and migration22. In our study, we identified ODC1 as a downstream target of TET1 using transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis following TET1 knockdown. ODC1 is a rate-limiting enzyme in polyamine biosynthesis and plays a crucial role in maintaining intracellular polyamine homeostasis, which has been implicated in PE pathogenesis25,41. Previous studies have demonstrated that inhibition of ODC1 in mice and mink trophoblast cells disrupts polyamine synthesis and metabolism, leading to impaired trophoblast proliferation and invasion26,42. In support of this, we found elevated ODC1 expression in PE placental tissues and the oxidative stress model of HTR8/SVneo cells. Knockdown of ODC1 partially restored the H2O2-induced impairment of cell invasion and migration, mirroring the effects observed with TET1 knockdown. Earlier research has revealed that the promoter activity of ODC1 is suppressed by DNA methylation in early-stage uroepithelial cancer cells43,44. Analysis of the ChIPBase database in our study demonstrated a strong correlation between TET1 and ODC1 expression. In HTR8/SVneo cells, the CUT&RUN analysis demonstrated that TET1 binds to CpG-rich regions of the ODC1 promoter, specifically at peak4512 and peak4513. Dual-luciferase assays showed that TET1 enhances ODC1 promoter activity and upregulates its expression. These results identify ODC1 as a direct downstream target of TET1 and suggest that TET1 regulates cell functions via ODC1.

Additionally, in the L-NAME induced PE model of mice, we observed an increased embryo resorption rate along with reduced fetal weight, placental weight, and global placental 5-mC levels compared to the normal group. Both mRNA and protein expression levels of Tet1 and Odc1 were upregulated in the placenta of L-NAME induced mice. This proposes that Tet1 and Odc1 contribute to the development of PE in this animal model.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that TET1 directly regulates ODC1 epigenetically in trophoblast cells by binding to its promoter, thereby maintaining a hypomethylated state and modulating its aberrant expression. This study establishes a link between epigenetic dysregulation (TET1) and metabolic imbalance (ODC1) within a unified pathogenic pathway in PE, revealing how their interplay contributes to placental dysfunction. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitation of the present study. The L-NAME-induced PE model remains a widely utilized tool in research due to its ability to reproducibly induce key pathophysiological features of the syndrome. Notably, it is crucial to note that this model does not fully recapitulate the complex and multifactorial etiology of human PE, particularly the early placental dysfunction and diverse molecular pathways involved in the human condition45. In the present study, we employed this model to assess expression changes of TET1 and ODC1, and while we observed significant alterations. Nevertheless, the precise causal relationship between these two factors remains to be established using more precise genetic or physiological animal models.

Conclusion

Our results indicate that TET1-mediated DNA hydroxymethylation is associated with the occurrence of PE. We further demonstrate that TET1 regulates ODC1 expression by altering the methylation level of its promoter, thereby impairing trophoblast invasion and migration. These functional disruptions lead to abnormal placental development and ultimately contribute to the pathogenesis of PE. This study not only provides understanding of epigenetic mechanisms in PE, but also identifies novel potential targets and strategies for its early diagnosis and intervention.
