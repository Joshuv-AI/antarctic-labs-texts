ASNS Regulates H2O2 ‐Induced Senescence, Oxidative Stress, and Glucose Metabolism in ARPE‐19 Cells by Modulating USP13 Expression
Authors: Xiangyang Xin, Xin Zhao, Xiaobo Han, Wenhan Pu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAge‐related macular degeneration (AMD) is a common degenerative disease of the eye that ultimately leads to irreversible vision loss. Asparagine synthase (ASNS) is an aminotransferase, and its low expression is associated with retinal damage. The present study centered on the protective effect of ASNS on retinal epithelial cells. We found that in the AMD cell model, overexpression of ASNS reduced SA‐β‐gal staining and ROS production, and increased cell viability in H2O2‐treated ARPE‐19 cells. In addition, overexpression of ASNS increased glucose consumption, lactate production, extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and enhanced the expression of glycolytic markers. Molecular mechanistic studies revealed that ASNS was highly bound to USP13 protein and increased USP13 expression. Furthermore, ASNS protected the retinal epithelium from oxidative stress damage in an animal model of AMD. Taken together, these findings suggest that the ASNS/USP13 axis plays an important regulatory role in AMD development. Our findings not only emphasized the understanding of the role of glucose metabolism in AMD, but also identified a promising target for future AMD therapy.

Introduction

Age‐related macular degeneration (AMD) is a complex, multifactorial disease of the elderly that can cause retinal degeneration and ultimately blindness [1, 2]. There is a 25% risk of early AMD and 8% risk of late AMD in patients over the age of 75, with the number of cases expected to increase because of the aging population [3]. AMD is driven by a combination of genetic predisposition, natural aging changes, and lifestyle factors (such as smoking or nutritional intake) [4]. The mechanisms by which these risk factors interact and converge to form AMD are not fully understood, making drug development challenging, and no treatment to date has been fully effective [5, 6]. Consequently, it is urgent to understand the molecular mechanisms that underlie the generation and pathogenesis of AMD.

Retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cell damage is an important cause of visual impairment in patients with AMD [7]. Photoreceptor growth and survival depend on glucose transport in RPE cells. Glucose from the choroidal circulation must cross the RPE cytoplasm to reach the outer retina. Therefore, to meet the large demand for glucose in the retina, the retinal RPE expresses high levels of glucose transporter proteins [8]. The balance of glucose metabolic processes is therefore critical for retinal viability. It has been shown that aerobic glycolysis has a potentially important role in providing energy to photoreceptor cells and cellular anabolism [9, 10, 11, 12]. On the one hand, epithelial cell proliferation is dependent on glycolysis, and increasing aerobic glycolytic processes can promote cell proliferation [13]; on the other hand, the occurrence of aerobic glycolysis can counteract the process of epithelial cell senescence [14, 15]. Therefore, the glycolytic pathway has an important role in the regulation of AMD pathogenesis and needs to be studied in depth.

Asparagine synthase (ASNS) catalyzes the synthesis of asparagine and glutamate from aspartate and glutamine in an ATP‐dependent aminotransferase reaction accompanied by glutamine deamidation [16]. ASNS plays an important role in metabolic regulation. For example, transcriptional activation of ASNS promoted a corresponding increase in asparagine levels, reprogramming levels of asparagine metabolism to promote hepatocellular carcinoma progression [17]. NUCKS1 transcriptionally upregulated ASNS expression and promoted the synthesis of asparagine in osteosarcoma cells, which led to increased cell growth and metastasis [18]. Endothelial cell proliferation was impaired by the silencing of ASNS, and the combination of asparagine with the supplemental substrate α‐ketoglutarate rescued the defects in glutamine‐deficient endothelial cells, whereas asparagine alone partially rescued the proliferation of these endothelial cells, suggesting that asparagine plays a key role in the endothelial cell response to glutamine deficiency [19]. In addition, after retinal cell damage was induced with aldosterone, ASNS expression was found to be reduced in retinal tissue [20], suggesting that low expression of ASNS may be associated with retinal damage. However, the regulatory role of ASNS in retinal cells still needs to be further elucidated.

The present study centered on the role and mechanism of ASNS in retinal epithelial cells to reveal the potential pathogenesis of AMD. The results revealed that ASNS restored glycolysis and promoted proliferation to alleviate cellular senescence in H2O2‐induced retinal cells by interacting with the USP13 protein. This finding also suggested that ASNS‐regulated glucose metabolism might be a regulatory factor in AMD disease progression.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Treatment

Human retinal pigment epithelial cells ARPE‐19 were brought from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). ARPE‐19 cells were cultured in DMEM/F‐12 media (Beyotime, China) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% streptomycin/penicillin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Afterwards, 400 μM H2O2 (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was added to the cells and incubated for 6 h to construct an in vitro model of AMD [21].

Cell Infection

Lentiviral vectors interfering with ASNS and USP13 expression (shASNS and shUSP13) and the negative control shCtrl were synthesized by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Additionally, human overexpression of ASNS (ASNS‐OE) and control (NC) sequences were also designed by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). After treatment with H2O2, ARPE‐19 cells would be transfected with lentiviral vectors in the presence of 5 g/mL polybrene (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), and then selected for 7 days with puromycin (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) and cells resistant to puromycin were isolated for further studies.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (RT‐qPCR)

4 μg of total RNA was converted to cDNA using MultiScribe reverse transcriptase (Applied Biosystems, USA) after RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). cDNA was amplified using Applied Biosystems' Fast SYBR Green Master Mix. RT‐qPCR was used to quantify the amount of RNA extracted using the StepOnePlusTM. Primer sequences used in this study are shown in Table 1. Relative gene expression was quantitated using the 2−(△Ct sample−△Ct control) method.

Western Blotting Analysis (WB)

From cells or tissues, the whole protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). PVDF membranes were used to transfer the 20 μg of protein extracts loaded onto SDS‐10% polyacrylamide gels. These membranes were immersed in 5% (w/v) skim milk for a duration of 2 h. Each membrane was then probed using primary antibodies, and it was incubated for a further night at 4°C. The membranes were treated with three TBST washes before being incubated with HRP‐labeled secondary antibody for an hour at room temperature. The membrane was then washed again and subjected to an analysis utilizing the ECL Western blotting detection method. Table 2 displays the antibodies utilized in this investigation.

Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK8)

After infection with a lentiviral vector, H2O2‐treated ARPE‐19 cells were plated at a density of 2000 cells per well in a 96‐well plate. After that, Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK8; Beyotime, China) solution was added. The OD values were measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer (Multiscan MK3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Senescence‐Associated β‐Galactosidase (SA‐β‐Gal) Activity

SA‐β‐gal staining was performed using the SA‐β‐GAL staining kit (CST, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. ARPE‐19 cells were inoculated in 6‐well plates, and then the cells were stained with SA‐β‐GAL, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Cells were subsequently placed in culture in a dry incubator at 37°C. Samples were analyzed using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Determination of Reactive Oxygen Species

ARPE‐19 cells were cultured in 24‐well plates and received the appropriate treatments at the given times according to the groups indicated. Then, they were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS solution for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were incubated with Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (CA1420, solarbio, China) in serum‐free medium according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cell nuclei were stained by DAPI. Finally, ROS levels in ARPE‐19 cells were measured using a TE‐2000 fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan) with an excitation (Ex) wavelength of 485 nm and an emission (Em) wavelength of 530 nm.

Furthermore, intracellular levels of glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA) were measured using commercial enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for human glutathione peroxidase (GSH‐Px; JL10355; Jonlnbio, China) and MDA (JL53632; Jonlnbio, China), respectively, following the manufacturers' instructions.

Indicators of Glucose Metabolism

To investigate the level of cellular glucose metabolism, a glucose assay kit (GOD‐POD colorimetric assay) (ml095022, mlbio, China) and a lactic acid (LA) content assay kit (bc2235, solarbio, China) were used, respectively. Cells (5 × 105) were added to the kit working solution according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance of the samples was then measured using a spectrophotometer (Multiscan MK3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Glucose uptake and lactate production levels were calculated using the absorbance values by comparing them with the standard curve.

Seahorse Assay

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) were assessed in real time using the XF‐96 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, USA). OCR was measured after injection of a combination of 2 μM oligomycin, 1 μM FCCP, and 0.5 μM rotenone and antimycin A at specific time points. For ECAR measurements, cells were sequentially injected with 10 mM glucose, 1 μM oligomycin, and 100 mM 2‐DG where indicated.

Metabolites Extraction

ARPE‐19 cells transfected with shASNS and shCtrl, or shUSP13 and shCtrl were used to extract metabolites. Metabolite extraction and cell isolation were performed simultaneously by scraping cells with 800 L of water/methanol/chloroform (10:27:3) solution containing 0.375 g/mL reserpine and 0.075 g/mL sulfadimethoxine as internal standard (IS). The cell extract/suspension was transferred to a clean test tube.

LC/MS/MS Technology for Metabolomic Analysis

All samples were analyzed for metabolites by the LC–MS technique. An Agilent 1290 Infinity II system with a 6545A Q‐TOF equipped with dual AJS ESI sources (Agilent Technologies, USA) was used. The flow rate of the mobile phase was 0.5 mL/min, and the injection volume was 10 μL for each sample. Organic phase separation was performed on an Agilent Eclipse XDB‐C8, 4.6 × 150 mm2, 5 μm column using two solvents: 0.1% formic acid (A) and 0.1% formic acid methanol solution (B). A gradient elution program was performed from 10% to 100% B over 20 min and then maintained at 100% B for 5 min. The column temperature was maintained at 40°C throughout the separation. The samples were stored in an autosampler at 4°C during all LC–MS/MS analyses.

The ESI source was operated in positive ion mode with the following conditions: nebulizer air pressure set to 35 psig, nozzle voltage set to 500 V, capillary voltage set to 3500 V, dry gas flow rate and temperature set to 10 L/min and 300°C, sheath gas flow rate and temperature set to 10 L/min and 280°C, respectively, and fragmentation voltage set to 120 V. For MS/MS measurements, a collision energy ramp was used. MS measurements, a collision energy ramp was used to promote fragmentation with a slope of 3 and an offset of 15, and 5 parent ions were selected per cycle using the automatic MS/MS acquisition mode. Data were acquired using MassHunter Workstation LC/MS Data Acquisition B.08.00 software in center of mass and profile mode using high resolution mode (4 GHz). The mass spectral range was set to 100–1000 m/z (MS) and 50–1000 m/z (MS/MS mode). The scan rate was 3 spectra/s in both cases. All data were collected using Agilent MassHunter Workstation Software LC/MS data acquisition software (for 6200 series TOF/6500 series Q‐TOF, version B.08.00, internal version 8.00.8058.3 SP1) (Agilent Technologies, USA) Acquisition.

Data Processing and Analysis

In this study, data processing was performed using Progenesis QI (version 3.0.3) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) and EZinfo (version 3.0) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) software. First, the raw data were preprocessed with Progenesis QI for peak detection and comparison to obtain a list of data information such as retention time, molecular weight and normalized peak area for each sample. Afterwards, the obtained data were imported into EZinfo for multivariate pattern recognition analysis, including unsupervised PCA and supervised PLS‐DA after mean‐centering and Pareto scaling. The importance of the projection variable (VIP) values generated in the PLS‐DA processing indicates the contribution to each metabolite grouping. In this study, VIP ≥ 1 was considered one of the criteria for distinguishing features or metabolites. Statistical significance of the means was tested by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using EZinfo, and differences were considered significant when p < 0.05.

Metabolic Pathway Analysis

To identify the pathways most relevant to ASNS or USP13, the differential metabolites (DMs) in the comparison were submitted to pathway analysis in the web‐based tool MetaboAnalyst 5.0, which combines the results of a powerful pathway enrichment analysis with the results of a pathway topology analysis.

Immunoprecipitation (CO‐IP) Assay

Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease inhibitors (Roche, USA). The lysates were sonicated for 30 s and then placed on ice for 1 h. The supernatant was collected. To obtain antigen–antibody–magnetic bead complexes, the corresponding 10 μL of primary antibody (Table 2) and pretreated Protein A/G magnetic beads (YJ003, Epizyme Biomedical Technology Co. Ltd., China) were added to the remaining supernatant and incubated for 3 h on a shaker. The magnetic beads were precipitated using a magnetic rack. The magnetic beads were boiled with SDS sampling buffer and then analyzed by immunoblotting. The magnetic beads are also used for immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry (IP‐MS).

Mass Spectrometry

Following sample preparation, an EASY‐nLC 1200 liquid phase LC/MS system and a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) were used to gather mass spectrometry data. The program MaxQuant (V1.6.6) was utilized to extract the mass spectrometry data, while the database search technique Andromeda was employed to get the human proteome reference database in UniProt.

Enrichment Analysis

Differentially expressed proteins from IP‐MS analysis were analyzed by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) biological processes using the clusterProfiler R package (Version 6.8). p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Animal Experiment

10 weeks old Male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats 180–200 g were provided by the State Food and Drug Administration (Beijing, China). Animals were housed at 22°C and 12 h/12 h light/dark cycles for 1 week prior to use in experiments.

60 SD rats were randomly divided into 6 groups (MOCK group, shCtrl group, and shASNS group; MOCK group, shCtrl group, and shUSP13 group) with 10 rats in each group. All rats were injected with sodium iodate (30 mg/kg body weight) injection via tail vein on days 0 and 15, respectively. For the shCtrl, shASNS and USP13 groups, lentiviral administration was performed via tail vein injection once a day from day 0 to 7. At day 28, blood was collected from rats via the abdominal aorta, and the eyeballs of the rats were taken for the study. The animal experiments in this study followed the ARRIVE reporting guidelines and were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Baogang Hospital of Inner Mongolia (2023‐MER‐011).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

Retinal tissue was dehydrated with alcohol for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. Whole retinal samples were cut into 4‐μm‐thick sagittal sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Beyotime, China). Each section was then examined for pathologic changes using a light microscope (Olympus BX‐50, Olympus, Japan).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Retinal tissues were fixed in cold 100% methanol only, permeabilized with 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4) and 150 mM NaCl for 10 min, and then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with the indicated primary antibodies (Table 2) diluted in a blocking buffer. Samples were then incubated with secondary antibodies (Table 2) for an hour at room temperature. Nuclear staining was achieved using DAPI (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) for 10 min. Next, cells were observed using fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism software 8.0 (San Diego, USA) was used to analyze all the data, which was then shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). To find statistically significant differences, a one‐way ANOVA with post hoc Bonferroni adjustments for multiple comparisons was employed. The unpaired Student's t‐test was used to compare the two groups. p < 0.05 was then regarded as significant.

Results

ASNS Enhanced H2O2 ‐Induced Retinal Cells Proliferation and Attenuated Senescence

The retinal epithelial cells ARPE‐19 were exposed to H2O2, to mimic the AMD injury environment. To evaluate the role of ASNS in AMD, the shRNA silencing ASNS (shASNS) and the plasmid vector (ASNS‐OE) were transfected into ARPE‐19 cells. ASNS overexpression or knockdown efficiencies were verified by RT‐qPCR and WB (Figure 1A,B). These results confirm the successful establishment of cells with stable ASNS knockdown or overexpression. Subsequently, we found that overexpression of ASNS increased H2O2‐induced cell proliferation, while knockdown of ASNS inhibited cell proliferation (Figure 1C). Moreover, the addition of ASNS decreased SA‐β‐gal levels and ROS production in H2O2‐treated ARPE‐19 cells (Figure 1D,E). However, SA‐β‐gal levels were decreased after ASNS knockdown (Figure 1D,E). Furthermore, we examined the expression of senescence markers (P53, P21, P16). The results showed that ASNS overexpression reduced the expression of these senescence markers in H2O2‐treated ARPE‐19 cells, whereas ASNS knockdown increased their expression (Figure 1F). In addition, ASNS knockdown suppressed GSH levels and elevated MDA content, while ASNS overexpression exhibited the opposite effects (Figure 1G). These findings reveal that enhancing ASNS expression improves cell viability and reduces ROS production to ameliorate senescence in ARPE‐19 cells exposed to H2O2.

ASNS Regulated Glucose Metabolism Pathways in H2O2 ‐Induced Retinal Cell

Previous studies have indicated that ASNS can regulate metabolic pathways such as amino acid synthesis. However, it is unknown whether ASNS regulates metabolic pathways in AMD. Therefore, we explored the metabolic pathways regulated by ASNS through untargeted metabolomics analysis. LC/MS/MS was employed to identify the differential metabolites (DMs) after knockdown of ASNS, and sh‐Ctrl as a control. There were a total of 2270 up‐regulated metabolites and 3355 down‐regulated metabolites (Figure S1A). By comprehensively analyzing the pathways where the DMs were located (including enrichment analysis and topology analysis), we could find the key pathways with the highest correlation with the metabolite differences. Among them, the DMs after knockdown of ASNS were mainly enriched in “Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism”, “Fructose and mannose metabolism”, and “Starch and sucrose metabolism metabolic pathways”, which are all related to sugar metabolism (Figure S1B). The radar chart analysis of DMs also revealed that the glucose category of DMs was reduced by ASNS knockdown (Figure S1C). Moreover, the results of KEGG enrichment also revealed that these DMs were involved in sugar metabolism pathways, such as “Fructose and mannose metabolism”, “Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism”, “Galactose metabolism” (Figure S1D). In addition, the significantly expressed DMs were also shown in the matchstick plot (Figure S1E). In summary, the above results indicated that the metabolites after knockdown of ASNS were mainly involved in sugar metabolism‐related pathways, suggesting that ASNS might regulate sugar metabolism.

Subsequently, we verified whether ASNS was associated with glucose metabolism. The results showed that knockdown of ASNS inhibited glucose uptake and lactate production in H2O2‐treated ARPE‐19 cells, whereas ASNS overexpression had the opposite effect (Figure 2A,B). ECAR and OCR values were used as indicators to assess glycolysis, and ECAR and OCR were significantly down‐regulated by shASNS and up‐regulated by ASNS‐OE (Figure 2C,D). In addition, the expression of key genes for glucose metabolism was analyzed. It was found that knockdown of ASNS inhibited the mRNA and protein levels of Glut1, Glut4, HK2, and PGK1, while overexpression of ASNS upregulated the levels of these genes, compared to controls (Figure 2E,F). Given the close relationship between hypoxic signaling and glucose metabolism, we further investigated the effect of ASNS on the expression of hypoxia‐related factors. The results showed that ASNS knockdown increased HIF‐1α expression in H2O2‐induced ARPE‐19 cells, whereas ASNS overexpression suppressed HIF‐1α expression (Figure 2F). The aforementioned results demonstrate that overexpression in ASNS can re‐induce the process of glucose metabolism in cells.

ASNS Could Interact With the Downstream Protein USP13

To screen for substrate proteins directly bound by ASNS, immunoprecipitation‐mass spectrometry (IP‐MS) was performed in cells by the protein ASNS antibody. A total of 3749 proteins were screened, of which 2186 were up‐regulated by ASNS and 270 were down‐regulated (Figure S2A). These ASNS‐regulated proteins were subsequently enriched. KEGG analysis revealed that these proteins were involved in metabolic pathways, such as “Fatty acid metabolism”, “Metabolic pathways”, “Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism” and so on (Figure S2D). GO analysis showed that these proteins were enriched in biological processes, such as “transferase activity”, “catalytic activity”, “protein modification process”, etc. (Figure S2C). Next, CO‐IP was used to validate 6 of these substrate proteins with high binding to ASNS. ASNS was found to bind to GSK3B, ITCH, and USP13 proteins (Figure 3A–F).

To find the specific structural domains of ASNS binding to protein USP13, truncation of different regions of protein USP13 was constructed to co‐transfect ARPE‐19 cells with ASNS. The CO‐IP results showed that there was binding of ASNS to USP13‐truncation2 (Figure 4A). And IF results demonstrated that ASNS co‐localized with USP13‐truncation2 in the cytoplasm of ARPE‐19 cells (Figure 4B). These results demonstrate that ASNS positively regulates USP13 protein expression by binding to USP13‐truncation2.

ASNS Enhanced H2O2 ‐Induced Retinal Cells Proliferation and Attenuated Senescence by Regulation of USP13

To investigate whether ASNS depended on the expression of the downstream protein USP13 for its effect, we co‐transfected shUSP13 with ASNS‐OE into the cells to perform the investigation. RT‐qPCR and WB results showed that overexpression of ASNS increased USP13 expression and knockdown of USP13 decreased USP13 expression (Figure 5A,B). CCK8 results showed that knockdown of USP13 inhibited cell viability, yet ASNS overexpression rescued these changes (Figure 5C). In addition, knockdown of USP13 increased SA‐β‐gal level and ROS production compared to controls (NC), while simultaneous overexpression of ASNS reversed this change (Figure 5D,E). Furthermore, we found that shUSP13 overexpression reduced the expression of senescence markers P53, P21, P16 in H2O2‐treated ARPE‐19 cells, while simultaneous overexpression of ASNS reversed this change (Figure 5F). In addition, USP13 knockdown suppressed GSH levels and elevated MDA content, while ASNS overexpression rescued these changes (Figure 5G). These results suggest that overexpression of ASNS alleviates H2O2‐induced cellular senescence by positively regulating USP13 expression.

ASNS Regulated Glucose Metabolism Pathways in H2O2 ‐Induced Retinal Cell by Regulation of USP13

We explored the metabolic pathways regulated by USP13 through untargeted metabolomics analysis. LC/MS/MS was employed to identify the DMs after knockdown of USP13, and sh‐Ctrl as a control. As shown in Figure S3A, there were a total of 712 up‐regulated metabolites and 4236 down‐regulated metabolites. Additionally, we could find the key pathways with the highest correlation with the metabolite differences. Among them, the DMs after knockdown of USP13 were mainly enriched in “Glycine, serine and threonine metabolism”, “Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism”, and “Citrate cycle (TCA cycle)” (Figure S3B). These metabolic pathways are dominated by amino acid metabolism, and aerobic metabolic pathways. The radar chart analysis of DMs also revealed that the amino acid category (such as Cytidine, Guanine, Glycine) was reduced by USP13 knockdown (Figure S3C). Moreover, the results of KEGG enrichment also revealed that these DMs were involved in sugar metabolism pathways, such as “Arginine biosynthesis”, “Carbon metabolism”, “Aminoacyl−tRNA biosynthesis” (Figure S3D). In addition, the significantly expressed DMs were also shown in the matchstick plot (Figure S3E). In summary, the above results indicated that the metabolites after knockdown of USP13 were mainly involved in amino acid metabolism‐related pathways.

Subsequently, we demonstrated whether ASNS was dependent on USP13 to regulate glucose metabolic pathways. In H2O2‐treated ARPE‐19 cells, glucose uptake and lactate production were blocked by knockdown of USP13, with ASNS partially counteracting the effects of shUSP13 (Figure 6A,B). ECAR and OCR were significantly down‐regulated by shUSP13, yet ASNS restored the ECAR and OCR values (Figure 6C). Furthermore, the expression of key genes for glucose metabolism was analyzed and found that knockdown of USP13 inhibited the mRNA and protein levels of Glut1, Glut4, HK2, and PGK1, while overexpression of ASNS reversed this change (Figure 6D,E). USP13 knockdown increased HIF‐1α expression in H2O2‐induced ARPE‐19 cells, whereas overexpression of ASNS reversed this change (Figure 6F). The aforementioned results demonstrate that overexpression of ASNS can re‐induce the process of glucose metabolism by positively regulating USP13 expression.

Validation of ASNS or USP13's Impact on the AMD Disease Process In Vivo

The AMD rat model was constructed using tail vein injection of sodium iodate. Lentivirus shASNS was subsequently injected to knock down ASNS. H&E staining of retinal tissues showed that the NC and shCtrl group rats had a disorganized retinal structure with a reduced number of cell layers and disorganized outer and inner nuclear layers, which was exacerbated by the knockdown of ASNS (Figure 7A). Knockdown of ASNS also increased SA‐β‐gal level in retinal tissues (Figure 7B). We also found that ASNS knockdown significantly increased the expression of senescence markers (P53, P21, P16) (Figure S4A). In addition, alterations in glucose metabolism were also verified in vivo. It was found that knockdown of ASNS significantly reduced ECAR and OCR values (Figure 7C). Glucose metabolism related genes, such as Glut1, Glut4, HK2, and PGK1, were reduced by shASNS (Figure 7D,E). Finally, the expression of USP13 was also explored, and WB and IF results showed that knockdown of ASNS significantly reduced the protein expression and fluorescence intensity of USP13 (Figure 7F,G). Moreover, USP13 and ASNS were found to be co‐localized in retinal tissues (Figure 7G). Additionally, ASNS knockdown further increased the expression of the hypoxia‐inducible factor HIF‐1α in retinal tissues. The above results demonstrate that ASNS regulates USP13 expression in animal models and alleviates AMD‐induced retinal damage by regulating glucose metabolic processes.

Subsequently, we investigated the role of USP13 in AMD pathogenesis in vivo by injecting lentiviral shUSP13 to knock down USP13 expression. H&E staining revealed disordered retinal structure, reduced cellular layers, and disrupted organization of the outer and inner nuclear layers in control rats, and these pathological features were exacerbated by USP13 knockdown (Figure 8A). Similar to ASNS knockdown, USP13 knockdown also increased the level of senescence‐associated β‐galactosidase (SA‐β‐gal) in retinal tissues (Figure 8B). Additionally, we found that USP13 knockdown significantly increased the expression of senescence markers (P53, P21, P16) (Figure S4B). Furthermore, USP13 knockdown significantly decreased both the ECAR and OCR (Figure 8C). shUSP13 downregulated the expression of glucose metabolism‐related genes (including Glut1, Glut4, HK2, and PGK1) and reduced USP13 expression (Figure 8D–F). Notably, USP13 knockdown further enhanced the expression of the hypoxia‐inducible factor HIF‐1α in retinal tissues (Figure 8G). In summary, these results indicate that knockdown of USP13 expression also aggravates AMD‐related retinal damage in the animal model by dysregulating glucose metabolism.

Discussion

ASNS catalyzes the synthesis of asparagine and glutamine from aspartate and glutamine in an ATP‐dependent aminotransferase reaction accompanied by glutamine deamidation [16]. Reduced expression of ASNS has been reported to correlate with retinal cell damage [20]. However, the metabolic regulatory role of ASNS in retinal cells still needs to be further elucidated. In the current study, we characterized the metabolic pathways and molecular mechanisms of ASNS enrichment by untargeted metabolomics and IP‐MS. Through experimental validation, it turned out that ASNS restored the glycolytic process and promoted proliferation to alleviate cellular senescence in H2O2‐induced ARPE‐19 by interacting with USP13 protein. Our results demonstrate the potential of the ASNS/USP13 as therapeutic targets for AMD.

Oxidative damage to the retinal epithelium and degeneration of photoreceptors are often thought to characterize the development of AMD [22, 23, 24]. Therefore, we used H2O2 induced ARPE‐19 cells to construct an in vitro model of AMD. Senescence of the retinal epithelium plays a key role in the pathogenesis of AMD [25, 26]. Both cellular senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction are classical hallmarks of the aging process and cause increased levels of intracellular ROS [27, 28]. ASNS is an aminotransferase, and its low expression is closely associated with retinal damage [20]. Furthermore, inhibition of ASNS impaired cell proliferation and induced p53/p21‐dependent senescence and cell cycle arrest [29]. However, the role of ASNS in the equally important pathological processes of senescence, oxidative stress, and proliferation in AMD cells has not been well studied. In this study, it was observed that overexpression of ASNS increased cell viability, reduced ROS formation, and alleviated cellular senescence in H2O2‐triggered ARPE‐19 cells. It suggested that ASNS could alleviate AMD symptoms.

The process of glycolysis involves glucose metabolism and the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP). The glycolytic pathway produces pyruvate and lactate from glucose [30]. In the presence of oxygen, acetyl coenzyme A is produced from pyruvate, which is then oxidized through the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle) in mitochondria [31, 32]. In contrast, photoreceptor growth and survival depend on glucose transport and energy expenditure in retinal epithelial cells. Therefore, the balance of glucose metabolic processes is critical for retinal viability. Glycolytic processes have been observed in fetal cells, rapidly proliferating tumor cells, and retinal cells [33, 34, 35]. Previous studies have shown that photoreceptors in retinal cells could metabolize glucose via aerobic glycolysis to protect them from oxidative damage [36]. ASNS has a role in regulating amino acid metabolism; however, the role of ASNS in the regulation of glucose metabolism is unknown. The current study, based on an untargeted metabolomics study, found that cells with knockdown of ASNS were mainly enriched in the glucose metabolism regulation pathway, suggesting that ASNS was also closely related to glucose metabolism processes. In addition, overexpression of ASNS in H2O2‐triggered ARPE‐19 cells restored the levels of glucose uptake and lactate production, increased the ECAR and OCR values, and enhanced the expression of glycolytic markers. The aforementioned results suggested that ASNS could restore the glycolysis process which was reduced by H2O2. And ASNS promoted glucose transport and energy production to protect retinal cells from oxidative damage. Given that the ocular environment is inherently hypoxic, we also investigated the expression of hypoxia‐related markers. Our results demonstrate that ASNS suppresses the expression of the hypoxia‐inducible factor HIF‐1α. It is noteworthy that the hypoxic pathway is closely linked to the regulation of glucose metabolism. For example, inhibition of HIF‐1α has been reported to restore glycolysis and ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby alleviating liver injury [37]. Based on our findings, we hypothesize that ASNS may restore glucose metabolic pathways by inhibiting HIF‐1α.

To investigate the mechanism of the protective effect of ASNS on retinal epithelial cells, we screened substrate proteins that bind directly to ASNS by IP‐MS analysis and verified them by CO‐IP experiments. We found that ASNS was highly bound to USP13 proteins and co‐localized in the cytoplasm. USP13 is a member of the USP superfamily of deubiquitinating enzymes and has been implicated in a number of diseases. The role of USP13 has been widely reported. USP13 has been implicated in cell cycle regulation, DNA damage repair, cell differentiation, autophagy, infection, inflammation, and cancer [38]. It has been reported that exosomes via USP13 shifted microglia towards M2 polarization, ameliorated mitochondrial damage, and reduced ROS production [39]. USP13 regulated cellular senescence by mediating MDM2 stabilization [40]. However, whether ASNS exerted its protective effects through USP13 is not clear and needs to be further explored. In this study, we found that ASNS alleviated H2O2‐triggered ARPE‐19 cell senescence by up‐regulating the expression of USP13. In addition, metabolomics and experimental validation revealed that USP13 was closely related to glucose metabolism processes. ASNS enhanced glucose uptake, lactate production, and ECAR and OCR values by up‐regulating the expression of USP13. It also promoted the expression of glycolytic markers. This result was similar to previous studies in which USP13 regulated glycolysis. Wang Ce et al. found that USP13 regulated glycolytic reprogramming in osteosarcoma by stabilizing the METTL3/m 6 A/ATG5 axis to promote tumor progression [41]. USP13 inhibitors slow down glioblastoma progression by independently regulating and reducing RAF–ERK‐mediated glycolysis [42]. To summarize, the above results suggested that ASNS was dependent on USP13 expression to restore glycolytic function in H2O2‐triggered ARPE‐19 cells to decelerate the senescence process of retinal epithelial cells. The role of ASNS/USP13 was subsequently also confirmed in the sodium iodate‐induced AMD model, in which ASNS protected the retinal epithelium from oxidative stress‐induced damage.

However, the present study revealed for the first time that ASNS/USP13 played a protective role in AMD through the gluconeogenic pathway. However, there were still limitations: USP13 is a deubiquitinating kinase that regulates the deubiquitination of downstream proteins. However, it remains to be clarified whether USP13 plays a role in AMD by regulating the ubiquitination modification of several downstream proteins.

In conclusion, in the present study, we demonstrated that ASNS restored glycolysis and attenuated retinal cellular senescence to protect retinal cells from oxidative stress‐induced damage by interacting with the USP13 protein. Our findings not only emphasized the understanding of the role of glucose metabolism in AMD but also identified a promising target for future AMD therapy.
