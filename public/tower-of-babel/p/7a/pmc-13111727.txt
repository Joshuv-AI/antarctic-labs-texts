Change in diurnal temperature range on the Tibetan plateau in the last 40 years and its influencing factors
Authors: Mei Chen, Xiaohang Wen, Maoshan Li, Xian Zhu, Juan Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Tibetan Plateau (TP) is a sensitive area for climate change, with its temperature affected by global warming. However, the variation in the diurnal temperature range (DTR) remains unclear. Therefore, using observational data from 115 stations and ERA5 reanalysis data for TP and the surrounding regions from 1980 to 2022, this study explores the change in DTR and its causes. It also examines temperature simulations of TP in 2000–2021. The results show that: (1) From 1980 to 2022, both the annual mean maximum temperature (Tmax) and minimum temperature (Tmin) in TP show an upward trend, increasing by 0.33 °C/10a and 0.47 °C/10a respectively. The annual mean DTR exhibits a downward trend, decreasing by -0.14 °C/10a. Moreover, the rates of change for Tmax and Tmin are highest during winter, at 0.47 °C/10a and 0.59 °C/10a, respectively. The DTR declines most rapidly in summer, at − 0.17 °C/10a. The current temperature trend in TP is expected to persist. (2) During 1980–2022, TCC, SMOIS and Rnl values in TP are higher in the east than in the west, with a significant increase in the western region. The rates of increase surpass 0.005/10a for TCC, 0.005 m3·m−3/10a for SMOIS, and 0.5 W·m-2/10a for Rnl. Rns is higher in the west and lower in the east, with significant increases in the east, reaching up to 2.5 W·m-2/10a. In summer, the rates of change for all four factors in the western part of TP pass the significance test of α = 95%. (3) Simulations based on the QTP-HRAD data are generally lower than observed values. Simulation results indicate that the annual mean Tmax and Tmin in TP are warming at rates over 0.6 °C/10a. The DTR is decreasing at rates below − 0.6 °C/10a in the eastern regions of TP. During autumn, Tmax and Tmin exhibit the most pronounced changes with increases exceeding 1.6 °C/10a, while DTR drops most sharply in summer, with decreases below − 1.6 °C/10a. (4) Analysis of observational and ERA5 reanalysis data from 1980 to 2022 reveals that the three temperature variables in TP are negatively correlated with TCC, SMOIS, and Rnl, and positively correlated with Rns. Validation using QTP-HRAD data from 2000 to 2021 shows that SMOIS negatively correlates with Tmax in the northern region and positively with Tmin in the southern region, and negatively with DTR. Rnl and Rns exhibit negative correlations with all three temperature variables and positive correlations respectively. This study enhances understanding of the impacts of climate change on TP and the surrounding areas.Keywords: Tibetan plateau, Climate change, Diurnal temperature range, Cloud cover, Radiation fluxSubject terms: Climate sciences, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

The Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) states that the global warming trend has intensified, with global surface temperatures in 2011–2020 being 1.1 °C higher than in 1850–1900, with land surface temperatures higher than ocean surface temperatures1. Global warming makes extreme events such as droughts, heat waves, and heavy rainfall more frequent and intense, seriously impacting human society and ecosystems2,3. Currently, most studies have found that both maximum and minimum temperatures (Tmax and Tmin) exhibit an increasing trend, and the rising trend of Tmin is more pronounced in most regions, which attracted widespread attention to this unsymmetrical change in temperature4–6. Diurnal temperature range (DTR) is an important factor characterizing climate change, closely associated with both Tmax and Tmin and providing more information than the mean temperature7,8. DTR reflects the amplitude of the diurnal variation of air temperature, which can directly affect human society and the ecosystem, and also has essential research value for measuring climate change and sustainable development9,10.

Factors such as climatic conditions, topography, and human activities in different regions can affect DTR. Studies show that the steady decline in DTR in the U.S. is due to a rapid increase in Tmin and that clouds are a major influence11,12. Jackson and Forster13 showed that changes in net longwave radiation reflect the combined effects of solar and hydrological variability on DTR, accounting for more than 95% of the seasonal variability in DTR in the tropics. Seasonal and annual trends in DTR decrease in Spain, where DTR correlates positively with sunshine duration and negatively with precipitation and relative humidity, with the most significant reduction in DTR in summer, which may be related to warming and humidification in the southeastern Mediterranean14. Zhou et al.15 and Sun et al.16 found that the downward trend of DTR decreases gradually from dry to wet regions, namely a stronger downward trend of DTR in drier areas. Shen et al.17 revealed that DTR decreases remarkably in China from 1962 to 2011, with the decline from northeast to southwest, and this change is attributed to a decrease in sunshine duration affecting Tmax. The downward trend in DTR in the North China Plain may be explained by the fact that the cooling action of aerosols dominates during the daytime, decreasing the solar radiation reaching the surface, resulting in cooler days and warmer nights, and shrinking DTR18,19. Overall, DTR varies remarkably in different regions and its influencing factors are not the same, which makes the study of DTR particularly important.

The Tibetan Plateau (TP), with its unique topography, is a sensitive area for climate change and has been described as a “driver and amplifier of global climate change” and an “initiator of climate change in China”20. Its thermal and dynamical role significantly impacts regional and global changes in temperature and precipitation21,22. Therefore, the temperature variation on TP exhibits distinct characteristics. The drop in DTR in TP is caused by Tmin warming exceeding Tmax, and the change in DTR in summer is related to total cloud cover (TCC) and atmospheric circulation23. Using quantitative analysis, Yang and Ren24 found that changes in radiative processes are the leading cause of the decrease in DTR in TP, as increased clouds and water vapor lead to greater radiative warming at night than in daytime. It was also found that the DTR variation of TP could be caused by radiation and TCC using the CMIP5 model25. Water vapor and sunshine duration are the major drivers of temperature change in the northern and southern parts of TP respectively, with the former influencing Tmin through longwave radiation, and the latter affecting Tmax and DTR via shortwave radiation26. In Tibet, the 5 °C DTR days increase, and the 20 °C DTR fluctuates wildly 27. In addition, asymmetric warming of TP has been revealed in extreme temperature index studies28,29.

Most studies have analyzed TP temperature using observational data, but this data is relatively sparse compared to other data. Moreover, the stations are mainly located in the eastern part of the TP, resulting in insufficient representation of DTR changes in the western part. In addition, the variation of DTR in the western part of the TP remains unclear. Therefore, using the observation data and ERA5 reanalysis data from 1980 to 2022, combined with the simulation data from 2000 to 2021, this study analyzes the characteristics of DTR over TP and explores the influence of various factors on DTR. It can provide scientific references for the study of climate change related to TP and for improving the local ecological environment.

Data and methods

Data

Observation data

The observed data comprise daily Tmax and Tmin from 115 stations in TP and its surroundings, maintained by the China Meteorological Administration, for 1980–2023, and are used to derive the daily DTR. Monthly averages of Tmax, Tmin, and DTR are computed separately, yielding annual and seasonal averages of Tmax, Tmin, and DTR for each year. The spring season is from March to May, summer from June to August, autumn from September to November, and winter from December to February. Since data are lacking for January and February 2024, to ensure a consistent length of the time series, the annual and spring/summer/autumn analyses are reduced by 1 year, so that data from 1980 to February 2023 are mainly used in this paper. The data obtained are likely to be missing or biased due to station relocations or instrument failures. To ensure accuracy and reliability, it is required to carry out quality control of the observed data: (1) The data of the selected stations must include that from 1980 to February 2023. (2) Check whether Tmin is greater than Tmax for the day, and anomalous data for which the observed values are seriously inconsistent with the actual climatic conditions. (3) Check for missing measurements at each station and interpolate using linear interpolation for missing and anomalous data. The locations and land use types of 115 stations are shown in Fig. 1.

Map of the study area, (a) land use types and stations distribution, (b) elevation and stations distribution.

ERA5 reanalysis data

The ERA5 reanalysis data30 is the fifth generation of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) reanalysis dataset, covering the period from 1940 to the present, with a horizontal resolution of 0.25° × 0.25°, making it suitable for use in climate studies31,32. In this paper, total cloud cover (TCC, unit: dimensionless), soil moisture (SMOIS, unit: m3·m−3), net longwave radiative flux (Rnl, unit: W·m−2), and net shortwave radiative flux (Rns, unit: W·m−2) for the ERA5 monthly data from 1980 to February 2023 in the regional range of 72.3° E to 103.2° E and 25.9° N to 40.3° N are selected and analyzed. In the ERA5 reanalysis data, TCC is the proportion of a grid cell covered by clouds, calculated from the occurrence of clouds in the atmosphere at different model levels, with values ranging from 0 to 133.

QTP-HRAD data

A high resolution temporal and spatial terrestrial atmospheric assimilation dataset of the Tibetan Plateau (QTP-HRAD) from 2000 to 2022 is generated by Wen et al.34 using the Weather Research and Forecasting Model (WRFv4.2.2) and the Three-Dimensional Variational Assimilation (3DVA) module. The spatial resolution of this dataset is 5 × 5 km and the temporal resolution is 1 h. The WRF model assimilates NCEP_ADP data, including radiosonde and aircraft reports from the Global Telecommunications System (GTS) and satellite cloud track wind data. These reports include pressure, geopotential height, air temperature, dew point temperature, wind direction and wind speed. The simulation area extends from 72.3° E to 103.2° E and 25.9° N to 40.3° N, covering the period from 00:00 (UTC) on 1 January 2000 to 23:00 (UTC) on 31 December 2022. The dataset adopts a cyclic assimilation approach, with a 6-h update cycle to ensure that the analyses and short-term forecasts are based on current data. The QTP-HRAD dataset is converted to Beijing time, filtered for 2000–2022, and then selected to obtain the daily Tmax(unit: °C), Tmin(unit: °C), soil moisture(SMOIS, unit: m3·m−3), downward longwave radiative flux(unit: W·m−2), upward longwave radiative flux(unit: W·m−2), downward shortwave radiative flux(unit: W·m−2), and upward shortwave radiative flux(unit: W·m−2), yielding the DTR(unit: °C), Rnl(unit: W·m−2), and Rns(unit: W·m−2). Similarly, to ensure a consistent length of the time series, the data from 2000 to February 2022, Beijing time, is used.

H is the Hurst index. When 0 < H < 0.5, it shows that if the time series is increasing in a range, it is likely to decrease in the next range. When H = 0.5, the time series is close to random wandering, with no directional movement. When 0.5 < H < 1, it means that there is a long term memory of the time series and a strong likelihood that the current trend will continue in the future. Results

Changes in DTR observations on the TP in the past 40 Years

Characteristics of temporal variation in DTR observation data

As shown in Fig. 2, the annual average Tmax and Tmin at 115 stations on TP exhibit an increasing trend during 1980–2022, whereas DTR shows a decreasing trend. The rate of warming for TP is 0.33 °C/10a for Tmax and 0.47 °C/10a for Tmin, both of which pass the significance test (Fig. 2a, b). Mean Tmax and Tmin are 13.65 °C and 0.10 °C, respectively, with lows in 2008 (11.97 °C, − 1.56 °C) and highs in 2013 (15.79 °C, 1.91 °C). As Fig. 2c, DTR increases modestly (− 0.14 °C/10a) with a mean of 13.53 °C, peaking in 2007 (14.32 °C) and troughing in 2006 (12.28 °C). Overall, Tmax’s rate of change exceeded Tmin’s, thereby more strongly affecting DTR from 1980 to 2022.

Trends in annual mean Tmax (a), Tmin (b) and DTR (d) at TP and nearby stations based on observational data in 2000–2022.

During 1980–2022, the annual mean Tmax and Tmin continued to show an upward trend in all seasons, and DTR remained downward (Figure omitted). The maximum warming trend for Tmax and Tmin at TP occurs in winter, reaching 0.47 °C/10a and 0.59 °C/10a respectively, whereas the weakest warming trend for both occurs in summer, with only 0.17 °C/10a for Tmax and 0.34 °C/10a for Tmin. DTR showed a low rate of decrease across all seasons, with the highest rate of − 0.17 °C/10a in autumn and the lowest rate of − 0.10 °C/10a in spring.

Characteristics of spatial variation in DTR observation data

The multiyear annual average spatial distribution and its trend spatial distribution of Tmax, Tmin and DTR of TP from 1980 to 2022 are shown in Fig. 3. The multiyear annual mean Tmax and Tmin mainly show smaller temperatures in the central part of TP, with minimum values of 2.63 °C and − 10.79 °C respectively, and higher temperatures in the southeastern part of TP, with maximum values of 26.92 °C and 15.71 °C respectively (Fig. 3a, b). The multiyear annual mean DTR of the TP generally ranges from 10 to 16 °C, reaching a maximum value of 16.98 °C and a minimum value of 6.72 °C (Fig. 3c). From Fig. 3d and Table 1, 114 stations experience warming in annual mean Tmax, with 89 stations significantly so, and a maximum change rate of 1.07 °C/10a. As shown in Fig. 3e, annual mean Tmin trends are similar to Tmax, with 113 stations warming (87 of which are significant), with a maximum rate of 0.91 °C/10a. This indicates a general increase in Tmax and Tmin across the TP. The annual mean DTR change is minor at 115 stations, with 88 stations within the range − 0.6 °C/10a to 0 °C/10a (Fig. 3f). Table 1 shows that 27 stations had increasing DTR trends (11 of which are significant), while 88 had decreasing trends (45 of which are significant). Based on Table 1, the number of Tmax and Tmin warming stations across all seasons exceeds the number of cooling stations, indicating that the TP predominantly warmed during 1980–2022. The number of stations with warming DTR is much smaller than that with cooling DTR. Therefore, the DTR decreases. However, the number of stations that heated or cooled with respect to Tmax and Tmin is comparable, and the seasonal change in DTR is small.

Persistence analysis of DTR observation data trends

An analysis of current trend persistence is assessed by R/S analysis based on annual and seasonal annual mean Tmax, Tmin, and DTR data for 115 sites from 1980 to 2022 (Fig. 4). Hurst indices for annual mean and seasonal annual mean Tmax, Tmin, and DTR exceed 0.5, indicating that current upward trends in annual mean and seasonal annual mean Tmax and Tmin will persist, whilst the downward trend in DTR will continue. As in Fig. 4a, the n corresponding to the point of first inflection of the annual mean Tmax occurs at about 3a, so the mean cycle length of the annual mean Tmax is 3a. Similarly, the mean cycle lengths of the annual mean Tmax for the four seasons are 3a, 5a, 3a, and 6a respectively. Except for autumn and winter when Tmin corresponds to a mean cycle length of 4a, the mean cycle lengths of annual mean Tmin are 6a, 3a, and 8a for the entire year, summer and autumn respectively. The average cycle lengths of the annual mean and the annual mean DTR in spring and summer are 3a, while in autumn and winter, they are 5a and 4a respectively. The persistence period of existing trends will be roughly consistent with their corresponding average cycle lengths.

The Rns values are high across the TP, with high value areas above 175 , mainly in the southwestern part of TP (Fig. 5d). TCC was a significant upward trend in the southwest part of TP, with a rate of increase of 0.005/10a, while the significant downward trend in the southeastern part of TP, with a rate of decrease of − 0.005/10a (Fig. 5e). The area of significant change in the SMOIS was mainly in the western part of the TP, with a rise rate of also 0.005 (Fig. 5f). The rising and falling regions of Rnl and Rns are opposite (Fig. 5g, h). Analysis of the effect of different factors on DTR

Correlation analysis (Table 2) indicates that the annual mean DTR is significantly correlated with the mean annual TCC at − 0.338. Mean annual DTR is positively correlated with mean annual SMOIS and Rns, and negatively correlated with Rnl, and they all fail the test of significance. Annual mean TCC, SMOIS and Rnl are negatively correlated with Tmax and Tmin, while Rns is positively correlated with Tmax and Tmin. All four factors significantly affect Tmax. In spring, the correlations with DTR fail the significance test for all four factors, although their effects on Tmax and Tmin are strong. By contrast, in summer, TCC, Rnl, and Rns are significantly correlated with DTR, with correlations of − 0.538, − 0.513 and 0.432, respectively. In autumn, since TCC and Rnl are significantly negatively correlated with Tmax but less so with Tmin, they are also significantly negatively correlated with DTR, with correlations of − 0.488 and − 0.318 respectively. Except for SMOIS, the correlations of the other three factors with Tmax, Tmin and DTR are statistically significant in winter. Therefore, these four factors primarily affect the temperature of TP in winter and TCC is the dominant factor with correlation coefficients of − 0.717, − 0.480 and − 0.506 respectively.

As shown in Fig. 7, Tmax has a strong correlation with TCC and SMOIS on a monthly scale of 0.7–1.8, reaching a correlation of 0.9 during the study period. Error analysis of simulated and observed data

Tmax, Tmin, and DTR have higher observed than simulated values, especially DTR, which shows a considerable distance between the simulated and observed values (Fig. 8a, b, c). However, the overall trend is consistent. According to Fig. 8d, the bias of Tmax, Tmin and DTR between the observed and simulated values are − 3.25 °C, − 2.01 °C and − 1.24 °C respectively, while the RMSE values are 3.56 °C, 2.48 °C and 2.07 °C. The correlation coefficients for Tmax and Tmin are as high as 0.99 and 0.98 respectively, whereas the correlation coefficient for DTR is lower at 0.07. All three correlation coefficients pass the significance test of α = 95%. The large simulation error for DTR likely results from the accumulation of errors in simulating Tmax and Tmin, given that DTR is calculated by subtracting Tmin from Tmax.

Numerous studies have previously evaluated the ERA5 and WRF models for the Tibetan Plateau37,38. This study provides an initial comparison of diurnal temperature variations and spatial distributions across the plateau, revealing good agreement (figure omitted). For Tmax, Tmin and DTR, the simulated values are generally lower than the observed values at most stations. According to existing studies, the ERA-40 reanalysis data show temperatures about 7 °C below the observed data39 and the ERA-Interim reanalyzed surface data’s annual mean is about 8.86 °C lower than the observed data40. There is a general underestimation of the temperature on the TP. This may be because the TP has complex terrain and the actual elevation differs significantly from that in the model, which impacts the temperature simulation, including Tmax, Tmin and DTR34,41.

The Tmax and Tmin trends in spring, summer, and autumn mirror their annual mean trends (Figure omitted). Notably, in autumn, Tmax increases across most of the TP, while Tmin in the southern part of the TP reaches more than 1.6 °C/10a. In spring and winter, the DTR mostly declines (− 0.8 °C/10a to 0.0 °C/10a) across the TP. In summer, significant DTR decreases in the southeast (− 1.5 °C/10a) and increases in the center (1.6 °C/10a). Autumn’s DTR changes are similar but less pronounced than those in summer.

Characteristics of changes in different factors

Based on the spatial distribution of multiyear annual mean SMOIS, Rnl and Rns from high resolution numerical simulations for 2000–2021 (Fig. 11a, b and c), it is evident that the SMOIS and Rnl over TP decrease from southeast to northwest. The area with the highest SMOIS remains the Nyingchi region, while the loss of Rnl exceeds − 100 in the western and northern parts of TP. The Rns in the southwestern and southeastern parts of TP exceed 200 . The annual mean SMOIS trend from 2000 to 2021 shows an increase across nearly the entire TP, indicating that the region is becoming wetter (Fig. 11d). Meanwhile, the annual mean change in Rnl from the simulation shows a tendency to increase in the eastern part of the TP and to decline in the western part. Conversely, Rns exhibits an upward trend from the central to the western areas of TP and a downward trend in the rest of TP (Fig. 11e and f). This suggests that both the loss of Rnl and the gain of Rns decrease in the eastern part of TP, whereas in the central regions both intensify.

Correlation analysis of different factors on DTR

Based on the simulated data, the correlations between annual mean Tmax, Tmin, and DTR with various factors on TP are shown in Fig. 12. In the northern part of TP, the annual mean Tmax negatively correlates with SMOIS, indicating that Tmax decreases as SMOIS increases and SMOIS has a mitigating effect on the rise of Tmax (Fig. 12a). Conversely, in the southern part of TP, the annual mean Tmin increases with higher annual mean SMOIS (Fig. 12d). Annual mean SMOIS is also negatively correlated with annual mean DTR, especially in the southern part of TP (Fig. 12g), meaning SMOIS reduces Tmax in the north and increases Tmin in the south, leading a decrease in DTR. Across TP, annual mean Rnl is primarily negatively correlated with annual mean Tmax, Tmin, and DTR, and regions with high correlations pass the significance test at α = 95%. Notably, the negative correlation between annual mean Rnl and annual mean Tmax exceeds – 0.8 in the central part of TP (Fig. 12 b, e and h). This suggests that as Tmax and Tmin increase on TP, most of the land surface releases more energy outward, helping to show or reduce rapid temperature rise. Similarly, the annual mean Rns positively correlates with the annual mean Tmax on TP, indicating that changes in Rnl directly influence changes in Tmax (Fig. 12c). Areas with rising Rns show a strong positive correlation with Tmax, while areas with decreasing Rns show a weak negative correlation (Fig. 12f). The positive correlation between annual mean Rns and DTR is mainly in the southern and eastern parts of TP (Fig. 12i), possibly because in areas with decreasing Rns, the solar energy absorbed by the surface affects Tmin more than Tmax, resulting in a positive correlation with DTR.

Discussions

This study focuses on the characteristics of DTR variations in the TP and explores the influence of various factors on DTR. Results indicate that an increase in TCC causes a decrease in Tmax and an increase in Tmin, thereby reducing DTR. When Rnl increases, both Tmax and Tmin decrease, with a stronger effect on Tmax, resulting in reduced DTR. Whereas enhanced Rns elevates both Tmax and Tmin, with a more pronounced effect on Tmax, ultimately increasing the DTR. Additionally, due to the numerous and complex factors affecting DTR in the TP region, coupled with their interactions, it is difficult to precisely identify and measure the specific effects of each factor on DTR, and further in-depth research is needed.

This study concludes that increased TCC causes Tmax to decrease and Tmin to increase, thereby reducing DTR. This finding aligns to some extent with previous research24,25 examining the relationship between TCC variations and DTR trends. Similar to those studies, increased cloud cover reduces the solar radiation received by the land surface during the day, resulting in lower Tmax. At night, the cloud layer acts as an insulator, reducing heat loss from the ground and raising Tmin, thus diminishing DTR. Regarding the impact of radiative variations on air temperature, results from this research indicate that increased Rnl reduces air temperature while enhanced Rns increases it. These findings align with the fundamental principle that radiative balance regulates air temperature. The outcomes of this study are broadly consistent with those of You et al.25 and Liu et al.26, namely that increased Rnl tends to cool the atmosphere, while enhanced Rns tends to warm it, thereby influencing the DTR.

Conclusions

Using the observation data of 115 stations on TP and ERA5 reanalysis data from 1980 to 2022, combined with the simulation data from 2000 to 2021, the characteristics of the annual mean changes of Tmax, Tmin and DTR on TP and the seasonal annual mean changes are analyzed, together with the causes of the changes of DTR, and the following main conclusions are obtained:

In 1980–2022, the annual mean Tmax and Tmin of TP increase with 0.33 °C/10a and 0.47 °C/10a respectively. The annual mean DTR showed a decreasing trend of − 0.14 °C/10a. The rates of change of Tmax and Tmin are highest during winter, reaching 0.47 °C/10a and 0.59 °C/10a, respectively, whereas the highest rate of change in DTR occurs during summer at − 0.17 °C/10a. The current temperature trend in TP is expected to persist.
