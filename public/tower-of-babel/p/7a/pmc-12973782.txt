Narrative exposure therapy for post-traumatic stress symptoms in women who are incarcerated: an open pilot study
Authors: Kirsten A Dickins, Mary Clare Houlihan, Sara Neely, Dale L Smith, Horace Nowell, Niranjan Karnik, Philip Held
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Women who are incarcerated (WI) face a disproportionately high prevalence of exposure to potentially traumatic events (PTEs) and related posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms. However, there remains a deficit of low-barrier, evidence-based, trauma-focused interventions for this highly vulnerable population.Aim: This study sought to determine the feasibility and preliminary effects of Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET) on PTSD and co-occurring mental health symptoms in WI across multiple correctional settings.Methods: We conducted a pilot study of NET across three women’s correctional facilities. Feasibility was operationalized via protocol completion rates. We used linear effects modelling and Cohen’s d to analyse pre- to post-NET changes and strength in effects in primary (PTSD, PCL-5) and secondary outcomes (depressive symptoms, PHQ-9; anxiety, GAD-7; somatization, SSS-8).Results: Thirty-eight women completed the intervention. Feasibility was demonstrated by an 86% NET intervention and assessment completion rate. Significant symptom reductions were observed in all measured symptom domains one-week post-NET. NET resulted in large effect size in reducing PTSD symptoms (PCL-5 = 21.8 points, d = 1.11) and depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 = 5.4 points, d = .92), and medium effect sizes in reducing anxiety (GAD-7 = 4.0 points, d = .68).Conclusion: NET appears to be a highly feasible and preliminarily beneficial intervention for reducing PTSD and co-occurring mental health symptoms in WI. These findings suggest the utility of NET in correctional contexts. A fully powered, randomized controlled trial is warranted to definitively determine long-term effects of NET in WI.KEYWORDS: Correctional facilities, mental health services, post-traumatic stress disorders, prisons, psychological trauma, women’s health

Background

The population of women who are incarcerated (WI) in the United States (US) is increasing at a significantly greater rate than the male incarcerated population and women in other economically developed countries (Fair & Walmsley, 2025). Of concern is the prevalence of exposure to potentially traumatic events (PTEs) in WI – both prior to and during incarceration (Anderson et al., 2020; Kelman et al., 2024; Piper & Berle, 2019). Compared to men, WI report higher rates of interpersonal trauma and sexual violence/victimization, which generally first occurs at a younger age (Ervin et al., 2020); research indicates that up to 86% of WI have faced sexual violence (Bridges et al., 2020), and 77% have faced intimate partner violence (Swavola et al., 2016), often under coercive control (Jones, 2020). Further, incarceration can inflict serious psychological distress and have a sustained, negative impact on women’s mental health (Dholakia, 2021; Quandt & Jones, 2021). Compounding the effects of previous traumas, WI face high rates of incarceration-based traumas, including traumatic interactions with correctional personnel (Kelman et al., 2024; Piper & Berle, 2019). In fact, WI are three times more likely than men to be sexually victimized by correctional staff (Kajstura & Sawyer, 2024).

While women in general are at consistently elevated risk for PTSD development (Olff et al., 2007), the prevalence of PTSD in WI is particularly marked: approximately five to six times higher than that of women in the general population (Baranyi et al., 2018). Without intervention, PTSD symptoms are not likely to improve, but rather likely to worsen, particularly in high-risk populations (Department of Veterans Affairs (VA)/Department of Defense (DoD), 2023; Smith et al., 2020). Untreated PTSD can inhibit daily functioning, contributing to the circumstances that can lead to a woman’s incarceration (Swavola et al., 2016). Additionally, untreated PTSD symptoms may undermine a woman’s successful re-entry into society, adversely affecting her, her children, families, and communities, ultimately increasing risk for recidivism (Anderson et al., 2020; Brock, 2024; Pettus, 2023; Wang, 2022). Conversely, receiving PTSD treatment while incarcerated can improve adherence to medical treatments, reduce barriers to completing other interventions, and promote successful long-term institutional and community re-entry (Malik et al., 2023).

Trauma-focused treatment for WI can promote both the health of individuals and families, thereby reducing the likelihood of future incarceration (Facer-Irwin et al., 2019). WI have expressed a strong desire for trauma-focused intervention, despite reporting unfair access to such services (Dickins, 2025; Dickins et al., 2025). Importantly, evidence suggests that effective treatment can facilitate healing from PTSD even within correctional settings, which are frequently described as traumagenic (Gajewski-Nemes & Messina, 2021; Zhong et al., 2021). In response to this critical service-need gap, we selected to test Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET), based on NET’s: (1) mechanistic capacity to address multiple and complex traumas; (2) short-term, structured timeline, which is optimal for correctional environments characterized by unpredictable participant transitions (e.g. facility transfer and release dates); and (3) inherent focus on developing a cohesive life narrative, which promotes a sense of agency, empowerment, and self-cohesion within an often disempowering context. Our study highlights an innovative application of NET, specifically through its delivery by nurses. In the US, nurses constitute the largest segment of the healthcare workforce and possess comprehensive training in the biopsychosocial drivers of health (National Council of State Boards of Nursing, 2026), ensuring that the delivery of NET falls within the nursing scope of practice. Beyond their clinical utility, nurses have consistently been ranked as the most trusted professionals in the country for decades (Saad, 2025). This unique position allows them to rapidly establish the relational rapport necessary for trauma-focused work, fostering higher intervention acceptability among underrepresented or ‘difficult-to-engage’ populations. By utilizing nurses, this model builds upon existing evidence of NET’s effectiveness when delivered by non-specialist local teachers with training or laypersons (Catani et al., 2009; Neuner et al., 2008), while enhancing potential for the sustainability of NET where nurses are already embedded within formal medical and correctional systems. Our pilot study therefore aimed to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary benefits of nurse-delivered NET on PTSD and co-occurring mental health symptoms in WI across multiple correctional settings.

Methods

Study design

We conducted this single-arm, open-label pilot study in one Midwestern US state between October 2022 and October 2024.

Ethical considerations

We conducted a preliminary, small-scale, proof-of-concept study (N = 8 WI) to qualitatively assess the feasibility and acceptability of NET within this population (Dickins, 2024). This initial work aimed to establish a preliminary signal of effect, identify essential adaptations necessary to optimize NET implementation and acceptability in WI, and determine the viability for this current, larger pilot study. The present pilot investigation significantly expands upon our initial findings by increasing the sample size and further testing the feasibility and effects of NET in an additional correctional setting (a re-entry programme). This study protocol received approval from the Rush University Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Setting and sample

We recruited participants from three state-run correctional sites: (a) a mixed-security women’s prison (minimum, medium, maximum); (b) a minimum-security women’s prison; and (c) a re-entry/employment training programme for women serving the last six to twelve months of their sentences. Eligibility criteria included age ≥ 18 years, self-identifying as a woman, and minimum of six weeks remaining on their correctional sentence. Participants were required to report direct exposure to at least one Criterion A traumatic event on the Life Events Checklist (LEC-5) and meet or exceed the probable PTSD diagnostic threshold on the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5 score ≥28) (Arora et al., 2024; Marx et al., 2022). All participants had to demonstrate decisional capacity to consent (UBACC score ≥14.5) (Jeste et al., 2007).

Recruitment

Study staff facilitated two on-site informational sessions at each study site (six sessions total). Individuals who were interested in participating signed up on an eligibility screening interest sheet and were screened for eligibility via a secure video platform or telephone call within one month. At eligibility screening, a member of our study team administered a social and demographic instrument that was based on PhenX toolkit items; the LEC-5, to assess exposure to PTEs; and the PCL-5 to evaluate PTSD symptoms over the past one month. In a random sub-sample of participants, we used the TAPS-1 to assess substance use behaviours. A nurse used real-time, read-aloud approach for each item of every instrument, documenting answers in an electronic database (Research Electronic Data Capture [REDCap]); this approach was intended to mitigate literacy barriers to participation and potential self-report bias. If eligible, the individual was then placed on a waitlist for the open pilot trial. When an opening with a NET interventionist became available, participants were categorized by remaining time at their correctional site (shorter duration until release date prioritized) and subsequently selected using a computer-based random number generator.

Prior to initiating eligibility screening procedures, a member of the study team documented verbal consent in REDCap®. We formally assessed decisional capacity to consent, as part of the eligibility screening process (Jeste et al., 2007). A study team member obtained written informed consent from any individual who fully enrolled in the NET intervention. The informed consent process was led by an interventionist/clinician and was continuous in nature; that is, the interventionist routinely reminded individuals of the voluntary nature of their participation, including the right to withdraw from the study without penalty or repercussion, at any point (Dickins, 2025).

Narrative exposure therapy (NET) intervention

NET is a treatment method for trauma-related disorders, particularly for those living with complex and multiple traumas (Robjant & Fazel, 2010; Schauer et al., 2025). The core clinical mechanism of NET involves the construction of a coherent, chronological autobiography that integrates ‘hot’ traumatic memories, or fragmented sensory and emotional experiences, into ‘cold’ declarative memory, which provides context to the trauma. By narrating life events in detail, while maintaining awareness of the present moment, the participant can achieve cognitive restructuring and emotional habituation of their traumatic experiences. Table 1 provides an overview of the NET intervention protocol for this study. The theoretical foundation of NET holds that an individual's autobiographical narrative significantly shapes their experience and overall psychological health. When a person's life story becomes dominated by the mental consequences of past traumatic events, this focus contributes to persistent psychological distress (Schauer et al., 2025). In this study, the NET intervention was delivered following the procedures outlined in the published NET manual (Schauer et al., 2025) (Table 1). To optimize the feasibility and acceptability of NET within the structural constraints correctional settings, we implemented several protocol adaptations to address institutional logistical constraints and high attrition risks. First, we utilized a brief NET protocol (minimum of 4–6 sessions) rather than the standard course of up to 12 sessions (Hijazi et al., 2014; Neuner et al., 2004). This briefer format was intended to acknowledge high churn within carceral settings and the need for rapid clinical impact. Specifically, our decision to implement a condensed 4–6 session NET protocol, rather than the standard 12-session course, was driven by both clinical safety and institutional pragmatism. In correctional settings, anticipated discharge dates and facility transfers are subject to change on short notice. From a clinical perspective, initiating trauma-focused therapy with a high risk for attrition can pose a significant risk; participants lost to follow-up mid-treatment may be left without having reached adequate narrative integration and habituation. By utilizing a brief format, we prioritized the delivery of a full dose of NET within a predictable timeframe, thus minimizing the risk of therapeutic termination common in high-attrition environments. Furthermore, our study faced a significant imbalance between high participant demand, evidenced by an extensive waitlist, and limited interventionist supply due to modest funding. Guided by the principle of fair access (Dickins, 2025), we opted to provide a brief NET to a larger number of WI with demonstrated clinical need, rather than providing a longer protocol to a smaller, exclusive cohort. This approach optimized the feasibility and reach of our pilot study while acknowledging the structural and logistical realities within the US correctional system. Second, individual sessions were capped at 60 min, a reduction from traditional 90-to-120-minute NET sessions, to ensure the intervention did not conflict with correctional schedules, mandatory programming, or work assignments. The 60-minute duration was established a priori to increase feasibility within highly scheduled carceral environments. Despite being briefer than traditional NET sessions, this timeframe was adhered to and proved sufficient. Feedback from interventionists indicated that the sessions were highly focused. While the pace was consistent, they did not report feeling that therapeutic efficacy was compromised. They noted that the 60-minute ‘hard stop’ often helped participants remain contained and focused on the specific life events scheduled for that session. Each session began with a brief check-in, a short somatic grounding activity, and a brief ‘re-orientation’ prior to session end, ensuring that participants were not discharged in a state of high arousal. All participants were connected with case managers within the correctional facility for additional support, if needed, between sessions. Finally, to accommodate the unpredictable nature of earlier-than-anticipated release dates or facility transfers, supported by evidence showing the feasibility and effects of NET with several sessions per week, we offered a flexible, accelerated delivery schedule allowing for up to two sessions per week (Strijk et al., 2020; Strijk et al., 2025). This format permitted participants to complete the full NET protocol (including assessments) in as little as four weeks. We sought to maintain the integrity of NET’s lifeline process within the brief protocol with 60-minute sessions. In the initial NET session 1, the interventionist supported the participants to identify the most important life events using virtual symbols, of varying designs/sizes (images): flowers for positive milestones, stones for traumatic events, sticks for acts of aggression (perpetrated violence), and candles for grief or loss. This visual map is intended to serve as clinical guidance for the therapeutic process; by placing events in chronological order, the participant begins to see their trauma as part of a larger, lived history versus an all-encompassing present. Using the lifeline enabled all subsequent sessions to be contextualized within the participant’s full autobiography. Given the time constraints, prioritization was essential. Following the lifeline created in session 1, interventionists focused on the most impactful (largest) stones (traumas) in chronological order. While the primary focus was on processing stones to reduce PTSD symptoms, flowers were integrated during the narration to provide emotional resource-anchoring and to avoid implicit or explicit perpetuation of a trauma-focused identity. If new events (symbols) emerged upon reflection between sessions, participants were permitted to update their lifeline in subsequent sessions, ensuring the lifeline remained a dynamic tool that accurately reflected lived experience. Collectively, these modifications were designed to balance the clinical integrity of the NET with the structural realities of the correctional contexts, thereby maximizing intervention completion and participant retention. Virtual Delivery/Confidentiality Protocols. All treatment was conducted individually (participant-to-interventionist) via a secure/HIPAA-compliant virtual platform to accommodate the geographical dispersion of the correctional facilities. To ensure clinical privacy and a confidential therapeutic environment, participants attended sessions from a dedicated, private room within the correctional facility; participants received a ‘hall pass’ to attend each session and were escorted to the room by a member of the correctional staff. At the outset of each session, the interventionist performed an environmental safety check by asking the participant to confirm they were alone and felt secure to speak freely; the interventionist also visually assessed the room. Additionally, all interventionists conducted sessions from private, secure professional offices using encrypted internet connections to maintain data security and participant anonymity. Scheduling sessions was determined based on participant and facilitator availability (participants often worked in the prisons or community, in the re-entry programme). Interventionists maintained manageable caseloads, typically seeing 2–4 participants concurrently. We incentivized adherence and successful study engagement with a $10.00 commissary transfer (participants in prison sites) or a $10.00 gift card (participants in re-entry site) for each completed study visit.

NET interventionists

The NET interventionists included six individuals with advanced nursing degrees (five Doctorate-level, one Master’s-level). To ensure clinical objectivity and minimize potential power imbalances, all nurse-interventionists were recruited from an external academic medical centre rather than being employed as internal staff within the correctional facilities. In the US, healthcare services in prisons are often staffed by privatized, external personnel (Shalev, 2009), with some services provided by internal facility personnel. The nurses in this study functioned as an external clinical team with no prior interactions with the participants outside of the study context. This external status was critical for establishing the relational rapport necessary for successfully implementing NET, as it positioned the nurses as neutral healthcare providers unaffiliated with the disciplinary or parole-related functions of the carceral system. Furthermore, all participating nurses has extensive prior experience working in community settings with justice-impacted populations, ensuring a high degree of competency regarding the unique needs and trauma histories of WI. Drawing on prior studies that demonstrated the successful delivery of NET by lay counsellors and non-professionals, we trialled nurse-delivered NET, capitalizing on nurses’ training as biopsychosocial clinicians and their position as highly trusted members of the healthcare community. Given existing literature that supports the administration of NET by non-specialist or paraprofessional personnel, we investigated a nurse-delivered intervention model. Nurses were specifically chosen due to their established position as highly trusted clinicians and their biopsychosocially informed clinical foundation (Ibrahim, 2015; Neuner et al., 2002; Saad, 2025). The Principal Investigator (PI) first completed the NET Fundamentals Workshop, hosted by the NET developments and trainers from the NET Institute (Konstanz, Germany). Subsequently, all NET interventionists on our study team participated in a comprehensive, multi-day training programme led by the PI. This training specifically addressed both the NET intervention theory and application and our study-specific NET protocol. Additionally, NET interventionists received targeted training in essential therapeutic skills (e.g. active listening, positive regard), critical for establishing a strong therapeutic alliance with the client population. To ensure high levels of treatment fidelity and integrity, we established a comprehensive oversight structure. This structure included weekly supervision sessions for case discussion of participants, daily, on-demand clinical oversight/consultation, and structured adherence monitoring via fidelity checks by the study PI. Interventionist competency was maintained through weekly professional development forums that integrated advanced theoretical instruction on trauma and PTSD via reflective practice. On a weekly basis, nurses engaged in active, practice-based discussions to bridge NET theory with real-world application. These forums served as a mechanism for shared learning, enabling interventionists to troubleshoot clinical complexities and ensure the appropriate application of NET principles within carceral contexts. Further, sessions facilitated shared learning through the active discussion of applied NET principles, allowing the team to collaboratively refine their clinical approach and address the unique logistical and psychological complexities of delivering NET within correctional settings.

Data collection

Demographic and social information (PhenX Toolkit), including information on age, race, ethnicity, sex and gender identification, educational attainment, relationship status was collected by interventionists (Hamilton et al., 2011). Interventionists also collected data regarding exposure to potentially traumatic events (LEC-5), and PTSD symptoms experienced in the past month (re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition/mood, and hyperarousal) (PCL-5) during pre-assessment visits. Pre- and post-NET data included measures for depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), and somatic symptoms (SSS-8).

Measures

We used the self-report Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5) to screen for exposure to potentially traumatic events over a participant’s lifetime (Weathers, Blake, et al., 2013). The LEC-5 screens for 16 PTEs experienced by participants. The LEC-5 demonstrates good one-week test-retest reliability (mean kappa = .61, r = .82) (Gray et al., 2004).

Interventionists employed the PCL-5 to evaluate the 20 DSM-5 criteria for PTSD experienced by an individual over the past one month. Respondents self-report by rating the severity of each symptom using a 5-point Likert scale for 20 survey questions (0 = not at all, 1 = a little bit, 2 = moderately, 3 = quite a bit, 4 = extremely). Answers to each question are added to create a total score (range 0-80) and can be broken down into symptom cluster type. Higher scores demonstrate more severe PTSD symptoms. A cutoff score of 28 has been observed to maximize sensitivity in both female and male samples (Arora et al., 2024; Marx et al., 2022). The PCL-5 demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = .89-.94) and has been shown in other studies to have adequate test-retest reliability (.58-.87 at 10–48 days) (Roberts et al., 2021; Weathers, Litz, et al., 2013).

The self-report Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 is a 9-item measure using a 4-point Likert scale to assess depressive symptoms experienced by the respondent during the previous two weeks. Scores range from 0-27, with higher scores reflecting more severe depressive symptoms. The PHQ-9 demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = .80-.81) and in other studies has shown high sensitivity (88%) and specificity (88%) (Kroenke et al., 2001).

We used the 7-item, self-report Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale to assess anxiety. The GAD-7 uses a 4-point Likert scale to assess anxiety symptoms experienced by respondents over the past two weeks; scores range from 0-21, with higher scores reflecting more severe anxiety. Cronbach’s alpha was .86-.89 across pre- and post-programme timepoints, suggesting good internal consistency. Other studies of the GAD-7 have shown high sensitivity (89%) and specificity (82%) (Spitzer et al., 2006).

We used the 8-item self-report Somatic Symptoms Scale-8 (SSS-8) to assess somatization. The SSS-8 uses a 5-point Likert scale to assess burden of common somatic symptoms experienced by the respondent over the past seven days. SSS-8 scores range from 0 to 32, with higher scores reflective of a higher somatic symptom burden. The SSS-8 demonstrated adequate internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = .64-.80).

We assessed substance use using the Tobacco, Alcohol, Prescription medication, and other Substance use (TAPS 1) Tool. The TAPS 1 tool contains four initial screening questions asking about the respondent’s frequency of use of tobacco, alcohol, illicit drugs, and non-medical use of prescription medications over the past 12 months (Gryczynski et al., 2017).

Data preparation and analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to assess and report social, demographic, and select PTEs information for the sample. Feasibility was operationalized and is reported as the NET treatment completion rate. NET completion was defined as attending and fully participating in all study visits (pre-assessment visit, a minimum of four NET sessions [average six], and a post-assessment visit). To assess pre- and post-intervention PTSD and mental health symptom change, we used linear mixed effects regression models with random intercepts to quantitatively analyse and report trends in change. Models adjusted for race, ethnicity, education level, relationship status, and prior incarceration. We used Gibbons et al.’s (1993) variant of Cohen’s d for repeated measures to examine effect sizes for change for outcome variables (PCL-5, PHQ-9; GAD-7; SSS-8). Due to covariate number and power concerns, we performed a sensitivity analysis examining time effects with fewer and no covariates. Since results were nearly identical, we do not report the results. Data missingness was evaluated and we determined it to be unrelated to measured variables, thus the missing at random assumption appears plausible. We based inference on a nominal significance level (p < .05) for all quantitative comparisons. We used IBM SPSS Statistics Version 30.0 for the data cleaning/organization and development of initial descriptive statistics. We used Stata version 17 to conduct all statistical analyses.

Results

A total of 44 WI began NET. Feasibility was demonstrated by an 86% of NET participants completing all required study visits (including pre – and post-assessment visits and a minimum of four NET visits) (n = 38). Attrition was minimal (14%), with the primary reason for non-completion being earlier-than-anticipated facility discharge. The average duration of engagement with the protocol was 6 weeks, ranging 4–8 weeks; this timeframe included a pre-assessment visit, all NET sessions (1-2 sessions per week x 4–6 sessions), and a one-week post-assessment visit. Qualitative feedback regarding NET acceptability in WI is published, describing a high degree of acceptability, specifically citing the power of emotional release and relief, appreciation of the lifeline (narrative process), trust and professional rapport with nurse interventionists, and a sense of gaining perspective and meaning-making of one’s life experiences (Dickins, 2024).

Participants reported extensive histories of exposure to interpersonal PTEs, with many endorsing multiple types of severe violence (Table 3). The most prevalent PTE exposures included physical assault (n = 39, 89%), sexual assault (n = 34, 77%), and assault with a weapon (n = 31, 71%). A high percentage of participants reported exposure to sudden violent death (n = 32, 73%) and sudden accidental death (n = 25, 57%). Notably, a significant portion of the sample had directly witnessed these events, with 53% of those reporting witnessing the sudden violent death and 36% of those reporting witnessing the sudden accidental death. These high rates of interpersonal trauma provided the context for the subsequent PTSD symptom assessments.

In the 38 NET completers, significant symptom reductions were observed across all measured symptom domains except for somatization scores one-week post-NET. The average pre-NET PCL-5 score was 50.1 (SD = 13.6), which decreased to a post-NET score of 28.3 (SD = 18.7), representing a large effect size for PTSD severity change (21.8-point change, p < .001; d = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.65, 1.57). The average pre-NET PHQ-9 score was 13.2 (SD = 6.2) which dropped to a post-NET score of 7.8 (SD = 5.9), also representing a large effect size for depression change (5.4-point change, p < .001, d = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.51, 1.33). The average pre-NET GAD-7 score was 13.1 (SD = 5.6), which decreased to a post-NET score of 9.1 (SD = 6.3), showing medium effects (4.0-point change, p = .005, d = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.34, 1.02). The average pre-NET SSS-8 score was 13.4 (SD = 5.8), which decreased to a post-NET score of 10.5 (SD = 7.4), though this change was not significant (2.9-point change, p = .067, d = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.13, 0.77) (Table 4). The intervention yielded clinical improvements across all measured domains. Most notably, 90% of participants achieved a clinically meaningful reduction in PTSD symptoms by the one-week follow-up. Within this group, 82% demonstrated a meaningful response (≥10-point reduction on the PCL-5), while an additional 8% showed a ≥8-point reduction improvement. Over half of the sample (53%) has a PCL-5 score <28 immediately following NET (one-week follow-up assessment), signalling some resolution of probable PTSD symptoms. Beyond primary PTSD outcomes, NET facilitated a significant downward shift in the severity of co-occurring mental health symptoms. Approximately 42% of participants transitioned from ‘moderate-to-severe’ depressive symptoms to ‘minimal-or-mild’ depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), and 39.5% of participants from ‘moderate-to-severe’ anxiety to ‘minimal-or-mild’ anxiety (GAD-7). Furthermore, one-third of participants (34%) experienced a meaningful reduction in somatic symptom burden, moving from ‘high/very high’ to ‘minimal/medium’ somatization (SSS-8). Race, ethnicity, education level, relationship status, presence of children, and existence of prior incarcerations were not significant predictors of any of the intervention outcomes (Table 5).

Discussion

The findings of this pilot study yield several important points pertaining to the implementation of trauma-focused care in carceral settings. First, the high completion rate (86%) suggests that NET is a feasible modality for WI when delivered via telehealth by trained nurses. Second, while the pilot nature of this study warrants cautious interpretation of efficacy, our results show promising initial indications of benefit in reducing symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety. These preliminary findings are particularly notable given the use of a virtual delivery model and external nurse providers; these factors may enhance feasibility by bypassing the staffing shortages and logistical constraints common to internal correctional healthcare systems, and mental health services, more generally. Given the dearth of research on NET in US correctional contexts, these results support the potential utility of a nurse-led, telehealth-enabled protocol as a scalable, evidence-based treatment option for highly trauma-affected individuals in limited access environments.

Most WI (86%) who began NET in this study completed the full study protocol. This completion rate is markedly higher than other forms of trauma treatment (STAIR, Seeking Safety) in WI (∼49% completion) (Tripodi et al., 2022). Other populations of women, such as veterans and those who use substances, show PTSD and co-occurring mental health symptom treatment completion rates, including assessments, ranging from 47% to 59% (Kehle-Forbes et al., 2016; Killeen et al., 2023; Szafranski et al., 2017). However, other studies of exposure-based PTSD therapies (SHARE), show comparably high completion rates, suggesting that WI may be agreeable to certain types of PTSD interventions, particularly exposure-based therapies (Karlsson et al., 2022). While these comparisons suggest that exposure-based therapies like NET and SHARE may be especially acceptable to WI, these results must be interpreted within the context of the delivery model and correctional environment. The high feasibility observed in this study likely reflects a synergy between the intervention's mechanics and its operationalization. The fully virtual delivery model bypassed traditional on-site logistical barriers (e.g. lack of private space, interventionist travel delays), while the condensed 4–6 session format was explicitly designed to outpace facility churn and the potential for unplanned transfers. While the human-rights philosophy of NET and the trust inherent in the nursing profession (Saad, 2025) likely enhanced engagement, the success of the model may at least be in-part attributable to a delivery framework that accounts for the volatile nature of carceral environments. We are, however, unable at this point to disentangle whether the high completion rates were driven primarily by the therapeutic modality, the interventionists, or the specifics of the NET protocol with WI (e.g. session number/frequency, virtual delivery platform).

The 86% completion rate observed in this study is particularly notable given the myriad barriers that traditionally impede trauma-focused care for WI (Bridges et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2021). Contrary to literature suggesting these factors lead to under-engagement, our results signal that the virtual, nurse-led delivery of NET appears to successfully bypass many of these logistical and psychological hurdles. Central to this success was the establishment of a high-fidelity therapeutic alliance through a virtual platform, demonstrating that WI will consistently engage in ‘deep’ trauma work despite the anticipatory anxiety that can contribute to avoidance of healthcare services (Kohler et al., 2021). This high level of engagement underscores the successful mobilization of the nursing workforce to deliver PTSD treatment, a critical innovation for expanding mental health capacity in under-resourced settings. While NET is often delivered by psychologists, our findings suggest that nurses can deliver these high-fidelity services with significant clinical impact (Aurizki & Wilson, 2022). Nurses are uniquely positioned for this role, given NET’s narrative structure alignment with the nursing profession’s foundational focus on holistic history-taking and the biopsychosocial impact of trauma. Furthermore, the established public perception of nurses as trusted, non-judgmental providers facilitates the rapid development of the therapeutic alliance, which is a core requirement for trauma-focused treatment (Dickins, 2024; Wilkins, 2018). By leveraging this existing workforce, the study addresses the critical treatment gap created by the severe shortage of specialized mental health clinicians in correctional facilities. Complementing this approach to mobilizing the nursing workforce was our virtual delivery model, which mitigated the geographical and security barriers that often limit access to services. The resulting data suggest that telehealth is not only a feasible alternative but a scalable blueprint for bridging the gap between academic expertise and dispersed correctional facilities (Turgoose et al., 2018). Ultimately, the significance of these findings extends beyond individual symptom reduction. Care that does not comprehensively address whole person health contributes to frequent ethical dilemmas and clinician burnout, which can further degrade the quality of carceral care (Simon et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2021). By proving that NET can serve as a vital clinical entry point, our study suggests that providing such interventions can facilitate more comprehensive engagement to systematically offer holistic health services to this historically underserved population.

In addition to demonstrating the feasibility of NET, this study showed that NET had large effects in reducing PTSD symptoms, and medium effects in reducing depressive symptoms and anxiety. To date, there is limited research available on PTSD treatment in WI. Most PTSD treatment research focuses on men who are incarcerated and community samples of women, largely attributable to the ‘male model designs of correctional facilities’ (Zhong et al., 2021). The marked prevalence of PTSD among participants in this study aligns with broader epidemiological trends, indicating that WI face significantly higher rates of interpersonal and sexual violence victimization compared to the general population (Baker et al., 2021; Karlsson et al., 2020). Our study begins to act on the findings of an Illinois Criminal Justice Information Authority (ICJIA) study, which found that 61% of women in Illinois correctional facilities met criteria for PTSD, and 83% were negatively affected by a PTSD symptom within the past month (Adams et al., 2017). These findings underscore the documentation of disproportionate PTE exposure and PTSD impact in WI (Baranyi et al., 2018). A vast majority of participants in our study endorsed exposure to PTEs, notwithstanding consideration of incarceration as a potential trauma. Further, incarceration and trauma may represent a recursive relationship, particularly among vulnerable populations of women (Dickins et al., 2023), whereby a woman may be more likely to become incarcerated due to traumatic circumstances and may face more PTEs while incarcerated. Complex PTSD – resulting from exposure to multiple or sustained PTEs (Maercker et al., 2022), is more common in WI than in the general public (Briere et al., 2016; Malik et al., 2023). In this pilot, we demonstrated the feasibility of treating PTSD, beginning to address the long-standing need for tailored interventions for WI, particularly those with likely complex PTSD (Malik et al., 2023). The successful engagement of WI suggests that the barriers to treating complex trauma in carceral settings are not insurmountable when the delivery model is adapted to the demonstrated needs of the target population and specific constraints of the environment.

WI who suffer from PTSD and concurrent behavioural health comorbidities, such as substance use disorders, are at highest risk for recidivism and demonstrate the greatest clinical need for integrated treatment (National Institute of Justice, 2024). PTSD, if left unaddressed, can exacerbate the risk of recidivism, as untreated mental health issues and inadequate support systems hinder their successful adaptation and increase the likelihood of reoffending, and further, negatively impact children, families and communities (Pettus, 2023). Given the multifinality of trauma, by treating PTSD, we can ameliorate the risk of other co-occurring health issues (Guina et al., 2018). That is, interventions that address the root causes of adversity – such as PTSD – are imperative to meaningfully interrupting cycles of health disparities. NET may be one answer to the call for efforts to address the frequent overlooked health needs, improve the lack of mental health services available to WI, and minimize the pervasive effects of PTSD among women in correctional settings (Adams et al., 2017; McLeod et al., 2025; Tripodi et al., 2022). The utility of NET in such high-stakes environments can be seen by its success with other complex, highly trauma-affected populations. Specifically, Robjant et al. (2019) demonstrated that NET successfully reduced symptoms of PTSD, aggression, and depression among female former child soldiers, a group that, similar to many WI, is characterized by both extreme trauma exposure and the perpetration of violence. By extending this evidence-based approach to WI in the US, the clinical benefits may create a significant ripple effect. Beyond primary symptom reduction, improving trauma recovery shows potential to reduce barriers to completing other services/engaging with interventions and improve adherence to medical treatments, longer-term institutional and community rehabilitation, and outcomes among children/families (Baranyi et al., 2018; Brock, 2024; Wang, 2022). Expanding on integrated models of care that centre PTSD treatment with other critical coordinated may be a scalable approach to offering person-centred care (Facer-Irwin et al., 2019).

Future directions

Building on the feasibility and initial clinical signals observed in this pilot study, we have prioritized three key areas of investigation to transition this work from preliminary to a scalable model. First, our future research must determine the optimal timing and integration of NET within the carceral cycle. Comparative trials are needed to evaluate whether treatment gains are better sustained if delivered immediately prior to community re-entry, particularly when co-located with supplementary services (Bridges et al., 2020). Second, to address the limitations regarding the duration of follow-up, we plan for future large-scale randomized controlled trials to definitively establish the clinical efficacy and durability of effects for this nurse-led NET delivery model. While the current study demonstrated immediate post-intervention improvements, extended follow-up periods (e.g. 6 and 12 months) are necessary to assess the sustainability of clinical improvement over time. These future studies should utilize diverse correctional settings to determine if leveraging nurses for PTSD treatment consistently yields high-fidelity outcomes compared to traditional mental health providers. Furthermore, while our pilot demonstrated that telemedicine delivery is feasible, future work should employ head-to-head comparisons between virtual and in-person NET to determine if telehealth is truly an optimal modality or if its efficacy is influenced by facility-specific factors. These trials should also track social factors, such as post-release employment and recidivism, which this small-scale pilot is underpowered to evaluate (Facer-Irwin et al., 2019). Finally, our future work should explore the intergenerational impact of this nurse-led intervention. Given that WI often cite family reconnection as a primary motivator for healing, evaluating how maternal PTSD recovery through NET influences child and family stability could provide the necessary justification for the scalable implementation of trauma-focused protocols throughout correctional systems (Baranyi et al., 2018; Wang, 2022).

Limitations

The primary limitation of our open pilot study is the absence of a randomized control group. While our large effect sizes are encouraging, lack of a comparison group means we cannot definitively attribute the symptom reduction exclusively to NET; improvements may at least in-part be secondary to non-specific factors (e.g. research participation and/or therapeutic alliance with the NET interventionist). Caution is necessary in interpreting our findings as definitive evidence. A recognized limitation of this pilot study is the one-week follow-up period, which is insufficient to evaluate the long-term maintenance of treatment effects or the sustainability of psychological recovery. Data collected at this proximity to NET likely reflect the acute therapeutic impact of the intervention. the utilization of a one-week follow-up was a deliberate strategic choice for this pilot phase to maximize data retention and establish initial feasibility. In the high-churn environment of a correctional facility, extending the follow-up window significantly increases the risk of participant attrition due to facility transfers or sudden releases. By assessing symptoms immediately post-intervention, we were able to capture a high-fidelity signal of change, verifying that the brief NET protocol was sufficient to elicit a clinical response, before proceeding to larger-scale trials. Our future longitudinal research incorporating three-, six-, and twelve-month follow-up assessments is necessary to determine the durability of clinical benefits. Due to the exploratory nature and limited sample size of our open pilot, we could not evaluate whether treatment effects differed across correctional settings (e.g. mixed/maximum security prison, minimum security prison, re-entry programme). Future research is necessary to investigate potential setting-specific differences. Furthermore, our open pilot did not control for potential confounding external factors that may have influenced outcomes, such as concurrent life events or changes in psychotropic medication (e.g. initiation, titration); future studies will collect and track these changes closely. Another methodological constraint is the reliance on self-report measures (PCL-5, PHQ-9, GAD-7, SSS-8), which carries an inherent risk of response bias. However, to mitigate potential overestimation of PTSD and co-occurring mental health symptoms (e.g. depression, anxiety), a check-and-balance protocol was implemented in which measures were administered (read aloud and recorded) by nurses trained in standardized administration of study measures. All nurses were extensively trained clinicians, proficient in standardized clinical evaluation, therapeutic communication, and research protocol fidelity, including training in PTSD and common co-occurring mental health conditions. Another notable limitation is the ecological validity of the primary outcome measure, the PCL-5, which lacks extensive validation in correctional settings (Fazel et al., 2016). The measure includes items (e.g. ‘Being ‘super alert’ or watchful or on guard,’ ‘Feeling jumpy or easily startled,’ ‘Having difficulty concentrating’) that may reflect a normal, adaptive psychological response to an environment perceived as unsafe and lacking control, rather than a specific mental health symptom. However, we believe that a measurable reduction in symptoms, even those potentially affected by the setting, following an evidence-based intervention is likely indicative of a therapeutic response. Despite the promising preliminary effects on mental health, our study did not assess behavioural outcomes such as aggressiveness or recidivism (Ardino, 2012; Ardino et al., 2013). Given that these factors are highly relevant to WI, their exclusion limits our understanding of NET’s broader systemic impact. Aggressiveness in WI is often a manifestation of untreated PTSD; however, it remains an unmeasured variable in this pilot study. Future research should adopt a more comprehensive evaluation framework, incorporating biological, social, and behavioural outcomes, including aggressiveness and re-entry success, to fully capture the utility of nurse-delivered NET in correctional settings. While baseline medication data were collected prior to enrolment, exhaustive tracking of pharmacological changes was not conducted during the brief course of NET. Although pharmacological management is a standard treatment modality for PTSD in correctional settings (Friedman et al., 2019), a significant portion of our sample expressed a preference for psychotherapeutic over pharmacological interventions. This sentiment was demonstrated in our preliminary feasibility/acceptability pilot work, where WI reported that their prior trauma care consisted primarily of medication management rather than the interpersonal ‘deep work’ they desired (Dickins et al., 2025). As one participant noted, rather than ‘’taking some pills that make you … feel like a zombie,’’ there was a distinct preference for addressing the root of the trauma: ‘'you have to open the wounds back up and get over it'’ (Dickins et al., 2025, p. 315). Consequently, the high engagement with NET in this study likely reflects the participants’ articulated need for relational, narrative-based healing over traditional medication-heavy approaches. To more precisely delineate the clinical impact of NET, future research should incorporate rigorous protocols for tracking the initiation, cessation, and dosage alterations of both prescribed and non-prescribed medications throughout the intervention period. Longitudinal monitoring of these pharmacological variables would allow for a more nuanced understanding of the interaction between NET and pharmacological symptom management in WI. Finally, the durability of the observed treatment effects over time is unknown, as we did not collect long-term follow-up data post-intervention or following discharge from incarceration. These limitations underscore the need for a larger-scale, definitive randomized controlled trial to establish the long-term effectiveness of NET in highly trauma-affected populations, such as WI.

Conclusion

The findings from this open pilot study demonstrate that brief PTSD interventions are capable of measurably improving mental health outcomes in WI. Specifically, NET successfully reduced both PTSD and co-occurring mental health symptoms, including depression and anxiety in this population. Furthermore, this study demonstrated strong indicators of feasibility, with the vast majority of WI who initiated NET successfully completing the full study protocol. Specifically, our findings highlight the innovation of a nurse-led, telehealth-delivered model of NET, proving it to be a viable solution for high rates of PTE exposure and PTSD symptoms within the carceral system. This approach redefines the role of the nurse as a primary agent of trauma recovery, demonstrating that specialized psychological healing via successful nurse-led and virtual delivery methods. Next research steps include a fully powered, randomized controlled trial to definitively determine the longitudinal effects of nurse-delivered NET across varied health and social outcomes, considering virtual formats. The continued testing, tailoring, and scaling of NET with and for WI constitutes a critical step towards developing integrated, low-barrier interventions to improve the health of heavily trauma-affected populations. This work is essential to address the long-unmet, trauma-related needs of the growing population of WI, and moreover, their families and communities.
