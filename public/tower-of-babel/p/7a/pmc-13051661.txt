Intravenous cangrelor as rescue therapy for acute thrombosis in neuroendovascular procedures: illustrative cases
Authors: Mehlika Esra Sahin, Amy T. Fulton, Sunishka Deshpande, Kenan Alkhalili, Erik F. Hauck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDClot formation can lead to serious complications associated with endovascular procedures. Cangrelor, a novel intravenous (IV) antiplatelet medication, has been FDA approved for percutaneous coronary intervention. However, the use of cangrelor for the purpose of rescue therapy in neurovascular procedures has not been established.OBSERVATIONSAt the authors’ institution, 4 patients received IV cangrelor for emergency thrombolysis during cerebral aneurysm embolization. In 3 cases, thrombus resolution was observed within minutes of cangrelor administration, restoring normal flow. In 1 case, cangrelor facilitated partial thrombolysis in conjuction with mechanical thrombectomy. No patient experienced an ischemic stroke or hemorrhagic complication. All patients were discharged home at their neurological baseline. Follow-up demonstrated no recurrent thrombosis or aneurysm recurrence.LESSONSIn this limited series, cangrelor was found to be a safe and effective IV platelet inhibitor with emergency thrombolysis capability after cerebral aneurysm embolization. Acute periprocedural thromboembolic events represent a significant complication during neuroendovascular procedures. Cangrelor, a short-acting intravenous (IV) P2Y12 inhibitor with rapid onset and reversibility, offers potential advantages in managing such events by providing immediate platelet inhibition without prolonged bleeding risk. This series describes the cases of 4 patients who developed acute thrombus formation during or shortly after neurointerventional procedures. All patients were successfully treated with IV cangrelor, underscoring its role as a potential rescue agent.

Illustrative Cases

Four patients were treated at our institution between 2022 and 2025 in whom IV cangrelor was administered as rescue therapy for acute thrombotic events during aneurysm embolization. Demographics, aneurysm characteristics, procedure type, antiplatelet regimen, cangrelor dosing, angiographic response, and clinical outcomes were recorded. All patients had been pretreated with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). Cangrelor dosing ranged from 15 to 30 μg/kg bolus, with or without maintenance infusion (1.5–2 μg/kg/min). The recommendation of our pharmacy team was that the cangrelor effect would be equivalent if given intravenously or intra-arterially. Accordingly, we chose IV application for all patients. Thrombi resolved/improved within minutes in all cases.

Case 1

A 52-year-old female presented with a known 5-mm right superior hypophyseal artery aneurysm. Her medical history included hypertension and left middle cerebral artery (MCA) stroke in 2022 with residual right facial weakness and mild expressive aphasia. In preparation for elective treatment with flow diversion, she started aspirin (81 mg) and clopidogrel (75 mg) 7 days prior. On the day of the procedure, her P2Y12 level was 178, just 2 points below our institutional cutoff of 180.

Intervention

The patient was placed under general anesthesia. The groin was accessed via a 6-Fr sheath and exchanged for an 80-cm BMX guide catheter (Penumbra). The patient was given a full dose of heparin (4500 U [50 U/kg]). The guide was further advanced into the right common carotid artery and then the internal carotid artery (ICA). A Marksman catheter (Medtronic), supported by a Distal Delivery Catheter (Penumbra), was passed beyond the aneurysm into the MCA. A 5 × 15–mm Surpass Evolve device (Stryker) was deployed across the aneurysm neck. There was some concern that the distal tip of the device did not completely cover the neck of the aneurysm. Therefore, a second Surpass Evolve 5 × 20–mm embolization device was deployed across the aneurysm neck. The second flow diverter landed distally just short of the ICA terminus. Aneurysm coverage was excellent.

During further follow-up angiography, slow flow was noted in the fetal posterior communicating artery (Pcom) adjacent to the aneurysm. Subsequently, more clot formed within the stent (Fig. 1). A cangrelor bolus was given at the half cardiac dose (15 μg/kg), with improvement. An additional 5 μg/kg was given to three-fourths of the cardiac dose. This was followed by a maintenance infusion at a half cardiac dose of 2 μg/kg/min. Simultaneously, the patient was loaded with ticagrelor via a nasogastric tube. Final follow-up catheter angiography demonstrated restoration of normal blood flow intracranially with resolution of clots (Fig. 1). The patient was then awakened and extubated in the catheter laboratory. She was neurologically intact. She was transferred to the ICU for further monitoring and care. The maintenance cangrelor infusion was discontinued after 90 minutes when a trace of blood was noticed in her urine. Outcome

The patient was discharged home from the ICU on postoperative day 1 at her neurological baseline. No postoperative complications were noted. Her discharge medications were aspirin 81 mg daily and ticagrelor 90 mg every 12 hours.

Case 2

A 53-year-old male presented with an incidental left 11-mm ICA terminus aneurysm. The aneurysm was embolized with coils electively, resulting in near-complete occlusion of the aneurysm with a minimal residual at the neck. Several hours postoperatively, the patient was more confused with subtle right facial weakness and a right pronator drift. His sensation was reduced on the right side. A new CT/CT angiography (CTA) study demonstrated a filling defect at the ICA terminus at the base of the coil mass. The patient was urgently returned to the cathether laboratory for emergency thrombectomy.

Intervention

Under general anesthesia, the right groin was accessed. No additional heparin or DAP was administered. A Neuron Max guide catheter (Penumbra) was advanced into the left ICA. Angiography revealed a left A1 occlusion as well as complete obliteration of the aneurysm. Four milligrams of intra-arterial (IA) tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) was administered with no effect. Instead, additional clot formation was noted in the M1 at this time (Fig. 1). IV cangrelor was administered at 30 μg/kg. This resolved the M1 clot but failed to recanalize the A1 (larger clot burden). A Red 72 aspiration catheter (Penumbra) was advanced over a Marksman microcatheter and Synchro2 wire (Stryker) to the face of the clot close to the aneurysm neck. A stent retriever was used to trap the clot distally. Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade 2c recanalization was obtained with two passes. Several follow-up angiograms confirmed patency of the large vessels. The aneurysm remained occluded. The patient was then maintained on a cangrelor drip of 1.5 μg/kg/min for 2 hours.

Outcome

Postoperatively, the patient made a full recovery. The next morning, he was back to his baseline, neurologically intact and feeling well. He was ambulating and tolerating an oral diet. He was cleared for discharge on postoperative day 1 on ticagrelor. After 3 years, he remains without recurrence of his aneurysm.

Case 3

A 30-year-old female presented to the emergency department with headaches. CT confirmed a Hunt & Hess grade I subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) predominantly within the left sylvian fissure. There was no SAH in the interhemispheric fissure. The patient had a history of a previously ruptured 3-mm anterior communicating artery (Acom) aneurysm, which had been treated with coil embolization the year prior. Preoperative CTA revealed a newly developed 3-mm Pcom aneurysm, believed to represent the site of rupture. Because of the emergency situation, the patient had not been pretreated with DAPT.

Intervention

The patient was placed under general anesthesia. Access was obtained from the right common femoral artery. Prior to stent deployment, a loading dose of ticagrelor 180 mg and aspirin 325 mg, as well as heparin 4500 U (50 U/kg), was administered. The right ICA was catheterized with a 6-Fr Envoy catheter (Cordis), and angiography revealed a minimal residual Acom aneurysm (Fig. 1A). To exclude any possibility of rerupture, we decided to retreat the Acom aneurysm with a stent/coil as well. We advanced an SL-10 microcatheter (Stryker) supported by a Distal Access Catheter (Stryker) from the right A1 into the contralateral left A2. A 3 × 21–mm Neuroform Atlas stent (Stryker) was then deployed across the aneurysm neck via the Acom. Next, two additional 2 mm coils were deployed into the aneurysm. A follow-up angiogram demonstrated clot formation across the stent with occlusion of the contralateral A2 and possible clotting in the ipsilateral A2 (Fig. 2). Two thousand units of heparin was given intravenously without effect. For definitive thrombolysis, a cangrelor bolus was administered next intravenously (15 μg/kg), which resulted in prompt and lasting resolution of the clot with full recanalization of the Acom complex (Fig. 1C). Thereafter, the left Pcom aneurysm was successfully embolized with a stent/coil. Outcome

The patient tolerated the procedure well. She was extubated following her procedure, remained neurologically intact, and was discharged early on postadmission day 5 on DAPT. At the 1-year follow-up, she remained neurologically intact without recurrence of her aneurysms.

Case 4

A 67-year-old female presented with an unruptured 5-mm left MCA bifurcation aneurysm. Her medical history included connective tissue disease, depression, migraine, prior tobacco use, Crohn’s disease, and a recent minor stroke unrelated to the aneurysm. In preparation for elective treatment and possible stent insertion, she was started on standard DAPT of clopidogrel 75 mg and aspirin 81 mg 7 days prior. Her P2Y12 level was 260 on the day of the procedure. However, as stent insertion was not intended, and because of increased bleeding risk secondary to the patient’s Crohn’s disease, we decided to not escalate DAPT to ticagrelor preoperatively.

Intervention

The patient was placed under general anesthesia. The right common femoral artery was accessed with a BMX guide catheter, which was advanced into the left ICA. The patient was given a full dose of heparin (4500 U [50 U/kg]). A Via 17 catheter (MicroVention) was then advanced into the left MCA, supported by a 5-Fr Sofia catheter (MicroVention). A 6 × 3–mm WEB SL device (MicroVention) was then successfully deployed into the aneurysm. Follow-up angiography demonstrated some degree of protrusion of the WEB device into the inferior MCA division. A 3 × 15–mm Neuroform Atlas stent was deployed across the aneurysm neck into the inferior M2 division to prevent any obstruction of flow. Additional angiography was concerning for thrombus formation along the inferior M2 division (Fig. 2). A 15-μg/kg cangrelor bolus was administered intravenously, which resulted in resolution of the filling defect (thrombus).

Outcome

Following the procedure, the patient was admitted to the neuro-ICU in stable condition without any neurological complication. She was discharged home per routine on postoperative day 1. We switched her DAPT medication from clopidogrel to ticagrelor.

Discussion

Observations

All 4 patients experienced acute intra-/periprocedural thrombus formation. Cangrelor was administered intravenously in 3 cases and intra-arterially in 1 with adjunct maintenance infusion in 2. Thrombus resolution was achieved in all cases, rapidly in 3 and after additional thrombectomy in 1. No hemorrhagic complications occurred, including in the SAH patient. All patients were discharged at neurological baseline. No thromboembolic recurrence or aneurysm regrowth was observed on follow-up (1–3 years).

Cangrelor is a fast-acting, reversible, IV P2Y12 receptor antagonist that inhibits adenosine diphosphate–mediated platelet activation and aggregation. Unlike oral P2Y12 inhibitors such as clopidogrel or ticagrelor, which require hepatic metabolism and may take hours to achieve therapeutic levels, cangrelor exerts antiplatelet effects within 2 minutes of administration. Platelet function is restored within approximately 1 hour of discontinuation due to its short plasma half-life of 3–6 minutes.1–3

This unique pharmacokinetic profile enables rapid titration and reversibility of platelet inhibition, which is particularly advantageous in neurointerventional radiology, where acute thrombotic complications can occur in the setting of flow diversion or stent-assisted coiling. In such scenarios, the ability to quickly establish platelet inhibition without long-lasting effects is essential, especially when emergency neurosurgical intervention may still be a consideration.4–6

Despite standardized preprocedural DAPT, thromboembolic events may still occur due to several well-recognized factors. One major contributor is interpatient variability in response to clopidogrel, largely influenced by genetic polymorphisms in the CYP2C19 enzyme responsible for its hepatic activation. Patients with reduced-function alleles may be hyporesponsive, resulting in persistently high P2Y12 reaction unit values and inadequate platelet inhibition at the time of treatment. Additionally, delayed gastric absorption, drug-drug interactions, and nonadherence can further reduce the efficacy of oral agents.7–9

In emergency or semielective cases, there may be insufficient time for oral P2Y12 inhibitors to reach therapeutic levels, particularly in patients preloaded only hours before the procedure. These limitations underscore the need for fast-acting, titratable IV alternatives like cangrelor, especially in procedures involving high thrombogenic risk devices. Several IV agents have been used as rescue therapies for intraprocedural thrombosis during neurointerventions, including glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors such as abciximab, eptifibatide, and tirofiban.10 While these agents effectively inhibit the final common pathway of platelet aggregation, they are associated with longer half-lives, irreversible or slowly reversible platelet effects, and a higher risk of hemorrhagic complications.10 These characteristics make them less ideal in cases in which quick reversal of platelet inhibition may be needed. In contrast, cangrelor’s selective P2Y12 receptor blockade, rapid onset, and short half-life allow for precise titration of platelet activity with swift reversibility. Compared to systemic thrombolytics like IA tPA, which carry a higher risk of hemorrhage and are less effective for platelet-rich thrombi, cangrelor offers a more targeted and controlled approach to resolving intraprocedural thrombus, especially in the context of SAH or recent ischemia. Studies have suggested that cangrelor is at least as effective and safe as glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors and oral antiplatelets, with some evidence of reduced hemorrhagic risk and improved reperfusion rates in acute stent placement and thrombectomy settings. However, direct head-to-head comparisons with IA thrombolytics in neurointervention are limited, and further prospective studies are needed to establish optimal dosing and confirm superiority in specific clinical scenarios.5,6,11

The cases presented in this series exemplify the advantages of cangrelor in a range of complex neuroendovascular scenarios. In each instance—whether during elective flow diversion, stent-assisted coiling, or emergency reintervention—cangrelor was administered in response to acute thrombus formation despite standard DAPT or failed thrombolysis. Notably, in 3 of 4 cases, thrombus resolution occurred within minutes of administration, highlighting its reliable and rapid efficacy. In case 2, cangrelor succeeded where IA tPA failed, underscoring its value as a safer and more targeted alternative to thrombolytics. Across all 4 cases, the use of cangrelor was associated with favorable neurological outcomes and no major hemorrhagic complications, even in the setting of recent SAH (case 3). These clinical experiences support cangrelor’s unique role as a rescue agent that bridges the gap between slow-acting oral P2Y12 inhibitors and less controllable IV antiplatelet or thrombolytic therapies, making it an increasingly valuable tool in the neurointerventional armamentarium.

An important consideration when using cangrelor in neurointerventional procedures is the strategy for bridging to long-term oral P2Y12 inhibition, particularly in patients who are clopidogrel nonresponders or in whom oral administration is delayed (e.g., due to intubation or impaired consciousness). Because cangrelor binds reversibly and competitively to the P2Y12 receptor, concurrent administration of oral agents like clopidogrel may be ineffective if given during the cangrelor infusion, as receptor occupancy blocks their binding.12,13 Therefore, agents such as ticagrelor, which bind to a different allosteric site on the P2Y12 receptor, are preferred when oral loading is required during or shortly after cangrelor infusion.12 In our cases, patients were transitioned to ticagrelor via a nasogastric tube or orally after the cangrelor bolus or infusion had been completed. This approach minimizes gaps in platelet inhibition during the perioperative period and allows for seamless transition to maintenance DAPT without compromising thromboembolic protection. Current best practice suggests administering ticagrelor at least 30–60 minutes before discontinuing cangrelor, ensuring overlapping activity while avoiding competitive inhibition.3 When implemented carefully, this bridging strategy supports safe and effective procedural recovery with sustained antiplatelet coverage.

Lessons

Cangrelor’s rapid onset, reversible P2Y12 inhibition, and short half-life make it an effective and safe rescue therapy for acute intraprocedural thrombosis during neurointerventions, particularly in clopidogrel nonresponders or when immediate platelet inhibition is required. Its favorable safety profile and efficacy, even after failed thrombolysis, support its inclusion into the neurointerventional armamentarium, with careful attention to transition strategies for long-term oral antiplatelet therapy. Further prospective studies are warranted to define optimal dosing, compare efficacy with existing rescue agents, and better characterize safety in both elective and ruptured aneurysm settings.
