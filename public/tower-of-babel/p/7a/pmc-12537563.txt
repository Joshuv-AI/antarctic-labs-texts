Establishing a multidisciplinary bariatric arterial embolization program: A two-year clinical experience
Authors: Adham Khalil, Alan J. Kim, Lawrence J. Cheskin, Selvi Rajagopal, Bill Morefield, Zoobia Chaudhry, Kimberly A. Gudzune, Clifford R. Weiss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBariatric arterial embolization (BAE) is an emerging, minimally invasive, image-guided procedure for treating obesity in patients for whom nonoperative treatments have failed and metabolic-bariatric surgery is not an option. BAE has shown success in clinical studies, but guidance on integrating BAE into weight management programs (WMPs) is lacking. We describe our experience with building a multidisciplinary clinical program that integrates BAE into routine care for obesity.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-center observational case series of patients treated with BAE through our WMP between November 2021 and September 2023. Patient chart review was performed for the following criteria for consideration of BAE: had a body mass index ≥30 kg/m2, was unable to achieve weight and health goals through nonoperative management, and was ineligible or unwilling to undergo metabolic-bariatric surgery. Through multidisciplinary discussion, BAE was deemed suitable based on various patient-specific medical and technical reasons.ResultsThirty-one patients were considered for BAE through the program. Of those, 9 patients were cleared for BAE and 5 patients (4 women and 1 man) eventually underwent BAE. The mean age of the embolized patients was 42 years (range: 28–56). The mean pre-procedure weight was 116 kg (range: 100–134). Patients achieved a mean total body weight loss of 7.0% at 4–5 months’ follow-up and 4.2% at 12 months. BAE was performed successfully with only transient nausea and vomiting postoperatively. The mean cost for BAE was $14,329 US dollars.ConclusionsBAE offers an obesity treatment option with low complication risk and potential benefit when used as part of a comprehensive WMP. Close collaboration between interventional radiologists and obesity medicine physicians is key for success. BAE should be offered as a component within a comprehensive WMP rather than as a stand-alone procedure.

Introduction

Obesity, defined as a body mass index (BMI) of ≥30 kg/m2, has become a worldwide public health issue. During the past 50 years, the prevalence of obesity has increased dramatically, affecting approximately 42% of adults in the United States [1]. Obesity is a chronic disease that contributes to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare system costs [2]. It increases the risk of all-cause mortality, in part by leading to diseases such as type-2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, sleep apnea, and certain cancers [2,3].

Therapeutic approaches to obesity include lifestyle modifications (behavior, nutrition, physical activity), pharmacotherapy, and metabolic-bariatric surgery. Lifestyle measures typically result in modest weight loss and high rates of weight recurrence, especially over the long term [[4], [5], [6]]. Obesity medications (OMs) produce varying degrees of weight loss; however, long-term OM use promotes weight-loss maintenance. A new generation of OMs, specifically semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, and tirzepatide, a dual-receptor agonist for GLP-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), have produced substantial weight reduction in recent trials [[7], [8], [9]]. However, the high cost of these medications may limit their accessibility to patients.

Metabolic-bariatric surgery, including Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy, produces substantial and sustainable weight loss [[10], [11], [12]]. These procedures mechanically restrict the stomach to limit food intake and induce secondary neural and physiologic changes that decrease secretion of the hunger-stimulating hormone ghrelin, and increase secretion of several satiety-stimulating hormones, including GLP-1, peptide YY, oxyntomodulin, and leptin [13]. Despite advances in laparoscopic technology, metabolic-bariatric surgery is highly invasive and costly and can cause major complications [14]. Therefore, many patients prefer to rely on lifestyle measures and pharmacotherapy [15]. Minimally invasive endobariatric procedures (e.g., intragastric balloon, endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty) offer another treatment option, although long-term efficacy remains unclear. These treatments are used infrequently because of their high cost, lack of insurance coverage, and limited eligibility for certain patient populations [16,17].

Bariatric arterial embolization (BAE) is an emerging, minimally invasive, image-guided treatment for effective weight loss. During BAE, the arteries supplying the stomach fundus are embolized via small beads delivered under fluoroscopic guidance [18]. BAE is hypothesized to promote weight loss by reducing ghrelin production and increasing satiety hormones such as polypeptide YY [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. BAE is safe and effective, and no major complications have been reported [[23], [24], [25]]. However, since its development, BAE has been reported only in the context of clinical studies.

The next step is to translate and disseminate this treatment option into clinical practice. Key to meeting this goal is establishing multidisciplinary weight management programs (WMPs) that incorporate BAE as a component of routine treatment for patients with obesity. At minimum, this endeavor requires collaboration between interventional radiology (IR) and obesity medicine (OM) physicians. Here, we summarize our experience integrating BAE as a clinical component in such a program. We reflect on pragmatic solutions to challenges encountered that may help others to build effective BAE programs.

Methods

This study was approved by our institutional review board at Johns Hopkins University under protocol number 00294913. Informed consent was waived for this analysis, which was based on a retrospective single-center observational review of multiple cases classified as research intended to contribute to generalizable knowledge, and therefore did not meet the criteria for a case report. Chart review of patients in our WMP who were considered for BAE between November 2021 and September 2023 was performed.

Structure of the weight management program

Recognizing the need for a multidisciplinary approach, physicians from IR and OM worked together to create a strategy to offer BAE to patients with obesity. Given that the academic institution has an established WMP, BAE was integrated as a treatment option within that program. The WMP refers patients to the IR clinic for evaluation of the benefits and risks of BAE for each candidate, and interested patients who approach IR independently are referred to the WMP to establish care first before being considered for BAE. The WMP consists primarily of OM physician diplomates of the American Board of Obesity Medicine, along with metabolic-bariatric surgeons, bariatric endoscopists, hepatologists, and an endocrinologist; it is also affiliated with registered dietitians and behavioral psychologists. The program provides a patient-centered experience through multidisciplinary management of obesity and associated diseases to achieve long-term weight loss. Patient insurance is billed for all clinical services provided. Before integrating BAE into the program, OM physicians participated in an educational session led by an IR physician on the procedure's efficacy and safety outcomes.

Patient referral and evaluation process for BAE

In the WMP, patients receive a comprehensive OM evaluation during their initial visit. Their life-long weight history, including prior weight-loss attempts; lifestyle history, including nutrition, eating habits, physical activity, stress, and sleep; and history of obesity-related diseases and complications are obtained. Patients undergo a comprehensive physical examination, along with laboratory and diagnostic testing, as appropriate. Through a shared decision-making approach, each patient receives an individualized weight management plan and schedules regular follow-up visits with an OM physician.

Patients are considered for BAE if they meet the following 3 criteria: (1) BMI ≥30 kg/m2; (2) inability to achieve or maintain weight and health goals through medical management (i.e., lifestyle changes and/or pharmacotherapy); and (3) ineligibility or unwillingness to undergo metabolic-bariatric surgery. Patients with morbidity risk and/or limited weight-loss options who might benefit from BAE include (1) patients recommended (or required) to lose weight before undergoing other needed procedures (e.g., hernia repair, organ transplantation, orthopedic surgery); (2) patients who need to improve obesity-related diseases and complications (e.g., polycystic ovary syndrome, infertility, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, idiopathic intracranial hypertension); and (3) patients for whom OMs or metabolic-bariatric surgery are unavailable (e.g., lack of insurance coverage).

At the IR clinic, patients are examined and their obesity and surgical history is documented. They are educated about the procedure, the expected weight-loss outcome, benefits and risks, periprocedural care, and follow-up. Screening aims to identify patients with conditions and disorders that may preclude BAE or increase the risk of BAE-related complications (Table 1). In a monthly multidisciplinary “obesity board” meeting, physicians review all patients who have been evaluated by IR and OM. A joint decision is made to proceed in 1 of 3 directions (Fig. 1): (1) BAE is suitable for the patient, who then proceeds with computed tomographic angiography (CTA) assessment of the upper gastrointestinal tract and gastric blood supply, as well as procedure scheduling; (2) the patient requires additional evaluation or investigation (e.g., psychologic evaluation for eating disorder, 3D CTA for history of gastric surgery, gastric emptying study for motility disorders); (3) BAE is unsuitable at present owing to a contraindication (Table 1) or insufficient time given for noninvasive weight-loss measures with possible reconsideration of BAE in the future.

Data collection and analysis

Variables collected for each patient included basic demographics including age and sex, obesity-related comorbidities, initial weight at presentation to the WMP, baseline weight before undergoing BAE and weight trajectory through the time period of follow-up. Data collected also included any procedure-related complication and weight loss medications given. Given the small sample size, statistical analysis was not applicable and results were presented in a descriptive form. The main outcome of the study, total body weight loss at 4–5 and 12 months’ follow up, was calculated as percentage change in weight from the baseline before the procedure. A brief summary of each case is also presented including more details on obesity-related comorbidities, weight loss history, weight loss trajectory before and after BAE and anti-obesity medications given, if any.

BAE procedure

BAE is performed under moderate sedation as described previously [24,26]. Embolization of the left gastric artery with or without the gastroepiploic artery is performed with 300- to 500-μm embolic microspheres (Bead Block, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA). Bead mixing is performed per product protocol, with 2 mL of microspheres added to 18 mL of 50:50 contrast-to-saline mixture. The embolization endpoint is complete stasis of the antegrade arterial flow in the target artery, defined as visualization of contrast for at least 5 cardiac cycles.

Periprocedural care

For 2 weeks before BAE, patients are prescribed a gastroprotective regimen of an oral proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole, 40 mg daily) and an oral cytoprotective agent (sucralfate, 1 g twice daily).

Preoperatively, a transdermal scopolamine (1.5 mg) patch is applied for postoperative nausea and vomiting prevention. Intravenous acetaminophen is administered simultaneously during embolization if patients start to experience gastric discomfort. Antiemetics can be administered during embolization as needed.

After BAE, patients are monitored in the recovery room until they meet the institution's admission criteria for overnight observation. Patients receive continuous intravenous fluids for adequate hydration, intravenous analgesic (acetaminophen, 1000 mg every 6 h) as needed to ease stomach ischemic pain, antiemetics (ondansetron, 4 mg every 6 h; metoclopramide, 10 mg every 6 h), antihistamines and antacids (famotidine, 20 mg twice daily; promethazine, 12.5 mg every 6 h). Patient-controlled analgesia (fentanyl, 20 mcg/mL) can be considered if pain is severe. If patients continue to experience pain and vomiting, hospitalization is extended until they are stable.

On discharge, the gastroprotective regimen is continued for 4 weeks, in addition to an antiemetic (ondansetron, 8 mg orally disintegrating tablets, every 8 h as needed).

For 12 h after BAE, patients are allowed only sips of water and ice chips. Diet is then advanced slowly as tolerated, starting with 1–2 days of clear liquids, then 1–2 days of full liquids. Finally, solids are introduced. Subsequently, patients can resume their nutritional plan as directed by the WMP. Patients should avoid alcoholic, carbonated, and acidic beverages for the first month after BAE to avoid gastric irritation. Similarly, spicy food, large intake of caffeinated beverages, and other dietary habits that can increase stomach acidity are discouraged. Patients are asked to avoid exertion for 48 h after BAE. Otherwise, physical activity is permitted, as tolerated, after hospital discharge.

Patients are seen in the IR clinic within 1 month after BAE. In addition to regular assessment after endovascular procedures, patients are asked about nausea, emesis, abdominal pain, acid reflux symptoms, diarrhea, constipation, and melena. Their weight-loss progress, appetite, eating behavior (e.g., cravings, food preference, meal schedule, and eating pattern), and quality of life are also assessed. The post-BAE course is variable between patients. However, patients typically report decreased appetite with smaller meal portions and a quicker feeling of fullness compared with before BAE. Additional follow-up beyond 1 month may be needed on a case-by-case basis. Patients follow up with their OM physician for continued long-term obesity care.

Results

Thirty-one consultations for BAE were received between November 2021 and September 2023 (Fig. 2). Of the 31 referrals, 24 patients (77%) attended their appointments at the IR clinic and were considered for interdisciplinary review. Eight patients were excluded for technical or medical contraindications (Fig. 2). In 6 patients, the decision was to give more time for medical management before reconsidering BAE. Patient 1

A 49-year-old woman with class I obesity who had an initial weight of 91.6 kg (BMI 32.6 kg/m2) was started on a diet and exercise plan. After approximately 9 months, her weight continued to increase, reaching 100.0 kg before BAE. She successfully underwent the procedure with no complications. Thereafter, she had a steady weight loss of 2.0% and 8.8% at 2 and 5 months, respectively. She was prescribed phentermine-topiramate 6 months after BAE, which she used for ∼5 months before switching to weekly semaglutide injections. Since then, she reported further weight loss reaching 10.2% at 12 months, 14.3% at 15 months, and 19.7% at 20 months after BAE.

Patient 2

A 49-year-old man with binge-eating disorder and class II obesity complicated by metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, hypertension, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and asthma had difficulty losing weight with multiple attempts at commercial weight-loss programs. On enrollment to the WMP, his initial weight was 123.8 kg (BMI 38.1 kg/m2). He was started on a structured nutrition and physical activity plan, initiated on topiramate—which was later discontinued due to an episode of nephrolithiasis—and referred to a psychologist. Fluoxetine was also prescribed to manage his binge-eating symptoms. He was also started on semaglutide 1 mg weekly subcutaneous injections, which he maintained for approximately 10 months prior to undergoing BAE. During this period, he initially achieved a 9.5% reduction in body weight relative to his weight at WMP enrollment but subsequently regained weight, reaching a pre-BAE weight of 119.9 kg despite continued pharmacologic treatment. He underwent BAE successfully and without complications. After the procedure, he maintained his nutritional and physical activity plan, along with fluoxetine and semaglutide. At 2-week follow-up, he had a 4.5% weight loss and reported a significant reduction in appetite compared with baseline. His weight-loss trajectory was 6.0%, 4.7%, 5.7%, 11.8%, and 14.8% at 2, 5, 7, 12, and 14 months, respectively. At the 12-month follow-up, he commented that “the feeling of always thinking and planning for the next meal has gone away” and that the procedure was, therefore, a “life-changing experience” for him.

Patient 3

A 30-year-old woman with class III obesity and polycystic ovary syndrome who had an initial weight of 138.3 kg (BMI 48.7 kg/m2) was managed on a nutrition and physical activity plan, with phentermine. She achieved a 3.5% weight loss over 5 months on this regimen. At 6 months, BAE was performed successfully with no complications. Thereafter, she reported significant reduction in her appetite at the 2-week follow-up, compared with pre-BAE. She experienced a weight loss of 4.9% at 2 months. However, she was unable to continue following up with her obesity physician for the next 6 months owing to external stressors. At 8 months, she resumed her weight management when her weight loss was only 0.8% from baseline. At 18 months after BAE, she achieved a 5.5% weight loss.

Patient 4

A 56-year-old woman with class III obesity who had an initial weight of 102.3 kg (BMI 44.1 kg/m2) had unsuccessfully attempted weight loss several times using commercial weight-loss programs. At the WMP, she was managed on a nutritional and physical activity plan, along with phentermine for ∼11 months. However, she could not sustain a satisfactory weight loss and had a pre-BAE weight of 102.5 kg. She successfully underwent the procedure with no complications. At 1-month follow-up, she exhibited 16.4% weight loss compared with baseline and reported significant reduction in appetite, as well as an alteration in taste. Over the 12 months that followed BAE, the trajectory to weight loss from baseline was 14.2% at 4 months, 9.5% at 6 months, 4.0% at 9 months and 2.8% at 12 months. With her increasing weight, doctors recommended that she start a GLP-1 agonist, but her insurance claims have been denied multiple times.

Patient 5

A 28-year-old woman with class II obesity and comorbid depression and anxiety who had an initial weight of 97.5 kg (BMI 34 kg/m2) was managed on a nutrition and physical activity plan. The patient had a history of unsuccessful weight-loss attempts, including prior use of phentermine and liraglutide. At the WMP, she was prescribed phentermine, which she used for approximately 3 months before switching to naltrexone-bupropion. However, the latter medication was discontinued because she experienced intolerable adverse effects. Consequently, she was prescribed phentermine-topiramate for ∼4 months. Despite adherence to the WMP and medication, the patient faced challenges reaching her weight goals and had a baseline pre-BAE weight of 108.9 kg. She underwent the procedure successfully and without complications. After BAE, she continued phentermine-topiramate and concurrently initiated semaglutide, which was discontinued after a brief period due to lack of insurance coverage. Despite close adherence to the WMP, she experienced significant difficulty with weight loss and had an upward trend in weight over the 12 months after BAE, reaching 117.5 kg.

Discussion

Obesity is a chronic disease complicated by relapses and the need for long-term treatment and follow-up [29]. In collaboration with OM physicians, BAE may be offered as a component within a comprehensive WMP, rather than as a stand-alone procedure. Based on our experience, BAE may be combined with long-term obesity medications; additional research is needed to determine outcomes with a combination strategy. As with other WMPs, an effective BAE program should include counseling on the risks, benefits, and alternatives specific to the procedure. These programs also carry an ethical responsibility to inform patients of all treatment options, including metabolic-bariatric surgery. The final decision between BAE and other treatments should be made by the patient.

Starting a clinical program requires approval from institutional and departmental leadership. In academic institutions and healthcare systems, permissions from IR leadership and the hospital administration can take a long time. However, these approvals should be completed before patient recruitment because BAE is not currently a bariatric procedure approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

To ensure program success, OM physicians must be adequately trained about BAE and periprocedural weight management strategies. At our institution, all OM physicians received live training and a recorded lecture on BAE as part of the OM fellowship curriculum. Similarly, IR physicians interested in performing BAE should consider basic education on obesity care. This training and knowledge development can be achieved through continuing medical educational courses and at major radiological society meetings that present recent updates.

We recommend monitoring program outcomes by building and maintaining a patient database to track weight loss, weight management engagement, and other outcomes to establish evidence for the procedure and to support quality-improvement activities. As more practices begin to offer BAE, a national data registry will become crucial for comparing data and outcomes between programs.

Program evolution is expected as more patients enroll. Practices can be expanded in multiple ways, for example by training and hiring more practitioners, collaborating with other practices, and marketing. Programs can be advertised through education of primary care physicians, educational prints, and flat-screen ads in hospitals and care centers, as well as through media advertisements.

Various BAE technical points can be modified according to the operator's preference, including the size of embolic beads, the choice of arteries to embolize, the use of an anti-reflux catheter, and the use of cone-beam CT (CBCT) imaging [18,[24], [25], [26],[30], [31], [32], [33]]. In our program, we use 300- to 500-μm microspheres, which have been the most tested for safety and efficacy. BAE is performed primarily on the left gastric artery, which usually supplies most of the gastric fundus where the majority of ghrelin-producing cells reside. In select cases, the gastroepiploic artery is embolized if it is observed to supply the fundus [18,[24], [25], [26],[30], [31], [32], [33]]. To avoid excessive ischemia and increased risk of complications, the short gastric arteries and the left inferior phrenic artery should not be embolized. We do not typically use CBCT or intra-arterial CT, though they can be helpful to confirm arterial supply and adequate fundal coverage. However, consideration of such imaging should be balanced against increased radiation exposure.

The coding and billing process can be problematic when starting a new practice because the services provided lack terminology and clear descriptions. Many unforeseen issues can arise, and providers must be vigilant for erroneous processing. For example, the Current Procedural Terminology code 37243 used for billing is not specific to BAE. Our institution's coding team can access the information in patient medical records required to determine the appropriate level of service and bills accordingly. The clinical team may need to work with the coding team to confirm correct description of service and facilitate correct billing. It is also important to clarify that pre- and postoperative care including clinic visits, tests and medications, hospital admission and surgical intervention for complications are not covered under BAE expenses and will be billed separately to insurance as applicable. Such information should be explained in the procedure consent or service contract.

In our program, we confirm insurance coverage for the BAE procedure before seeing patients in the IR clinic. This step can decrease the chance of unexpected rejection by the insurance company, which may disrupt care at later stages. It also saves time, effort, and money for the patient in case the insurance company is unwilling to support the procedure. However, such a step may become less important in the future as BAE becomes more common.

Limitations

Several important limitations must be acknowledged in this study. 1) Our study was conducted at a single academic center with a specialized multidisciplinary program, which may not reflect the resources and capabilities available in other practice settings. 2) Patient selection was necessarily conservative given the investigational nature of BAE, potentially introducing selection bias toward highly motivated patients. 3) Most patients were managed concurrently with anti-obesity medication, making it difficult to isolate the specific impact of BAE. 4) While the mean weight loss results were promising, the individual outcomes of the 5 patients were highly heterogeneous ranging from significant weight loss to weight regain close to or even above baseline weight. 5) Our cohort size was limited, with only 5 patients undergoing BAE during the study period, which restricted our ability to perform statistical analysis or definitely comment on the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

This study describes the integration of bariatric artery embolization within an established multidisciplinary weight management program. When incorporated into a comprehensive WMP, BAE may offer an alternative obesity treatment that fills the treatment gap for patients for whom lifestyle modifications alone have been unsuccessful and who do not qualify for bariatric surgery. Our two-year experience resulted in modest but meaningful weight loss in some of the patients undergoing the procedure, with no major procedural complications observed. However, further study of BAE outcomes in larger patient populations is needed to establish factors that correlate with weight loss success in order to optimize the outcomes for patients with obesity undergoing this procedure. Through our experience, as described in this paper, we have demonstrated that new and minimally invasive treatment options can be integrated into a weight management program relatively quickly with an experienced, multidisciplinary team.

Clinical takeaway messages
