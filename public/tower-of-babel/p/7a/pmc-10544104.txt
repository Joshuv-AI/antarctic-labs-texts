Understanding the Benefits of SGLT2 Inhibition∗
Authors: Rick B Vega
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: epicardial adipose tissue, interleukin-6, paracrine effect, preadipocyte, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor

Inhibition of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) has revolutionized the treatment of heart failure. Several large outcome studies have demonstrated that SGLT2 inhibition lowers the risk of cardiovascular death or hospitalization for heart failure. Remarkably, SGLT2 inhibitors show benefit in heart failure across the spectrum of ejection fraction, including heart failure with reduced, mildly reduced, and preserved ejection fraction (HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF, respectively). In HFrEF, SGLT2 inhibitors are now included as one of the four medication classes that show benefit. This benefit is also observed regardless of the diabetic status of the patient.1 These results have been unexpected, given that the primary effect of SGLT2 inhibition is on glucose resorption in the proximal tubule of the kidney. Multiple hypotheses have been proposed to explain the cardioprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors. These include effects on cardiac remodeling, changes in energy substrates, natriuresis and diuresis, etc.2

Among the myriad of physiologic effects of this drug class, it has previously been demonstrated that SGLT2 inhibition affects epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) mass and function.3,4 For example, dapagliflozin treatment for 6 months in patients with diabetes and coronary artery disease resulted in significantly decreased EAT mass.3 Separately, SGLT2 inhibition was shown to be expressed in explanted EAT fat pads.4 In the same study, inhibition of SGLT2 also affected expression of secreted adipokines and increased glucose uptake in explanted EAT. EAT, that is, the adipose tissue located between the epicardial surface of the myocardium and the visceral layer of the epicardium, is thought to play a key role in the interplay of diabetes and obesity with cardiovascular disease.5 Under normal conditions, EAT plays an important role in providing fuel substrates and buffering the myocardium from excessive free fatty acid levels. However, this balance may become disrupted under pathophysiologic conditions when EAT takes on a more proinflammatory profile. Therefore, given the role it plays in normal cardiac physiology and under disease conditions, do effects on EAT explain, at least in part, the cardioprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors?

In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, Takano et al6 seek to provide answers to this question by investigating the direct effects of SGLT2 inhibition on EAT. The authors were able to access EAT from distinct patient populations undergoing cardiac surgery, including nondiabetic patients, diabetic patients treated with empagliflozin, and patients with atrial fibrillation. In preadipocytes isolated from EAT, SGLT2 expression was predominantly in the preadipocytes and decreased with differentiation of the cells into mature adipocytes. Interestingly, treatment of the preadipocytes with empagliflozin seemed to affect maturation of the adipocytes (lipid accumulation) without interfering with the general differentiation program (eg, peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor γ expression). This also correlated with decreased expression of a number of proinflammatory factors, such as interleukin (IL)-1α, IL-1β, and IL-6, when the preadipocytes were treated with empagliflozin but not when treated after the cells were differentiated. A functional impact of this effect was examined by co-culture of empagliflozin-treated adipocytes with human induced pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes. Reduced reactive oxygen species generation, oxidative stress markers, and expression of NPPA/NPPB (encoding the natriuretic peptides A-type natriuretic factor and B-type natriuretic peptide [BNP]) was observed in the cardiomyocytes co-cultured with adipocytes treated with empagliflozin. Finally, expression of IL-6 was shown to be lower in EAT of empagliflozin-treated patients, and this expression correlated with plasma levels of N-terminal proBNP.

These results raise several intriguing questions. First, does SGLT2 inhibition have a direct effect on EAT? The authors do an admirable job demonstrating the expression pattern of SGLT2 during differentiation of these cells. The fact that the cells used were primary preadipocytes isolated from human EAT strengthens these data. Treatment of the cells with empagliflozin demonstrate clearly a direct effect. However, some caution should be exercised in the interpretation of these results, because the concentration of empagliflozin was significantly higher than the IC50 for SGLT2 (single-digit nanomolar) and could inhibit the other SGLT family members. Second, do the effects on EAT contribute to the cardioprotective actions of SGLT2 inhibitors? Expressions of several proinflammatory cytokines and markers are reduced with treatment of the adipocytes with empagliflozin. Importantly, this effect is observed only when the preadipocytes are treated and not after differentiation of the cells into mature adipocytes. This seems to suggest a reprogramming with SGLT2 inhibition of the cells, to some extent, to a diminished proinflammatory state. EAT does assume a more proinflammatory phenotype in disease states such as diabetes/obesity and coronary artery disease. The data in the current manuscript imply that newly differentiated adipocytes exhibiting a less inflammatory phenotype would be required to realize the benefits of SGLT2 inhibition. It is not clear that the formation of new adipocytes in EAT occurs at a sufficient rate to observe this benefit. However, the authors have shown that IL-6 expression is lower in EAT from diabetic patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors. A thorough examination of the molecular profile of these cells would be valuable to more completely understand the impact of SGLT2 inhibition on EAT.

There has been a greater appreciation recently of the interplay between EAT and the myocardium in normal physiology and in disease states. The direct contact of EAT with the myocardium allows for bidirectional communication between these two tissues. As EAT becomes remodeled by pathophysiologic stimuli to negatively affect the heart, it is also quite possible that restoration of the normal EAT phenotype could provide benefit in disease states such as diabetes and heart failure. The present study adds to a growing body of literature that suggests that SGLT2 inhibition restores the normal function of EAT, presumably through direct actions on the tissue. This work supports the intriguing possibility that this mechanism could in part explain the cardioprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibition. Ultimately, this may also identify patients most likely to benefit therapeutically from SGLT2 inhibitors.
