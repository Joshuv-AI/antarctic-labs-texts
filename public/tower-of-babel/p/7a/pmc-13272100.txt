Minimal Perceived Discomfort During Oscillometric Blood Pressure Measurement in Children: A Cross‐Sectional Study With Implications for Systematic Screening
Authors: Lise Fischer Mikkelsen, Ann‐Kristine Mandøe Svendsen, Sofie Axelgaard, Malene Klinkby Lind, Malene Nørskov, Christina Ellervik, Luise Borch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRoutine blood pressure measurement in children has been proposed for systematic screening, but concerns remain regarding potential harm, including procedural discomfort. This has not previously been studied. The primary aim of this study was to assess the perceived discomfort of routine blood pressure (BP) measurement in children and adolescents, and thereby inform the procedure's suitability for potential systematic screening. Secondly, we aimed to examine whether sex, age, and previous experience with blood pressure measurement were associated with the perceived discomfort. This cross‐sectional study enrolled Danish children aged 5–17 years attending the pediatric outpatient clinic of Gødstrup Hospital between the first of June and the 30th of August 2025. Each child underwent oscillometric BP measurement and rated their discomfort using the Wong‐Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale. The distribution of the reported scores was assessed through descriptive statistics. Odinal logistic regression was used to examine the association between discomfort and sex, age and prior experience with blood pressure measurement. In total, 127 children and adolescents (63 boys, 64 girls) were enrolled in this study. WBS scores were distributed as follows: 63% “No Hurt”, 28% “Hurts a Little Bit”, and 9% across the remaining categories; no child reported “Hurts Worst”. Regression models revealed non‐significant associations with WBS‐score for age, sex and prior experience with blood pressure measurement. In conclusion, oscillometric BP measurement with adaptive cuff inflation was well‐tolerated in this pediatric cohort. This finding supports the feasibility and acceptability of routine oscillometric blood pressure measurement, highlighting its suitability for potential systematic screening programs.

Introduction

Elevated blood pressure(BP) in childhood is associated with long‐term cardiovascular risk [1, 2], yet it remains frequently undiagnosed [3, 4], posing a significant challenge for healthcare systems worldwide; although international guidelines recommend systematic screening to reduce underdiagnosis [5, 6], the supporting evidence continues to be debated [7].

According to the principles outlined by Wilson and Jungner [8] and endorsed by the World Health Organization [9], the accuracy, cost‐effectiveness, and acceptability of any screening method—including the potential discomfort it may cause—must be carefully considered before its implementation in clinical or public health practice [8]. However, the potential procedural discomfort associated with BP screening in children and adolescents has not been previously studied [7].

BP measurement is essential for detecting hypertension in children and adolescents, as well as for tracking the effectiveness of treatment over time [5, 10]. Accurate office BP readings are crucial to ensure proper referral for further assessment and to enable reliable follow‐up during management. A commonly recognized challenge in this process is the phenomenon of reactive or “white coat” hypertension, where children's discomfort or anxiety during measurement heightens their BP due to sympathetic activation [11]. This can lead to misinterpretation of BP readings and result in unnecessary follow‐up visits and referrals. Physical discomfort during BP measurement may also contribute to a broader anxiety response.

Oscillometric devices are increasingly used in clinical practice, while the auscultatory method is used less frequently, particularly for screening purposes [10]. In many settings, office BP measurements form the basis for referral to more comprehensive diagnostic assessments, such as 24‐hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) [5, 11, 12].

This study aims to quantify children's perceived discomfort during routine oscillometric BP measurement using the validated Wong‐Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale(WBS) [13] and to determine whether age, sex and previous experience with BP measurement affect reported discomfort. This question is relevant both for everyday clinical use of the method and in the broader context of evaluating the feasibility of potential national screening programs.

Methods

This cross‐sectional study was conducted at the pediatric outpatient clinic of Gødstrup Regional Hospital, Denmark, on selected dates between the first of June and the 30th of August 2025. Children were eligible if they were 5–17 years old and attended one of the department's outpatient clinics on the study dates (the general pediatric, diabetes, asthma and allergy, gastroenterology, or nephrology clinics). BP was measured either as part of routine clinical assessment during scheduled consultations or specifically for the purposes of this study. Participants were recruited from across the above‐mentioned pediatric outpatient clinics, comprising children attending for a broad range of clinical evaluations and follow‐up visits. Detailed information regarding underlying morbidity, including cardiovascular, renal, or neurodevelopmental conditions, was not systematically collected in this study.

Of the eligible children, two declined to participate. In total, 128 children were consecutively enrolled. Data from one child was excluded due to missing information, leaving data from 127 children for analysis. The final cohort consisted of 63 boys (49,6%) and 64 girls (50,4%).

Exposure

BP was measured using the pediatric department's standard automated oscillometric devices (Welch Allyn Spot Vital Signs LXi, GE CARESCAPE VC150, and Mindray BeneVision N12l), which employ adaptive inflation algorithms that dynamically estimate the required cuff inflation pressure during inflation at each measurement. Device allocation was random, and they were used in approximately equal proportions, as their use depended on the consultation room in which the assessment took place, with each room equipped with a specific device. The procedure followed international guidelines for office BP measurements. The cuff size was selected based on measurement of the mid‐upper arm circumference, as specified by the manufacturer (15–18cm: child; 18–22cm: size S; 22–32cm: size M; 32–45cm: size L), following routine clinical practice and securing that the bladder covered 80–100% of the mid–upper arm circumference and at least 40% of the acromion–olecranon length [5, 12]. However, detailed anthropometric data, including arm circumference measurements and selected cuff sizes, were not recorded in the study dataset. Measurements were conducted in a quiet environment with participants seated upright and the arm supported; the right arm was used for all measurements. Participants rested for 5 min prior to measurement. Three BP readings were obtained at 1‐minute intervals for all participants; if a measurement failed, additional readings were performed until three successful measurements were achieved. A parent or guardian was present during the procedure. The BP measurement was conducted by either pediatric residents or nurses with pediatric experience, who were experienced in introducing the procedure in an age‐appropriate manner.

Outcome

Discomfort during BP measurement was assessed using the Wong‐Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale (WBS) [13] (Figure 1, Danish version in Figure S1). Participants were asked to rate their discomfort immediately after BP measurement. The WBS is a validated self‐report measure widely used to assess pain intensity in children and adolescents aged 5 to 17 years [13, 14]. It involves a series of six illustrated faces (A–F) corresponding to increasing discomfort levels (score 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10) and was presented in laminated paper format. Parents were instructed not to respond on behalf of the child.

Percentage of children reporting each level of the Wong‐Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10).

For the purpose of this study, discomfort was rated after completion of the three measurements; however, BP values themselves were not included in the analyses.

Other Variables

The following variables were recorded: age, sex (based on self‐report) and prior experience with BP measurement (recorded as yes or no). No data were collected on indication for clinic attendance, underlying comorbidities, or baseline anxiety. Anthropometric measures such as height, weight, and arm circumference were not recorded beyond what was required for clinical cuff selection.

Statistical Analysis

WBS scores were analyzed as an ordinal outcome with six ordered categories (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10), with higher values indicating greater pain intensity.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and WBS scores. These included medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables.

Associations between WBS scores and participant characteristics were assessed using univariate and multivariate proportional odds logistic regression models. The multivariate model included age (continuous), sex (male/female), and previous experience of BP measurement (yes/no) as independent variables. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional odds assumption underlying the ordinal logistic regression model was evaluated using the Brant test. A two‐sided p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.3, 2026).

Results

This study cohort included 127 children and adolescents aged 5–17 years, with a median age of 9 years (IQR 6–13) for analysis (Table 1). The cohort was composed of 63 boys (49,6%) and 64 girls (50.4%). 56.7% of the participants had previous experience with BP measurement. Overall, the procedure was well tolerated. The vast majority of the children reported minimal or no discomfort, with 63% selected “No Hurt” (WBS score 0) and 28% selected “Hurts Little Bit” (WBS score 2). Only 9% of the children reported higher levels of discomfort, and notably, none reported the maximum score of “Hurts Worst” (Figure 1).

Discussion

This study suggests that routine office BP measurement with oscillometric devices used for the purpose of screening is a generally well‐tolerated procedure among children aged 5–17 years. Using the validated Wong‐Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale, we found that the majority of participants (91%) reported minimal to no discomfort. Increasing age was associated with lower odds of reporting higher discomfort in the unadjusted analysis; however, this association was not statistically significant after adjustment, although a similar trend persisted. No significant associations were observed for sex or prior experience with BP measurement.

We used the Wong‐Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale [13], whose psychometric properties have been rigorously evaluated in pediatric populations, demonstrating strong reliability and validity across diverse clinical contexts. Tomlinson et al. [14] conducted a systematic review confirming that the Wong‐Baker FACES scale is among the most valid and reliable tools for pediatric pain self‐report, suitable for both clinical practice and research involving children and adolescents.

The low level of reported physical discomfort, as reported in this study, must be carefully distinguished from the separate psychological constructs of distress and anxiety, which are known drivers of “white coat” hypertension. Our results do not refute the existence of procedural anxiety; rather, they clarify its nature. A child can experience significant anxiety and sympathetic activation—leading to an elevated BP reading—without perceiving the physical sensation of the cuff inflation as painful. This disjunct is crucial for clinical practice and future research. It suggests that interventions aimed at reducing reactive hypertension should focus less on the physical sensation of the procedure and more on the broader psychological and environmental factors that contribute to a child's anxiety in a clinical setting.

While this study provides novel descriptive insight into the tolerability of pediatric BP measurement for the purpose of screening, its findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. The study was conducted at a single center with a modest sample size and a relatively homogenous Danish population, which limits the external generalizability of our findings to more diverse pediatric populations. Furthermore, contextual factors such as parental presence and the expertise of the pediatric staff may have contributed to lower discomfort scores [15, 16], and results might differ in other settings, such as school‐based screenings conducted by personnel without pediatric‐specific training.

Several potentially relevant covariates were not available in this study. These include indication for outpatient attendance, underlying comorbidities, and baseline anxiety levels, all of which may influence perceived discomfort. Detailed information regarding underlying cardiovascular, renal or neurodevelopmental conditions was not systematically collected. In addition, anthropometric data, including arm circumference and cuff size selection, were not recorded, limiting reproducibility of cuff size selection and precluding assessment of whether anthropometrics, cuff size or cuff fit influenced perceived discomfort. Furthermore, the specific device used for each participant's BP measurement was not recorded, and individual BP values and maximum cuff inflation pressure were unavailable. As discomfort during oscillometric BP measurement is likely associated with cuff inflation pressure, the absence of these variables represents an important limitation of this study.

The absence of the above‐mentioned variables limits the ability to adjust for potential confounding and should be considered when interpreting the results, as residual confounding may have influenced the observed associations.

Additionally, there may be age‐related differences in how the WBS captures perceived discomfort. However, in this study, the WBS was applied uniformly across all age groups without adjustment for age. This may have influenced our finding that age was not a significant factor in perceived discomfort during BT measurement.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide important evidence supporting the feasibility of oscillometric BP measurement for the purpose of screening, when using devices with adaptive automatic cuff inflation, in a clinical outpatient setting and may support its potential use in pediatric BP screening programs. The high acceptability and minimal physical discomfort associated with the procedure fulfill a key criterion for screening established by Wilson and Jungner [8]. The Wong‐Baker FACES Pain Rating Scale itself may also be a useful clinical tool. Presenting the scale beforehand could help prepare children for procedures by showing them how their peers typically experience it, potentially reducing anticipatory anxiety. Given that pain and discomfort in children are often underrecognized [17], further research quantifying the tolerability of other common pediatric procedures is warranted to improve patient experience and diagnostic accuracy.

In conclusion, oscillometric office BP measurement with adaptive automatic cuff inflation in pediatric outpatient settings was associated with low levels of self‐reported physical discomfort among children undergoing routine outpatient assessment. These findings should be interpreted with caution, given the absence of anthropometric data and cuff inflation pressures. Importantly, generalizability is limited to screening contexts, as higher cuff inflation pressures and repeated measurements in children requiring BP assessment beyond routine screening are likely to increase perceived discomfort. Overall, the study supports the feasibility and acceptability of routine oscillometric BP measurement in children and adolescents.
