How interactions between oxidized DNA and the NLRP3 inflammasome fuel inflammatory disease
Authors: Angela Lackner, Lemuel Leonidas, Alijah Macapagal, Hannah Lee, Reginald McNulty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent discoveries have emphasized the critical role of oxidized DNA (ox-DNA) in inflammation and immune regulation. Produced during oxidative stress from infection or tissue damage, ox-DNA activates signaling pathways that drive the release of proinflammatory cytokines, specifically engaging the NLRP3 inflammasome, a key player in cytokine maturation and host defense. NLRP3 is increasingly implicated in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, with ox-DNA recognized as a central activator of this inflammasome. This review examines the role of ox-DNA in inflammasome activation, its broader impact on inflammatory processes, and promising therapeutic approaches targeting ox-DNA through both immunological and structural lenses. These insights highlight oxDNA’s relevance in inflammation and offer potential avenues for the treatment of a range of immune-related disorders.

NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the emerging role of ox-DNA in disease

Inflammation, a key component of innate immunity, is a major driver of diseases ranging from metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders to cancer and autoimmunity. Inflammation is a double-edged sword, whereby it plays protective roles in bacterial clearance during infection but its dysregulation is linked to both acute and chronic pathologies, creating new opportunities for therapeutic intervention [1–3]. These growing opportunities underscore the need to unravel the complex mechanisms linking inflammation to disease.

Recent discoveries have shed light on the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying inflammation, revealing novel pathways and targets for intervention [4]. In particular, advances in structural biology have uncovered intricate inflammatory pathways, including NLRP3 inflammasome (see Glossary) activation [3,4]. Emerging evidence now implicates ox-DNA, particularly oxidized mitochondrial DNA (ox-mtDNA), as a potent endogenous danger signal that directly activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, linking oxidative stress to innate immune activation and chronic inflammation [5]. With inflammation and ox-DNA recognized as key contributors to numerous pathologies, and no FDA-approved drugs targeting NLRP3 directly, a review of the mechanism and relationship between NLRP3 and ox-DNA is critical to guide future research and therapeutic development [6].

This review explores NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the role that ox-DNA plays in inflammasome activation. We dive into the possible mechanisms of association between NLRP3 and ox-DNA based on the relationships between NLRP3, the mitochondria, and DNA repair proteins. Finally, we examine the broader context of inflammatory mechanisms involving ox-DNA and discuss the clinical relevance of ox-DNA as a drug target against inflammatory disorders and diseases.

Canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation

The NLRP3 inflammasome is a multiprotein complex essential for innate immunity and host defense [7]. Monomeric NLRP3 is 120 kDa, comprising an N-terminal signaling pyrin domain, a central ATPase NACHT domain, and a scaffolding and autoinhibitory C-terminal leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain (Figure 1A) [8]. The protein recruits other members of the inflammasome, ASC, NEK7, and pro-caspase-1 to form a functional inflammasome (Figure 1). The NLRP3 inflammasome functions in the maturation of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 and assembly of the membrane pore gasdermin-D, which is responsible for pyroptosis (Figure 1B).

NLRP3 activation occurs in a two-step process: priming and activation (Figure 1B). During the priming phase, pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the cell surface recognize extracellular damage- or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs or PAMPs), resulting in NF-κB-dependent upregulation of NLRP3 and the pro-forms of inflammatory cytokines [9] (Figure 1B, left).

A second signal is required to fully activate the NLRP3 inflammasome. This activation signal often originates from DAMPs released during tissue injury, toxin exposure, or infection [10]. One well-characterized DAMP is extracellular ATP, which is released by stressed or dying cells. ATP binds to the P2X7 receptor, an ATP-gated ion channel, leading to a rapid influx of calcium and efflux of potassium [11]. These ionic shifts are key indicators of cellular distress and potent triggers of NLRP3 activation [12]. Activation culminates in the oligomerization of multiple NLRP3 monomers with ASC, NEK7, and pro-caspase-1 and the activation of caspase-1 through autocleavage. Active caspase-1 mediates cleavage of the cytokines pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms, as well as the protein gasdermin-D into its N-terminal form [13]. N-terminal gasdermin-D forms a pore in the cell membrane, releasing inflammatory cytokines and eventually resulting in pyroptosis (Figure 1B, right) [14]. Prolonged activity or dysregulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome has been established as a commonality among many autoinflammatory diseases, infections, and cancers, thereby making it a critical target of interest when developing putative treatments.

Notably, reactive oxygen species (ROS) act as both priming agents and activation signals for the NLRP3 inflammasome. During priming, mitochondrial ROS production increases as PRR signaling disrupts electron transport, particularly at complexes I and III of the electron transport chain (ETC), major sites of electron transfer vulnerable to leakage under stress [15]. This leakage allows electrons to reduce oxygen, forming superoxide (O2•−), which is rapidly converted by mitochondrial superoxide dismutase to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), a more stable, membrane-permeable ROS [16]. Mitochondrial H2O2 can diffuse into the cytosol via aquaporin channels, oxidizing cysteine residues on NF-κB pathway proteins and amplifying transcriptional priming [17].

Following priming, inflammasome activation involves additional mitochondrial dysfunction, including events such as mitochondrial membrane depolarization or release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). In contrast to priming, mitochondrial ROS can also induce inflammasome activation, specifically through the oxidation and subsequent cytosolic release of ox-mtDNA as a cytosolic DAMP. As discussed herein, the role of mitochondria and ox-mtDNA in NLRP3 inflammasome activation is complex and context dependent, involving both metabolic and structural cues such as ion flux, DNA, mitochondrial proteins, and ROS, which remain under active investigation [18].

ox-DNA as an inflammatory signal

ROS production and activity in response to inflammation is well established [5], specifically in the oxidation of mtDNA. Normally, mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) shields mtDNA in nucleoid-like structures [19], but during replication transient TFAM displacement exposes mtDNA to ROS [20]. Cells manage this damage via repair enzymes like 8-oxoguanine glycosylase 1 (OGG1), a base excision repair (BER) enzyme that excises oxidized bases [21], and through mitochondrial autophagy or mitophagy, especially in mitochondria-rich cells like macrophages, to eliminate damaged mitochondria [22]. However, when these systems are overwhelmed, ox-mtDNA is released into the cytosol, triggering inflammation (Figures 1 and 2). ROS affects a variety of cellular compartments and can also oxidize nuclear DNA, which then acts as an inflammatory DAMP when released (Figure 2B) [23].

In 2011, a link between ox-mtDNA and NLRP3 inflammasome activation was made, where mitochondrial ROS and NLRP3 were shown to be essential for cytosolic ox-mtDNA release [24]. NLRP3-deficient cells exposed to rotenone, a mitochondrial complex I inhibitor, failed to release ox-mtDNA and secreted less IL-1β, implicating NLRP3 in mitochondrial ROS regulation and signaling [25]. Furthermore, NLRP3-deficient cells also showed reduced cytosolic mtDNA on activation, reinforcing the hypothesis that NLRP3 can facilitate mtDNA release. Subsequent studies showed that NLRP3 colocalizes with cytosolic ox-mtDNA [25–27] and that enhancing OGG1 repair or blocking mtDNA synthesis inhibits NLRP3 activation [28,29]. To better understand this relationship, purified NLRP3 protein was used and shown to bind ox-mtDNA directly via its pyrin domain, which prefers oxidized over native mtDNA [30] (Figure 2C). The McNulty group has shown that the pyrin domain of NLRP3 is structurally similar to the active site of human OGG1 [30], with subsequent studies showing that the NLRP3 pyrin domain binds and cleaves ox-mtDNA at a subnanomolar KD, and that OGG1 inhibitors bind the NLRP3 pyrin and NACHT domains, blocking NLRP3’s interaction with ox-mtDNA and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Full-length NLRP3 also binds non-oxidized DNA, but no cleavage activity was observed in vitro, and non-oxidized DNA does not activate the inflammasome [27,31–33]. Other comparisons have shown that the NACHT domain of NLRP3 shares sequence similarity with the active site of bacterial OGG1 [30]. This presents a paradox: NLRP3 both cleaves ox-DNA and is activated by it, suggesting dual, potentially opposing roles. These functions may depend on oxidative stress levels or subcellular localization. For instance, oxoG excision activity could predominate under mild stress, while excess ox-DNA may favor inflammasome assembly and activation. Furthermore, cytosolic NLRP3 may sense and cleave ox-DNA, while NLRP3 localized to the transGolgi or microtubule organizing center may use ox-DNA to initiate inflammasome assembly. OGG1 overexpression reduces ox-mtDNA and NLRP3-driven inflammation, indicating competition for a shared substrate pool [29]. Intriguingly, CAPS-associated NLRP3 mutants exhibit enhanced ox-DNA cleavage activity in vitro, suggesting that increased catalytic function may contribute to the chronic inflammation observed in these patients, and implying that increased repair activity leads to heightened inflammatory disease, possibly through enhanced generation of ox-DNA fragments that serve as potent DAMPs [27]. Resolving how these roles are balanced will be key to understanding NLRP3’s regulation.

Beyond NLRP3, the absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) inflammasome is activated by cytosolic double-stranded (ds)DNA from a variety of origins (Figure 2C) [34]. Like NLRP3, AIM2 recruits ASC and pro-caspase-1 to trigger IL-1β and IL-18 release, but AIM2 prefers non-oxidized dsDNA and has a preference for DNA structure over sequence [33,35]. Although both AIM2 and NLRP3 activation converge on caspase-1 activation, IL-1β/IL-18 maturation, and pyroptosis, their distinct activation pathways and different DNA specificities diversify the innate immune response to a broad range of threats, functioning as complementary inflammasomes.

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway also detects cytosolic DNA, activating IRF3 and NF-κB to induce type I interferon (IFN) [36–38] (Figure 2C) [39,40]. cGAS–STING can counterbalance inflammasome signaling, where type I IFN inhibits IL-1β production via STATs [41]. By contrast, canonical NLRP3 activation induces cleavage of cGAS by caspase-1, suppressing cGAS activity and type I IFN production [42,43]. Thus, NLRP3, by engaging caspase-1, limits cGAS activation by inducing caspase-1-mediated inactivation of cGAS. Together, these findings highlight DNA sensing as a dynamic network balancing activation and regulation to distinguish DNA damage types and tailor immune responses.

Structural insights into NLRP3 inflammasome activation and regulation by ox-DNA

Structural organization of the inactive and active NLRP3 inflammasome

Each NLRP3 monomer has three key domains [44], and advances in cryoelectron microscopy (cryo-EM) have revealed its oligomeric assembly with inflammasome binding partners (Figure 3A,B) [45]. The pyrin domain (residues 1–91) forms a six-helix death domain, enabling homotypic interactions with itself and ASC to scaffold the inflammasome (Figure 3B) [46,47]. As noted earlier, the pyrin domain has been shown to bind ox-mtDNA, which may act as a chaperone to promote structural rearrangements that enhance ASC accessibility [27,44]. The NACHT domain includes five subdomains: FINSA (residues 131–218), a nucleotide-binding domain (residues 219–372), two helical domains (residues 373–434 and 542–649), and a winged helix domain (residues 435–541) [8]. The NACHT domain contains nucleotide-binding motifs that bind and hydrolyze ATP. ATP binding induces conformational changes that drive NLRP3 activation and oligomerization, while hydrolysis may regulate disassembly [48]. After a 92-amino-acid transition between residues 650 and 742, the LRR domain (residues 743–1036) forms an alpha-beta horseshoe and associates with NEK7 (Figure 3B). Functionally, the LRR domain is autoinhibitory, folding back onto the NACHT domain to stabilize NLRP3 in an inactive state.

The inactive NLRP3 decamer, solved by the Geyer group and stabilized by a small molecule inhibitor, is supported by three structural interfaces between each NACHT and LRR domain of the inactive decameric human NLRP3 inflammasome [8]. Of the ten pyrin domains, only two were resolved and shown to be trapped in the structure’s center (Figure 3C). This sequestration prevents interaction of the pyrin domains with ASC, blocking inflammasome assembly. As mentioned earlier, binding of ox-mtDNA by the pyrin domain may trigger structural rearrangement, exposing pyrin domains and enabling inflammasome assembly. Although the in vivo relevance of the inactive decamer is still under investigation, groups have shown that the inactive decamer, potentially stabilized by high ADP, may localize to cellular membranes (trans-Golgi or mitochondria) and sequester NLRP3 to prevent premature activation, promoting immune homeostasis [49].

On activation by DAMPs like ATP and ox-mtDNA described earlier, NLRP3 transitions from an inactive decamer to an active inflammasome via interactions with ASC, pro-caspase-1, and NEK7 (Figure 1 and Figure 3C–E). Cryo-EM studies performed by the Wu group reveal a 32-nm disc structure, with centralized NACHT domains and NEK7-bound LRRs projecting outward (Figure 3B,D). Pyrin domains insert through the disc center to engage ASC (Figure 3B,D) [50]. This structural shift is driven in part by ADP–ATP exchange within the NACHT domains, triggering a 90° rigid-body rotation that exposes pyrin domains (Figure 3D,E).

Other cryo-EM structures of NLRP3 have helped to inform the functional mechanisms behind inflammasome activation. Cryo-EM structures show that NEK7 is stimulus dependent for NLRP3 activation and that ASC binding depends on pyrin filament orientation [46,51]. Further, structures of the inactive and active mouse NLRP3 inflammasome highlighted key similarities and species-specific differences between NLRP3 inflammasomes [49,52]. Finally, a structure of the NACHT domain bound to the inhibitor NP3–562 shows improved specificity and bioavailability over the inhibitor MCC950, offering therapeutic potential [53]. These structural insights deepen our understanding of NLRP3 activation, pinpointing key interactions and conformational changes.

Structural basis for the interaction between NLRP3 and ox-DNA

Structural support for ox-mtDNA binding by the NLRP3 pyrin domain stems from its similarity to OGG1. OGG1 repairs oxoG lesions by bending DNA ~70° and extracting oxoG into its active site (Figure 2A,B) [27,30,54]. OGG1’s catalytic activity relies on key active-site residues between amino acids 240 and 330, including Lys249, Asp268, and Phe319, as well as Gly42, unique in OGG1’s β-sheet domain, which distinguishes oxoG from guanine [55].

NLRP3 shares structural and sequence homology with OGG1, particularly in its pyrin domain, where Lys2, Asp21, and Phe75 correspond to OGG1’s catalytic residues Lys249, Asp268, and Phe319, respectively [56,57]. Superposing the NLRP3 pyrin domain with the OGG1 active site highlights these shared active-site residues (Figure 3F), suggesting that NLRP3 may also recognize and process ox-DNA. Although mutagenesis has not yet confirmed this function, OGG1 inhibitors block both NLRP3 activation and its ability to bind and cleave ox-mtDNA [27,36,58]. NLRP3’s pyrin domain includes multiple lysines and aspartic acids positioned for possible interaction with ox-DNA. While Lys2 and Asp21 align with OGG1’s active residues, redundant nearby residues may also fulfill functional roles. These structural and functional similarities underscore the importance of ox-DNA in NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Inflammatory mechanisms and pathways involving the mitochondria

As discussed earlier, disruption of mitochondrial membrane potentials impairs the ETC and promotes superoxide formation(Figures 1 and 4A,B) [59], where compromised TFAM exposes mtDNA to oxidative lesions like oxoG (Figure 4C). Although NOX4, an NADPH oxidase isoform, is not the primary source of ROS, it does contribute to mitochondrial ROS generation, such that inhibition of either NOX4 or NLRP3 reduces ROS levels. SIRT1 enhancers like liraglutide suppress ROS by downregulating NOX4 expression, even when NLRP3 is present (Figure 4D) [60]. As the Karin group showed, when damage exceeds OGG1’s repair capacity, ox-mtDNA is cleaved and released into the cytosol [29,61] (Figures 1, 2A, and 4E). Importantly, NLRP3 knockout macrophages show reduced cytosolic ox-mtDNA, highlighting NLRP3’s role in mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS-linked inflammation [27].

NLRP3 is also associated with the mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS), an outer mitochondrial membrane protein, which typically signals via IRF3 and NF-κB to induce type I IFNs [62,63] (Figures 1 and 4F). Studies have shown that direct NLRP3–MAVS binding activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, while MAVS knockdown reduces inflammasome activation [62,63]. Notably, NLRP3 overexpression suppressed MAVS-dependent IFN-β signaling by over 80%, further highlighting the counterbalanced relationship between different types of inflammatory signals mentioned earlier [62].

Other mitochondrial partners of NLRP3 include mitofusin 2 (Mtfn2), a mitochondrial outer membrane fusion protein that tethers the endoplasmic reticulum to mitochondria [64]. NLRP3 can also bind cardiolipin, a stress-exposed phospholipid bound to the inner mitochondrial membrane. Data suggest that cardiolipin binds NLRP3 directly through its LRR region [65]. In response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), cardiolipin may serve as a scaffold for ASC recruitment in a calcium-dependent manner [66]. Finally, recent studies suggest that NLRP3 may associate directly with the mitochondrial membrane through an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence [67]. Collectively, these interactions highlight the mitochondria as a central hub for NLRP3 activation, integrating ROS, mitochondrial proteins, and ox-mtDNA to drive inflammation.

Cell-specific roles of ox-DNA in inflammation and innate immune response

The relationship between NLRP3, oxidative stress, and inflammation can vary by cell type, as seen in the differing responses of mature macrophages and neutrophils to inflammatory stimuli (Figure 5). Neutrophils are typically the first responders to tissue injury, outnumbering macrophages at the site of damage, while macrophages serve as a secondary defense [68,69].

In macrophages, mitochondrial respiration is the primary source of ROS production. In response to inflammatory signals like LPS, mitochondrial respiration is inhibited, increasing mitochondrial ROS and mtDNA oxidation (Figure 5A) [70]. This activity recruits NLRP3 and induces the inflammatory signaling cascade [63]. Disruption of mitochondrial respiration also reduces calcium uptake by mitochondria, leading to increased cytosolic calcium, a marker of cellular stress that activates NF-κB and inflammation [12]. Macrophages, with multiple mitochondria, may regulate this cascade by inducing mitophagy [22].

By contrast, neutrophils have fewer mitochondria, and their primary ROS production comes from the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) in the cytosol, which produces NADPH for NOX2-dependent ROS (Figure 5B) [69]. Mitochondria in neutrophils primarily assist in chemotaxis, not ROS production. In response to similar PAMPs and DAMPs, such as extracellular DNA that binds to TLR9 and LPS that binds to TLR4, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are formed in the cytosol of neutrophils [71]. Increased calcium uptake into mitochondria induces the secretion of chemotactic signals and NETosis, a form of neutrophil death that releases NETs to combat microorganisms or aid tissue repair through vesicle-mediated fission or cell lysis [72]. These differences highlight a cell-type-specific reliance on mitochondrial ROS and ox-mtDNA as inflammatory triggers.

Clinical and disease implications

While NLRP3 inflammasome activity can aid in clearing pathogens during infection [68], it is more often implicated in chronic inflammatory and autoimmune diseases such as gout, type 2 diabetes (T2D), atherosclerosis, inflammatory bowel disease, and cancer [73–75]. Furthermore, a single point mutation in NLRP3 can cause the autoinflammatory disease known as cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome (CAPS). Depending on the subset of the disease, this chronic systemic inflammation is marked by systemic inflammation and neurological symptoms [76].

ROS and ox-DNA are key triggers of NLRP3 across diseases (Table 1). In cardiovascular disease, NLRP3 activation is linked to oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, driving disease progression [77–79]. In acute myocardial infarction (AMI), patients show elevated levels of NLRP3 and pro-caspase-1 compared with healthy individuals [80]. In cardiomyocytes, ROS promotes the release of thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) from thioredoxin, which binds and activates NLRP3, and silencing TXNIP reduces NLRP3 activity in AMI, highlighting this pathway’s therapeutic potential [81].

The contribution of ox-DNA-linked inflammation to disease depends on cell type. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), ox-DNA acts as a danger signal that activates NLRP3 in microglia, exacerbating neuroinflammation and neuronal loss [82]. During AD progression, DNA damage products act as damage signals, prompting the migration of peripheral monocytes across the blood–brain barrier, initiating NLRP3-dependent inflammation [83]. Here, amyloid β plaques and tau tangles, two hallmarks of AD, activate NLRP3, leading to sustained inflammation, the release of proinflammatory cytokines, neuronal dysfunction, and apoptosis [84,85]. While the exact mechanism remains unclear, double-knockout Tau/ASC−/− and Tau/NLRP3−/− mice showed reduced tau phosphorylation and cognitive decline [84,85].

In T2D, NLRP3 activation and oxidative stress are linked to insulin resistance, particularly in adipose tissue, endothelial cells, and macrophages [86], where specific damage to the enzyme GAPDH causes a buildup of glycolytic intermediates that activate deleterious metabolic pathways. Mitochondrial dysfunction, a hallmark of insulin resistance, exacerbates oxidative stress, further triggering NLRP3 inflammasome activation [87]. NLRP3 also plays a role in the development of complications such as atherosclerosis, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and diabetic nephropathy, highlighting the clinical relevance of targeting NLRP3 in T2D.

NLRP3’s involvement in immune cells is best hallmarked by cancer. ROS-driven DNA damage causes genomic instability and impairs the DNA damage response [88]. Additionally, ROS upregulation during chemotherapy or radiation can lead to treatment resistance [88,89]. ROS and DNA damage contribute to cancer progression through NLRP3 by initiating the inflammasome’s assembly [90]. Pathological signaling of NLRP3, leading to the production of IL-1β, allows the widespread effects of cancer cells [91]. Inhibition of IL-1β production has been shown to decrease both the incidence and the mortality of lung cancer [92]. Given the effects of ROS and NLRP3 on cell survival signaling pathways, ROS modulators may improve the treatment and prevention of various cancers.

Circulating mtDNA, often elevated during cell stress and death, also reflects inflammasome activity. ICU patients with high plasma mtDNA show greater mortality risk, positioning mtDNA as a potential biomarker for trauma or infection severity [93]. Drugs inhibiting NLRP3 decrease cytosolic mtDNA [27], offering a therapeutic route to reduce ROS- and ox-DNA-linked inflammation in disease.

Concluding remarks

The NLRP3 inflammasome plays a pivotal role in the immune system by acting as a sensor for cellular stress and damage, with ox-DNA emerging as a significant trigger in its activation. The NLRP3 inflammasome serves as a central node in the innate immune response, detecting ox-DNA and promoting inflammasome assembly, leading to the activation of caspase-1 and the subsequent release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18. This pathway underscores a vital link between cellular stress, immune activation, and inflammation, suggesting that dysregulated NLRP3 activation by ox-DNA could contribute to pathological conditions such as autoimmune diseases or chronic inflammation. Although many questions remain (see Outstanding questions), including whether inhibiting NLRP3 will improve overall mitochondrial function, understanding this relationship further could pave the way for targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating NLRP3 activity to balance immune responses and mitigate inflammatory diseases.

Highlights

Inflammation, once seen as solely protective, is now recognized as a driver of diseases such as neurodegeneration, type 2 diabetes, and autoimmunity, prompting renewed interest in targeting upstream triggers like oxidized DNA (ox-DNA).

ox-DNA, particularly from damaged mitochondria or stressed nuclei, acts as a potent activator of innate immune sensors, including the NLRP3 inflammasome, linking oxidative stress directly to chronic inflammation.

Rather than being a passive byproduct, ox-DNA functions as an active inflammatory signal, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target in diseases marked by unresolved or sterile inflammation.

Advances in structural biology have shed light on how NLRP3 recognizes ox-DNA and undergoes conformational changes during activation, although the precise molecular site of inflammasome assembly remains to be fully defined.

Crosstalk between DNA-sensing pathways, including NLRP3, absent in melanoma 2, and cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes, enables the immune system to distinguish between different types of DNA damage, shaping the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses.
