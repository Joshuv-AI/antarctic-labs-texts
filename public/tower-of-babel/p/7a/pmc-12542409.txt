Whole-genome sequencing reveals resistance mechanisms and molecular epidemiology of carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa bloodstream infections
Authors: Yongqin Guo, Yangyang Hao, Mingchen Huang, Yihua Sun, Zhibo Tao, Yang Liu, Shanshan Huang, Peng Liu, Dandan Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA) has emerged as a critical threat in bloodstream infections (BSIs), with rising global prevalence and elevated mortality rates. Traditional surveillance methods often lacks resolution for resistance-virulence-transmission interplay, highlighting the importance of high-resolution genomics. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) has enabled unprecedented resolution in dissecting CRPA’s genetic landscape, revealing links between resistance, virulence, and outcomes.ResultsThis study employed WGS to characterize 61 P. aeruginosa isolates from BSIs, with a focus on 18 CRPA strains. Clinical data linked central venous catheterization to CRPA BSI development (OR = 6.6, p = 0.002) and identified carbapenem exposure, mechanical ventilation, and low hemoglobin as independent mortality risk factors. WGS identified 33.3% (n = 6, 6/18) of the strains harbored β-lactamase genes, and 44.4%(n = 8, 8/18) of the strains carried truncated OprD protein due to frameshift mutations or point mutations inducing translational truncation. Efflux pump overexpression (61.1% with ≥ 2-fold upregulation) further contributed to this resistance phenotype. MLST identified 49 distinct STs (including 2 novel types) and a pattern of endemic diversification. O11 is strongly linked to carbapenem resistance (CRPA: p = 0.02; MDRPA: p = 0.004), correlating with oprD mutations (p = 0.008) and exoU+/exoS-, indicating enhanced nosocomial adaptability.ConclusionsA very high genetic diversity was noted amongst P. aeruginosa strains isolated from BSIs cases. The mechanism of carbapenem resistance is mainly attributed to oprD mutations and efflux pumps activation, with carbapenemases emerging as an additional mechanism of concern. These resistance mechanisms with high-risk clinical factors collectively indicate that strict policies are essential for in CRPA BSIs management.Keywords: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Bloodstream infections, Risk factor, Carbapenemase, OprD, Efflux pump

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa, PA) is a common Gram-negative bacterium known for its remarkable adaptability to diverse environmental conditions [1–3]. This adaptability allows it to cause infections across diverse settings, making it a formidable opportunistic pathogen. Particularly in hospital-acquired infections, it causes a range of life-threatening diseases, including wound infections, pulmonary infections, bacteremia, and sepsis, with bloodstream infections (BSIs) being the most lethal. Recent global epidemiological studies have shown that P. aeruginosa ranks among the top five pathogens isolated from BSIs. The mortality rate of P. aeruginosa BSIs varies from 7.67 to 31.65% globally [4]. Carbapenems have long been regarded as the last-resort treatment for P. aeruginosa BSIs. However, the extensive use of carbapenems has led to increased drug resistance in P. aeruginosa, resulting in the emergence of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA), multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa (MDRPA) and difficult-to-treat resistant P. aeruginosa (DTRPA). Under typical conditions, the proportion of CRPA from BSIs has risen steadily, ranging from 0 to 34% over the past decade, with some hospitals reporting rates as high as 55% [4, 5]. Recent researches have documented mortality rates of 18–44% [6, 7], which are 1.3 to 2 times higher than those observed with carbapenem-susceptible strains, reflecting the difficulty in managing CPRA infections for critically ill patients. CRPA resistance is primarily mediated by chromosomal mutations, such as oprD alterations, overexpression of multidrug efflux pumps, and mutation or overexpression of AmpC [8–10]. However, recent years have shown a growing prevalence of CRPA strains harboring acquired resistance mechanisms, including the Ambler Class A β-lactamases, metallo β-lactamases (MBLs) and Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemases (KPCs) [11, 12]. These enzymes are often encoded on mobile genetic elements, enabling horizontal gene transfer and facilitating rapid dissemination across strains, further complicating efforts to control CRPA-associated infections.

Beyond resistance genes, virulence factors like the Type III Secretion System (T3SS) also drive P. aeruginosa pathogenesis. T3SS enhances the pathogenicity by secreting effector proteins, with exoS and exoU playing key roles [3, 13]. These effector proteins can directly disrupt the defense functions of host immune cells and inhibit local inflammatory responses [14], resulting in infections such as acute lung injury and sepsis [15]. Particularly in patients with P. aeruginosa BSIs, these effector proteins are closely associated with high mortality rates [16–18].

Carbapenem resistance and hypervirulence are key contributing factors of poor prognosis in BSIs. Timely local monitoring of strains’ genetic and molecular features is crucial for guiding treatment and tracking spread. The emergence of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) technologies has enabled the development of genome-based methods applicable for routine typing and surveillance of bacterial pathogens. These capabilities make WGS invaluable for unraveling resistance mechanisms and tracking pathogen evolution. Against this backdrop, our study integrates epidemiological data with WGS analyses of clinical isolates to investigate the resistance mechanisms and the molecular epidemiology of P. aeruginosa associated with BSIs.

Methods

Bacterial isolates and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

We collected a total of 61 clinical P. aeruginosa isolates between 2021 and 2023 from the first positive blood cultures of patients hospitalized at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. Only a single bacterial isolate per patient was evaluated. The identification was made using a MALDI-TOF MS method performed on a MALDI Biotyper device (Bruker, Mannheim, Germany). Antibiotic susceptibility and drug resistance of each strain was measured using the VITEK 2 Compact system (BioMérieux, Lyon, France), with results interpreted according to the breakpoint criteria defined by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Clinical data of the patients were collected from the electronic medical records. Given its retrospective nature and the use of anonymous clinical data, the need for informed consent was waived by the Ethical Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, which provided ethical approval for this study (Approval number: IIT2025369).

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS)

Following genomic DNA extraction, paired-end 150 bp sequencing was performed on the BGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI, China). De novo assembly of the raw reads was performed using Velvet v1.2.10 with a k-mer size of 55. Mutations were identified by aligning the assembled contigs against the reference strain PAO1 (NC_002516) using BLASTn v2.9.0. Antimicrobial resistance genes and virulence genes were predicted by the comprehensive antibiotic resistance database (CARD) and virulence factor database (VFDB), respectively. Serotypes of P. aeruginosa were predicted using Pseudomonas aeruginosa serotyper (PAst) 1.0 tool.

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) of P. aeruginosa

MLST analysis of 61 P. aeruginosa isolates was performed as described in the PubMLST database of P. aeruginosa through sequencing of seven housekeeping genes (acsA, aroE, guaA, mutL, nuoD, ppsA, and trpE). Gene sequences were then submitted to the PubMLST database (http://pubmlst.org/paeruginosa/) to determine allelic numbers and sequence types (STs). The types that could not match any known types were submitted to obtain new STs. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was inferred using FastTreev2.1.11 based on MLST data, with node support evaluated via 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Transcriptional levels of multidrug efflux pump genes in CRPA

To evaluate the correlation of multidrug efflux system and carbapenem resistance, the relative transcriptional levels of 4 mex transporter genes (corresponding to the efflux pumps MexAB-OprM, MexCD-OprJ, MexEF-OprN, and MexXY) in all 18 CRPA isolates were determined using qRT-PCR, with P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 as the reference strain. Total RNA of these strains was extracted and purified according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed on the QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The 2^-ΔΔCt relative quantification method was used for data analysis, with the housekeeping gene rspL serving as the internal reference to normalize the expression levels of target genes. Each sample was assayed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0. For categorical variables, the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for group comparisons. Regarding continuous variables, the t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test was applied for group comparisons. Selected variables with p values ≤ 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate analyses. For identifying predictors associated with the development of CRPA BSI, binary logistic regression was used. Concerning determining risk factors associated with the outcomes of CRPA BSI, Cox regression was employed. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to compare time-to-mortality differences. All statistical analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics v.23.0, with a p values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

In the univariate analysis comparing patients with CRPA vs. CSPA BSI, CRPA BSIs was more frequently associated with receipt of mechanical ventilation (p = 0.03), central venous catheter (p = 0.001) and parenteral nutrition (p = 0.003). When entered into a multivariate logistic analysis, central venous catheterization (OR 6.6; 95% CI 1.971–22.104; p = 0.002) was the only independent risk factor for development of CRPA BSI.

During the 30-day follow-up, death occurred in 50.8% (n = 31) of patients with P. aeruginosa BSI. When stratified by carbapenem susceptibility, the 7-day crude mortality rates were 37.2% for CSPA and 38.9% (p = 0.902) for CRPA, while the 30-day rates were 44.2% and 50% (p = 0.678), respectively. Further multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed three factors independently associated with both 7-day and 30-day mortality: prior exposure to carbapenem antibiotics, receipt of mechanical ventilation, and low hemoglobin levels (Table 2 ).

Antibiotic resistance spectrum and resistance genes profiles of P. aeruginosa isolates

The antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of 61 P. aeruginosa isolates are presented in Fig. 1. The 18 CRPA strains showed significant resistance, with 100% resistance to imipenem and 61.1% resistance to meropenem. Resistance rates to levofloxacin and ciprofloxacin among CRPA strains were 55.6% and 44.4%, respectively. In contrast, lower resistance rates were observed for cefepime (16.7%) and piperacillin/tazobactam (16.7%). The proportion of MDRPA (33.3%, p = 0.002) and DTRPA (16.7%, p = 0.023) strains among CRPA strains were significantly higher compared to CSPA strains.

Antimicrobial susceptibility of 61 P. aeruginosa isolates stratified by carbapenems susceptibility

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) identified a total of 115 AMR genes across 61 P. aeruginosa isolates, with substantial variation in the number of AMR genes carried by individual isolates. Genomic annotation demonstrated that 88.9% (n = 105) of these gene families represented intrinsic chromosomal determinants, including conserved β-lactamase-encoding genes (blaPDC, blaOXA-50, or blaOXA-50-like, etc.); specifically, each strain carried 2 to 4 such conserved β-lactamase genes. Thirteen exogenous AMR gene families were detected, displaying great variability in distribution: only 16.4% (n = 10) of the isolates harbored these exogenous gene familys, with individual carriers containing 0 to 7 genes each. Of particular interest, the presence of these exogenous AMR gene familys was significantly associated with carbapenem resistance (CR) and multidrug resistance (MDR) phenotypes. Among the 61 P. aeruginosa isolates, the most prevalent exogenous AMR genes were AAC(3)-IId (4.9%, 3/61), blaGES-5 (4.9%, 3/61), sul1 (4.9%, 3/61), tet(G) (6.6%, 4/61) and cmlA (8.2%, 5/61). Additionally, five transferable β-lactamase genes included blaKPC-99, blaKPC-2, blaGES-5, blaVEB-1 and blaNDM-5. Most of these β-lactamase genes are located in mobile resistance elements, promoting the spread of resistance traits (fig. 2).

Antibiotic resistance genes, virulence factor determinants, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing results of 61 P. aeruginosa isolates

WGS further revealed divergent carbapenem resistance mechanisms among the 18 CRPA isolates. Six isolates (33.3%) carried β-lactamase genes, including blaKPC-99, blaKPC-2, blaGES-5, blaVEB-1 and blaNDM-5, which exhibited high-level imipenem resistance (MIC ≥ 8 µg/mL). In contrast, the remaining 12 CRPA strains (66.7%) lacked detectable β-lactamase genes, suggesting non-carbapenemase-mediated resistance mechanisms.

Mutational inactivation of OprD is primarily responsible for the resistance to imipenem. Based on sequencing results, carbapenem susceptibility patterns in CRPA strains were stratified according to oprD mutational profiles (Table 3). Almost all CRPA strains exhibited amino acid substitution of oprD. Type 1 included strains with wild-type oprD or silent mutations (preserving amino acid sequences). Remarkably, four CRPA isolates in this group had oprD sequences identical to CSPA strains but remained carbapenem-resistant. Type 2 included strains with significant changes in the oprD amino acid sequence, caused by premature termination codons or frameshift mutations. All isolates in this group demonstrated high-level imipenem resistance (MIC ≥ 8 µg/mL), producing truncated OprD proteins. Among them, clusters 12–14 had oprD point mutations leading to premature translation termination, while clusters15-19 had frameshift mutations in oprD due to nucleotide insertions or deletions. Overall, 44.4% of CRPA strains carried truncated/extended oprD genes (912–1425 bp) instead of the intact 1332-bp form. However, three CRPA strains were excluded from the Type 1 and Type 2 classification because oprD was not detected in their genome sequences.

Multidrug efflux pumps are key to carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa through extrusion of antimicrobial agents. Using RT-qPCR for gene expression analysis, 61.1% (11/18) of CRPA strains showed significant overexpression (≥ 2-fold change) of efflux pumps (Fig. 3). 50.0% (n = 9) of CRPA strains exhibited increased mexY transcription mRNA levels (2.1- to 38.6-fold). 38.9% (n = 7) of CRPA strains showed downregulated efflux pumps transcription; two of these carried oprD mutations or downregulation, which likely contributes to their carbapenem resistance. However, no known resistance determinants were identified in 22.2% (n = 4) of the CRPA strains.

Virulence of exoU-positive in CRPA strains

WGS was employed to the intersection of virulence and antimicrobial resistance by genotyping exoU across the 61 P. aeruginosa isolates. The prevalence of exoU showed no significant difference between CRPA and CSPA strains, but a strong association was found when comparing MDRPA with non-MDRPA strains (p = 0.007). Among 17 isolates with oprD mutations, four strains co-harbored exoU+. This proportion was significantly higher compared to that in strains with wild-type oprD phenotypes (p = 0.032). Notably, among these four isolates carrying the oprD mutation and exoU, three also carried blaOXA-488, blaPDC-35, and blaGES-5. These strains exhibited high level carbapenem resistance, with MIC values for imipenem and meropenem both exceeding 8 µg/mL, suggesting a correlation between carbapenem resistance and virulence factor in P. aeruginosa (Table 4).

Epidemiological analysis of P. aeruginosa isolates

MLST was performed on the 61 P. aeruginosa isolates to analyze their clonal relationships. The results revealed high clonal diversity, with 47 known STs identified among 59 isolates, and 2 new STs among 2 isolates. The most common STs were ST244 (n = 4, 6.5%), ST235 (n = 3, 4.9%), and ST274 (n = 3, 4.9%). Phylogenetic tree of STs revealed 3 clonal complexes (CCs) among the 61 isolates: the largest CC contained 9 STs (ST1233, ST244, ST1129, ST3755, ST970, ST884, ST480, ST1339, and ST708) with 13 strains, while the remaining 2 CCs each included only 2 STs. Of the total 49 STs, 39 were singleton types. CRPA strains displayed sporadic distribution and demonstrated distant genetic relationships (Fig. 4).

All 61 P. aeruginosa isolates were serotyped using PAst 1.0 based on sequencing data. The most prevalent serotypes were O6 (n = 16, 26.2%) and O11 (n = 12, 19.6%). When comparing resistance groups, the proportion of O11 is significantly higher in CRPA than in CSPA (p = 0.02); this trend was also observed when comparing MDRPA and non-MDRPA (p = 0.004). Discussion

The World Health Organization (WHO) established a priority list for antibiotic-resistant bacteria in 2016, aimed at supporting research and development of effective drugs. As expected, CRPA was listed as one of the critical-priority tiers [19]. In 2023, WHO updated reclassified CRPA to the High-priority tier. This reclassification reflects partial progress in global antimicrobial resistance containment. It also highlights CRPA’s ongoing clinical threat: its resistance mechanisms continue to evolve, making updated epidemiological surveillance and regional strain characterization necessary. In this study, we analyzed the clinical prevalence, epidemiology and carbapenem resistance mechanisms of 61P. aeruginosa isolates collected from a tertiary hospital in east China between 2021 and 2023. It was found that elderly male patients in ICU and hematology wards were identified as high-risk populations. Central venous catheterization emerged as the sole independent risk factor for CRPA BSI development (OR 6.6; 95% CI 1.971–22.104; p = 0.002). Prognostic analysis identified three key factors: mechanical ventilation, low hemoglobin levels, and prior carbapenem exposure. All three significantly increased 7- and 30-day mortality rates. These findings are consistent with existing evidence showing that inappropriate initial antibiotic therapy is linked to higher mortality in P. aeruginosa BSIs [18]. Our findings underscore the importance of judicious carbapenem prescribing to mitigate the incidence of CRPA infections.

Although carbapenemase remains a relatively uncommon mechanism in P. aeruginosa, it is becoming an increasingly significant cause of carbapenem resistance [20]. Among the CRPA strains collected in Guangdong, the positive rate of carbapenemase genes was 22.9% (n = 16, 16/70), with blaIMP and blaVIM being the most prevalent [21]; Another study on global CRPA collections showed that 33% of strains carry carbapenemases, but this proportion varied significantly between regions, ranging from 11 to 68%, with blaVIM and blaGES being common [22]. In our study, 29.5% (n = 18, 18/61) isolates were CRPA strains. Among these CRPA strains, the positive rate for β-lactamases was 33.3% (n = 6, 6/18), with blaGES−5 being relatively common. Although the worldwide prevalence of GES carbapenemase is rather low, we believe that differences in geographical regions between studies might explain these disparate findings. Other detected β-lactamase genes included blaKPC−99, blaKPC−2, blaVEB−1, and blaNDM−5, though blaKPC−99 and blaVEB−1 have not yet been confirmed to hydrolyze carbapenems in existing papers. In this study, all strains carrying blaKPC−99 and blaVEB−1 showed high-level imipenem resistance. This may hint at their potential function in carbapenem hydrolysis, though further verification is needed.

All CRPA strains in our study demonstrated resistance to imipenem, only 61.1% exhibited concurrent resistance to meropenem. This differential resistance profile suggests that carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa is mediated through multiple distinct mechanisms rather than solely through carbapenemase production. opr Dmutations are common among global collections of CRPA strains. Reyes et al. [23] found that 10% CRPA isolates collected from 44 hospitals in 10 countries were identified to harboroprD mutations. In a study of burn patients in southwestern China, 125 (88.65%) isolates were found to have various inactivating mutations in oprD, which may result in truncated or abnormal proteins [24]. In our study, loss or reduced OprD confers resistance to carbapenem in P. aeruginosa isloated from BSIs. Compared with the OprD protein of PAO1, numerous amino acid substitutions were observed in the deduced OprD amino acid sequences of CRPA strains. Some of these substitutions led to premature termination codons or frameshift mutations (Type 2), while others showed the same amino acid substitution patterns as CSPA but exhibited carbapenem resistance (Type 1). These results suggest that these frequent combined amino acid substitutions in oprD may be associated with carbapenem resistance, but will not inevitably lead to it. This discrepancy may be attributed to the differences between the standard strain PAO1 and clinical isolates.

In combination with the expression of efflux pump genes, we also observed an interesting phenomenon: strains carrying oprD mutations highly overlapped with those with efflux pump overexpression. The high proportion of overlap between efflux pump overexpression (especially mexY) and oprD mutations reinforces their synergistic effect, which reduces drug intake and accelerates drug efflux. Although β-lactamases in this study had a more significant effect on increasing the MIC values of carbapenems, the potential synergistic mechanism between oprD and efflux pumps should not be overlooked. It can enhance the survival ability of bacteria in environments with subinhibitory concentrations of antibiotics, serving as a critical transitional stage in the evolution toward high-level drug resistance.

Acquired resistance can sometimes lower a strain’s fitness and virulence, easing disease severity and mortality [25–27]. However, this effect varies with the strain’s genetic background [27]. P. aeruginosa pathogenicity depends not just on how it adapts to antimicrobial resistance [28, 29], but also on factors like TTSS and O-antigen serotype. In this study, CRPA strains were more likely to carry exoU than CSPA, linking carbapenem resistance to higher virulence. O11, mostly from ICU, often showed MDR and carried exoU+/exoS⁻. However, the majority of O11 strains in our study also carried exoU. This makes it hard to tell whether O11 itself is a virulence factor or just a marker for dangerous strains. Definitive conclusions that O11 is associated with poorer outcomes should be treated with caution.

Several factors may limit the generalizability of these findings: first, the number of P. aeruginosa BSI cases encountered at our center is relatively small, which directly limited the number of strains included in this study. Thus, the results may not apply universally. Second, the selection of resistance genes and virulence genes in this study was based on our previous research findings and relevant paper, involving subjective judgment.

Our study revealed the BSI rate of P. aeruginosa is continuously rising, and its drug resistance characteristics is also deteriorating. This highlights the urgent need for systematic monitoring of P. aeruginosa blood culture to guide evidence-based antimicrobial therapy. Thereafter, our ongoing research will focuse on clinical isolates with similar genetic backgrounds but different carbapenem resistance and analyze the possible mechanisms underlying the carbapenem resistance development.

Conclusion

Our study identified oprD mutations and efflux pumps hyperexpression as dominant drivers of carbapenem resistance in CRPA strains, with carbapenemase emerging as a clinically significant pathway. The convergence of these resistance mechanisms with high-risk clinical factors underscores urgent implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs.
