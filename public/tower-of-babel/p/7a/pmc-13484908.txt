Dietary guidelines adherence and pregnancy exposure to 10 classes of priority chemicals: an observational study in the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes Cohort
Authors: Diana C Pacyga, Xiaoshuang Xun, Deborah H Bennett, Michael S Bloom, Joseph M Braun, Courtney C Carignan, Caitlin G Howe, Margaret R Karagas, Kurunthachalam Kannan, John D Meeker, Melissa M Melough, Rachel Morello-Frosch, Wei Perng, Christina A Porucznik, Lesliam Quirós-Alcalá, Zorimar Rivera-Núñez, Sheela Sathyanarayana, Rebecca J Schmidt, Rita S Strakovsky, Ana K Rosen Vollmar, Yeyi Zhu, Tracey J Woodruff, Jessie P Buckley, P Brian Smith, P Brian Smith, L Kristin Newby, Linda Adair, Lisa P Jacobson, Diane Catellier, Monica McGrath, Christian Douglas, Priya Duggal, Emily A Knapp, Amii M Kress, Courtney K Blackwell, Maxwell A Mansolf, Jin-Shei Lai, Emily Ho, David Cella, Richard Gershon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiet is a source of environmental toxicants; however, chemical exposures are not considered in the United States Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA).ObjectivesWe tested whether higher DGA adherence was associated with gestational chemical exposures.MethodsPregnant participants in the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes Cohort completed a food frequency questionnaire or 24-h recall at a median of 21 wk of gestation, which we used to calculate the Healthy Eating Index (HEI)-2015 total and individual component scores to assess DGA adherence. In spot urine samples collected at a median of 22 wk of gestation, we measured 113 analytes representing 10 chemical classes, including insecticides, fungicides/herbicides, organophosphate esters (OPEs), halogenated phenols, benzophenones, bisphenols, parabens, antimicrobials, phthalates/alternatives, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). Using Bayesian generalized linear mixed-effects regression, we estimated covariate-adjusted percentage differences (β; biomarkers with ≥70% detection) or detection risk ratios (biomarkers with 30%–69% detection) and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) in urinary chemical biomarker concentrations for each 10-point increase in HEI-2015 total score. We used quantile-based g-computation to identify the top HEI-2015 component contributors to associations.ResultsMost participants (n = 1492) self-identified as non-Hispanic White (40%) or Black (36%), and 50% had incomes ≥$50,000. The median (25th, 75th percentile) HEI-2015 score was 61.9 points (53.6, 70.2; of 100). We detected 53 analytes, representing biomarkers of 45 chemical parent compounds, in >30% of participants. Higher HEI-2015 scores were associated with lower OPE, halogenated phenol, bisphenol, phthalate, and PAH biomarker concentrations, most notably monobenzyl phthalate (β: –13.5%; 95% CrI: –21.0%, –5.8%). However, higher HEI-2015 scores were also associated with higher insecticide, paraben, and benzophenone biomarker concentrations, most notably benzophenone-3 (β: 16.2%; 95% CrI: 5.9%, 26.8%). Lower added sugar and higher protein, vegetable, fruit, and whole-grain intake were consistent predictors of lower or higher chemical biomarker concentrations.ConclusionsGreater DGA adherence may minimize exposure to some, but not all, toxicants.

Introduction

Pregnancy dietary guidelines promote optimal nutrient intake to support maternal and child health [1], but the extent to which these diets influence prenatal exposure to environmental chemicals is not well-understood. Pregnancy involves higher energy requirements for fetal growth and maternal tissue expansion [2]. Beyond energy intake, healthy dietary patterns reduce the risk of pregnancy hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes, preterm birth, and fetal growth problems [1,3]. However, diet is a major source of reproductive and developmental toxicants to which pregnant persons are ubiquitously exposed [[4], [5], [6]]. In a recent study from the nationwide Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Cohort, 73 of >100 chemicals measured in urine were detected in ≥1 pregnant person, including those with known dietary sources, such as pesticides, phthalates, bisphenols, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and organophosphate esters (OPEs) [7]. These chemicals enter the food supply through agricultural, production, processing, packaging, and cooking practices [4,8]. Their widespread presence is concerning because some may disrupt critical hormonal, immune, and metabolic pathways [9,10], and increase the risk of adverse lifelong maternal and child health outcomes [[11], [12], [13]]. Recommendations are needed to promote nutritious diets during pregnancy while minimizing toxic chemical exposures.

Prior research on diet and chemicals has identified specific food sources and broader dietary patterns associated with chemical exposures, but evidence remains restricted to well-studied chemicals. Food monitoring and observational studies indicate that consuming foods that are ultraprocessed [14,15], high in saturated fat/added sugar [4,15], or packaged in plastic/cans [4,8,16] may increase exposure to phthalates and bisphenols. In contrast, diets higher in fruits and vegetables may increase exposure to pesticides [17]. The complexity in dietary chemical exposures necessitates studies examining associations between dietary patterns, rather than individual foods, with a comprehensive panel of chemicals. As reviewed by Corbett et al. [18], dietary pattern interventions in nonpregnant adults involving consumption of organic, fresh foods and limiting the intake of fast foods significantly reduced exposure to bisphenol A, along with certain phthalates and pesticides. More recently, small observational studies in pregnant persons have applied established diet quality indices to evaluate the impact of dietary patterns on chemical exposures [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. However, these studies largely focused on phthalates and PAHs, limiting understanding of how pregnancy diets influence exposure to a broader range of toxicants [7].

Dietary patterns that align with existing pregnancy recommendations may clarify how maternal diet influences exposure to multiple contemporary and understudied environmental chemicals. To meet pregnancy energy and nutrient demands, the United States Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA) encourage the consumption of nutrient-dense foods, such as vegetables, fruits, whole grains, dairy, protein, and foods high in essential fatty acids, while limiting the intake of refined grains and foods high in sodium, added sugar, and saturated fats [23]. Consumption of many of these components has been individually linked to chemical exposures [4,5,15,24]. The Healthy Eating Index (HEI) is a validated measure that quantifies DGA adherence [25] and provides a policy-relevant framework to assess whether existing pregnancy nutrition guidance can also be leveraged to modify dietary chemical exposures. Our primary objective was to evaluate associations between HEI scores and pregnancy exposure to over 100 contemporary and understudied chemicals. Our secondary objective was to identify which HEI components were most strongly associated with chemical exposures. On the basis of known dietary sources, we hypothesized that associations of DGA adherence with chemical exposures would differ by class.

Methods

Study participants

This study used data from the ECHO Cohort, a United States consortium comprising >60,000 children [26,27]. Supplemental Figure 1 presents how the analytic sample was derived. Of 20,584 pregnant participants from 21 ECHO study sites (enrolled from 2006 to 2020 and followed until delivery), 6107 provided urine samples that were assayed for chemical biomarkers. Because of cost constraints, each study site selected participants for chemical assays according to their own criteria, such as remaining urine volumes and longitudinal child follow-up [[28], [29]]. Of these, 1539 had complete diet data. We excluded participants who were missing urine dilution data and those from 2 sites that each contributed <30 participants. The final analytic sample included 1492 pregnant persons from 6 sites located in Northern and Southern California, Illinois, Michigan, Tennessee, and Georgia (Supplemental Table 1). The study protocol was approved by the single ECHO institutional review board (IRB), WCG IRB. Written informed consent was obtained for participation in the ECHO Cohort Data and Biospecimen Collection Protocol and participation at specific study sites. The Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health IRB approved the involvement of the ECHO Data Analysis Center (DAC). The Epidemiology-Nutritional Epidemiology (STROBE-nut) guidelines for observational studies were followed in the reporting of this study [30,31].

Quantification of urinary chemical biomarker concentrations

Each participant provided 1 urine sample at median 22 wks of gestation (25th percentile: 18 wks, 75th percentile: 24 wks). Urine was shipped on dry ice to the Wadsworth Center’s Human Health Exposure Analysis Resource (HHEAR) laboratory for simultaneous quantification of 113 biomarkers, representing parent chemicals or their metabolites, using an ExionLC system (SCIEX) coupled to an AB SCIEX QTRAP 5500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems) equipped with an electrospray ionization source as described previously [7,[29], [32]]. The HHEAR laboratory implements rigorous quality control and assurance protocols, including reagent blanks, matrix blanks, matrix spikes, QC pools, and Standard Reference Materials [33], with interassay and intraassay coefficients of variation ≤20% for most urinary chemical biomarkers [28,[29], [32]]. Measured biomarkers (number) represent the following 10 chemical classes: insecticides (20), fungicides/herbicides (11), OPEs (10), halogenated phenols (5), benzophenones (6), bisphenols (14), parabens (6), antimicrobials (2), phthalates/alternatives (32), and PAHs (7) (Supplemental Table 2). Because some chemical biomarkers could not be individually quantified, the following 7 biomarkers reflect the composites of multiple biomarkers: TCP245/TCP246 (a composite of 2,4,5-trichlorophenol and 2,4,6-trichlorophenol), DBUP/DiBP (a composite of dibutyl phosphate and di-isobutyl phosphate), TnBP/TiBP (a composite of tri-n-butyl phosphate and tri-isobutyl phosphate), MnBP/MiBP (a composite of mono-isobutyl phthalate and mono-n-butyl phthalate), PHEN1/PHEN9 (a composite of 1-hydroxphenanthrene and 9-hydroxyphenanthrene), PHEN2/PHEN3 (a composite of 2-hydroxyphenanthrene and 3-hydroxyphenanthrene), and FLUO2/3/9 (a composite of 2-hydroxyfluorene, 3-hydroxyfluorene, and 9-hydroxyfluorene).

Maternal diet collection and calculation of the HEI-2015 scores

As described previously [34,35], information about dietary intake was collected via either the Block-Bodnar Food Frequency Questionnaire for Women of Reproductive Age (Block FFQ) or the Automated Self-Administered 24-h Dietary Assessment Tool (ASA-24). Both have been implemented in studies including reproductive-age and pregnant females [[36], [37], [38]]. Different instruments were used because some study sites enrolled participants and collected data before joining the ECHO Cohort, whereas other sites began collecting data under a common ECHO-wide Cohort Data Collection Protocol [27]. Four study sites (Tennessee, Illinois, Georgia, and Northern California) used the Block FFQ (n = 1181), whereas 2 study sites (Southern California and Michigan) collected diet data via the ASA-24 (n = 311). The Block FFQ assessed the habitual intake of over 100 food items since becoming pregnant and included 9 intake frequency options and 3 portion size options per food item based on the NHANES II or III dietary recall [36,39]. Nutrient information from the ASA-24 was derived from the reported intake of all foods consumed within a 24-h period in pregnancy. At some study sites, participants completed multiple dietary assessments in pregnancy. Given that the chemicals of interest in this study are typically metabolized and excreted within 24 to 48 h of exposure, the diet assessment collected closest to the urine collection was prioritized to correspond to the relevant chemical exposure window. In cases where diet collection did not occur around the same time as urine collection, we averaged multiple recalls if available. Diet data were collected at median 21 wks of gestation (25th percentile: 14 wks, 75th percentile: 26 wks).

Block FFQ and ASA-24 data were used to calculate the HEI-2015 total score using the simple algorithm method as defined and described by the National Cancer Institute [40]. The HEI-2015 is a 13-component index (scored out of 100 points) that measures adherence to the 2015–2020 DGA, where each individual dietary component is standardized to 1000 kcal to account for the varying energy intake and scored out of 5 or 10 points [25]. The index consists of 9 adequacy components (total fruits, whole fruits, total vegetables, greens and beans, total protein foods, seafood and plant proteins, whole grains, total dairy, and fatty acids), and higher scores for these components reflect higher consumption. In addition, the index includes 4 moderation components (refined grains, sodium, saturated fats, and added sugars) that are reverse scored, with higher scores representing lower consumption. A higher HEI-2015 total score reflects better overall diet quality and greater adherence to the 2015–2020 DGA. The HEI-2015 has been previously used in pregnant populations [41].

Maternal sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics

Participants self-reported information about their sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics in pregnancy. Harmonized covariate information was available for maternal age in years, prepregnancy BMI in kg/m2, and parity. Race and ethnicity, which are social constructs reflecting differences in lived experiences and structural factors relevant to nutrition research, were harmonized by the ECHO DAC using information collected by each study site using their own protocols. For descriptive purposes, we used a combined variable for race and ethnicity that was harmonized to the following categories: Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian, and Other Race, including Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, American Indian or Alaska Native, Multiple Race, or participants who selected “Other Race.” We categorized the highest educational attainment using 5 categories (less than high school, high school/General Educational Development or equivalent, some college or associate’s degree, bachelor’s degree, and graduate degree), marital status using 3 categories (married/living with a partner, widowed/separated/divorced, and single), and annual household income using 4 categories (<$30,000, $30,000–$49,999, $50,000–$74,999, and ≥$75,000). Season and year, which may be related to temporal variation in diet and chemical exposures, were extracted from the date of urine collection. We categorized self-reported alcohol intake and tobacco use in pregnancy as “yes” or “no.”

Statistical analysis

Operationalization of urinary chemical biomarker concentrations

We used a tiered approach for biomarker concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD). The laboratory provided instrument values for concentrations <LOD when possible, and we used the reported instrument value if the instrument value was ≥0.01 ng/mL. If the instrument value was ≤0.01 ng/mL (including negative values), we replaced it with 0.01 ng/mL. If no instrument value was reported, we used the (LOD/√2) [42]. To account for hydration status, we adjusted all biomarker concentrations for specific gravity [43]. Although most biomarkers were evaluated individually, we created molar sums (nmol/mL) of urinary biomarkers originating from common precursors, specifically biomarkers of di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate metabolites (∑DEHP), di-(2-propylheptyl) phthalate, di-iso-decyl phthalate, diisononyl phthalate, di-n-octyl phthalate, and cyclohexane-1,2-dicarboxylic acid-diisononyl ester (Supplemental Table 2). The molar concentrations were back-converted to ng/mL by multiplying the molar sum by the mean molecular weight of the biomarkers comprising the sum. For biomarkers with detection frequencies ≥70%, we modeled sums and urinary biomarkers as specific gravity–corrected continuous outcomes that were natural log-transformed to improve model fit [44]. For biomarkers with detection frequencies of 30% to 69%, we modeled biomarkers as binary variables because instrument-derived values around the LOD are prone to higher uncertainty. Because of small cell sizes, we did not evaluate biomarkers with detection frequencies <30% beyond reporting basic descriptive statistics.

Descriptive statistics

The characteristics of the analytic sample are presented as n (%) or median (25th, 75th percentiles), including urinary chemical biomarker concentrations. In addition, we report the n (%) of pregnant participants with chemical biomarker concentrations ≥LOD. We summarize the HEI-2015 total score and individual component scores using the minimum, 25th, 50th, 75th, and maximum percentiles. We evaluated Spearman correlations among chemical biomarkers with detection frequencies ≥70% and between the HEI-2015 total scores and the 13 individual component scores.

We assessed the bivariate associations of each covariate with the HEI-2015 total score, its individual component scores, and chemical biomarker levels by comparing the median (25th, 75th percentile) values across covariate categories. We checked for potential multicollinearity by confirming that covariates were only weakly or moderately correlated (r ≤ |0.40|). All covariate-adjusted models accounted for confounding by maternal age and prepregnancy BMI as continuous variables along with educational attainment, marital status, season of urine collection, tobacco use in pregnancy, and alcohol intake in pregnancy as categorical variables. These covariates were chosen as proxies for socioeconomic status (maternal age, educational attainment, marital status), healthy lifestyle (prepregnancy BMI, tobacco use, alcohol intake), and seasonal variations in chemical exposures and diet (season of urine collection). Marital status was strongly related to annual household income (polychoric r > 0.80) [45], which we did not include because of substantial missingness (22%). We did not adjust for race and ethnicity or year of sample collection due to the limited overlap of these factors across study sites. These factors are inherently accounted for in our main modeling approach, which includes the study site as a random effect.

Main analytic statistics

To address our main objective of evaluating the association between the HEI-2015 total score (independent variable) and urinary chemical biomarker concentrations (dependent variables), we used Bayesian generalized linear mixed models (R package blme) [46,47]. We used the Bayesian modeling framework with package default priors to improve model stability and convergence after experiencing convergence challenges with traditional frequentist methods for some models. We modeled the HEI-2015 total score as a continuous untransformed variable. We modeled continuous ln-transformed specific gravity–adjusted chemical biomarkers (≥70% detection) in Bayesian mixed models assuming a Gaussian likelihood. The resulting β-estimates and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were back-transformed using [(e(β∗10)–1)∗100] to represent the percentage difference (%Δ) in chemical biomarker concentration for each 10-point increase in HEI-2015 total score. We modeled dichotomized chemical biomarkers (30%–69% detection frequency) in Bayesian mixed models with a Poisson distribution. We back-transformed the resulting risk ratios (RRs) and 95% CrIs using [(e(log(RR)∗10)] to represent the risk of having chemical biomarker concentrations ≥LOD compared with <LOD for each 10-point increase in HEI-2015 total score. We evaluated crude and covariate-adjusted models. We included a random intercept for study site in all models to account for within-site clustering. All other covariates were included as fixed effects. We imputed missing covariate data (<6% missing) with multiple imputations by chained equations using m = 50 imputed datasets, each generated after 5 iterations to ensure convergence (R package mice; R Studio version 4.4.0; R Core Team 2024) [48]. We evaluated regression diagnostics based on residuals to ensure that all model assumptions were met. To identify notable findings, we evaluated the strength and uncertainty of the resulting posterior effect estimates, along with the consistency of associations within each chemical class. We considered associations noteworthy when the 95% CrI excluded or minimally crossed the null, reflecting a high posterior probability (limited uncertainty) in the association direction.

Secondary analyses

Participants may achieve similar HEI-2015 total scores through different dietary patterns, which may explain any heterogeneity in the associations with chemical exposures. In addition, diet data are often correlated because individuals consume combinations of foods and nutrients. Therefore, we jointly examined individual HEI-2015 components to address our second objective of identifying which components were driving associations between the total score and urinary chemical biomarker levels. We kept the 2 vegetable and fruit components separate for consistency and to capture differences in dietary chemical exposures because greens/beans have been shown to have the highest pesticide residues among vegetables, and total fruit additionally includes packaged fruit juices [49]. We used quantile-based g-computation (QGComp; R package qgcomp: Quantile G-Computation) [50], an approach used to evaluate complex mixtures. QGComp estimates the total effect on the outcome of simultaneously increasing a set of correlated exposures by 1 quartile (psi), partial effects in the positive and negative directions (these partial effects sum to the total effect, psi), and positive and negative weights that represent the relative influence of each mixture component on the partial effects. Because not all HEI-2015 components are scored out of a maximum of 10 points (some are scored out of 5 points), we first transformed all 13 HEI-2015 component scores into quartiles to create the standardized diet mixture index. We generated results with 1000 bootstraps to obtain the point and interval estimates for the combined effect of all HEI-2015 components, along with the point estimates for the contribution of the individual HEI-2015 components to partial positive and negative associations. The resulting overall psi estimate and 95% confidence interval can be interpreted as the %Δ (Gaussian likelihood) or RR (Poisson distribution) in urinary chemical biomarker levels if all HEI-2015 component scores simultaneously increased by 1 quartile. We also specified models without bootstrapping to obtain the weights for the contribution of each HEI-2015 component to either the partial positive or negative association. Top components were those that had median weights >20% and contributed to a stronger partial effect. For example, if the partial negative effect was stronger than the partial positive effect, the top HEI-2015 components were those with weights for the negative partial effect >20%.

Sensitivity analyses

We performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we compared our primary Bayesian approach to a frequentist generalized linear mixed model framework with the appropriate distribution (i.e., Gaussian for continuous outcomes, Poisson for binary outcomes) [47] to ensure that results were consistent. Second, most prior studies have examined the relationship between diet and chemical biomarkers by modeling food or nutrients individually. In contrast to our QGComp results, we also specified models that evaluated each individual HEI-2015 component separately (i.e., 1 at a time) with urinary chemical biomarker concentrations. Third, to address concerns regarding temporality, we specified Bayesian generalized linear mixed models excluding participants who provided urine before diet collection (21%). Fourth, given that diet was collected using different instruments (FFQ compared with 24-h recall), we specified models excluding participants who provided diet information via a 24-h recall (Southern California, Michigan sites). Fifth, we evaluated models where we standardized HEI-2015 total scores within each diet instrument to determine whether systematic differences in diet collection methods influenced our primary findings. Finally, we conducted leave-one-site-out analyses, excluding participants from 1 site at a time, to determine the influence of each site on the main results.

Results

Characteristics of the analytic sample

We included 1492 pregnant participants from 6 sites across the United States (Supplemental Figure 1 and Supplemental Table 1). Participants had a median age of 30 y and a prepregnancy BMI of 26 kg/m2 (Table 1). Most participants were college-educated (63%); married or living with a partner (71%); had annual household incomes >$50,000 (50%); and self-identified as non-Hispanic White (40%), non-Hispanic Black (36%), or Hispanic (17%). Approximately 7% used tobacco and 20% consumed alcohol during their pregnancy. The timing of urine collection, which occurred around 22 wks of gestation, was evenly distributed across seasons, and 79% of samples were collected between 2009 and 2020 (Table 1). Distributions of HEI-2015 total score and individual component scores

The median HEI-2015 total score was 61.9 of 100 possible points, and the median total energy intake was 1894 kcals/d (Supplemental Table 4). The median scores were generally high for total vegetables, greens and beans, total fruit, whole fruit, total protein, seafood and plant protein, and total dairy (reflecting higher intakes), as well as refined grain and added sugar (reverse scored, reflecting lower intakes), which was consistent with greater guideline adherence (Supplemental Table 4). In contrast, the median scores were lower for whole grains and fatty acids (reflecting lower intakes), along with sodium and saturated fats (reverse scored, reflecting higher intakes), indicating lower adherence to guidelines. Some dietary components were moderately to strongly correlated (r > 0.50), including total vegetables with greens and beans, total fruit with whole fruit, and fatty acids with added sugar (Supplemental Figure 2). Supplemental Table 5 presents the HEI-2015 total score and individual component scores by participant characteristics.

Distributions of urinary chemical biomarker concentrations

A total of 53 analytes, representing biomarkers of 45 chemical parent compounds, were detected in >30% of urine samples from participants and had sufficient detection frequencies to be included in subsequent analyses (Figure 1). 3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (insecticide metabolite), benzophenone 1 (BP1), methyl paraben (MePB), and phthalate metabolites mono (2-ethyl-5-hydroxyhexyl) phthalate, mono (2-ethyl-5-oxohexyl) phthalate, monoethyl phthalate (MEP), and MnBP/MiBP were detected in all samples (Supplemental Table 2). The highest median concentrations were observed for MePB (81.0 ng/mL), MEP (56.1 ng/mL), benzophenone 3 (BP3, 44.5 ng/mL), ∑DEHP (30.5 ng/mL), and MnBP/MiBP (27.5 ng/mL). Except for fungicides/herbicides and OPEs, most biomarkers exhibited moderate-to-strong correlations within chemical classes, whereas correlations between classes were weaker (Supplemental Figure 3). Supplemental Tables 6 and 7 present the concentrations of biomarkers detected in ≥70% of samples and the detection frequencies of biomarkers found in 30% to 69% of samples, respectively, by participant characteristics. Associations between HEI-2015 total score and urinary chemical biomarker concentrations

In crude models, the HEI-2015 total score was inversely associated with 16 urinary chemical biomarkers and positively associated with 5 chemical biomarkers (Supplemental Table 8). Several associations remained after accounting for study site, maternal age, prepregnancy BMI, educational attainment, marital status, alcohol intake, tobacco use, and season of urine collection (Figure 2). Specifically, each 10-point increase in the total score, indicating higher adherence to the DGA, was associated with 7.3% to 13.5% lower urinary biomarker concentrations for the following chemicals: one halogenated phenol, pentachlorophenol (PCP); one OPE, diphenyl phosphate (DPP); one bisphenol, bisphenol S (BPS); two phthalates, mono-benzyl phthalate (MBzP) and MnBP/MiBP; and four PAHs, 2-hydroxynaphthalene (NAP2), PHEN1/PHEN9, 1-hydroxypyrene (PYR1), and PHEN2/PHEN3. Sensitivity analyses

The main associations between the HEI-2015 total score and urinary chemical biomarker concentrations were consistent across Bayesian and frequentist methods, thereby demonstrating agreement between statistical approaches (Supplemental Table 8). The associations between individual HEI-2015 dietary components and urinary biomarker concentrations estimated in models that were not mutually adjusted are presented in Supplemental Table 10. The top contributors identified through QGComp differed from the individual components that were associated with chemical biomarkers in nonmutually adjusted models. For example, although total protein was the top contributor to the inverse association of the HEI-2015 total score with PCP, DPP, BPS, MBzP, MnBP/MiBP, PHEN1/PHEN9, PYR1, and PHEN2/PHEN3, in the QGComp results, total protein was only inversely associated with MBzP, MnBP/MiBP, and PHEN2/PHEN3 in nonmutually adjusted analyses. When participants who provided urine samples before the dietary assessment or diet information through a 24-h recall were excluded, the results remained consistent with stronger effect estimates (Supplemental Table 11). Our main results also did not change when modeling HEI-2015 total scores standardized within each diet instrument (Supplemental Table 11). Excluding individual study sites 1 at a time yielded consistent association directions and generally similar effect sizes, although removal of the Tennessee site, which accounted for over 40% of the total sample, resulted in less precise estimates (Supplemental Table 12).

Discussion

In support of our hypothesis, higher HEI-2015 scores in pregnancy were associated with lower urinary concentrations of several halogenated phenol, OPE, bisphenol, phthalate, and PAH biomarkers. However, higher HEI-2015 scores were also associated with higher urinary concentrations of some insecticide, benzophenone, and paraben biomarkers. This indicates that adherence to the DGA in pregnancy, which reflects a healthier dietary pattern, was associated with lower exposure to some, but not all, chemical classes. In secondary analyses of HEI-2015 components, total protein, whole grain, whole fruit, and/or total vegetables contributed most to lower halogenated phenol, OPE, bisphenol, phthalate, and PAH biomarker concentrations, whereas total protein, greens and beans, whole fruit, and added sugar were top contributors to greater insecticide, benzophenone, and paraben biomarker levels. This study offers novel evidence in a large, socioeconomically and geographically diverse United States population that pregnancy DGA adherence can reduce exposure to some environmental chemicals.

Evaluating the HEI-2015 in relation to urinary chemical biomarkers allowed us to model real-world dietary patterns with a validated, policy-relevant measure [23,25], and explore whether existing dietary recommendations may offer a practical approach to modifying chemical exposures. Higher HEI-2015 scores were associated with lower urinary halogenated phenol (PCP), OPE (DPP), bisphenol (BPS), phthalate (MBzP, MnBP/MiBP), and PAH (NAP2, PHEN1/PHEN9, PYR1, PHEN2/PHEN3) biomarker concentrations, a protective pattern reported in prior pregnancy studies [19,21,22]. For example, among pregnant persons from Los Angeles, California (n = 125), higher HEI-2015 scores were associated with lower urinary levels of early-pregnancy PAH biomarkers NAP2, PHEN1, and PYR1 (consistent with our findings), but also a composite of 2-hydroxyfluorene and 3-hydroxyfluorene (not consistent, limited detection in our study) [21]. Pregnancy studies using the Alternative HEI, Mediterranean diet score, or the Nova index (classifying foods by degree of processing) have reported similar patterns with urinary OPE, phthalate, and PAH biomarkers, some of which were not included in our chemical panel [19,21,22]. For example, in cohorts from the Midwestern United States (n = 295) and Spain (n = 640), higher diet quality or lower ultraprocessed food intake was associated with lower concentrations of phthalates (∑DEHP, di-2-ethylhexyl terephthalate, MEP), OPEs (DPP, diethyl phosphate), or PAHs (NAP2) [19,22]. Beyond pregnancy, a national study of United States adults (n = 6862) reported no association of diet quality with bisphenols, phthalates, and PAHs [51]. United States pregnant persons (similar to those in the ECHO Cohort) report higher HEI-2015 scores than nonpregnant adult females (mean 63 compared with 54 points) [23]. Differences in DGA adherence, representing variation in the intake of foods that serve as chemical exposure sources, may explain the inconsistent results between these studies.

To provide insight into which aspects of this dietary pattern may influence urinary chemical biomarker concentrations, we modeled all 13 individual HEI-2015 component scores simultaneously in a single model using a supervised statistical approach to identify the top independent food component contributors. Associations of higher HEI-2015 scores and lower urinary OPE, halogenated phenol, phthalate, and PAH biomarker concentrations were consistently driven by higher total protein scores (reflecting higher intake). The 2 exceptions were NAP2 and BPS, where lower urinary levels were primarily driven by higher whole grain, total vegetables, and/or whole fruit scores (reflecting higher intake). Our findings suggest that a pregnancy diet that meets protein, whole grain, vegetable, and fruit needs may reduce exposure to these chemicals. Such dietary patterns have been associated with lower consumption of ultraprocessed and packaged foods, which may contain halogenated phenols, OPEs, bisphenols, phthalates, and PAHs [14,16,[52], [53], [54]].

Despite mostly inverse associations, HEI-2015 scores were positively associated with urinary pyrethroid (TDCCA), neonicotinoid insecticide (NDMA), benzophenone (BP1, BP3), and paraben (EtPB) concentrations. These associations were driven by higher scores (reflecting higher intake) of total protein (NDMA, BP1, BP3), greens and beans (NDMA, TDCCA), and whole fruit (TDCCA, BP3, BP1), as well as higher scores (reflecting lower intake) of added sugar (TDCCA, EtPB). These findings suggest that pregnancy dietary patterns characterized by greater consumption of plants and protein-rich foods may also coincide with higher pesticide and phenol exposures. For pesticides, our results align with prior food monitoring studies that detected NDMA and TDCCA parent compounds in eggs, meat, and nonorganic fresh produce [17,18,55,56]. Associations with EtPB are contradictory, given its permitted use as a preservative in confectionery, jams, and beverages [[56], [57], [58]]. Because the HEI-2015 does not differentiate organic from nonorganic foods or specific product formulations, we could not directly attribute observed associations to food production practices or product types. Sunscreen is a major source of BP1 and BP3 [59]; therefore, positive associations with benzophenones may reflect health-conscious lifestyle behaviors co-occurring with better diets. For the partially lipophilic compounds BP3, BP1, and EtPB [60,61], unexpectedly higher urinary concentrations with greater DGA adherence may also be due to lower gestational fat accretion, limiting sequestration in adipose tissue. However, further investigation is warranted because this explanation is not fully consistent with our findings for other partially lipophilic compounds (e.g., PAHs) [62], which were lower with greater HEI adherence, and evidence linking HEI to pregnancy weight gain remains mixed [[63], [64], [65], [66]].

This study has some limitations and several strengths. First, dietary assessment methods differed by study site, potentially introducing variability in recall bias, the time frame assessed, and the level of nutrient/food group details captured [67]. However, instruments were consistent within sites, and our conclusions remained largely unchanged when we excluded 1 site at a time. Second, although the HEI-2015 may not fully capture cultural or population-specific dietary patterns outside national guidelines that could influence chemical exposures, it provides a comprehensive and standardized measure of overall diet quality that is generalizable. Third, because these chemical biomarkers are metabolized and excreted within 24 to 48 h of exposure, a single urine measurement may not represent exposure throughout an entire pregnancy and may lead to misalignment between the timing of dietary assessment and urine collection, especially for diet data collected via FFQs [4,68]. However, most diet information was collected before (42%) or at the same time (37%) as urine, and associations became stronger when excluding participants who provided diet information after urine collection. Fourth, our findings with benzophenones and parabens could indicate residual confounding by other lifestyle behaviors, such as use of personal care products [59,69]. However, the absence of associations with other chemicals (MEP, a metabolite of a chemical used in fragranced personal care products, and triclosan, historically found in toothpaste and antibacterial soaps [70]) suggests that lifestyle factors do not fully explain these associations. Fifth, although we did not adjust for multiple comparisons, which may have led to some chance findings, this is not recommended for hypothesis-driven studies because they can increase type II error [71]. We qualitatively interpreted findings in the context of the existing literature and focused on identifying robust patterns. Finally, although not nationally representative [27], our study population was diverse in race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, making our results more generalizable than previous single-cohort studies.

To our knowledge, this was the largest United States-based pregnancy study to date that examined the associations between dietary patterns and a broad panel of chemicals, representing 10 classes, in pregnant persons from diverse geographic regions and socioeconomic backgrounds. Adherence to the national dietary guidelines supports lower exposure to some, but not all, chemicals. For chemicals whose concentrations were higher with greater DGA adherence, refinements to the dietary recommendations could help minimize pesticide and other chemical exposures. The current guidelines incorporate advice from the United States Food and Drug Administration and Environmental Protection Agency on selecting seafoods that support the intake of essential fatty acids while minimizing methylmercury exposure in pregnancy [23]. Similar recommendations could be developed for insecticides, such as advising pregnant persons to choose organic produce when possible or to wash fruits and vegetables thoroughly before consumption [55,72]. Although updated recommendations can help on the individual level, given the widespread exposure to many known reproductive and developmental toxicants, efforts should ultimately focus on reducing these contaminants throughout the food supply.
