Biochar and ZnO-Se nanocomposites enhanced biochemical and antioxidant profiling of soybean: insight into GC-MS metabolomics, and KEGG pathway analysis
Authors: Ubaidur Rahman, Maaz Ahmad, Zohaib Younas, Ilyas Ahmad, Mohammad Hamid Hamdard, Zia-ur-Rehman Mashwani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoybean (Glycine max L.) is a significant oilseed crop that is frequently limited by soil fertility, micronutrient deficiencies and oxidative stress. Although biochar and nano enable micronutrients have individually been reported to improve crop performance. There synergistic effect on the biochemical traits, antioxidants defense, and metabolic reactions of soybean have not yet been explored. This lack of knowledge gap affects the ability to develop integrated and sustainable nutrient management practices for producing soybean. Hence, we hypothesized that the synergistic effect of sugarcane bagasse-based biochar and green-synthesized zinc oxide–selenium nanocomposites (ZnO–Se NCs) would enhance soybean biochemical performance, antioxidant capacity and metabolite accumulation. For testing this hypothesis, a field experiment was carried out by using the biochar produced by the pyrolysis process at 450 °C and the foliar-applied ZnO–Se NCs synthesized from garlic extract. Biochemical properties, antioxidant activities, metabolite profile using GC–MS and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were assessed. The biochar blended with 40 ppm of ZnO–Se NCs had highest soluble protein content (3.73 ± 0.22 µg g⁻¹ FW), total flavonoids (187.3 ± 1.82 µg mL⁻¹ QE), total phenolics (70.64 ± 0.57 µg mL⁻¹ GAE), DPPH radical scavenging activity (48.46 ± 0.94%)), and total antioxidant activity (57.18 ± 1.45 mg AAE g⁻¹ FW) in comparison with the control. Biochar + 50 ppm ZnO–Se NC showed the most abundant total soluble sugar content (215.5 ± 2.9 µg mL⁻¹). GC–MS characterization revealed the presence of important bioactive metabolites, such as linoleic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, and dl-α-tocopherol, and KEGG enrichment showed that amino acid metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways were the most significantly enriched pathways. This study supports novel evidence for soil improvement using biochar, with ZnO–Se nano-enabled micronutrient delivery that can affect both the biochemical and antioxidant responses of soybean plants, and alter the soybean metabolites. The results indicate that biochar and optimized ZnO–Se nanocomposite are promising potential strategies for boosting soybean physiological performance and metabolic resistance in field environments.Graphical abstract Keywords: Biochar amendment, Nanocomposite, Phytochemical analysis, Metabolic pathway analysis

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max L.) is one of the world’s most important oilseed crops that are produced for their protein and oil contents which are valuable for the production of food, feed, and industrial products [1, 2]. The productivity of soybean is however hampered by the reduction of soil fertility, micronutrient deficiencies and environmental stresses which act together to reduce plant growth, metabolic activity and yield stability [3]. In many agroecosystems it is necessary to develop strategies that are sustainable to improve the performance of the crop and reduce the environmental impact, especially in developing regions [4]. Pyrolysis, the low-oxygen thermal decomposition of biomass to create biochar, a carbon-rich product, has shown potential as a soil amendment for enhancing the soil physicochemical properties and water retention, and nutrient availability [5, 6]. Biochar has also been shown to affect the physiological and biochemical processes of plants, which can lead to improved uptake efficiency of nutrients and stress response regulation [7]. However, biochar application was found to be effective in improving antioxidant enzyme activity and accumulation of secondary metabolites in crops which results in better stress tolerance in the past studies. The magnitude and consistency of these effects, however, is highly dependent on the characteristics of biochar, feedstocks and application rates, as well as the interaction with other agronomic inputs needs to be explored in more details [8].

At the same time, agriculture applications based on nanotechnology have been put forward, as they could enhance the delivery of nutrients and the resistance of plants to stress [9]. Both zinc (Zn) and selenium (Se) are micronutrients that are essential in enzyme activation, protein synthesis and in regulating oxidative stress; whereas, in low concentrations, Se can improve antioxidant defense systems and reduce abiotic stresses [10]. Nanostructured forms of these elements like nanoparticles of ZnO and Se provide more bioavailable and controlled release than traditional fertilizers. Recent investigations have shown that the use of nanoparticles can increase the efficiency of photosynthetic processes, antioxidant activity and accumulation of bioactive compounds in plants under stress conditions [10]. However, due to the potential for toxicity, dose optimization is very important.

Although biochar and nanoparticles have shown individual advantages, few studies have examined the use of both combined, and specifically in soybean systems [11]. Biochar can be used as a buffering and carrier matrix which could reduce the toxicity of the nanoparticles and increase their nutrient retention and availability in the rhizosphere [12]. On the other hand, foliar application of ZnO–Se nanocomposites could directly affect the plant metabolic pathways such as antioxidant defense and production of secondary metabolites [13]. Combining these two strategies might thus be a synergistic approach to enhancing plant biochemical performance and resilience, but the interactions at the basis of such combinations and their effects on metabolite composition are poorly understood.

Metabolite profiling, using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), can offer information regarding the variations in primary and secondary metabolites which include fatty acids and other bioactive compounds that affect the nutritional and functional quality of soybean. Pathway analysis tools can also be used to interpret metabolomics data by associating identified compounds with biochemical pathways, thus giving a systemic insight into the plant responses to the treatments. Thus, we proposed that the synergistic impact of biochar and ZnO–Se nanocomposites would boost the biochemical features, antioxidant potential and metabolite profile of soybean plants relative to the use of biochar or ZnO–Se nanocomposites alone. Thus, the aim of this study was to: (i) assess the effect of biochar and ZnO–Se nanocomposites on the important biochemical parameters and antioxidant activity of soybean, (ii) investigate the biochemical changes in the metabolites by GC–MS analysis, and (iii) elucidate the related metabolic pathways to elucidate the biochemical response under the influence of treatment.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of ZnO-Se nanocomposite

ZnO–Se nanocomposites were prepared by the green synthesis method with the help of garlics extract as a reducing and stabilizing agent. Fresh garlic cloves were washed, peeled, homogenized in distilled water and then filtered using Whatman No.1 filter paper to get filtered garlic extract. 0.1 M Zn (NO₃) ₂ (zinc nitrate hexahydrate) solution and 0.1 M Na₂SeO₃ (sodium selenite) solution were prepared with distilled water. The amounts of zinc precursor and selenium precursor used for the synthesis were 0.66 g and 0.5 g, respectively. The garlic extract (30 mL) was introduced in the precursor solutions, and the mixture was stirred continuously at room temperature. Then the reaction mixtures (garlic extract) were treated with the selenium and zinc precursor solutions dropwise [14].

The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 10 by adding sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to ensure the synthesis of nanocomposites. This reaction mixture was then stirred for 2 h at 70 °C. A colour change in the sample from light yellow to reddish brown was observed, which is a sign of the formation of ZnO–Se nanocomposites. The precipitate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min and then washed many times with distilled water and ethanol to get rid of impurities. The purified product was then dried in a hot air oven at 60 °C to obtain the final ZnO–Se nanocomposite powder. The nanocomposites of ZnO–Se were dispersed in distilled water and sonicated to get uniform dispersion before application. The foliar sprays were applied at the critical growth stages which include seedling stage (15 days after sowing), vegetative stage and flowering stage by hand sprayer. A total of 12 treatments were evaluated and each of the treatments had the different concentration of nanocomposite as per the experimental design. A spray volume of 800 ml/Treatment was used for each application, to ensure uniform spray coverage of the foliage. Distilled water was used for spraying the control plants under the same conditions.

Characterization of ZnO-Se nanocomposites

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

The ZnO-Se nanocomposite is dried, mounted on a conductive carbon tape, and sputter-coated with gold or platinum to prevent charging. The sample is imaged in vacuum using a high-energy electron beam that produces secondary and backscattered electrons for imaging. SEM provides high-resolution information on morphology, particle size, and agglomeration.

Biochar production in the muffle furnace and post-treatment

The oxygen-limited pyrolysis of sugarcane bagasse was carried out in a muffle furnace to produce biochar [15]. The impurities in the bagasse were removed by washing and the moisture was removed by oven drying until a constant weight for 70 °C to eliminate any water. The dried material was then individually broken into small pieces (2–5 cm) to ensure that heat is distributed evenly during pyrolysis. The prepared biomass was then placed in a covered ceramic-crucible to limit the availability of oxygen and then pyrolyzed at 450 °C for 2 h in a muffle furnace. Once the pyrolysis process was completed, the furnace was cooled gradually to room temperature to avoid causing oxidation and degradation of the biochar. The biochar obtained was collected, ground into fine particles and sieved to get uniform sized biochar (2 mm). The biochar was packaged in dry containers to protect it from the moisture and contamination before field use.

Plant samples and experimental site

This field experiment was carried out at the National Agricultural Research Centre (NARC). Islamabad, during summer season of the growing period. The experimental site is located in a subtropical semi-arid climate with hot summers and moderate rainfall amounts. Average temperatures were 25–38 °C during the cropping period, with an average of 1,000–1,200 mm of precipitation annually, of which about 70% falls in the monsoon (July to September). The soybean (Glycine max L.) seeds of NARC SOY-21 cultivar were obtained from Oilseed Research Department, National Agricultural Research Centre (NARC), Islamabad for the experiment.

Experimental design and treatments

The experimental design was randomized complete block design (RCBD) and the factorial design consisted of biochar and foliar application of ZnO–Se nanocomposites. biochar and nanocomposites were applied (control: T1) and 0.67 t ha⁻¹ (Biochar: T2). The ZnO–Se nanocomposites were applied at five concentrations: T3 (10 ppm), T4 (20 ppm), T5 (30 ppm), T6 (40 ppm), and T7 (50 ppm). These factors gave a total of 12 treatment combinations (2 × 5) with each combination being replicated three times (36 experimental units/plot). The experiment was carried out in ridges with each ridge serving as an experimental unit, a total of 36 ridges were used with an average length of approximately 1.5 m and an average width of 0.30 m (equal to 5 ft × 1 ft). Within each block treatments were randomly assigned to reduce experimental error. The foliar application of the ZnO–Se nanocomposites was done at three critical growth stages, that is, seedling stage (15 days), vegetative stage (30 days) and flowering stage (45 days), at the corresponding concentration (10–50 ppm) as per treatment design. All agronomic practices such as uniform irrigation, weed management, pest management were followed as per standard practices and seedling density was fixed as 45 cm plant to plant distance and 15 cm row to row distance in all the treatments. Biochemical attributes

At the flowering stage fresh, fully expanded soybean leaf samples were taken from a random selection of plants from each treatment replicate. The samples of leaves were immediately transported to the lab on ice and kept at 4 °C until analysis. All biochemical assays were carried out with 0.5 g fresh leaf tissue that was homogenized in 5 mL of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) in a chilled mortar and pestle. This homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was subjected to further biochemical analyses. Each assay was run three times.

Determination of Soluble Protein Content (SPC)

Soluble protein content was determined using the method of Lowry by [16], with slight modifications. In brief, 0.5 mL of frond extract was combined with 2.5 mL alkaline copper reagent and allowed to incubate at room temperature for 10 min and 0.25 mL Folin–Ciocalteu reagent (1 N) was added and the mixture was further incubated in dark for 30 min. Absorbance was then recorded at 750 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan). The standard was bovine serum albumin (BSA) and the soluble protein content was presented as µg g⁻¹ fresh weight (FW).

Evaluation of Flavonoids Content (TFC)

Total flavonoids content was quantified using the aluminium chloride colorimetric method of [17], with some minor modifications. In brief, 0.5 mL methanolic leaf extract was mixed with 1.5 mL methanol, 0.1 mL of 10% aluminum chloride (AlCl₃), 0.1 mL of potassium acetate (1 M) and 2.8 mL distilled water. The resulting reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min after which absorbance was taken at 415 nm using UV–Visible spectrophotometer. Quercetin was used as a calibration standard and the results were given as µg mL-1 quercetin equivalent (QE).

Quantification of Sugar Content (TSC)

The total soluble sugar content was measured according to the phenol–sulfuric acid assay method of [18]. Leaf extract was mixed with 5% phenol solution for a few minutes and immediately poured into the mixture concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄). Reaction mixture was allowed to incubate for 20 min at room temperature and absorbance was read at 490 nm. The TSC were expressed as µg mL-1 glucose equivalent and glucose was used as standard.

Estimation of Total Phenolic Content (TPC)

Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu (FC) assay as described by [19], with some modifications. In short, 100 µL methanolic leaf extract was mixed with 750 µL of 10-fold diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and allowed to react at room temperature for 10 min. Then 750 µL of 2% sodium carbonate solution was added and the reaction mixture was incubated for 45 min in dark. Absorbance was then measured at 765 nm using UV–Visible spectrophotometer. Gallic acid was used as Calibration standard, and the results were expressed as µg mL-1 gallic acid equivalent (GAE).

Antioxidant activities

DPPH assay

The antioxidant assay of soybean leaf extracts was assessed using the DPPH assay following the procedure of [20]. A 0.1mM solution of DPPH was prepared in methanol. Briefly, 3 mL of DPPH solution was mixed with 100 µL of plant extract and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was recorded at 517 nm using UV–Visible spectrophotometer. Methanol was used as a blank, while DPPH solution without extract was used as a control. ABTS analysis

ABTS activity was determined following the method of [21]. The ABTS radical cation (ABTS•⁺) was generated by reacting 7 mM ABTS solution with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate solution and allowing the mixture to stand in the dark at room temperature for 16 h. prior to analysis the solution was diluted with ethanol to obtained an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. subsequently, 20 µL of plant extract was mixed with 200 µL of ABTS solution and incubate for 6 min and absorbance was then recorded at 734 nm. The reference antioxidant standard used was Trolox and % inhibition is calculated as [(A₀ - Aₛ) / A₀] × 100, where A₀ and Aₛ are the control and sample absorbances.

Total antioxidant assay

Total antioxidant activity was measured using the phosphomolybdenum method of [22]. In brief, 1mL of reagent solution containing 0.6 M sulfuric acid, 28 mM sodium phosphate, 4 mM ammonium molybdate) was mixed with 0.1mL of plant extract. This reaction mixture was heated in a water bath at 95 °C for 90 min, then cooled to room temperature. Measuring the absorbance at 695 nm. Ascorbic acid was used as the reference compound and results presented as mg ascorbic acid equivalent (AAE) g⁻¹ FW.

GC-MS analysis

Oil was extracted from a sample of finely ground, dried and cleaned soybean seeds from each treatment replicate. For extracting soybean oil, Soxhlet extraction apparatus was used according to the method of [23], with slight modifications. In brief, 10 g finely powdered soybean seed sample was extracted with 250 mL n-hexane for 6 h with Soxhlet apparatus. Extracted oil was then subjected to a GC–MS analysis after the removal of the solvent under reduced pressure at 40 °C by a rotary evaporator (RE-52AA, Yarong Biochemical Instrument Factory, China) to yield crude soybean oil. To prepare fatty acid methyl ester (FAME), 100 mg of extracted oil was dissolved in 2 mL n-hexane and 2 mL of methanolic KOH solution (0.5 M) was added to this solution. The mixture was then vortexed for 2 min and stood until phase separation took place. The top layer of the organic material (FAMEs) was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter, then analyzed by GC–MS. For the GC–MS analysis, a gas chromatograph (GCMS-QP2010 Ultra, Shimadzu, Japan) with a mass spectrometer system was used, which was equipped with a Rxi-5Sil MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Restek, USA). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL min⁻¹. The temperature of the injector was kept at 250 °C and the injection volume was 1 µL under split injection mode (split ratio 1:20).

The oven temperature program was designed as follows: initial temperature 60 °C for 2 min, ramp 180 °C min⁻¹, ramp 280 °C min⁻¹ to 280 °C for 10 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, and interfaced temperature 250 °C with an ion source temperature of 200 °C, and recorded between m/z 40 and 500. The mass spectra obtained were compared with the NIST Mass Spectral Library (National Institute of Standards and Technology, USA, NIST 14). Relative fatty acid composition was determined by peak area normalisation and percentage (%) of total identified compounds. Each biological replicate was analysed in three replicates and each replicate was injected separately into the GC–MS system. Compounds that were detected in every replicate were only included if they were interpreted.

Pathway analysis

Enrichment analyses for all the metabolomics discovered were conducted using the KEGG compound database alongside MetaboAnalyst 6.0, along with conducting another enrichment analysis.

Statistical evaluation

All treatment were performed in triplicates. The data were analysed using SPSS 16.0 version. Analysis of variance (ANOVA), was conducted to evaluate treatment effects. Difference among treatments means were determined using Tukey post hoc test. For the graph generation Minitab 18.1 was used. Metabolomics analysis was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 and Genes and KEGG Encyclopaedia was used for KEGG annotation.

Biochemical attributes

Total Sugar Content (TSC)

Sugar content in a plant indicates the rate of photosynthesis. An abundant amount of sugar represents the high rate of photosynthesis in plants. Different treatments of nanocomposite, biochar, and a combination of biochar and nanocomposite affect the sugar contents in Glycine max. The combined use of biochar and ZnO-Se NCs (50ppm) significantly increased the sugar content, which is 45% (215.5 ± 2.9), then followed by biochar and 40ppm concentration of BC + ZnO-Se NCs which is 28% (167.08 ± 0.66) as compared to the rest of the treatments, and a decline in sugar content was noted at foliar spray of 30ppm concentration of ZnO-Se nanocomposite which is -7% (137.3 ± 1.10) as compared to the control as shown in Fig. 3B. The TSC content for soybean is statistically found significant for all factors with (F = 139.99; p-Value < 0.001), which is given in Fig. 3B.

Total Flavonoid Contents (TFC)

Total flavonoid contents were estimated by using different treatments of biochar and nanocomposites, either separately or in combination, as shown in Fig. 3C. The higher contents of flavonoid were noted at the combined used of biochar and 40ppm concentration of ZnO-Se nanocomposite (BC+40ppm ZnO-Se), which is 16%, (187.3 ± 1.82) followed by BC+50ppm concentration of ZnO-Se NCs and 40 ppm concentration of ZnO-Se NCs, which were 15.43% (171.8 ± 1.41) and 15.11% (167.2 ± 1.96). similarly, a reduction in total flavonoid contents was noted at 50ppm foliar spray of ZnO-Se nanocomposite, which is -5% (139.8 ± 1.01) as compared to the control. All the data were statistically found significant for all treatments with (F = 91.07; p-value < 0.001), which is shown in Fig. 3C.

Total Phenolic Content (TPC)

When a plant is under stress, it begins to produce the enzyme phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), which leads to the production of phenolic compounds. Plant treated with various concentrations of biochar and nanocomposite either separately or in combination, so the highest number of total phenolic content were noticed due to the combine used of biochar and 40ppm ZnO-Se nanocomposite (BC+40ppm ZnO-Se) which is 10%, (70.64 ± 0.57) followed by BC+30ppm concentration of ZnO-Se NCs which were 5% (67.40 ± 2.65) and the highest concentration of ZnO-Se NCs applied alone (50ppm) reduced TPC by 18% as compared to the control as shown in Fig. 3A. The decline in the TPC occur due to the higher concentration of nanocomposites which may lead to the formation of ROS which can affect the phenolic biosynthesis pathway. The statistical results showed that all the data are significant for all factors, which were (F = 18.17; p-Value < 0.001).

Soluble protein content (SPC)

Biochar and nanocomposite significantly affected the soluble protein content of Glycine max. the greatest number of soluble proteins was observed at the combine use of biochar and 40ppm concentration of foliar spray of ZnO-Se nanocomposite (BC+40ppm ZnO-Se) which is 55% (3.73 ± 0.22) as compared to the other treatments and the lowest number of soluble protein content was noted at 50ppm foliar spray of ZnO-Se nanocomposite which is -22% (2.92 ± 0.04) as relative to the control treatment as mentioned in Fig. 3D. The results for all treatments were significant (F = 27.75; p-value < 0.001).

Antioxidant activity

DPPH Activity

Glycine max plant extract was estimated for its antioxidant potential by using biochar and foliar application of ZnO-Se nanocomposite. The outcomes of the DPPH experiment revealed that biochar and Allium sativum-based ZnO-Se nanocomposite efficiently obstructs the free radicals. The soybean plants were subjected to different biochar and nanocomposite treatments, either separately or in combination, to find out the antioxidant potential of the plant. The greatest level of scavenging potential was recorded at the integrated treatment of biochar and 40ppm concentration of foliar application of ZnO-Se NCs (BC+40ppm ZnO-Se) which is (48.46 ± 0.94) as compared to other treatments, and the lowest free radical scavenging potential was observed for the untreated plant, as mentioned in Fig. 4A. the statistical analysis revealed that the results are highly significant with (F = 38.21; p-value = 0.001). Overall, the DPPH scavenging potential is high due to the combined application of biochar and ZnO-Se nanocomposite, so it indicates that biochar and ZnO-Se nanocomposite have the potential to scavenge free radicals. In contrast the higher concentration of nanocomposite 50ppm show low scavenging activity, show that excessive nanocomposite concentration may negatively influence the antioxidant metabolism. The values are presented as mean ± standard deviation of three replicates.

ABTS assay

The ABTS assay is highly variable among different treatments (F = 45.52; p-value = 0.001). It was high in the control and the biochar-only treatments, whereas the nanocomposites (NCs) treatments decreased with concentration dependency, and the lowest activity was noted at 50ppm which is (51.03 ± 1.86). However, the biochar mixed with NCs enhanced scavenging activity, especially at lower concentrations of NCs. The BC + 10ppm (72.01 ± 1.30) treatment had the highest antioxidant activity, and it was noted that there was a synergistic effect here, as mentioned in Fig. 4B. In general, biochar was able to counteract the adverse effects of high concentrations of ZnO-Se nanocomposites on antioxidant capacity. Generally, plants treated with biochar exhibited greater levels of antioxidant activity than those that were not treated with biochar. The result was, however, different from that in DPPH assay suggesting that there were assay dependent antioxidant responses. Based on these results, it can be concluded that moderate concentrations of ZnO–Se NCs with the addition of biochar could be beneficial for antioxidant stability while higher concentrations of nanoparticles may cause oxidative imbalance. All values are shown as mean ± SD (n = 3, independent replicates).

Total antioxidant activity (Phosphomolybdenum assay)

The phosphomolybdenum method was also used to evaluate the total antioxidant activity. There was a significant difference among the treatments (F = 44.30, p < 0.001). The biochar and ZnO–Se NCs when used together were more effective in antioxidant activity than the control and the nanocomposite alone (Fig. 4C). The maximum total antioxidant activity was observed in BC + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NCs (57.18 ± 1. 45) treatment compared to BC + 30 ppm (55.60 ± 0.35) and BC + 20 ppm (50.70 ± 2.11). The 50 ppm ZnO-Se NCs, (34.03 ± 2.20) however, showed the least antioxidant activity. The above observations suggest that the combined use of biochar may be beneficial to sustain the antioxidant activity at moderate nanocomposite use rates. The results are given as mean ± standard deviation (SD; n = 3).

GC-MS analysis

GC–MS analysis was employed to determine the major fatty acid components of the soybean oil obtained from various treatments. The compounds detected and the corresponding percentage peak areas are summarized in (Table 2) and representative chromatograms are shown in Fig. 5. The analytical study showed the presence of several fatty acids and their related compounds such as linoleic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid and dl-α-tocopherol. Linoleic acid and oleic acid were found as the major unsaturated fatty acids in all treatments whereas palmitic acid and stearic acid were found in comparatively lower proportions. After applying biochar and ZnO–Se nanocomposites, differences in the percentage of peak areas were found between the treatments. The amount of unsaturated fatty acids was relatively higher when the biochar was combined with the treatment of moderate concentration of ZnO–Se NCs than in the absence of any treatment. Furthermore, dl-α-tocopherol was found in some treatments, suggesting possible changes in metabolisms related to antioxidants after the application of biochar and nanocomposites. The differences among treatments observed were, however, viewed with a degree of caution since some compounds were relatively rare.

KEGG-based pathway enrichments and metabolite variability analysis

Metabolomic pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the metabolites that were tentatively identified in the GC–MS analysis to investigate possible metabolic associations. The analysis showed that the pathways associated with glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, and phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis pathways were associated. The pathways had either higher enrichment ratio or lower p-value relative to other pathways identified. The impact plot (Fig. 6A) indicated that there were a number of pathways related to amino acids that made a significant contribution to the overall metabolite response between treatments. The pathway impact values and statistical significance levels were relatively higher for the pathways related to amino acid metabolism. Pathway associations, however, should be used with caution as the KEGG analysis was not done with the goal of targeted metabolomic validation (Fig. 6B). The results can thus not directly confirm the regulation of metabolic fluxes or the activation of metabolic pathways. Rather, the analysis offers predictive bioinformatic understanding of potential metabolic responses to the treatments with Biochar and the ZnO–Se nanocomposite. The enrichment ratios and pathway visualization were provided for the purposes of presenting treatment associated metabolite variability or potential metabolic relationships, and not to make biochemical conclusions (Fig. 6C).

Statistical evaluation

A statistical analysis was carried out to assess the variability of the biochemical and antioxidant parameters associated with the treatment. The biplot representation of the principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 7A) shows that the Total Sugar Content (TSC), Total phenolic Content (TPC) and assays related to the antioxidant properties were major contributors to the variations between treatments. Treatment BC + 50 ppm and BC + 30 ppm were separated from the control treatment along the principal component axes indicating differences in biochemical responses. The heatmap (Fig. 7B) shows the relative variation of biochemical and antioxidant parameters between the different treatment groups. The treated responses with combined biochar and ZnO–Se nanocomposite applications generally had comparatively higher scaled responses for some antioxidant associated parameters than the untreated application. The dendrogram (Fig. 7C) classified the treatments into clear clusters based on the biochemical similarity. Higher and moderate concentrations of Combined BC+ treatments clustered separately from the other treatments, suggesting treatment-dependent variation in the parameters measured. The positive relationships between Total Phenolic Content (TPC), Total Flavonoid Content (TFC) and antioxidant-related responses were indicated in the path diagram (Fig. 7D). These factors, however, are all relatively well correlated with the antioxidant-related latent factor, with the strongest association seen by TPC.

Such multivariate analyses, however, are only preliminary and exploratory statistical analyses of patterns associated with treatment and do not constitute definitive evidence of mechanism of action interaction or synergistic effects. The results showed that the biochar and ZnO–Se nanocomposite treatments were linked to the variation of biochemical and antioxidant profiles under the experimental conditions of the study.

Discussion

In the present study, the combined effect of sugarcane bagasse (SCB) based biochar and green-synthesized ZnO–Se nanocomposites (ZnO–Se NCs) on biochemical attributes, antioxidant response and metabolite profile of soybean (Glycine max L.) under field conditions were evaluated. The results indicated that overall, the application of biochar along with moderate concentration of ZnO–Se NCs (especially 40 ppm of ZnO–Se NCs) enhanced various biochemical and antioxidant parameters than the untreated control and single applications treatments. The observations indicate that the combination of soil applied biochar and foliar applied ZnO–Se NCs could have a positive effect on soybean physiological performance and antioxidant metabolism.

The observed increase in SPC in the case of BC + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC treatment could be related to the availability of nutrients and better metabolic activity. The treatments with 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC and 50 ppm ZnO–Se NC alone recorded the lowest SPC values, of 2.92 ± 0.04 and 2.95 ± 0.05 µg g⁻¹ FW, respectively, which are approximately 45% lower than those of the control. The lowest SPC values, 2.92 ± 0.04 µg g⁻¹ FW and 2.95 ± 0.05 µg g⁻¹ FW, respectively, were obtained in the treatments with 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC and 50 ppm ZnO–Se NC alone, and are about 45% lower than in the control. Zinc is involved in the activation of several enzymes, chlorophyll biosynthesis, and protein metabolism, and selenium has also been shown to have antioxidant and redox regulation properties at lower concentrations [24]. Biochar amendments also could affect additional soil physicochemical properties such as nutrient retention and moisture availability to aid plant growth and metabolic functions [8]. Previously, similar increases in protein content after application of nanoparticles have been reported in soybean and other plant species under stress [25]. Thus, based on the statistically significant increase in SPC (F = 27.75, p < 0.001), it was concluded that the moderate concentration of ZnO–Se NCs with biochar was a beneficial effect on the metabolic performance of soybean.

Moreover, the total flavonoid content (TFC) and total phenolic content (TPC) also showed an increase, especially in the combined treatment of BC + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC. The highest TFC was recorded in BC + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC-treated plants (187.3 ± 1.82), followed by BC + 50 ppm (171.8 ± 1.41) and ZnO–Se 40 ppm alone (167.2 ± 1.96), whereas a decline was observed under 50 ppm ZnO–Se NC treatment alone (139.8 ± 1.01). Likewise, the highest TPC (70.64 ± 0.57) was recorded by BC + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC treatment and the lowest TPC (52.03 ± 0.44) was observed in the 50 ppm ZnO–Se NC treatment alone. Phenolic and flavonoid compounds are crucial non-enzymatic antioxidants that play a role in scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and adapting to stress [26]. These metabolites increased at moderate concentration of nanoparticles, indicating an activation of secondary metabolism process related to antioxidants. In other crops such as soybean and oilseed crops, the application of nano-enabled micronutrients and bio stimulants resulted in comparable increases in phenolic compounds and antioxidant metabolites [27]. Microorganism-based sustainable bioamendment treatments using mycorrhizal and seaweed extracts under drought stress also showed similar improvements in antioxidant related traits and oil quality in quinoa, indicating that biological amendments can act as biostimulants to boost plant defense metabolism and enhance crop resilience by [28]. The reduction in both TFC and TPC at high concentrations of nanoparticles, however, suggests that high exposure of Se NCs to the cells may have adverse effects on cellular homeostasis and antioxidant level. Previously, concentration-dependent effects have been reported for nanoparticles, with moderate concentrations having stimulatory effects on metabolism, and high concentrations having a negative effect on metabolism by causing oxidative stress and phytotoxicity [29].

The combined treatments showed the increase in Total soluble Sugar content (TSC) which could be due to better carbohydrate metabolism and physiological adjustment. The maximum TSC was recorded in BC + 50 ppm ZnO–Se NC treatment with its sugar content being increased by 45%, followed by BC + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC treatment with 28% increase compared to control. In addition to being a metabolic substrate, soluble sugars play a role in stress tolerance as osmoprotectants. Sugar accumulation is positively affected by biochar amendments and their ability to increase soil water holding capacity and nutrient availability, which boosts photosynthesis [30]. Similar metabolically protective effects and stress response have been reported in quinoa after treatment with mycorrhizal fungi and seaweed biostimulants under drought conditions, indicating the importance of sustainable amendments to protect metabolic homeostasis under stress [28]. The enhanced TSC values observed with BC + ZnO–Se NC treatments are thus indicative of better metabolic activity at optimized nanoparticles concentration. However, the simultaneous decrease in both proteins and phenolic compounds at 50 ppm ZnO–Se NCs indicates that the excessive exposure of the nanoparticles could also cause oxidative stress, even in the presence of sugar accumulation.

The antioxidant assays showed the ability to vary according to treatment, between DPPH, ABTS and phosphomolybdenum assays. The most radical scavenging activity was obtained by the DPPH assay when treated with BC + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC (48.46 ± 0.94) and the lowest scavenging activity was obtained by the untreated control. Similarly, the highest level of total antioxidant activity was in BC + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NC (57.18 ± 1.45) followed by BC + 30 ppm (55.60 ± 0.35) and BC + 20 ppm (50.70 ± 2.11) while the lowest activity was observed in the treatment of 50 ppm ZnO–Se NC (34.03 ± 2.20). This response follows a similar trend as the higher phenolic & flavonoid levels at moderate application of ZnO–Se NCs. Moreover, previous research has also demonstrated that ZnO and selenium nanoparticles can positively impact antioxidant defense systems and tolerance to oxidative stress in plants when applied at optimal concentrations [24]. The ABTS assay, on the other hand, demonstrated greater overall antioxidant activity for control and biochar-only treatments and decreased activity with increasing concentration of the nanocomposites. The BC + 10 ppm ZnO–Se NC treatment showed the highest ABTS activity (72.01 ± 1.30), and the lowest ABTS activity (51.03 ± 1.86) was found under 50 ppm ZnO–Se NC treatment. The discrepancy between the various assays of antioxidants suggests that different antioxidant systems could be affected by the exposure to nanoparticles in different ways. ABTS measures hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidant compounds, while DPPH predominantly measures lipophilic radical scavenging activity [31], so it is expected that there are differences between assays. Hence, the present results indicated that the overall effect of combined treatment was neither consistent in improving all the antioxidant pathways nor the responses are to be taken as definitive.

GC–MS analysis revealed the presence of various fatty acids and lipid related metabolites in soybean oil such as linoleic acid, oleic acid, palmitic acid, stearic acid and dl-α-tocopherol. Of the identified compounds, n-hexadecanoic acid (palmitic acid) accounted for 2.94% of the total peak area and 9,12-octadecadienoic acid (linoleic acid) accounted for 2.85% of the total peak area. The unsaturated fatty acids detected were mostly linoleic acid and oleic acid, which is in line with the fatty acid profile of soybean oil [32, 33]. Combine biochar with nanoparticles and similar observations have been made in soybean and oilseed crops. The morpho–physiological, biochemical, and oil parameters of G. max were improved by synergistically using the novel nanomaterials based on ZnO–Se with biochar amendments, which were observed by GC–MS profiling and in vitro analysis. In the current study, moderate differences between the treatments with respect to fatty acid abundance could thus suggest treatment-related differences in metabolites related to lipids. It should be noted, however, that these metabolite differences are relative peak area normalized by GC–MS analysis and should be interpreted conservatively with regard to their biological significance. There would be a need for further specific metabolomic analysis to confirm changes in fatty acid composition and antioxidant-associated metabolites that are induced by a treatment.

The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated potential links to amino acid metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways, such as glycine, serine, threonine, and phenylalanine biosynthesis pathways. Yet, the results are preliminary as the pathway predictions were not based on metabolomic validation but on metabolites tentatively identified by GC–MS analysis. Therefore, the KEGG results should be interpreted as indications rather than as direct evidence of pathway activation or metabolic regulation and are bioinformatic explorations. Treatment-effects on biochemical and antioxidant response were also illustrated by multivariate analyses such as principal component analysis, heatmap clustering and dendrogram analysis. The principal component analysis revealed the separation of combined BC + ZnO–Se NC treatments from the control with Dim1 explaining 50.3% of the variance. The interrelationships between antioxidant related parameters such as TPC, TFC and DPPH activity were also observed from the clustering pattern. The mechanisms involved in these analyses, however, were ones of an exploratory nature, and are not meant to be seen as a confirmation of synergy.

Improvements in the agronomic characteristics, such as antioxidant and biochemical properties, observed in this study could be associated with greater plant physiological resilience and metabolic stability under field conditions. The application of sugarcane bagasse biochar is also environmentally friendly approach for agricultural waste utilization and can enhance the soil quality. Likewise, application of ZnO–Se NCs for micronutrient delivery could be a cost-effective option to provide micronutrient supplementation at reduced application levels. However, more widespread testing with different environments and seasons is required prior to making practical recommendations for agriculture. The improved results for the biochar and ZnO–Se NCs composite amendment in soybean plant growth suggest that the compound can be used to enhance the biochemical and antioxidant parameters, but certain restrictions have to be taken into account. The study was carried out under one field situation and one growing season; thus, wider agronomic validation remains to be done. In the same way, focused metabolomic validation would help gain greater understanding of the KEGG pathway predictions made in this study.

The overall results indicate that this biochar from sugarcane bagasse in combination with moderate application rates of ZnO–Se nanocomposites could potentially positively influence the biochemical characteristics, antioxidant metabolism and metabolite profile of soybean under field conditions. This study offers preliminary evidence that biochar-mediated soil improvement, together with delivery of nanoenabled micronutrients, can impact physiological and metabolic responses in soybean. Future studies should involve multi-location field trials, direct yield assessment, and specific metabolomic studies to confirm the agronomic value and feasibility of this multiple approach.

Conclusion

The current study shows that the integrated amendment of biochar derived from sugarcane bagasse and green synthesized ZnO–Se nanocomposites enhanced the biochemical attributes, antioxidant defence system, and metabolites composition in the field experiment in soybean. Biochar + 40 ppm ZnO–Se NCs resulted in the highest soluble proteins, flavonoids, phenolics and antioxidant activity than control and BC + 50 ppm ZnO–Se NCs increased the sugar accumulation among all the treatments. GC–MS analysis revealed metabolites that are both nutritionally important fatty acids and antioxidants, while the KEGG pathway analysis indicated the involvement of amino acid metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways. Nevertheless, some biochemical and antioxidant parameters were negatively influenced by higher concentration of nanoparticles (50 ppm), thus emphasizing the need of optimization of dosage. In conclusion, the coupling of biochar and moderate amounts of ZnO–Se nanocomposites is a promising and sustainable approach for enhancing soybean physiological and biochemical growth. Further multi-location field studies, and targeted metabolomics validation are needed before large-scale agricultural application.
