Comparative and diagnostic evaluation of maternal serum prostasin in early-onset preeclampsia
Authors: Gökhan Bolluk, Süleyman Cemil Oğlak, Zeynep Kayaoğlu Yildirim, Oğuzhan Zengi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We aimed to compare maternal serum prostasin levels between women with early-onset preeclampsia (PE) and healthy pregnant controls to establish pathophysiological differences. We also sought to evaluate the diagnostic performance of maternal serum prostasin as a potential biomarker for early-onset PE, including determination of optimal cutoff values for clinical application. This was a prospective case–control study including 43 pregnant women who were diagnosed with early-onset PE between 200/7 to 340/7 weeks of gestation from March to September 2022. Also, 45 healthy pregnant women were included in the control group. Demographic characteristics, complete blood count and biochemical parameters, and serum prostasin concentrations were documented. A receiver operating characteristic curve was generated to demonstrate the sensitivity and specificity of prostasin, and the optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index. The median maternal serum prostasin level was found to be significantly higher in the early-onset PE group (31.66 [27.12–51.00] ng/mL) compared to the control group (22.33 [16.03–25.89] ng/mL) with a P-value <.001. The area under the curve was found to be 0.820 ± 0.047 (95% CI: 0.729–0.912), indicating a statistically significant discriminative ability (P < .001). The optimal cutoff value for maternal serum prostasin level was determined using the Youden index, with a threshold of ≥26.44 ng/mL corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.837 and a specificity of 0.778 for the diagnosis of early-onset PE. Based on the multivariate model results, a maternal serum prostasin value of >26.44 ng/mL increases the risk of early-onset PE by approximately 20-fold. Maternal serum prostasin levels are significantly elevated in early-onset preeclamptic mothers compared to the healthy controls. These findings suggest that prostasin may play a role in early-onset PE pathophysiology and could serve as a biomarker for its diagnosis.

1. Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a serious complication of pregnancy that affects around 5% to 8% of pregnancies globally. It is characterized by high blood pressure and organ damage, especially in the liver and kidneys, and occurs after 20 weeks of gestation in women who were previously normotensive.[1–4] It can have serious consequences, both maternal (such as eclampsia, liver and kidney failure, stroke, and bleeding disorders), fetal (including fetal growth restriction, placental abruption, and preterm birth) and even maternal or fetal death.[5–7] Early-onset PE (<34 weeks gestation) represents approximately 5% to 20% of all PE cases but accounts for the majority of severe maternal and perinatal morbidity, requiring distinct diagnostic approaches. Early detection and management of early-onset PE are critical to ensure the best possible outcomes.[8]

Maternal serum biomarkers have emerged as a promising tool for predicting and diagnosing PE and monitoring disease severity. In recent years, numerous studies have investigated various serum biomarkers, including angiogenic factors, inflammatory markers, and oxidative stress markers. While established biomarkers such as sFlt-1/PlGF ratios show promise, their performance varies significantly across PE subtypes, with early-onset disease requiring more specific molecular markers. Moreover, serum biomarkers may offer advantages over traditional clinical predictors, such as blood pressure and proteinuria, by providing earlier and more specific information about the underlying pathophysiology of PE.[9,10]

Prostasin is an active serine protease that is anchored to the membrane by glycosylphosphatidylinositol and has enzymatic activities similar to trypsin. It is encoded by the PRSS8 gene located on chromosome 16 and has been found to play an important role in the regulation of the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) in the distal nephrons of the kidney, which is a key target for aldosterone.[11,12] Aldosterone, which acts on the kidney to increase sodium reabsorption, is responsible for the regulation of extracellular fluid volume. Moreover, increased expression of prostasin has been shown to inhibit the invasion ability of trophoblasts and remodeling of spiral arteries in cell line studies.[13,14] Both mechanisms, described above, suggest that elevated prostasin levels in maternal serum may play a critical role in the underlying pathophysiology of early-onset PE.

Given the potential mechanistic role of prostasin in PE pathophysiology and the clinical need for improved early-onset PE biomarkers, this study had dual objectives. First, we aimed to compare maternal serum prostasin levels between women with early-onset PE and healthy pregnant controls to establish pathophysiological differences. Second, we sought to evaluate the diagnostic performance of maternal serum prostasin as a potential biomarker for early-onset PE, including determination of optimal cutoff values for clinical application.

2. Materials and methods

This prospective case–control study was conducted from March to September 2022 in the Department of Perinatology at Başaksehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital. Referrals of women with early-onset PE are regularly received by our clinic, which is a leading referral hospital for maternal care in Istanbul, Türkiye. All participants were asked to provide written informed consent before their enrollment in the study. The local ethics committee approved the study (approval number: 2022.05.160) and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study group included 43 patients aged between 18 to 45 years with a single pregnancy complicated with early-onset PE. The control group consisted of 45 uncomplicated normotensive healthy pregnant women.

PE was diagnosed with the new onset of hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg on 2 occasions at least 4 hours apart while on bed rest unless) and proteinuria (urinary protein > 3 g/day, a spot urine protein/creatinine ratio > 3 mg/dL, or >3+ by dipstick) after 20 weeks of gestation. In the absence of proteinuria, new-onset hypertension with severe clinical and laboratory features is also diagnostic for PE.[15]

2.1. Patient selection

As no definitive data on serum prostasin levels which is the primary outcome of our study, in healthy pregnant women could be identified in the literature, a pilot study was conducted with 15 healthy pregnant participants. Based on the findings of this preliminary study, the mean prostasin level was estimated as 29.60 ng/mL with a standard deviation of 22.20 ng/mL. Using these values as the basis, the required sample size was calculated to detect a 30% difference between group means with a minimum power of 80% and a two-sided significance level of 0.05. According to the results of this calculation using a two-sample Student t test, the minimum required sample size was 39 participants per group.

Gestational age was determined based on the last day of the menstrual period and the first trimester was confirmed with the crown-rump length measurement.

We did not include pregnant women with multiple pregnancies, pregestational diabetes, chronic hypertension, systemic diseases (such as chronic kidney or autoimmune disease), fetal anomalies, and premature rupture of membranes in the study.

2.2. Collection of blood samples

The blood samples were obtained at the admission of patients before any medical therapy for PE. The peripheral blood samples were collected in EDTA or serum tubes and centrifuged for 15 minutes at 1000 g and 2 to 8 °C within 30 minutes of collection. The upper serum or plasma phase was collected and immediately frozen at −80 °C before further analysis. Quantitative measurement of maternal serum prostasin levels was performed using Human Prostasin Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay Kit (Shanghai Korain Biotech, Shanghai, China). The sensitivity of the assay was 0.28 ng/mL with a detection range between 0.5 ng/mL to 300 ng/mL. The Human Prostasin Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay kit demonstrated inter-assay CV of <10% and intra-assay CV of <5%. All samples were analyzed in duplicate by technicians blinded to clinical group assignment.

2.3. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp. Released 2020. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0. Armonk: IBM Corp.). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, which is appropriate for the sample sizes in this study. For group comparisons, Student t test was applied to normally distributed variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed variables.

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for parametric variables and as median (Q1–Q3) for nonparametric variables.

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of maternal serum prostasin levels in identifying early-onset PE, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. The area under the curve (AUC), standard error, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index, and corresponding sensitivity and specificity values were reported. ROC analyses were performed using MedCalc Statistical Software version 22.0 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium).

To identify risk factors independently associated with PE, both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted. Variables considered for inclusion in the multivariate model were selected based on their statistical significance in univariate analysis and the assessment of correlations among independent variables. The multivariate model was constructed using the backward likelihood ratio method. Results were expressed as β coefficients ± standard errors, odds ratios (ORs), and 95% CIs.

3. Results

During the study period, 45 patients with early-onset PE were admitted to our hospital. Two patients were excluded per the exclusion criteria. Therefore, 43 cases with early-onset PE were enrolled for the analysis. Also, 45 uncomplicated gestational age-matched pregnant women were included in the control group. Post hoc power analysis based on the observed effect sizes demonstrated 95% statistical power for detecting the observed group differences at α = 0.05. Results are presented according to the dual study objectives, with comparative analysis followed by diagnostic evaluation.

Table 1 provides a summary of the demographic variables and maternal serum prostasin levels for the groups included in the study. There were no statistically significant differences found between the groups being compared with regard to age, gravida, parity, BMI, or gestational week at the time of blood sampling. Therefore, we can conclude that the groups were similar in terms of these variables. The early-onset PE group showed significantly higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels and serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and creatinine, whereas platelet counts, gestational age at delivery, and birth weight were significantly lower compared to the control group. The median maternal serum prostasin level was found to be significantly higher in the early-onset PE group (31.66 [27.12–51.00] ng/mL) compared to the control group (22.33 [16.03–25.89] ng/mL), with a P-value <.001 (Fig. 1).

To test the primary hypothesis of our study, the diagnostic performance of prostasin, our primary outcome variable, was evaluated for identifying early-onset PE using the AUC analysis. The AUC was found to be 0.820 ± 0.047 (95% CI: 0.729–0.912), indicating a statistically significant discriminative ability (P < .001). The optimal cutoff value for maternal serum prostasin level was determined using the Youden index, with a threshold of ≥26.44 ng/mL corresponding to a sensitivity of 0.837 and a specificity of 0.778 for the diagnosis of early-onset PE (Fig. 2).

Univariate logistic regression analysis (Table 2) identified several variables significantly associated with the outcome. Maternal serum prostasin levels ≥26.44 ng/mL were strongly associated with increased OR (OR: 18.0, 95% CI: 6.16–52.58, P < .001). Elevated levels of ALT (OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01–1.11, P = .016), AST (OR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.16, P = .002), and LDH (OR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03, P < .001) were also significantly associated with increased risk in univariate analysis. Platelet count did not show a statistically significant association (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.99–1.00, P = .506).

In the multivariate logistic regression model (Table 2), maternal serum prostasin level ≥26.44 ng/mL, AST, ALT, and LDH levels, and platelet count were included as independent variables. Among these, maternal serum prostasin and LDH levels remained statistically significant. Prostasin demonstrated a markedly increased OR (OR: 20.24, 95% CI: 4.95–82.79, P < .001). Based on the multivariate model results, a maternal serum prostasin value of >26.44 ng/mL increases the risk of early-onset PE by approximately 20-fold (Table 2). The selection of variables for the multivariate model was based on their univariate significance as well as the evaluation of potential multicollinearity and interrelationships among the independent variables.

4. Discussion

This study employed a comprehensive approach combining comparative pathophysiological analysis with diagnostic biomarker evaluation to investigate maternal serum prostasin in early-onset preeclampsia. Our findings demonstrate that prostasin levels were markedly elevated in the early-onset PE group compared to healthy controls, supporting the hypothesis that dysregulated protease activity contributes to the disease process. The strong discriminative performance observed in the ROC analysis further highlights the potential of prostasin as a clinically useful biomarker. Moreover, the significant odds ratio identified in the multivariate analysis suggests that elevated prostasin is not only associated with but may also serve as an independent predictor of early-onset PE.

Studying the role of serine proteases is a promising and relatively new area of research in understanding the development and progression of PE.[16] Prostasin, a serine protease associated with ENaC-activating ability, is excreted in urine and has been shown to correlate with plasma aldosterone in hypertensive patients.[12] Moreover, novel data showed that levels of prostasin are increased in the urine of women with PE.[17] However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has yet examined the relationship between maternal serum prostasin levels and early-onset PE. Our study is the first to investigate this area and demonstrates that maternal serum prostasin levels are elevated in women with early-onset PE.

Physiologically, a normal pregnancy is characterized by an augmentation in the extracellular fluid compartment and escalated activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system.[18] The positive sodium balance is achieved by renal sodium retention through ENACs in the kidney. There has been an interest in the involvement of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system in the pathogenesis of PE in recent years. It is documented that pregnancies complicated by PE are characterized by reduced plasma aldosterone concentration.[19] Increased concentrations of agonistic angiotensin II type 1 receptor antibodies are seen in the serum of preeclamptic women. Increased plasma volume expansion, which contributes to impaired perfusion of the uteroplacental unit, can lead to the development of maternal and fetal clinical symptoms of PE.[20]

Furthermore, Szabo et al revealed that elevated prostasin level is associated with aberrant placental development in a murine model.[21] This data has increased the suspicions that the prostasin may be a significant molecule in the etiology of PE. In a subsequent study, Frederiksen-Møller et al extended these findings to human samples and reported a noteworthy increase in prostasin levels in the urine of individuals with PE.[17] Overall, the findings of these studies suggest that prostasin plays a role in the development of PE and may serve as a potential biomarker for this condition. In our study, the median maternal serum prostasin level was significantly elevated in the early-onset PE group (31.66 ng/mL) compared to the control group (22.33 ng/mL), with a P-value of <.001. The optimal cutoff for maternal serum prostasin was identified as ≥26.44 ng/mL, yielding a sensitivity of 0.837 and a specificity of 0.778 for diagnosing early-onset PE. Based on the multivariate model results, a maternal serum prostasin level exceeding 26.44 ng/mL is associated with an approximately 20-fold increased risk of early-onset PE.

In the study of Frederiksen-Møller et al, preeclamptic and normotensive individuals were matched for gestational age at delivery.[17] Consequently, the exploration of prostasin in women with early-onset PE (<34 weeks’ gestation) is missed. Also, Yang et al showed increased expression of prostasin in placental tissues of cases with early-onset severe PE compared with the late-onset and control groups in human trophoblast-derived cell lines.[14] No maternal serum levels of prostasin were reported in the study. Moreover, in the study of Frederiksen-Møller et al, women in the PE group were also clinically obese, which could be a confounding factor as urinary prostasin has been found to correlate with increased adiposity in adolescents.

The choice of 32 gestational weeks for the assay is based on established literature indicating that, whereas studies on the predictive efficacy of the biomarkers like as sFlt-1/PlGF ratio in the first trimester have yielded contradictory results,[22] the predictive efficacy of these biomarkers is more reliable from the mid-trimester onwards.[23] Studies have suggested using this timeframe (30–33 gestational weeks) for screening to identify women who will develop PE and require delivery within 4 weeks.[24] This aligns with our study’s goal to assess the severity of PE during a critical period when the condition becomes clinically significant.

Against these, in our case–control study, we matched early-onset preeclamptic women with healthy normotensive controls around 32 gestational weeks and determined maternal serum prostasin levels at the time of inclusion to the study (32.55 ± 2.25 gestational weeks in the control group and 32.72 ± 1.86 gestational weeks in early-onset PE group, P = .765). We followed both groups until delivery and also the groups were similar in terms of body mass indexes. 30.24 ± 6.55 kg/m2 in control group and 31.82 ± 7.03 kg/m2 in PE group, P = .258). Based on all these reasons, we believe we were able to perform a rigorous analysis of prostasin levels in this population.

It has been found that the serine protease inhibitor aprotinin blocks the secretion of prostasin in a mouse cortical collecting duct cell line. Additionally, a synthetic serine protease inhibitor, nafamostat mesilate, which is commonly used for the treatment of pancreatitis and disseminated intravascular coagulation, also inhibits the secretion of prostasin in M-1 cells.[25] Observations from experimental animal disease models and human patients indicate that sodium restriction, inhibition of serine protease activity, and blockage of ENaC could be new causal treatment strategies for reducing blood pressure, sodium, and water retention in pregnancies suffering from PE in the future.

In our study, the BMI of all groups was calculated based on patient weights at the gestational week samples were obtained. We acknowledge the increasing importance of obesity in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. However, given that BMIs were assessed around the 32nd week of gestation, it is insufficient to establish a direct connection to the pathogenesis of the disease. Furthermore, the BMIs were similar across all groups, leading us to believe that BMI would not have a significant effect on prostasin levels. Therefore, while obesity is indeed a crucial factor in the context of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, in this study, its influence on prostasin levels was controlled by the comparable BMIs across the study groups.

This study has several limitations. First, our analysis was limited to second-trimester pregnancies. A longitudinal study evaluating maternal prostasin levels beginning in the first trimester would be valuable, considering prostasin’s involvement in early trophoblast invasion during gestation. Additionally, potential confounding factors such as underlying maternal conditions or medication use were not extensively analyzed. The extremely high multivariate ORs, while statistically significant, may reflect model overfitting due to sample size constraints and the strong association observed in this early-onset population. These findings require confirmation in larger, independent cohorts. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the study prevents the assessment of longitudinal changes in prostasin levels throughout pregnancy.

While our study is among the first to demonstrate the relationship between maternal serum prostasin levels and early-onset PE, it is important to note that further cost-analysis studies are required to evaluate the feasibility of routine use. Currently, the implementation of prostasin as a routine biomarker in clinical practice is limited by the lack of comprehensive cost-effectiveness data.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that maternal serum prostasin levels are significantly elevated in early-onset preeclamptic mothers compared to the healthy controls. These findings suggest that prostasin may play a role in early-onset PE pathophysiology and could serve as a biomarker for its diagnosis. Further studies are needed to determine the underlying mechanisms and clinical utility of this marker in predicting and monitoring early-onset PE or a therapeutic target for the disease.
