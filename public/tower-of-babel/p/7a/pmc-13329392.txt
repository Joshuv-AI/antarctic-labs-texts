Skin Cosmesis assessment tools and methods in patients with non-melanoma skin cancers treated with radiotherapy: Systematic review on behalf of the ESTRO skin and soft tissue focus group and the GEC-ESTRO head and neck and skin working group
Authors: Romaana Mir, Marcin Miszczyk, Tomasz Krzysztofiak, Bruno Fionda, Luca Tagliaferri, Vassilios Vassiliou, Agata Rembielak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-melanoma skin cancers (NMSC) are highly prevalent and rarely impact mortality; post-treatment cosmesis is paramount as the sequelae of treatment are outwardly visible. There is no agreement on reporting of post-treatment cosmesis, thereby precluding standardised comparisons between treatment modalities. We conducted this systematic review (PROSPERO: CRD420251020168) on behalf of the ESTRO Skin and Soft Tissue Focus Group and the Head and Neck and Skin GEC-ESTRO Working Group to synthesise data on how cosmesis is reported. MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science were searched for original studies including cosmesis assessment following radiotherapy for NMSC. Data was collated on the methodology, cosmesis assessment items, and assessment timeframe. Eighty-five studies were included, comprising 17,231 patients treated 1955–2023. Most studies were retrospective (72;85.0%). Three, 26, 18, 2, and 15 studies employed 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 cosmesis grades; 22 (25.9%) studies did not specify cosmesis grade. Cosmesis assessment methodology was detailed in 63 studies; 25/63 provided insufficient detail to replicate the assessment. Cosmesis assessment items: pigmentation change (87.3%), telangiectasia (84.1%), deformity (66.6%), skin atrophy and fibrosis (60.3%), and epilation (33.3%). Acute (<90d) cosmesis was reported by 25 records, late (>90d) in 35 records, with 32/35 late assessments reporting cosmesis beyond 12 months; 44 (51.8%) did not specify assessment timeframe. The identified skin cosmesis assessments are limited by heterogeneity, subjectivity, and simplified compound grades. Prospective reporting will capture the temporal changes within a newly developed granular and validated skin cosmesis reporting framework.Keywords: Skin, Cosmesis, NMSC, Radiotherapy, Radiation, Brachytherapy, Electrons, Orthovoltage, Photons, Skin cancer

Introduction

Non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSC) are highly prevalent malignancies that constitute an often-overlooked category. Oncologic research is primarily driven by improvements in survival outcomes; however, the vast majority of NMSC lesions are unlikely to progress beyond the localised stage, with little impact on mortality. NMSCs predominantly develop on the sun-exposed face and head-and-neck anatomical sites. As these anatomical sites are outwardly visible, post-treatment skin cosmesis, which could simply be described as “how good the skin looks”, is paramount to patients as the sequelae of treatment can significantly impact upon outward appearance and social functioning [1].

Despite the importance of skin cosmesis, there is no agreement on how to report or assess cosmesis in patients who have received definitive local treatments for NMSC. Moreover, the concept of cosmesis can be confused with secondary radiation effects and functional outcomes, both of which are important and distinct categories. These evidence gaps limit the understanding of short and long-term cosmetic outcomes and preclude standardised comparisons between the available treatment modalities.

We conducted this systematic review on behalf of the ESTRO Skin and Soft Tissue Focus Group and the Head and Neck and Skin GEC-ESTRO Working Group to better understand how cosmesis has been assessed in published scientific literature, and to report available standardised cosmesis scales and tools used in the context of local NMSC treatments. The project was endorsed by the ESTRO Professions and Partnerships Council and the GEC-ESTRO Committee.

Methods

The systematic review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251020168) and complied with the Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement (Supplement PRISMA 2020 Checklist) [2]. The research question was formulated using the PICOS framework (Supplementary File A). The search strategy was conducted on 3rd April 2025, and updated on 20th March 2026, in MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase (via Embase.com), and Web of Science Core Collection. No language or date filters were applied (Supplementary File B). The references were merged and deduplicated and subsequently screened using the Rayyan.ai platform [3]. Each record was screened independently by two authors at both abstract and full-text screening, with results for exclusion noted at the second stage, and conflicts resolved through consensus.

We included full text original data studies that reported on skin cosmetic outcomes following definitive radiotherapy-based treatment in patients with localised NMSC. Case reports, conference proceedings, commentaries, review articles, and non-English-language reports were excluded. The data extracted included bibliographic information, study design, cohort size, period of accrual, median follow-up, median age, sex, histopathology, lesion location, radiotherapy technique, dose, and details on cosmesis reporting including method of assessment (who and how), inclusion of skin appearance parameters, use of tools, and period / interval of assessment.

Each included study was evaluated as level 1 through to level 3 with regard to cosmesis reporting, defined as using level 1: a categorised system with defined parameters and their respective thresholds, level 2: subjective assessment based on given parameters, or level 3: no information on the methodology of cosmesis assessment. All data were extracted by two authors independently and compared. The results were tabularised and presented descriptively. Considering that the primary interest of this systematic review was methodology of cosmesis assessment, not the results of the studies, a formal risk-of-bias analysis was omitted.

Results

Of 1172 individual records screened, 85 reports published between 1979 and 2026 were included in this systematic review (Supplementary File C). These studies comprised 17,231 patients, with a median number of 69 patients per study, treated between 1955 and 2023 for 19,113 lesions, including 13,901 basal cell carcinomas (BCC), 3953 cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (cSCC), 676 other, and 583 lesions of otherwise unspecified histopathology. The average median age was 73 years with a median of 60.0% male patients per study.

Most studies were single-centre and retrospective (67; 78.8%), with five multi-centre retrospective studies (5.9%), seven single centre prospective non-randomised trials (8.2%), five multi-centre prospective non-randomised trials (5.9%), and one single-centre randomised controlled trial (1.2%). The majority (50; 58.8%) were performed in Europe, primarily in Italy (17; 20.0%) and France (11; 12.9%). Twenty (23.5%) were conducted in North America, majority of which in the United States of America (18; 21.2%). Eight studies were performed in Asia (9.4%), three in Australia (3.5%), and four studies were collaborations between North American and European centres (4.7%). Included studies are listed in Supplementary File D.

Most of the studies included patients treated primarily (>50%) for lesions located in the head and neck region (60; 70.6%), which accounted for a total of 87.7% treated lesions (Table 1). Forty-six studies (54.1%) included patients with head and neck lesions exclusively. Three studies reported approximately half of patients having lesions located in the trunk or extremities, one study focused on cancers located in the extremities, and two studies did not report on lesion location.

Brachytherapy (BT), also known as Interventional Radiotherapy (IRT), was the most common radiation delivery method, used in 51 (60.0%) studies, followed by superficial x-rays (23; 27.1%). External Beam Radiotherapy (EBRT) delivered with several modalities such as electrons, megavoltage photons, and orthovoltage photons were used in 15 (17.6%), 11 (12.9%), and 8 (9.4%) studies, respectively. Three studies (3.5%) did not specify the radiation treatment modality used. Several studies used combinations of two (11; 12.9%), three (6; 7.1%), or four (1; 1.2%) different radiation delivery techniques. Details of radiotherapy regimens are summarised in Supplementary File E.

Thirty-eight studies (44.7%) used level 1 categorised cosmesis assessment with defined assessed items and scoring systems, 25 studies (29.4%) provided basic information on categories and/or assessed items, without specifying thresholds for each category and denoted as level 2 reporting, and 22 studies (25.9%) did not specify categories or aspects that were included in the cosmesis assessment, level 3.

Among the 63 studies assessed as level 1 or 2, the majority used custom scales (37; 58.7%). The cosmesis assessment criteria included pigmentation change (55/63; 87.3%) and telangiectasia (53/63; 84.1%) in majority of the studies, more than half referred to skin deformity (42/63; 66.6%), atrophy (38/63; 60.3%), or fibrosis (38/63; 60.3%), and only one in three studies (21/63; 33.3%) mentioned epilation among assessed items (Table 2A).

The timing of assessment was specified in 41 studies (48.2%). Where timing of assessment was reported, the respective studies assessed late cosmetic outcomes during the first year (85.4%) and/or later (78.0%), with early cosmetic effects (defined as occurring within the first three months) assessed less often (61.0%; Table 2B). The question of who assessed cosmesis was addressed in 53 studies (62.4%). Where reported cosmesis was assessed most by clinicians (46/53; 86.8%), patients were asked to score cosmesis in one third of these studies (16/53; 30.2%), and three studies included independent assessors (3/53; 5.7%; Table 2C). Six studies included two independent assessors (6/53; 11.3%), and two studies - three or more (2/53; 3.8%).

[5] used in 6 studies, and a scale developed by Caccialanza et al. [6] used in three studies, neither of which were externally validated as dedicated cosmesis tools. Most scales used severity-based compound definitions, using general terms such as “slight, moderate, or severe”, facilitating quick and intuitive assessment, but not detailed assessment by clinicians. Three studies defined the severity by the distance at which a given adverse effect can be observed (e.g., “at a close inspection, at first glance”), providing a practical reference for each assessment grade. Few studies employed point systems, assigning scores to defined aspects of the skin cosmesis assessment and producing a final compound score. None of these scales included patient opinion or quality of life aspects, for example the impact of cosmetic outcome on the activities of daily living.

Discussion

This ESTRO-endorsed systematic review is the first to evaluate reporting measures for skin cosmesis following radiation therapy for NMSC. A wide variation of radiation delivery techniques are in use internationally; this variation in radiation delivery modality is a direct consequence of local radiation expertise and the prevalence of NMSC in the respective patient cohorts. The methodology of skin cosmesis assessment is poorly described in one quarter of evaluated reports, with a further third providing only the very basic information. Whilst selected cosmesis assessment items were defined in most scales, such as pigmentation change or telangiectasia, there are limited consensus regarding other items to be included in the cosmesis assessment, the timing of cosmesis assessment, or the use of tools to assess skin cosmesis. Of the six externally referenced cosmesis assessment scales, half were not skin cancer specific; moreover, most publications used systems designed for simplicity and ease of use, with highly subjective components and no reporting granularity regarding the assessment tool. Cosmesis assessment by patients was occasionally mentioned, however, none of the structured systems included patient opinion nor impact on quality of life, including social functioning. This systematic review highlights the lack of skin cancer specific cosmesis assessment tools with frequent lack of distinction between acute secondary effects from radiation therapy and functional outcomes. There is an unmet need to develop a structured and validated cosmesis assessment tool for use in studies assessing skin cosmetic outcomes following radiotherapy for NMSC.

The language used to describe skin cosmesis ranged from simply “good” and “fair” to qualitative compound definitions of the degree of cosmesis change alongside the respective cosmesis parameters i.e. “slight, moderate, or severe telangiectasia”. Two authors independently ranked the cosmesis scales in use as level 1, 2, or 3; only half (44.7%) of the studies were deemed as “level 1” cosmesis reporting, a categorised system with defined parameters and their respective thresholds. Definitive skin cosmesis tools should employ multiple qualitative parameters to assess cosmesis, as doing so will enable granular output datasets which can then discriminate between subtle threshold changes in skin cosmesis between radiation treatment modalities.

There is significant variation in the timing of cosmesis assessment, this is confounded by the finding that most of the evaluated studies were conducted retrospectively, and cosmesis outcomes were often documented as “good” or “fair” at time intervals that were not specified. Established skin cosmesis changes are seen once the short-term secondary effects of radiation therapy have resolved. Skin cosmesis change accelerates over time with hypo/hyperpigmentation, telangiectasia, and “depressed scar” appearance increasing up to 4 years post-irradiation; there is no reported parallel temporal change in keratosis or induration [18]. The majority of NMSC develop in the older patient population, this temporal change in skin appearance is not fully understood and the knowledge of temporal skin cosmesis change along with the chance of local control, perceived time-toxicity from treatment, and sequelae of treatment on psycho-social functioning will help guide patients to make informed decisions between NMSC radiotherapy modalities. Furthermore, integration of patient reported outcomes [19] through interpretation of digital tools [20] and applications, including advanced 3D and tomographic reconstruction powered by artificial intelligence will further enhance precision assessment and personalise NMSC treatment.

This ESTRO-endorsed systematic review highlights the unmet need for standardised and granular reporting of outcome measures for skin cosmesis following radiation therapy. The time points of assessment with respect to radiation delivery should be defined in a way to reflect cosmesis changes accurately. A standardised tool with granular output data would enable comparison of skin cosmetic outcomes between radiation therapy treatment modalities for NMSC and help patients make informed decisions regarding their care.

The European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) Quality of Life Group (QLG) equally recognised early NMSC are important as they are the most prevalent malignancy, with an estimated two in three Australian residents developing at least one NMSC in their lifetime [21]. The established EORTC QLG four-phase methodological framework used to develop domain-specific health-related quality of life modules in cancer is in progress regarding early BCC and cSCC, with submitted publication of phase I outcomes and application for phase II in development. The items identified as important in phase I by both patients and clinicians include both organ-site specific function and skin cosmesis, with the patient placing more importance on skin cosmesis as a clinical outcome when compared to the clinician [22].

There are several limitations to our study. Most of the evaluable studies were single-centre retrospective design, where cosmesis was not assessed in real time at given timepoints, thereby making the data interpretation challenging and notably in instances where skin cosmesis is confused with late secondary radiation effect. None of the evaluated studies detailed variation in skin type [23], nor confidently reported the impact of biological effective radiation dose, nor the use of bolus in electron and megavoltage photon therapy on cosmesis outcomes, all of which are known to impact radiation dose to the skin surface and onward skin cosmesis. Finally, considering the lack of validation for assessment of cosmesis – the frequent use of selected scales is not equivalent to confirmation of their appropriate use.

Conclusions

Based on evidence primarily from retrospective single-centre reports, this systematic review demonstrates that less than half of the studies reporting on skin cosmetic outcomes in patients treated with radiation for NMSCs provide sufficient methodologic information to replicate the cosmetic assessment. The identified skin cosmesis assessment tools are further limited by the heterogeneity, subjectivity, and simplified compound grades employed by the evaluable studies. There is an unmet need for a robust, granular, and validated skin cosmesis reporting framework with prospective reporting to capture the temporal changes in skin cosmesis. The ESTRO Skin and Soft Tissue Focus Group and the GEC-ESTRO Head and Neck and Skin Working Group will conduct work to define skin cosmesis evaluation assessments in this area of unmet need.
