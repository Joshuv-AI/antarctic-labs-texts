Early infection after aseptic revision shoulder arthroplasty: prevalence and predisposing risk factors
Authors: Samuel Lorentz, Mark M Cullen, Alyssa Henriquez, Eoghan T Hurley, Tom R Doyle, Bryan S Crook, Mikhail Bethell, Sofiu Ogunbiyi, Thorsten M Seyler, Oke Anakwenze, Christopher S Klifto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of acute prosthetic joint infections (PJIs) that develop within one year following aseptic revision total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) has been poorly described. The purpose of this study was to identify the rate of PJI following aseptic revision TSA.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed of patients who underwent aseptic revision TSA identified by Current Procedural Terminology codes 23473 and 23474 at a single academic institution between 2015 and 2022. Only patients with negative intraoperative cultures held for a minimum of 14 days were included. Patients were followed for one year postoperatively to identify acute periprosthetic joint infections, which were classified using the 2018 International Consensus Meeting shoulder-specific criteria. Demographic, perioperative, and clinical variables were compared between infected and noninfected cohorts.ResultsOverall, 140 patients met the inclusion criteria. Of these, 7 developed an acute PJI (5.0% infection rate). There was no statistical difference in patient age (64.9 vs. 69.0; P = .29), body mass index (30.4 vs. 31.0; P = .75), male sex (71% vs. 40%; P = .10), or smoking history (71% vs. 45%; P = .19) between the infected and noninfected cohorts.ConclusionAseptic revision TSA was found to have a 5.0% rate of acute PJIs within the first year postoperatively. There was no significant difference in age, body mass index, gender, history of tobacco use, underlying comorbidities, length of operative time, or length of hospitalization between patients who developed acute PJI and those who did not.Keywords: Total shoulder arthroplasty, Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty, Revision shoulder arthroplasty, Periprosthetic joint infection

With the increasing utilization of shoulder arthroplasty, it follows that the number of revision surgeries and prosthetic joint infections (PJIs) are also increasing.2,22 PJIs represent a rare yet serious complication following total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA), with reported incidence rates ranging from 1% to 3% after primary surgeries.7,15,16 These often require revision surgery and prolonged antimicrobial therapy, but outcomes remain limited, with lower functional recovery and higher complication rates.18,20 Furthermore, shoulder PJIs are associated with a 17% 5-year mortality compared to 5.1% in noninfected patients.3 Because of the increased morbidity and mortality associated with PJI, there is growing interest in researching the optimal treatment for shoulder PJIs. The 2018 International Consensus Meeting on Musculoskeletal Infection (ICM) developed criteria to define shoulder PJI, with the aim of improving identification and treatment of PJI.10

Within the hip and knee arthroplasty literature, infection following aseptic revisions has been better characterized. Cochrane et al reported a 9% infection rate following aseptic revision total knee arthroplasty, compared to a 1% infection rate reported in primary total knee arthroplasty.5,14 However, the literature has not recently quantified infection rates in revision TSA. Cofield et al cite a 15% infection rate with revision shoulder arthroplasty; however, this publication is over 30 years old.6 PJIs are important to remain aware of in the perioperative setting, as they are associated with increased morbidity and mortality as well as increased hospital stays and cost of care, representing a significant economic burden on both patients and the healthcare system.1,3,15

The purpose of this study was to determine the incidence of PJI following aseptic revision shoulder arthroplasty and to determine risk factors associated with PJI following aseptic revision surgery. The hypothesis was that the PJI rate for patients undergoing revision surgery would be higher than PJI rates reported in the literature following primary shoulder arthroplasty.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study at a single academic institution examining all patients who underwent revision shoulder arthroplasty from 2015 to 2022. Patient data were obtained through review of the electronic medical record. Inclusion criteria required patients to undergo either anatomic TSA or reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) identified by Current Procedural Terminology codes 23473 and 23474. Cases were reviewed to determine patients undergoing revision surgery. Only patients with negative intraoperative cultures from a first single-stage revision surgery were included. All cultures were held in the lab for a minimum of 14 days per lab protocol. All revision shoulder surgeries were performed by fellowship-trained shoulder surgeons.

Patient demographics

Pre-operative patient characteristics were obtained for all patients, including age, body mass index (BMI), sex, smoking history, previous surgeries, underlying medical comorbidities, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score. Additionally, perioperative information was collected, including operative time, length of hospital stay, and readmission rates. The type of revision procedure was documented for each case, along with the specific indication for revision. Indications included humeral or glenoid component loosening, glenosphere dissociation, instability, and combined issues such as instability with humeral bone loss.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint of this study was defined as either definite or probable PJI as defined by the 2018 ICM criteria within one year of aseptic revision surgery.10 The 2018 shoulder-specific ICM criteria include major criteria (sinus tract or 2 positive cultures with the same organism) and minor criteria such as elevated inflammatory markers, abnormal synovial fluid parameters, positive aspiration results, intraoperative purulence, positive histology, or a single positive culture. ICM criteria were applied retrospectively based on the documented preoperative evaluation and intraoperative findings. All patients underwent a standardized pre-operative workup that routinely included laboratory testing, joint aspiration when indicated, and pre-operative imaging. Only patients with sufficient documentation to determine the presence or absence of each ICM criterion were included. Secondary outcomes collected included final outcomes for patients, infectious organisms identified, and treatment of infection.

Statistical analysis

Standard statistical tests were used to compare the cohorts: Student’s t-test for continuous variables and Pearson χ2 test for categorical variables. Binomial univariable and multivariable regression was used to evaluate for factors related to PJI after aseptic revision. Age, BMI, diabetes, ASA scores, number of ICM minor criteria, and the number of previous surgeries were included in the analysis. The alpha level for statistical significance was set at 0.05. Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel software (version 16.71; Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and SPSS software (version 29; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Overall cohort and infected cohort

There were 140 aseptic revision TSA cases performed over the period of the study. All patients had negative intraoperative cultures at the time of revision. Seven patients (5.0%) subsequently developed an acute culture-positive infection as defined by ICM criteria. Table I summarizes the indication for initial revision surgery, timeline to infection, infection treatment, and outcome of infection of these patients. Of the 7 infected patients, 3 were considered to have definite PJIs, and 4 were considered to have probable PJIs based on ICM criteria. Four patients were infected with Cutibacterium acnes. At final follow-up, 5 of the 7 patients were infection free. Initially, 3 patients were treated with irrigation and débridement with exchange of modular components. All 3 of these patients were infection free at final follow-up. One patient was treated with single-stage revision arthroplasty and was infection free at final follow-up. Three patients were treated with planned two-stage revision. Of these, 1 patient was successfully reimplanted and was infection free at final follow-up. One patient expired prior to reimplantation. The final patient developed repeat infection after reimplantation and underwent irrigation and débridement with exchange of modular components, followed by management with chronic suppressive antibiotic therapy.

Comparison of demographics between the infected and noninfected groups

The demographic data for both patient cohorts are shown in Table II. Patients who developed an acute post-operative infection were compared with patients who did not develop an infection. There were no statistically significant differences in age (64.9 vs. 69.0; P = .29), BMI (30.4 vs. 31.0; P = .75), percent male (71% vs. 40%; P = .10), or smoking history (71% vs. 45%; P = .19) between the 2 groups. Mean follow-up was similar between the cohorts (704 days vs. 433 days; P = .11). Patients with acute infections had a greater number of prior surgeries on the affected shoulder (3.1 vs. 1.9; P < .001). No differences were found in any collected medical comorbidities, which are summarized in Table III.

Factors associated with PJI after aseptic revision

In univariate analysis (Table VI), the number of previous surgeries was found to have increased odds of infection (4.88 [1.98-12.01]; P = <.001). This persisted in multivariate analysis after controlling for factors associated with PJI (6.26 [1.95-20.08]; P = .002). Age, increased BMI, ASA score, active smoking, and increased number of ICM minor criteria were not associated with increased odds of PJI in both univariate and multivariate regression.

Discussion

The primary finding from this study was that there was a 5.0% acute infection rate in patients who underwent aseptic revision TSA. As expected, this rate is higher than infection rates seen in primary shoulder arthroplasty. PJI is an inherent risk with both primary and reverse shoulder arthroplasty that can have debilitating outcomes. Our reported infection rate appears to be the only reported infection rate of revision shoulder arthroplasty in recent literature. There was no significant difference in age, BMI, gender, history of tobacco use, underlying comorbidities, length of operative time, or length of hospitalization between the 2 cohorts.

In total this study found 7 patients with acute PJIs. Three of these patients had definite PJIs based on ICM criteria. Two of these patients were managed with planned two-stage revision. One of these patients expired prior to reimplantation. One patient was reimplanted; however, had a recurrence of infection and was ultimately managed with chronic suppressive antibiotics. The final patient classified with definite PJI was managed with a single surgery and was infection free at final follow-up. Four patients were classified with probable PJI. All 4 of these patients were managed with a single-stage surgical intervention and remained infection free at final follow-up. While it is difficult to draw conclusions from such a small cohort, these results may suggest that ICM criteria could assist in determining treatment algorithms. All 4 of our patients with probable PJI were infected with C acnes and were ultimately infection free at final follow-up. Although further research is necessary to support broad claims, patients in this study who were diagnosed with definite PJI seemed to have worse outcomes than those who were diagnosed with probable PJI and those who had infections associated with lower-virulence organisms. These patients with probable PJIs and lower virulent infections may be more appropriate candidates for single-stage surgical intervention.

Revision TSA has innate challenges, including buildup of scar tissue distorting tissue planes, altered neurovascular anatomy, and diminished bone stock.18 All of these contribute to case complexity and post-operative complications, including infection. Within this study, we expected there to be an increased incidence of comorbid medical conditions in the infected group, as prior studies have identified that patients with diabetes, liver disease, alcohol abuse, iron deficiency anemia, and rheumatoid arthritis are at an increased risk of PJI following primary shoulder arthroplasty.8,19 Furthermore, Saltzman et al found that all complications following reverse TSA are higher in revision surgery compared to primary surgery.18 In this study we did not find a statistically significant difference with regards to these comorbidities. Given the small number of infected cases, the study was not powered to detect modest differences between groups. Interestingly, the only factor that did reach significance was the number of prior surgeries, with infected patients having undergone more previous procedures on the affected shoulder, and an increased number of surgeries on both univariate and multivariate analysis. We anticipate this is due to the aforementioned innate challenges associated with revision surgeries including scar tissue buildup and decreased tissue perfusion ultimately leading to delayed healing and infection.18

Furthermore, male gender and smoking history, while not reaching statistical significance, were increased in the infected cohort. Male gender has been reported as a risk factor for shoulder PJI,11,20 with males having an increased risk of a C acnes infection.4,13,17 In regard to smoking history, Hatta et al examined a group of 814 smokers and 1,020 non-smokers who underwent primary TSA with a minimum 2-year follow-up.12 They found that smokers had lower infection-free survival and complication-free survival compared to nonsmokers. Further, when they examined current smokers, they found higher rates of periprosthetic fractures compared to former smokers and nonsmokers. Thus, we anticipate that with a larger cohort of patients our study would have the power to detect statistically significant differences in male gender and smoking as risk factors for acute PJI following revision rTSA.

This study did not evaluate technical factors or patient factors as potential causes for infection, although these may play a role. In 2011, Umscheid et al estimated that up to 55% of surgical site infections are preventable with evidence-based strategies including appropriate use of perioperative antibiotics, decreased use of shaving, and improving perioperative glucose control.21 While many of these have become common practice now, Umscheid's study used data largely from the late 1990s when these practices may not have been as widely utilized. Thus, surgeons should be attuned to current changes and developments in the infection literature, as this may improve patient outcomes.

Limitations

This study was limited by its retrospective design. Additionally, this study is reliant on data included in the electronic medical record; thus, it is possible that some acute infections could have been missed if patients did not follow up within our medical system or if documentation was inaccurate. In addition, the ICM criteria were applied retrospectively, and preoperative evaluations were not uniformly performed across all patients, which may have introduced variability in how infection was classified. Overall, our total number of infections was relatively low, with only 7 patients diagnosed with acute PJI. This limits our ability to detect small differences between groups, and a formal power analysis was not performed. However, our cohort of 140 patients does appear to be the largest study to date evaluating acute infection rate in revision TSA, given that infections are relatively rare events. It would be beneficial to accumulate a larger sample size in the future, which may provide the power necessary to detect further statistically significant differences.

Furthermore, shoulder infections can be difficult to diagnose and manage as they often present with nonspecific clinical symptoms and are often due to low-virulence organisms.9,17 These infections can remain indolent and are often difficult to culture or require weeks to grow in culture. This should be considered when interpreting our results, as there may be patients in both our infected and noninfected cohorts who were infected at the time of initial revision surgery despite negative cultures.

Conclusion

Aseptic revision TSA had a 5% rate of acute infection within the first postoperative year. Although no statistically significant differences in demographic or medical risk factors were identified between infected and noninfected patients, the number of prior surgeries on the affected shoulder was associated with increased risk of infection. These findings suggest that the cumulative burden of previous procedures may be an important contributor to postoperative infection in revision TSA. Continued attention to surgical technique, preoperative evaluation, and strategies that limit repeated operations may help reduce the risk of acute infection in this challenging population.
