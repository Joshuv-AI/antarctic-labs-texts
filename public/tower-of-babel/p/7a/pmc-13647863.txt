Socio-demographic determinants, mediators and moderators of body image dissatisfaction and distortion in Black South African women aged 18 years and older: SANHANES-1
Authors: Antony Kudakwashe Chiromba, Zandile June-Rose Mchiza, Christine Taljaard-Krugell, Kim Anh Nguyen, Johann Jerling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBody image concerns are important as they negatively affect health and nutrition status. With rising obesity rates among Black South African women, understanding obesity from a socio‑behavioural perspective is imperative. Despite extensive work on obesity determinants, little is known about the causal pathways underlying body image dissatisfaction (BID) and body size distortion (BSD) in this population. This study provides the first causal‑pathway analysis of BID and BSD among Black South African women using nationally representative survey data.MethodsWe analysed nationally representative data from SANHANES‑1 (2012) for Black South African women aged ≥ 18 years. A complete case analysis approach was used (n = 1,514). BID and BSD were categorised (none/low/moderate/high). Determinants were examined using multinomial logistic regression (unadjusted, age‑adjusted, fully adjusted). Multiple mediation tested whether body mass index (BMI) and education mediated socio‑demographic factors (age, marital status, income, geographic location) on BID and BSD. Moderation tested interactions among socio‑demographics.ResultsObesity, low education (Grades 1–5), being married or divorced/widowed and urban informal residence were strong predictors of high BID. BSD was primarily driven by BMI, with both obesity and underweight status showing the highest distortion. Income and marital status partially predicted BSD. BMI emerged as a consistent mediator linking age, marital status, and income to both BID and BSD. Education did not mediate any of these relationships. A significant location × income interaction indicated that BSD increased at higher income levels depending on geographic location.ConclusionsFindings distinguish BID (socio‑culturally patterned) from BSD (predominantly BMI‑anchored). They identify BMI as the central mediator linking socio‑demographic factors to body image outcomes and highlight contextual amplification of BSD among higher‑income women by location. These causal pathways provide important insights for targeted and culturally relevant interventions aimed at addressing body image concerns and informing obesity‑prevention strategies among South African women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28531-6.Keywords: Determinants, Mediators, Socio-demography, Body image dissatisfaction, Body size distortion, Black women, South Africa

Introduction

Obesity has doubled in many low- and middle-income countries over the past decade [15]. However, sub-Saharan African women are disproportionately affected [1]. An estimated 50% of South African adults live with overweight or obesity [22]. A high body mass index (BMI) contributes to approximately 11% of premature mortality [7, 23]. Obesity and related non-communicable diseases (NCDs) have substantial economic implications for healthcare spending. South Africa's healthcare costs related to obesity were estimated at R33–36 billion annually [5, 27].

Obesity is a complex multi-dimensional condition shaped by biological, behavioural, socio-economic, and cultural influences. Obesity in South African women was described as an intersection of multiple personal and social risk factors [19]. Risk factors include educational attainment, socioeconomic status, urbanisation, and sociocultural norms around eating and body size [19]. Body image is a key socio-behavioural factor underlying obesity risk in African women [17, 19]. There is growing global recognition of the impact of body image on health across societies [33]. Body image is an individual’s perceptions, thoughts, and feelings about their body [12]. Despite appearing conceptually simple, body image is multifaceted and dynamic. Body image is influenced by personal beliefs, social expectations, and broader cultural environments [13].

Body image has several constructs and measurements. Body image dissatisfaction (BID) and body size distortion (BSD) are commonly used constructs. BID is the perceived discrepancy between one’s current and ideal body size [8]. BSD is the inaccuracy with which individuals estimate their own body size [11]. About 84.5% of South Africans aged ≥ 15 years failed to accurately estimate their body size and nearly half underestimated their weight [18]. BSD may arise from internalised beliefs and lived experiences [10]. Although African evidence is limited, BSD has been associated with negative psychological and behavioural outcomes. For example, BSD was linked to low self-esteem, disordered eating and poor health-seeking practices in Korean women [14]. African societies tend to normalise larger bodies and equate obesity with extremely large, incapacitating body sizes [31]. Thus, obesity may be perceived to have a low-risk to health [6, 25, 26].

Body image in African women is shaped by a broad range of determinants (Fig. 1). These include demographic, social, cultural and psychological influences [29]. Mchiza et al. [17] analysed SANHANES-1 data and reported associations between BID/BSD with geographic location, gender, ethnicity, marital status, education level and income. However, the reported associations did not fully disaggregate by gender and race. Evidence suggests that African women's body image ideals vary by age, urbanisation levels, cultural exposure and social identities [4, 9, 30, 38, 41].

Determinants of body image in African women

Current public health goals in South Africa emphasise reducing obesity. Yet body image, as a macro-level determinant of eating behaviour and weight-related practices, may hinder progress if its drivers remain poorly understood. Existing literature highlights several socio-demographic correlates of body image. However, little evidence exists on the causal pathways linking socio-demographic factors to BID and BSD in Black South African women. Identifying these pathways is crucial for understanding how structural and behavioural factors interact to shape body image outcomes. This evidence contributes towards informing culturally appropriate obesity-prevention strategies. To address the knowledge gap, this study applied advanced causal-pathway modelling to clarify how socio-demographic factors influence BID and BSD.

The study investigated the socio-demographic determinants, mediators and moderators of BID and BSD among Black South African women aged 18 years and older. The study hypothesised that age, geographic location, income, and marital status function as predictors, while BMI and education level act as potential mediators. SANHANES-1 data were used to analyse the causal pathways underlying BID and BSD. Findings aim to advance understanding of body image in African women and support identifying key leverage points for intervention.

Methods

Study design

This study analysed data from the 2012 South African National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (SANHANES‑1), a nationally representative cross‑sectional survey conducted by the Human Sciences Research Council (HSRC). SANHANES‑1 employed a multistage, stratified cluster sampling design to select households across all nine provinces, stratified by province and locality type (rural formal, rural informal, urban informal, urban formal) [37]. The present analysis included 1,514 Black South African women aged ≥ 18 years with complete anthropometric and body image data.

Measures

Body image outcomes

Body Image Dissatisfaction (BID) was measured using the Feel‑Ideal Discrepancy (FID) derived from the Stunkard silhouette scale. BID was calculated as the difference between the participant’s perceived body size and ideal body size [8, 39]. BID scores were categorised into no discrepancy (x = 0), low (−2 < x ≠ 0 < 2), moderate (−3 < x < −2,2 < x < 3) and high (x < −3; x > 3). As the values were expressed in BMI units, no discrepancy was defined as an exact match between perceived and ideal body size. Low discrepancy (± < 2 BMI units) reflected minor changes that are not barely perceptible visually. Moderate and high discrepancies (≥ 2 BMI units) represented progressively more noticeable and meaningful deviations that correspond to visually perceptible changes.

BSD assessed perceptual accuracy by subtracting the BMI‑correlated “feel” silhouette from the participant’s measured BMI [11]. BSD was categorised as no discrepancy, low, moderate and high. BSD scores were categorised into no discrepancy (−0.5 < x < 0.5), low (− 2 < x < − 0.5,0.5 < x < 2), moderate (−3 < x < −2; 2 < x < 3) and high (x < −3; x > 3). For BSD, the “no discrepancy” category was defined as a range to capture small, non-zero differences between the BMI estimate derived from the selected silhouette and the participant’s actual BMI, allowing for mapping discrete silhouettes onto continuous BMI values.

Anthropometry

Weight and standing height were measured using standardised SANHANES‑1 procedures [37]. BMI was calculated as kg/m2 and categorised as underweight (< 18.5), normal (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9), and obese (≥ 30).

Socio‑demographic determinants

Age was grouped into six categories, 15–24 to 65 + years. Marital status was in four categories (never married; married; divorced/separated/widowed; living together). Educational attainment was in five categories (no schooling; Grades 1–5; Grades 6–11; Grade 12; tertiary). Household income (South African Rands) was in four categories (no income; R401–4800; R4801–38400; R38401 +). Geographic location had four categories (rural formal, rural informal, urban informal, urban formal) as per the SANHANES-1 categorisation.

Data analysis

Analyses were conducted in SPSS version 30 and R version 4.5.2 (RStudio 2024.09.2). Significance was set at p < 0.05. A complete case analysis was conducted. Participants with missing outcome or predictor data were excluded using listwise deletion. To minimise sample attrition for each outcome due to missing values, separate datasets were prepared.

Descriptive analysis

Weighted descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics. Cross‑tabulations of socio‑demographic variables by BID and BSD categories were assessed using chi‑square tests with Monte‑Carlo correction where expected cell counts were < 5.

Determinant analysis

Multinomial logistic regression was used to identify determinants of BID and BSD as both outcomes were nominal with four categories. Three models were estimated for each outcome variable: unadjusted, age‑adjusted and fully adjusted model including all socio‑demographic predictors. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals.

Mediation analysis

Multiple mediation analysis examined whether BMI and education mediated relationships between socio‑demographic predictors and BID/BSD. Models were estimated in R using the Lavaan package. BMI and education were selected based on literature suggesting their theoretical roles in pathways between socio‑demographic factors and body image [17, 42].

Moderation analysis

Moderation was assessed by introducing interaction terms between socio‑demographic variables (age × location, income × location, marital status × income, etc.) in fully adjusted models. Significant interactions were probed using marginal effects.

Ethics approval

Ethical approval for SANHANES‑1 was obtained from the Human Science Research Council (HSRC) Research Ethics Committee. SANHANES‑1 participants provided written informed consent (or parental consent for minors). This secondary analysis was approved by the NWU Health Research Ethics Committee (NWU‑00079‑25‑A1). Data access for this secondary analysis was reviewed and approved by the HSRC.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics by BID and BSD

Data from 1,511 and 1,514 Black South African women aged ≥ 18 years were analysed for BID and BSD respectively. Variation in total sample for each outcome was a result of preparing each outcome’s dataset independently to minimise sample attrition by listwise deletion of participants with complete data on one outcome but not the other. The distribution across age groups was relatively even, with each group comprising over 200 individuals. Most participants were classified as overweight or obese, with underweight women forming the smallest subgroup. Elevated levels of both body image dissatisfaction (BID) and body size distortion (BSD) were observed across socio‑demographic categories. For instance, high BID ranged from 26% among women aged 15–24 to over 40% in older groups, while high BSD consistently exceeded 60% across all age categories.

Patterns in BID and BSD differed across BMI categories. High BID was most prevalent among obese (49%) and underweight women (43%) while high BSD was very prevalent among obese women (84%) and underweight women (65%). By education level, women with Grades 1–5 had the highest prevalence of high BID (51%) and high BSD (67%). Geographic location showed minimal variation in BSD, with high discrepancy exceeding 60% regardless of setting. Full descriptive statistics are shown in Table 1.

Determinants of body image dissatisfaction (BID)

Unadjusted models

In unadjusted multinomial regression analyses, several socio‑demographic factors were significantly associated with BID (Supplementary Table 1). Women aged 35–44, 45–54, and 55–64 years showed higher odds of high BID compared with women aged 15–24 (e.g., 35–44 years: OR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.36–3.09). By BMI, both obesity and underweight status increased odds of high BID, with obesity showing a strong association (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.24–2.19). Low education (Grades 1–5) also predicted higher BID (OR = 1.67), while Grade 12 and tertiary education predicted lower BID or higher likelihood of no discrepancy.

Marital status was also positively associated with BID. Married and divorced/widowed women had higher odds of high BID than never‑married women. Income showed weak or non‑significant relationships, except the middle‑income group (R4801–38400) which was associated with lower odds of low and moderate BID. Women in both rural formal and urban informal areas had elevated odds of high BID relative to women in rural informal areas.

Age‑adjusted models

After adjusting for age (Supplementary Table 2), BMI remained a strong predictor of BID. Obese women continued to show higher odds of high BID (OR = 1.62) with significantly lower odds of both low and moderate BID. Low education (Grades 1–5) remained positively associated with high BID (OR = 1.74). Tertiary education continued to show increased odds of low BID. Positive associations by marital status patterns persisted. Married and divorced/widowed women maintained higher odds of high BID (e.g., married: OR = 1.45). Income associations diminished after age adjustment suggesting confounding by age. Location effects remained significant for rural formal and urban informal areas.

Fully adjusted models

In the fully adjusted models (Table 2 below), BMI remained the strongest determinant of high BID with obesity (OR = 1.58) and borderline for underweight (OR = 1.83). Women with low education (Grades 1–5) maintained higher odds of a high BID (OR = 1.65). Tertiary‑educated women showed the strongest likelihood of low BID compared with those with no schooling.

Determinants of body size distortion (BSD)

Unadjusted models

Supplementary Table 3 shows the unadjusted analysis. Age was initially associated with BSD, with all age groups ≥ 25 years showing significantly higher odds of high BSD than the 15–24 group. Odds of high BSD by age group were 25–34 years (OR = 2.24), 35–44 years (OR = 2.14), 45–54 years (OR = 3.43), 55–64 years (OR = 2.61) and 65 + years (OR = 2.11). BMI dominated all BSD predictors, with both obese (OR = 22.06) and underweight women (OR = 17.21) showing extremely high odds of high BSD. Overweight status showed increased odds of high BSD (OR = 1.83) but decreased odds of low BSD (OR = 0.46).

Education was not significantly associated with BSD, although Grades 1–5 showed borderline associations (OR = 2.01). High income (R38,401 +) was associated with reduced odds of moderate BSD (OR = 0.25). Marital status effects were limited, with married women showing increased odds of high BSD and cohabiting women showing reduced odds of low BSD. Geographic location did not significantly predict BSD.

Age‑adjusted models

Supplementary Table 4 shows the age-adjusted analysis. BMI remained the strongest predictor of BSD. Obese (OR = 21.71) and underweight women (OR = 17.03) women had increased odds for high BSD. High income continued to show lower odds of moderate BSD (OR = 0.27). Marital status showed minor changes. Married women showed borderline higher odds of high BSD (OR = 2.02) and cohabiting remained protective for low BSD (OR = 0.46). Both education and location remained non‑significant.

Fully adjusted models

In the fully adjusted models (Table 3 below), age effects disappeared, confirming that age was not an independent predictor of BSD. BMI continued to show the largest effect sizes of any variable (obesity: OR = 21.76; underweight: OR = 16.83), with overweight status also associated with higher odds of high BSD (OR = 1.80). High income remained associated with substantially lower odds of both moderate (OR = 0.24) and high BSD (OR = 0.42). Education and location remained non‑significant. Cohabiting women consistently showed lower odds of low BSD (OR = 0.46).

BMI as a mediator of BID

For BID, BMI significantly mediated the effects of age, marital status, and income. Among age groups, significant BMI‑mediated indirect effects were observed for women aged 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, and 65 +, with the strongest mediated pathways in the 35–44 and 45–54 age categories (e.g., 35–44 years, β = 0.065, p = 0.004). For marital status, BMI mediated the effects of married or divorced/separated/widowed women, consistent with higher BMI levels in these subgroups and their elevated BID scores (β = 0.026–0.041; p < 0.05). For income, BMI mediated effects primarily for the middle‑income group (R4,801–38,400) (β = 0.018; p = 0.045) with borderline effects for the highest‑income category. Geographic location did not show mediation through BMI, indicating that location effects on BID operate directly and not via BMI or education.

BMI as a mediator of BSD

Similarly, for BSD, BMI significantly mediated the effects of age, income, and marital status, but not geographic location. Across all age categories, BMI showed strong indirect effects, indicating that age‑related BSD differences are primarily attributable to BMI differences (e.g., 45–54 years: β = 0.387; p < 0.001). For marital status, BMI significantly mediated relationships for married and divorced/separated/widowed women (β = 0.122–0.192; p < 0.01). For income, BMI mediated associations for the middle‑income category, with borderline mediation in both the low‑ and high‑income categories.

Education as a mediator

Education did not significantly mediate any socio‑demographic pathway for either BID or BSD. This aligns with regression findings showing inconsistent or weak direct associations between education and the outcomes.

Discussion

This study examined the socio‑demographic determinants, mediators, and moderators of body image dissatisfaction (BID) and body size distortion (BSD) among Black South African women using a nationally representative dataset. The findings provide new causal‑pathway insights into body image formation in African women where evidence remains limited.

Determinants of BID and BSD

Several socio‑demographic characteristics independently predicted BID, with BMI emerging as the strongest determinant, consistent with prior South African research [17]. Women with obesity demonstrated substantially higher BID, aligning with evidence that overweight and obese women tend to desire smaller body sizes. Interestingly, married and divorced/widowed women, groups with higher obesity prevalence, also showed elevated BID. These finding challenges longstanding assumptions that social expectations of weight gain after marriage signals stability and wellbeing [26]. These patterns suggest that shifting cultural norms, globalisation and increased health awareness may be transforming body image preferences among contemporary African women, especially in urbanised contexts [4, 38].

Education level also predicted BID. Low education was associated with higher BID whereas tertiary education was associated with reduced BID. This inverse pattern may reflect broader structural advantages such as improved health literacy and critical reasoning that are associated with higher educational attainment [32]. Education may thus enable women to resist internalised body ideal pressures or to cultivate more positive body image constructs.

Age effects were modest but indicated that middle‑aged women (35–44 years) were more likely to report high BID than younger women. This corresponds with evidence showing life‑stage diversity in body image preferences, where younger women tend to prefer slimmer bodies for health and aesthetic reasons while older women often value fuller figures for cultural and social reasons [9].

For BSD, BMI was the most dominant predictor. Women at both BMI extremes (i.e., underweight and obese) were far more likely to misjudge their body size, consistent with international and African findings [17]. This suggests that perceptual distortions may arise from emotional distress, stigma, or difficulties aligning self‑perception with cultural or biomedical norms [36]. In contrast to BID, education and geographic location did not predict BSD indicating that BSD may be less shaped by structural or socio‑cultural factors.

Income showed protective effects for BSD at higher income levels, possibly reflecting greater access to health information, self‑monitoring, or exposure to thin‑ideal norms [43]. Marital status only weakly predicted BSD, with cohabiting women consistently showing lower odds of low BSD. Further studies are needed to explore this pattern. Together, these findings reveal that whereas BID is socio-culturally patterned, BSD is overwhelmingly BMI‑driven, with minimal influence from structural determinants.

Mediators of BID and BSD

A key contribution of this study is the demonstration that BMI is a consistent mediator linking socio‑demographic characteristics to both BID and BSD. BMI mediated the effects of age, marital status, and income on both outcomes but did not mediate geographic location. These patterns align with prior work showing that socio‑demographic factors shape adiposity in African populations [2, 3, 16, 20, 21, 24, 35], which in turn influences body image perceptions.

Aging and marital status are associated with increased adiposity, partly due to lifestyle changes and metabolic shifts [28, 40]. Higher income similarly intersects with obesogenic environments in urban settings [3, 19]. These pathways reinforce that BMI is a central leverage point linking socio-demographic factors to body image outcomes.

In contrast, education did not mediate any relationships. This finding suggests that education’s influence on BID is likely direct and may operate through psychological or social routes. Education improves critical thinking skills which may buffer against social pressures on beauty standards [34]. Thus, it may function more as a confounder than a mediator in these pathways as highlighted by the non-significant associations observed in regression models.

Moderators of BID and BSD

Another key finding is the absence of moderation between socio‑demographic variables for BID. This finding indicates that the relationships between predictors and BID are additive and not multiplicative. This suggests that socio‑demographic factors such as age, marital status and education influence BID independently rather than synergistically.

For BSD, however, a significant location × income interaction emerged. BSD was higher among women with greater incomes in certain geographic settings, suggesting that exposure to urban environments and associated social pressures may intensify distortion among higher‑income women [4, 30]. This finding highlights contextual amplification, where socio‑economic resources intersect with environmental factors such as media exposure or social comparison to shape body size perception.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of a large nationally representative dataset, the application of advanced statistical modelling and the focus on an under‑studied population group. Limitations include the cross‑sectional design of the data which prevents causal inference. In addition, the SANHANES-1 was conducted 14 years ago and as body image evolves over time, it may not adequately reflect the current situation. Lastly, sparse data for some subgroups (e.g., tertiary education, underweight women) warrant the interpretation of the findings with some caution.

Conclusions

This study contributes significant new evidence on the causal pathways linking socio‑demographic characteristics to BID and BSD among Black South African women. BMI emerged as the dominant determinant and mediator across both outcomes, highlighting its central role in the relationship between social context and body image. BID was shaped by broader socio‑demographic factors including education, marital status and urban informal residence. However, BSD was driven almost entirely by extremes in BMI with limited contributions from structural determinants. The identification of BMI as a consistent mediator across age, marital status, and income underscores the need to integrate body‑image considerations into obesity‑prevention efforts, especially for populations experiencing rapid nutrition transition. The identification of a location × income interaction for BSD suggests that the influence of increased social comparison from exposure to urbanised beauty standards.

These findings offer useful and actionable insights for designing culturally relevant public‑health interventions. By clarifying how socio‑demographic factors and contextual influences interact, it identifies key leverage points for interventions aimed at supporting healthier body image and reducing obesity‑related risks among South African women. Specifically, BID prevention and support strategies should consider socio‑economic disparities and shifting cultural ideals. On the other hand, BSD interventions may require perceptual and psychological components to address distorted self‑assessments. Despite the cross‑sectional data that limits causal inference, the study offers a stronger understanding of body‑image pathways than previously available. Future research should employ longitudinal and qualitative approaches to deepen understanding of these pathways to explore how cultural identity, media exposure and evolving gender norms shape body image among African women.
