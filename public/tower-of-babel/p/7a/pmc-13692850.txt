Challenges of AI for cost-effectiveness in health: a hybrid MCDM model
Authors: Yeter Uslu, Erman Gedikli, Emre Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to elucidate the role of artificial intelligence (AI) in achieving cost-effectiveness within healthcare services, and to address the challenges that hinder its integration.MethodsA hybrid multi-criteria decision-making model based on the DEMATEL and ARAS methods was designed. Data were collected with the participation of ten experts working in the fields of health management, health economics and health policy in Canada, India, Switzerland, USA and Türkiye. The causal relationships between the challenges were analyzed using DEMATEL analysis and the solutions to these challenges were evaluated using the ARAS method.ResultsAs a result of the DEMATEL analysis, the “Regulatory and ethical concerns (REC)” criterion reached the highest level of importance (13.94%), followed by “High initial costs and financial constraints (ICFC)” (13.65%) and “Lack of data standardization and biases (DBHQ)” (13.29%). In the ARAS evaluation, among the proposed solutions, “Carrying out comprehensive awareness-raising activities for patients and their relatives (SS3)” achieved the highest performance value (Ki = 0.93), followed by “Providing inclusive incentives for AI transformation (SS5)” (Ki = 0.87) and “Establishing a blockchain-based cybersecurity system (SS2)” (Ki = 0.85).ConclusionsTo achieve cost-effectiveness through AI in healthcare, it is imperative to establish a robust, integrated, and standardized data infrastructure, develop comprehensive ethical and regulatory frameworks, and implement patient education programs to foster trust and acceptance of AI technologies. This study provides policymakers and practitioners with evidence-based recommendations to facilitate the successful integration of AI in healthcare, ultimately enhancing cost-efficiency and service delivery.Keywords: AI, Cost-effectiveness, DEMATEL, ARAS

Introduction

The addition of AI to healthcare systems is one of the factors that has the potential of enhancing efficiency, quality, and accessibility of healthcare services. This involves a few related sustainability risks, as well as cost-effectiveness concerns, as well as integration barriers. Amid challenges of healthcare systems maintaining costs high while demands of their services escalate, AI has been numbered as an ideal solution due to its potential of enhancing efficiency as well as health outcomes [6, 52, 53].

AI can meaningfully reduce costs while improving individual and community health interventions through the contributions it can provide to decision-makers, policymakers in diagnosis and treatment processes. Indeed, research on these issues shows that AI applications can facilitate processes, increase diagnostic accuracy and optimize treatment plans, leading to significant cost savings in the long term [4, 32]. AI systems have shown significant potential in early disease detection, particularly in medical imaging [2]. In addition, AI’s ability to analyze big data could enable early detection of diseases and personalized treatments, unlike conventional treatment methods, reducing rising healthcare costs and the overall burden on healthcare systems [17, 24, 40].

Understanding both the direct and indirect economic impacts of AI in health is crucial for healthcare providers, payers, third parties and policy makers when evaluating investment decisions and resource allocation strategies [59]. In fact, it may be possible to create a cost-effective healthcare ecosystem by minimizing inefficiencies using AI [23]. However, while cost-effectiveness is heavily discussed in the literature, the economic sustainability of AI in healthcare can also have an impact through cost savings achieved through the capabilities of AI. Indeed, single-source-of-care studies and local research support the potential for AI technologies to deliver tangible cost reductions, operational efficiencies, and improved clinical outcomes in healthcare. Evidence from cost-effectiveness studies in other areas, such as cancer, chronic disease management (e.g., early diagnosis and personalized treatment), and medical imaging, has demonstrated that future projects and AI integration can support varying levels of return on investment [56, 57].

Despite its benefits towards the future and sustainability of healthcare systems, economic analysis of artificial intelligence in healthcare also reveals some drawbacks. In fact, several studies featured the long-term effectiveness and related ambiguities of AI [4, 59]. In that respect, several studies have reflected that the initial costs connected with AI deployments, against the need for continuous maintenance and upgrades, create appreciable financial barriers specifically within healthcare systems that are placed in developing countries [3, 35]. Further, it has been noted that the use of AI reveals remarkable heterogeneity across different healthcare environments and populations [48].

Within this context, while there are multiple opportunities pertaining to the achievement of cost efficiency, it is also crucial to identify the barriers that exist pertaining to the introduction of artificial intelligence (AI). A systematic literature review of the Web of Science and Scopus databases was conducted to identify the issues that deterred the use of AI towards embracing cost efficiency in the healthcare industry. Articles that were published over the last five years (2020–2024) were examined by using the terms "Artificial Intelligence," "Healthcare," "Cost-Effectiveness," "Economic Evaluation," "Integration Barriers," and "Sustainability." The barriers towards integrating AI that aim at achieving cost-effectiveness in healthcare may be summarized as follows:

High initial costs and financial constraints (ICFC): Particularly for healthcare services, safeguarding and anonymizing of health data may involve constructing expensive data store technologies and tailor-made algorithms that are appropriate for every society/territory. These may also entail investments that are appropriate for infrastructures at the physical level as well as software requirements [4, 7, 8, 25].

Data privacy and cyber security concerns (DPCC): In order to protect and store health data, it is necessary to take advanced technology security measures and dynamically control data relationships [2, 21, 36, 37].

Lack of data standardization, data biases/need for high-quality data (DBHQ): The need for large, integrated datasets is emphasized, with the underutilization of existing data identified as a significant impediment to achieving the desired cost-effectiveness in healthcare AI applications. [4, 23, 25, 29, 37].

Algorithmic biases and transparency challenges (ABTC): Algorithmic biases can lead to inequitable treatment outcomes and diminish the effectiveness of AI applications. These factors contribute to the complexity of integrating AI into healthcare systems, which, in turn, may hinder cost-effectiveness [41, 42].

Necessity for tailored (personalized) AI solutions (NTAS): The requirement for personalized treatment plans, necessitating the development of similarly tailored AI solutions, presents a challenge to achieving cost-effectiveness in healthcare [7].

Emphasizing patient concerns that could hinder cost-effectiveness (EPCC): Patients may harbor concerns about the misuse of their data or being directed towards inappropriate treatment. Such apprehensions can impede the adoption of AI solutions and impact cost-effectiveness in healthcare. Furthermore, addressing these concerns may necessitate additional education, explanations, and transparency initiatives to build trust [41, 47].

Regulatory and ethical concerns (REC): Challenges such as bias in AI technologies, the need for explainability, and regulatory approvals are also identified. Furthermore, the necessity of legal regulations to prevent dilemmas between individual health and economic evaluation, or to provide guidance in such situations, can be considered a significant challenge. [4, 21, 25, 46, 47].

Competency gap and training needs (CGTN): These factors contribute to the reluctance of healthcare providers to adopt AI technologies, thereby hindering potential cost-saving benefits. [8, 13, 49].

In existing literature, with methodological perspective, multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) approaches are widely used in healthcare for topics such as cost-effectiveness analysis, resource allocation, and health technology assessment [9, 54]. However, these approaches generally analyze independent criteria and fail to adequately consider the interactions and causal structures between criteria. For example, the DEMATEL method has been used in some health and management studies due to its capacity to model causal relationships between criteria [11]. Furthermore, ARAS-based ranking methods are among the preferred techniques for systematically comparing the relative performance of alternatives. However, although hybrid methods such as COPRAS–ARAS have been developed in the literature [15], these studies do not directly integrate causal mapping and strategy ranking. At this point, our study presents a methodological innovation by presenting a hybrid structure that identifies cause-effect relationships with DEMATEL while also listing strategic solutions with ARAS, and provides a more evidence-based direction to the decision-making processes of policy makers in the context of health economics. Moreover, it contributes to the literature in several ways. First, while previous studies have separately examined AI adoption challenges [21, 46] and cost-effectiveness [59], few have combined causal mapping with solution prioritization in a single framework. Second, by directly integrating cause-effect analysis with strategy sequencing, our hybrid DEMATEL-ARAS model provides policymakers with a more evidence-based and actionable roadmap for cost-effectively implementing AI. Third, the study expands the empirical base by using a diverse panel of experts from different countries and disciplines, thereby increasing external validity [27].

This study employs a rigorous analysis of the challenges encountered in leveraging AI to achieve cost-effectiveness within healthcare services. By revealing the influence and dependencies among various obstacles, the analysis will enable the formulation of targeted recommendations for both practitioners and policymakers. By identifying the influence and dependencies of these obstacles, the research provides a prioritized ranking of challenges and offers evidence-based policy recommendations to facilitate the successful integration of AI in healthcare, ultimately optimizing cost-efficiency. These steps collectively support the main objective of providing policy-relevant recommendations for integrating AI into healthcare cost-effectively.

Material and methods

Data for this cross-sectional study was collected between 10 and 20th March 2025. This study is a hybrid multi-criteria decision-making study designed according to DEMATEL and ARAS approaches. Written informed consent was provided by all participants using the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The scientific suitability of the research was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of a university (Date: 06/03/2025, Decision No: 254).

Participants

According to literature review results, the challenges that prevent the use of Ai in healthcare services from contributing to cost-effective processes have been revealed and categorized. Ten different experts, from the fields of health management, health economics, health financing, health policies and global health systems, living and working in Canada, India, Switzerland, USA and Türkiye evaluated the challenges obtained and the solution proposals to be developed against these challenges. In the selection of participants, the possibility of having at least ten years of academic or professional experience for their areas of expertise was sought (Table 1). Participants made their judgments on the subject through DEMATEL and ARAS questionnaires.

Details of analysis

In this study, we first compared the challenges faced by AI in the process of achieving cost-effectiveness in healthcare using the DEMATEL method. In line with the values obtained as a result of this comparison, we categorized the challenges into 'dispatcher' (influencer) and 'receiver' (affected) ones. Based on the dispatcher challenges we identified, we argued with the authors of the article to develop solution suggestions for these challenges. We then evaluated these suggested solutions, together with the dispatcher criteria, using the ARAS method. As a result of this analysis, we have systematically identified which solutions should be prioritized for policymakers and the order in which they can be implemented. This process aims to provide a strategic pathway for effectively addressing the challenges of integrating AI into health services and ensuring cost-effectiveness. DEMATEL method

DEMATEL is a methodology for constructing and analyzing a structural model that includes the causal relationships between complex factors such as [62, 63]. Apart from the other multicriteria decision-making techniques, DEMATEL assumes that there is a causal relationship between criteria. DEMATEL is based on graph theory and solves problems with directed graphs, known as digraphs. They visualize factors into cause group and effect group and represent a communication network [30, 63]. In DEMATEL analysis, the factors are compared to each other with the numbers between 0 and 4, and their influence levels are obtained. ARAS Method

Additive Ratio Assessment (ARAS) is an MCDM technique that permits priorities of alternatives to be determined by comparing their performances against a range of criteria. The ARAS technique was mentioned for the first time by Zavadskas and Turskis in 2010 as an effective and also simple technique of analyzing qualitative as well as quantitative data [18, 51]. The concept of utility degree forms the basis of the ARAS procedure that permits the estimation of the overall index of the alternatives of the optimal alternative [51]. ARAS procedure entails a number of essential steps [14, 18, 31]:

Step 1: Identifying the Decision Problem.

The process begins with a clear definition of the decision problem, including the alternatives to be assessed and the criteria for evaluation.

Step 2: Formulating the Decision Matrix.

A decision matrix is constructed, where each alternative is assessed against the specified criteria. This matrix serves as the basis for subsequent analysis. It consists of m alternatives (rows) and n criteria (columns) as demonstrated Eq. (9).

In the matrix, m represents the number of alternatives, n represents the number of criteria, xij represents the performance value of the i alternative according to the j criterion and x0j represents the optimal value of the j criterion. If the optimal value of the j criterion is unknown, Eq. (10) is used.

Step 3: Normalizing the Decision Matrix.

The values within the decision matrix are normalized to prevent discrepancies due to differing units of measurement. Normalization can be performed through techniques such as linear or vector normalization. Equation (12) is used for criteria that are desired to be maximum in normalization, and Eq. (13) is used for criteria that are desired to be minimum.

Weights are assigned to each criterion based on their significance, and a weighted normalized decision matrix is generated. This step is crucial as it reflects the relative importance of each criterion in the decision-making process. Criteria weights take a value between 0 and 1. (0 < wj < 1). Also, the sum of the criteria weights is equal to 1. In the equation, wj represents the weight (importance level) of criterion j and ij represents the normalized value of criterion j.

The utility degree of each alternative is calculated by summing the products of the normalized values and their corresponding weights. Lastly, alternatives are ranked based on their utility degrees, with the highest-ranked alternative being the most optimal choice.

ARAS technique has been implemented with success in diverse areas such as technology selection, supplier evaluation, and project management, proving its applicability and efficiency in solving complex issues of decision-making [34, 39, 43]. During the current study, while implementing the ARAS technique, subjective judgments obtained from expert experiences were converted to a numerical scale of 1–9 and then underwent utility-centered normalization procedures. This conversion enabled the transformation of subjective feelings towards a numerical matrix while keeping consistency within the calculation procedure. All the criteria were treated as utility-centered because of the aim of detecting the strategy that best overcomes existing barriers. Because of that, fuzzification was not adopted while the impact of subjective variabilities was eliminated by normalization procedures.

DEMATEL was selected because it enables discovering of cause–effect relationships of complex and interactive criteria that are not entirely represented by traditional weight-setting MCDM such as AHP or TOPSIS [30, 62]. By distinguishing dispatcher (influencing) and receiver (affected) factors, DEMATEL provides a more robust foundation for prioritizing barriers in health policy contexts [27]. In parallel, the ARAS method was adopted for ranking solution alternatives due to its simplicity and proven ability to handle both qualitative and quantitative data in health and technology decision-making [18, 39, 64]. While other hybrid MCDM models such as COPRAS–ARAS exist [15], these studies do not directly integrate causal mapping and strategy ranking in a unified framework.

The novelty of our approach lies in first using DEMATEL to reveal the causal structure of barriers and then applying ARAS to rank strategies based on these causal weights, thus ensuring methodological continuity and policy relevance in healthcare economics [9]. This decision is based on the fundamental logic of MCDM methods: achieving the highest possible outcome with limited resources by prioritizing actions targeting root causes. Designing around causal factors allows decision makers to create broader improvements and efficiency-based interventions. This approach has been adopted in several integrated DEMATEL-ARAS frameworks (e.g., [27]), where focusing on causal factors ensures that policy makers' solutions achieve the highest impact across interconnected challenges. The underlying principle in this study aligns with this approach. For this reason, the evaluation of the solution proposals to be used in the ARAS method was made only on the basis of dispatcher criteria (ICFC, DPCC, DBHQ, REC).

The DEMATEL and ARAS calculations in this study were performed using Microsoft 360 Excel. Normalization, creation of total correlation matrices, and weight calculations were performed using Excel's matrix operations and data analysis tools. Iteration and convergence criteria were established based on the standard procedures of the respective methods.

After optimality is calculated, suggestions are ranked from highest to lowest. Suggested solutions to combine challenges for achieving cost-effectiveness ranked as SS3, SS5, SS2, SS4 and SS1. The final ranking highlights the relative positions of the proposed solutions. Among the five recommendations, SS3 (carrying out comprehensive awareness-raising activities for patients and their relatives) emerged as the most effective option. This suggests that raising patient and caregiver awareness is perceived by experts as the most effective and cost-effective way to simultaneously reduce multiple barriers, particularly those related to data use, transparency, and ethical concerns. Following this, SS5 (providing inclusive incentives for AI transformation) and SS2 (establishing a blockchain based cyber security system) emphasized the importance of both financial support mechanisms and data safeguards. Meanwhile, SS4 (developing regulations that fully comply with ethical rules and prevent dilemmas) and SS1 (redesigning the data production and use system) were ranked next, indicating that their relative effectiveness in overcoming their challenges was less pronounced compared to SS3.

Sensitivity analysis

Table 12 presents the aggregated results of the DEMATEL analysis before and after the sensitivity analysis under different weighting scenarios. In Scenario 1, where experts were weighted equally, REC achieved the highest prominence and ranked first, followed by ICFC and DBHQ. Conversely, NTAS ranked last. When sensitivity analysis was performed by assigning a dominant weight of 60% to a single expert and distributing the remaining weights equally among the others, the overall ranking order showed only minor changes. For example, in Scenario 2 (Exp. 5 dominant), REC, ICFC, and DBHQ continued to occupy the top three positions, while NTAS remained the lowest-ranked obstacle. Scenario 3 (Exp. 8 dominant) and Scenario 4 (Exp. 4 dominant) yielded similar results. Scenario 5 (Exp. 9 dominant) confirmed this pattern, with REC remaining first and ICFC second. In general, across all tested scenarios, the top-ranked barrier (REC) and subsequent positions (ICFC and DBHQ) exhibited stability, while only minor changes were observed in the middle and bottom ranks. All these variations provide evidence of robustness to significant changes. In this regard, the weights determined for a dispatcher barrier and tested for sensitivity were integrated into the ARAS method.

Following DEMATEL-based sensitivity analysis, the weights of the same dispatchers (ICFC, DPCC, DBHQ, REC) were integrated into the ARAS method to assess the stability of the ranking of the alternatives under different expert weighting scenarios. Table 12 summarizes the performance scores (Ki) and corresponding rankings in the five sensitivity scenarios. In Scenario 1 (equally weighted distribution), alternative SS3 achieved the highest relative performance, followed by SS5 and SS2, while SS1 ranked fifth. When the dominant weight of 60% was assigned to different experts (Scenarios 1–5), the ranking structure remained completely unchanged. Although small numerical fluctuations in the performance scores (Ki) were observed across scenarios, these changes were minor and did not affect the ranking. This result clearly demonstrates that the ARAS results are robust and insensitive to changes in expert weighting, reinforcing the overall reliability of the model.

Discussion

In this study, important findings were obtained as a result of the analyses conducted to understand the role of AI in achieving cost-effectiveness in healthcare services and to eliminate the difficulties encountered. The most critical challenges in the healthcare system were determined with DEMATEL analysis and strategic suggestions to overcome these challenges were prioritized with the ARAS method. In the DEMATEL study procedure, the interaction network was studied rather than the criterion weightings, and the most influential criteria were identified. This method helped understand the cause-effect relationships of the most fundamental factors that complicate the cost-effectiveness of AI in the healthcare industry. The study results identify that there is a need for a multidimensional and systematic approach for the effective fulfillment of the transformation of AI towards cost-effectiveness in healthcare services.

The analysis indicated that “Carrying out comprehensive awareness-raising activities for patients and their relatives (SS3)” is the foremost strategic priority. Policymakers ought to allocate resources towards this particular strategy. Enhancing awareness among patients and their relatives is essential for the effective implementation of AI and digital health systems. These initiatives can promote the appropriate utilization of such systems, thereby minimizing errors and preventing additional expenses. Indeed, aligning with this viewpoint, [26] underscored the significance of AI training in their research, asserting that it can alleviate ethical dilemmas often encountered in AI operations, such as accountability and transparency in healthcare, through education and awareness directed at patients and their families. Hoseini et al. [20] highlighted that when patients and their relatives receive training concerning the capabilities and limitations of AI, they are more inclined to accept and utilize these technologies efficiently. Likewise, [50] asserted the necessity of providing adequate training to patients in order to foster trust in AI applications. Initiatives aimed at raising awareness are also shaped by the broader environment involving stakeholders and regulatory frameworks. For instance, in OECD nations characterized by more robust digital health governance, awareness campaigns can be integrated into national e-health strategies and bolstered by formal frameworks concerning patient rights. Conversely, in emerging markets, patient education tends to depend more significantly on community health workers, non-governmental organizations, and international agencies, highlighting variations in institutional capacity and policy priorities [3, 33]. Moreover, professional bodies along with patients' advocacy groups also play an important role in the framing both the conception as well as the execution of awareness programs, thereby ensuring that such programs do more than enhance digital literacy, they also foster trust in the moral use of AI technologies [46].

The subsequent highest ranked strategy was identified as "Providing inclusive incentives for AI transformation (SS5)." This observation is consistent with the current body of literature that underscores the necessity of financial and institutional support frameworks. Numerous studies indicate that substantial initial expenditures and ongoing maintenance demands associated with AI technologies persist as a major hindrance, particularly for smaller healthcare facilities and developing health systems [3, 59]. In this context, targeted incentives—such as governmental subsidies, public–private collaborations, or pay-for-performance reimbursement frameworks—represent a vital mechanism to alleviate the financial burdens of AI investments and guarantee equitable access across various settings [38, 58]. Furthermore, policy analyses suggest that incentive frameworks not only promote adoption but also foster innovation ecosystems, ensuring that healthcare providers remain competitive and responsive to patients in the digital era [46, 60]. Accordingly, prioritized incentives are congruent with both theoretical constructs in health economics and empirical observations derived from policy-driven AI integration efforts worldwide. Nonetheless, the formulation and effectiveness of inclusive incentives are considerably influenced by stakeholder dynamics and the regulatory landscape. For instance, in OECD nations possessing advanced reimbursement frameworks, policy incentives may emphasize pay-for-performance funding or innovation grants, whereas countries at earlier developmental stages may depend on international donor initiatives and public–private partnerships as catalysts for AI adoption [38, 58]. Additionally, stakeholder considerations such as insurers' reimbursement protocols, regulatory compliance mandates, and advocacy by patient groups can significantly influence which incentive mechanisms are deemed most feasible and effective [46].

Third strategic initiative, 'Establishing a blockchain-based cybersecurity system (SS2)', describes a rising recognition that secure and transparent data management is the key to achieving cost-effectiveness in AI-based healthcare. Application of blockchain technology provides an immutable and decentralized ledger structure that strengthens the integrity of the exchanges of health data by ensuring transparency, protection, and traceability [2, 60]. It has been reflected in previous research that data privacy and cybersecurity concerns are among the most important factors hindering AI adoption, especially where the trust of patients as well as compliance with regulations are paramount [37]. By adopting blockchain, healthcare platforms can prevent the risks related to data breach, improve interoperability across platforms, and reduce the cost of unnecessary protection measures [7, 28]. Moreover, blockchain's capability to facilitate the secure distribution of inclusive datasets is most relevant to the creation of bias-free AI constructs, while also tackling issues related to the quality of data as well as patients' anxieties [47]. In this scenario, the development of blockchain-based cybersecurity systems has the concomitant goal of securing confidential information as well as achieving the cost-effectiveness of AI integration. Similarly, the functionality of blockchain-based cybersecurity systems also depends on compliance with the regulations as well as group participation by the parties. In those jurisdictions that have stronger high-strength data protection legislations like the GDPR that underlies the European Union framework of laws, blockchain would strengthen compliance as well as transparency of data flows, while jurisdictions that have weaker institutions normally offer high institutional as well as infrastructural hurdles to adoption of similar systems [2, 60]. In addition, whether blockchain-based solutions will emerge beyond pilot programs to systemic influence depends on the stakeholder dynamics—a term that refers to the readiness among the healthcare professionals, IT vendors, as well as the regulators, to come together towards common standards [28].

Conclusion

It will only be attainable to highlight the cost-effectiveness of AI in healthcare and to overcome the obstacles to its integration by creating comprehensive awareness campaigns that involve patients and their families, putting in place incentive systems to promote change, and firmly enforcing blockchain-based cybersecurity standards.

Initially, national healthcare strategies must include awareness-raising activities. Systematic training sessions with patients and their families promote trust, lessen resistance, and maximize the efficient application of AI technologies. Because resistance and a lack of proper digital literacy are typically more severe in health systems, these programs are also crucial to their development. Furthermore, to offset the high costs associated with the deployment of AI, equitable incentive systems must be constructed. Developing countries may require international funding platforms, development bank assistance, or continental partnerships to overcome financing obstacles, even though OECD member countries could focus on individual subsidies, grants for innovation, or pay-for-performance reimbursement models. Specific reward systems that take into account local circumstances. Localized incentive structures tailored to specific circumstances, such as location, system, culture, and values, will help increase the penetration of AI technologies across different healthcare systems. Furthermore, there is a need to promote blockchain-based cybersecurity platforms to protect confidential information, increase transparency, and build public trust. For high-capacity OECD systems, blockchain technologies can be integrated with existing advanced digital platforms, but implementing and supporting full-scale pilot projects with feasible costs in developing countries can serve as a steppingstone to transforming existing systems.

The applicability of the results should be assessed and adapted based on healthcare system diversity (financing system, organization, technological infrastructure, and culture, etc.). While the recommendations may guide decision-makers globally, their practicality in terms of rapid adoption and overall effectiveness will vary significantly, as noted above, from developed healthcare infrastructures to relatively resource-poor ones. Therefore, future studies should analyze and compare regional and global scales, taking into account the factors that contribute to diversity. Policymakers should provide comparative information to adapt AI integration policies in accordance with socioeconomic and regulatory factors. This will further expand the potential for implementing AI-powered, cost-effective, ethical, and sustainable healthcare services tailored to local conditions.

Limitations

Although the participant group included both academics and practitioners, the academic perspective may have been relatively more dominant due to the practitioners' academic backgrounds. To balance this, international consultants and health policy experts were also included to bring together real-world and global perspectives. However, because the expert composition was heavily weighted in Türkiye, the analysis may not have been fully generalizable. Future studies should include broader international and sectoral representation to enhance global generalizability.
