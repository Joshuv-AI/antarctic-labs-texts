Clinical Characteristics and Outcomes of Patients with Intracerebral Hemorrhage: Nonsurgical Versus Surgical Treatment
Authors: Warawut Kittiwattanagul, Puthachad Namwaing, Sittichai Khamsai, Kittisak Sawanyawisuth
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Primary intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a stroke subtype with high morbidity and mortality. Surgical treatments for ICH may be one of the beneficial modalities. There are inconsistent clinical outcomes of surgical treatments in several randomized controlled trials. This study aimed to evaluate if surgical treatment can reduce mortality in patients with ICH in a real-world setting.Methods:This was a retrospective analytical study. The inclusion criteria were consecutive adult patients aged 18 years or over admitted to neurosurgery ward due to ICH, and indicated for surgical treatment according to the 2015 guideline for the management of spontaneous ICH. The outcomes of this study included mortality, length of stay, Barthel index, Glasgow Outcome Score (GOS), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Descriptive statistics were used to execute the differences between those who underwent and did not undergo surgical treatments. Factors associated with mortality were computed by multivariate logistic regression analysis.Results:There were 110 patients with ICH who met the study criteria. Of those, 34 (30.91%) patients underwent surgical treatment: mainly craniotomy (16 patients; 47.06%). The surgical treatment group had significantly higher proportions of large ICH of 30 mL or over (62.96% vs. 27.54%; P = 0.002) and intraventricular hemorrhage (70.59% vs. 46.05%; P = 0.023) than the nonsurgical treatment group. However, both groups had comparable outcomes in terms of mortality, length of stay, Barthel index, GOS, and GCS. The mortality rate in the surgery group was 47.06%, whereas the nonsurgery group had a mortality rate of 39.47 (P = 0.532). There were three independent factors associated with mortality, including age, GCS, and intraventricular hemorrhage. The adjusted odds ratio (95% confidence interval) of these factors was 1.06 (1.02–1.12), 5.42 (1.48–19.81), and 5.30 (1.65–17.01). Intraventricular hemorrhage was more common in the elderly than in the nonelderly group (66.00% vs. 43.33%; P = 0.022).Conclusions:Surgical treatment may not be beneficial in patients with severe ICH, particularly with intraventricular hemorrhage, large ICH volume, or low GCS. Elderly patients with ICH may also have high mortality if intraventricular hemorrhage is present.

INTRODUCTION

Primary intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a stroke subtype with high morbidity and mortality. It accounted for approximately 20% of all strokes, with an incidence rate of 20/100,000 population per year.[12] The main risk factors for ICH include hypertension, age, alcohol consumption, and diabetes.[3456789] Even though there are several treatment modalities, the mortality rate is still high as 50% after 1 month.[2]

Surgical treatments for ICH may be one of the beneficial modalities. The 2015 guidelines for the management of spontaneous ICH recommended surgical treatment in cerebellar hemorrhage or supratentorial ICH who had worsening symptoms, brainstem compression, or hydrocephalus.[10] However, there are inconsistent clinical outcomes of surgical treatments in several trials, particularly in supratentorial ICH. Two international, multicenter, randomized trials found no significant outcomes on mortality with odds ratio of 0.95 (95% confidence interval of 0.73, 1.23; P = 0.707) and 0.71 (95% confidence interval of 0.48, 1.06; P = 0.095).[1112] While a meta-analysis found that patients with delayed deterioration or superficial lobar ICH without intraventricular hemorrhage may have better survival benefits from surgery with an odds ratio of 0.74 (95% confidence interval of 0.64, 0.86; P < 0.0001).[13] These studies were conducted in a randomized controlled trial. This study aimed to evaluate if surgical treatment can reduce mortality in patients with ICH in a real-world setting.

METHODS

This was a retrospective analytical study conducted at Khon Kaen Regional Hospital, Khon Kaen, Thailand. The inclusion criteria were consecutive adult patients aged 18 years or over, admitted to the neurosurgery ward due to ICH, and indicated for surgical treatment according to the 2015 guideline for the management of spontaneous ICH.[10] Those who were pregnant or had contraindications for surgical treatment were excluded. The study period was between 2012 and 2021. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board. This study was a part of the stroke fast track project.

We reviewed a medical chart of all eligible patients to collect data on baseline characteristics, laboratory results, surgical treatment, and outcomes. The baseline characteristics included age, sex, previous medical treatment, history of smoking, history of alcohol drinking, comorbid diseases, Barthel index, Glasgow Outcome Score (GOS), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and presenting blood pressure. GOS was categorized into five categories: dead, vegetative state, severe disability, moderate disability, and no disability, while GCS was divided into three categories: GCS of 3–4, 5–12, and 13–15. A hypertensive emergency was defined as the presence of diastolic blood pressure of more than 110 mmHg.

Laboratory tests were complete blood count, coagulogram, glucose, renal function, electrolytes, and computed tomography of the brain. The modified Fisher Scale was used to evaluate the subarachnoid hemorrhage. Details of surgical treatment were also recorded as well as reasons for denial of surgical treatment. The primary outcome of this study was mortality, whereas the secondary outcomes included length of stay, Barthel index at discharge, GOS at discharge, and GCS at discharge.

Statistical analyses

The eligible patients were divided into two groups: With and without surgical treatment. Descriptive statistics were used to compute the mean (standard deviation) for numerical factors and the number (percentage) for categorical factors. An independent t-test was used to compare between the mean of both groups, whereas Fisher’s exact test was used to execute the differences between the two groups. Factors associated with mortality were computed by univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis. Hosmer–Lemeshow Chi-square was used to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of the predictive model.

A subgroup analysis was performed by age group and GCS as these two factors may influence other clinical features or outcomes. As a definition of an elderly person is 65 years, we used this cut-off point to compare clinical features and outcomes between those with age of 65 years or over versus those with age of <65 years. Similarly, GCS of 13–15 is defined as mild cases. Therefore, a comparison between those with GCS of 13–15 versus those with GCS of 12 or lower. All analyses were performed using STATA software (College Station, Texas, USA).

RESULTS

There were 110 patients with ICH who met the study criteria. Of those, 34 patients (30.91%) underwent the surgical treatment categorized as craniotomy (16 patients; 47.06%), ventriculostomy (13 patients, 38.24%), craniectomy (3 patients, 8.82%), and burr hole (2 patients, 5.88%). Surgical procedures were performed with a median of 142.50 min (1st to 3rd quartile range of 59.00–197.50 min). For the nonsurgical treatment group, the main reason for refusing surgical treatment was a decision by relatives (26 patients; 34.21%). The nonsurgical treatment group received blood pressure control, medications to reduce brain swelling, rehabilitation, and prevention of complications such as infection or pressure sore.

Regarding baseline characteristics [Table 1], there were two significantly different factors between those who underwent and did not undergo surgical treatment: age and GCS. Those who underwent surgical treatment had significantly younger ages than those who did not receive surgical treatment (59.41 vs. 65.35 years; P = 0.031), while those who did not receive surgical treatment had more proportion of patients with GCS of 13–15 than those who underwent surgical treatment (44.44% vs. 14.71%; P = 0.002).

The surgical treatment group also had significantly higher proportions of large ICH of 30 mL or over (62.96% vs. 27.54%; P = 0.002) and intraventricular hemorrhage (70.59% vs. 46.05%; P = 0.023) than the nonsurgical treatment group [Table 2]. However, both groups had comparable outcomes in terms of mortality, length of stay, Barthel index, GOS, and GCS [Table 3]. The mortality rate in the surgery group was 47.06%, whereas the nonsurgery group had a mortality rate of 39.47 (P = 0.532). There were three independent factors associated with mortality, including age, GCS, and intraventricular hemorrhage [Table 4]. The adjusted odds ratio (95% confidence interval) of these factors was 1.06 (1.02–1.12), 5.42 (1.48–19.81), and 5.30 (1.65–17.01). Note that surgery was not a significant factor associated with mortality (adjusted odds ratio of 0.60 and 95% confidence interval of 0.17–2.16). The Hosmer–Lemeshow Chi-square of the predictive model was 4.41 (P = 0.818), indicating goodness of fit.

A subgroup analysis by age group of 65 years was performed to evaluate factors associated with mortality [Table 5]. There were three different factors between these two age groups: Mortality rate, surgical intervention rate, and proportion of intraventricular hemorrhage. The age group of 65 years or over had a higher mortality rate (60.00% vs. 26.67%) but a lower surgical intervention rate (16.00% vs. 43.33%) than those younger than 65 years. DISCUSSION

As recommended by the guideline, surgical intervention was performed in more severe cases of ICH than in the nonsurgical group [Tables 1 and 2]. However, the surgical intervention did not declare significant outcomes than the nonsurgical group by both univariate and multivariate analysis. These results may imply that severe cases of ICH may not have benefits from surgical treatment. In addition, those who were not severe may not need surgical intervention. Note that the outcomes of this study were assessed at the discharge. In this study, surgical procedures were performed with a median of 142.50 min. Even though the timing of surgical intervention in ICH is still debating, the time frame from randomized trials was between 4 and 96 h.[10]

There were four factors that may be an indicator of whether surgical treatments were needed or not: Age, GCS, ICH volume, and intraventricular hemorrhage. Regarding mortality rate, the average mortality rate in this study was comparable with a previous study (41.81% vs. 50%).[2] A previous review found that patients with ICH who had GCS <9 or ICH volume of over 60 mL had mortality at 90%.[2] This study had a similar concept but the cut point for GCS and ICH volume were <12 and 30 mL, respectively. Neurosurgeons may need to consider these factors carefully before perform surgical treatments.

Intraventricular hemorrhage was another predictor for poor outcome as this may indicate a large hemorrhagic volume. It causes periventricular tissue injuries and obstructive hydrocephalus as a complication leading to more severe or poor functional status or death.[1415] Elderly patients with ICH in this study did not undergo surgical treatment as their relatives decided not to perform the invasive treatment. These patients served as control for the surgical group in the real-world setting. The subgroup analysis found that the elderly group underwent less surgical intervention, but the mortality was higher than the nonelderly group (60.00% vs. 26.67%; P = 0.001). The factor that drives a higher mortality rate in the elderly group was intraventricular hemorrhage [Table 5]. Both age and intraventricular hemorrhage were independently associated with mortality according to the multivariate analysis [Table 4]. These two factors may result in a low GCS, another predictor of mortality significantly. In addition, elderly patients with severe conditions such as low GCS may be a reason for choosing nonsurgical treatment by family members.

In this study, we found that 34.21% of patients in the nonsurgical treatment group were due to a decision from relatives. In some patients, the condition may be stable if they passed the acute phase of the first 3 days resulting in a nonsignificant mortality rate between these two groups. The surgical intervention group may have more severe cases leading to a slightly higher mortality rate than the nonsurgical intervention group [Table 1].

There are some limitations in this study. First, this pragmatic study did not run by a randomized method, but surgical intervention may decide by physicians and the patient’s relatives. However, these situations indicate real-world setting, not the other types of studies like systematic reviews.[16171819] Second, some factors were not studied such as statin treatment, obstructive sleep apnea, and its treatment.[20212223] Finally, the results of this study may not be specific to any surgical treatment: Mostly craniotomy and ventriculostomy. In addition, we did not evaluate the role of surgical timing and outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

The surgical treatment may not be beneficial in patients with severe ICH, particularly with intraventricular hemorrhage, large ICH volume, or low GCS. Elderly patients with ICH may also have high mortality if intraventricular hemorrhage is present.
