Exposure to an environmentally relevant mixture of polychlorinated biphenyls affects mitochondrial bioenergetics and plasticity
Authors: Maria Carolina Peixoto-Rodrigues, Vladimir Pedro Peralva Borges-Martins, José Raphael Monteiro-Neto, Bruno Vicente, Lucas José Guimarães, Clara Fernandes-Carvalho, Antonio Galina, Victor Midlej, Michal Toborek, Rachel Ann Hauser-Davis, Daniel Adesse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adaptations of cells to environmental insults typically require tightly regulated processes to preserve the organismal steady state, particularly in metabolically active cells such as neural cells. Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) are persistent organic pollutants widely recognized for their neurotoxic potential. Due to their lipophilic nature, these compounds readily accumulate in the brain, where they can disrupt neuronal homeostasis. Herein, we examined the effects of exposure to an environmentally relevant PCB mixture on mitochondrial dynamics, ultrastructure, and function in the mouse neuroblastoma Neuro2a cell line. Ultrastructural examinations indicated evident signs of mitochondrial damage, including swelling, cristae disruption, and increased frequency of autophagic structures. Quantification of mitochondrial networks confirmed a shift from tubular to fragmented morphologies, accompanied with the modulation of the gene expression of genes involved in mitochondrial fusion and fission. Specifically, mitofusin 2 protein levels were increased at 24 and 48 h of treatment, and OPA1 at 48 h, whereas Drp1, phosphorylated at Ser616 was increased at 24 h. Markers of mitophagy PINK1 and Parkin were elevated at 72 and 48 h of exposure, respectively, whereas Atg5 and Atg7, markers of autophagy were increased at 24 h. We observed a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential and increase in mtDNA levels in PCB-treated cultures at 24 h. Oxidative stress was also implicated by overexpression and increased enzymatic activity of superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1). Functional tests revealed a transient impairment of mitochondrial respiration and ATP synthesis, which was later restored, pointing to the recruitment of compensatory mechanisms. Together, these results indicate that PCB exposure activates an integrated stress response with oxidative imbalance, mitochondrial bioenergetics, remodeling, and autophagy features, revealing the neural cell vulnerability and plasticity to environmental insult.

Introduction

Mitochondria are powerful organelles that ensure energy equilibrium and metabolic homeostasis [1]. As metabolic integrators, these organelles are fast to react to environmental and metabolic changes in response to bioenergetic cell needs, a process termed mitochondrial plasticity. Among the most important mechanisms for ensuring such flexibility is a series of incessant fusion and fission activities, referred to as mitochondrial dynamics, that regulate mitochondrial shape and function [2]. Both processes are necessary to ensure mitochondrial integrity and function [3]. Fusion allows mitochondria to merge, with mixing of mitochondrial contents, which tend to oppose damage by complementing defective components [4]. Conversely, fission enables mitochondria to divide aiming at segregation of damaged parts through mitophagy and the supply of an adequate number of mitochondria during cell division [5]. Interference with the equilibrium between these processes can lead to fragmented or hyperfused mitochondria, resulting in cellular dysfunction [6]. For instance, mitochondrial dynamics defects have been linked to many neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, in which fission and fusion alterations result in synaptic dysfunction and neuronal death [[7], [8], [9]]. Interruptions of these processes may also disturb the mitochondrial network, leading to defective ATP synthesis and increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [10]. These bioenergetic deficits can also result in different metabolic diseases, such a type 2 diabetes and obesity, in which mitochondrial dysfunction affects both insulin sensitivity and the energy metabolism [11]. Due to this inherent plasticity and reactivity, mitochondria are key mediators of the relationships between environmental stimulation and cellular fate choice [12,13]. In this context, anthropogenic pollution effects on mitochondrial function are an emerging concern for human health and ecological stability [14]. Establishing how mitotoxicants impact organismal fitness and viability, especially in the context of environmental stress, is increasingly paramount [15].

Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), a group comprising more than 200 molecules, were first produced commercially in the 1930s, with their widespread use and industrial popularization mainly from the 1950s to the 1970s. These molecules, known for their insulating and non-flammable properties [16], were commonly used by the industry in electrical equipment like transformers, capacitors, and voltage regulators, as they can withstand high temperatures without degrading [17]. Their chemical stability also made them valuable in industrial applications, i.e., hydraulic fluids, lubricants, and as additives in paints, sealants, and plastics. However, evidence regarding the toxic potential and environmental persistence of PCBs began to arise, especially after the 1970s, leading to bans or severe restrictions in many countries. In the United States, for example, production was banned in 1979 by the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) [18].

PCBs can easily bioaccumulate in tissues, largely in fatty tissues [19], as they are highly lipophilic, which also leads to slow excretion rates [20]. Moreover, they are very stable molecules and, thus, resistant to chemical breakdown, leading to high environmental persistence of both parental and metabolized compounds [21]. Exposure to even trace amounts of PCBs can cause massive buildups, especially in long-lived species [22], which may also result in biomagnification processes in exposed food chains. In this sense, top-of-the-food-chain species, like humans, can accumulate these molecules at multiple times their environmental concentrations [23].

Human exposure to PCBs can result in both minor and severe health issues, which are driven, at least in part, by oxidative stress and proinflammatory impact [24,25]. These compounds have been associated with a wide range of chronic diseases. Such as endocrine disease [26], type 2 diabetes [27], cardiovascular disease [28], obesity [29], liver disease [30], and neurodegenerative impairment [31]. Furthermore, PCBs are also toxic to the immune [32], reproductive [33], nervous [25], and endocrine [34] systems and have been proven carcinogenic [35].

In this context, this study aims to elucidate mechanisms through which exposure to PCBs can promote mitochondrial homeostasis imbalances and subsequent cellular metabolism alterations.

Methods

Neuro2a cells

A mouse neuroblastoma cell line (N2a/Neuro2a cells; BCRJ under the code: 0189) was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) High-Glucose (ThermoFisher, #11965092) containing 1 % MEM non-essential amino acids (ThermoFisher, #11140050), 1 % GlutaMAX (ThermoFisher, #35050061), 1 % Penicillin-Streptomycin (5000 U/mL) (ThermoFisher, #15070063) and 10 % of fetal bovine serum, at 37 °C at a 5 % CO2 concentration.

Polychlorinated biphenyl exposure

The PCB congeners 2,2′,3,4,4′,5′-hexachlorobiphenyl (PCB138), 2,2′,4,4′,5,5′-hexachlorobiphenyl (PCB153), and 2,2′,3,4,4′,5,5′-heptachlorobiphenyl (PCB180) were obtained from AccuStandard (New Haven, CT, USA; >99 % purity) and prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich), comprising a representative mixture of environmentally relevant exposure scenarios, as described by Teglas et al. [36]. This mix corresponds to a molar ratio of 1.7:3.2:1 of PCB138, PCB153, and PCB180. The compounds were dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution at a total PCB concentration of 50 mM. PCB working solutions were prepared by serial dilution of the 50 mM stock in culture medium, with the final DMSO concentration kept constant across all treatment groups. Control cells were exposed to the same final concentration of DMSO in culture medium (1:10,000). The mixture was freshly diluted from the stock solution immediately prior to use.

Cytotoxicity assay

Cell viability was determined using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Briefly, 5000 cells were plated onto 96-well plates with clear bottoms. After 24 h of plating, cultures were incubated with different PCB mix concentrations (1, 5, 10 and 20 μM) added to the culture medium for 24, 48, and 72 h at 37 °C. The medium was not replaced after the exposure was initiated. At the desired times, the cultures were incubated with a 50 μg/mL MTT solution for 4 h at 37 °C. Absorption at 570 nm was measured after solubilization of the formazan crystals with DMSO. MTT-reducing activity was expressed as a percentage of the control.

mtDNA assay

DNA was extracted using PureLink™ Genomic DNA Mini kit (ThermoFisher, #K182002) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RT-qPCR was used to determine the ratio of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and genomic DNA (gnDNA), as described by Zacharioudakis et al. [37]. Briefly, each reaction consisted of 5 ng of DNA, 5 μL Power SYBR Green master mix (ThermoFisher, #4368708), 200 nM primers [mtDNA (Nd2): CCTATCACCCTTGCCATCAT (forward) and GAGGCTGTTGCTTGTGTGAC (reverse); gnDNA (Pecam1): ATGGAAAGCCTGCCATCATG (forward) and TCCTTGTTGTTCAGCATCAC (reverse)], and RNase-free water up to 10 μL. RT-qPCR was performed on the QuantStudio 5 PCR System (ThermoFisher) at 95 °C for 10 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 1 min, followed by a melting curve analysis. As a positive control for mitochondrial DNA damage, cells were treated with 50 μM H2O2, a well-established oxidative stress inducer. The 2−ΔΔct method was applied to determine the mtDNA/nDNA ratio, as ΔCT = CT (mtDNA gene)−CT (nDNA gene). Primers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies.

JC-1 staining

At the desired exposure times, cells were incubated with JC-1 reagent (ThermoFisher) 2 μM for 30 min at 37 °C, washed in PBS and fixed in 4 % PFA. Cells were visualized using an Axio Observer. Z1 microscope equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 40x/1.4 Oil DIC (UV) VIS-IR M27 objective. The ZEN software version 3.9 (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Germany) was used to analyze the mean intensities of both red and green fluorescence, corresponding to different mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨM) states. In healthy mitochondria (with high ΔΨm), JC-1 migrates into the mitochondrial matrix and forms aggregates, emitting a “red” fluorescence signal (∼590 nm). In low membrane potential mitochondria (i.e., stressed or apoptotic mitochondria) JC-1 presents as a monomer in the cytosol and fluoresces “green” (∼530 nm). Thus, the red/green fluorescence ratio comprises a mitochondrial integrity indicator, with high ratios representative of intact mitochondria and low ratios, mitochondrial depolarization [38].

Briefly, the red-to-green fluorescence ratio was calculated for each image by dividing the mean intensity of the red channel by that of the green channel, reflecting the overall mitochondrial health of the investigated cells [38]. Each dot in the graphs represents the average from six distinct regions of the same coverslip independently analyzed to obtain a representative mitochondrial membrane potential value in each experimental condition.

Mitochondria morphological analyses

The mitochondria of both control and PCB-treated cultures were immunostained at 24, 48 and 72 h post-treatment for translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOMM20), as described above. Imaging was performed using a ZEISS Axio Observer. Z1 Spinning Disk Confocal Microscope equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 100 × /1.40 Oil DIC M27 objective. To maintain consistency across conditions, all acquisition settings, i.e., laser power, exposure time, gain, and pinhole size, were kept constant. At least five cells were imaged per condition across three independent experiments.

Raw z-stack images were analyzed using the Mitochondria Analyzer plugin in Fiji [39]. A 3D thresholding approach was applied using the 3D Threshold Optimizer. Background and subtraction was performed with a rolling ball radius of 1.25 μm to remove uneven illumination and enhance mitochondrial structures. A local contrast enhancement with a scale factor of 2.6 was applied to improve differentiation between mitochondria and the background, and gamma correction was adjusted to 0.9 to optimize intensity distribution. Furthermore, thresholding was performed using the weighted mean method with a C value of 12. Post-processing steps included despeckling to reduce noise, outlie removals to eliminate artifacts, and 3D hole filling with an outlier radius of 0.7 pixels to maintain mitochondrial structural integrity.

After segmentation, binary conversion was applied to facilitate further analyses of mitochondrial morphology and connectivity, and skeletonization was performed to extract mitochondrial network structures, also employing the Mitochondria Analyzer plugin. The following parameters were determined: mitochondrial counts per cell volume, sphericity, total branch length, mean branch diameter, and number of branch junctions per mitochondrion.

Mitochondrial respiration

Respirometry experiments were conducted using the Oroboros Oxygraph-2k system, following previously described protocols [40]. Control and treated Neuro2a cells were cultured in DMEM High Glucose and seeded at a density of 2 million cells per chamber (1 million cells/mL) in an Oroboros O2k respirometer. For calibration and throughout the Oroboros respirometry experiments, the same medium used for cell culture was employed. A total of 0.5 μg/mL of oligomycin were added to the chamber to inhibit ATP synthase, followed by carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP) as the uncoupling agent at a final concentration of 1 μM and, finally, 4 μM of antimycin A, a mitochondrial complex III inhibitor. Mitochondrial physiology parameters were assessed under specific experimental conditions. Non-mitochondrial respiration was determined after the addition of Antimycin A to establish a baseline for respiratory activity non-linked to mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos). Basal respiration was computed by subtracting non-mitochondrial respiration from the basal respiration recorded at the beginning of the experiment, prior to any of the compound additions. An O2 flux coupled to OxPhos representing oxygen consumption linked to ADP phosphorylation was calculated by subtracting oligomycin-induced effects on respiration from basal respiration. Maximal respiration was determined in the presence of FCCP, with non-OxPhos respiration subtracted to isolate mitochondrial contributions to this maximal activity. Spare capacity, indicative of respiratory reserves, was derived by subtracting basal respiration from the maximal respiration. H+ leak amounts were determined by evaluating the effects induced by oligomycin on respiration, with the non-mitochondrial component subtracted for precise evaluations. Lastly, coupling efficiency was determined as 1- (H+ Leak/ATP production), providing insights into the mitochondrial coupling efficiency of under experimental conditions. The data were analyzed using the Oroboros DatLab software.

SOD activity assay

Cells were collected in PBS supplemented with proteinase inhibitors (cOmplete, mini, EDTA-free protease mixture inhibitor tablets; Sigma-Aldrich Merck) and proteins were quantified by the BCA™ Assay Kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). Superoxide activity was measured using the nitrobluetetrazolium (NBT) method, as described previously [41]. Briefly, 60 μg of protein were fractionated by native-PAGE and then incubated for 20 min with 5 mL of a 2 mg/mL NBT solution. The NBT solution was then discarded, and 5 mL of a solution containing 20 μL of TEMED + riboflavin were added followed by incubation for 15 min in the dark. The solution was placed under the light until the appearance of white bands and quantification was performed using the ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy

To investigate the ultrastructural impact of PCB exposure on organelles and cellular structures, biological samples were processed following previously established protocols [42]. Briefly, the cultures were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde (Sigma, USA) diluted in Cacodylate buffer 0.1 M for 1 h at room temperature. The cells subsequently underwent post-fixation with 1 % osmium tetroxide (Sigma, USA) and 8 % potassium ferrocyanide and dehydration in an increasing acetone series (Merck, Germany) until reaching 100 %. They were then gradually embedded in epoxy resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, USA) and polymerized at 60 °C. The polymerized samples were then sectioned into 60 nm slices employing an ultramicrotome and collected on 300-mesh copper grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate to improve contrasting. Samples were examined using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi/HT7800, Japan) at the Electron Microscopy Facility at the Oswaldo Cruz Institute, Rio de Janeiro.

ATG5 and SOD1 fluorescence intensities

To quantify ATG5 and SOD1 protein contents, fluorescence intensity analyses were performed using the Corrected Total Cell Fluorescence (CTCF) method as previously described [43]. This accounts for background signal and cell area, providing a more accurate estimation of total fluorescence per cell. Images were acquired on an Axio Observer Z1 microscope (Zeiss) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 100 × /1.40 Oil DIC M27 objective, a 1.0 × tubelens, and an extended optovar, using a fixed exposure time of 300 ms across all conditions. Z-stacks were collected to capture the full volume of each cell, and a Sum Z-projection was applied using Fiji (ImageJ) to preserve the cumulative fluorescence signal throughout the cellular depth, critical for spatially distributed proteins such as ATG5 and SOD1. Individual cells were manually segmented, and three parameters were recorded: Integrated Density (sum of pixel intensities within the ROI), Area, and Mean Background Fluorescence, measured from a nearby unstained region in the same field. The CTCF was calculated using the formula:

CTCF = Integrated Density – (Area of selected cell X Mean fluorescence of background readings).

Statistical analyses

Experiments were performed at least three times. The cytotoxicity results were analyzed by a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and the remaining, that consisted in more than one variable (time and PCB exposure), were analyzed by Two-way ANOVA with a Bonferroni post-hoc test, using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.6.0). p-values ≤0.05 were considered as significant. Results were expressed as means ± Standard Error of the Means (SEM). Morphometric analyses (mitochondrial networks and fluorescence intensity) were performed blindly.

Results

PCBs alter Neuro2a cell mitochondrial networks

Cellular stress is known to trigger morphological mitochondria changes, often altering their size, shape, and network dynamics as part of adaptive or damage responses [44]. To test whether the PCB Mix dose employed previously in brain microvascular endothelial cells [36] would have a selective effect on the mouse neuroblastoma cell line Neuro2a, different concentrations (1, 5, 10 and 20 μM) were tested over time. The MTT assay revealed significant effects only of time (F (2, 25) = 10,52, p < 0.001), but not of treatment (F (4, 25) = 1.728 p = 0.1754), or their interaction (F (8, 25) = 1.088, p = 0.4034). Although a reduction in viability was observed at 10 μM PCB Mix in post-hoc analysis (p = 0.0259), this effect was not confirmed by the overall two-way ANOVA and may be related to variability at 24 h. To minimize potential confounding, all subsequent experiments were conducted with 5 μM, a concentration without detectable effects on viability. Mitochondrial shapes and network parameters were further analyzed. Mitochondrial sphericity significantly increased at 48 h and 72 h in PCB-treated cells, indicating a shift towards more rounded, possibly fragmented mitochondria (Fig. 2C). The statistical analysis indicated significant main effects of time (F (2,154) = 4.00; p = 0.0182), exposure (F (1,154) = 12.68; p < 0.0001), and their interaction (F (2,154) = 4.47; p = 0.0133), suggesting a synergistic influence of PCB exposure and time on mitochondrial shape.

Total branch length per mitochondrion was significantly reduced 48 h post-treatment (Fig. 2D), with a significant interaction effect (F (2,154) = 4.43; p = 0.0152). However, neither time (F (2,154) = 1.65; p = 0.1927) nor exposure (F (1,154) = 2.33; p = 0.1326) alone reached statistical significance, suggesting a specific temporal window of network disruption. Mean branch diameter was significantly decreased at 24 h and 72 h (Fig. 2E). While the interaction between factors did not reach significance (F (2,154) = 2.90; p = 0.0580), both time (F (2,154) = 5.22; p = 0.0064) and exposure (F (1,154) = 8.69; p = 0.0037) led to significant main effects, indicating overall mitochondrial branch thinning following the PCB exposure. Finally, the ratio of branch junctions per mitochondrion was significantly reduced at 48 h and 72 h (Fig. 2F), with significant interaction (F (2,154) = 6.94; p = 0.0013), time (F (2,154) = 4.37; p = 0.0143), and exposure (F (1,154) = 15.26; p = 0.0001) effects. This reduction in junction complexity is consistent with impaired mitochondrial fusion and diminished network organization.

This has functional implications for organelle function and cell fate, with disruptions potentially leading to aberrant mitochondrial morphology and decreased energy production [47], both of which are consistent with our observations.

Discussion

The chronic effects of PCB exposure on CNS cells is a global concern and requires continuous studies and public health efforts. Herein we used Neuro2a cell cultures as a system to mimic neural progenitor cells and exposed them to different doses of an environmentally relevant PCB mix. Consistent with our previous report [36], a 5 μM dose did not severely reduce cell viability at any given time, implying that it is not overtly cytotoxic. Importantly, this concentration is similar to what was recently described with human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs) as being representative of an environmentally relevant mixture and concentration of PCBs found in human cohorts [36]. Moreover, PCBs were shown to disrupt the blood-brain barrier, which would allow circulating PCB levels to reach the brain parenchyma, which justifies the use of the described PCB plasma levels in Neuro2a cells. Neurons and neural progenitor cells greatly rely on their bioenergetics and mitochondrial physiology to exert their functions [59] [[60], [61], [62]]). Although being an immortalized tumoral cell line, Neuro2a is widely utilized in cellular and molecular biology as well as toxicological studies [63], with proven reproducibility and, to some extent, translatability to primary brain neurons responses.

Mitochondrial morphology is highly plastic and plays an essential role in cellular stress responses. The regulation between fission and fusion of mitochondria enables these organelles to regulate shape and function to achieve cellular homeostasis under stress conditions [3]. Mitochondria can undergo increased fission by stressors like oxidative stress or metabolic disturbances, resulting in fragmentation used to compartmentalize damaged areas and allow their mitophagy degradation [64].

An ultrastructural TEM analysis of PCB-exposed cells revealed a sequential cascade of mitochondrial and cellular injury consistent with mitochondrial dysfunction, as well as the activation of autophagic machinery. Swollen mitochondria and disrupted cristae ultrastructure reflect the onset of mitochondrial stress, a hallmark of initial mitotoxicity, in as early as 24 h following PCB exposure [65]. These mitochondrial alterations likely reflect loss of membrane potential and potential PINK1 stabilization on the outer mitochondrial membrane, initiating Parkin recruitment and mitophagy signaling [66].

The presence of vacuole-like structures, some empty and others containing amorphous content, are consistent with the hypothesis of heightened autophagic activity. These autophagosome- and autophagolysosome-like structures became more pronounced at 48 and 72 h post-exposure, indicating ongoing or heightened cellular stress. The same has been observed in neurons treated with other environmental toxins, where autophagy is either a cell-protective mechanism or a cell-death marker [67]. Collectively, our ultrastructural findings are consistent with a scenario in which PCBs induce mitochondrial injury, trigger mitophagic pathways, and disrupt cellular homeostasis in a time-dependent manner. This corroborates the reported neurotoxicity profile of these xenobiotics in causing oxidative stress [68], calcium deregulation, and mitochondrial damage leading to neuronal dysfunction and degeneration [69].

Mitochondrial fusion prevents damage accumulation and enables functional complementarity by the sharing of components like mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and proteins, allowing for the exchange of healthy and damaged components between mitochondria, ensuring mitochondrial function maintenance, especially under stress [70]. Strengthened fusion under moderate stress conditions can improve mitochondrial resilience by maintaining bioenergetic competence and reducing ROS production [71]. The regulation of these two antagonistic processes is strictly controlled by certain proteins, i.e., Drp1 and Fis1 for fission and Mfn1, Mfn2, and Opa1 for fusion which, under most instances, are differentially phosphorylated or expressed based on the nature and magnitude of stress [72].

The enhanced expression of mitochondrial fission and fusion markers observed herein is an indication that PCBs disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis in mouse neural cells. In this sense, genes associated with fission, such as Fis1 and Drp1, and fusion markers, including Mfn2 and Opa1, were upregulated at the mRNA and/or protein levels. Such a combined upregulation can be postulated as a cell attempt to mitigate mitochondrial stress, possibly as an adaptive response for mitochondrial integrity and function maintenance under toxic conditions [73].

Upregulation of Fis1 and Drp1 expressions indicate increased mitochondrial fission, which has classically been associated with cellular stress and mitochondria fragmentation [74]. While mitochondrial fission is crucial for quality control, overactivation leads to mitochondrial pathology, which is associated with neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's [75]. On the other hand, increased expressions of the fusion markers Opa1 and Mfn2 can be assumed to be a countermeasure to mitochondrial contact and function preservation, especially under stress. The co-upregulation of both fission and fusion events highlights a state of mitochondrial imbalance, in which dynamic remodeling is most likely heightened in response to PCB-induced cellular stress. This disruption can, in turn, hinder mitochondrial efficiency and create the energy deficits and synaptic dysfunction described in larger transcriptomic analysis [76]. One limitation of our study was the analysis of fusion/fission markers on total cell lysates, which could mask the sub-cellular localization of these proteins. In future studies, the use of mitochondrial fractions for western blotting assays would accurately determine their association with the inner or outer mitochondrial membranes.

The PINK1/Parkin cascade plays a key role in maintaining mitochondria under normal quality control and is a key regulator of cellular processes initiated as a mitochondrial stress response [77]. Upon mitochondrial depolarization or injury, PINK1 is retained on the outer mitochondrial membrane, autophosphorylated and phosphorylating ubiquitin on serine 65 [78]. Phospho-ubiquitin acts as a recruitment and activation signal for cytosolic E3 ubiquitin ligase parkin. Activated parkin enhances the mitophagic signal by outer mitochondrial membrane protein ubiquitination for recognition by autophagic machinery [79]. Activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway is, thus, an adaptive response, allowing cells to clear malfunctioning mitochondria and restore energy homeostasis. Therefore, this pathway's activation following exposure to environmental toxicants that target mitochondria, such as pesticides, organic pollutants and metals, may comprise a cellular survival strategy. Its repeated or persistent hyperactivation, however, may indicate chronic mitochondrial damage and cellular stress. Markedly, whereas fusion and fission markers, as well as autophagy markers (ATG5 and ATG7) were upregulated at earlier time points following PCB exposure, both Pink1 and Parkin levels were upregulated at later time points, suggesting a sustained removal of damaged mitochondria.

In agreement with these findings, previous studies have indicated that oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage display the potential to trigger ATG5 and ATG7 upregulation as an adaptive survival mechanism [80].

Enhanced SOD1 expression and activity in PCB-treated cells indicates an interesting connection that associates the diverse cellular stress responses observed herein. This enzyme, which reduces superoxide radicals to hydrogen peroxide, is generally induced following increased ROS production, characteristic of oxidative stress [81]. The enhanced SOD1 expression and activity suggests that oxidative stress is provoked following PCB exposure, potentially due to mitochondrial impairment. Moreover, autophagy and mitochondrial fragmentation have both been linked to oxidative stress [82,83]. Thus, SOD1 induction may be viewed as one component of a general cytoprotective mechanism in response to mitochondrial stress caused by PCB exposure, comprising links to mitochondrial remodeling and autophagy activation aiming at cellular homeostasis restoration. However, chronic stress and long-term fragmentation may not be corrected by these compensatory responses, potentially leading to irreversible damage.

The respirometry assay indicated a reversible mitochondrial impairment following PCB treatment. Decreases in key bioenergetic parameters, basal respiration, and ATP production were noted after 24 h of exposure, illustrating that PCBs can impair mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, in accordance with our morphological and molecular findings collectively pointing to mitochondrial distress. However, at later points, respiratory parameters reverted towards control levels, suggesting that damaged cells presented a recovery response. Mitochondrial function restoration is likely to be mediated by the coordinated activation of compensatory mechanisms including antioxidant defenses (SOD1 upregulation), autophagy, and mitochondrial quality control by fission and mitophagy. The time course of the observed bioenergetic disruption and recovery emphasizes the dynamic cellular response to toxic insult. Nevertheless, acute bioenergetic disruption can result in significant neuronal function implications, notably under chronic or repetitive exposure conditions, where potential for recovery may be compromised or, ultimately, fail.

Mitochondrial dynamics changes have been shown to be a hallmark in the pathophysiology of many central nervous system disorders, especially neurodegenerative diseases. Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial dynamics are required to maintain mitochondrial function, energy production, calcium homeostasis, and cellular resistance [84]. Imbalances towards enhanced fission or impaired fusion, however, often mediated by the dysregulation of proteins like Drp1, OPA1, and Mfn2, give rise to mitochondrial fragmentation and neuronal dysfunction [85,86]. For example, Drp1 hyperactivation in Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease models has been associated to mitochondrial fission accumulation, ROS overload, and synaptic impairment [87,88]. Huntington's disease models have also revealed fragmented mitochondrial networks through 3D electron microscopy, suggesting that structural mitochondria decay is an early neurodegeneration indicator [89]. Moreover, in multiple sclerosis, mitochondria damage with DNA deletions and reduced respiratory chain activity plays a role in inducing axonal degeneration and persistent neuroinflammation [90,91].

Conclusion

The findings reported herein reveal a coordinated cellular response to PCB stress involving mitochondrial injury, mitochondrial dynamics imbalances, oxidative stress, and autophagy induction, resulting in abnormal mitochondrial function. This suggests mitochondrial homeostasis interruption that is most likely to shift the balance towards fragmentation and mitophagy in response to damage. This dynamic adaptation signals cellular attempts to compensate and re-establish homeostasis following injury, but also implies in adverse effects through repeated or long-term exposure, such as in chronic environmental exposure cases, beyond the boundaries of compensation, potentially contributing to long-term neurodegenerative outcomes.
