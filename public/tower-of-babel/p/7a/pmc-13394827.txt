Expanding the electroclinical spectrum of TANC2 ‐related disorders: Lennox–Gastaut syndrome and related developmental epileptic phenotypes

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNeurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) and epilepsy are often associated. Increasing evidence highlights a pivotal role for pathogenic variants in genes encoding synaptic scaffolding proteins. Within this group, TANC2 has recently been implicated in intellectual developmental disorder with autistic features and language delay, with or without seizures; however, its full clinical spectrum and contribution to specific epileptic encephalopathies remain incompletely defined.MethodsWe describe the electroclinical and developmental features of three patients carrying truncating TANC2 variants identified through trio‐exome sequencing within the European collaborative platform NETRE. Changes in this gene have been linked to neurodevelopmental disorders, autism, intellectual disability, and epilepsy. INTRODUCTION

Neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) are a heterogeneous group of conditions, including intellectual disability (ID), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and epilepsy. Despite the contribution of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors, many affected individuals still lack a definitive diagnosis. 1 The implementation of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) has been pivotal in elucidating the genetic basis of NDDs and their frequent association with epilepsy. 2 Among the implicated genes, those encoding synaptic proteins are particularly prominent, highlighting the central role of synaptic function in neurodevelopment and neurotransmission. Synaptic processes regulate the formation, maturation, and plasticity of neural circuits underlying cognition, behavior, and motor control, and depend on scaffolding proteins that organize receptors, ion channels, and signaling complexes. 1 Notably, large‐scale genomic studies have demonstrated an enrichment of disruptive variants in multiple postsynaptic gene sets across psychiatric disorders, including ASD and schizophrenia. 3 The term “synaptopathies” refers to a broad spectrum of neurological disorders caused by alterations in synaptic transmission and/or plasticity due to dysfunction of the molecular pathways regulating synaptic function. 4 At the synaptic level, pathogenic variants disrupt key processes regulating neurotransmission and synaptic organization. These alterations, together with defects in upstream transcriptional regulation, are consistently associated with neurodevelopmental phenotypes, including ASD, ID, and developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE). 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Within this spectrum, DEEs represent the most severe forms, characterized by early‐onset, often drug‐resistant epilepsy, severe neurodevelopmental impairment, and an increasingly expanding genetic landscape involving more than 900 genes, including both established disease drivers and ultra‐rare monogenic disorders. 2 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

The tetratricopeptide repeat, ankyrin repeat and coiled‐coil containing protein 2 (TANC2) gene (HGNC:30212), located at chr17q23.2–q23.3, encodes a postsynaptic scaffolding protein that interacts with multiple partners, including KIF1A, PSD‐95, and NMDA receptor subunits. 13 , 14 Its expression peaks during early fetal development, highlighting a key role in early neurodevelopment. 14

Heterozygous variants in TANC2 disrupt synaptic scaffolding and underlie a complex neurodevelopmental disorder (OMIM #618906). In line with its central role in postsynaptic organization, TANC2 emerges as a novel candidate in severe epileptic network disorders. However, its phenotypic spectrum and overall function remain incompletely defined.

Here, we report three patients with previously unreported pathogenic TANC2 variants, aiming to further delineate the electroclinical and neurodevelopmental spectrum of TANC2‐related disorders. These cases span confirmed Lennox–Gastaut Syndrome (LGS), LGS‐like DEE features, and milder NDD‐associated epilepsy, supporting LGS as part of the broader TANC2‐related DEE spectrum. Together with the limited available literature, the longitudinal observation of the patient fulfilling LGS criteria raises the possibility that selected TANC2‐related LGS cases may follow a comparatively less severe course than other etiologies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study cohort and recruitment

Patients were identified through the European collaborative platform NETRE (Network for Rare Therapies in Epilepsy). Both pediatric and adult individuals were included if they harbored a TANC2 variant classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria. Clinical information was obtained through a systematic review of medical records and direct communication with the referring clinicians. Data were extracted according to predefined domains to allow comparability across cases. For descriptive analyses, denominators were adjusted according to data availability, and patients with missing or not reported information for a given variable were excluded from the corresponding calculation. Written informed consent for genetic testing, data collection, and inclusion in the study was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians, in accordance with local ethical regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Trio‐based whole‐exome sequencing

Trio‐based whole‐exome sequencing (WES) was performed on patients and available parents, as previously described. 15 Further methodological and clinical details are provided in the Supplementary material.

CASE DESCRIPTIONS

Case 1

A 23‐year‐old female, born at term to non‐consanguineous parents. Early motor and speech milestones were acquired within normal limits throughout infancy. Seizure onset occurred at 4 years and 6 months with afebrile bilateral tonic–clonic seizures, followed by rapid evolution into multiple seizure types (tonic, atonic, myoclonic, and atypical absences) and drug resistance, accompanied by concomitant psychomotor regression. EEG demonstrated generalized slow spike‐and‐wave discharges (GSSW) and generalized paroxysmal fast activity in sleep (GFPA), consistent with LGS. Multiple antiseizure medications, including valproate, phenobarbital, ethosuximide, clobazam, carbamazepine, levetiracetam, topiramate, and ACTH, were attempted with limited benefit. Introduction of felbamate resulted in a sustained reduction in seizure frequency and a marked improvement in EEG abnormalities (Figure S1), with persistence mainly of sporadic atypical absences, ultimately allowing transition to felbamate monotherapy. This improvement was paralleled by partial recovery of developmental skills. At follow‐up, the patient showed mild intellectual disability (WAIS full‐scale IQ: 67) with partial autonomy in daily living, although residual executive and adaptive difficulties persisted.

Case 2

A 20‐year‐old male, born at term, showed normal early development until 18 months, when stagnation of expressive language and subsequent social and behavioral difficulties led to a diagnosis of ASD. He maintained good autonomy with support. Seizure onset occurred at 8 years of age with focal impaired‐awareness seizures, later evolving to include atypical absences and tonic seizures, preceded by autonomic symptoms. Initial treatment with levetiracetam achieved prolonged seizure remission, although relapse occurred in late adolescence. Following recurrence, a switch to lamotrigine led to renewed seizure control, with no further events reported. EEG evolution showed persistent focal and diffuse epileptiform abnormalities over time, including bilateral posterior epileptiform discharges with sleep activation, focal paroxysmal abnormalities during wakefulness and sleep, right temporal slowing, atypical rapid rhythms, and a photoparoxysmal response. The most recent EEG continued to demonstrate bilateral posterior epileptiform abnormalities with a tendency to diffuse. Neuropsychological evaluation revealed borderline intellectual functioning (WAIS full‐scale IQ: 74).

Case 3

A 13‐year‐old female, born at term after an unremarkable pregnancy. The toddler showed normal early motor development, achieving object grasping at 6 months and independent walking at 14 months. Seizure onset occurred at 2 years of age with febrile seizures, followed by early drug resistance and the emergence of multiple seizure types, including generalized tonic–clonic, tonic, atonic, and myoclonic seizures, accompanied by developmental regression. Surprisingly, after several failed treatment attempts with valproate, sulthiame, and methylprednisolone, seizure freedom was achieved by 4 years of age with combination therapy consisting of lamotrigine and ethosuximide. EEG showed persistent bifrontal epileptiform discharges, predominantly during sleep. Despite seizure freedom, no clear neurodevelopmental improvement was observed, and the patient continued to exhibit severe intellectual and language impairment. Neurocognitive assessment revealed severely below‐average performance across all domains, including gross and fine motor skills, cognition, language, and socio‐emotional functioning.

GENETIC TESTING

All patients underwent exome sequencing, which identified de novo variants in the TANC2 gene. In Case #1, a frameshift variant was detected (NM_001394998.1:c.461dupC; p.[P155Sfs*5]), introducing a premature stop codon. In Case #2, previous genetic investigations, including CGH array, FMR1 (FRAXA), PTEN sequencing, and quantitative analysis of the 22q13 region, yielded negative results. The subsequent trio‐WES clarified the genetic etiology, identifying a frameshift variant NM_001394998.1:c.4606_4607del; p.(A1537Lfs*14) in the TANC2 gene. In Case #3, analysis revealed a nonsense variant NM_001394998.1:c.2856C>A; p.(Y952*), predicted to undergo nonsense‐mediated decay. All variants were classified as likely pathogenic (Cases #1 and #2) and pathogenic (Case #3) according to ACMG criteria and were absent from population databases and predicted to result in loss of function.

DISCUSSION

Literature overview

Experimental studies have shown that TANC2 disruption impairs dendritic spine dynamics and excitatory synapse formation. Consistent with this, animal models demonstrate that TANC2 loss leads to severe developmental consequences, including embryonic lethality in mice and drosophila melanogaster, and synaptic imbalance with autism‐like behaviors in zebrafish. 13 , 14 , 16 Large‐scale human exome and genome studies have further supported its role in neurodevelopmental disorders. 17 TANC2 pathogenic variants are associated with a neurodevelopmental syndrome characterized by ASD, ID, language and motor delay, variable epilepsy, and dysmorphic features, with additional psychiatric and behavioral manifestations reported in some individuals. In early cohorts, epilepsy or recurrent seizures were observed in more than half of affected patients. 18 Subsequent studies suggested a genotype–phenotype correlation, with de novo null variants associated with combined neurodevelopmental disorders and epilepsy, and missense variants showing more variable phenotypes, being associated either with neurodevelopmental impairment or with epilepsy alone. 14

Across published patients and the present cohort, 22 individuals had available electroclinical data (See Table 1). The median age at last follow‐up was 13.50 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 9.00–22.00 years; N = 24), while the median age of epilepsy onset was 3.00 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 1.92–5.50 years; N = 19). Overall, males accounted for 62.5% of the cohort and females for 37.5%. Focal seizures were reported in 33% of patients (n = 6/18), whereas bilateral seizure types occurred in 89% (n = 17/19); three patients exhibited both focal and bilateral seizures. ID or significant cognitive impairment was present in 19/22 patients (86%), being absent only in two individuals carrying missense variants, and not clearly assessable in the previously reported LGS patient because of the very young age and limited longitudinal follow‐up, although developmental delay was already evident after seizure onset. From a neurodevelopmental perspective, speech and motor delay were reported in 88% and 62% of patients, respectively (n = 15/18 and n = 13/21), while developmental regression occurred in 5/13 patients (38%). About half of the cohort presented autistic features (57%; n = 12/21). Structural brain MRI abnormalities were uncommon, being reported in only one patient. Among cases with available outcome data, seizure response appeared relatively favorable, with seizure improvement or seizure freedom observed in 13/17 patients (76%). Although detailed ASM data were not consistently available for many patients, the most frequently reported responses were observed with VPA, KD, LTG, CNZ, and OXC. Interestingly, several reported patients presented LGS‐like electroclinical features, including atypical absences, tonic and atonic seizures, with EEG evidence of GPFA and GSSW. 14 , 18 , 19 , 20 The main clinical features are outlined in Figure 1.

Case series and comparative observations

Among the prototypical DEEs, LGS exemplifies the particularly severe disease course: children typically present with early‐onset, refractory seizures and developmental regression. Intellectual disability, behavioral, and autistic features are frequent, and mortality rates are markedly higher than in other epilepsies. 21 , 22 Despite ongoing debate regarding its precise definition, etiology, and diagnostic framework, 23 LGS is increasingly recognized as a secondary network epilepsy. 24 , 25 In this context, synaptic dysfunction provides a mechanistic framework to understand the involvement of synaptic genes, including TANC2, in LGS pathogenesis.

In our cohort, Case #1 presented electroclinical findings consistent with a classic LGS phenotype, fulfilling core ILAE criteria, 23 including multiple seizure types (tonic, atonic, myoclonic, and atypical absence seizures), developmental regression, GSSW activity, and GPFA during sleep (Figure 2).

Case #1 EEG recordings obtained at 5 (A–C) and 8 years old (D). During wakefulness, atypical absences were observed, beginning with 3 Hz spike–wave activity, followed by slowing to 1.5–2 Hz and subsequent periodic generalized polyspike‐wave discharges (red arrows—A). During sleep onset, polyspikes were followed by brief generalized flattening (1–2 s) with subtle diffuse fast rhythmic activity (Blue arrows—B and C). Sleep onset immediately followed by diffuse fast rhythmic activity consistent with a tonic seizure pattern, without associated motor manifestations (green arrow—C).

Case 3 showed several overlapping electroclinical features strongly suggestive of an evolving LGS phenotype, including tonic, atonic, and myoclonic seizures, developmental impairment, and sleep‐activated bilateral spike–wave abnormalities. Even though the available data were insufficient to establish a definite LGS diagnosis. In contrast, Case 2 appeared more consistent with a broader NDD/DEE phenotype, showing only partial electroclinical overlap with the LGS spectrum (e.g., atypical absences, tonic seizure, and developmental involvement).

Tian et al. reported a pediatric patient carrying a heterozygous nonsense TANC2 variant who developed a severe epilepsy phenotype consistent with LGS, with multiple seizure types and early developmental regression. Seizures were drug‐resistant but eventually controlled with add‐on ketogenic diet therapy. 20 Similarly, Case #1 achieved seizure freedom. However, while the previously reported patient required sustained polytherapy, including a ketogenic diet, with only short‐term follow‐up available, Case #1 was successfully maintained on felbamate monotherapy. Notably, long‐term follow‐up indicated a milder clinical trajectory than typically observed in LGS: after an initial phase of drug resistance and classical electroclinical features, neurocognitive outcome remained relatively preserved, with partial recovery and maintained functional autonomy into adulthood.

Felbamate was introduced to reduce seizure frequency and improve EEG background by limiting diffuse spike‐and‐wave discharges, with the aim of achieving monotherapy and reducing the encephalopathic burden to support cognitive function. The persistent intellectual disability in this patient likely reflects the underlying TANC2 defect.

The sustained response to felbamate in Case #1 suggests that NMDA‐modulating therapies may represent a biologically rational precision‐medicine strategy in TANC2‐related epileptic encephalopathies. This observation supports a potential genotype‐guided therapeutic approach targeting synaptic glutamatergic dysfunction.

In contrast, Case #2 showed a later seizure onset, including tonic seizures, prolonged remission, and a relatively mild neurocognitive profile, with lamotrigine achieving seizure freedom after levetiracetam failure. Case #3 showed early‐onset, drug‐resistant polymorphic seizures with persistent bifrontal epileptiform discharges and severe developmental impairment, showing partial overlap with the LGS spectrum. Notably, after several failed therapeutic attempts with valproate, sulthiame, and intermittent methylprednisolone pulses, seizure control was eventually achieved with the combination of lamotrigine and ethosuximide. Interestingly, as for Case #1 and felbamate, lamotrigine's efficacy may be partially related to its indirect modulation of glutamatergic neurotransmission (see details in Table 2).

Interestingly, while this manuscript was under peer review, an additional child carrying a truncating TANC2 variant and presenting with severe drug‐resistant generalized epilepsy was reported. 19 Notably, this patient initially showed global motor and language delay, but subsequently improved over time, particularly in language development. Although still anecdotal, this observation is in line with the possibility that selected TANC2‐related phenotypes may show a more favorable developmental trajectory than expected.

Given the role of TANC2 in organizing postsynaptic NMDA receptor complexes and dendritic spine maturation, its disruption may lead to excitatory–inhibitory imbalance and large‐scale network instability, providing a plausible mechanistic substrate for the generalized slow spike‐and‐wave activity and network features typical of DEE, such as LGS. During brain development, TANC2 appears to exert a precise temporal regulation of mTOR activity during neuronal maturation, showing distinct inhibition patterns in early‐stage versus late‐stage neurons. 26 Subsequently, other regulatory mechanisms may take over, reducing critical dependence on TANC2. We hypothesized that this could explain the somewhat improved phenotype observed in selected patients. Furthermore, synaptic plasticity mediated by glutamatergic receptors (particularly long‐term depression)—intensified during adolescence—could facilitate the elimination of dysfunctional synapses and strengthening of compensatory circuits. 27

LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations. The cohort was identified through a tertiary referral network, introducing a potential selection bias toward more severe or genetically investigated phenotypes. Furthermore, the absence of functional validation limits causal inference regarding the pathogenic mechanisms linking TANC2 disruption to epileptic network dysfunction. In addition, the partial retrospective design and heterogeneity of follow‐up assessments may have introduced variability in phenotypic characterization.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Larger cohorts and functional studies are needed to validate these findings. Given the rarity of the condition, multicenter collaborations will be essential to expand patient collections and establish robust natural‐history data, which remain limited and hinder both therapeutic development and future research.

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, this case series expands the electroclinical and neurodevelopmental spectrum of TANC2‐related disorders by reporting three previously undescribed truncating variants associated with heterogeneous epilepsy phenotypes. Notably, within this broader phenotypic spectrum, available data suggest a relatively favorable seizure trajectory in several cases, including both the previously reported patient and the present patient fulfilling LGS criteria.

In our Case #1, sustained seizure improvement was accompanied by partial functional recovery over long‐term follow‐up, raising potential therapeutic implications. These findings support the inclusion of LGS within the severe end of the disease spectrum, while suggesting that TANC2‐related LGS may follow a less severe course than other etiologies.
