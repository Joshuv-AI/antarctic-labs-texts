One-year clinical, radiological, and functional outcomes following antifungal therapy for aspergillus infection in lung transplant recipients
Authors: Ariane Lelièvre, Marlène Murris, Marion Dupuis, Jean Mourlanette, Aurélie Le Borgne, Samia Collot, Olivier Marion, Nassim Kamar, Nicolas Guibert, Thomas Villeneuve
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAspergillus is the most frequent fungal pathogen after lung transplantation, ranging from asymptomatic colonization to invasive disease. Treatment outcomes of aspergillosis in real-life settings remain insufficiently characterized because of the complexity and heterogeneity of its clinical presentations.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of all adult lung transplant recipients followed at Toulouse University Hospital from 2014 to 2024. Patients were stratified as Aspergillus-free, colonized, or treated for pulmonary aspergillosis (PA). Clinical, radiological, microbiological, functional, and survival outcomes were assessed in PA group.ResultsAmong 174 patients, 46% were Aspergillus-free, 30% colonized, and 24% treated for pulmonary aspergillosis. The overall isolation rate was 54%, predominantly A. fumigatus (60%), with Aspergillus bronchitis as the most frequent form (57%). At diagnosis, 86% of treated patients were symptomatic, mainly with dyspnea (60%) and cough (48%). First-line therapy, most commonly voriconazole or isavuconazole combined with inhaled liposomal amphotericin B, required second-line adjustment in 62% of cases. At 12 months, 60% showed clinical and 67% radiological improvement, with significant gains in FVC and FEV₁ at 6 months (p = 0.0067), maintained at 12 months. Overall survival did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.33), and five-year survival reached 80%, exceeding historical data.ConclusionIn our cohort, patients treated for Aspergillus infection after lung transplantation had survival outcomes comparable to those with colonization or without Aspergillus. Despite heterogeneity in treatment approaches, proactive antifungal therapy was associated with clinical, functional, and radiological improvements, supporting early and individualized treatment strategies.Keywords: Aspergillus, Aspergillosis, Lung Transplantation

Introduction

Lung transplantation (LTx) is a life-saving intervention for patients with end-stage respiratory failure, offering significant gains in both survival and quality of life.1 However, its long-term success depends on sustained immunosuppressive therapy, which substantially increases susceptibility to infections. Among these, fungal infections are particularly concerning due to the allograft’s continuous exposure to airborne pathogens, with Aspergillus species being the most frequently implicated fungal pathogens in this population.2 Aspergillus infections encompass a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from asymptomatic airway colonization to invasive pulmonary aspergillosis.3 Despite advancements in diagnostics and antifungal therapeutics, these infections remain a major clinical challenge.4 Although previous studies have indicated that Aspergillus infection may contribute to chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) and negatively impact survival,5 substantial gaps remain in understanding the clinical course of these infections in the era of updated preventive and therapeutic strategies—particularly regarding radiological, functional, and survival outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate treatment response over a one-year period using clinical, radiological, and functional assessments, to determine the effectiveness of antifungal therapy and its impact on long-term outcomes after lung transplantation.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study included all adult patients (≥18 years) who underwent LTx and were followed at the Department of Respiratory Medicine, Toulouse University Hospital, from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2024. Patients surviving less than the three postoperative days (n = 3) were excluded.

Compliance with ethical standards

This retrospective study was conducted at Toulouse University Hospital (RnIPH 2025–107) in accordance with the MR-004 reference methodology and registered with the CNIL (2206723 v 0). In accordance with French law, ethics committee approval was not required. All patients were informed about the use of their data in compliance with GDPR (EU Regulation 2016/679).

Transplant care

Induction immunosuppression included basiliximab (20 mg on Day 0 and Day 4) and glucocorticoids (tapering to 5 mg/day prednisolone by six months), continuing this dose thereafter. Maintenance therapy consisted of tacrolimus (or ciclosporin), mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), and prednisolone. Routine antifungal prophylaxis began with caspofungin until postoperative mycological results were available, then continued with inhaled liposomal amphotericin B (25 mg twice weekly) for three months. Antifungal treatments were documented, including start and stop dates (1st and 2nd line) and reasons for discontinuation; third-line or higher therapies were recorded without further detail. A standardized bronchoscopy protocol included procedures on postoperative Days 3 and 7, and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, followed by annual surveillance. Bronchoscopies involved tracheobronchial aspirate and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) using 50–150 mL saline, with samples analyzed microbiologically. Concurrent serum galactomannan (GM) and Aspergillus serology were systematically collected at each therapeutic reassessment and then annually, while Aspergillus PCR in BAL or bronchial aspirates was performed at the clinician's discretion rather than systematically. Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) including forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), and maximal mid-expiratory flow (MMEF) were performed at baseline, every month during the first year, then every three months during the first two years. All chest CT scans were independently reviewed by a thoracic radiologist (S.C.) and two pulmonologists (A.L., T.V.) and described according to the Glossary of Terms for Thoracic Imaging.6.

Aspergillus-related pulmonary diseases

Aspergillus-related diseases were classified according to established criteria.7 Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis was defined according to the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) criteria.8 Mycological evidence included a positive culture from respiratory samples (sputum, BAL fluid, or tracheobronchial aspirate), two positive Aspergillus PCR results from BAL fluid, or a galactomannan index ≥1.0 in serum, plasma, or BAL fluid. Colonization was defined as the detection of Aspergillus species in respiratory specimens (sputum, tracheobronchial aspirate, or BAL fluid) by culture or PCR, in the absence of clinical, biological, bronchoscopic, or radiological evidence of active infection. Fungal tracheobronchitis was defined according to the 2010 International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) consensus criteria.3 Diagnosis required all three of the following components1: clinical symptoms (new or increased sputum production, cough, dyspnea)2; bronchoscopic evidence of endobronchial abnormalities (erythema, ulceration, or pseudomembrane formation), without radiological or biological signs of invasive parenchymal disease; and3 microbiological confirmation, based on sputum and BAL analyses, with at least one positive culture or PCR from BAL fluid and two independent positive sputum analyses.

Treatment failure was defined using a composite criteria of clinical (persistence/worsening of symptoms), radiological (stable/progressive lesions), and microbiological (persistent microbiological or serological positivity) factors despite appropriate therapy.

Statistical analysis

Median and mean values were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. Patients were stratified into three groups based on Aspergillus status: Aspergillus-free, colonized, and treated for PA. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to estimate overall survival from transplantation to death or last follow-up. Survival curves were compared using the log-rank test and presented with 95% confidence intervals. Patients alive at the end of the study were censored at their last recorded follow-up. Within-group comparisons of pulmonary function (FEV₁,FVC, MMEF) at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months post-treatment initiation were performed using paired-sample t-tests. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 8.

Results

Baseline characteristics and survival outcomes

The cohort included 174 lung transplant recipients: Aspergillus-free (46%), colonized (30%), and treated for pulmonary aspergillosis (PA) (24%). Detailed baseline characteristics for the overall cohort and each subgroup are presented in Table 1. The mean follow-up duration was 4.5 ± 2.9 years. The mean age at transplantation was 58 ± 12.7 years, and 138 patients (79%) were male. Double lung transplantation was the predominant procedure (80%). The leading indications for transplantation were emphysema/COPD (38%), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (17%), and cystic fibrosis (15%). Rates of ACR and CLAD were comparable across groups. Five-year survival rates were 79% for treated patients, 90% for colonized patients, and 80% for Aspergillus-free patients, with no statistically significant differences observed among groups (log-rank p = 0.33, Figure 1).

Characteristics of pulmonary aspergillosis

Details on Aspergillus species, forms of disease, diagnostic samples, and immunosuppressive regimens at diagnosis are summarized in Table 2. Cases were evenly distributed across the 10-year period, with comparable numbers observed in 2014–2017, 2018–2020, and 2021–2024 (15, 14, and 13 cases). Among the 94 patients (54%) with a positive culture, 42 (45%) received antifungal treatment; 10 (24%) had pre-transplant colonization. Aspergillus fumigatus was the most frequent species (60%), followed by A. niger (22%). Aspergillus bronchitis was most common (57%), followed by bronchoinvasive (17%), angioinvasive (12%), aspergilloma (7%), and necrotizing tracheobronchial forms (7%). Bronchial stenosis was present in 19 patients (45%), 5 of whom required stenting. Fungal isolation relied on broncho-tracheal aspirates (52%), BAL fluid (24%), and sputum (19%). Aspergillus PCR was positive in 11 cases (26%). At diagnosis, serology was positive in 8 patients (19%), in three cases Aspergillus IgG was the only microbiologic sign. Other infectious complications identified in the Aspergillus-treated group included CMV disease (n=8, including 3 concurrent cases), Candida spp. colonization or infection (n=10, 5 concurrent), and 9 cases of non-Aspergillus filamentous fungi, specifically Fusarium, Mucorales (3 concurrent cases), and Paecilomyces species.

Treatment of pulmonary aspergillosis

Mean time from transplantation to treatment initiation was 15 (±18) months, 62% within the first year post-transplantation, we found no significant differences between patients treated early and those treated late. The median interval from first Aspergillus isolation to treatment initiation was 25 days [5−269], and 11 days [0−29] from the last positive bronchoscopy sample (Table 2). Among treated patients (n=42), 19 patients (45%) were on antifungal prophylaxis at diagnosis, often with multiple agents (45%) (Table 3). The most common were caspofungin (50%), inhaled amphotericin B (40%), and inhaled liposomal amphotericin B (38%). Treatment duration and sequencing were highly individualized (Figure 2). Voriconazole was the predominant first-line agent (38%), followed by isavuconazole (19%), sometimes combined with inhaled liposomal amphotericin B (9%). Parenteral agents such as caspofungin and IV liposomal amphotericin B were mainly used early post-transplant in hospitalized patients, with subsequent transition to oral azoles. Isavuconazole was often prescribed as second-line therapy (14%), frequently combined with inhaled liposomal amphotericin B (31%). Overall, 26 patients (62%) required second-line treatment and 9 (21%) received ≥3 lines due to intolerance or failure (Table 3). Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed in 28 isolates (67%). Susceptibility testing was not systematic and was performed at the discretion of the microbiologist or upon clinical request.

Clinical outcomes

At treatment initiation, 86% of patients were symptomatic at the time of antifungal therapy. The most frequently reported symptoms were dyspnea (60%), cough (48%), and oxygen requirement (19%). Hemoptysis was observed in 4 patients (10%), and 2 patients (5%) presented with acute respiratory failure. Clinical outcomes at one year indicated improvement in approximately 60% of patients, stability in 20%, and deterioration in 10%.

Pulmonary function test

The impact of antifungal therapy on lung function was assessed by pulmonary function tests (PFTs) at baseline (T0), six months (M+6), and twelve months (M+12) after treatment initiation. A significant improvement was observed in both FVC from 3.02 ± 1.09 L at baseline to 3.34 ± 1.15 L at six months (p = 0.0067), reaching 3.41 ± 1.22 L at twelve months; and FEV₁ from 2.24 ± 0.88 L at baseline to 2.48 ± 0.92 L at six months (p = 0.0067). In contrast, MMEF did not show significant changes over the follow-up period (Figure 3). A detailed summary of PFTs is provided in Table 4.

Radiological Findings and Evolution on Chest CT

At diagnosis, 52% of patients presented radiological abnormalities consistent with PA, most frequently with invasive features (38%). The most common CT findings were ground-glass opacities (52%), consolidations (50%), and bronchial wall thickening (40%); a halo sign was observed in 10% of cases (Figure 4). Composite clinical–radiological patterns are summarized in Table 5. At six months (±1), 5 of 42 patients had died; among the 37 survivors, CT scans were available for 87%, showing complete resolution in 9%, partial improvement in 53%, stable findings in 9%, and progression in 28%. At twelve months (±1), imaging was available for 30 patients, demonstrating complete resolution in 30%, partial improvement in 37%, stable disease in 17%, and worsening in 33% (Table 5).

Discussion

Although preventive and therapeutic approaches for Aspergillus infection have evolved, real-world outcomes after antifungal treatment in lung transplant recipients remain insufficiently described. We therefore analyzed clinical, radiological, microbiological, and functional responses over twelve months following treatment initiation. Aspergillus is a ubiquitous environmental fungus responsible for a wide spectrum of pulmonary manifestations, ranging from asymptomatic colonization to invasive aspergillosis3 rending the diagnosis challenging in lung transplant recipients. In solid organ transplantation, Aspergillus infections account for up to 63% of invasive fungal infections post-transplant and are associated with mortality rates ranging from 23% to 82%, depending on clinical presentation.9 In our lung cohort, prevalence was 24%, consistent with the 4–23% reported in the 2019 AST Guidelines.9

Despite this burden, antifungal prophylaxis practices remain heterogeneous, depending on the underlying indication, center-specific protocols, and the recipient’s history.3, 9, 10 The distribution of transplant indications in our population—COPD (38%), cystic fibrosis (15%), and ILD (30%)—closely mirrored ISHLT registry data (36.5%, 15.8%, 29.7%).1This supports the representativeness of our cohort in this context. We therefore analyzed patient survival according to Aspergillus status—classified as Aspergillus-free, Aspergillus isolation without treatment, and treated infection. In our center, antifungal management followed a proactive approach. Routine prophylaxis consisted of caspofungin, followed by inhaled liposomal amphotericin B (25 mg twice weekly) for three months. Fungal sampling was systematically integrated into the bronchoscopy surveillance protocol, and treatment strategies were subsequently individualized, with therapeutic sequencing tailored to each patient’s clinical course. The overall rate of Aspergillus isolation was (54%) with A. fumigatus the predominant species (60%), consistent with multicenter studies such as TRANSNET.2, 11 Our colonization rate (30%) was similar to previously reported rates of 30–46%.4

In our cohort, 24% of patients required curative antifungal therapy, exceeding the 6–16% incidence of PA reported by Silveira and Husain in 2007.4 This difference may reflect advances in microbiological diagnostics and the adoption of a more proactive and earlier therapeutic approach. In 45% of patients, standard prophylaxis alone proved insufficient, underscoring the need for tailored curative treatment. Classical prophylactic regimens included inhaled liposomal amphotericin B, voriconazole, itraconazole, or posaconazole.9 The choice of caspofungin for early post-operative prophylaxis was based on clinical and pharmacological considerations: Caspofungin offers broad activity against Candida species commonly isolated early after transplantation, demonstrates favorable compatibility with ECMO due to limited membrane adsorption, and has a predictable pharmacokinetic and safety profile, without the need for early therapeutic drug monitoring or complex drug–drug interaction management. These findings highlight the importance of identifying risk factors for Aspergillus disease and suggest that, in some cases, a more aggressive preventive strategy may be needed before clinical symptoms develop.

Regarding curative management, antifungal therapy in our study was typically initiated within ten days of Aspergillus detection on bronchoscopy, once fungal confirmation was obtained. First-line regimens most often involved voriconazole or isavuconazole, frequently combined with inhaled liposomal amphotericin B to ensure both systemic and local antifungal coverage. Management was often complex due to diagnostic uncertainty, antifungal toxicity, and drug–drug interactions with immunosuppressants. Sequential or combination regimens were frequently required to address intolerance, toxicity, or pharmacokinetic interactions, underscoring the challenge of applying standardized treatment protocols in real-world practice. At treatment initiation, most patients were symptomatic, predominantly with dyspnea and cough. At one year following treatment, two-thirds of patients experienced clinical improvement. Nonetheless, this response is likely multifactorial, as respiratory symptoms are common in the early post-transplant period and may also reflect concurrent recovery processes rather than antifungal therapy alone. Radiologically, ground-glass opacities (52%) and consolidations (50%) were the most frequent findings, as described in non-neutropenic populations.10 The air crescent sign, typically associated with invasive aspergillosis in neutropenic hosts, was absent—consistent with its recognized rarity among lung transplant recipients.12, 13 Radiological improvement was observed in two-thirds of patients; however, these changes also should not be interpreted in isolation but in conjunction with clinical and microbiological data. From a functional perspective, spirometric parameters improved over time, with significant gains in FVC and FEV₁ at six months remained stable at one year. Once again, these functional improvements likely reflect a combination of antifungal efficacy and broader post-transplant recovery, including improved graft function and physical rehabilitation. We hypothesize that this improvement is multifactorial, reflecting advances in patient management, including early detection through systematic surveillance, the implementation of modern prophylactic protocols, the widespread use of newer and better-tolerated triazoles such as isavuconazole, and the adoption of individualized therapeutic strategies.

The favorable short-term clinical, radiological, and functional responses observed in approximately two-thirds of patients translated into encouraging long-term survival outcomes. Indeed, our overall mortality rate (35%) was markedly lower than earlier reports of lung transplant recipients with Aspergillus infection (∼50–60% in the literature).2, 11 Then the 3 months mortality was only 2%, compared with 19% reported in the PATH Alliance® registry for lung transplant patients with invasive aspergillosis.14 To finish, the comparable five-year survival observed between treated, colonized, and Aspergillus-free patients was consistent with the recent study by Boden et al..15 These findings highlight the importance of recent antifungal strategies—both prophylactic and curative—in lung transplant recipients with suspected Aspergillus infection.

Our study adds to the literature by focusing on treatment response, whereas prior investigations mainly assessed risk factors or associations with CLAD. Van Gemert et al. reported overall mortality of 30%,16 similarly, Solé et al. described a link between invasive aspergillosis and BOS17 but they did not treatment outcomes. Reinforcing this association, Dhanani et al. recently showed an earlier onset of BOS in patients with Aspergillus colonization but likewise did not detail treatment specifics or outcomes.18 In our study, the incidence of BOS did not differ significantly among Aspergillus-free, colonized, and treated groups. Nonetheless, our work provides detailed clinical, functional, and radiological outcome data in response to antifungal therapy, though it was not designed to resolve the complex pathogenesis of CLAD. Strengths of our study include systematic and longitudinal data collection across multiple domains, integrated into routine clinical care, thereby minimizing missing data and enhancing reliability. Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, its retrospective and single-center design may introduce selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings to centers with different epidemiology or antifungal prophylaxis protocols. Second, while the 10-year study period provided a longitudinal view, the sample size for certain subgroups remains relatively small, potentially reducing the statistical power. Furthermore, we observed significant clinical and therapeutic heterogeneity; the inclusion of various Aspergillus presentations and the use of diverse antifungal molecules and treatment durations reflect the complexity of real-world management but may confound the assessment of specific treatment outcomes. Finally, the long duration of the study may have captured shifts in surgical techniques and antifungal treatments, which could act as unmeasured confounding variables.

Conclusion

In our study, antifungal management followed an integrated and individualized approach, combining prophylactic strategies, proactive diagnostic surveillance, and multiple lines of curative therapy. This strategy may have contributed, in the short term, to improved clinical and radiological outcomes in two-thirds of patient, together with significant gains in FVC and FEV₁ at six months, which remained stable at one year. In the long term, Aspergillus infection was associated with survival comparable to that of Aspergillus-free and colonized lung transplant recipients. Given the challenges posed by population heterogeneity, international observational cohorts and standardized registries may offer a viable solution for more robust characterization of treatment response. Challenges are multifaceted, involving prophylactic and preventive, pre-emptive and curative treatment strategies that vary with post-transplant timing and Aspergillus clinical presentation.
