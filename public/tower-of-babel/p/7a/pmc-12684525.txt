Individual and County‐Level Social Determinants of Health and Acute Reperfusion Therapies: Get With The Guidelines‐Stroke Registry Results
Authors: Manav V Vyas, Moira K Kapral, Amy Y X Yu, Raed A Joundi, Peter C Austin, Jiming Fang, Mathew J Reeves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe associations between individual‐ and county‐level social determinants of health and reperfusion therapies (thrombolysis or thrombectomy) for acute ischemic stroke have been described separately, but they are rarely studied together.Methods and ResultsWe identified 1.5 million patients aged ≥40 years with acute ischemic stroke between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2019 from the Get With The Guidelines‐Stroke registry in the United States. We ascertained age, sex, rural residence, and ethnicity or race at the individual level, and poverty, unemployment, and lower education (defined as less than high school) at the county level. We used multivariable log‐binomial regression models estimated using generalized estimating equations methods to account for county‐level clustering and adjusted for comorbidities. About 13.4% (n=203 800) patients received reperfusion therapy. Black (adjusted risk ratio [aRR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.04–1.07]) and Hispanic (aRR, 1.36 [95% CI, 1.33–1.40]) patients were more likely to receive it compared with White patients, as were those in counties with lower education (aRR, 1.08 [95% CI, 1.07–1.09]). Older adults (5‐year increase in age aRR, 97 [95% CI, 0.97–0.97]), rural residents (aRR, 0.58 [95% CI, 0.56–0.59]), and those with missing last known well time (aRR, 0.30 [95% CI, 0.29–0.30]) were less likely to receive it. Missing last known well was less likely in Hispanic (aRR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.92–0.95]) and Asian (aRR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.90–0.96]) patients compared with White patients and more likely in those residing in counties with high unemployment (aRR, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.06–1.08]).ConclusionsIndividual‐ and county‐level social determinants of health were associated with reperfusion therapies and missing last known well times. Understanding the mechanisms by which these factors could affect treatment eligibility through time‐based criteria can help increase reperfusion therapies for all.Keywords: county‐level, missing time, social determinants of health, thrombectomy, thrombolysisSubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke

Research Perspective

What is New?

In patients aged 40 years and older with acute ischemic stroke seen at a Get With The Guidelines‐Stroke Registry hospital in the United States, county‐level lower education (less than high school) was associated with a higher likelihood of receiving reperfusion therapy and county‐level unemployment was associated with higher likelihood of having missing last known well time.

In patients aged 40 years and older with acute ischemic stroke seen at a Get With The Guidelines‐Stroke Registry hospital in the United States, county‐level lower education (less than high school) was associated with a higher likelihood of receiving reperfusion therapy and county‐level unemployment was associated with higher likelihood of having missing last known well time.

Older patients and those in rural area were less likely to receive reperfusion therapies despite the lack of association with missing last known well time.

What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

It is important to understand how and to what extent the observed differential effects of individual‐ and county‐level social determinants of health on acute reperfusion therapies are mediated by time‐based eligibility.

It is important to understand how and to what extent the observed differential effects of individual‐ and county‐level social determinants of health on acute reperfusion therapies are mediated by time‐based eligibility.

Stroke is a leading cause of disability worldwide. 1 In recent years, hyperacute reperfusion therapies including intravenous thrombolysis and thrombectomy have revolutionized stroke care and improved outcomes of patients with ischemic stroke by reducing both disability and mortality. 2 However, a critical determinant of treatment eligibility is knowing the time when someone was LKW and the time between LKW and arrival to a hospital.

When evaluating social determinants of receipt of hyperacute reperfusion therapies, previous reports have found that older age, Black race, and rural residence were associated with lower receipt of thrombolysis 3 and thrombectomy, 4 whereas women had similar odds of receiving thrombolysis 5 but marginally higher odds of thrombectomy compared with men. 6 , 7 , 8 However, many of these studies included only patients who were broadly eligible for hyperacute therapies based on arrival time (eg, <4.5 hours, <6 hours from LKW time) that was calculated only among those who did not have missing data on LKW or arrival time. This is important because retrospective patient chart reviews have found that LKW time is not documented in 25% to 40% patients: greater stroke severity and occurrence of stroke at a location other than home both associated with having higher rates of missing time. 9 , 10 It is also possible that both individual‐level factors and county‐level social determinants of health (SDOH) may be associated with having missing LKW or arrival time, which could in turn affect treatment eligibility. However, there is little known on this topic.

Our overall objective was to determine whether individual‐level and county‐level SDOH variables influence the receipt of reperfusion therapies in the United States among all patients with acute ischemic stroke admitted to a GWTG (Get With The Guidelines)‐Stroke hospital. We first evaluated the association between individual‐ and county‐level SDOH on the receipt of hyperacute stroke therapies (thrombolysis, thrombectomy or both) in all patients with acute ischemic stroke, including those with missing LKW or arrival time, and then evaluated the association between individual‐ and county‐level SDOH in subgroups eligible for reperfusion therapies based on known LKW to arrival times. We hypothesized that the associations between SDOH and receipt of reperfusion therapy and having missing LKW times would be in opposite directions (eg, female versus male sex would be associated with lower rates of reperfusion therapy and higher rates of missing LKW times), suggesting that missing LKW time or arrival time could be one common pathway through which SDOH exert their influence on receipt of treatment.

METHODS

Data Availability

GWTG‐Stroke data were collected by the American Heart Association (the steward of the data according to contracts between the American Heart Association and participating hospitals) and are stored securely at the Duke Clinical Research Institute. Given that data were collected for clinical care and quality improvement, rather than primarily for research, data‐sharing agreements require an application process for other researchers to access the data. Interested researchers can submit proposals to use GWTG for research purposes, including for validation purposes. Proposals can be submitted at www.heart.org/qualityresearch.

Setting

We used the GWTG‐Stroke data the details of which have been previously described. 11 , 12 The American Heart Association Precision Medicine Platform (https://precision.heart.org) was used for data analysis (with analyses done by M.V.V.). In brief, this is a US‐based quality improvement program developed by the American Heart Association that includes patients with stroke or transient ischemic attack admitted to participating acute care centers. Each participating hospital received either human research approval to enroll cases without individual patient consent under the common rule or a waiver of authorization and exemption from subsequent review by their institutional review board. Advarra, the institutional review board for the American Heart Association, determined that this study is exempt from institutional review board oversight. IQVIA (Parsippany, NJ) serves as the data collection and coordination center for GWTG. Trained hospital‐based personnel use a structured data abstraction form to collect data on consecutive patients admitted to the hospital with a principal clinical diagnosis of stroke or transient ischemic attack. Case ascertainment of admissions for ischemic stroke is conducted by either prospective clinical identification or retrospective identification using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10 I63) discharge codes for ischemic stroke, followed by chart review to confirm case eligibility.

Cohort Creation

We included all patients aged 40 years or older hospitalized with acute ischemic stroke at one of the participating GWTG‐Stroke hospitals between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2019. We included only people above this age cutoff because a larger proportion of patients with ischemic stroke below the age of 40 years may have a stroke cause that may not be directly influenced by SDOH (eg, carotid dissection or reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome). In order to generalize our findings to the broadest possible population we applied the following limited number of exclusion criteria: those with missing data for our exposure variables (age, sex, race or ethnicity, rural versus urban residence and county‐level data on income, unemployment and education likely due to missing county/zip code information), those who had an inpatient stroke, and those with missing data on medical comorbidities (Figure 1). Lastly, we excluded patients from those hospitals that enrolled <30 patients per year in the registry. We did not apply any time‐based exclusions for our primary cohort.

Exposures

We focused on the following individual patient‐level sociodemographic factors: age, defined in years; sex, defined as female or male; rural residence, defined as those outside of Metropolitan Statistical Areas as designated by US Office of Management and Budget; and race or ethnicity as recorded in patient charts. Race or ethnicity was based on a variety of potential data sources including patient self‐designation, from administrative personnel during registration process, from the admitting provider, from the nurse intake forms, or from any combination of above. Race or ethnicity was categorized in this study as (1) non‐Hispanic White (herein referred as “White”), (2) non‐Hispanic Black (“Black”), (3) Hispanic, (4) Asian, or (5) Other (eg, native Hawaiian, Pacific Islander, American Indian, and Alaska Native).

We obtained information on county of residence from the patient’s zip code collected by the GWTG Registry. We obtained data on county‐level SDOH from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME). 13 We focused on 3 county‐level measures: poverty, defined as a percentage of the population living below the US poverty line; unemployment, defined as the percentage of the population aged 16 years or older that is unemployed; and lower education, defined as percentage of population without a high‐school diploma. Because these metrics of county‐level SDOH were in percentage form, we standardized them (by subtracting the mean county‐level SDOH from each metric and then dividing the resultant quantity by the SD of the county‐level SDOH) to evaluate the association between a 1 SD increase in a county‐level SDOH and outcomes of interest (eg, 1 SD increase in the percentage of people with lower education). We assessed the pairwise correlation between the 3 county‐level SDOH using Pearson’s correlation coefficient.

Outcomes

We created a primary cohort of all patients with ischemic stroke without any additional exclusions and 2 secondary cohorts of treatment‐eligible patients (thrombolysis and thrombectomy eligible). In the primary cohort, we measured our primary outcome as the receipt of hyperacute reperfusion therapy (thrombolysis, thrombectomy, or both). We then studied our secondary outcome of having missing LKW or arrival time in this cohort as well.

We created secondary cohorts in the following manner: (1) for the outcome of receipt of thrombolysis in eligible patients, we created a cohort of patients who were not on anticoagulants and arrived within 4.5 hours from LKW time (and also did not have missing LKW time); and (2) for the outcome of receipt of thrombectomy in eligible patients, we created a cohort of patients who arrived within 6 hours of LKW (and did not have missing LKW time), had a moderate to severe stroke (a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 6 or more), and had information on receipt of thrombectomy included. These secondary cohorts of treatment‐eligible patients were created to compare our findings to prior work on GWTG that have used similar criteria for inclusion (Figure 1).

Lastly, in a subgroup of patients without missing LKW or arrival time, we studied the association between SDOH and time‐based eligibility. We studied the following outcomes: (1) arrival to the hospital within 4.5 hours of LKW; and (2) arrival to hospital within 6 hours.

Covariates of Interest

We included the following covariates, abstracted from patient records, in all regression models regardless of statistical significance: arrival after hours, defined as arrival on weekends or between 6 PM to 7 AM on Monday to Friday (yes or no), type of insurance coverage (categorized as: private, Medicaid, Medicare, self‐pay, or other), region of the United States (North East, Midwest, South, and West), academic versus nonacademic site, and the following comorbidities specified as binary variables: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, depression, migraine, obesity, smoking status, chronic kidney disease, prior history of stroke, and family history of stroke (yes versus no). This set of variables was selected based on prior reports using this data set. 14 These variables are widely regarded as being clinically relevant factors that may act as confounders when examining independent factors associated with receipt of thrombolysis or thrombectomy.

Statistical Analysis

We evaluated the differences in patient characteristics between those who received any reperfusion therapy versus those who did not receive any reperfusion therapy using standardized differences, with values >0.10 considered to reflect a meaningful difference. 15 We fit a series of regression models estimated using generalized estimating equations methods that accounted for the clustering of patients within their county of residence. 16 We used multivariable models that included individual patient‐level sociodemographic factors of interest and county‐level SDOH variables of interest and adjusted for the patient‐level covariates of interest previously described (under the heading covariates of interest). We fit log‐binomial generalized linear models estimated using generalized estimating equations methods with an exchangeable working correlation matrix, allowing us to report adjusted relative risks (aRR) for each of the binary outcomes. We first report adjusted relative risks for each SDOH of interest for the primary outcome: receipt of thrombolysis or thrombectomy in the primary cohort. We then report the aRRs for the secondary outcome of having missing LKW or arrival time.

Finally, we report the aRRs for the receipt of thrombolysis in eligible patients, receipt of thrombectomy in eligible patients, and the aRRs for arrival within 4.5 hours from LKW and arrival within 6 hours from LKW.

Because of substantial missing data on NIHSS scores (n=139 490, 9.2%), we added NIHSS (a measure of stroke severity as a categorical variable: mild, moderate, severe or missing) to our models of primary outcome (any reperfusion therapy) only in the primary cohort in a sensitivity analysis.

RESULTS

Of the 3 281 690 patients with ischemic stroke included in the GWTG registry, we included 1.5 million adults with ischemic stroke in our primary cohort who had complete information on the exposures of interest and who met our inclusion criteria (Figure 1). The overall cohort characteristics are listed in Table 1. The mean age of the cohort was 71.2 (SD=13.2) years and 756 707 (49.8%) were female. Of the cohort, 274 464 (18.0%) were Black and 110 848 (7.3%) were of Hispanic ethnicity, and 62 151 (4.1%) lived in rural areas. Of the total, 381 781 (25.1%) had a missing LKW time or arrival time, with most of these having missing LKW time (n=325 804; 85.3%). County‐level poverty and unemployment were highly correlated (r=0.83), whereas poverty and lower education (r=0.67) and unemployment and lower education (r=0.47) were less so.

SDOH and Any Reperfusion Treatment

Of the 1.5 million patients in the entire cohort, 203 800 (13.4%) received thrombolysis, thrombectomy, or both. Table 1 shows the differences between those who received any reperfusion therapy and those who did not. Final multivariable log‐binomial model results are shown in Table 2 and Figure 2. Female sex was marginally associated with higher use of reperfusion therapy (aRR, 1.03 [95% CI, 1.02–1.04]). Compared with White patients, Black (aRR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.04–1.07]), Hispanic (aRR, 1.36 [95% CI, 1.33–1.40]), and Asian (aRR, [95% CI, 1.05 1.02–1.07]) patients were more likely to receive reperfusion therapies; whereas those who were older (aRR for 5‐year increase in age, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.97–0.97]) or lived in rural versus urban areas (aRR, 0.58 [95% CI, 0.56–0.59]) were less likely to receive reperfusion therapies. Those who lived in counties with lower levels of education (for 1 SD increase percentage of people with below high school education aRR, 1.08 [95% CI, 1.07–1.09]) were more likely to receive reperfusion therapies, but county‐level poverty or unemployment was not associated with reperfusion therapies (Table 2). Patients with missing LKW were substantially less likely to receive reperfusion therapies (aRR, 0.30 [95% CI, 0.29–0.30]). In sensitivity analyses, the addition of NIHSS score to the regression models did not alter the observed associations, except for sex, which was no longer associated with reperfusion therapies (Table S1). Of note, having a missing NIHSS score was associated with 78% lower risk of receiving any reperfusion therapy.

SDOH and Missing LKW or Arrival Time

About one quarter of the total sample had missing LKW or arrival time, of which >85% was missing LKW time (Table S2). Compared with White patients, Hispanic (aRR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.92–0.95]) and Asian (aRR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.90–0.96]) patients but not Black patients (aRR, 1.01 [95% CI, 1.00–1.02]) were less likely to have missing time. and patients in counties with higher unemployment rates were more likely to have missing time (aRR, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.06–1.08]) (Figure 3).

Among Those Eligible for Intravenous Thrombolysis

Of 414 172 (27.3% of the primary cohort) people potentially eligible for thrombolysis (defined on the basis of arrival within 4.5 hours on onset and not on an anticoagulant), 149 439 (36.1%) received this treatment. Overall, the direction of the associations between individual factors and receipt of thrombolysis in eligible patients was similar to that noted in the entire cohort, although the magnitudes of the associations were smaller (ie, the point estimates were closer to null values) and female sex was no longer associated with receipt of thrombolysis (Figure 2). For county‐level SDOH, residence in counties with lower education was associated with a lower use of thrombolysis (aRR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.92–0.94]), opposite of what was observed in the overall cohort, but there was no association between county‐level poverty or unemployment and receipt of thrombolysis (Figure 2).

Among Those Eligible for Thrombectomy

Overall, 208 543 (13.7%) were potentially eligible for thrombectomy as defined by arrival within 6 hours of LKW and NIHSS score ≥6. Of those eligible, 26 902 (12.9%) received thrombectomy. As in the overall cohort, Black, Asian, and Hispanic patients had a higher likelihood of receiving thrombectomy than White patients; whereas those residing in rural versus urban areas (aRR, 0.18 [95% CI, 0.16–0.21]) were substantially less likely to receive thrombectomy (Figure 2). Residence in a county with higher rates of unemployment was associated with a lower use of thrombectomy (aRR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.89–0.92] per 1 SD increase in unemployment), but there was no effect of poverty or education.

Association With Arrival Within 4.5 Hours and 6 Hours From LKW Time

Among those who had complete data on LWK and arrival time (n=1 138 312, 75% of the primary cohort), 415 583 (36.5%) arrived within 4.5 hours of LKW and 478 893 (42.1%) arrived within 6 hours of LKW. The direction of association between each SDOH and arrival within 4.5 hour and arrival within 6 hours was the same, with minimal differences in the point estimates (Figure 3). Compared with White patients, Hispanic patients were more likely to arrive within eligible treatment time; whereas, Black, Asian, and other racial or ethnic groups were less likely to arrive within eligible treatment times. Those living in rural areas were also less likely to arrive within treatment eligible windows. Patients residing in counties with lower education were less likely to arrive within eligible treatment times (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

In patients with ischemic stroke seen in a GWTG‐registry hospital in the United States, both individual‐ and county‐level SDOH were associated with receipt of reperfusion therapies. County‐level lower education (less than high school) and Black and Asian race or Hispanic ethnicity were associated with a higher use of reperfusion therapy, whereas older age, rural residence, and having missing LKW time were associated with a lower use of reperfusion therapy. Hispanic and Asian patients were less likely to have missing LKW time than White patients, and patients in counties with higher unemployment were more likely to have missing LKW time. Most of these associations had small magnitudes of effect on a relative scale, which may limit the clinical significance of the findings. These associations should be replicated in future analyses accounting for other potential confounding variables.

We found that 1 SD increase in the county‐level proportion of people with less than high school diploma (lower education) was associated with an 8% increase in reperfusion therapies, but it was not associated with having missing LKW or arrival time; however, it was negatively associated with both arriving in time (within 4.5 hours or 6 hours of LKW) and receipt of thrombolysis if restricted to those who are eligible. In contrast, a higher rate of county‐level unemployment was negatively associated with reperfusion therapy and positively associated with missing time. These findings support our hypothesis that at least one pathway for SDOH to exert their effects on therapies may be through having missing LKW time or delayed arrival when these data are recorded. County‐level poverty was not associated with reperfusion therapy or with missing LKW time in our cohort. The association between other neighborhood or regional measures of SDOH such as material deprivation or income and thrombolysis and thrombectomy have been previously studied, 17 , 18 but county‐level measures of poverty, unemployment, and lower education have not been studied before. One study found a positive correlation between county‐level poverty and county‐level unemployment and the county‐level prevalence of stroke; however, individual treatments and outcomes were not studied. 19 We did not study the interaction between county‐level measures of SDOH and rural residence, which may shed further light on whether some observed associations are explained or modified by rural residence. Also, it is possible that county‐level SDOH have a nonlinear relationship with reperfusion treatments, and this needs to be explored in future studies. Despite moderate correlation between them, we included all 3 county‐level SDOH measures in our regression models, which could diminish their associations with reperfusion therapies. Understanding how these county‐level SDOH are associated with reperfusion therapies and treatment eligibility could help further refine education campaigns that improve awareness of symptoms of stroke or increase access to resources for reperfusion therapies. 20

Knowing the time of LKW is a crucial piece of clinical information that determines a patient’s eligibility for reperfusion therapies, and this was missing in 25% of patients. This may be due to patient‐specific factors, such as living alone, or with someone unable to provide the information at the time of the stroke (too old/young, partner with dementia). It is also possible that provider‐ or facility‐specific factors may impede recording these times, and if identified, it could be an avenue for quality improvement interventions to increase patient eligibility. Lastly, it is possible that a higher proportion of people had missing LKW or arrival time because they arrived too late to be eligible for acute reperfusion therapies. However, examining the associations between county‐level SDOH variables and thrombolysis or thrombectomy among only those in the treatment‐eligible time windows did not alter the overall associations seen in the overall cohort.

Sex differences in reperfusion treatments in our study had very small magnitudes and are generally in keeping with previous reports. 5 , 8 Compared with male patients, female patients were equally likely to arrive in treatment‐eligible windows and were marginally more likely to having missing data on LKW or arrival time. The latter could be because women are more likely to be living alone at the time of their stroke 21 and so there may not be anyone to provide information on LKW time. Our finding of an association between older age and rural residence and lower use of thrombolysis and thrombectomy is consistent with previous studies. 4 , 6 , 7 , 22 We found no association between older age and arrival in treatment‐eligible windows or having missing LKW or arrival time. Thus, age‐related disparity in thrombolysis or thrombectomy is likely not driven by delay in presentation. In contrast, we found that residing in rural areas was associated with an 8% decrease in not arriving in time (ie, < 4.5 hours or <6 hours from LKW), although it was not associated with having missing LKW or arrival time. Thus, one explanation for the observed rural–urban disparity may directly relate to the travel time from home to a hospital, or in some patients, may be due to the time taken for interhospital transfers to a hospital that can provide reperfusion therapies. Yet, even in those who arrived within 4.5 or 6 hours, rural residents were 22% less likely to receive thrombolysis and 82% less likely to get thrombectomy compared with urban residents, suggesting additional clinical‐ or hospital‐level factors at play or that they are arriving in the latter time windows of these cutoffs.

Our findings are in contrast to prior studies where Black race is associated with lower use of thrombolysis and thrombectomy. 3 , 23 However, there has been a decrease in the racial disparity in thrombolysis and thrombectomy between Black and White patients over time. 4 , 24 In our cohort, Black patients were less likely to arrive in treatment‐eligible time windows, but when arriving in treatment‐eligible windows, they were more likely to receive reperfusion therapies. Thus, improving arrival times in Black patients could be one avenue of intervention that can potentially reduce treatment disparities as shown by quality improvement initiatives (Target Stroke). 24 A previous report identified equal rates of thrombectomy in Hispanic patients when compared with White patients. 4 We found that Hispanic patients were more likely to receive reperfusion therapies, more likely to arrive in treatment‐eligible windows, and less likely to have missing time. Although the improved thrombolysis and thrombectomy among racial and ethnic groups observed in our study needs to be replicated, our findings do not capture existing geographic disparities 25 or quality of acute treatment (ie, receipt within a specific door‐to‐needle time), which may be equally important than the receipt alone. 24

This study has several limitations. First, we excluded people with missing information on the individual sociodemographic factors of interest in our cohorts, and it is possible that factors such as not speaking English or being older could be associated a higher likelihood of missing data. Ideally, we would include information on large vessel occlusion to determine eligibility for thrombectomy, but it is an optional field in the GWTG data set when patients did not undergo thrombectomy. We could evaluate only time from LKW to first arrival to a GWTG hospital and did not use the information on potential preceding interhospital transfers, which may have altered the arrival times for some individuals, especially those living in rural residence. We did not undertake thrombolysis eligibility based on GWTG metrics such arrive by “2 and treat by 3” because these metrics rely on known LKW, which as we show with our analyses, can be directly related to both individual factors and county‐level SDOH, potentially leading to selection bias. We did not compare the observed associations stratified by US region nor did we evaluate the interaction of individual factors and county‐level SDOH. Further, it is not clear if individual factors or county‐level SDOH are likely to influence coding of these variables. Although we acknowledge the observed differences have small magnitude of effects on a relative scale, their impact might be magnified when considering across an entire population. Like almost all previous work using GWTG Stroke Registry, 8 , 12 , 14 we used complete‐case analyses whereby those with missing information on exposures and covariates were excluded. Although we explored the impact of not accounting for missing LKW time in the analyses, future work is needed to’understand whether and to what extent key covariates in the GWTG‐stroke registry are missing in nonrandom pattern and the implications for future research using these data. There is also a chance of ecological fallacy because we used county‐level data on poverty, unemployment, and lower education, which are measured at an aggregate level and may not accurately quantify the true relationships of factors at the individual level. 26 Some of the covariates in our models may have been measured with error, possibly leading to biased estimates of relative risks. However, previous audit on GWTG‐Stroke registry data showed accurate data collection and the measurement errors are likely to be nondifferential, that is, errors in measurement are not associated with our exposures of interest, so any effects of measurement error would tend toward the null. 27

Conclusions

In summary, among US hospitals participating in GWTG‐Stroke registry, individual factors and county‐level SDOH have a varying influence on the receipt of hyperacute therapies and time to presentation. Recognizing these differences can help direct appropriate interventions that can improve use of reperfusion therapies, irrespective of individual factors or county‐level SDOH.
