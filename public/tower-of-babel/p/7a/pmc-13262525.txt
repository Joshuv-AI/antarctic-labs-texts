CPVT1 point mutations in RyR2 S5 and S6 segments and their Ca2+ signaling consequence
Authors: Xiao-hua Zhang, Grace Ellen Donch, Naohiro Yamaguchi, Martin Morad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecise activation of cardiac ryanodine receptor (RyR2) by small influx of Ca2+ during the action potential triggers the release of SR Ca2+ that activates contraction, a process known as Ca2+-induced Ca2+ release (CICR). Missense mutations in RyR2 often cause aberrant and unregulated Ca2+ releases that are associated with catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT), often lethal arrhythmias. Here using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing in human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), we extended our previous studies to include two new arrhythmogenic mutations one, R4822H, located in S5-S6 transmembrane luminal loop near RyR2 selective filter and the other, L4865V, located on S6 segment. TIRF-imaging of voltage-clamped mutant myocytes showed that ICa and caffeine-triggered cytosolic Ca2+ rise (Fura-2 signal) or ER-GCaMP6 SR Ca2+ release signals were significantly suppressed in R4822H but not in L4865V myocytes. Spontaneous Ca2+ transients, however, persisted in both mutant lines activating both Fura-2 and ER-GCaMP6 Ca2+ transients in L4865V cells, but only Fura-2 Ca2+ transients in R4822H mutant. Spontaneous Ca2+ sparks igniting frequencies were similar in both mutants, but spark durations were significantly shorter. Although both of these mutations are located at S5 and S6 transmembrane regions of RyR2, their phenotypes diverge markedly. L4865V mutant does not show suppressed E-C coupling function, while R4822H mutant has completely suppressed CICR suggesting that the spontaneous beating in R4822H mutant results from remodeling of dormant Ca2+ signaling pathway expressed in hiPSC–CMs.Keywords: hiPSC-CMs, CRISPR/Cas9, R4822H and L4865V, RyR2 S5 and S6, ER-GCaMP6 SR Ca2+ probe

Introduction

Cardiac contraction is activated by influx of Ca2+ through Ca2+ channels (Cav1.2), triggering larger release of Ca2+ from sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) through ryanodine receptor [1–3], a process known as Ca2+-induced Ca2+ release (CICR). RyR2 protein is a homotetramer of ~5000 amino acids that is physiologically regulated by Ca2+, Mg2+, ATP, FKBP12.6, CaM, protein kinases, and phosphatases [4–6]. RyR2 protein expresses 6 transmembrane domains where the fifth (S5) and sixth (S6) transmembrane segments form the ion channel pore [7,8]. While the cytosolic side of S5 stabilizes the channel activity by interacting with the S6 inner helix, the luminal loop between S5 and S6 transmembrane regions serve as the ion selective filter, with the luminal side of S5 regulating the RyR channel opening [9].

Over 200-point mutations, spread over 4-disease related hotspot domains (N-terminal, helical, central and C-terminal domains) of RYR2 gene, have been identified to associate with type-1 catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT1) or Calcium release deficiency syndrome (CRDS) [10–13]. The RyR2 S6 inner helix is a mutation hotspot carrying numerous disease-associated mutations [11,14]. HEK293 cells recombinant RyR2 expression of mutations in the S6 helix facing the U-motif show mostly gain of function phenotype while mutations on the opposite side of S6 show loss of function in E-C coupling [15]. From loss of function mutations in RyR2 S6 inner helix I4855M, A4860G, and Q4879H, only I4855M and A4860G have been studied in both HEK293 cells and transgenic mice [16–18]. One human variant in S5 transmembrane, L4769S-RyR2, has been studied to exhibit a potential Ca2+ release deficient syndrome phenotype in HEK293 cell expression platform [14]; however, S5-S6 loop disease-associated mutations have yet to be characterized with respect to their RyR2 ion channel function or Ca2+ signaling phenotypes.

Even though we have reported on Ca2+ signaling consequences of eight RyR2-mutations in human cardiomyocyte platform [19–23], we have not reported on CPTV1-mutations in the pore region. Here, we report on Ca2+-signaling consequences of RyR2-R4822H and L4865V mutations located respectively in S5-S6 transmembrane loop and in S6 segment that induce arrhythmias in the hiPSC cardiomyocyte platform [24,25]. Our studies support the possibility that mutations in S5-S6 region induce arrhythmogenesis by causing remodeling of Ca2+ signaling pathways. Unexpectedly, myocytes expressing R4822H mutation although caused complete suppression of CICR continued to beat spontaneously, consistent with the remodeling of the Ca2+ signaling pathway when CICR is deleted, reported earlier [21].

Materials and methods

Maintenance of undifferentiated hiPSC lines

hiPSC K3 line [26] was used as the WT cell line as described in previous publications [20,21]. Undifferentiated hiPSCs were maintained in StemFlex medium (Gibco) on Vitronectin (Gibco) coated 60 mm culture dishes at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. The culture medium was replaced every 2 days and hiPSCs were passaged with Accutase (Thermo Fisher) every 4–6 days. 10 μM Y-27632 (Tocris) was added in the culture media for 24 h after each passage.

CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing in hiPSCs

CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing technique was applied to introduce RyR2 mutation R4822H and L4865V into hiPSC genome with Cas9 protein (IDT, Alt-R™ S.p. HiFi Cas9 Nuclease V3). The guide sequence 5′-ATGTATGTTGGAGTTCGTGC −3′ for R4822H was designed by CRISPR Design Tool (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/) and was synthesized by Synthego. Site-directed mutations were introduced by homology-directed repair of the Cas9-digested site using single stranded oligo donor nucleotide (ssODN) (5′-CAAGAAGTGATACCATGTCTTTTTTCCAGTGCTATATGTTCCA-CATGTATGTTGGAGTgCacGCTGGAGGAGGGATCGGGGATGAAATCGAAGACCCAGCAGGAGATGAATA TGAGATC), which carries the R4822H mutation (CGT to Cac) and a silent mutation (GTT to GTg). The guide sequence for targeting L4865V mutation 5′-GGTCTAATTATTGATGCTTT-3′ was designed with the same tool. The ssODN (5′-CAAGGAACTGAATTATTCATTGCTTGATAAA-GATTAAATTGTGTTTTTATTTAA-CACCAGGTgTgATcATTGATGCTTTTGGAGAACTAAGAGACCAA-CAGGAACAAGTCAAAGAAGACATGGAGGTAAGC −3′) carries two mutations, a mutation of L4865V (CTA to gTg) and a silent mutation (ATT to ATc). Cas9 protein 12 μg and sgRNA 2 μg were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 15mins and then transfected together with the single stranded oligonucleotide (0.1 nmol) and pEGFP-puro plasmid (1 μg) [27](addgene #45561) by electroporation with the setting of one pulse for 30 ms at 1200 V. The transfected cells were then plated in the 60 mm vitronectin coated dish with 10 μM Y-27632 ROCK inhibitor in the culture medium. 24 h after transfection, the cells were incubated with 1 μg/mL puromycin (Tocris) for 48 h. Surviving cell colonies were harvested 7–10 days after puromycin treatment. Genomic DNAs were extracted with QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (LGC biosearch technologies) from the harvested cells, and the R4822H and L4865V RyR2 mutations were screened by PCR and sequencing of PCR products.

Differentiation of hiPSCs into functional cardiomyocytes

CRISPR/Cas9 gene edited R4822H and L4865V hiPSCs were differentiated into cardiomyocytes by using the standard protocol as described previously [19,28]. Briefly, hiPSCs were dissociated and plated in Matrigel (Corning) coated 12 well plates. The next day, cells were treated with 10 μM CHIR99021 (Tocris), a GSK3β inhibitor in RPMI/B-27 medium without insulin (B27(−) medium) for 24 h. Cells were then cultured in B27(−) medium for 48 h and then the medium was switched to B27(−) medium containing 5 μM IWR-1, (a Wnt signaling inhibitor) for another 48 h and then was changed back to B27(−) medium. Once the spontaneous beating appeared, the culture medium was switched to RPMI/B-27 medium containing insulin (B27(+) medium). After 5–6 weeks of spontaneous beating, cell clusters were dissociated into single cardiomyocytes using 10x TrypLE™ Select Enzyme (Gibco) for electrophysiological and Ca2+ imaging experiments. For molecular biology and protein experiments, the beating colonies were collected around day 40 after beating. Cells from 4–5 different differentiations were used for the experiments. For electrophysiological and Ca2+ imaging experiments, myocytes were harvested from the same batch of cells maintained in culture media for up to 4 months.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNAs of WT and mutant hiPSCs-CMs were extracted with TRIzol LS reagent (Ambion, Life technology) and then purified by RNeasy kit (Qiagen). The cDNAs were synthesized from total RNAs by reverse transcription with Verso cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems TM, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the CFX Opus Real-Time PCR Systems (Bio-Rad). The thermal profile was 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. To obtain a relative quantization, the results were normalized to those obtained for the corresponding GAPDH expression level.

Measurements of Ca2+ transients and sparks

Single beating hiPSC–CMs were plated on vitronectin coated 25 mm glass coverslips and allowed to recover in culture medium for at least three days prior to imaging. Cells were incubated in 1 μM of Fluo-4AM containing solution for 30 mins and imaged using a multicolor total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) imaging system (Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, IL) fitted with a 100 × oil-immersion objective lens and K8 Scientific CMOS Camera. The laser used for excitation of Fluo-4 and ER-GCaMP6 was at 488 nm and 405 nm for Fura-2 fluorescence cells, and the emission was measured at 502–548nm and 420–470 respectively. Cells were super-fused with normal Tyrode’s solution containing (in mM): 137 NaCl, 5.4 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 glucose, and 10 HEPES (titrated to pH 7.4 with NaOH). Ca2+ sparks were recorded at 80–100 Hz with a depth of penetration of 110–150 nm to focus primarily on the sub-sarcolemmal Ca2+release. Sparks data was exported and analyzed with Leica LAS X and a computerized algorithm described previously [20,21].

Electrophysiology

Membrane currents of wild type and mutant hiPSC–CMs were recorded at 30–32 °C using the whole cell patch clamp technique, employing a Dagan amplifier and pClamp software (Clampex 10.6). Borosilicate patch pipettes with a resistance of 4–6 MΩ were pulled using a horizontal pipette puller (Model P-87, Sutter Instruments, CA). Cells were dialyzed with a Cs+-based internal solution containing (in mM): 100 Cs+-Aspartate, 5 NaCl, 30 TEA-Cl, 5 Mg-ATP, 0.5 MgCl2, 0.1 EGTA, 0.3 CaCl2, 20 HEPES and 0.1 Fluo-4 penta-potassium salt, buffered at pH 7.2. The dialyzing pipette solutions had osmolarity of 300 mOsm/L. Standard Tyrode’s solution was used as external bath solution. L-type Ca2+ current (ICa) and the corresponding Ca2+ transients were recorded simultaneously using depolarizing pulses from −50 mV to 0 mV. Caffeine induced Ca2+ transients and Na+-Ca2+ exchanger current (INCX) were recorded by rapid application of 5 mM caffeine for 1 s. Results were analyzed with the Clampfit10.6 software and the final figures were made by origin 8 (Origin Lab Corporation).

ER-GCaMP6 Ca2+ probe

ER-GCaMP6–150 cDNA plasmid (#86918) was purchased from Addgene (Watertown, MA). Adenovirus carrying ER-GCaMP6–150 was produced by Vector Biosystems Inc (Malvern, PA). Some 24–48 h after plating the hiPSC–CMs on 25 mm coverslips, the cells were infected by a solution containing ER-GCaMP6 adenovirus containing (MOI) of 300–500 virus particles per cell (vp/cell)[20]. After 6–8 h, the virus medium was removed, and cells were supplemented with B27+ medium and kept in the incubator at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 until their use between 48–72 h post infection.

Statistical analysis

Results are indicated as the means ± SEM. Comparative analysis was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test or Student’s t-test. Significant differences are labeled with one (p < 0.05, *) or two stars (p < 0.01, **). Origin 8 was used to obtain statistical values.

Results

Creation of R4822H and L4865V hiPSC lines

Cas9 ribonucleoprotein was used to introduce R4822H and L4865V mutations into WT hiPSCs. The RyR2 structure and the cartoon in Fig. 1A shows the locations of the R4822H and L4865V mutations. R4822H is located between S5 and S6 transmembrane luminal loop near the RyR2 selective filter while L4865V mutation is located at S6 segment. Figure 1B shows the design we used to introduce R4822H mutation (CGT to CAC) and the silent mutation (GTT to GTG) that targets RYR2 Exon 101. Genomic DNA of the gene-edited locus was amplified by PCR and was screened by two sets of primers WT-F/Mut-R and Mut-F/WT-R, shown in Fig. 1B. Possible colonies were identified by successful PCR amplification of both primer pairs. Positive colonies were then confirmed by sequencing of the WT PCR product, in which the primer binding sites were outside the homologous arms (ssODN). We were able to obtain two homozygous clones of R4822H mutation. Fig. 1C shows the sequencing result of R4822H mutation (CGT to CAC) and the silent mutation (GTT to GTG).

Quantitative PCR analysis of RyR2 expression

Quantitative PCR was used to detect the mRNA levels of RyR2 in WT and mutant hiPSC–CMs. Total RNA and protein levels were measured around Day 40 after the initiation of spontaneous beating. Quantitative RT–PCR analyses showed that the expression level of RyR2 messenger RNA in R4822H and L4865V mutant hiPSC–CMs were essentially the same as WT cells, figure S1.

L-type Ca2+ current and ICa-triggered Ca2+ transients in R4822H and L4865V mutant cardiomyocytes

Calcium currents

L-type Ca2+ current and their triggered Ca2+ transients were measured in WT and mutant hiPSC–CMs dialyzed with pipette solutions containing 0.1 mM Fluo-4 penta-potassium salt and 0.1 mM EGTA plus 0.3 mM Ca2+ using TIRF-imaged whole-cell voltage-clamped cells. To activate ICa, cardiomyocytes were either depolarized from −50 to 0 mV or repolarized from +80 to −50 mV, activating rapidly decaying ICa tail currents. Peak ICa current densities of R4822H mutant cardiomyocytes were comparable to those of WT cells, averaging about −10.86± 1.27 pA/pF (n = 17) in control cells, as compared to −12.19± 1.13 pA/pF (n = 15) in R4822H homozygous cell line1 and −10.35 ± 1.68 pA/pF (n = 9) in homozygous cell line 2, Fig. 2A and C. Similarly, cardiomyocytes from L4865V clone1 (mutant carrying the deletion) and heterozygous L4865V mutant cell lines expressed peak ICa current densities that were also not significantly different than those of WT cells, averaging about −8.65 ± 1.07, n = 14, and −11.25 ± 2.07, n = 10, respectively Fig. 2A and C, but the kinetics of inactivation of ICa in both R4822H and L4865V mutant cells were significantly slower as compared to the WT cells, Fig. 2D.

Calcium transients

Calcium transients in WT and mutant cells were activated by both depolarizing and repolarizing pulses. The latter protocol activated fully developed but rapidly deactivating and brief ICa tail-currents. Fig. 2A shows that the Ca2+ transients activated by depolarization-triggered ICa in R4822H mutant cardiomyocytes were significantly suppressed compared to those activated in WT myocytes. Since significant Ca2+ enters the cell during the 100 ms long depolarizing pulses, the recorded Ca2+-transients represent the sum of Ca2+ influx and SR Ca2+ release. The Ca2+-tail paradigm, on the other hand, not only provides a measure of fully activated ICa but also because little Ca2+ enters the cell during the brief time course of rapidly deactivating ICa, allowing minimal Ca2+ influx, better represents the SR calcium-release component of Ca2+-transients, Fig. 2B and E. Note that ICa tail currents fail almost completely to activate significant Ca2+-transients in R4822H mutant as compared to depolarizing pulses, consistent with significant transmembrane influx of Ca2+ during the depolarizing pulses.

To better quantitate the mutation effects on the SR Ca2+ release, in another set of cells, we first infected them with a genetically engineered ER-GCaMP6 Ca2+ probe targeted to the SR and then dialyzed them with Fura-2 containing internal solution. Fig. 3A and B show that while depolarizing-pulses activated ICa triggered significant SR Ca2+ release (ER-GCaMP6 signal), in WT and L4865V mutant cells, they failed to activate SR Ca2+-transients signals in R4822H mutant cell lines. Comparison of Fura-2 and ER probe signals in R4822H mutant once again shows that cytosolic Fura-2 signals are less affected by the mutation compared to the SR-targeted ER-GCaMP6 signals most likely because of significant transmembrane Ca2+ influx during 100 ms depolarizing-activated currents.

Fig. 3C shows that trains of depolarizing pulses from −50 to 0mVs activated ICa-triggered rise in cytosolic Ca2+ (Fura-2 signal) and triggered SR Ca2+ release signals (ER-GCaMP6 signals) in WT and both clones of L4865V mutant cardiomyocytes. In sharp contrast, trains of depolarizing pulses in R4822H mutant cells, though activating a smaller Fura-2 Ca-transients, failed to trigger significant ICa-triggered ER-GCaMP6 signals.

SR Ca2+ content of R4822H and L4865V mutant myocytes

Since ICa-triggered SR Ca2+ releases were significantly suppressed in R4822H, but not in L4865V mutant lines, we tested whether the suppression was caused by a decrease in SR Ca2+ content, using 5- and 20-mM caffeine or 5 mM 4-CmC (another RyR2 agonist). In WT and L4865V mutant cardiomyocytes, 5- and 20-mM caffeine or 5 mM 4-CmC activated equivalent cytosolic and SR Ca2+ signals, Fig. 4. In sharp contrast, 5 and 20 mM caffeine failed to trigger significant SR Ca2+ release in most of the tested cells in both R4822H mutant cell lines. 4-CmC, on the other hand, triggered only significantly smaller cytosolic and SR Ca2+ signals in both R4822H mutant lines, Fig. 4. Interestingly, even though the two RyR2 agonists failed to activate significant ER-GCaMP6 signals, in some R4822H mutant cells 20 mM caffeine activated significant rise in cytosolic Ca2+ (Fura-2 signal, Fig. 4B) even in absence of SR Ca2+ release, suggestive of activation of another Ca2+ signaling pathway, consistent with previously reported findings that RyR2 Ca2+ binding sites mutations that suppress CICR remodel Ca2+ signaling [20].

To confirm the reliability of suppressive effects of R4822H mutation on the SR Ca2+ release monitored with ER-GCaMP6 probe, we also measured the activation of INCX in response to 5- and 20-mM caffeine in another set of patch clamped cells. Fig. 5A and C shows that 5- or 20-mM caffeine activates a large NCX current that accompanies the Fura-2 and ER-GCaMP6 signal in WT and L4865V mutants, which is strongly suppressed in R4822H mutants, Fig. 5B. Statistical analysis of Na/Ca exchanger current in control and mutant cells, Fig. 5D & E suggests that R4822H mutation strongly suppresses CICR as the cells either become insensitive to caffeine applications or have compromised SR Ca2+ content.

Spontaneously igniting focal Ca2+ releases in R4822H and L4865V mutant cells

To test the effects of transmembrane domain mutation on focal Ca2+ releases, we measured the morphology and the frequency of spontaneously igniting Ca2+ sparks in WT, R4822H and L4865V cells. Fig. 6A–D shows representative spontaneously igniting Ca2+ sparks in WT, R4822H and L4865V mutant hiPSC–CMs. WT and mutant cells Ca2+ sparks ignited rapidly and lasted for a brief period. Quantification of sparks frequency, Fig. 6E, suggests no significant difference in the frequency of spark ignition between WT and mutant cells. Histogram of spark duration, measured at half-maximum amplitude, however, showed greatly abbreviated spark duration, averaging 32 ± 1.83 ms in R4822H and 46 ± 1.99 ms in L4865V-1 mutant, as compared to 57.7 ± 1.98 ms in WT cells, consistent with greatly suppressed CICR, Fig. 6F. The distribution of sparks amplitude, Fig. 6G, suggests the average sparks amplitude (ΔF/F0) ranges between 0.86 to 0.94, showing no significant difference between WT and mutant hiPSCs.

Spontaneous beating in intact WT and mutant cells

We have previously reported that mutations in RyR2 Ca2+ binding site disabling CICR continue to beat spontaneously, activating Ca2+ transients in absence of SR Ca2+ release signals [21]. To test whether the transmembrane domain mutations also similarly fail to trigger SR Ca2+ release signals, we compared the occurrence of spontaneously activating Ca2+ transients measured with both the SR and cytosolic Ca2+ probes in WT, R4822H and L4865V mutant cells. While WT cells expressed regular beating rates Fig. 7A, 3 out of 11 R4822H-1 mutant cells had arrhythmic and variable beating frequencies, with individual Ca2+ transients showing variable durations and amplitudes, Fig. 7B. In addition, 6 out 19 L4865V, clone 1 and 1 out 7 L4865V-clone 2 (heterozygous) showed arrhythmic and variable beating frequencies, Fig. 7C–E. Note that L4865V cells exhibit prominent SR Ca2+ release and cytosolic Ca2+ rise (fura-2 signals) just like the WT cells, Fig. 7A and C–E. In sharp contrast, even though R4822H mutant hiPSC–CMs continued to beat spontaneously, 3 out of 11 R4822H-1 arrhythmically, they all failed to activate significant SR Ca2+-release signals, Fig. 7B. Thus, the R4822H mutant cells consistently failed to generate significant SR Ca2+ release signals, Fig. 7B and F, suggestive of loss of CICR mechanism. The decay rate of the Ca2+ transients are variable in both WT and mutant cells, but no significant difference was detected between WT and mutant groups, Fig. 7F.

Discussion

This is the first reported functional study of two arrhythmogenic RyR2 S5-S6 transmembrane linker point mutations expressed in human cardiomyocytes. Both mutations, though clinically identified as CPVT1-associated, have yet to be studied in recombinant HEK 293 cells or hiPSC–CM platforms. Here, we used CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to introduce the point mutations in hiPSCs derived cardiomyocytes while infecting the myocytes with the SR-targeted ER-GCaMP6 Ca2+-sensitive fluorescent probe, to also directly measure the contribution of SR Ca2+ release to the cytosolic Ca-transients monitored with Fura-2 probe. The R4822H S5-S6 linker loop mutation, appeared to disable CICR such that ICa- and caffeine- triggered Ca2+ release were strongly suppressed. L4865V mutation, located at S6 segment, despite its CPVT1-hotspot location, however, did not affect CICR significantly such that caffeine-triggered Ca2+ release signals were comparable to those of WT cells. Since both mutations are equally arrhythmogenic in patients, their cellular Ca2+ signaling defects might be the underlying cause of their arrhythmogenesis.

RyR2 mutations in S5-S6 segments

It has been already reported that the S6 inner helix is a hotspot for RyR2 loss of function mutations that include I4855M, A4860G, and Q4879H, associated with sudden cardiac death [11,14]. Although all three mutants are considered loss of function mutations, they express variable Ca2+ signaling phenotypes. Studies in HEK293 cells suggest that RYR2-I4855M and Q4879H mutations suppress caffeine-induced Ca2+ release [11,16], but the data in genetically modified mice show that heterozygous I4855M mice have enhanced SR Ca2+ stores [17]. A4860G mutation similarly exhibits larger caffeine induced caffeine release in both HEK293 cells and mouse cardiomyocyte platforms, even though CICR was suppressed [18,29]. Compared to other loss of function mutations of S6 inner helix, L4865V mutants retain normal caffeine and ICa induced Ca2+ release. There is only one L4865V mutation case-report of a Japanese CPVT patient that became arrhythmogenic when subjected to exercise [25]. Because hiPSC–CMs respond to adrenergic agonists variably, making the measurements unreliable, we could not directly test this clinical observation in our cells.

Since R4822H mutation site is near the GGIG (4825–4828 residues) motif, where the proposed ion selectivity filter is located, mutations in this or neighboring residues may alter the divalent to monovalent selectivity. The R4822H mutation may thus reduce Ca2+ selectivity over the monovalent. Consistent with this possibility a point mutation, R4822A (R4824A in rabbit sequence), reported in recombinant heterologous expression system, showed decreased caffeine-induced Ca2+ release and reduced divalent cation selectivity, using single channel measurements [30].

Is L4865V mutant a loss of function mutation?

Comparing the Ca2+ signaling data of both L4865V mutant clones with those of WT hiPSC–CMs (Figs. 2, 3 & 4) does not conform strictly with a greatly compromised Ca2+ signaling as might be expected from a loss of function mutation as is clearly seen for R4822H mutation. Even though the depolarization activated peak ICa triggered Fura-2 signal in Fig. 3 appears to suggest smaller Ca2+-transients for both L4865V clones compared to WT cells, direct measurements of ICa- or caffeine triggered SR Ca2+ release and spontaneous Ca2+ transients using ER-GCaMP6, Fig. 3, 4 and 7, do not support a compromised SR Ca2+ release, see also the robust 4-CmC Ca2+ transients activated in both L4865V clones, comparable to WT. Since ER-GCaMP6 probe directly measures the SR Ca2+ release and Fura-2 measures cytosolic rise of Ca2+ including transmembrane Ca2+ influx and Ca2+ release from other stores, we feel that the ER-GCaMP6 signal reflects better the activity of the SR. The reduced CICR Ca2+ signal measured with Fura-2 in L4865V cells may be related to oxidizing excitation wavelength used for Fura-2 when cells were exposed to two lasers simultaneously. The severity in the pathology of Ca2+ signaling in different cells and in the absence of adrenergic stimulation suggests that patients harboring this mutation are likely to be free of arrhythmic episodes and are less likely to encounter lethal bouts of arrhythmia unless confronted with severe stressful conditions.

Comparison of R4822H mutation with Ca2+ binding site mutations and spontaneous beating

The finding that spontaneous beating is preserved in R4822H mutation despite the loss of its CICR may allow patients harboring this mutation to maintain sufficient cardiac function necessary for patient’s survival. In this respect the R4822H mutation phenotype resembles the Ca2+ signaling phenotype of RyR2 Ca2+ binding site mutations, Q3925E and E3848A [21], which also were reported to maintain spontaneous beating despite the loss of their CICR. The diversity in calcium signaling phenotype of R4822H and L4865V mutations is striking especially when considering their location in S6 and S5-S6 linker. R4822H mutation has all the hallmarks of CICR compromised myocyte of Q3925E mutation of RyR2-Ca2+ binding site. Even though the locus of mutation is far from the RyR2 calcium-binding site, it may induce confirmational changes in the RyR2 structure that affects the RyR2 calcium-binding site. Since the mutant harboring S5-S6 linker mutation disables CICR fully and yet the cells continue to exhibit spontaneous Ca2+ transients like the phenotypes observed for Q3925E and E3848A mutations of RyR2 calcium binding site, it is likely that the loss of CICR provides the molecular signaling to shift the calcium signaling pathway from ICa-gated calcium mechanism to other pathways gated either by hemi-channels, IP3 receptors or mitochondria. It might be instructive to investigate whether such remodeling takes place in patients that harbor mutations that disable CICR fully. Our attempts to generate a transgenic mouse line harboring a mutation that deletes CICR were unsuccessful [31], because the founder mice died within 3 weeks of birth often with massive cardiac tissue and myocyte hypertrophy. Perhaps attempts to introduce CICR killing mutation may be more successful using a conditional knock-in approach.
