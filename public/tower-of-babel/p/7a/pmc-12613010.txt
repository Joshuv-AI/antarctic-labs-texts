Observation of type-II and type-III Dirac points in circuit lattices
Authors: Wencai Wang, Yubin Ma, Muhammad Imran, Bo Lv, Xueqing He, Yongtao Jia, Huibin Tao, Ying Liu, Rujiang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDirac fermions are low-energy quasi-particle excitations found in Dirac materials, with their band crossings referred to as Dirac points (DPs). Unlike type-I DPs, type-II, and type-III DPs have attracted significant attention due to their unique magneto-transport properties in condensed matter materials. However, type-II and type-III Dirac materials are notably rare and realizing DPs in macroscopic classical systems presents challenges because of the complex sample fabrication required for mirror symmetry. Here, we report the experimental observation of type-II and type-III DPs in circuit lattices. We confirm the presence of type-II DPs by observing strongly tilted Dirac cones. By varying the coupling capacitors, we demonstrate a transition from type-II DPs to type-III DPs, with the latter characterized by a flat band. Additionally, edge states emerge from the degeneracies associated with both type-II and type-III DPs. Our findings offer valuable insights into novel quantum phenomena in condensed matter materials, including anisotropic magneto-transport and topological phase transitions.

Introduction

Dirac fermions manifest as low energy quasi-particle excitations in Dirac materials, described by the massless Dirac equation and protected by the product of parity inversion symmetry (P) and time-reversal symmetry (T).1,2,3,4 Their band crossings, known as Dirac points (DPs), can be classified based on their Fermiology.5 Type-I DPs are characterized by point-like Fermi surfaces near the tips of linear Dirac cones. In contrast, type-II DPs manifest themselves as strongly tilted Dirac cones with open electron and hole pockets. Type-III DPs are the transition between type-I and type-II DPs. They have critically tilted cones with flat bands and their Fermi surfaces are single Dirac lines. In condensed matter physics, Dirac materials with different band topologies exhibit distinct magneto-transport properties. In contrast to the omnidirectional negative magneto-resistance (NMR) observed in materials with type-I DPs, the magneto-transport properties of materials with type-II DPs are highly anisotropic, with NMR expected only along specific momentum directions. Meanwhile, materials with type-III DPs display nearly omnidirectional NMR, with the exception of a critical direction. These characteristics provide exciting opportunities to explore novel quantum phenomena and topological phase transitions.6,7

In recent years, type-I DPs have been observed in a variety of materials,1,8,9,10 with graphene being the most notable example.11 In contrast, type-II and type-III DPs are much less common because of the challenge to synthesize materials whose constituent atoms are arranged in lattices with intricate electron hopping.5 Only recently, the existence of type-II DPs has been identified experimentally in PtTe2,6 PdTe2,12 PtSe2,13 and La1.77Sr0.23CuO4.14 To the best of our knowledge, type-III DPs have been predicted only theoretically in Zn2In2S5,7 with no experimental validation to date. Given the rareness of type-II and type-III Dirac materials, researchers have shifted their focus to macroscopic classical systems that also support Bloch modes. Type-II DPs have been reported in electromagnetics15 and acoustics,16 and type-III DPs have been reported in photonics.5 In all these systems, maintaining the underlying mirror symmetry to protect the degeneracy of DPs necessitates delicate sample fabrication and fine-tuning. Another approach to realizing type-II and type-III DPs is to utilize atomic systems, where honeycomb photonic lattices can be reconfigured instantaneously.17,18 However, this method requires precise tuning of the laser parameters.

Here, we propose the realization of two-dimensional (2D) type-II and type-III DPs in circuit lattices. The mirror symmetry is ensured through the use of commercially available coupling capacitors with low tolerances. Because of dimensionality, the 2D DPs correspond to 2-fold degeneracy in bands, instead of a 4-fold degeneracy point in the three-dimensional (3D) case.2,19 Utilizing direct voltage measurements to map out the band structures, we confirm the existence of a tilted band structure with two group velocities having the same sign near type-II DPs. By changing the coupling capacitors, we also confirm the existence of type-III DPs accompanied with a flat band. Based on the validation of these intriguing features, we further observe the edge states emerging from the type-II and type-III degeneracies, respectively. Compared to previous proposals, our approach is notably less challenging due to its flexible and controllable circuit connectivity, distance-independent hopping, and the use of commercially available circuit components.20,21,22,23,24,25,26,27 Our results may provide guidance for investigating novel quantum phenomena in condensed matter materials, such as anisotropic magneto-transport and topological phase transitions.

Results

Design of the circuit lattices

The general Dirac Hamiltonian describing a 2D type-II or type-III DP is H(k) = kiAijσj (i,j∈{0,1,2,3} and repeated indices summed), where k is the wave vector, A is a coefficient matrix, and σj are the unitary matrix with j = 0 and three Pauli matrices with j = 1,2,3.28 The energy is E±(k) = T(k)±U(k), where T(k) and U(k) are the kinetic and potential components, respectively. In momentum space, the potential component is symmetric with respect to the Fermi level E = 0. The kinetic component which is linearly dependent on k tilts the Dirac cone and breaks the Lorentz invariance. For a type-II DP, T(k) is dominant over U(k) for a particular direction. While for a type-III DP, T(k) equals to U(k) for a particular direction.

Our topological circuit that exhibits the type-II or type-III DPs is constructed by stacking the one-dimensional (1D) chains of LC resonator dimers.29,30,31,32,33 As depicted in Figure 1A, the blue and red nodes are wired to two types of grounded parallel LC resonators, respectively. The inductors L in all the resonators are identical, and the two types of grounding capacitors are denoted as CA and CB. The nearest nodes are wired by the coupling capacitors C1 in the x direction, and C2 and C3 in the y direction. For simplicity, the spacing between the nearest circuit nodes in both the x and y directions is defined as a. In this Hamiltonian, κx = C1/C is the hopping strength in the x direction, and κy = C2/C and νy = C3/C are the hopping strengths in the y direction. In the LC resonators, the grounded capacitors with two different values are CA = C+δC and CB = C-δC, respectively, with δC = δC/C. Here, the capacitor C is introduced for normalization purpose. We confirm that the Hamiltonian of this two-band system satisfies the symmetries of both P and T, since PH(k)P−1 = H(-k) and TH(k)T−1 = H(-k). The validity of these two symmetries ensures the existence of DPs. We define the normalized eigenfrequencies as(Equation 6)ω¯=1+2κx+κy+νy−ω02ω2,where ω is the eigenfrequency of the circuit lattice and ω0=1/LC. After diagonalizing the Hamiltonian, the normalized eigenfrequencies are(Equation 7)ω¯=d0±d12+d22+d32.

It is worth to note that a zero normalized eigenfrequency ω¯=0 plays the same role as the Fermi level E = 0 in condensed matter systems. And the corresponding actual frequency is the analogous Fermi level in this circuit system. The existence of type-II and type-III DPs at the analogous Fermi level requires that d1 = d2 = d3 = 0 and the band structure is linearly degenerate in momentum space.

From the circuit Hamiltonian and normalized eigenfrequencies, we find that there are band degeneracy points (BDPs) residing at the analogous Fermi level. Specifically, when -δC = κy-νy, there are four type-II or type-III DPs which are located at (±π2a,±π2a) in the first BZ, as shown in Figure 1B. The band structures at these type-II or type-III DPs are linear, which can be verified using the k·p model.37 Taking the DP located at (π2a,π2a) as an example, we can reduce the Hamiltonian in Equation 1 near the DP using Taylor expansion. This reduction yields the following parameters: d0 = −(κy+νy) (kya-π/2), d1 = −2κx(kxa-π/2), d2 = 0, and d3 = −(κy-νy) (kya-π/2). Considering Equation 7, we find that ω¯=∓2κx(kxa−π/2) when ky = π/2, and ω¯=−2κy(kya−π/2) or ω¯=−2νy(kya−π/2) when kx = π/2. Thus, the band structure exhibits linear dispersions in both the x and y directions. The group velocities are defined as vg=∂ω¯/∂kx,y. Using these definitions, we find that the group velocities in the x direction have opposite signs, while those in the y direction are both negative. The presence of two group velocities in the same direction is a characteristic of type-II DPs. Notably, if either κy or νy equals zero, one of the two bands will have a zero group velocity in the y direction, indicating a transition from the original type-II DPs to type-III DPs.

Figure 1C shows the band structure of the type-II DPs, where the circuit parameters are L = 1 mH, CA = 1 nF, CB = 1.3 nF, C1 = 220 pF, C2 = 330 pF, and C3 = 180 pF. From the magnified figure, the band structure at the DPs is strongly tilted in the ky direction. When changing C2 to 150 pF and disconnecting C3 from the circuit lattice (effectively setting it to zero), the type-II DPs transition to type-III DPs, which exhibit a flat band along the ky direction, as shown in Figure 1D. Note that although the linear dispersions are confirmed by examining the relationship between ω¯ and kx,y near the DPs located at ω¯=0, the actual frequency ω can be approximated from Equation 6 as(Equation 8)ω=ω0(1+2κx+κy+νy−ω¯2),which implies that the validity of the linear dispersions remains intact when examining the relationship between ω and kx,y.

To gain a more intuitive understanding of the construction of type-II and type-III DPs, we can also study the BDPs starting from a 1D chain. We first consider an isolated circuit chain in the x direction with identical grounded capacitors, where CA = CB. Since all the coupling capacitors in this chain are the same, there is a mirror symmetry in the x direction, denoted as Mx: = x→-x. Consequently, the two bands are linearly degenerate at the 1D BZ boundary at kx=±π2a, as illustrated in Figure 2A. Next, we stack identical chains along the y direction with C2 = C3. In the Hamiltonian, both d2 and d3 are equal to zero. The BDPs satisfy the condition cos(kxa) = 0, and they are independent of ky. As a result, the BDPs form a pair of lines along the ky direction at kx=±π2a, as shown in Figure 2B. To realize the isolated BDPs in the 2D BZ, we further break the line degeneracy by allowing C2≠C3. In this scenario, we obtain two BDPs in the ky direction, located at (±π2a,0), as depicted in Figure 2C. However, these BDPs are not type-II DPs since they exhibit quadratic dispersion in the ky direction. To shift the BDPs to positions with linear dispersions, we employ resonators with two different resonant frequencies by setting CA≠CB. The frequency of the BDPs is related to the grounding capacitors CA and CB. By adjusting these grounding capacitors, we can move the BDPs to positions that are above or below the analogous Fermi level. A special relation, CA-CB = −2(C2-C3), is used to ensure that the BDPs reside exactly at the analogous Fermi level. After applying this adjustment, the original two BDPs finally split into four points at (±π2a,±π2a) in the first BZ, as shown in Figure 2D. Furthermore, the capacitive coupling from the identical resonators in the y direction breaks Lorentz symmetry, leading to a strongly tilted band structure in the kx–ky plane. These BDPs are precisely the type-II DPs that we have realized. Notably, type-II DPs transition to type-III DPs when C3 = 0 while adjusting C2 according to the relation CA-CB = −2(C2-C3). It is important to note that type-I DPs cannot be realized in our circuit lattices, as the kinetic component T(k) is always larger than the potential component U(k) in the ky direction.Figure 2BDPs in momentum space(A) BDPs for a 1D circuit chain where CA = CB.(B) BDPs after stacking the 1D circuit chains with C2 = C3.(C) BDPs of the stacked structure when C2≠C3.(D) BDPs of the structure under the condition CA-CB = −2(C2-C3).

Observation of type-II and type-III DPs

To experimentally verify the existence of type-II and type-III DPs, we implement a circuit lattice using a printed circuit board (PCB) (see STAR Methods, sample fabrication and measurement). Figure 3A shows the fabricated circuit lattice sample, with an enlarged view of one unit cell presented in the inset. The circuit components are labeled for clarity. The number of unit cells in the x and y directions are Nx = 8 and Ny = 16, respectively. We connect the circuit nodes at one boundary to the nodes at the opposite boundary using cables to establish periodic boundary conditions in both the x and y directions. We then excite and probe the resonator nodes of the circuit lattice using a network analyzer. The bulk states are experimentally excited and probed through site-resolved measurements. From Figure 3B, sweeping kx at ky=π2a reveals one pair of BDPs at kx=±π2a. Similarly, from Figure 3C, sweeping ky at kx=π2a uncovers two degenerate points at ky=±π2a. These BDPs exhibit linear dispersion in both the kx and ky directions. Notably, in the ky direction, the band structure is strongly tilted, and the two group velocities are aligned in the same direction. This key physical signature indicates that type-II DPs are experimentally observed.

Under another set of circuit parameters with C2 = 150 pF and C3 = 0 pF, while keeping the other parameters fixed, the 1D slices in the kx and ky directions of the measured 2D band structure are shown in Figures 3D and 3E, respectively. The corresponding theoretical bands are indicated as white curves. The BDPs appear at the same positions in momentum space as those in Figures 3B and 3C, and they continue to exhibit linear dispersion in both the kx and ky directions. Notably, as illustrated in Figure 3E, a flat band appears along the ky direction, which serves as a key physical signature of type-III DPs.

Similar to the edge states found in graphene, which support type-I DPs,38,39,40,41,42 edge states also exist in lattices that host type-II or type-III DPs. Based on the circuit sample depicted in Figure 3A, we implement the open boundary condition in the x direction by connecting the boundary nodes to ground, while maintaining the periodic boundary condition in the y direction. We excite the resonator nodes from the two opposite boundaries along the y direction and reconstruct the band structure by averaging the voltage distributions obtained from the excitations at each boundary. The experimental band structure of the lattice hosting the type-II DPs is shown in Figure 4A. For comparison, the theoretical band structures are also plotted as white curves. Besides the bulk states (solid white curves), there are two dispersion bands that correspond to a pair of edge states (dashed white curves). To observe the edge state, as an example, we fix the excitation frequency at 108.8 kHz (denoted by the dashed green line in Figure 4A) and extract the voltage distribution along the x direction at the same row as the excitation port. As shown in Figure 4B, the experimental measurement result (red curve) agrees well with the theoretical data (blue curve). Discussion

In conclusion, we report the experimental observation of type-II and type-III DPs in circuit lattices. Unlike the intricate sample fabrication processes that require fine-tuning, the mirror symmetry in our circuit lattice is easily achieved using commercially available circuit components. Through the measurement of the band structures, we confirm the existence of type-II DPs, characterized by a strongly tilted band structure, and type-III DPs, which feature flat bands. Additionally, we observe edge states originating from type-II and type-III DPs, respectively. Our work provides valuable insights for exploring novel quantum phenomena in condensed matter materials, including anisotropic magneto-transport and topological phase transitions. We anticipate that more novel types of band degeneracies can be discovered in circuit lattices.43,44,45,46

Limitations of the study

Similar to the previously proposed artificial lattices,5,15,16 the circuit lattices implemented in our work also belong to a macroscopic classical system. Therefore, the quantum phenomena arising in condensed matter systems, such as magneto-transport, cannot be measured in our circuit lattice. Nevertheless, due to the periodicity of the circuit lattices, band structures can be defined, and the band degeneracies of type-II and type-III DPs are observed.

Sample fabrication and measurement

To fabricate the circuit lattice hosting type-II DPs, we utilize the following set of circuit elements: (1) inductors with L=1mH (Part No. 22R105C, ±1% tolerance, Q≈80 at 100kHz), and (2) capacitors with the following values: CA=1nF, CB=1.3nF, C1=220pF, C2=330pF, and C3=180pF (Murata GRM series, 0.6mm×0.3mm, ±1% tolerance). After measuring the related phenomena of type-II DPs, we desolder the original capacitors C2 and C3 to fabricate the circuit lattice for type-III DPs. We replace C2 with another value of 150pF and leave the bonding pads for C3 empty. Note that all of these circuit components are commercially available.

All resonator nodes of the circuit lattices are probed using a Network Analyzer HP 4195A with a frequency step of 10Hz, when the circuit is excited at two different types of resonators, respectively. This measurement is equivalent to assessing voltage distributions when the circuit is driven by a current source. The experimental resonant frequencies of the circuit lattice are validated through simulations conducted using Advanced Design System (ADS) software. Given that one or both directions of the circuit lattice have periodic boundaries, we obtain a discrete mesh in momentum space. To reconstruct the band structure, we apply a Fourier transform to the complex voltage distributions and establish a correspondence between the experimental resonant frequencies and discrete momentum values.
