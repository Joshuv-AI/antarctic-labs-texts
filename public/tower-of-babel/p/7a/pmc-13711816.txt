Trust is essential: implementing paediatric care coordination in rural Australia
Authors: Karen Hutchinson, Anneliese de Groot, Hayley Smithers-Sheedy, Raghu Lingam, Yvonne Zurynski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrust is essential to effective healthcare relationships with service users and a key mechanism for successful implementation of service improvements. In rural settings, systemic barriers, such as workforce shortages, fragmented services, and access inequities, can erode trust in the healthcare system. Integrated care models that prioritise relational approaches may help build trust, particularly for families of children with medical complexity (CMC) who frequently navigate multiple healthcare services.ObjectiveThis study explored how trust was operationalised between families of CMC and care coordinators in the Rural Kids Guided Personalised Service (RuralKidsGPS) model, and how relational and contextual factors shaped trust-building processes.MethodsSemi-structured interviews with parents (n = 13), family groups (n = 9), and Paediatric Care Coordinators (PCCs) (n = 8). A hybrid inductive/deductive thematic analysis guided by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), was used to evaluate the implementation barriers and enablers of RuralKidsGPS. Drawing on established theories, including the Trust Triangle and leveraging validated frameworks, we developed the Trust in Implementation Practice (TIP), comprising five relational constructs: Authenticity, Empathy, Competency, Vulnerability, and Reciprocity. These constructs guided analysis of trust-building mechanisms across families, PCCs, and the healthcare system.ResultsFactors enabling or undermining trust-building emerged as key themes from the CFIR-guided thematic analysis. Trust was fostered through PCCs’ relational competencies, authenticity, empathy, and local knowledge, enabling responsive care and sustained healthcare relationships. Trust dynamics were shaped by psychosocial and economic disadvantage, geographic isolation, systemic inequities in service access, and previous adverse healthcare experiences. PCCs were perceived as trustworthy intermediaries who promoted equity, continuity, and access to care. Thematic findings identified six key contextual modifiers, leading to expansion into the TIP-Context (TIP-C) framework.ConclusionTrust operates as both a mechanism and an outcome in the implementation of integrated care. The TIP-C framework offers a novel, theory-informed lens for understanding and operationalising trust-building in healthcare implementation. 1. This study helps advance implementation science by showing how relational competencies and local knowledge can mitigate systemic inequities and foster sustained healthcare relationships.

Background

Trust is fundamental to establishing and sustaining effective relationships in healthcare. Often, trust is described as ‘the glue that makes partnerships work’ [1]. Trust fosters collaboration among healthcare providers (HCPs), patients, and their families. Trust enables open communication, strengthens engagement, and improves healthcare experiences and outcomes [2, 3]. It also plays a critical role in enabling meaningful engagement in healthcare research [4]. However, trust-building is a complex and multifaceted process that requires time, effort, and a nuanced understanding of different stakeholders’ diverse attitudes, interests and priorities. This complexity is compounded by power imbalances, varying levels of vulnerability of service users, and the emotional and psychological investments involved in healthcare interactions and relationships [5].

Mayer et al. (1995) defined trust as ‘the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party’ [6–8]. This definition underscores that trust involves a conscious choice to be vulnerable, expressed through willingness to rely on others perceived as trustworthy, despite inherent risks [9]. In healthcare, trust influences every aspect of care delivery, operates at multiple levels, and encompasses interactions between individuals, organisations and systems [8, 10].

The development of trust is shaped by multiple factors, including individual attitudes, beliefs, experiences, systemic structures, available resources, and the capacity of healthcare organisations and networks to foster trust among HCPs, patients and families [10, 11]. Trust is fragile and may be eroded more easily when individuals have existing low trust, distrust, or mistrust toward healthcare systems and providers [4]. While distrust is often rooted in prior negative experiences, mistrust represents ‘a general sense of unease or suspicion’ [4, 10] towards services. Therefore, when services and HCPs demonstrate integrity and evidence of trustworthiness, trusting relationships are more likely to be built with patients and families [12]. High-quality relationships serve as a ‘critical factor for achieving implementation results’ and ‘to promote evidence use’, yet trust-building strategies are often overlooked [13].

Despite its significance, research on trust related to healthcare remains limited. Taylor et al. (2023) identified two key challenges: conceptual ambiguity in terms and theories, and difficulties observing the trust phenomena [10]. Implementation science theoretical frameworks [14, 15] rarely explicitly incorporate trust, with a notable exception of Metz et al. (2021, 2022), who called for conceptual clarity and research into how trust is both formed [16, 17] and changes over time [10, 17].

The intersection of trust, care delivery, and implementation of service innovations is particularly salient in the care of children with medical complexity (CMC) and their families, where trust underpins every aspect of service access and delivery [11, 12, 18]. CMC have at least one chronic medical condition and require frequent care from multiple healthcare services, HCPs and care teams [19, 20]. Significant family impacts arise from intensive caring responsibilities and challenges in navigating fragmented healthcare systems [20–22]. These challenges are amplified in rural locations with inequitable access to care, leading to poor health outcomes [23] and underscoring the urgent need for cohesive, integrated, and family-centred care models [24, 25]. When models of care overlook the diverse, individualised needs of CMC and their families and fail to account for family contextual factors, families may experience inappropriate, duplicated, or missed care [26]. This can erode trust, contributing to the ongoing development of distrust or mistrust of health services, which are essential to CMC’s well-being [4, 5].

The successful implementation of innovative, integrated, family-centred care models for CMC relies on strong partnerships and relationships with families, health services, HCPs and other interest-holders involved [26–28]. Trusting relationships between HCPs and families of CMC are central to family engagement to drive adoption of new care models, fostering commitment, and enabling sustained success [16, 27, 29–31]. These collaborations have led to improvements for families of CMC in service navigation, care accessibility, quality, continuity, and satisfaction, whilst also enhancing care connectedness and reducing overall health service utilisation [24, 27, 28, 32, 33].

Albers et al. (2022) highlighted how trust functions as a key implementation strategy, optimising implementation efforts and moderating health outcomes [31]. For trust to serve as an effective implementation driver, it must be built across multiple system levels [10, 29]. Yet, there remains a gap in the literature on how trust is understood, measured, operationalised and built to support implementation [10, 16].

This study explores the strategies and interpersonal attributes of paediatric care coordinators (PCCs) that foster trust, the contextual factors that influence these efforts, and how families of CMC experience and interpret trust, leveraging the RuralKidsGPS model of paediatric care coordination as a case study. By positioning trust as both a mechanism and an outcome, this study aims to explore how trust operates within the implementation of the Rural Kids Guided Personalised Service (RuralKidsGPS), a care coordination model for CMC, and how relational and contextual factors shape trust-building processes.

Methods

Study design

This study is based on the published protocol guiding the implementation and evaluation of RuralKidsGPS [34]. This case-study centres on a theory-informed inductive and deductive thematic qualitative analysis [35, 36] of semi-structured interviews conducted with all PCCs and CMC families who had engaged with RuralKidsGPS, six to 12 months after initial implementation [34].

Intervention and context

RuralKidsGPS is a co-designed, innovative model of integrated care for CMC that relies on PCCs as key workers who collaborate closely with CMC and families to facilitate care coordination [34]. It was based on the KidsGPS model, which had been successfully implemented and sustained in a metropolitan setting across the Sydney Children’s Hospitals Network in New South Wales (NSW), Australia [22, 34]. Both models incorporated the ‘Circle of Coordination’ [21], ensuring the needs of CMC and their families were central in the healthcare system to support integrated care [28, 34].

Australia operates under a federated governance system, with separate health systems across the states and territories, in addition to national-level services. Families living near state or territory borders can face cross-jurisdictional barriers to specialised care, with their nearest tertiary children’s hospital located in a neighbouring state (Table 1). RuralKidsGPS was implemented in four rural local health districts (LHDs) across NSW: Murrumbidgee, Southern NSW, Northern NSW and Western NSW [37]. To provide context, the Western NSW LHD covers an area comparable in size to Germany.

Each LHD employed a part-time PCC at 0.5 full-time-equivalent (FTE) to enhance the capacity to meet demand for managing multiple CMC with unique and significant healthcare needs closer to home. These roles were funded through a research project to implement and evaluate RuralKidsGPS. However, the four LHDs varied in their pre-existing care coordination capacity, for example, in one LHD, a permanent 0.6 FTE PCC had been in place for several years, with the RuralKidsGPS PCC role additional to this. In two other LHDs, local funding temporarily enhanced the RuralKidsGPS PCC’s hours to 0.6 FTE and 1 FTE, respectively, at different points during the project, while in one LHD, the 0.5FTE position was not enhanced.

PCCs were central to the implementation and delivery of RuralKidsGPS, assisting families navigating fragmented care and support systems, fostering self-management capacity, and coordinating care across tertiary children’s specialist services, primary and community care, local hospitals, and disability services. Due to the large geographical areas serviced and long travel distances, PCCs interacted with families and health and social care providers primarily via telehealth (phone or videoconferencing), with occasional face-to-face contact in healthcare facilities.

Recruitment

As described in the published protocol [34], purposive recruitment of families of CMC accessing RuralKidsGPS was conducted. A research assistant embedded in each LHD provided families with written and verbal information about the study. CMC and siblings aged seven years or older could participate in a joint family interview or have their parents present for support during the interview, provided they could comprehend the research purpose, communicate verbally or using augmentative and alternative communication, and make an informed decision about participation [38]. Similarly, PCCs were invited to participate and provided with study information. Participation was voluntary for both families and PCCs, who could withdraw at any time without any consequences. All participants provided written consent before participating in interviews.

Data collection

Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted with PCCs and parents. Joint family interviews were also conducted, according to family capacity and preferences, with various participants, including one or more parents, CMC, and/or siblings. All interviews with CMC or siblings as the primary participant included at least one parent for support as needed. Separate interview guides were developed for CMC, families and PCCs (see Appendix 1), drawing on the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [39, 40]. The interviews primarily explored participants’ experiences and perspectives during the implementation of RuralKidsGPS.

Interviews were scheduled at the participants’ convenience and conducted via Microsoft Teams, Zoom or in-person. Most in-person interviews were conducted in local LHD facilities, with one conducted at a parent’s workplace. Interviews, lasting between 45 and 60 min, were audio-recorded via videoconferencing platforms or a digital audio-recorder when conducted in-person. The interviews with families and CMC were conducted by a researcher with experience in qualitative interviewing (KH) and a doctoral student (AdG) who received interview technique training from the research team. Interviews with PCCs were conducted by senior qualitative researchers (YZ, KH). A professional transcription service transcribed all interview recordings [https://daats.com.au]. Researchers (KH, AdG) deidentified transcripts and assigned participant codes: P (parent), PCC (paediatric care coordinator), C (CMC), S (Sibling) or Fam (family group).

Analysis

We undertook a phased analytic process comprising three sequential steps, with each step informing the next. Firstly, we conducted a thematic analysis of experiences, barriers, and enablers, guided by the CFIR. Insights from this stage identified trust as a central, cross-cutting theme, prompting the development of a theory-informed framework of validated trust constructs (the Trust in Implementation Practice (TIP) framework, Fig. 1). We then returned to the coded dataset and applied a framework analysis approach [41], organising themes according to the TIP constructs and identifying contextual factors shaping the experiences of PCCs, CMCs, and families.

Thematic analysis of experiences, barriers and enablers guided by the CFIR

We conducted an initial hybrid deductive–inductive analysis informed by the CFIR. Three authors (KH, YZ and AdG) read the transcripts to familiarise themselves with the data; the first author (KH) identified candidate codes, which were discussed and refined with two co-authors (YZ and AdG). We then developed a codebook based on CFIR constructs and deductively coded all transcripts, while allowing inductive codes to capture concepts not represented in the CFIR. We did not restrict analysis to a pre-defined subset of CFIR constructs [39], all constructs were available for use, and their application was grounded in and guided by the data. The coding was performed by one author (KH) and discussed and refined with two co-authors (YZ, AdG). This approach generated both CFIR-aligned insights and a set of recurrent, cross-cutting themes that extended beyond the CFIR framework.

2. Developing the Trust in Implementation Practice (TIP): a theory-informed analytical framework to explore the role of trust in RuralKidsGPS implementation

Developing the Trust in Implementation Practice (TIP): a theory-informed analytical framework to explore the role of trust in RuralKidsGPS implementation

From the analysis conducted in step 1, trust emerged as a consistent and influential cross-cutting theme shaping experiences and relationships of PCCs, CMCs and their families during the RuralKidsGPS implementation. To develop a deeper understanding of the role of trust and factors that enabled trust building, as well as those that created barriers by undermining trust, a theory-informed framework was developed to guide the next phase of analysis. Together with co-authors (AdG and YZ), KH leveraged existing frameworks and theories to develop the Trust in Implementation Practice (TIP) framework.

The TIP draws on established trust theory and its application in real-world contexts, particularly the ‘Trust Triangle’ described by Frei and Morriss [42], Brene Browne’s work on trust [43], the Center for Implementation’s ‘Trust Trifecta’ [44], and the theoretical model connecting trust and implementation outcomes, developed by Metz et al. (2022) [16, 45]. The authors (KH, YZ, AdG) were guided by these theoretical foundations and the conceptual framework development principles described by Imenda (2014) [46] and Varpio et al. (2021) [36], to develop the TIP (Fig. 1).

The TIP includes five core constructs: Authenticity, Empathy, and Competency, adapted from the ‘Trust Triangle’ [42, 47], the ‘Trust Trifecta’ [44] and the theoretical model by Metz et al. (2022) [16] and Vulnerability and Reciprocity, identified in the literature as essential to trust-building [4, 48, 49]. The constructs of Authenticity, Empathy, and Competency are considered drivers of trust and mutually reinforcing, though their relative importance may vary across care contexts, cultural backgrounds and/or personal experiences [9, 16, 18, 50]. Vulnerability and Reciprocity are consistently recognised as foundational for building trust, fostering mutual cooperation, collective benefit, and relational openness [4, 44, 48, 50–52].

3. Applying the TIP to explore the role of trust in RuralKidsGPS implementation

Applying the TIP to explore the role of trust in RuralKidsGPS implementation

The five constructs of the TIP provided a structured analytical lens for interpreting the emerging themes identified during the thematic analysis undertaken in step 1. The subsequent qualitative analysis [35, 41] focused on trust, trust-building strategies and trusting relationships as integral constructs shaping interactions among PCCs, families, and the broader health system. The first author, KH, undertook a targeted review of the coded transcripts, with a specific focus on trust-related themes and contextual influences [46]. The emerging themes were cross-checked by co-authors (YZ and AdG), and themes were iteratively discussed and refined by three authors (KH, YZ, AdG). The themes were organised according to the TIP in keeping with the framework analysis approach [41] Additional inductively identified themes not directly aligned with the TIP but having an influence on trust, were described.

Results

Interview participants included eight PCCs, who worked in RuralKidsGPS during the study period, 13 parents (12 mothers and one father), and nine family groups, which collectively included mothers (n = 9), fathers (n = 4), CMC (11–15 years) (n = 4) and siblings (10–18 years) (n = 3). Each interview lasted on average 60 min, with group interviews lasting slightly longer (75–90 min).

The results are presented in two parts. The first part outlines the thematic analysis of experiences, relationships, barriers, and enablers, guided by the CFIR and aligned with step 1 of the methods. The second part presents the framework analysis of emerging concepts informed by the TIP framework, as described in steps 2 and 3 of the methods.

b. Contextual factors influencing implementation

Contextual factors influencing implementation

Understanding the contextual factors that influenced the implementation, specifically the engagement with and adoption of RuralKidsGPS, emerged as important. Six influential contextual factors were identified in the CFIR analysis: System structure, Culture, Rurality, Capacity, Networks, and Local resources. Rurality was an overarching context that shaped all aspects of care delivery, system design, and implementation. TIP provided a framework to interpret the two overarching themes that shaped how PCCs and families experienced trust during the implementation and delivery of RuralKidsGPS: (1) Factors enabling trust-building, (2) Factors undermining trust-building.

Theme 1: Factors enabling trust-building

Five sub-themes emerged under Theme 1 (Fig. 2) as key contributors to trust-building during the implementation and delivery of RuralKidsGPS.

Sub-themes underpinning theme 1, trust building, and trust constructs

These sub-themes reflect relational mechanisms central to the TIP framework (Fig. 1). PCCs and families built meaningful relationships grounded in interpersonal trust and shared responsibility, strengthening family engagement with RuralKidsGPS and the broader healthcare system and contributing to more positive experiences, as illustrated in Table 3.

Subtheme 1.1: Paediatric care coordinators understand the family’s circumstances

PCCs, embedded within the LHDs accessed by families of CMC, brought first-hand knowledge of local healthcare challenges and resources. This contextual embeddedness fostered trust through empathy, respect, cultural sensitivity, and alignment with family values. Authentic communication created safe spaces where families felt heard: ‘I’m not an annoyance… I genuinely feel like she cares about [child]’ (P13).

Families valued tailored support that reduced disruption to family life and administrative burden: ‘[PCC] understands where you’re coming from… just have some normal [family] time which was beyond valuable’(P2). This relational understanding enabled families to feel seen and supported, thereby reinforcing trust.

Empathy and authenticity were consistently described as central to meaningful engagement with PCCs: ’She’s [PCC] really interested in genuinely trying to help us, which is awesome’ (Fam2). Being offered support by the PCC at a time of heightened stress offered tangible relief: ‘I felt like I could drop that couple of balls [let a few things go]. Feels nice’ (Fam3). These examples illustrate how trust in the PCCs’ responsive, context-aware practices played a critical role in alleviating parental strain and supporting RuralKidsGPS engagement (Fig. 1).

Subtheme 1.2: Paediatric care coordinators demonstrate competency in assisting families

Families consistently identified PCCs’ expertise, decisiveness, and advocacy as central to trust-building. PCCs’ coordination of clinical services, holistic oversight, and logistical problem-solving were demonstrated through context-responsive practices and competencies, which were key elements of the TIP (Fig. 1). Families described PCCs as the ‘puppet master’ and ‘across the whole picture [of child and family needs]’ (P2).

Their authenticity and empathy reassured families: ‘When it all starts going pear-shaped [wrong], I’ve got someone [PCC] to call’ (Fam3). Confidence in PCCs’ system navigation was strong: ‘A fairy godmother [PCC] that comes in and just makes everything work’ (P2). However, families often tested PCCs’ problem-solving capacity through complex requests, such as aligning multiple specialist appointments to reduce family burden and costs. PCCs’ competency was determined by their ability to source medical information and develop personalised care plans, fostering trust by enhancing transparency and reciprocity among families, care providers, and PCCs. Care plans were grounded in mutually agreed goals and shared information integrated into electronic medical records (EMR) where possible, reinforcing shared accountability.

Subtheme 1.3: Cooperative expectations within care relationships

Trust in RuralKidsGPS was reinforced through mutual cooperation and shared decision-making among PCCs and CMC families. Consistent with TIP (Fig. 1), PCCs demonstrated transparent communication and actively involved families in care decisions, thereby fostering collaborative care pathways and avoiding unilateral control. One parent highlighted that some HCPs ‘…try and over control the situation, which makes it a lot worse too. PCC, let me have so much input into how things go as well. She hasn’t just taken the reins [control] and said, this is what you do, this is how we do it’ (P16). This demonstrated that relational accountability, and shared power and shared responsibility are foundational aspects of trust supporting confidence, experiences and timely access to needed services.

Subtheme 1.4: Parental peer advocacy for RuralKidsGPS driving engagement and equity

Families built strong peer networks, sharing lived experiences and trusted information to navigate services. These informal relational networks built through conversations, social media, and support groups were vital in connecting new CMC families to RuralKidsGPS: ‘You know you [families with CMC] can access it [RuralKidsGPS]? That’s what we [parents] do, otherwise people miss out’ (P13).

Parents advocated for other families with CMC, promoting equitable care and encouraging RuralKidsGPS uptake based on their positive experiences and trust in PCCs. These relational networks were central in sustaining and expanding RuralKidsGPS access.

Subtheme 1.5: Strong relational ties enabling family advocacy

Advocacy was central to holistic, responsive care for CMC, with trust enabling both PCCs and families to speak up, share vulnerabilities, and take action. This reflected accountability and shared power as shown in TIP (Fig. 1). PCCs often escalated concerns to clinicians or services when needed: ‘I [PCC] had to escalate [family need] and appeal’ (PCC4), a critical role in gaining access to care in resource-limited rural settings.

Clear, authentic guidance from PCCs built parental confidence: ‘She told us what was what and what we had to do’ (Fam5). Trust in PCCs was profound: ‘Complete trust… more than anybody at any hospital’ (P16). This level of trust extended to lobbying for the sustainment of PCC roles beyond the research project: ‘Why should we have to fight for that?’ (P17).

Reciprocal advocacy strengthened PCCs’ role legitimacy within the health system, with families helping build trust among specialist teams: ‘…to relinquish some trust and distribute the load’ (P2). PCCs also supported system-level change, including helping families lodge complaints to improve care: ‘She helped me lodge a complaint… to activate some changes’ (P17).

The quote in Box 1 demonstrates how trust was tested and built during a period of parental vulnerability. The PCC’s focused, empathetic care coordination supported the parent through mental health challenges, demonstrating professional competence, relational depth, and mutual commitment, key elements of trust-building consistent with the TIP (Figure 1).

Theme 2: Factors undermining trust-building

Theme 2 recognises the fragility of trust in healthcare relationships and its shaping by lived experience. Four interrelated sub-themes (Fig. 3) were identified as undermining trust building in the RuralKidsGPS context:

Sub-themes underpinning Theme 2

Within the TIP framework, the interplay between trust constructs and the six contextual factors was evident, including systemic and interpersonal limitations. When these issues remained unaddressed, trust-building efforts and meaningful engagement with RuralKidsGPS and other services were undermined, as illustrated in Table 5.

Subtheme 2.1: Past negative healthcare experiences

Families of CMC frequently described prior healthcare encounters as frustrating, traumatic, and isolating, eroding trust in HCPs and services, and shaping initial scepticism toward RuralKidsGPS. Many reported poor health system responsiveness, requiring relentless advocacy: ‘I don’t think the health department realise how closed those doors are’ (P13).

Parents often assumed sole responsibility for care coordination, ‘hold[ing] the nucleus [control of care]’ (P9). Some attributed experiences resulted in ‘post-traumatic stress disorder’ (P9), citing superficial, dismissive care, like a ‘band aid’ (P13), that provided temporary solutions without addressing the larger underlying problems. One parent described the need for ‘fighting really hard’ (P17) with local ED [emergency department] staff to secure essential care for their CMC, amid highly emotional and stressful circumstances.

These accounts reflect systemic failures, complexity, and the resulting persistent distrust in the health system. To foster meaningful engagement in RuralKidsGPS, PCCs worked to rebuild trust by ensuring they understood family priorities and contexts. Through authenticity, empathy, and demonstrated competency, PCCs enacted trust-building mechanisms illustrated in Fig. 2, providing person- and family-centred care coordination. Over time, PCCs became the families ‘trust companion’ [9] within the healthcare system.

Subtheme 2.2: Health system fragmentation and information gaps

Families of CMC described the health system as fragmented and difficult to navigate, eroding trust in care coordination and collaborative efforts. A breakdown in communication between HCPs and families left some feeling excluded from decisions and unsupported. A mother reflected on her CMC being discharged back home after an unplanned tertiary hospital admission: ‘Nobody [locally] has touched base with me at all’ (P5).

Before RuralKidsGPS, inconsistent documentation and delayed sharing of medical information across specialist and local care teams resulted in outdated or conflicting multiple care plans, undermining care continuity and confidence: ‘They [HCPs] don’t know which one [care plan] is the most recent…then they [HCPs] just don’t take it seriously’ (PCC3). Despite PCCs’ dedication and efforts to consolidate care information, jurisdictional barriers when accessing care across state or territory borders, compounded care fragmentation, sometimes described as ‘a nightmare’ (PCC5). PCCs were at times bypassed by other HCPs who did not understand their coordinator role, leaving families burdened with unnecessary phone calls for administrative reasons, ‘[HCPs] still calling them [families]’ (PCC3).

Unclear referral pathways and a lack of integrated EMR systems often led to care duplication and disrupted coordination, which weakened family trust in both the system and PCCs’ roles. PCCs reported their frustration with the system as ‘sometimes you can’t coordinate care’ (PCC3). In rural areas, some families felt particularly isolated and excluded from health care: ‘All the doors shut… it’s very hard’ (P14). This is reflected in TIP (Fig. 1), which emphasises that trust needs both interpersonal accountability and system responsiveness.

Subtheme 2.3: Unclear paediatric care coordinators’ role

Some families initially struggled to understand the purpose and scope of the PCCs’ role, leading to hesitation and limited engagement with RuralKidsGPS. Some parents were sceptical about the PCCs role capacity: ‘I [parent] don’t even know what to do with you [said to a PCC]’ (Fam3), and others questioned ‘Can I trust it [RuralKidsGPS]?’ (P8).

PCCs were sometimes perceived as an added burden, ‘just another person I [parent] had to answer to’ (P3), and there was a ‘fear of [PCC] making more work’ (P6). Without clear orientation to RuralKidsGPS, some families continued to lead care coordination: ‘…to make sure it gets done properly’ (P8).

Family engagement improved when PCCs were introduced by trusted HCPs or peers, or during crisis situations: ‘PCC [name] is propping me [and family] up’ (P17), and PCCs were described as ‘lifesavers’ (Fam5). Over time, trust and reciprocity developed through families’ personal experiences of PCCs’ authentic, empathetic care, and demonstrated competence in responding to family needs.

Subtheme 2.4: Paediatric care coordinators’ role complexity and constrained capacity

PCCs faced significant strain due to the high care demands of CMC, rural service gaps, and systemic fragmentation, which undermined responsiveness and eroded trust in RuralKidsGPS. The expectation to support ‘any medically complex child… with any medical issue’ (PCC4) stretched PCCs beyond their scope of practice, especially in areas with limited-service availability. The absence of local social workers further compounded role complexity, with PCCs providing social support in addition to care coordination.

Funding of RuralKidsGPS through a time-limited research grant created uncertainty, threatened service sustainability and stakeholder engagement, contributed to PCC turnover (resignations), disrupted care continuity and relational trust. Both PCCs and families acknowledged the challenge of balancing research demands with implementing a new service for a high-needs population; ‘I [PCC] probably underestimated the involvement of the research project’ (PCC4).

These pressures constrain the mechanisms by which trust is built and sustained (Fig. 2), underscoring the need for secure funding and supportive infrastructure to ensure consistent, trusted care in high‑need rural contexts.

3. Proposing the Trust in Implementation Practice – Context (TIP-C)

Proposing the Trust in Implementation Practice – Context (TIP-C)

The analysis identified six contextual factors described in Table 5 that acted as implementation modifiers influencing how the TIP constructs were operationalised in practice. These contextual factors were subsequently integrated into TIP, producing the Trust in Implementation Practice - Context (TIP-C) framework (Fig. 4). By explicitly incorporating contextual influences, TIP-C addresses the conceptual gap between relational dimensions of trust and contextual modifying factors, providing a more comprehensive understanding of how trust operates within specific implementation settings, such as RuralKidsGPS.

The Trust in Implementation Practice-Context (TIP-C) framework

Discussion

Trust is widely recognised as a cornerstone of effective, high-quality care, and, in this study, emerged as central to the implementation of the RuralKidsGPS care coordination model [3, 10, 26]. Trust functioned as both the catalyst for change and the engine for sustainability [11, 48, 50–52]. Our findings align with Metz et al.’s (2022) framework, which conceptualises trust-building as an iterative, relational process shaped by competence, reliability, communication, and integrity. We extend this work by developing the TIP, which shows how trust operates in rural paediatric care coordination, where it is shaped not only by interpersonal relationships but also by geography, service fragmentation, and the unique role of PCCs. The TIP-C framework (Fig. 4), an extension of TIP, offers a more structured lens for examining contextual influences and their effects on trust-building in implementation practice [16, 54].

Relational trust drivers: competency, empathy and authenticity

The TIP framework (Fig. 1) highlighted three interdependent drivers of trust in RuralKidsGPS implementation: competency, empathy, and authenticity. PCCs demonstrated competency through delivering person-and family-centred care, through skilled navigation of complex and fragmented health and social care systems, providing practical problem-solving and supporting reliable continuity of care within and across sectors [18, 55–57]. PCCs’ ability to address power imbalances and recognise families as experts in their own experience positioned them as reliable advocates and facilitators of informed shared decision-making [18, 31, 58].

Authenticity was expressed through honest, transparent and reciprocal relationships, while empathy was especially valued by families, and often shaped whether trust was sustained or eroded [4, 47, 48]. Families consistently prioritised empathy as a critical enabler of trust, aligning with Metz et al. (2020) [13]. When families perceived empathy was lacking, trust eroded, undermining perceptions of PCC authenticity and competence, aligning with previous research [4, 47, 48]. However, by acknowledging family vulnerability without judgment, PCCs fostered agency by responding genuinely, which deepened relational trust [18, 59, 60]. However, early in the implementation process, families often tested trust through low-risk interactions to gauge PCCs’ capacity to deliver practical benefits [48], a pattern that resonates with other research [56, 59].

RuralKidsGPS was viewed as a trust-driven model, with benefits extending beyond PCCs to broader trust in other healthcare engagement [4, 61]. Yet sustaining trust required ongoing transparency, as rebuilding it after erosion was time-consuming and often complex [4]. This study contributes real-world evidence to implementation research, reinforcing the need to explicitly consider and build trust [16, 58].

Contextual influences and the TIP-C framework

This study reinforced that trust is context-dependent and shaped by dynamic interactions between PCCs, families, and the health system [48, 61, 62]. Rurality influenced every aspect of service delivery with system-level barriers, such as workforce shortages, fragmented services and limited access to services, undermining trust in the broader health system. In this context, PCCs often acted as trusted intermediaries [22, 27], who helped families navigate care, maintain continuity, and access support [48, 61, 62]. Empathy emerging as a critical driver of family engagement with RuralKidsGPS, consistent with previous studies [2, 4, 62, 63].

These contextual influences showed that TIP (Fig. 1), as a theory-driven analytic lens on trust, did not fully capture how trust operated in practice. Integrating the contextual influences identified in the CFIR analysis into the proposed TIP-C (Fig. 4) provides a clearer understanding of the links between relational trust behaviours and how context-modifying factors can shape the formation and erosion of trust. However, the applicability and transferability of TIP-C (Fig. 4) framework requires further testing in future implementation studies of different models of care.

Practical considerations for implementation: Barriers, enablers, and strategies for trust building

Implementing RuralKidsGPS alongside a research program revealed tensions between service delivery, evaluation and sustainability. PCCs balanced immediate family needs with research demands, while maintaining relational integrity [64]. The time-limited funding for RuralKidsGPS introduced uncertainty, undermining confidence in the service’s long-term future, even when families valued the service and trusted the PCCs. However, families’ active involvement in the research amplified their voices and reinforced the legitimacy of RuralKidsGPS as a valuable service, highlighting both the power and fragility of trust in time-limited models [13, 31].

Authentic, adaptive, and co-designed interventions that respond to real-world needs have been shown to translate implementation research evidence into practice, supporting healthcare equity, accessibility, and sustainability [54, 58, 65]. These features are particularly important in rural and cross-sector contexts where models of care must be shaped through ongoing engagement with patients and families, frontline staff, and the broader health system.

Our findings underscore that co-design, transparent communication, and LHD-led networks supported local adaptation and fostered early relational trust by demonstrating responsiveness to community priorities and service realities [16, 66]. However, the absence of a clearly articulated sustainability pathway prior to implementation, such as long-term resourcing, PCC role security and system-level commitments, created uncertainty about the model’s future. This uncertainty undermined trust in RuralKidsGPS among families, PCCs, and other HCPS, suggesting that authenticity and co-design alone are insufficient without parallel planning for continuity, stability and long-term system integration. Although all LHDs committed ongoing funding for the service beyond the project period, this was viewed as a short-term solution, with varying durations between LHDs.

Trust-building practices and strategies

PCCs fostered trust-building through authentic engagement with families, grounding care in lived experience and rural realities. Key trust-building practices included: (1) Empathy and person-centred engagement, promoted respect and emotional safety [63, 67, 68]; (2) Transparent communication, clarified PCCs’ roles, and supported family agency [51, 59]; (3) Honesty and reliability, strengthened perceptions of authenticity and trustworthiness [48]; and (4) Local presence and continuity, with PCCs often described as the main point of contact [26] and “someone on the inside” [48] of the healthcare system, providing reassurance and navigation [22].

PCCs thus played a central role not only in implementing RuralKidsGPS but also in rebuilding trust and engagement with the broader health system. Through advocacy and sustained relationship-building, they promoted equity, accessibility, and family agency for socially disadvantaged families [52, 65, 69]. Consistent with Metz et al. (2022), these findings suggest that trust should be recognised as a deliberate implementation strategy because of its impact on implementation processes and outcomes [14, 58]. Yet, implementing care coordination models for CMC remains challenging, given the multiple care components and fragmented healthcare systems [28, 64].

Adopting the Circle of Coordination framework [21, 34] and co-designing RuralKidsGPS with families helped ensure the model reflected lived experience and adapted to context [18, 22, 70]. Similar to other complex care contexts, some families described PCCs as essential ‘trust companions’ [9]. Open dialogue and shared decision-making supported family engagement in CMC care planning and advocacy for system improvements aimed at reducing disparities [63, 65]. Informal peer networks further supported trust-building and information sharing [69, 71].

Understanding the drivers of trust and context-responsive trust-building strategies is essential for strengthening relationships and promoting equitable care delivery [4, 9, 54]. Trust in individual healthcare providers can extend to trust in health organisations and systems, improving engagement, effectiveness, and shared decision-making in healthcare [4, 10, 72]. However, over-reliance on interpersonal trust [8, 16] may obscure structural issues, reinforce inequities and shift accountability from healthcare systems to individual healthcare providers [4, 9, 54].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength is the development of the TIP framework (Fig. 1), and its contextual extension, TIP-C (Fig. 4), which complement and extend existing implementation trust models through a theory‑ and data‑informed approach to examining trust in implementation practice. TIP-C offers a more contextually grounded and relationally focused understanding of trust, helping to pinpoint where trust breaks down and highlights specific practices that can strengthen trust within complex service environments. Embedding the evaluation within implementation also enabled insights into real-world relational dynamics and trust‑building.

Limitations include the absence of other implementation stakeholders, such as HCPs, health managers, and embedded researchers, narrowing the scope of trust‑related insights. Trust also emerged inductively rather than as a pre‑specified analytic focus, limiting exploration across system levels. Contextual differences across the four RuralKidsGPS sites were not examined due to small site‑level samples or the potential influence of pre-existing factors, including the maturity of coordination services. Future implementation research could explicitly apply and adapt the TIP‑C framework across diverse contexts, enabling multilevel and multi‑centre analysis of trust within implementation processes.

Conclusion

This study reinforces trust as a moderating factor in healthcare implementation and as a bridge between research and practice. Within the RuralKidsGPS context, trust was foundational to family engagement and care coordination, while also shaping service adoption, delivery, and sustainability. Building on the TIP framework, we developed the TIP-C framework, integrating the contextual factors identified in this study to better capture how trust is shaped within implementation settings. Creating the TIP-C highlights the importance of understanding context and embedding trust-building principles, competency, authenticity, and empathy into service co-design and implementation. These relational practices foster meaningful relationships, strengthen service engagement and support responsive care. For families, they provide emotional reassurance and a trusted companion through complex health systems, particularly in rural and under-resourced settings. However, sustaining these practices requires alignment with long-term research, policy, and funding structures. Ultimately, recognising trust as a deliberate implementation strategy may strengthen the success and sustainability of innovative models, such as RuralKidsGPS, by supporting context-responsive care, reducing disparities, and advancing equity-driven, sustainable, integrated healthcare systems.
