Anatomic Medial Collateral Ligament, Posterior Oblique Ligament, and Double-Bundle Posterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstructions With Concomitant Distal Femoral Osteotomy in the Setting of a Chronic Injury
Authors: Rylan A Behnke, Kaitlyn H Smith, Dustin R Lee, Matthew T Rasmussen, Benjiman J Wilebski, Luke V Tollefson, Robert F LaPrade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe medial collateral ligament provides stability against valgus stress applied to the knee. Medial knee ligament injuries often occur concomitantly with posterior cruciate ligament injuries and may be aggravated by valgus malalignment. Intentional care and preparation must be taken to reconstruct all injured structures to their anatomic positions to best restore native knee biomechanics. This article describes an anatomic medial collateral ligament and posterior oblique ligament reconstruction using hamstring autograft, with a distal femoral osteotomy and a concomitant double-bundle posterior cruciate ligament reconstruction using Achilles tendon and tibialis anterior allografts for the anterolateral bundle and posteromedial bundle, respectively, in the setting of chronic ligament injuries.

Medial knee injuries are common in the United States, with an incidence rate of 0.24 per 1,000 individuals.1 Primary stabilizers of the medial knee include the superficial medial collateral ligament (sMCL), posterior oblique ligament (POL), and deep medial collateral ligament (dMCL).2,3 The sMCL stabilizes the knee against valgus stresses across all flexion angles and acts as a secondary stabilizer for external and internal rotation.2 The POL further contributes to medial knee stabilization and serves as a primary stabilizer for internal rotation.2,4

Injuries to the medial knee commonly result from valgus forces at near full knee extension.1 Valgus malalignment may also accentuate chronic sMCL and POL injuries or increase the risk of graft failure after reconstruction.5 Additionally, medial knee injuries are common with a posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) tear.6 In the setting of chronic sMCL, POL, and PCL injuries with valgus alignment, a distal femoral osteotomy (DFO) is indicated to correct the valgus alignment and optimize medial ligament healing.7 This technical note describes a reproducible, anatomic surgical technique for addressing chronic posteromedial instability with coronal valgus malalignment.

Patient Evaluation

Preoperatively, a thorough history should be obtained and a comprehensive clinical examination should be performed. Additionally, stress radiographs, long-leg alignment radiographs, and magnetic resonance imaging should be obtained to establish an objective diagnosis for the PCL (Fig 1), MCL, and POL (Fig 2) tears and to establish recommended correction sizes for coronal malalignment (Figs 3 and 4).

Coronal alignment is measured by drawing a line from the center of the femoral head to the center of the patient's talus (yellow line). A patient that is in valgus mechanical alignment displays a line that crosses lateral to the apex of the lateral tibial eminence. Bilateral valgus coronal malalignment is observed in this case.

Zoomed-in view of the left knee from a long-leg standing radiograph with measurements to determine the desired angle of correction for a valgus-correcting distal femoral osteotomy. To determine the correction size, two lines are drawn, one from the center of the femoral head through the apex of the medial eminence of the tibial plateau (white arrow) and the other from the center of the talus to an intersection point with the first line at the apex of the medial tibial eminence (red arrow). The angle created by these two lines (4.5°) should then be transposed to the lateral aspect of the femur. The surgeon must be sure to keep a 1-cm cortical hinge medially. A non-ossifying fibroma is viewed on the distal-medial femur in this case and will be taken into consideration when performing the distal femoral osteotomy.

Anesthesia and Positioning

The patient is positioned in the supine position and induced under general anesthesia. A knee examination is performed to validate the clinical examination findings. The surgical leg is placed in a leg holder (Mizho OSI, Union City, CA) with a high-thigh tourniquet proximally, and the nonsurgical leg is placed into an abduction stirrup (Birkova Product, Gothenburg, NE). For infection prophylaxis, 2 g of perioperative cefazolin is administered if no allergy is documented.

DFO Site Preparation

The DFO is first approached by making a 10-cm incision along the mid aspect of the iliotibial band, ending just proximal to the Gerdy tubercle (Fig 5). After dissection down to the superficial layer of the iliotibial band, the vastus lateralis is elevated anteriorly and the periosteum is reflected posteriorly until the intercondylar notch is palpated.

Left lateral knee incision for a left distal femoral opening wedge osteotomy with the patient in the supine position and the knee flexed to 90°. The vastus lateralis muscle is elevated anteriorly (white arrow), while the periosteum is reflected posteriorly (black arrow). This location should be cleared of all soft tissue to allow for level placement of the osteotomy plate.

MCL-POL Autograft Harvest

Next, an anteromedial incision is centered over the vastus medialis oblique muscle and continued 15 cm distally. Dissection is continued until the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are identified. The adhesions of the tendons are cut, and the tendons are harvested using an open hamstring harvester, remaining attached distally.

MCL-POL Tunnel Preparation

A ruler is used to measure 6 cm distal to the joint line to the sMCL distal tibial attachment. The 2 grafts are fixed at this location using 2 Q-FIX anchors (Smith & Nephew, London, England) (Fig 6). Next, the adductor tubercle is identified, and a ruler is used to measure 12.6 mm distally and 8.3 mm anterior to identify the medial epicondyle and, subsequently, the remnant femoral sMCL attachment.8 A Beath pin is drilled at the femoral sMCL attachment, ensuring that its trajectory is more anterior and less proximal (Fig 7). The POL femoral attachment is then identified by measuring 11 mm posterior and proximal to the sMCL femoral attachment (Fig 8).3 A Beath pin is drilled through the POL femoral attachment at the same trajectory. The sMCL and POL femoral tunnels are then reamed with a 7-mm reamer to a depth of 35 mm, and passing sutures are placed.

Fixation of the distal tibial superficial medial collateral ligament attachment in a left knee with the patient in the supine position. A 6-cm measurement is made distal to the joint line (white line), where 2 Q-FIX anchors are placed, and sutures are used to tie down the semitendinosus and gracilis autografts constituting the new distal tibial attachment of the superficial medial collateral ligament.

A Beath pin is drilled through a guide for the femoral superficial medial collateral ligament tunnel with the left knee flexed to 90°. At 12.6 mm distal and 8.3 mm anterior to the adductor tubercle, the medial epicondyle is identified. Just posterior and proximal to this point, a Beath pin is drilled (white arrow) at a 20° angle anterior and proximal to avoid the osteotomy site. A subsequent tunnel is then reamed with a 7-mm reamer to a depth of 35 mm.

Identification of the femoral attachment of the posterior oblique ligament in relation to the femoral superficial medial collateral ligament (MCL) Beath pin with the left knee flexed to 90° and the patient in the supine position. A measurement is made 11 mm posterior and proximal to the superficial MCL tunnel. A Beath pin is drilled parallel to the MCL femoral tunnel. The Beath pin is over-reamed with a 7-mm reamer to a depth of 35 mm.

Next, the POL tibial tunnel is prepared by dissecting down and identifying the semimembranosus tendon and associated POL tibial attachment point, inferior to the anterior arm of the semimembranosus tendon.3 A guide pin is drilled anterolaterally aiming 15 mm medial to the Gerdy tubercle (Fig 9). This area is then reamed with a 6-mm reamer, and passing sutures are placed. The MCL graft is passed under the sartorius fascia and whipstitched so that 30 mm will fit into the tunnel; the remainder is used for the POL graft.

A tibial guide is used to drill a guide pin through the posterior oblique ligament (POL) tibial attachment with the left knee flexed to 90°. Located just superficial and anterior to the semimembranosus tendon, the tibial attachment of the POL can be identified along the anterior arm of the semimembranosus, and a tibial guide can be used to drill a guide pin (white arrow) for the POL tibial tunnel, aimed 15 mm medial to the Gerdy tubercle.

MCL-POL Autograft and PCL Allograft Preparation

The anterolateral bundle (ALB) of the PCL is fashioned from an Achilles tendon allograft with an 11 × 20-mm bone plug and whipstitched distally. The posteromedial bundle (PMB) of the PCL is prepared from a tibialis anterior allograft and whipstitched on each end with No. 2 nonabsorbable sutures. The 12-cm-long POL graft is prepared from the reminder of the MCL autograft and similarly whipstitched.

Arthroscopy

Medial and lateral portals are created, and an assessment of the knee joint is performed. An 11-mm reamer is used as the guide for the ALB femoral tunnel, which is located midway between the trochlear point and medial arch point and touches the cartilage margin.9 A 7-mm reamer is used as the guide for the PMB, which is midway between the medial arch point and posterior point and is about 5 to 6 mm from the cartilage margin.9 Tunnels are drilled to a depth of 25 mm, maintaining a 2-mm bone bridge, and the PMB tunnel is notched posteriorly; passing sutures are placed.

Next, dissection is extended to the PCL tibial attachment at the bundle ridge. A tibial guide pin is drilled at 55° with fluoroscopic confirmation. A reamer is used to ream to 70% of the tunnel depth. The remainder of the tunnel is finished using the reamer by hand with a large curette placed over the tip of the guide pin to protect against over-penetration. A large Gore smoother (Smith & Nephew, London, England) is passed through the tibial tunnel to remove any bone spicules and passed out the anterolateral portal. The PMB is fixated into the femoral tunnel with a 7 × 20-mm bioabsorbable screw, and the ALB bone plug is positioned in its respective tunnel with a 7 × 20-mm titanium screw.

MCL-POL and PCL Graft Placement

The PMB and ALB graft sutures are passed through the tibial tunnel with the Gore smoother. The sMCL and POL grafts are then passed into their respective femoral tunnels. The POL graft is fixated in the femoral tunnel with a 7 × 20-mm bioabsorbable screw. The ALB and PMB grafts should be fixed independently on the tibia; the ALB should be fixed first at 90° of knee flexion, whereas the PMB should be fixed in full extension, both with 6.5-mm screws and spiked washers (Fig 10).10 Next, the sMCL graft is fixed into its femoral tunnel with 7 × 20-mm bioabsorbable screws at 20° of knee flexion and the foot in neutral rotation (Fig 11). The proximal tibial attachment of the sMCL is reconstituted by placing a Q-FIX anchor 15 mm distal to the joint line and tying the graft at this location. After the POL graft is passed into the tibial tunnel, it is fixed in full extension with a 7 × 20-mm bioabsorbable screw (Fig 12).

(B) The posteromedial-bundle PCL graft is fixated at its tibial attachment posterior to the bundle ridge (white arrow) in full extension.

Passage of the superficial medial collateral ligament autograft into the femoral tunnel of a left knee with the patient in the supine position. The femoral end of the superficial medial collateral ligament graft (black arrow) is passed into its respective tunnel, 12.6 mm distally and 8.3 mm anteriorly to the adductor tubercle. Graft fixation is performed with the knee flexed at 20° and the foot in a neutral position.

Passage of the posterior oblique ligament (POL) autograft into the tibial tunnel of a left knee with the patient in the supine position. The POL graft (white arrow) is passed into its tibial tunnel, which was previously made superficially and anteriorly to the semimembranosus tendon on the medial side of the knee. Fixation of this graft is performed with the knee in full extension. Guide pins are placed in the POL and medial collateral ligament tunnels to ensure there is no overlap with the fixation on the femur via fluoroscopic imaging for confirmation.

DFO and Closure

Laterally, 2 guide pins are drilled at 45° toward the adductor tubercle. A 5-mm-deep cut is made with an oscillating saw. The anterior and posterior aspects of the osteotomy are completed with a small osteotome, whereas the middle aspect is completed with a medium osteotome, ensuring a 1-cm medial cortical hinge. A finger is placed posteriorly to protect the popliteal neurovasculature.

An opening spreader device is used to achieve the calculated correction, before a tine device is positioned and a DFO plate is concurrently placed (Fig 13). The osteotomy plate is fixed with four 4.5-mm fully threaded cortical screws proximally and three 6.5-mm fully threaded cancellous screws distally to avoid convergence with the previously made tunnels.11 The osteotomy site is filled with allograft, and anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopy confirms final plate and graft placement. The tourniquet is let down, and deep and superficial tissues are closed.

The osteotomy plate of the distal femoral osteotomy is placed with the patient in the supine position. A 5-mm distal femoral osteotomy plate is placed using fluoroscopic imaging as a guide to ensure there is no overlap with the femoral superficial medial collateral ligament and posterior oblique ligament tunnels. The osteotomy plate is fixed to the femur with 7 total screws, and bone allograft is used to supplement the healing process. In the case of a non-ossifying fibroma, screws should be placed in a manner that ensures there is minimal convergence with the fibroma.

Discussion

Medial knee ligament injuries commonly occur with PCL injuries and can be exacerbated by valgus malalignment in chronic cases. Correcting valgus malalignment may reduce the risk of MCL graft failure after reconstruction of a chronic tear.5,10 DFOs are proven effective in correcting valgus alignment in the coronal plane. Patient satisfaction after a DFO has also been promising, with one study reporting that 71% of valgus-aligned patients were satisfied with their DFO results.8 However, there is a risk of nonunion or delayed union, in addition to a high rate of hardware removal, especially in smokers.8 A full list of advantages and disadvantages is presented in Table 2.
