A retrospective and comprehensive analysis of excess life-years lost, mortality risk, and cause of death among severe mental illness in China
Authors: Xinyu Fang, Hao Yu, Chaoran Wu, Junhua Tan, Feng Sun, Ning Qian, Hui Zhou, Jinyi Zhou, Xiangrong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with severe mental illness (SMI) experience higher mortality rates than the general population. We conducted a comprehensive, population-based study to quantify the excess life-years lost (LYLs), mortality risks, and cause of death among SMIs.MethodsUsing data from the Jiangsu Province Management Information System for Patients with SMI, we identified 321,436 SMI patients in Jiangsu, China, between January 2014 and December 2022; 33,947 of them died during follow-up. We calculated the excess LYLs for each type of SMI and estimated the standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) for all-cause and specific-cause mortality among individuals with four types of SMIs.ResultsThe excess LYLs in four types of SMIs were as follows: for schizophrenia, 12.87 years in males and 11.92 years in females; for bipolar disorder, 8.43 years in males and 5.54 years in females; for paranoid disorder, 13.90 years in males and 14.03 years in females; and for schizoaffective disorder, 16.69 years in males and 15.82 years in females, when compared to the general population. Individuals with SMI had nearly twice the all-cause mortality of the general population (adjusted SMR 1.96, 95% CI 1.94–1.98). Elevated mortality rates were observed across all types of SMIs, with SMRs varying from 1.23 to 2.96. The SMRs for specific causes showed distinct patterns, with infectious diseases ranging from 1.60 to 3.08, neoplasms from 0.73 to 1.15, diabetes from 1.82 to 3.89, nervous system diseases from 1.44 to 3.15, cardiovascular diseases from 1.05 to 2.35, cerebrovascular diseases from 1.26 to 2.18, respiratory diseases from 0.86 to 1.69, digestive diseases from 1.25 to 2.66, accidents from 2.15 to 4.01, suicide from 6.16 to 14.81, and all other causes from 2.06 to 4.33 across four types of SMIs; alcohol misuse was elevated only in schizophrenia (SMR 6.57), with < 10 events in the other disorders.ConclusionsIndividuals with SMI in China experienced marked loss of life expectancy and faced nearly double the mortality, with cause-specific patterns varying by diagnosis. Our findings provide insights into the need for our government to continually enhance physical and mental health policies to prevent and decrease premature mortality in individuals with SMI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04380-9.Keywords: Severe mental illness, Mortality, Life-years lost, Death causes

Background

Mental health issues have emerged as significant public health and societal challenges, impacting economic and social progress. As per statistics from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), mental disorders account for 20% of the total disease burden in China [1]. Among these, severe mental illnesses (SMI) drastically affect patients’ quality of life and create substantial disease burdens. Currently, there is inconsistency in the practical definition of SMI, with narrower definitions focusing on psychotic disorders like schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, while broader definitions encompass a wider array of conditions [2]. In China, the government focuses on addressing priority mental health conditions that pose a heightened risk of violent or socially disruptive behaviors, driven by resource limitations and a primary concern for social stability [3]. Thus, six mental disorders: schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, schizoaffective disorder, paranoid disorder, mental disorder caused by epilepsy, and intellectual disability, are classified as priority mental health conditions in China [4, 5]. It is estimated that there were over six million patients registered with those priority mental health conditions in China, with a reported prevalence rate of 0.43% [6]. Since the strengthening of the government’s supervision of these individuals, the proportion of aggressive behavior decreased markedly [7]. The persistent issue of excessive and premature mortality rates among individuals with SMI remains a grave concern, warranting further investigations to understand and address this public health challenge effectively.

To date, numerous studies have explored premature mortality in individuals with SMI, revealing a stark reality where individuals with SMI experience significantly shorter life expectancies compared to the general population. Reports indicate that individuals with SMI may face death 10 to 20 years earlier than the general population, particularly impacting male patients [8]. Furthermore, the mortality rate among individuals with SMI is estimated to be 2 to 3 times higher than that of the general population [9, 10]. Evidence also suggests that the gap of life expectancy between SMI and the general population may be increasing over time [11]. Additionally, due to the differences in diet structure, lifestyle, and even genetic background, the life expectancy of patients with SMI is also different across countries and regions [8, 12]. For instance, a study in Ethiopia revealed a 30-year lower life expectancy for individuals with SMI compared to the general population [10], while a systematic review highlighted an average of 14.5 years of potential life lost in schizophrenia patients compared to the general population [13]. A recent study in China reported a premature mortality of 15.82 years in schizophrenia patients, with male and female patients experiencing 15.82 and 14.59 years of premature mortality, respectively [14]. Most studies have focused on specific types of SMIs and have primarily been conducted in Western countries, leaving limited evidence on the lifespan of individuals with SMI in China, particularly concerning premature mortality across the spectrum of these disorders within the same timeframe.

The excess mortality observed in SMI is driven predominantly by natural rather than unnatural causes [14, 15]. It is widely acknowledged that individuals with SMI face an elevated risk of developing physical comorbidities due to poor dietary habits, higher rates of substance use, limited physical activity, social engagement, and the adverse effects of antipsychotic and other medications [12, 16]. Population-based studies indicate that approximately 79% of the disparity in life expectancy between individuals with SMI and the general population can be linked to cardiovascular, respiratory, cancer, metabolic, and infectious diseases [17]. A comprehensive meta-analysis revealed that 67.3% of deaths in individuals with mental disorders result from physical illnesses, while 17.5% are due to unnatural causes[18]. Among physical illnesses, cardiovascular disease emerges as the leading cause of premature mortality in individuals with SMI, as supported by substantial evidence [19–22]. However, a cohort study conducted in London during 2013–2017 suggested that deaths from cancer may surpass those from cardiovascular disease in patients with SMI [23]. While a limited number of domestic studies have explored the causes of death in individuals with SMI, they either lack specific descriptions of physical disease causes [24, 25]or focus on a singular type of SMI [14, 26].

Although the decreased life expectancy in individuals with SMI is driven chiefly by natural causes, the toll of unnatural deaths, such as suicide and accidents (including road accidents, drownings, accidental falls, and others), remains alarmingly high among those individuals. A prospective cohort study conducted in the USA revealed that nearly half of individuals who died by suicide had a history of mental health diagnoses or had received related treatment [27]. In a recent study from Taiwan, it was found that 12% of schizophrenia patients died by suicide, representing the primary cause of unnatural death in this population [28]. However, past research has indicated that individuals with SMI are two to four times more likely to die from accidents compared to the general population, with accidents being a more prevalent unnatural cause of death than suicide in this patient group [29]. Hence, a comprehensive assessment of both natural and unnatural causes of death is essential to fully understand the life-expectancy gap in this vulnerable population.

In the present study, we conducted a retrospective study to systematically collect mortality data for individuals with SMIs (including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, schizoaffective disorder and paranoid disorder) in Jiangsu, China, alongside data for the general population in the same geographical area during the same timeframe. The objectives of this study were (1) to calculate the excess life-years lost (LYLs) from age at first diagnosis in individuals with each specific SMI, separately by sex, as an indicator of the differences in average life expectancy compared to the general population; and (2) to analyze the causes of death among individuals with different types of SMIs and estimate the all-cause and cause-specific relative risks in SMI patients compared to the general population.

Methods

Sample and data sources

In this retrospective study, we collected data on all SMI from the Jiangsu Province Management Information System for Patients with SMI, which is the official only mental health surveillance system in Jiangsu. Jiangsu Province is the center of the east coast of Mainland China, with more than 85 million residents in 13 municipalities, and is the most densely populated province and one of the most representative provinces in China. All SMI patients entered into the Jiangsu Province Management Information System for Patients with SMI must receive concordant diagnoses by at least two specialist psychiatrists according to the International Classification of Disease (ICD), ensuring diagnostic accuracy (the detailed workflow is shown in the Supplementary Material). We considered only the first recorded diagnosis of SMI for each individual, disregarding any subsequent multiple diagnoses[30]. To allow international comparability, we only focus on four types of mental disorders as SMIs. The ICD-10 diagnostic codes for these four types of SMIs are as follows: schizophrenia (F20.0–F20.9), bipolar disorder (F31.0–F31.9), schizoaffective disorder (F25.0–F25.9) and paranoid disorder (F22.0).

Since the Jiangsu Province Management Information System for Patients with SMI was officially launched in 2011 and fully operational by the end of 2013. Hence, we collected data on individuals with SMI from January 2014 to December 2022. A total of 321,436 individuals with SMIs were identified, with 33,947 dying during the following period. Their sex (male or female), birth date, diagnosis, date of first diagnosis, death date, and primary death cause were extracted, while their name and identity card number were anonymized. Cause of death data in this system were derived from the Mortality Registration System in the China Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which is a national official death registration database, and all records in the databases had undergone quality control. All primary death causes were classified into 12 non-overlapping groups according to ICD-10 codes: infectious diseases (A00–B99), neoplasms (C00–D09), diabetes (E10–E14), alcohol misuse (F10, G31.2, G62.1, G72.1, I42.6, K29.2, K70, K85.2, K86.0), nervous system diseases (G00–G25.9, G30.0–G31.1, G31.8–G62.0, G62.2–G72.0, G72.2–G99), cardiovascular diseases (I00–I25, I27, I30–I42.5, I42.7–I52), cerebrovascular diseases (I60–I69), respiratory diseases (J00–J99), digestive diseases (K00–K29.1, K29.3–K69, K71–K85.1, K85.3–K93), accidents (V01–X59, X85–Y89), suicide (X60–X84, Y87.0), and all other causes. Death cause classifications were based on established literature with appropriate modifications [31].

The Jiangsu Province Management Information System for Patients with SMI has ethical approval from the Jiangsu health department to support research using anonymized data. The present study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Nanjing Brain Hospital and strictly follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. All data were deidentified and not recognizable at the individual level.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were done in R version 3.5.2. Descriptive statistics (frequency distribution, mean and standard deviation) were used to characterize the investigated variables.

Differences in average life expectancy between individuals with a specific SMI and the general population were calculated as excess LYLs, separately for males and females. The method has been previously detailed [31]. Briefly, for the SMI group, the expected residual Lifetime from age at first diagnosis to age 95 (a specified maximum age) was estimated as the area under the survival curve using age-specific mortality rates. LYLs measure the average years lost before age 95 by comparing this expected remaining life expectancy to 95. For the general population, it measures the average years lost before age 95 for a same-sex group alive at ages matching the onset-age distribution of the SMI group. The difference in LYLs between the two groups, defined as excess LYLs, can be interpreted as the average number of years that people with a specific mental disorder lose in excess of that found in the general population of the same sex and age. The 95% CIs of the excess LYLs were obtained using non-parametric bootstrap with 10,000 iterations.

Standardized mortality ratios (SMRs) were calculated by dividing the observed deaths in each disease group and death cause group by the corresponding number of deaths expected from an equivalent age, sex, and calendar time standardized population with mortality rates from the general population in Jiangsu Province during the same period (Jiangsu Bureau of Statistics website). The person-time of observation was computed from the date of first SMI diagnosis until death, or administrative censoring (31 December 2022), whichever occurred first. The 95% CI of SMRs was estimated for each specific cause of death and for all causes combined in SMI using Poisson regression models, adjusting for sex, age, and calendar time[31]. Data on general population mortality risks were obtained from the CDC system.

Results

A total of 321,436 individuals (139,958 males, 43.54%) diagnosed with SMI were identified. Of whom, 251,976 with a primary diagnosis of schizophrenia (113,465 males, 45.03%), 60,651 with a primary diagnosis of bipolar disorder (22,787 males, 37.57%), 2630 were paranoid disorder (1290 males, 49.05%), and 6179 were schizoaffective disorder (2416 males, 39.10%). During the 9-year follow-up period, 33,947 individuals with SMI died (16,433 males, 50.83%), with an average death age of 65.71 ± 15.05 years (male: 63.43 ± 15.08, female: 67.84 ± 14.70). Among these deceased SMI patients, 27,531 (81.10%) had schizophrenia (13,415 males, 48.73%), 4939 (14.55%) had bipolar disorder (2326 males, 47.09%), 407 (1.20%) had paranoid disorder (211 males, 51.84%), and 1070 (3.15%) had schizoaffective disorder (481 males, 44.95%)..

Individuals with SMIs had a shorter remaining life expectancy than the general population. As shown in Fig. 1, compared to the general population of the same age and sex, excess LYLs from age at first diagnosis were as follows: for schizophrenia, 12.87 years in males (95% CI 12.85–12.88) and 11.92 years in females (95% CI 11.91–11.93); for bipolar disorder, 8.43 years in males (95% CI 8.41–8.45) and 5.54 years in females (95% CI 5.53–5.55); for paranoid disorder, 13.90 years in males (95% CI 13.76–14.05) and 14.03 years in females (95% CI 13.94–14.12); and for schizoaffective disorder, 16.69 years in males (95% CI 16.59–16.79) and 15.82 years in females (95% CI 15.71–15.93).

Excess life-years lost for individuals with each type of severe mental illness compared with the general population of the same age and sex

Overall, individuals with SMI experienced a mortality rate from all causes that is nearly double that of the general population (adjusted SMR 1.96, 95% CI 1.94–1.98). Elevated mortality rates were observed across all types of SMIs, with SMRs varying from 1.23 to 2.19. Specifically, the all-cause SMRs were 2.19 for schizophrenia (95% CI 2.17–2.22), 1.23 for bipolar disorder (95% CI 1.19–1.26), 1.94 for paranoid psychosis (95% CI 1.76–2.14), and 2.15 for schizoaffective disorder (95% CI 2.03–2.29) (Fig. 2). Sex-specific estimates showed similar results for females (all-cause SMR 1.93, 95% CI 1.90–1.96) and males (1.99, 1.96–2.02). All types of SMIs were Linked to higher mortality rates, with SMRs spanning from 1.31 (95% CI 1.26–1.37) in males and 1.16 (95% CI 1.12–1.20) in females for bipolar disorder to 2.19 (95% CI 2.15–2.23) in males and 2.19 (95% CI 2.16–2.23) in females for schizophrenia (Fig. 2).

Figure 3 shows the distribution of deaths from various causes among individuals with four types of SMIs. The top four leading causes of death in each type of SMI were cerebrovascular disease (proportions ranging from 22.04% to 25.06%), neoplasms (ranging from 13.27% to 17.94%), cardiovascular disease (ranging from 12.80% to 15.23%), and accidents (Ranging from 12.06% to 13.13%). Meanwhile, deaths attributable to alcohol misuse were the lowest across all four types of SMIs, with proportions ranging from 0% to 0.25% (Fig. 3 and Table 1).

As shown in Table 1, mortality rates for all specific causes of death were higher for individuals with any mental disorder compared to the general population, except for neoplasms (SMR 0.73, 95% CI 0.69–0.78) and respiratory diseases (SMR 0.86, 95% CI 0.78–0.95) in bipolar disorder, neoplasms (SMR 0.86, 95% CI 0.66–1.12) in paranoid disorder. The SMRs for deaths due to neoplasms were not particularly high across all types of SMIs, with the highest SMR of 1.15 (95% CI 0.99–1.33) in schizoaffective disorder. The SMRs for all specific causes of death reveal distinct mortality patterns across the different SMIs. Schizophrenia patients exhibited the highest SMR for suicide (SMR 10.89, 95% CI 10.25–11.56), alcohol misuse (SMR 6.57, 95% CI 5.20–8.30) and accidents (SMR 4.01, 95% CI 3.88–4.14). Patients with bipolar disorder had the highest SMR for suicide (SMR 9.71, 95% CI 8.68–10.87), accidents (SMR 2.15, 95% CI 1.98–2.32), and digestive diseases (SMR 5.64, 95% CI 5.10–6.22). Similarly, patients with paranoid disorder and schizoaffective disorder also had the highest SMRs for suicide (6.16, 95% CI 3.31–11.44, and 14.81, 95% CI 11.43–19.20, respectively) and accidents (3.46, 95% CI 2.63–4.54, and 3.62, 95% CI 3.05–4.30, respectively). Additionally, except for bipolar disorder, the SMRs for deaths due to cerebrovascular diseases and diabetes exceeded 2.00 across the other three types of SMIs (Table 1). We did not calculate the SMRs for digestive diseases in paranoid disorder, for infectious diseases in paranoid disorder and schizoaffective disorder, or for alcohol misuse in bipolar disorder, paranoid disorder, and schizoaffective disorder because each of these cause-specific death counts was below ten.

Discussion

To date, only a scant few studies in China have examined the life expectancy and years of life lost in individuals with SMI, yet none have concurrently addressed schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, paranoid psychosis, and schizoaffective disorder in a single investigation. In a study by Liu et al., which involved 7907 deceased individuals with schizophrenia in western China in 2017, it was reported that the overall life expectancy of schizophrenia patients was 55.7 years, resulting in approximately 18.4 years of life lost compared to the general population [32]. An early longitudinal study in rural China, dating back to 1994, followed 510 patients with schizophrenia, finding that the life expectancy was 50.6 years for males and 58.5 years for females based on data from 139 deceased individuals by 2015, equating to a shortfall of 13.4 and 17.1 years compared to the general population during the same period [33]. Research from Taiwan revealed Life expectancies at birth for individuals with schizophrenia, with figures of 59.53 years for men and 65.65 years for women in cohort 2005, and 60.65 years for men and 67.18 years for women in cohort 2010. Meanwhile, life expectancies at birth were reported at 74.5 years for men and 80.8 years for women with bipolar disorder in Taiwan in 2005, and 76.15 years for men and 82.66 years for women in 2010, resulting in a loss of 12.69 to 15.53 years for patients with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder in Taiwan [34]. In the present study, we comprehensively analyzed mortality data for the entire province of Jiangsu from 2014 to 2022. Our findings revealed that individuals with SMIs had a shorter remaining life expectancy than the general population. The excess LYLs from age at diagnosis in the four types of SMIs were as follows: for schizophrenia, 12.87 years in males and 11.92 years in females; for bipolar disorder, 8.43 years in males and 5.54 years in females; for paranoid disorder, 13.90 years in males and 14.03 years in females; and for schizoaffective disorder, 16.69 years in males and 15.82 years in females, when compared to the general population. Together, our results highlight the profound mortality burden associated with SMIs in China. Studies conducted in South East London have reported similar alarming findings. In the 2008–2012 cohort, the life expectancy gap was 16.4 years for schizophrenia and 12.3 years for bipolar disorder. In the 2013–2017 cohort, the gap was 14.6 years for schizophrenia and 14.0 years for bipolar disorder [23]. Another study conducted in the UK with data from 2007 to 2014 demonstrated that the life expectancy gap was 15.0 for males and 13.3 for females in schizophrenia-spectrum disorder, and 12.6 for males and 13.1 for females in bipolar disorder [35]. A recent meta-analysis encompassing studies from 24 countries or regions calculated a pooled years of potential life lost of 14.66 years (male: 14.94, female: 13.14) in individuals with any mental disorders [36]. While direct comparisons among existing studies may be challenging due to varying observation periods, geographical regions, and definitions of severe mental disorders, the consensus remains that individuals with SMI have a diminished life expectancy compared to the general population. Thus, it is crucial to gain a deeper understanding of the phenomena and underlying causes of premature mortality in individuals with SMI.

Furthermore, the significantly elevated mortality burden among individuals with SMI was also supported by the results of SMR calculation. Overall, the all-cause SMR for individuals with SMI was nearly double that of the general population, underscoring the profound impact of SMI on premature mortality. This elevated risk was consistent across all types of SMIs, with SMRs Ranging from 1.23 for bipolar disorder to 2.19 for schizophrenia. The sex- and diagnosis-specific differences in all-cause SMR may reflect variations in biological factors, healthcare access, or behavioral risks associated with different SMIs. These results align with earlier studies, such as the meta-analysis by Oakley et al. (2018), which reported SMRs fell to 2.8 for Males and 2.7 for females with schizophrenia in studies conducted after 2010, indicating a persistent but slightly declining mortality gap over time [37]. Similarly, our findings for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder are consistent with these trends, suggesting that while mortality risks remain high, they may have modestly decreased compared to earlier decades. However, Hayes et al. (2017) found that while overall mortality rates for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder decreased from 2000 to 2014, the relative mortality risks compared to the general population increased after adjusting for socio-demographic factors [38]. These findings, together with our results, underscore the persistent mortality risks for individuals with SMI, despite advances in mental and physical healthcare.

Numerous studies have highlighted cardiovascular diseases as a predominant cause of death among individuals with SMI [20, 39, 40]. However, most of this evidence stems from research conducted in Western countries, and China’s domestic studies on the causes of death among SMI patients are scarce. Our systematic investigation aimed to address this gap and revealed that cerebrovascular diseases and cardiovascular diseases both rank as the primary causes of death in SMI patients, with the former accounting for a higher proportion. A recent 5-year follow-up cohort study in China also supported these findings, indicating that more individuals with schizophrenia succumbed to cerebrovascular diseases compared to cardiovascular events [14]. Additionally, the SMRs for cardiovascular diseases and cerebrovascular diseases among individuals with schizophrenia were approximately twice those of the general population [14]. In the present study, our findings also revealed that, while individuals with bipolar disorder had only slightly elevated risks of death from cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases compared to the general population, the SMRs for these causes of death in the other three types of SMI were nearly or more than double those of the general population. Therefore, there is a crucial need to monitor not only cardiovascular disease but also cerebrovascular disease in individuals with SMI.

While several studies have suggested a lower incidence rate of neoplasms in individuals with, potentially attributed to protective biological and other mechanisms in these patients [41–43], there is growing evidence indicating a gradual increase in the incidence of neoplasms among individuals with SMI [44–46]. A national cohort study conducted in Denmark reported higher mortality rate ratios for neoplasm-related deaths among individuals with schizophrenia (1.38) and bipolar disorder (1.25) [31]. In our current study, we found that neoplasm accounted for a high proportion of deaths among individuals with all types of SMIs. Interestingly, the risk of death from neoplasm was lower in patients with bipolar disorder and paranoid psychosis compared to the general population, while for schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorders, the risk of death from neoplasm did not exceed 1.15 times that of the general population. Thus, it is essential for future research to delve deeper into the comorbidity of neoplasms and the associated burden of premature mortality in individuals with SMI.

There is substantial evidence indicating that individuals with SMI are at a significantly higher risk of developing diabetes-related metabolic diseases, which is associated with the use of antipsychotic medications [47] and genetic comorbidities [48]. In this study, we found that the SMRs for diabetes-related deaths among individuals with all four types of SMIs were at least 1.86, with specific SMRs of 3.87 for schizophrenia, 1.82 for bipolar disorder, 3.13 for paranoid disorder, and 3.89 for schizoaffective disorder. These findings are largely consistent with previously reported international data, which documented diabetes-specific mortality rate ratios of 3.11 for schizophrenia and 2.09 for mood disorder [31]. Together, these results highlight the critical need for integrated care approaches that address mental and physical health challenges faced by individuals with SMI, to reduce premature mortality and improve long-term outcomes.

It is well-established that patients with SMI are at a significantly higher risk of suicide and accidents compared to the general population [10, 49]. In the present study, we found elevated mortality risks for suicide and accidents across all types of SMIs, with SMRs ranging from 6.16 to 14.81 for suicide and from 2.15 to 4.01 for accidents. These findings are consistent with recent domestic studies, which reported prominent premature deaths due to suicide and accidents in patients with schizophrenia, with SMRs of 18.70 and 6.22, respectively [32]. Another contemporaneous study also demonstrated high SMRs for suicide (3.87) and accidents (4.13) in schizophrenia patients [25]. Similarly, data from Denmark revealed a mortality rate of 10.76 for suicide and 5.39 for accidents in schizophrenia [31], which closely align with our findings. These consistent findings highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to reduce the risk of suicide and accidents, which could significantly mitigate years of potential life lost in individuals with SMI.

Alcohol misuse is also a significant issue among individuals with SMI, contributing not only to increased rates of suicide attempts but also to mortality[50]. In this study, we found that individuals with schizophrenia had significantly elevated SMR for deaths related to alcohol misuse. The findings align with previous research from Denmark, highlighting the severe impact of alcohol misuse on mortality in these populations [31]. However, since the event of alcohol misuse death is less than ten among bipolar disorder, paranoid disorder, and schizoaffective disorder, precise SMR estimates could not be obtained. Hence, future studies with larger samples are warranted. The high mortality rate due to alcohol misuse in individuals with schizophrenia underscores the need for tailored interventions addressing alcohol misuse in these populations.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the large size of authoritative data of SMI from a representative province in China, providing an opportunity to comprehensively and accurately analyze the excess LYLs and mortality ratios from all causes and specific causes in individuals with SMI compared to the general population during the same time period. To our knowledge, this is the first study in mainland China to simultaneously report excess life-years lost and mortality ratios for four major categories of SMIs (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, schizoaffective disorder and paranoid disorder). The study, however, had some limitations. First, it is possible that a very small percentage of individuals with SMI were not identified and diagnosed and therefore were not included as death data of SMI. Second, inconsistent with other countries and regions, major depressive disorder is not regarded as SMI, thus preventing us from reporting death data for patients with major depressive disorder. Third, while the sample size in this study was sufficiently large to ensure a representative analysis of SMI patients in China, expanding the dataset to encompass the entire country would further enhance the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, although we adjusted for age, sex, and calendar time in our calculations of life expectancy and SMR, some unmeasured confounding factors, such as economic status, could potentially affect the results. Fifth, individuals with SMI may experience diagnostic changes over time, but we considered only the earliest observed diagnosis for each individual, which may impact the comprehensiveness of our findings.

Conclusions

In summary, individuals with SMI in China, across all categories, face significant reductions in life expectancy lost compared to the general population. In addition, individuals with SMI experienced a mortality rate from all causes that is nearly double that of the general population, with distinct patterns observed in the specific causes across different types of SMIs. Future studies in our region should delve deeper into this issue. It is imperative for our government to continually enhance physical and mental health policies to prevent and decrease premature mortality in Chinese patients with SMI.
