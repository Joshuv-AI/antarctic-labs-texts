Facilitating the spread prediction of public health emergencies based on spatio-temporal neural network
Authors: Ziwei Cai, Haiyan Wang, Fuyou Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo overcome the shortcomings of traditional models in capturing the spatiotemporal dynamics of epidemic spread, this study introduces an innovative model, hybrid spatio-temporal neural network architecture (MI–GCN–LSTM), which integrates mutual information (MI), long short term memory (LSTM), and graph convolutional network (GCN) to collaboratively predict epidemic trends across multiple regions during public health emergencies. First, an adjacency matrix is constructed using mutual information theory to quantify the influence of complex behaviors such as interregional population movement, thus capturing potential spatial dependencies more accurately. Second, GCN are applied to identify transmission patterns across regions and extract spatial features. Ultimately, LSTM are employed to model long-term temporal dependencies and dynamic trends in the development of epidemics. To evaluate the model’s effectiveness, a case study uses COVID-19 data from several European countries provided by the World Health Organization (WHO), as well as city-level data from Hubei Province released by its Health Commission. Results show that the proposed model achieves superior performance compared to the suboptimal LSTM–GCN model on datasets from several European countries, reducing MAE and RMSE by 9.22% and 4.24%, respectively. On the Hubei dataset, the improvements are more substantial, MAE decreased by approximately 27.74%. This study provides decision making support for emergency response and lays the foundation for future forecasting and resource allocation.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, public health emergencies have become increasingly frequent. Over the past two decades, the world has faced eight major events, including the 2003 SARS outbreak, the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic, the 2014 West African Ebola epidemic, the COVID-19 pandemic that began in 2019, and the 2022 monkeypox outbreak. These emergencies have posed serious threats to human life, health, and property, and more importantly, have disrupted national economic development and social stability. Among them, COVID-19 has been the most widespread and prolonged, with over 700 million confirmed cases and more than 7 million deaths worldwide. It was not until 2023 that the pandemic was brought under effective and comprehensive control.

In the aftermath of a public health emergency, swift emergency response in affected areas is crucial to minimize losses and mitigate impact. Timely and effective delivery of emergency supplies is critical for epidemic prevention and control. However, this depends on efficiently acquiring demand information from the affected regions. emergencies often involve uncertainty, unclear pathogenesis, and complex transmission mechanisms, which can delay or even prevent the availability of demand data.

Therefore, forecasting the development of epidemics has become a vital component of emergency response. Accurate predictions of new infections enable proactive response planning, estimation of emergency supply needs, formulation of rational distribution strategies, optimization of response times, and ultimately, reduction of public losses. Despite its importance, accurate epidemic forecasting remains a significant challenge due to the multidimensional complexity of public health emergencies and continues to draw substantial scholarly interest.

Currently, mainstream forecasting methods fall into three categories: statistical-based mathematical models, dynamic models of infectious diseases capturing transmission mechanisms, and data-driven artificial intelligence approaches. Among these, mathematical models describe epidemic evolution via statistical relationships between variables. Regression and grey prediction models serve as benchmarks for investigating causal relationships. Rath1 applied multiple linear regression to analyze correlations among active cases, recoveries, deaths, and positive cases, performing multivariate modeling using the latest WHO data. The results showed high model accuracy. Considering nonlinear interactions among variables, as well as the uncertainty and ambiguity in epidemic progression, Zixue2 et al. integrated fuzzy set theory with linear regression to develop a multivariate fuzzy regression model. Traditional regression models perform poorly when historical data are limited or of low quality.

To the end, Dou3 applied a grey prediction model to forecast Ebola infection proportions in African countries. However, grey prediction models face limitations, including a single method for generating background values and drawbacks of least squares parameter estimation. Li4 optimized GM (1,1) model parameters using a genetic algorithm. Beyond parameter optimization, input data can be enhanced. Ceylan5 introduced a rolling mechanism to the particle swarm-optimized grey prediction model, dynamically updating the input window and overcoming limitations of traditional models and nonlinear regression networks. Nonetheless, these models do not account for the evolutionary characteristics of the epidemic, resulting in large prediction errors.

Infectious disease dynamic models use differential equations to describe transmission, with the SIR model as a classical example6,7. In 1927, Kermack8 introduced the SIR model, classifying individuals as susceptible (S), infected (I), or recovered (R), pioneering systemic dynamic modeling of infectious diseases. Following the COVID-19 outbreak, the model rapidly evolved. Some researchers have applied algorithms to optimize the parameters of the SEIR model. Godio9 integrated a generalized SEIR model with a particle swarm optimization solver to improve medium-term epidemic trend forecasts. However, it assumes constant infection and recovery probabilities, which may not reflect reality, limiting model performance.

In the big data era, artificial intelligence algorithms have excelled in forecasting and become a mainstream approach. Many researchers employ neural networks for trend forecasting. Chang et al.10 applied particle swarm optimization to finetune thresholds and initial weights of BP neural networks for predicting mortality and casualty rates in earthquakes, outperforming standalone BP networks. Chimmula11 employed long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to forecast COVID-19 trends and potential end dates in Canada and globally. Shao12 proposed a COVID-19 case prediction method based on multifactor driven LSTM and collected data on four types of indicators in 15 countries during the COVID-19 period. Considering the association between disease transmission and intervention policies, Chen13 introduced a hybrid model incorporating breakpoint detection, the SIR model, bidirectional gated recurrent units, and self attention mechanisms to forecast the epidemic transmission trend.

Although these methods have yielded some results, they are often restricted to modeling epidemic trends in a single temporal dimension or within a single region. Models considering spatiotemporal dimensions usually focus on static adjacency relationships and do not fully capture factors such as population mobility and temporal data similarity. Using the dataset from this study as an example, Fig. 1 illustrates that epidemic transmission displays spatial correlations among different countries. Geographical proximity and overlapping transport networks14 underlie similarities in epidemic trends. Moreover, the epidemic displays distinct temporal characteristics, with new infection cases fluctuating over time in a phased, hump-shaped pattern. The spatiotemporal patterns of infectious disease data should be carefully considered when developing predictive models.

COVID-19 trend chart for parts of Europe.

To address the aforementioned research limitations and achieve more accurate predictions. The study proposes a Hybrid Spatio-Temporal Neural Network Architecture (MI–GCN–LSTM) for epidemic forecasting in public health emergencies. MI–GCN–LSTM integrates mutual information (MI), Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), and LSTM to capture spatiotemporal dependencies for trend forecasting. LSTM effectively models nonlinear features and temporal patterns, while GCN captures interregional dependencies and reveals spatial patterns of public health emergencies. To better represent internode correlations, the adjacency matrix is constructed using mutual information, replacing the traditional approach based solely on geographic proximity. This matrix is then used as the input to the GCN. With this innovative adjacency matrix and full consideration of spatiotemporal patterns, MI–GCN–LSTM enables more accurate and dynamic trend forecasting. It aims to assist authorities and healthcare institutions in demand assessment and resource allocation, ultimately reducing the impact of public health emergencies.

Materials and methods

Construction of adjacency matrix based on mutual information matrix

In GCN, graphs are constructed to ensure that variables with strong information correlations are interconnected. Various methods have been developed to construct graph data from independent variables. Some approaches construct adjacency matrices using first-order distances15–17, which intuitively determine whether two nodes are directly connected. Others employ second-order or higher-order distances18, in which two nodes (e.g., A and B) are considered connected if A can reach B indirectly via an intermediate node (e.g., C), even if no direct edge exists. However, these methods are limited in that they fail to capture the varying strengths of relationships between nodes. Regardless of whether the relationship is weak or strong, the graph neural network assigns a value of 1, which oversimplifies the spatial associations. Some researchers have proposed constructing graphs based on correlation coefficients19 to preserve as much information as possible between nodes. However, while effective for linear relationships, this method is less capable of capturing nonlinear dependencies. To address this issue, this study introduces mutual information theory, which effectively quantifies both linear and nonlinear associations between variables.

Mutual information quantifies the dependence between two random variables and captures nonlinear relationships. It is widely used to reveal shared information between discrete variables. Given two discrete signals X and Y, their mutual information I(X; Y) is calculated as shown in Eq. (1).

In this context, P(X, Y) denotes the joint probability distribution of variables X and Y, while P(X) and P(Y) represent their respective marginal distributions.

If I(X; Y) equals zero, the variables are independent, indicating no shared information. If I(X; Y) reaches its maximum, defined as min(H(X), H(Y)), where H(X) and H(Y) are the entropies of X and Y, respectively, it implies that one variable is entirely determined by the other. If I(X; Y) lies between zero and its maximum, it reflects partial dependency between the variables—the higher the mutual information, the stronger the correlation.

In constructing the graph for the GCN, pairwise mutual information (MI) determines the presence and strength of edges. An MI of zero indicates complete independence and results in no edge; any positive MI signifies a latent association, with the edge weight proportional to the MI magnitude. Long short-term memory neural network

LSTM are a specialized type of recurrent neural networks (RNNs). Unlike traditional RNN architectures, LSTM incorporates a gating mechanism that selectively retains or discards information via input, forget, and output gates. This mechanism helps stabilize gradients during backpropagation, effectively mitigating the vanishing and exploding gradient problems often observed in standard RNNs. Furthermore, this flexibility enables LSTM to perform effectively in extracting nonlinear features. As a result, LSTM has been widely applied in natural language processing, machine translation, and time series forecasting. The LSTM architecture follows a sequential structure. Its hidden layers comprise multiple memory blocks, each of which contains several memory cells. Each memory cell includes a cell state and three gates. Graph Convolutional Neural Network.

Researchers initially proposed Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to handle graph data structures; however, CNNs effectively process only Euclidean data and are unsuitable for non-Euclidean spatial structures. To overcome this limitation, Kipf20 introduced GCN, which excel in processing non-Euclidean structures like social and traffic networks. The core principle of GCN is to update each node’s feature representation by aggregating information from its neighbors. This is achieved by performing convolution operations on the features of the node and20 its neighbors, guided by the adjacency matrix, to generate new feature representations.

Spatio-temporal deep neural networks based on mutual information.

To capture the temporal dynamics and regional correlations in infection sequence data after public health emergencies, this study proposes a hybrid model combining GCN and LSTM. This approach leverages GCN’s ability to capture local spatial features and LSTM’s strength in modeling long-term temporal dependencies in sequential data. Moreover, to better represent node closeness, the adjacency matrix is constructed using mutual information between sequences instead of relying solely on geographic adjacency.

The overall architecture of the proposed model is shown in Fig. 3. The model comprises three main modules: the mutual information matrix, the GCN, and the LSTM. The workflow for training the model is as follows. First, in the data preprocessing stage, missing values are imputed via linear interpolation to maintain continuity in the time series. Subsequently, the dataset is chronologically divided into training and testing sets, and time series samples are generated for model input using a sliding window method. During training, the data are first processed through a mutual information computation module to quantify associations between spatial units, producing a mutual information matrix. This matrix, along with the original data, is then fed into GCN. The GCN layers propagate and aggregate features to capture spatial dependencies and regional transmission trends, generating new feature representations for each spatial unit at every time step. The resulting spatiotemporal feature sequences are then fed into LSTM network to model temporal dynamics. The LSTM layers effectively capture both long- and short-term patterns, enabling joint extraction of spatiotemporal features. Finally, the extracted deep spatiotemporal features are passed through a fully connected layer to generate predictions for the target horizon, thereby forecasting future infection trends for each region.

Evaluation criteria and experimental setup

To assess predictive performance, this study uses Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R²) as evaluation metrics. MAE measures the average absolute difference between predicted and actual values. RMSE reflects model stability but is sensitive to large errors. R² indicates how well the predictions fit the actual values. Lower MAE and RMSE values correspond to more accurate predictions and better model performance, while higher R² values indicate a superior fit.

Model parameter setting

This study’s experiments were implemented using the PyTorch framework and conducted on a system running Python 3.6 with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-8250U CPU (8 cores) clocked at approximately 1.8 GHz for both training and prediction. The training was performed over 500 epochs. Hyperparameter optimization during training was primarily conducted using the Optuna framework. The search spaces for batch size, learning rate, and hidden layer dimensions were set to [16, 32, 64], 1e−4 to 1e−2, and 32 to 128, respectively. Early stopping was applied to avoid overfitting. This is mainly because the GCN model captures only spatial dependencies and does not account for temporal trends. Discussion

This study evaluates the performance of the proposed model using two representative datasets from China and Europe. Experimental results show that, on the European dataset, the predictive performance of the models follows the order: GCN < ARIMA < Seq2Seq < Transformer < BiLSTM < LSTM < STGCN < LSTM–GCN < the proposed model.

On the Chinese dataset, considering MAE, RMSE, and R² jointly, the overall predictive performance of the models is ranked as follows: GCN < Transformer < STGCN < BiLSTM < Seq2Seq < LSTM–GCN < LSTM < the proposed model. While achieving a level of goodness of fit comparable to that of the best-performing model (R² = 0.8854), the proposed model attains a substantially lower MAE of 42.56 than all benchmark models, corresponding to an approximate reduction of 27.74% relative to the second-best model; its RMSE of 133.93 also remains at a competitive level. Overall, the proposed model strikes an optimal balance among prediction accuracy, stability, and trend interpretability, thereby demonstrating superior comprehensive performance.

To account for these observed differences, the study further examines their underlying causes. During the epidemic spread, the number of infections exhibits a clear dynamic trend over time. The GCN model excels at capturing spatial relationships among nodes but lacks sufficient ability to model temporal sequences. Consequently, it fails to capture the epidemic’s temporal progression, leading to the poorest performance. The ARIMA model performs slightly better than the GCN model but worse than the LSTM model, which effectively captures long-term dependencies in sequences. This is because ARIMA is better suited for stationary data and assumes a linear relationship between input features and predicted values. It cannot capture nonlinear features caused by abrupt sequence changes from lockdowns, vaccinations, and policy shifts21,22, whereas LSTM can effectively model both linear and nonlinear patterns23. Compared to ARIMA and LSTM, which only capture temporal trends, the LSTM–GCN model achieves lower error rates and better overall performance24. This is because ARIMA and LSTM consider only the temporal dynamics of infection cases within individual regions and neglect spatial correlations between regions. Consequently, they fail to effectively account for the impact of interregional population movements and medical referrals on epidemic spread, resulting in increased prediction errors. This further underscores the importance of capturing spatial dependencies. The LSTM–GCN model that incorporates the mutual information matrix to measure interregional connectivity further improves predictive performance, as it can more effectively capture hidden spatial dependencies between nonadjacent regions. This demonstrates that including mutual information provides a significant advantage in capturing spatiotemporal correlations. LSTM–GCN relies exclusively on adjacency matrices to represent spatial relationships and overlooks the potential effects of population mobility and transportation development, limiting its predictive accuracy25. Therefore, this study introduces a mutual information component derived from the raw data to capture interdependencies among spatial units, thereby enhancing LSTM–GCN inputs and improving predictive performance.

The results in Tables 3 and 4 show that the proposed model performs better on the Chinese dataset than on the European dataset. At the early stage of the outbreak, which coincided with the Spring Festival in China, largescale population movements facilitated the rapid spread of the virus. China promptly implemented strict control measures, effectively cutting off transmission chains and creating distinct stagespecific characteristics of the COVID-19 epidemic. In the later stages, the data patterns became clearer26,27, which made them easier for the proposed model to capture. The later data of Hubei also show a clear pattern, as also can be seen in Fig. 6. In contrast, European countries adopted relatively loose control measures and experienced frequent cross-border population movements, resulting in unstable epidemic trends and greater prediction challenges for the proposed model.

Nevertheless, this study is subject to certain limitations. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the proposed model shows notable deviations at several peak points. Specifically, at approximately the 100th time step (corresponding to February 11, 2022), Germany experienced a sharp surge in newly confirmed daily cases, primarily driven by the emergence of the Omicron BA.2 subvariant. Because the proposed model is fundamentally built on learning patterns from historical data, it cannot adequately capture the effects of viral mutations and thus has difficulty providing accurate predictions at extreme points triggered by such abrupt events28–30.

Conclusions

This paper presents a model for predicting the spread of sudden public health events by integrating spatiotemporal correlation features, enabling accurate epidemic trend forecasting across regions. The proposed spatiotemporal deep learning model effectively captures complex temporal dependencies within regional time series and spatial correlations across areas, considering how trends in related regions affect the target region. Experimental results on public COVID-19 datasets confirm the model’s effectiveness and robustness. On the European dataset, the proposed model reduced MAE and RMSE by 9.22% and 4.24%, respectively, compared to the second-best model, LSTM–GCN. On the Hubei Province dataset, the proposed model demonstrates strong performance across most metrics, with its RMSE ranking as the second-best among all compared models, while the MAE shows a significant reduction of approximately 27.74% compared to the baseline BiLSTM model.

Analysis of the experimental results indicates that this study still has room for improvement. Future work could take viral mutations into account and utilize predictive models to estimate mutation rates, thereby overcoming the limitations of datadriven models in capturing these characteristics. Additionally, future research could incorporate factors such as public health interventions, population density, and age distribution to assess their impact on disease spread. Moreover, exploring alternative methods for constructing adjacency matrices could further enhance the representation of graph data.
