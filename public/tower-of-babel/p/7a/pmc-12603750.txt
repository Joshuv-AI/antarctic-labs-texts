Endobronchial aspergilloma mimicking metastatic recurrence in a patient with metastatic sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma: a case report
Authors: Farhan Ishaq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 71-year-old male patient with metastatic sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma developed persistent cough while showing a hypermetabolic endobronchial mass on PET/CT which suggested possible cancer recurrence. The bronchoscopy examination showed a pale lobulated lesion which, histopathological examination confirmed to be endobronchial aspergilloma. The patient received antifungal treatment which resulted in both clinical recovery and reduction of the lesion. The case demonstrates how fungal airway colonization can present as metastatic cancer thus demonstrating the need for bronchoscopy and tissue diagnosis when evaluating PET-avid endobronchial masses in oncologic patients.Keywords: Aspergillus spp,9,9; Endobronchial obstruction; Endobronchial aspergilloma; Aspergilloma; Renal metastasis; Malignancy

Introduction

Aspergillus spp. are ubiquitous environmental molds capable of causing a range of pulmonary diseases, ranging from allergic reactions to invasive infections, depending on the host's immune status and structural integrity of the airways. Among these, endobronchial aspergilloma (EA) is an exceptionally rare manifestation characterized by non-invasive colonization of the bronchial lumen by Aspergillus, forming a mass-like fungal ball. Presentation and chief complaint can vary highly in these patients' diagnosis and can be due to incidental finding. Common complaints and signs of presentation include shortness of breath, hemoptysis, hypoxia, dyspnea, productive cough, blood-tinged sputum. EA may mimic endobronchial malignancy; given the nonspecific symptoms can obscure diagnosis, particularly in patients with a prior history of malignancy.

This case report presents a unique case of EA diagnosed via bronchoscopy in a patient with metastatic sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma (SRCC), a highly aggressive and uncommon malignancy. The bronchial lesion initially raised concern for recurrence of malignancy due to its FDG uptake on PET/CT. However, histopathological examination revealed fungal colonization consistent with Aspergillus spp. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis for endobronchial masses in immunocompromised or oncologic patients and underscores the diagnostic value of bronchoscopy and histopathology in distinguishing infectious mimics from malignancy.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old male who presented with a persistent dry cough for 3 months. He had been seen seven weeks prior and was noted to have a left lower lobe mass, that was metabolically active on PET/CT Scan. Past medical history included SRCC (stage 4) with metastatic bone cancer in multiple regions including mediastinum, humerus and sacrum. He was seen nine months prior for right humeral fracture caused by the metastatic SRCC; He was treated with intramedullary nail fixation, postoperative radiation therapy and sunitinib; CT scan of the chest at that time incidentally showed multiple pulmonary nodules, multiple enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes and left hilar lymph nodes with central necrosis (consistent with metastatic disease). Chest wall subcutaneous nodules were also present. Pulmonary metastases involved the left lower lobe, and the patient underwent surgical treatment followed by bronchial stent placement in the left lower lobe bronchial tree to address post-obstructive or post-surgical airway narrowing. He also had a history of gastroesophageal reflux disease, mild aortic regurgitation, right ventricular dilation, ascending aortic aneurysm (AAA) and bilateral partial nephrectomies. The patient previously worked for several decades in a supervisory role in construction involving roofing, with potential exposure to chemical compounds such as polyvinyl chloride. He retired several years prior to presentation.

Differential diagnosis

Metastatic malignancy was the initial suspected diagnosis given the advanced cancer and existence of metastasis however, during the time of EA diagnosis most of the metastatic lesions of the left lower lobe had resolved with surgeries and the use of a stent. Malignancy of endobronchial origin was another possibility although these are rare and less probable with the given clinical scenario. Pulmonary nodule was another possible diagnosis; foreign body may have been another possible diagnosis however this was less likely given the chronic and enlarging mass. Endobronchial aspergilloma was another differential due to the patient's immunocompromised status and metastatic disease. Bronchoscopy, histology and imaging results further validated this diagnosis.

Treatment

Once EA was diagnosed, the patient was treated with voriconazole 300 mg twice daily and had a gradual improvement of his symptoms over eight weeks. Two months later, he developed an allergic dermatological reaction from voriconazole and was switched to isavuconazonium sulfate (per standard dosage), which was tolerated well. and continued treatment over the course of the next four months.

Outcome

Eight days After starting treatment with voriconazole and bronchoscopy, symptoms of dyspnea resolved however, overtime the patient reported feeling short of breath with exertion. Physical examination revealed clear lungs bilaterally. On follow up six months after diagnosis of EA, CT imaging demonstrated that the mass had decreased in size from 3.4 × 5.5 cm to 4.7 × 2.4 cm. (Fig. 4). Persistent atelectasis was also seen. New masses, ground glass opacities and nodules were seen in the right lung. At this point isavuconazonium sulfate was stopped and a follow up was scheduled in three months. Due to progression of advanced metastatic malignancy, he expired eight months after diagnosis of EA.

Discussion

Classification and definition

Aspergillus spp. are ubiquitous saprophytic molds present in soil, decomposing vegetation, and organic matter. They are opportunistic pathogens capable of producing a diverse range of respiratory syndromes, with the pattern of disease determined by the host's immune competence and the structural integrity of the lungs. According to the 2016 Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and ESCMID–ECMM–ERS guidelines, pulmonary aspergillosis is broadly categorized into allergic, chronic, and invasive forms [11]. These classifications are clinically useful because they reflect not only the pathophysiologic mechanisms but also the prognosis and therapeutic strategies required.

Although the guidelines do not explicitly describe endobronchial aspergilloma (EA), it is best conceptualized as a localized, non-invasive form of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis. The defining feature of EA is its confinement to the bronchial lumen without histologic evidence of tissue invasion. This intraluminal growth pattern distinguishes it from parenchymal aspergillomas, which develop within pre-existing pulmonary cavities, typically in the upper lobes. From an anatomic perspective, aspergillomas are sometimes classified by location into pulmonary, pleural, and endobronchial types [1]. In this context, EA is an uncommon but clinically significant variant, notable for its ability to mimic neoplastic lesions and thereby complicate diagnostic decision-making, especially in oncology patients.

Epidemiology and pathogenesis

EA is rare compared to other forms of pulmonary aspergillosis. Estimates suggest that only 5–10 % of airway Aspergillus infections present without invasive parenchymal disease [2]. the published literature consists mainly of isolated case reports and small series, which collectively describe EA as occurring most often in patients with underlying structural lung abnormalities such as healed post-tuberculous cavities, bronchiectasis, emphysematous bullae, or surgical resection sites.

Malignancy—both primary lung cancer and metastatic disease—is another well-recognized risk factor, not only because tumor masses can cause bronchial distortion and impaired clearance, but also because oncologic treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy compromise local mucosal immunity [[3], [4], [5]]. Additional associations include prolonged corticosteroid use, hematologic malignancies, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, foreign body aspiration, and prior airway instrumentation such as stent placement or bronchoscopic interventions [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

The pathogenesis of EA involves colonization of devitalized or inflamed bronchial mucosa by inhaled Aspergillus conidia. When the bronchial wall is compromised—by ischemia, mechanical trauma, or chronic inflammation—local clearance mechanisms, including ciliary function and mucosal immunity, are impaired. Over time, the fungus proliferates within the airway lumen, incorporating mucus, necrotic debris, and inflammatory cells to form a mass-like lesion or “fungus ball.” In cases with pre-existing airway stents, biofilm formation on the stent surface may provide an ideal substrate for fungal colonization. In our patient, the prior combination of segmental resection, stent placement, and recurrent bronchial inflammation created an anatomically altered and immunologically vulnerable airway segment. His occupational history, which included long-term exposure to synthetic chemicals such as polyvinyl chloride, may have contributed to chronic airway irritation, although this remains speculative due to the lack of direct causal evidence in the literature.

Clinical and radiologic features

The clinical manifestations of EA are heterogeneous. Some patients are entirely asymptomatic, with the lesion detected incidentally during imaging for unrelated reasons. Others present with cough, hemoptysis, dyspnea, or wheezing. Hemoptysis is a particularly important presentation, as it may be minor or massive depending on the degree of vascular involvement. In advanced disease, mechanical obstruction of the airway can lead to post-obstructive pneumonia, mucus plugging, segmental atelectasis, and in severe cases, acute respiratory failure [1]. Importantly, EA is not restricted to the immunocompromised; cases have been described in immunocompetent individuals with localized structural lung changes.

Radiologic features are often non-specific. On CT imaging, EA usually appears as an endoluminal soft-tissue mass, sometimes associated with distal consolidation, volume loss, or collapse. In our case, PET/CT demonstrated intense FDG avidity, a finding most often associated with malignant neoplasms. However, high metabolic uptake is increasingly recognized in infectious and inflammatory conditions, including aspergillosis, where activated immune cells and inflammatory cytokine production lead to increased glucose metabolism within the lesion [7]. This imaging overlap can lead to misdiagnosis, especially in patients with a known history of malignancy where any new lesion is assumed to be recurrent cancer.

Diagnosis and differential

Because imaging findings are not pathognomonic, bronchoscopy remains the diagnostic gold standard. It allows direct visualization of the lesion, targeted sampling for histopathology, and, in some cases, therapeutic debridement. Bronchoscopic appearance can vary but typically includes a lobulated, friable, pale or yellow mass, sometimes with a mucoid or necrotic surface [8]. In our patient, the lesion was partially covered with clear white mucus and did not cause complete airway obstruction, but its appearance raised concern for tumor recurrence.

Histopathologic examination is essential for diagnosis, revealing septate hyphae with acute-angle branching consistent with Aspergillus spp. Cytology helps exclude malignancy, and while fungal cultures can provide supportive evidence and species identification, they are frequently negative, as in many published EA cases. The differential diagnosis for an endobronchial mass is broad and includes primary bronchogenic carcinoma, metastatic tumors, lymphoma, carcinoid tumors, post-tuberculous fibrostenosis, and infections such as tuberculosis, histoplasmosis, and actinomycosis. Foreign body granulomas may also present similarly. In oncology patients, the foremost concern is distinguishing EA from tumor recurrence or a new primary lung malignancy, as management strategies differ fundamentally.

Management

No standardized treatment algorithm exists for EA due to its rarity. Management decisions are typically individualized, taking into account patient comorbidities, extent of disease, and symptom burden. Reported therapeutic approaches include systemic antifungal therapy, bronchoscopic removal or debridement, and, in select cases, surgical resection. Antifungal therapy is most commonly initiated with voriconazole, which is considered first-line for most forms of aspergillosis, but alternatives such as itraconazole, amphotericin B, and the newer triazole isavuconazonium sulfate have been used successfully [3]. Bronchoscopic interventions, including forceps excision, cryotherapy, and lavage, may be useful for debulking obstructive lesions or removing friable fungal masses [10,11]. Surgical resection is generally reserved for patients with significant complications such as massive hemoptysis, bronchopleural fistula, or recurrent infection refractory to medical therapy, but feasibility is often limited by underlying lung reserve and comorbid conditions [2,3,12].

In our patient, systemic therapy with voriconazole was initiated but discontinued due to a dermatologic adverse reaction. He was subsequently transitioned to isavuconazonium sulfate, a broad-spectrum triazole with improved tolerability and fewer drug–drug interactions, which resulted in partial regression of the lesion. Unfortunately, antifungal therapy was ultimately discontinued due to progression of metastatic disease in the contralateral lung, shifting the focus to palliative care. This course illustrates two important points: first, antifungal stewardship in complex oncology patients requires careful attention to drug tolerance and interactions; and second, the outcome in EA often depends more on the primary disease than on the fungal infection itself.

Prognosis and case significance

The prognosis of EA is variable. In otherwise healthy or immunocompetent individuals, isolated EA may respond well to antifungal therapy and/or bronchoscopic intervention, with low recurrence rates once the underlying predisposing factor is addressed. In contrast, patients with advanced malignancy, severe immunosuppression, or extensive structural lung disease often have a prognosis determined primarily by their underlying condition. EA in such settings may function more as a clinical complicating factor than as an independent determinant of mortality.

This case is significant for several reasons. It represents a rare occurrence of EA in a patient with sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma, an aggressive and uncommon malignancy not previously highlighted in the EA literature. The lesion demonstrated high FDG uptake on PET/CT, closely resembling a malignant recurrence and thereby creating a substantial diagnostic challenge. The patient's history of prior surgical resection and bronchial stenting likely predisposed to airway colonization by altering bronchial anatomy and impairing mucosal defenses. Occupational exposure to synthetic industrial chemicals may have contributed to chronic airway irritation, although this remains speculative. Finally, the clinical course was notable for a dermatologic adverse reaction to voriconazole necessitating a switch to isavuconazonium sulfate, underscoring the need for individualized antifungal regimens.

Overall, this case reinforces the importance of including EA in the differential diagnosis of PET-avid endobronchial lesions, particularly in patients with a history of malignancy. It highlights the diagnostic value of bronchoscopy and histopathology in differentiating fungal colonization from neoplasia, the potential for imaging misinterpretation, and the clinical necessity of tailoring antifungal therapy to patient-specific tolerability and pharmacologic considerations.
