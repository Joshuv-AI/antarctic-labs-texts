The vulnerable role of empathy in children who live in conflict zones
Authors: Eliya Kab, Shir Porat-Butman, Moriya Rachmani, Einat Levy-Gigi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective: Children in conflict zones face repeated and cumulative trauma exposure, increasing their risk for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Despite the critical role of empathy in coping with trauma, research on how children’s cognitive and affective empathy relates to their psychological adjustment remains limited. Adult studies suggest that cognitive empathy may support better coping, whereas affective empathy could increase vulnerability to PTSD symptoms. However, it remains unclear whether this differential pattern holds in children and how it interacts with levels of trauma exposure. This study investigated the moderating role of cognitive and affective empathy in the relationship between cumulative trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms in children.Method: Sixty-two children (Mean age = 9.64, SD = 1.15) from regions with varying proximity to conflict zones completed self-report questionnaires to evaluate cumulative trauma exposure, empathy, and anxiety, and were interviewed to assess PTSD symptoms.Results: While cognitive empathy moderated the relationship between cumulative trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms, it did not serve as a protective factor as hypothesised. Specifically, children with low cognitive empathy demonstrated a positive relationship between trauma exposure and PTSD symptom severity, whereas those with high cognitive empathy exhibited consistently high PTSD symptoms, regardless of exposure level. Affective empathy did not moderate this relationship but was positively associated with PTSD.Conclusions: High cognitive and affective empathy emerged as vulnerability factors for PTSD, potentially due to developmental challenges in its adaptive use during childhood. These findings question the traditional view of cognitive empathy as universally protective, highlighting the need to monitor its role throughout development.KEYWORDS: Children, cumulative trauma exposure, PTSD, affective empathy, cognitive empathy, conflict zone

Introduction

Approximately 60% of children worldwide are exposed to trauma during their childhood (Claxton et al., 2021; Copeland et al., 2018; Thielemann et al., 2022). Such traumatic exposure may cast its shadow for extended periods (Copeland et al., 2018; Qin et al., 2024; Wilson-Genderson et al., 2022), affecting the children’s mental, social, emotional, and physical development as well as their overall well-being (Gawee¸da et al., 2020; Karatzias et al., 2020; Nelson et al., 2020; Skandsen et al., 2023). Exposure to childhood trauma is a potent risk factor for multiple psychopathologies (Copeland et al., 2018; Lewis et al., 2019; McLaughlin et al., 2020). One of the most common ramifications is the development of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Hoppen et al., 2023; Karatzias et al., 2020; Kolaitis, 2017; Woolgar et al., 2022). PTSD symptoms can intensify over time as exposure becomes repetitive, cumulative and integrated into daily life (Anjum et al., 2023; Bisson et al., 2021; Haim-Nachum & Levy-Gigi, 2021a; Levy-Gigi et al., 2023; Levy-Gigi, Bonanno, et al., 2016).

One factor that has the potential to influence the psychological effects of cumulative trauma exposure is empathy, which includes two well-studied dimensions: Cognitive empathy, the extent to which a person understands why the other person is distressed, and affective empathy, the extent to which a person feels the emotional state of other suffering people (Bray et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021; Overgaauw et al., 2017; Shamay-Tsoory et al., 2009). Research on adults has found that cognitive empathy modulates emotional responses (Lei et al., 2019; Thompson et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021) and helps maintain emotional distance from the suffering of others (Dekel et al., 2018; Juczyński et al., 2022). In contrast, affective empathy is frequently linked to heightened negative emotions in distressing situations (Dekel et al., 2018; Guhn et al., 2020; Juczyński et al., 2022; Lei et al., 2019; Thompson et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021). Indeed, recent studies in various adult populations have shown that during an ongoing war, higher levels of affective empathy impair coping with cumulative trauma (Shamai-Leshem et al., 2025). In contrast, higher levels of cognitive empathy promote adaptive behaviours and well-being (Porat-Butman et al., under review).

Studies on children have demonstrated that from an early age, both cognitive empathy and affective empathy are essential for social success (Decety et al., 2016; Decety & Holvoet, 2021; De Waal & Preston, 2017; Sanchez, 2021). Specifically, these types of empathy are associated with improved interpersonal skills, enhanced conflict resolution, and a stronger ability to build trust with others (Qin et al., 2022; Simon & Nader-Grosbois, 2021; Spataro et al., 2020; Yin & Wang, 2023). Beyond their role in everyday social interactions, studies have also indicated that cognitive and affective empathy contribute differently to children’s responses under stress – a pattern that mirrors findings in adults. While affective empathy may heighten emotional distress, cognitive empathy is linked to more regulated responses and adaptive behaviour (Bray et al., 2021; Fredrick et al., 2020). However, no study has compared the relationship between cognitive and affective empathy and mental health in children exposed to cumulative trauma.

The current study aimed to test for the first time the interactive effect of cumulative trauma exposure and cognitive and affective empathy on the tendency to develop PTSD symptoms in children. It has several innovative aspects. First, whereas most studies have explored the potential effects of childhood trauma retrospectively (e.g. Doba et al., 2022; Klinger-König et al., 2024; Thumfart et al., 2022), the current study examined these possible effects as they unfold, focusing on a unique population of children living in varying proximity to a conflict zone, where exposure to traumatic incidents is an integral part of their daily lives (e.g. missile attacks, incendiary balloons). Second, most studies tested exposure to a single traumatic event, or treated trauma as a binary variable (exposed vs. unexposed) (e.g. Im et al., 2022; McLaughlin et al., 2020; Skandsen et al., 2023). The current study assessed cumulative trauma exposure as a continuous variable, enabling the investigation of its interactive effect with empathy on PTSD symptom severity. Third, although many studies have investigated the relationship between cognitive and affective empathy and functioning (Simon & Nader-Grosbois, 2021; Spataro et al., 2020; Thompson et al., 2021), it was rarely tested in the context of trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms. Fourth, unlike many studies that rely exclusively on self-report questionnaires, here PTSD symptoms were assessed using semi-structured interviews (e.g. Grant et al., 2020; Powers et al., 2020; Roberts et al., 2021). This method minimises potential biases and enables a more nuanced understanding of the children’s experiences.

Based on the literature on adults, we hypothesised that affective empathy would be associated with higher levels of PTSD symptoms across cumulative trauma exposure. In contrast, high levels of cognitive empathy would be associated with lower levels of PTSD symptoms across cumulative trauma exposure, and low levels of cognitive empathy would be associated with increased PTSD symptoms as a function of cumulative trauma exposure.

Methods

Participants

The required sample size was determined through G*Power software (Faul et al., 2007). The analysis revealed that a sample size of 59 children was needed to achieve the desired power of 0.85, with an alpha (α) error probability set at 0.05. To mitigate potential issues related to data loss or participant dropouts, the study included 62 elementary school children (50.8% girls; Mean Age = 9.64, SD = 1.15). The children were recruited from different geographical regions in Israel, which, while varying in proximity to the border, shared key characteristics, including similar socio-economic status and ethnic background, but were located at varying distances from the border. All participating children attended the state education system, with similar representation from secular and traditional backgrounds in all regions. See Table 1 for a detailed description of the sample. The university ethics committee approved the study in accordance with the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval number: 65). We received a written consent form signed by both parents and their children.

Measures

The Cumulative Traumatic Exposure Questionnaire (CUTE; Levy-Gigi, Richter-Levin et al., 2016) is a 10-item measure designed to assess cumulative trauma exposure by determining the frequency of encounters with common traumatic events (e.g. I saw others physically injured as a result of a terror attack). Children rate their exposure in the past month on a 1 (‘never’) to 6 (‘more than once a day’) scale. (Cronbach’s α = .87).

The Empathy Questionnaire for Children and Adolescents (EmQue-CA; Overgaauw et al., 2017) is a self-report measure consisting of 14 items across three scales: (1) affective empathy, measuring the extent to which someone is feeling the distress of others: (2) cognitive empathy, measuring the extent to which someone understands why others are in distress; (3) intention to comfort, measuring the extent to which someone wants to help distressed others. In the current study, we used only the first two scales. The children rate whether the empathy-related descriptions are true for them on a Likert scale from 1 (‘not true’) to 3 (‘true’). (Cronbach’s α = .81).

The Child PTSD Symptom Scale (CPSS-5; Foa et al., 2018) is a 20-item semi-structured interview of post-traumatic stress for children and youth ages 8–18, aligned with the DSM-5. The children were asked about the frequency of each symptom on a Likert scale from 0 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘six or more times a week/almost always’) (Cronbach’s α = .92).

The screen for child anxiety-related disorders (SCARED; Birmaher et al., 1999) is a self-report measure consisting of 41-item inventory designed to assess anxiety levels in children. The inventory includes six subscales: panic disorder, generalised anxiety, separation anxiety, social anxiety, and school avoidance. The children rate their answers on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘Not true’) to 2 (‘Very true’) (Cronbach’s α = .91).

Statistical analyses

The data were analyzed using SPSS version 28 statistical software. Descriptive statistics and correlations were calculated between all the study variables. Moderation analyses were conducted using Hayes’s PROCESS macro (Hayes, 2018). Following our predictions, and due to power constraints, we conducted two separate moderation models – one for each type of empathy – while statistically controlling for the other. This approach allowed us to assess the unique contribution of each empathy type above and beyond the influence of the other (see Haim-Nachum et al., 2022; Haim-Nachum & Levy-Gigi, 2021a; Shamai-Leshem et al., 2025 for a similar approach)

Results

Zero-order correlations of the study variables

Table 2 depicts zero-order correlations between age, traumatic exposure, distance from the border, cognitive and affective empathy, PTSD, and anxiety symptoms. The results revealed a significant negative correlation between distance from the border and trauma exposure, indicating that children living closer to the border are likely to report higher levels of trauma exposure. In addition, there were positive correlations between trauma exposure and both PTSD and anxiety symptom severity, suggesting that higher levels of trauma exposure are associated with increased severity of these symptoms. There was also a negative correlation between age and both PTSD and anxiety symptoms, indicating that the younger the child, the higher the likelihood of developing clinical symptoms. Finally, affective, but not cognitive, empathy was positively correlated with PTSD symptoms, suggesting that higher affective empathy is associated with more severe PTSD symptoms. Preliminary analyses showed that gender was not significantly related to any of the main study variables and was therefore not included in the subsequent models.

Cumulative exposure, empathy, and PTSD

To examine the moderation role of cognitive and affective empathy in the relationship between cumulative traumatic exposure and the emergence of post-traumatic symptoms in children, we employed Hayes’ moderation model (Hayes et al., 2017) with 5,000 bootstrap samples. We conducted two analyses, one for cognitive and one for affective empathy. In each analysis, trauma exposure, cognitive/affective empathy, and PTSD symptoms were treated as independent, moderator, and dependent variables, respectively. Age and anxiety significantly correlated with PTSD; hence, they were included as control variables in the analyses. Finally, to detect the unique effect of each empathy type, we controlled for affective empathy in the first analysis and for cognitive empathy in the second analysis.

The first analysis assessing the moderating role of cognitive empathy was significant (F(5,55) = 12.96, p < .001, η²p = .59) (Table 3). As predicted, core analyses revealed a significant positive main effect of trauma exposure, indicating that greater exposure is associated with increased PTSD symptom severity. In addition, contrary to our hypothesis, there was a significant positive main effect of cognitive empathy, indicating that higher levels of cognitive empathy are associated with increased PTSD symptom severity. Most importantly, there was significant trauma exposure by cognitive empathy interaction (F(1,55) = 9.54, p = .003, η²p = .15) (Figure 1). This interaction accounted for 7.19% of the variance in PTSD symptom severity. To interpret it, we computed bootstrapping confidence intervals (95%) evaluating the magnitude of the relationship between cumulative trauma exposure and PTSD symptom severity for children with low (–1 SD) and high (+1 SD) cognitive empathy. The results revealed that for children with low cognitive empathy, there was a significant positive association between trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms (t(55) = 4.22, p < .001, η²p = .24). Hence, greater traumatic exposure was associated with greater PTSD symptom severity. On the other hand, in children with high cognitive empathy, levels of PTSD symptoms remained high, independent of their level of traumatic exposure (t(55) = −.14, p = .89). These results indicate that increased cognitive empathy may serve as a vulnerability factor in trauma-exposed children. The second analysis, assessing the moderating role of affective empathy, was also significant (F(5,55) = 10.82, p < .001, η²p = .52). However, there were no significant main effects (t(55) = 1.28, p = .20; t(61) = 1.11, p = .27, for exposure and affective empathy respectively) nor significant trauma exposure by affective empathy interaction (F(1,55) = 1.39, p = .24) (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study examined the moderating role of cognitive and affective empathy in the relationship between cumulative trauma exposure and the tendency to develop PTSD symptoms in children living in various proximity to conflict zones. Simple correlations revealed a significant negative association between distance from the border and exposure to trauma, indicating that children living closer to the border are more likely to experience traumatic incidents. This finding adds to previous studies showing that proximity to a conflict zone increases trauma exposure (Levi-Belz et al., 2024; Sateemae et al., 2022). In addition, there was a significant positive association between exposure to trauma and PTSD symptom severity. Whereas it is well-known that trauma exposure increases the risk of developing PTSD in children (e.g. Hoppen et al., 2023; Kolaitis, 2017; Woolgar et al., 2022), the current finding extends this understanding by showing that the frequency of exposure to various traumatic incidents across time is associated with symptom severity. Finally, in line with our prediction, there was a positive correlation between affective, but not cognitive – empathy and PTSD symptom severity. These results add to growing evidence suggesting that affective empathy acts as a vulnerability factor in adverse situations, increasing the risk of developing psychopathology (Brown et al., 2021; Guhn et al., 2020; Porat-Butman et al., under review; Thompson et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021).

Most importantly, cognitive, but not affective – empathy played a significant role in the relationship between cumulative trauma exposure and the tendency to develop PTSD symptoms. Specifically, children with lower levels of cognitive empathy showed the expected positive relationship between trauma exposure and PTSD symptom severity. Hence, greater exposure was linked to increased symptom severity. In contrast, and opposed to our prediction, children with higher levels of cognitive empathy had consistently higher PTSD symptoms, regardless of their trauma exposure level. Hence, even lower levels of cumulative trauma were associated with heightened symptom severity. These results suggest that cognitive empathy increases vulnerability to developing PTSD in children. This is especially surprising given the growing evidence in adult studies, suggesting that cognitive empathy is associated with better coping in adverse situations (Lei et al., 2019; Thompson et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2021).

One possible explanation for these findings may be linked to cognitive development during childhood. In general, cognitive skills develop in stages, with different aspects of cognition maturing at varying ages (Battista et al., 2018; Demetriou et al., 2021; Nazareth et al., 2018; Rabindran & Madanagopal, 2020). Within this framework, cognitive empathy evolves gradually between the ages of 4 and 17, with age accounting for 33.5% to 39.1% of the variance in cognitive empathy during this period (Schwenck et al., 2014). Hence, the children in our sample have not yet developed a fully mature form of cognitive empathy. Moreover, in their developmental stage, cognitive empathy may lack the emotional boundaries between the self and others (Tei & Fujino, 2023) that are crucial for processing traumatic events effectively (Chiu et al., 2016; Greenberg et al., 2018; Regehr et al., 2002). In such conditions, understanding why other people are distressed is not beneficial, as it may also reflect on one’s own self-perception, impairing coping mechanisms and potentially increasing PTSD symptoms. Future research may aim to explore the moderating effect of cognitive empathy in older children and adolescents to see whether it develops over time and can be used adaptively. In addition, conducting a longitudinal study that tracks the development of cognitive empathy during childhood and adolescence, specifically focusing on exposure to trauma and post-traumatic symptoms, will provide important insights into the developmental stages of cognitive empathy and its possible evolvement as a protective factor.

An alternative explanation for our results may relate to the significant burden of cumulative trauma exposure, which may impair the ability to use cognitive resources adaptively (Barczyk et al., 2023; Levy-Gigi et al., 2014; Majer et al., 2010). Although there is limited research on the effects of cumulative trauma exposure in children, studies have found that exposure to childhood trauma is associated with reduced cognitive functioning (Chen et al., 2020; Rosa et al., 2023; Su et al., 2019). In the current study, we not only tested children, who may face greater challenges in monitoring their cognitive resources adaptively but also focused on a sample with rapid and cumulative trauma exposure, which inherently places higher cognitive demands on individuals. Hence, developmental immaturity of cognitive empathy and the cognitive burden associated with cumulative trauma exposure may operate in tandem. Limited regulatory capacity at younger ages, coupled with high cognitive demands, may hinder the effective use of cognitive empathy and increase vulnerability to PTSD. Future studies could explore how cognitive load influences the ability to adaptively use cognitive empathy throughout childhood and adolescence, as well as the impact of cumulative trauma exposure on this ability.

Study limitations and future directions

Whereas the current study provides new and important insights into the role of cognitive and affective empathy in the relationship between cumulative trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms in children, it may suffer several limitations. First, in the current study, children were assessed at a single time point. The results may suggest that cognitive empathy during childhood is not well-developed and may need further maturity. However, such a design does not enable tracking of developmental changes in empathy and in the ability to use it adaptively. Future longitudinal studies may test children at various time points to examine how cognitive empathy develops and interacts with trauma exposure across time.

Additionally, while the current study found that the effects were specific to PTSD and did not yield significant associations with anxiety, future research could expand this line of inquiry to depression, which frequently co-occurs with PTSD. Moreover, although our findings suggest meaningful associations between empathy and trauma-related symptoms, the study was designed to detect medium-to-large effect sizes. Consequently, it is possible that smaller effects, particularly those involving more subtle or complex interactions, may have gone undetected. Future research with larger sample sizes may enable the detection of more modest effects, which could be both theoretically and clinically meaningful. Furthermore, while we focused on the frequency of trauma exposure, future studies could benefit from a more nuanced evaluation comparing the interactive effects of empathy and various types of traumatic exposure (e.g. direct vs. indirect, interpersonal vs. general) on the tendency to develop clinical symptoms.

Finally, our study has focused on the role of cognitive and affective empathy in the relationship between cumulative trauma exposure and the tendency to develop PTSD symptoms. However, similar to adults, it is possible that not only the level of empathy matters but also the ability to apply it flexibly (Haim-Nachum & Levy-Gigi, 2021b; Levy-Gigi et al., 2020). Future studies may aim to test, for example, whether the ability to flexibly apply empathy and shift between the perspectives of self and others is essential for coping with trauma adaptively.

Conclusions

In summary, the present findings highlight the substantial impact of cumulative trauma exposure on PTSD symptom severity in children living in conflict zones and challenge the assumption that cognitive empathy uniformly serves as a protective factor under such conditions. These results may inform early interventions aimed at monitoring and enhancing both cognitive empathy and emotion regulation capacities to strengthen resilience and mitigate PTSD symptoms in trauma-exposed children.
