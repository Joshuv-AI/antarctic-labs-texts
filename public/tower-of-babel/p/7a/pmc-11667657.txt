Additive inclusion in plastic life cycle assessments part I: Review of mechanical recycling studies
Authors: H. Logan, T. F. Astrup, A. Damgaard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the European Union transitions to the circular use of plastics, robust life cycle assessments are crucial in understanding and preparing for this new economy. Additives are essential to the production of all plastics but were reported as missing from life cycle assessments (LCAs) of plastic materials a decade ago. This study expands upon previous research by investigating if plastic additive impacts are now included in LCAs of recycled plastic materials or if they are still absent. In part I, we conduct a systematic literature review of 93 LCAs on plastics, including mechanical recycling pathways, and distinguish if plastic additive impacts are considered in (i) in‐text discussions and (ii) the life cycle inventories (LCIs) of each study. We then compare the types of additive inclusion within the corpus to ascertain whether author knowledge or data availability dictates additive inclusion in plastic LCAs. We find that data disclosure and detailed discussions of specific additive impacts are missing across the corpus due to poor transparency in LCI disclosure or overly generic disclosures of additives. The lack of detailed and transparent discussions and disclosure indicates that additive impacts are missing from LCAs of recycled plastic materials, leading to incomplete analyses of their impacts. Until addressed, such a gap may lead to inaccurate or incomplete circular plastic material LCAs. In part II, we assess the quality of generic disclosures and explore how database quality and transparency have contributed to additive omissions in LCAs.

INTRODUCTION

The transition to the circular plastic economy offers the ability to rethink plastic consumption models and is supported by both the European Union (EU) and the United Nations (UN) (EC 2020; UNEP, 2022). However, current frameworks overlook the management of plastic additives in this transition (UNEP, 2023) despite the integral role additives play in plastic production and functionality. These additives are essential for virgin and recycled plastic materials, as they increase functionality, reduce production costs, enhance durability, and contribute 0.01%–70% of the net weight of plastics (ECHA 2019; Hahladakis et al., 2018; OECD, 2019; Schwarz et al., 2021). With over 13,000 additives within the EU market, they are a vital consideration in the safety and environmental impacts of plastic use, recycling, and circulation (UNEP, 2023).

Nevertheless, despite EU regulations relating to high‐risk additives (ECHA, 2020; Horodytska et al., 2020a), additives in plastics are under‐reported and under‐regulated (Aurisano et al., 2021; Wiesinger et al., 2021; UNEP, 2023). Moreover, regulations that do exist for their use in virgin plastics are difficult to extend to recycled plastics due to the inconsistency in reporting and labeling currently in use (ECHA, 2020; Horodytska et al., 2020a; Wagner & Schlummer, 2020).

Today, mechanical recycling is the most common method for plastic circulation (Chea et al., 2023; Schyns & Shaver, 2021). Additives are particularly pertinent to mechanical recycling pathways because, unlike in thermal and chemical recycling, additives cannot be removed from plastic materials (Schwarz et al., 2021; Ügdüler et al., 2020). Additives in the mechanical recycling of plastics may: (i) disrupt the quality and recycling rate of plastic materials (Chea et al., 2023; Horodytska et al., 2020a; Eriksen et al., 2019; Johansen et al., 2022‐b) and (ii) be added during recycling to restabilize and enhance the quality of plastics (Gu et al., 2016; La Mantia 1998; Schyns & Shaver, 2021). Hence, assessing the inclusion and fate of additives is essential to creating accurate pictures of recycling and circular plastic futures (Friege, 2017; Führ et al., 2019). For further reading on plastic recycling technologies, refer to Johansen et al. (2022), Schwarz et al. (2021), and Wagner and Schlummer (2020).

The fate and environmental impact of additives have often been excluded from studies into the mechanical recycling of plastics. Life cycle assessments (LCAs) are one such toolbox of methods used to investigate the environmental impacts of products and systems (ISO 2006a, 2006b; EC JRC 2010). These studies have historically omitted additives from plastic studies (Bishop et al., 2021; van Oers et al., 2012; van der Voet et al., 2013; NIST, 2022). van Oers et al. (2012), for instance, reviewed 110 LCAs, finding that ∼25% included additives, and often these were substances of very high concern. In a follow‐up case study, the authors proposed that additive omissions occurred due to a lack of available data from LCA databases and knowledge gaps relating to additives outside of the plastics industry (van Oers & van der Voet, 2013).

Numerous efforts have been made to address additive knowledge gaps, all of which could be used to inform the environmental fate and impacts of additives. The OECD explored additive emissions from plastics in a specialty report (2009), and in 2019, this report was expanded to include emissions during the use life of selected plastics and included risk assessments for specific case studies (i.e., phthalates) (OECD, 2019). From 2016 to 2019, the Plastic Additives Initiative (PLASI) quantified the use risk of more than 400 plastic additives in the EU (ECHA, 2019). Aurisano et al. (2021) expanded on these efforts by combining this list with 35 regulatory lists for plastic additives, resulting in a database of 6000 additives used in the EU. Wiesinger et al. (2021) went further by including company disclosures; Chemical Abstract Services Registry numbers (CAS RNs); usage, tonnage, and hazard; and geographic and regulatory data in their database of more than 10,000 plastic additives in the EU (Wiesinger et al., 2021). The latter two of these articles were combined into a comprehensive report summarizing the need for increased regulation and reporting of additives and included a database of 13,000+ additives and 38,000+ known CAS RNs for these additives (UNEP, 2023).

In addition, the leaching of additives through contact and plastic degradation has been increasingly explored throughout the last decade in the human health and risk assessment literature (Maddela et al., 2023). These studies have all worked to fill the knowledge gap in terms of additive use and fate and have provided ample evidence and data to support an increase in these factors, as well as environmental impacts.

Despite all these efforts to fill knowledge and data gaps on plastic additives, recent reviews of LCAs have reported that additives are still missing in LCAs (Bishop et al., 2021; NIST, 2022). During the decade between van Oers et al. (2012) and these recent literature reviews, mechanical recycling has remained the dominant path for plastic recycling (Chea et al., 2023; Schyns & Shaver, 2021), which means that both virgin additives and those added during recycling remain important considerations in evaluating the impacts and quality of recycled plastics. Moreover, despite these omissions, LCAs have increased to forecast the environmental impacts and potential of circular scenarios for plastics (NIST, 2022) and to assist policy development for circular plastic futures (Sala et al. 2021). Such discoveries raise the question, if ample efforts have been made to fill knowledge gaps on additives in plastic lifecycles, why are additives still omitted from plastic LCAs relating to mechanical recycling, and how does the omission of these additives impact affect circular transition planning?

Previous literature reviews on the role of additives in plastic LCAs have primarily focused on the inclusion of relevant data in scientific publications. However, these reviews often overlook how authors address additives within the written discourse of scientific publications; the way in which they introduce, discuss, and problematize additives is a crucial indicator of knowledge gaps. Therefore, this study highlights both textual trends in scientific publications and data from LCAs to provide a nuanced context regarding why additive omissions endure despite growing awareness of their importance and life cycle impacts.

This study aims to analyze and contextualize omissions and inclusions of additives in plastic LCAs relating to mechanical recycling, summarize common methodological approaches, and highlight opportunities for including additives in future works. Through paired bibliometric, hermeneutic, and empirical analysis of LCAs of plastics in mechanical recycling pathways, we explore how and when additives are included and omitted. This study is presented in two parts. Part I (this scientific publication) explores why additive omissions occur in the LCA literature. In contrast, part II explores additive availability in LCA databases to fill identified knowledge gaps and provide practitioners with tools to include additives going forward (Logan et al., 2024, part II).

METHODS

In this article, a systematic review of the retrieved scientific publications is conducted and analyzed using hermeneutic and bibliometric methods, followed by an empirical review of the life cycle inventory (LCI) disclosures accompanying each article. The following section summarizes the systematic three‐tiered search method and eligibility screening used to establish the corpus, the bibliometric and hermeneutic methods utilized to identify trends and context across the corpus, and the empirical approaches used to review additive data inclusion in the accompanying LCIs.

Search method

This article investigates additive inclusions and omissions in peer‐reviewed LCAs of plastics published in academic journals between 2010 and 2020. Recent critiques of LCA literature reviews state that keyword searches do not capture all available studies due to the variation in terms used to describe LCAs (Estrela, 2015; Michels & Fu, 2014). To ensure our review was exhaustive, we employed a three‐tier systematic search method combining search strings (SSs), hand‐searching (HS), and snowball searches (SBSs). These methods were developed following systematic literature review methods (Higgins et al. 2022).

The SS utilized was: [title:(Plastic OR plastics OR “∖” of plastic∖“” OR “∖”plastic waste∖“” OR PVC OR PP OR PE OR polyolefins OR “∖”plastic packaging∖“” OR PS) AND abstract:(“∖”life cycle assessment∖“” OR “∖”life cycle analysis∖“” OR LCA)].

The HS consisted of reviewing the indexes of all volumes from a selected journal within a specific timeframe (Lefebvre et al., 2022). All volumes of the top three LCA journals, namely The Journal of Industrial Ecology (JIE), International Journal of Life Cycle Assessment (IJLCA), and Resources, Conservation and Recycling (RCR), issued between 2010 and 2020, were reviewed. Conference proceedings were not included despite prior calls to include them (Michels & Fu, 2014), as they may not have included detailed enough supporting information to determine additive inclusion.

Following SS and HS eligibility sorting, bibliographies from all eligible papers were compared in the SBS (Dobrovolskyi & Keberle, 2019), which identifies common citations across eligible SS and HS articles and allows the inclusion of any major contributing publications outside of the time frame, search terms or journal selection of the complementing methodologies. The fully sorted corpus can be viewed in Supporting Information (S1).

Eligibility screening

The returned corpus for each method was then filtered through a three‐step eligibility screening process that reviewed the title, abstract, and full text of each article. All peer‐reviewed empirical scientific publications that: (i) presented an LCA study of a plastic consumable or a packaging material that was either derived from recycled plastic materials or included a mechanical recycling end‐of‐life scenario and (ii) passed the full‐text review were considered eligible and thus included in the final corpus. Bibliometric analysis

The search methods were confirmed and verified using bibliometric analysis to identify if the eligible corpus provided a representative and robust sample of the literature on plastics LCAs. Citation rates, title statistics, h‐indexes, and corpus keywords were retrieved using Harzing's Publish or Perish search tool (Harzing, 2007). These citation statistics were then correlated to the eligibility sorting of the corpus and used to identify trends in additive inclusion and the impact of these articles in plastic LCAs over time. Trends in title terms and keywords were assessed to confirm the relevance of the search string. This was done by checking them against citation statistics for the retrieved articles to ensure the terms used in the search strings retrieved the broadest range of impactful literature on the LCA of plastics.

Hermeneutic analysis

One of the goals of this research was to separate the discussion of additives in the text from additive inclusion in LCI data. To achieve this aim, a two‐step sorting process was applied to the final corpus. The first sorting focused on the full scientific publication of the eligible articles.

Each one was sorted into one of three categories: Additives included: The authors discussed additives in the text. Additives mentioned: The authors mentioned plastic additives but did not include specific details in the text. Additives not mentioned: The authors did not mention plastic additives in the study. As this study focused on the discussion of additive inclusion itself, the registration of individual additive CAS numbers or product names was not included when classifying additive inclusion.

Hermeneutic analysis examines large bodies of text and language, often using computing to assist in this regard (Rockwell & Sinclair, 2016). Hermeneutic analysis was performed in this study to identify a common language for additives, place of occurrence, and frequency of occurrence, using Voyant Tools (Rockwell & Sinclair, 2016), that is, an open‐source software package that helps identify trends within the language used in a corpus (Sinclair & Rockwell, 2016).

Common vocabularies were identified by assessing all texts and looking for additive‐related terms, which were then checked against a key term list developed from a review of the OECD classifications and PLASI projects. This sorting step focused on the inclusion of plastic additives within data used to conduct each study; however, some studies only disclosed the use of a database in their LCI instead of providing details on the selected process or elementary flow.

To investigate the presence of additives in these LCA databases, Ecoinvent reports 2010−2019 (Althaus et al., 2007; Frischknecht et al., 2005; Steubing et al., 2016; Weidema et al., 2013; Wernet et al., 2016) and GaBi Change Reports from 2015 to 2019 (GaBi, 2022) were reviewed. If an update to the plastic process was listed in the change report, documentation for the processes and elementary flows was reviewed. As both LCA databases heavily cite PlasticsEurope Environmental Profiles (PE EP), changes to these profiles were also reviewed (for a complete list of these documents, please consult the Supporting Information (S1)).

However, GaBi and Ecoinvent build upon PE EP for polymer compounding and plastic conversion data for all other plastics data. Although GaBi, Ecoinvent, and the PE EP are interdependent, they often cite each other's data or background processes (Bauer et al., 2022; van der Voet et al., 2013). Therefore, if additives are missing in a vital process within one database, they are likely missing in other datasets (for more details on this point, please refer to part II of this study). It is important to note that in the GaBi version 6.0 update and later, users can customize additive inclusion in plastic data by choosing granulate data nodes without additives (Baitz et al., 2012). This “without additives” classification implies that these processes exclude any chemicals used as additives. However, authors should confirm this when including these processes in their own studies.

Plastic processes that contained or accounted for one or more additives, either specifically (i.e., by chemical registration number or chemical name) or by vertical aggregation (i.e., grouping all additives into one proxy), were labeled additives present. If a database version was not disclosed, the article was labeled as additives present only if all versions of the data for the polymer dataset in the LCA database disclosed included additives. Otherwise, the label not enough data was applied.

Once the complete corpus was sorted for inclusion in the text, the discussed additives were grouped into categories using the OECD's additive classification method. A category for generic additive descriptions was added to this method to capture terms such as additives, general chemicals, and other chemicals. The final groupings are presented in Table 1. The articles were finally sorted according to the polymer studied to identify common trends in additive discussions per polymer(s) studied.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The following sections highlight the findings from separating the discussion of additives from data inclusion in the corpus. In each section, we explore the opportunities and barriers for LCA practitioners in including additives in the written discourse of scientific publications and data for LCAs of plastics. Of the articles that could be classified, 21 (23%) clearly included a discussion and an analysis of the impacts of additives in the LCA, 8 (9%) mentioned and analyzed additives, and 7 (8%) included additives in their analysis. Poor LCI transparency, either partially disclosed or missing, prevented the data classifications of 40 articles (43%). Clear LCI disclosures are required to adequately analyze the reliability and robustness of LCAs and are a requirement for ISO compliance (ISO 2006; 2018). Bibliometric additive inclusion trends across the corpus

Exploring bibliometric trends across the eligible corpus, we found that within this study's timeframe, there was an increase in LCAs of plastics considering mechanical recycling in both their discussions and LCI data. In Figure 3, trends in dialogical and data inclusion of additives (x‐axis) are illustrated over time (y‐axis), with bubble size indicating each article's respective yearly citations, thus highlighting the influence of the scientific publications included in the corpus. Although those including additives in their discussion and LCI data have increased since 2010, there has also been a steady rise in scientific publications that do not mention additives. Of the latter, there has been a consistent trend to also not include a transparent LCI, thereby making it impossible to confirm additive inclusion in these studies.

Trends overtime in additive inclusion of the corpus literature. This figure illustrates the trends in additive inclusion in inventory and discussions across the corpus over time. The size of the bubbles indicates how commonly the articles have been cited per year, illustrating their relative impact. The y‐axis denotes the additive status in the scientific publications, while the x‐axis denotes the year of publication.

Studies not mentioning, or missing, LCIs also accrued the largest average citations per year across the corpus. Moreover, while studies that either included or mentioned additives in their discussions and incorporated them within LCI data disclosures were increasingly cited throughout the period, publications that omitted them in both discussions and LCI data disclosures were the most referenced. Therefore, additive omissions in influential literature are a potential source for continued additive omission in future studies. Moreover, as this trend remained consistent over time, there is clear evidence that poor transparency in LCI data disrupts the inclusion and classification of additives in LCAs of mechanically recycled plastics.

Additive inclusion in scientific publications

This review found that there was an opportunity to improve the inclusion of additives in the written discourse addressing the environmental impact of mechanical plastic recycling in 63 (68%) corpus articles. Doing so is essential, as it clarifies the role and impact of additives in the life cycle of plastic recycling. In our hermeneutic analysis using Voyant Tools, in the articles that discussed (28) or mentioned (16) additives and additive terminology, as outlined in Table 1, additive terminology occurred 950 times across the 93‐article corpus. Mentions of or discussions about additives most commonly occurred at the start (or introduction section) of the article. The clustering of occurrences in the introduction section indicates that even when additives were mentioned or discussed, they were often not included in the results of the study.

The hermeneutic analysis also revealed that polymer focus plays a role in additive inclusion in the text. For example, LDPE was the most discussed polymer, with PP, PMMA, and PET all similarly explored; however, when the actual inclusion of additives within these polymers was explored, PET and LDPE were the most likely to be studied with additives included. When additives were explored individually, they were commonly functionalization agents, such as colorants, or physical–chemical property improvers, such as flame retardants and fillers. However, generic terms such as additive, other chemicals, or generic chemicals were still the most common words used to discuss additives. A complete list of searched terms and frequency is included in the SI.

Additive inclusion in life cycle inventory data of scientific publications

After reviewing all the LCIs and supporting information, this study found that additives were present in the LCI for 36 articles (39%), not present in 16 (17%), and there was not enough data (i.e., partially disclosed or missing LCI) to classify presence in 40 articles (43%). This study only examined the presence of additives in the scientific publication and supporting LCI data and did not assess the quality or completeness of the modeling of the additive or its representativeness in a specific polymer application. Therefore, it is important to note that the inclusion of additives does not necessarily reflect the completeness of their modeling.

If LCIs were provided within the article and additives were discussed or mentioned, it was uncommon for additives to be missing from the LCA. However, if additives were not discussed at all, additives were twice as likely to be missing from the data. While the omission of additives from both the text and LCIs may indicate either a knowledge gap or a choice made by practitioners, as neither was clearly declared in the scientific publication, we could not identify if it was due to knowledge, data, or author choice. As illustrated in Figure 4, generic disclosures were prominent in the corpus, occurring in over half of all scientific publications, including additive data (40% of the total corpus). In these cases, the datasets aggregated additives or utilized generic proxy data to model additives. Often, the use of proxy data also relied on vertical aggregation to combine the flows of multiple additives into one proxy process for all chemicals used in production. For further details on aggregation methods in LCAs, please refer to Kuczenski et al. (2017). The use of vertical aggregation, in this manner, is a fundamental limitation in modeling LCAs of plastics and mechanical recycling, as it aggregates important information about the amounts, flows, and fates of additives used throughout the life cycle. Furthermore, as plastics datasets are often interdependent (Bauer et al., 2022), generic disclosures can be seen as challenging across LCA databases.

Generic disclosures of additives limit what can be understood about their environmental and human health impacts. For example, Hottle et al. (2017) discussed the various chemicals and adjustments needed to recycle plastics and bio or starch‐based polymers. However, because additives are included generically, their significance cannot be assessed in an LCIA or discussion. Generic disclosures of additives in this manner hamper the usefulness of LCAs in identifying those that pose a substantial risk to humans and the opportunity to highlight those that are proven safe and sustainable.

When additives are included in the LCI but not discussed in detail within the text, assessing the impacts they may be responsible for in an overall product can be challenging. For example, although Gironi and Piemonte (2011) highlighted the importance of considering additives when designing for end‐of‐life pathways in the text and additionally considered various fillers, they did not mention the impact of the sensitivity of their selections in their results. Similarly, Beigbeder et al. (2019) compared end‐of‐life options for PP/wood flour and PLA/flax fiber composites by considering fillers and compatibilizers within the study. Even though they included these additives in their LCIs, more discussion was needed about the life cycle impact assessment of the additives, sensitivity, and the role of these additives on recycling and end‐of‐life processes.

In both cases, additive inclusion is crucial to the composition of the plastic material. Yet neither paper looked at the impacts of the sensitivity of these decisions within their study, nor did they discuss any additives‐related findings. Therefore, both including additive data and discussing any quantified impacts are essential for strengthening the robustness of a study and ensuring transparency about the inclusion, validity, and quality of the data used.

These results indicate that both increased communication about the role of additives in the results and the inclusion of LCIs are needed to improve knowledge and understanding of their environmental impacts in the mechanical recycling of plastics.

Advantages of additive inclusion in scientific publications

An author's inclusion of additives in a scientific publication often signals an inclusion in the LCI data. However, there are varying degrees in this regard. While some authors discussed additives in the text, their inclusion in the LCI data was general, often using a generic process to represent them (Gironi & Piemonte, 2011; Huysman et al., 2015; Lu et al., 2019; Nishijima et al., 2012; Toniolo et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2019; Bassi et al., 2020). Other authors only focused on key or regulated additives in both the text and the data, predominately physical–chemical property improvers such as flame retardants or fillers (Agarski et al., 2019; Jonkers et al., 2016; Rajendran et al., 2012; Aryan et al., 2019; Abejón et al., 2020; Beigbeder et al., 2019; Bracquené et al., 2020; Dewulf et al., 2019). The most comprehensive studies were those that included plastic additive compositions in both the text and the LCI data (Broeren et al., 2017; Civancik‐Uslu et al., 2019; Gu et al., 2017; Gu et al., 2018; Volpe et al., 2018).

Examining the additives used in recycling illustrates the importance of additive inclusion; for example, Gu et al. (2017) did so in the recycling of PP and PE and found additives to contribute the most in several impact categories: antioxidants and UV stabilizers to ozone depletion and impact modifiers to freshwater eutrophication. Additionally, the “filler” additive used was the most sensitive parameter in their impact assessment, leading to the recommendation that mechanical plastic recyclers prioritize selecting lower‐impact additives (Gu et al., 2017). While comprehensive studies present the most informed insight into the environmental impact of additives, focusing on key additives enhances our understanding of their role in the mechanical recycling of plastics. Aryan et al. (2019) considered ultramarine colorant in the recycling of PE and found it had the most significant impact on abiotic resource depletion. Focusing on key additives can also be used to assess when environmental impacts may indicate which plastics are ineligible for recycling. Bracquené et al. (2020) used this approach to filter out fiber‐reinforced plastics from their recycling scenarios, although the averted impacts were not discussed.

The strength of these studies is that additives are present in both the text and the data, thereby allowing authors to inform discussions regarding their impacts and to provide quantitative estimates thereof. Thus, the inclusion of additives in both scientific publications and their LCI data can help guide decision‐makers in understanding their influence on plastic recycling and how they can be used to evaluate the strength of circular plastic strategies.

Opportunities and recommendations for additive inclusion

The review of the corpus and results from our analysis identified barriers and opportunities in including additives in the LCA of plastics in mechanical recycling pathways. We found that significant barriers to verifying additive inclusion in the corpus encompassed the need for robust LCIs, the lack of detailed discussions in the text, and the use of generic data in LCIs. However, when we explored scientific publications that included comprehensive LCIs, discussed additives throughout their study, and used non‐generic data, additives often played a role in the environmental impacts of mechanical recycling of plastics. Thus, there is a need to improve their discussion in scientific publications, as well as their disclosure in the LCIs of LCAs.

Moreover, many of the studies in the corpus that included additives in their data did so only by including generic data using predefined plastic processes. However, these processes only sometimes included the correct additives data for all processes and only some additives necessary to produce finalized recycled plastic goods (see part II). Thus, insights into the robustness and completeness of additive modeling across the corpus are limited to determining inclusion only. As such, authors should confirm that the additives they need to consider in their study are represented in chosen LCA datasets and declare when these sets are not representative of their study.

Improving additive inclusion starts by refining clarity in this regard within the written discourse of the scientific publication. Authors need to indicate within the text whether additives are included or excluded from the scope of the study. They should also specify why either option occurred and declare if data availability, intellectual property, or other factors prevented inclusion. Such declarations only need to consist of a few sentences, but they will improve the transparency around additives and help to identify better where knowledge gaps need to be filled.

Authors also need to include a comprehensive LCI together with their scientific publications. As seen in this study, the need for transparent LCI disclosures constitutes a significant barrier to the reproducibility of LCA studies and ignores ISO (2006a) guidance. Thus, we recommend that LCA studies on plastics transparently disclose which additives were used to model plastics. This should include the selected LCA databases, any extensions and versions used, and the selected data node title in their supporting information. Moreover, authors should strive to avoid generic disclosures of additives such as chemicals or additives and instead use a naming framework to discuss those included or substituted by proxy in their study.

We demonstrate a possible naming approach in Table 3 using the OECD's plastic additives classification (minimum disclosure) or sub‐classification (preferred disclosure) systems. This framework was selected due to REACH's adoption of it in the PLASI project (ECHA, 2019). Practitioners should work closely with industry stakeholders to clarify and align on the appropriate aggregation approach. This approach would also help to standardize terminology across LCAs and between LCA practitioners and plastic industry stakeholders. Furthermore, it may also provide authors with a base layer of additive knowledge to help improve granularity while collecting data and structuring and executing the study.

Finally, we recommend that authors consult chemical data lists on additives to improve practitioner selection and inclusion of additives commonly used in plastics in their LCA study. Examples include the additive lists devised by PLASI (ECHA, 2019), Aurisano et al. (2021), Wiesinger et al. (2021), and the UNEP (2023). In part II (Logan et al., 2024), we offer authors a review of the additive data available in LCA lists by cross‐referencing the UNEP (2023) list. Not only can consulting these lists help practitioners reduce generic additive data in LCAs of mechanical plastic recycling, but it may also help them highlight and disclose which data are available to practitioners today. By declaring these limitations to stakeholders, and in the discussion of scientific publications, we can better highlight knowledge gaps and data needs to LCA practitioners and database developers.

CONCLUSION

This review found that despite increasing awareness of the importance of additives in plastic value chains, transparent additive data still needs to be included in the LCA literature considering mechanical recycling pathways for plastic consumables. This study also found that poor LCI disclosures in peer‐reviewed LCA articles are a barrier to determining the impacts of additives in plastic LCAs.

Not only is the provision of accurate and reproducible data vital for ISO compliance, but it is also crucial to ensuring the credibility of LCAs used to inform decision‐ and policymaking. Otherwise, impacts associated with additives throughout plastic life cycles may be underestimated or not accurately represented by those reading the LCA. Furthermore, this review demonstrates a clear need for increased research on additive data in LCAs and accounting for additive flows, both as substances and materials, within major LCA databases. In part II of this study, we explore how additives are included within plastics data and the availability of independent additive data to further aid practitioners in including additives in LCAs of plastics.
