The impact of supportive counseling on resilience in infertile women following unsuccessful in vitro fertilization: a randomized controlled trial study
Authors: Ida Kabirian, Fatemeh Zahra Karimi, Seyed Reza Mazloum, Malihe Amirian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the high prevalence of IVF failure and its psychological impact, this study aimed to determine the effect of supportive counseling on resilience in infertile women following unsuccessful in vitro fertilization. Sixty-five infertile women after the failure of the first IVF started the study, but only 60 completed the trial. In the intervention group, individual supportive counseling was conducted during four sessions lasting 60 min each once a week. For the control group, routine care of the infertility center has been done. Before the intervention, there was no statistically significant difference in the total score of resilience and its subscales between the two groups. However, four weeks post-intervention, the total score of resilience and its subscales increased significantly in the intervention group compared to the control group, with an increase of 15.06 ± 15.14 in the intervention group and a decrease of 12.93 ± 13.03 in the control group (p < 0.001). Eight weeks post-intervention, the total score of resilience and its subscales increased significantly in the intervention group compared to the control group, with an increase of 20.60 ± 16.86 in the intervention group and a decrease of 17.80 ± 17.34 in the control group (p < 0.001). Based on the findings of this study, supportive counseling effectively enhanced resilience in infertile women following IVF failure.Keywords: Infertility, IVF, Resilience, Counseling, Unsuccessful treatment

Introduction

Resilience refers to an individual’s ability to maintain biological and psychological balance in the face of adversity. It reflects positive adaptation after exposure to challenging conditions. Individuals with higher levels of resilience show improved psychological functioning, including stronger self-efficacy, reduced stress, better emotional regulation, and greater adaptive capacity compared to those with lower levels of resilience [1, 2].

Resilience serves as a protective factor against the adverse psychological effects of stressful situations and enhances an individual’s capacity for adaptive coping with distress [3, 4].

Individuals with higher resilience demonstrate greater adaptive capacity to life changes compared to their more vulnerable counterparts, they also show an enhanced ability to develop and employ effective coping strategies in challenging circumstances [5, 6].

Infertility has been identified as a significant psychosocial stressor that can adversely affect resilience in women [7]. Recent data from the WHO indicate a concerning increase in infertility rates, especially in developed nations where approximately 25% of reproductive-aged couples now experience infertility [8, 9]. The prevalence of infertility in Iran is estimated to be18.3% [10].

Infertility represents a significant biopsychosocial challenge that profoundly impacts individuals, marital relationships, and social wellbeing [11]. The growing prevalence of infertility and its associated consequences has led to increased utilization of ARTs.

IVF is one of the most widely employed ARTs for infertility treatment. However, treatment success remains limited, with live births achieved in only a subset of cases while the majority of couples experience unsuccessful outcomes [12, 13]. In Iran, the IVF success rate has been reported in various studies to be between 20.2 and 20.6% [14, 15]. The limited success rates of IVF and subsequent treatment failures are associated with significant psychological consequences.

Resilience is a key factor that can help infertile women cope with infertility. However, studies indicate that this protective effect diminishes after IVF failure, leading to a decrease in resilience levels in these women. Additionally, resilience levels show a progressive decline following each unsuccessful treatment cycle [16], Enhancing resilience in infertile women results in improved interpersonal relationships, psychological well-being, and helps to mitigate distress and dissatisfaction associated with IVF failure [17].

Support systems enhance resilience through various pathways: by improving conditions, providing care and attention, facilitating self-awareness of personal capabilities and resources, and creating participatory opportunities, these factors collectively work to promote resilience [18]. Supportive interventions improve coping strategies and promote health outcomes by boosting self-confidence and increasing awareness of personal strengths and limitations. This, in turn, enables better behavioral control [19–21].

Considering the high prevalence of infertility and IVF treatment failure, as well as their significant psychological effects, especially for women - such as reduced resilience and discontinuation of treatment - this study aimed to examine the impact of supportive counseling on resilience in infertile women following unsuccessful in vitro fertilization.

Materials and methods

This study is a two-arm parallel, randomized controlled trial conducted in 2023–2024 at the Infertility Center of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences in Iran.

The study included 65 infertile women who were referred to an infertility center for IVF treatment. These women had negative pregnancy test results after their first IVF attempt. Inclusion criteria included: a diagnosis of primary infertility, a negative pregnancy test after the first IVF, no history of mental illness, and no adopted children.

Convenience sampling was used to select participants, who were then randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group using a random sequence generated by a statistical analyst on the website www.randomization.com.

The sample size was estimated using the formula for comparing means with a confidence interval of 95% and a test power of 80%, this calculation was based on the study by Naderi et al. [22] 11,16.23, X1=64.6, X2=48.34 (Fig. 1).

Consort diagram

Data was collected using the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) [23]. The CD-RISC, designed and introduced by Connor and Davidson in 2003, is a standard tool for measuring resilience. It comprises 25 items, with each item rated on a 5-point scale (0–4). Higher scores on the scale reflect greater resilience and the items are grouped into five subscales [23]. In Iran, the reliability and validity of CD-RISC were evaluated by Bakhshayesh Eghbali et al. [24]. Alpha coefficients for CD-RISC and its subscales were 0.94 and 0.71–0.89, respectively [24]. The participants completed the CD-RISC before the intervention, as well as at 4 and 8 weeks after the intervention using a self-report method.

For the intervention group, supportive counseling was delivered individually across four weekly 60-minute sessions to women following unsuccessful IVF. The intervention incorporated counseling techniques including: empathic engagement, active listening with reflective explanation, therapeutic guidance, encouragement and facilitation of emotional catharsis.

The content covered in each session included: (1) establishing a therapeutic alliance, explaining the study purpose and methods, providing information on IVF: success rates, failure rates, potential outcomes and relaxation training (2). Understanding resilience: concept and importance, identifying personal strengths, building confidence and emotional awareness and discussing psychological defenses (3). Reviewing and reinforcing the client’s strengths. (4) Reviewing resilience wins, strengthening effective strategies and facilitating emotional disclosure.

The control group received routine care, which consisted of phone calls and guidance on continuing assisted reproductive treatment, Routine care did not include any emotional support.

The data were analyzed using SPSS-21. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, and frequency distribution) were used to create a frequency table. The chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, Mann-Whitney U test and independent t-test were used to examine the differences between the intervention and control groups. Repeated measures ANOVA was used for comparisons within each group. All tests were conducted at 95% confidence interval, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the impact of supportive counseling on resilience in infertile women following unsuccessful in vitro fertilization.

The results of the study showed statistically significant differences in total resilience scores and all subscales between the intervention and control groups at 4 and 8 weeks after the intervention. The intervention group had higher mean scores across all measurements, indicating that supportive counseling effectively enhanced resilience and all subscales among women experiencing IVF failure.

The increase in the score of personal competence, high standards and tenacity subscale indicates that participants reported a stronger sense of self-efficacy, confidence in problem-solving, and resilience in the face of adversity after the intervention. The one’s intuition, tolerance of negative affect, and the strengthening effects of stress subscale also showed significant improvements. This suggests that participants were better able to manage stressful emotions, act more calmly in ambiguous situations, and have more confidence in their own inner judgment. The significant increase in the positive acceptance of change and secure relationships subscale indicates that participants had a more flexible attitude toward life events and a better understanding that change is a part of life. It may also reflect the greater sense of social support that was created in the group setting of the sessions. The increase in scores in the control domain reflects the feeling that participants felt less overwhelmed and threatened after the intervention and had gained greater belief in their ability to influence their life outcomes. Improvement on the spiritual influences subscale could indicate a strengthening sense of connection to a higher power, meaning in life, or moral support. Although the supportive counseling intervention resulted in significant increases in resilience across all five domains, changes in the ‘spiritual influences’ subscale in intervention group were statistically less significant than in the other subscales. The ‘spiritual influences’ dimension, which addresses beliefs, practices, and supports related to spirituality and religion, was likely not directly affected by the short-term supportive counseling sessions (four sessions). Changes in these deep belief domains generally require more time or interventions with explicitly spiritual-religious content. This result may also reflect variation in participants’ spiritual beliefs.

Dokaneheeifard et al. [25] conducted a quasi-experimental pre-test-post-test study titled " The effectiveness of reality therapy on increasing resiliency, life expectancy and improve the quality of life of infertile women Infertility Center Sarim.” The results of the study showed that reality therapy increased resilience and improved the quality of life in infertile patients [25]. Borhani et al. [26] conducted a controlled trial study with a pretest-posttest design titled “The Effectiveness of Combining Commitment and Acceptance Group Counseling with Reality Therapy on Resilience and Body Image in Infertile Women.” The results showed that the combination of group counseling based on commitment and acceptance with reality therapy is effective in improving resilience and body image in infertile women [26].

Pandya et al. [27] conducted an intervention study titled “Women Undergoing IVF: Mitigating Anxiety and Stress and Building Infertility Self-Efficacy and Resilience through Mindfulness Care.” In this study, women in the intervention group received 20 weekly sessions of online mindfulness intervention, while the control group did not undergo any intervention. The results showed that the online mindfulness intervention was effective, as the intervention group reported lower levels of anxiety and stress, as well as increased self-efficacy and resilience post-test compared to the control group [27].

Safitri et al. [28] conducted a study titled “Social Support and Resilience in Women Who Experience Infertility.” The results indicated a positive and significant relationship between social support and resilience. The dimensions of social support that impact resilience include the emotional, instrumental, and friendship dimensions, with the emotional dimension showing the strongest correlation with resilience. Among women facing infertility, resilience tends to be lower, with 53% reporting lower levels, highlighting the need for social support, particularly in managing emotions [28].

Taghizadeh [29] conducted a study titled “Investigating the Effectiveness of Group Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy on the Resilience of Women with Breast Cancer.” Thirty women with breast cancer were randomly assigned to two cognitive-behavioral therapy groups and a control group. Cognitive-behavioral therapy was conducted face-to-face during 10 sessions in the cognitive-behavioral therapy groups. The results of this study indicate the effectiveness of cognitive-behavioral therapy in enhancing the resilience of women with breast cancer. Therefore, it is recommended to utilize this treatment approach to boost the resilience of these patients [29].

Consistent with the findings of the studies mentioned in the section above [25–28], our results also suggest that counseling can enhance resilience. Counseling interventions focus on improving an individual’s ability to cope with psychological challenges. Support also helps individuals approach situations and events more positively and adaptively, contributing to increased resilience in infertile women. Additionally, support assists women in overcoming their difficulties, acting as a form of immunization against stressful conditions. Therefore, supportive counseling can significantly affect the resilience of infertile women. Furthermore, increased resilience can enhance already happy relationships in couples, promoting satisfaction, independence, closeness, mutual acceptance, optimism, spirituality, psychological peace, and overall quality of life [30, 31].

Given that infertility reduces individuals’ resistance to life’s challenges and that women experiencing infertility exhibit lower resilience compared to non-infertile individuals when facing crises, integrating resilience-focused counseling services into infertility clinics is essential. This intervention could help strengthen psychological adjustment, foster healthier relationships, and enhance the overall quality of life for women experiencing infertility-related distress.

Limitations

One of the limitations of the present study was that it was not possible to blind the participants but the statistician was kept blinded and the CD-RISC questionnaire responses were self-reported by the participants themselves. Additionally, this study focuses on women who have experienced IVF treatment failure for the first time. The psychological reaction of this group may differ from women who have had repeated failures. Therefore, it is recommended that similar studies be conducted in other countries and among women with repeated treatment failures.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that supportive counseling is effective in enhancing the resilience of infertile women following IVF failure. Resilience plays a key role in better adaptation and coping with crises. Considering that greater support leads to higher resilience, the supportive counseling approach used in this study can be applied to improve the resilience of infertile women after IVF failure.
