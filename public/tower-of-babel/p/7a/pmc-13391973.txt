Percutaneous tendon lengthening and selective percutaneous myofascial lengthening in children with cerebral palsy: Establishing surgical indications using the Delphi method
Authors: Michael Wade Shrader, Benjamin J Shore, Henry G Chambers, Tim Theologis, Robert M Kay, Erich Rutz, James J McCarthy, Kristan A Pierz, Kerr Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:Percutaneous tendon lengthening (PTL), including selective percutaneous myofascial lengthening (SPML), is popular in cerebral palsy (CP) with advocates arguing that it is minimally invasive and may result in less pain and faster rehabilitation than open techniques, but supporting evidence is limited. The purpose of this study was to develop consensus for the surgical indications of PTL and SPML in CP.Methods:A 16-member panel, established experts with at least 10 years’ experience in the surgical management of CP, was created to determine opinion consensus on orthopaedic surgical indications. The modified Delphi method was used, utilising a five-level Likert scale. Consensus was defined at 80% of responses being in the highest or lowest two of the five Likert ratings. General agreement was defined as 60%–79%.Results:Thirty-two statements were surveyed; consensus or general achievement was achieved for 22 questions (69%). The panel reached consensus that percutaneous methods for plantar flexor lengthening (PFL) for equinus in CP should be avoided due to the risk of overlengthening. There was disagreement on 10 questions (31%), primarily on the need for more evidence of differences between PTL and SPML.Conclusions:Expert consensus suggests more evidence is needed to establish the safety and efficacy of all types of percutaneous techniques across anatomical levels but confirmed that all percutaneous PFL in CP should be avoided due to the consequences of overlengthening.Significance of Study:In the absence of prospective high-quality evidence, consensus opinion helps guide surgical decision-making for children with CP.Level of evidence:Level IVKeywords: Cerebral palsy, contracture, percutaneous tendon lengthening (PTL), selective percutaneous myofascial lengthening (SPML), single-event, multilevel surgery, (SEMLS) consensus, Delphi

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is the most common cause of physical disability in childhood, with an average prevalence in the United States reported to be 1.5–3.4 per 1000 children. 1 Many children with CP develop musculoskeletal contractures and torsional bony abnormalities during growth that can affect gait and function.2,3 Surgical correction of soft-tissue and/or bony torsional abnormalities at more than one level on a limb in one single surgical event has been described as single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS).4–6 SEMLS has been accepted as the standard of care to improve gait in children with CP and is supported by one randomised clinical trial, prospective studies with three-dimensional gait analysis (3DGA), studies with medium to long-term follow-up and three systematic reviews.4–9 SEMLS is typically performed through open soft-tissue muscle lengthening, through fascial releases preserving muscle architecture and strength and long bone osteotomies to improve lever arm dysfunction seen in ambulant children with CP.3–5 The golden window for SEMLS surgery intervention is typically between 8 and 11 years of age when children have reached their gross motor function plateau while still demonstrating improved rehabilitation potential, although specific surgical decision-making must be tailored for each individual patient, including choices for specific surgical procedures, surgical dose, and surgical timing. 10

Traditional percutaneous tendon lengthening: adductor longus, medial hamstrings, and gastrocsoleus

Percutaneous tendon lengthening (PTL) techniques for ambulatory children with CP have become increasingly common in clinical practice.11–15 Percutaneous lengthening of adductor longus in children with CP was found to be safe and of similar efficacy to open lengthening. 11 Percutaneous lengthening of the medial hamstrings was not found to be as accurate or as effective as open lengthening in one randomised study, but several studies utilising 3DGA reported similar functional outcomes between the two techniques.12–15 Percutaneous plantar flexor lengthening (PFL) in children with spastic diplegia is much more controversial than either percutaneous adductor or medial hamstring lengthening. This may be related to both anatomical and biomechanical factors. 16 Optimal PFL in diplegia requires selective gastrocnemius lengthening or differential PFL and intrinsic stability to avoid overlengthening with early weightbearing and rehabilitation.16–18 This can only be delivered by Zone 1 surgery and open visualisation of the gastrocsoleus aponeurosis and soleal fascia. 16 Selective lengthening of the gastrocnemius aponeurosis (Zone 1, Strayer procedure) requires separation of the gastrocnemius aponeurosis from the soleal fascia under direct vision.16,18 The inability of percutaneous techniques to achieve controlled differential lengthening of the gastrocnemius-soleus complex is the key problem related to this surgical technique.

Long-term outcome studies show that percutaneous PFL can lead to overlengthening, weakness in stance, and progressive crouch gait. 17 Borton et al. 17 reported that single-level PFL resulted in overlengthening and calcaneus gait in 36% of children with CP with the worst results following percutaneous tendo-Achilles lengthening. In contrast, open PFL from the same centre was associated with improved gait, improved ankle kinematics, and an overlengthening rate of 2.5%. 18

Selective percutaneous myofascial lengthening

Newer approaches to tendon lengthening, including selective percutaneous myofascial lengthening (SPML), have gained popularity in some centres.19–23 The techniques are not the same as the three percutaneous techniques discussed above, although the same three muscle-tendon units (MTU) are the ones most frequently addressed. The target tissue to be divided during SPML is not the tendon per se but ‘myofascial’ connective tissue, identified by palpation with the MTU under tension. Needles, microscalpels, or conventional scalpels can be used to divide the myofascia. The myofascia is divided at the tightest portion of the MTU where they ‘bowstring’ near their musculotendinous insertions. The stated primary goal is to decrease tension in the MTU, allowing increased ease of motion and reduced hypertonia. 21 In contrast to the percutaneous techniques described above, we were unable to find studies in which the precise tissues divided during SPML have been identified during open surgery or by imaging.

Advocates for SPML argue that it is minimally invasive, may be associated with less pain and faster rehabilitation than open surgery, and that these techniques are substantially different than traditional open percutaneous techniques.18–22 Most of the current literature on SPML is from single-centre series, often with relatively short-term follow-up. Few SPML studies have utilised 3D motion analysis as outcome assessments. To date, there have been no randomised clinical trials assessing the efficacy of SPML, and no head-to-head direct comparisons with traditional SEMLS surgical techniques. There are currently no reports with medium- to long-term follow-up after SPML.

In contrast to SEMLS, the literature to date on the safety and efficacy of SPML has been limited.18–22 Providers of this surgery indicate patients receiving SPML earlier than standard SEMLS open surgery, between the ages of 5–7 years, have better outcomes and fewer complications.18–22 However, the evidence backing these claims is limited by study design, including limitations in the use of objective outcome measures such as 3DGA, early age at surgery when gait and function are developing and undergoing spontaneous improvement, and very short-term follow-up.2,19–23 Functional outcome in some SPML studies has been reported as improvement in Gross Motor Function Classification System Levels (GMFCS) of between one and two levels for the majority of the patients. GMFCS is a clinically important classification which is of great importance to the families of children with CP because it is usually stable over time and predicts long-term prognosis.2,4–7 However, GMFCS is an ordinal classification system, and the author-originators of the system distinctly say that it should not be used as an outcome measure. 24 This is due mainly to fluctuations in the actual gross motor functional measure score for individual patients (which is what GMFCS is based upon) that can be somewhat noisy and not accurate at the borders between the levels. While claims associated with SPML surgery are impressive,19–23 there is a dearth of comparative evidence to support the superiority of this surgical intervention compared with SEMLS using open4–6 or percutaneous techniques.13–15,25

Substantial variation exists in the factors that influence the decision to perform surgery and the surgical approach to patients with CP. Best practice guidelines regarding the use of percutaneous techniques in the treatment of patients with CP would ideally be derived from objective, quantitative clinical research studies.26,27 When level 1 evidence is not available, management decisions may have to be made by relying on the evidence of expert opinion. 28 Recently, modified versions of the Delphi technique have been used to establish expert opinion through consensus building.29–32

In 2017, an international group of 16 paediatric orthopaedic surgeons (each with at least 10 years’ experience in the surgical management of children with CP) came together to identify practice variations and improve the quality of care provided to children with CP by seeking consensus on indications and techniques of surgical procedures commonly used to improve gait function.33–35 Prior publications have focused on the indications for hamstring lengthening, femoral derotational osteotomy, rectus femoris transfer, anterior distal femoral growth modulation, gastrocnemius lengthening, and the assessment of foot alignment and function.33–37

The purpose of this study was to use expert opinion obtained through a modified E-Delphi technique to establish consensus on the use of percutaneous surgical techniques in the treatment of children with CP and to determine the consensus of the group regarding the level of evidence for SPML. Specifically, SPML and traditional percutaneous surgical techniques were assessed as distinctly different surgical techniques and analysed separately.

Methods

Creation of expert panel

A 16-member panel of fellowship trained paediatric orthopaedic surgeons with expertise in 3DGA and musculoskeletal surgery for children with CP participated in this consensus thinking process. This panel has worked together on several other consensus projects identifying the indications for common surgical procedures used and SEMLS.33–37 Panel members had a mean of over 20 years’ experience (range: 10–41 years) in the orthopaedic care of children with CP and a mean of 19.8 years (range: 7–31 years) of experience using 3DGA for clinical decision-making in children with CP. No members of the panel withdrew during this consensus building process.

The consensus process incorporated a modified Delphi methodology, which took place between July 2023 and September 2024. Institutional review board approval for the study and from each participating member was obtained. All communications were electronic (email and virtual conferencing). The modified Delphi method is an iterative process that uses a systematic progression of repeated rounds of discussion and voting. 29 It is an effective process for determining expert group consensus where there is limited definitive evidence and where opinion based on clinical experience is important.

A subgroup of the overall panel, who had expertise and interest in percutaneous surgical techniques, met separately to create a structured format for the creation of questions regarding the indications of percutaneous procedures for both ambulatory and non-ambulatory children with CP including the use of SPML. The creation of these questions was based on a review of the literature, open discussion, and the overall process was based on previous consensus work by this panel. There were three stages for statement generation in which the facilitator (senior author) presented the draft questions to the overall larger group who provided feedback and edits, where members of the full panel were encouraged to share comments with the facilitator. A series of statements using a five-point Likert type scale was created.

The electronic survey was created in research electronic data capture (REDCap,38,39 Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee, version 9.1.0) and sent to all panel members. Consensus for ‘agreement’ occurred when at least 80% of the experts selected one of the two highest responses on the five-point Likert type scale (‘strongly agree’ or ‘agree’); consensus for ‘disagreement’ occurring when at least 80% of the panel selected one of the two lowest responses (strongly disagree or disagree). ‘General agreement’ occurred if 60%–79% of the respondents chose one of the highest two responses, ‘general disagreement’ if 60%–79% chose one of the two lowest responses. There was ‘no consensus’ if less than 60% of the responses were in either the highest or the lowest two categories for a given statement. The threshold for these levels of consensus was based on the historical standards recommended in the literature. Opportunity for comments was provided for all statements, and the panel met virtually after each round of questioning to provide feedback to the content of the questions and to suggest edits and modifications to the questions that did not have consensus. Panel members were blinded to each other’s responses. The iterative process was continued at the discretion of the facilitator and continued after three total rounds. All panel members were appraised of the final results of the survey and had the opportunity to review and edit the resulting article prior to submission for publication.

Results

Thirteen of the 16 members of the group reported personal experience in performing percutaneous procedures (81%). Four of the 16 members (25%) of the group expressed experience in SPML procedures. All members of the group completed all rounds of consensus questions. Overall, consensus or general agreement was obtained in 22 out of 32 questions (69%). Plantar flexors

There was consensus agreement on the use of percutaneous surgical techniques for PFL: ‘Percutaneous plantar flexor lengthening is associated with overlengthening of the gastrocnemius/soleus complex’ (100% strongly agree) and ‘SPML plantar flexor lengthening is associated with overlengthening of the gastrocnemius/soleus complex’ (100% strongly agree). There was consensus that SPML PFL increases the risk of neurovascular injury.

Adductor and medial hamstrings

The panel also reached consensus that there is currently not sufficient evidence to support performing SPML on adductors and medial hamstrings on either ambulatory or non-ambulatory children with CP.

General SPML consensus statements

Regarding patient requests for SPML, there was consensus agreement on the following general questions based on the panelists experience and perception: ‘I have had families request SPML as surgical treatment’ and ‘I have had patients tell me that they have read or heard (been told) that SPML was superior to standard surgical techniques.’ The panel strongly felt with consensus that more research and evidence are necessary before SPML could be considered a standard treatment option, and that the current use of marketing, social media, and information on provider webpages does not accurately represent the true efficacy of SPML as a treatment option.

General surgical decision-making consensus statements

The panel had consensus that surgical decision-making involves multiple data points, including information from all components of the diagnostic matrix such as clinical history, physical examination, 3DGA, and radiographs.4–6,40 A consensus was reached stating that the standard of surgical care should include regular postoperative follow-up visits.

Age-related consensus statement

The panel also had consensus recommending that soft-tissue surgery in GMFCS I-III children should be avoided under the age of 4 years whenever possible.

General agreement statements

The panel had general agreement for the statement: ‘For children at GMFCS I-III function, isolated soft-tissue surgery (without bony correction) is rarely indicated.’ There was also general agreement that compared with an open hamstring lengthening, percutaneous lengthening increases the risk of overlengthening in children with CP and that there are significant differences in the surgical technique for percutaneous medial hamstring lengthening and SPML. There was general agreement that compared with an open adductor or hamstring lengthening, SPML increases the risk of neurovascular injury. There was also general agreement that there are no meaningful differences in pain control or rehabilitation between percutaneous, SPML procedures, or open procedures.

All other statements (including other statements on adductor, hamstring, traditional percutaneous lengthenings, and SPML lengthenings not specifically listed above) did not reach consensus or general agreement (Table 1).

Discussion

Ideally, best practice guidelines would be available for surgical indications and decision-making for the orthopaedic care of children with CP based on high-level evidence such as definitive randomised clinical trials. 4 However, robust evidence for generating best practice guidelines may be lacking, and therefore, treatment decisions may rely on a combination of the best published evidence4–6,13–15 informed by expert opinion.33–37 Paediatric orthopaedic surgeons with expertise in the care of children with CP have produced a number of studies to help describe practice variation and develop consensus statements on surgical indications for children with CP.33–37 As in these previous studies, there was a relatively high degree of consensus and agreement on statements regarding percutaneous surgical techniques, including PTL and SPML.

Plantar flexors

There was clear consensus among the expert panel that percutaneous lengthening (both percutaneous ‘Hoke’ type hemi-transection and SPML) of the plantar flexors for equinus should be avoided in ambulatory children with CP. This is consistent with previous literature warning against ‘perc’ techniques to address equinus because of significant concerns of overlengthening the plantar flexors, specifically the soleus, which can cause iatrogenic crouch.16–18 This overlengthening is a well-known issue with percutaneous techniques that do not have the ability to selectively lengthen the gastrocnemius, and the inability to perform differential gastrocnemius and soleus lengthening. We think that there are strong anatomic data 16 and functional outcome data 18 to support these statements as well as consensus from the expert panel.

In general, the expert panel felt strongly that the current evidence in the literature supporting the routine use of SPML in children with CP was insufficient. Most of the panel had experience with percutaneous procedures, but only a minority had direct surgical experience with SPML, which certainly could be a confounding variable in this study.13–15 However, the majority of the panel members had clinical experience in caring for patients who had SPML procedures previously performed at other institutions.

All panel members had significant experience with patients and families who either asked directly for SPML or had seen other providers who had recommended SPML, with many panel members aware of adverse surgical outcomes of SPML. Therefore, the panel felt that they were appropriately knowledgeable about the state of the evidence around SPML to make adequate judgements about its quality and the application of the SPML technique to clinical care. The panel also felt that, in general, PTL surgical techniques are different in concept and practice than SPML.16,18,21 For example, open PFL in children with diplegia requires selective lengthening of the gastrocnemius aponeurosis or differential lengthening of the gastrocnemius aponeurosis and soleal fascia. This type of finely calibrated ‘dose-based’ surgery requires open visualisation of the MTU, and this is not feasible by percutaneous Hoke triple hemi-section (PTL) or by SPML.16–18,21 It is important to note that the panel was queried about their opinion for each muscle group and each technique (PTL and SPML) separately to ensure that there were no generalisations made across the two techniques.

The panel members’ experiences with families requesting SPML prompted a series of questions regarding the ethics of how a new surgical technique is presented and introduced into clinical care. Most panel members had patients who had been told that ‘SPML was better’ than traditional orthopaedic surgical care, and that ‘SPML procedures caused less pain with a faster recovery.’ A review of the current SPML literature has no direct comparative studies that can support such claims.18–22 The panel uniformly agreed that this information had been reported by patients, rather than from systematically collected data. The expert panel strongly suggested rigorous studies on the outcomes of SPML compared with a traditional control group be undertaken to better counsel families in the spirit of shared decision-making and family-centred care. 23 The panel strongly felt that unfounded claims not supported by evidence should not be made directly to patients, on webpages or via social media.

Aside from the consensus statements on percutaneous PFL, the main take-away from this effort is a call out that more rigorous studies of SPML should be produced before any definitive statement could be made about efficacy. A randomised controlled trial of open procedures vs SPML would provide the highest level of evidence but might be ethically and organizationally challenging. However, prospective studies of SPML with standardised outcome measures with medium to long-term follow-up would also be very useful.5,25,40 Outcome measures might include summary statistics of gait such as the Gait Deviation Index or the Gait Profile Score as well as validated functional measures such as the Functional Mobility Score or Functional Assessment Questionnaire.4–9,40

Finally, the panel felt that all surgical recommendations should be made after an appropriate in person history, physical examination and consideration of imaging and gait analysis. 40 The concept that surgical recommendations could be made either via purely telehealth encounters, review of videos or solely by email communications was not considered to be appropriate care by this expert panel.

There are several weaknesses inherent in a Delphi consensus study. The linear nature of a sequence of questions makes the conclusions on any given statement made in isolation an imperfect approximation of actual clinical care, where a multitude of variables are considered together to make an individual treatment recommendation for a given patient. One weakness of the study was also the relative lack of direct SPML surgical experience, with only 25% of our panel having had personal experience performing the procedures; this certainly could be a confounding variable in the interpretation of these results and a source of bias. However, the panel had significant experience in percutaneous procedures, the majority of the panel members had clinical experience in caring for patients who had SMPL procedures previously performed at other institutions, and overall, has one of the largest collections of paediatric orthopaedic surgical experience in the care of children with CP.

Conclusions

In conclusion, expert consensus confirmed that any type of percutaneous PFL for the correction of equinus in CP should be avoided due to the high likelihood of overlengthening. This Delphi effort achieved consensus in 69% of statements about percutaneous techniques in CP, but obviously did not reach consensus for all statements. The results of this study suggest that uncertainty remains over the use of percutaneous techniques and that more evidence is needed to determine the appropriateness, efficacy, and safety of SPML, as well as further investigations into the difference between traditional percutaneous techniques and SPML.
