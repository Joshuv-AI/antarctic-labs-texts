ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesLimb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD) type R1/2A, calpain-3–related, is a rare, autosomal recessive disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the CAPN3 gene. LGMDR1/2A causes progressive weakness of upper and lower limbs, leading to difficulty walking and use of arms for overhead activities. The rate of progression of LGMDR1/2A is unknown because of the limited number of published cohorts and the lack of validated outcome measures. Quantifying the natural history of LGMDR1/2A using standardized outcome measures is critical for understanding the rate of disease progression and improving clinical care and clinical trial readiness. The aim of this cross-sectional study was to evaluate the suitability of the North Star Assessment for limb-girdle type muscular dystrophies (NSAD), 100-meter timed test (100MTT), and Performance of Upper Limb 2.0 (PUL) as outcome measures for quantifying disease presentation and progression in individuals with LGMDR1/2A.MethodsNinety-two individuals were evaluated by physiotherapy teams at Nationwide Children's Hospital and the John Walton Muscular Dystrophy Research Centre. Psychometric analysis of NSAD and PUL was completed using Rasch measurement methods. Descriptive statistics and correlation coefficients explored the relationship among all outcomes.ResultsPsychometric analyses demonstrated that NSAD and PUL were valid and appropriate functional outcome measures for ambulant and nonambulant individuals with LGMDR1/2A. The NSAD and 100MTT velocity were highly correlated (Spearman rho Rs(88) = 0.89) The 100MTT was particularly useful for the strongest and asymptomatic cohort.DiscussionIn this largest reported cohort of individuals with LGMDR1/2A, NSAD, PUL, and 100MTT were suitable to quantify functional impact of the disease. International harmonization of outcome measures creates meaningful clinical data to inform clinical management and trial design.

Introduction

LGMDR1/2A or calpain-3–related muscular dystrophy is one of the most common forms of autosomal recessive limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD),1-4 caused by variants in the CAPN3 gene that encodes a skeletal muscle enzyme named calpain-3.2,5 Individuals with LGMDR1/2A present with progressive muscle weakness of the arms and legs and may experience loss of ambulation, but age at disease onset and rate of progression are heterogeneous.2,6,7 The rare nature of and variability within LGMDR1/2A present a challenge when designing clinical trials or comparing individuals around the world. Few natural history studies are available, and the lack of harmonized outcomes makes it difficult to quantify disease progression.

Previous natural history and cohort studies report disease onset in the first or second decade of life, with slow progression of muscle weakness.2 The largest natural history study of LGMDR1/2A8 conducted across 3 countries described phenotype/genotype variability, where patients with at least one missense variant were less affected than those with 2 null variants. The reported pattern of muscle involvement is symmetrical muscle weakness of the pelvic girdle, posterior thigh, and adductor muscles in the lower limbs. In the upper limb, LGMDR1/2A causes weakness of the shoulder muscles and scapular winging.2,5,6,9-11

The lack of standardized, validated clinical outcome assessments (COAs) for LGMDR1/2A significantly limits international efforts to quantify the impact of the disease on an individual.12 COAs are used to test the efficacy of treatment interventions,13,14 to construct accurate natural history data,15,16 and to inform patient care decisions.17,18 Although there are currently no treatments for LGMDR1/2A, there have been advances in the development of gene therapies19 and small-molecule treatments for several forms of LGMD (NCT04800874, NCT03652259).20

The importance of robust, standardized, and clinically meaningful patient-centered COAs has been well recognized by both clinical and research communities.12,21 The International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research group22 and the United States Food and Drug Administration23 have outlined quality measurement practices, including modern psychometrics such as Rasch Unidimensional Measurement Model (RUMM),24,25 in the selection and development of COAs. COAs must be of meaning to the population of interest, conducted in a standardized manner to allow comparison within and between individuals, and sensitive to change.

Functional COAs are assessments conducted by physiotherapists within clinical or clinical trial settings to evaluate the impact of disease on an individual's daily function. Within other LGMD subtypes and neuromuscular disorders (NMDs), natural history studies have facilitated the development and validation of clinically meaningful and psychometrically robust functional outcome measures such as the Rasch-built North Star Assessment for limb-girdle type muscular dystrophies (NSAD),15,26 Performance of Upper Limb (PUL),27 and 100-meter timed test (100MTT).16 The NSAD, 100MTT, and PUL have excellent inter-rater and intra-rater reliability and have previously been shown to be appropriate across other LGMD subtypes.15,20 The aim of this study was to evaluate the suitability of the NSAD, 100MTT, and PUL with psychometric analysis using RUMM to quantify disease presentation and progression in individuals with LGMDR1/2A.

Methods

Physiotherapy teams across Nationwide Children's Hospital (NCH), Columbus, OH, United States, and John Walton Muscular Dystrophy Research Centre (JWMDRC), Newcastle upon Tyne, United Kingdom (UK), completed data collection between January 2017 and November 2023.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

Data collected by NCH was obtained under the Natural History Study of NMD (NHS-NMD), with ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at NCH (IRB17-01086 and IRB 09-00139). The UK team submitted the study to the National Health Service Health Research Authority UK (audit 10,055), registered under the Newcastle upon Tyne Hospitals National Health Service Foundation Trust with Caldicott approval number 9485.

All individuals recruited into the NHS-NMD at NCH consented and assented, if appropriate, to participate. Data from the NCH team were obtained during research visits, including the International LGMD Patient Conferences in Chicago, IL, in August 2019 and Washington, DC, in October 2023. JWMDRC collected data within routine clinical appointments. All participants were genetically confirmed, or self-reported as genetically confirmed LGMDR1/2A.

Licensing Agreement

The NSAD and PUL are copyrighted (2020) by The Newcastle upon Tyne Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust. Permission was granted to both sites to use these assessments for clinical and research purposes.

Data Collection

Data were collected in a standardized manner using the assessment proformas for the PUL, NSAD, and 100MTT. Inter-rater reliability of the physiotherapy teams was previously established.26 NSAD was collected by physiotherapists at NCH and JWMDRC. The 100MTT and PUL were collected by the NCH team only because of clinic limitations at JWMDRC. Individual-reported sex, age at assessment, and ambulatory status were recorded.

Clinical Outcome Assessments

North Star Assessment for Limb-Girdle Type Muscular Dystrophies

The NSAD is a RUMM-built, 29-item scale of motor performance, validated for ambulant and nonambulant individuals with a limb-girdle presentation of muscle involvement.15,26 It includes items such as standing up from a chair, sitting up from lying, timed rise from floor (RFF), and 10-meter run/walk (10M).

One Hundred-Meter Timed Test

Testing maximal ambulatory capacity, the 100MTT quantifies the time taken for an individual to complete 2 laps around cones placed 25 m apart as quickly as possible, including running if safely able.16

Performance of Upper Limb 2.0

The PUL is a 22-item scale to assess upper limb function,28 originally used in DMD but increasingly used in LGMD.26 The scale evaluates functions at shoulder, elbow, and wrist-hand levels.27 The PUL entry item was modified from the Brooke upper extremity scale.29 The PUL entry item classifies overall upper limb function, from the ability to abduct the arms above the head and keep them straight (score 6) to no useful function of hands (score 0).

Statistical and Psychometric Analysis

Cross-sectional data were examined using RUMM24,30 as an innovative strategy for understanding the relationship between measures of function and the specific impact of disease on the motor function of the LGMDR1/2A patient group.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 28 (IBM, Inc.). Spearman correlation coefficients31 were calculated to explore the relationship between NSAD, timed RFF, 10M and 100MTT velocities, and PUL.

Psychometric Evaluation

Psychometric evaluation using RUMM24 examined NSAD and PUL performance across 7 areas: targeting, response categories, fit, reliability, dependency, stability, and unidimensionality using RUMM2030 software.30

Targeting

The match between the motor function measured by the items of the NSAD and PUL and the functional ability of the study population was examined using scale-to-sample targeting.17,32

Response Categories

The ordinal scoring of a scale item, for example, 0/1/2 in the NSAD and PUL, must reflect loss of function as disease progresses. Ordered item scoring thresholds support scale validity and use of a summed total score. RUMM2030 examines this statistically and graphically by threshold locations and plots.33

Fit

For valid use of a total summed score from ordinal-level data, items of a scale must work together (fit) both clinically and statistically. Three methods were used to evaluate fit. First, all items should lie within a fit residual range of SD ± 2.5. Second, evaluation of the fit of item and person to the model, where a mean close to 0 and a SD of 1 indicate good person and item fit. The third is the item-trait interaction statistic, given as a χ2 value. A significant χ2 value indicates misfit.

Reliability by Person Separation Index

Reliability of the scales was quantified using the Person Separation Index (PSI),34 similar to Cronbach alpha,35 comparing true scale variance with observed variance. Higher PSI indicates greater reliability.

Dependency

Because responses to one item on a scale should not unduly influence the response to another item,17,32 the number of pairs of dependent items is calculated. If dependency occurs, measurement estimates can be biased and reliability (PSI) is artificially elevated.

Stability: Differential Item Functioning

The stability of item performance across subgroups, for example, sex, is examined using differential item functioning (DIF). DIF for sex and age was calculated for NSAD and PUL. Age was divided into 2 categories: under 18 (child) and 18 and over (adult).

Unidimensionality

As a fundamental requirement for RUMM, items of a scale should measure a single construct, in this case, functional motor performance (NSAD) and functional upper limb performance (PUL). Unidimensionality was determined using principal component analysis and reported as a t test.

Data Availability

The authors confirm that aggregated data are available under reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Results

Ninety-two individuals with LGMDR1/2A were included for analysis (57 female and 35 male). Sixty-four were adults and 28 were younger than 18 years, including one child younger than 5. The average age at time of assessment for the adult cohort was 36.4 years (SD 13.3 years) and pediatric cohort was 14.7 years (SD 2.7 years). Fifty-nine were ambulant and 33 nonambulant, as defined by the ability to walk 10 m barefoot and unaided in under 30 seconds. Of the 33 nonambulant individuals, 3 were younger than 18 years.

A total of 139 NSAD, 113 PUL, and 45 100MTT evaluations were available for analysis. While most only had one evaluation, there were 23 individuals with 2 visits, 7 with 3, 1 with 4, 2 with 6, and 1 with 8 visits. Longitudinal data on available NSAD, 100MTT, and PUL evaluations are plotted in eFigures 1, A–D. Although not the primary focus of this study, the longitudinal data provide clinical insights into heterogeneity of disease progression. Rates of decline in motor function vary between individuals, as does age at onset of upper limb involvement.

The PUL items targeted both strong ambulant and weaker nonambulant individuals, without any significant floor effects (Figure 3B). There may be a potential ceiling issue for those individuals with no clinical signs of disease affecting overhead shoulder function. Response Categories

NSAD: All 29 items demonstrated ordered response categories, meaning that the scoring categories for each item are logical and appropriate for LGMDR1/2A (eFigure 2A). RUMM determined that the easiest item was lifts head and the most difficult was stand on heels.

PUL: 17 of 19 items demonstrated ordered response categories, meaning that the scoring categories for each item are logical and appropriate for LGMDR1/2A (eFigure 2B). Tears paper and push on light were very mildly disordered because most of the cohort achieved full scores on these items. Only 2 individuals scored 0 on tears paper, and only one was unable to push on the light.

Fit

NSAD: One item showed mild misfit (touches floor from standing), and 4 had a χ2 probability <0.01. Those items included lifts head and reaches forward.

PUL: 2 items showed mild misfit (lift 500 g to shoulder height and push on light), and 2 had a χ2 probability <0.01.

These items of NSAD and PUL functioned well, have significant clinical functional meaning, and will be retained in the scales.

Dependency

NSAD: 16 pairs of items had highly correlated residuals (>0.3). These pairs were items assessing component parts of the same movement (i.e., squatting down and standing from squat or climb and descend the box step) or bilateral performance. One item from each pair of dependent items was removed, and PSI was recalculated. The PSI remained stable at 0.96, indicating that the dependency had not artificially inflated the PSI. Items that test the ability on the left and right sides of the body were considered clinically meaningful, with improved sensitivity to asymmetrical weakness.

PUL: Eight pairs of items had highly correlated residuals (>0.3). These pairs mainly assessed the same movement but with differing levels of difficulty, e.g., moving weight on a table. One item from each pair of dependent items was removed, and the PSI was recalculated. The PSI remained stable at 0.93, indicating that the dependency had not artificially inflated the PSI.

Stability (Differential Item Functioning) Across Age and Sex

NSAD: When examined by age, 2 items demonstrated uniform DIF (lifts head and climb box step). These items are more likely to be performed in the younger and less affected cohort compared with the adult group. Because LGMD is a progressive, degenerative condition, age is a proxy for ability level. Thus, we consider this an acceptable difference relevant to the disease rather than an artificial difference between ages. No uniform or nonuniform DIF by sex was present.

PUL: There was no presence of uniform and nonuniform DIF by age and sex.

Discussion

The aim of this project was to generate recommendations for suitable COAs for both clinical care and clinical trials in LGMDR1/2A. This project examined high-quality clinical data, collected using standardized methods by expert physiotherapists at 2 sites and during 2 international LGMD patient conferences. Using data from commonly collected COAs has demonstrated that NSAD, PUL, and 100MTT are appropriate and clinically meaningful for assessing disease presentation and progression in LGMDR1/2A.

Rasch analysis has evaluated the psychometric properties of the NSAD in LGMDR1/2A. With the exception of a few very strong individuals, the NSAD items target the range of abilities in motor function of our cohort. The items provide evidence that the NSAD is a reliable and unidimensional scale of functional motor performance. The scoring of each item is logical, and the items fit well together to make use of the summed total score appropriate and valid. The NSAD items reflect many tasks related to daily living, such as bed mobility, getting out of bed, balance in sitting, getting out of a chair, climbing a step, picking an item up off the floor, getting off the floor, and walking 10 m.

Richard et al.2 reported longitudinal data on the motor function measure and only demonstrated significant decline over 4 years in the ‘standing and transfers’ dimension of the scale. Compared with our cohort, where change was captured in 6 months in some individuals, the NSAD may be a more appropriate and sensitive motor outcome measure.

Of interest, the Rasch analysis identified stand on heels as the most difficult item in the NSAD. The presence of ankle plantarflexor muscle contractures or tiptoe walking in LGMDR1/2A2,36,37 has been reported and may be responsible for the inability to perform this item, rather than anterior tibial muscle weakness alone. This hypothesis is supported by the existing natural history reports,2 where distal leg weakness was less common and severe than proximal lower limb weakness, but ankle plantarflexor contractures were common and progressive. Conversely, in other LGMD subtypes such as LGMDR9, the stand on heels item is easier for individuals to perform.38 The next most difficult tasks were those assessing proximal pelvic girdle weakness—high kneel to stand and squat items. Similarly, in other reported LGMD cohorts,15,26,38 these items that test pelvic girdle weakness are some of the most difficult ones for individuals with LGMD to perform. The timed rise from floor performed as part of the NSAD would not be a suitable test to use in isolation to monitor disease progression because it could be performed by less than half the participants.

This analysis of the PUL represents the first comprehensive assessment of upper limb function in LGMDR1/2A. With its 3 dimensions, the PUL provides a granular understanding of disease impact on strength and movement at the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and hand. This study explored the relationship between upper limb function and overall gross motor function. In this cohort, we observed both individuals with overall minimal gross motor concerns but early upper limb involvement and those with well-preserved upper limb function but more substantial difficulty with their mobility. The results suggest that both upper and lower limb functions should always be reviewed in a clinical environment. Understanding factors that may lead to more severe or early deterioration in the upper limb is important in future research.

Our cohort may be representative of likely stronger patients who are able to travel to clinic or international meetings and the distal items may be more useful for individuals with severely affected upper limb function. There was a ceiling effect of the PUL in nearly 10% of this cohort. We suggest that the entire PUL would not be required for those with minimally affected upper limb involvement. The PUL entry item and shoulder dimension as the most difficult items could be sufficient. Minimal distal involvement of the wrist and hands was present. The Rasch model excluded 3 distal items, and scoring on other distal items was high except for a few individuals. Although this cohort did not demonstrate deficits in the distal domain, we suggest that, when significant upper limb involvement is present, the full PUL is administered until a larger sample including additional individuals with significant weakness has been evaluated to ensure that distal weakness is accurately evaluated. Using the PUL entry item clinically early in the disease process will provide useful information and allow proactive counseling of expected disease progression.

The 100MTT has demonstrated utility across the ambulant cohort and has the benefit of no ceiling in the minimally affected individuals. Even those able to complete many high-level functions such as squat or high kneel to stand still demonstrate some impairment in speed to complete the 100MTT and do not perform as well as age-matched peers. Because the 100MTT allows individuals to run or walk as able, it has strong clinical meaning, as a time of 115 seconds predicts impending loss of ambulation,39 indicates when ambulation outside the home is likely to become difficult, and informs discussions around when a wheelchair might be beneficial.

This cross-sectional analysis used a mix of prospective and retrospective data. The available retrospective data were limited, however, because JWMDRC collected the NSAD only. We acknowledge that patient-reported outcome measures would have been a valuable addition to this study; however, they were not consistently collected by both sites. Because the patient voice is essential to both clinical and research outcome measures, this is an important area of future research.

Quantifying change over time with disease progression is critical for future trial primary efficacy outcomes and informing individuals of their personal disease trajectory. Longitudinal change analysis was limited in this study because of inconsistent periods between visits. We were unable to use repeated-measures analysis of variance or mixed-model methods to detect any changes from baseline in the mean score at follow-up visits because of no consistent periods between visits and the impact of missing data during COVID-19. This information is presented in table and graphical form as a supplement but should be interpreted with caution. Instead, we have explored a large group of individuals with a broad spectrum of disease progression using multiple clinical outcome assessments to understand the relationship between upper and lower limb functions. Access to all genetic reports was not possible because most data were collected at a patient advocacy event, not within hospital or primary care settings. The relationship between phenotype and specific pathogenic variants requires further exploration. Ongoing natural history studies with consistent visit windows, capturing genetic variants of CAPN3 and a wider battery of assessments, are needed to capture rates of disease progression.

As clinical trials approach, understanding and quantifying the natural history of motor function in LGMDR1/2A is essential. The ENMC workshop on calpainopathies in 2018 noted that the lack of standardized and appropriate clinical outcomes for LGMDR1/2A hampered and limited international efforts to develop and define robust natural history data.12 Using a global standardized battery of validated COAs has the potential to create meaningful clinical data that can be used to inform end point selection, power calculations in clinical trials, and clinical management.

Validated, robust, and clinically meaningful COAs are critical to monitor disease and for utilization as clinical trial end points. In the absence of natural history published cohorts using validated COAs, we report on the exploration of commonly used COAs in LGMD to specific LGMDR1/2A populations in a large cohort from the United States and United Kingdom and attendees from 2 international LGMD patient advocacy meetings. The NSAD, PUL, 100MTT, 10M, and RFF are clinically meaningful, reliable, and robust COAs for use in individuals with LGMDR1/2A in both clinical and research settings.

Our study results inform future research and clinical practice in LGMDR1/2A. This exploration to determine the most suitable COAs for LGMDR1/2A characterizes disease presentation and progression and informs end point selection and cohort design for future clinical trials.
