Readiness for home-based palliative care among primary caregivers: an explanatory sequential mixed methods study
Authors: Nithra Kitreerawutiwong, Romnalin Keanjoom, Keerati Kitreerawutiwong, Sunsanee Mekrungrongwong, Rojanasak Thongkhamcharoen, Thanapon Tangsakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe number of patients choosing home‑based palliative care (HBPC) has increased. This situation requires both patients and families to be prepared. However, the readiness for HBPC remains underexplored. This study aimed to understand primary caregivers’ readiness for HBPC.MethodsAn explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was used. Quantitative data were collected from 198 primary caregivers registered at Mae Sot Hospital, Thailand, using validated questionnaires. Quantitative survey results informed the selection of participants and interview questions for the qualitative phase. Qualitative data were obtained through in-depth interviews with 15 caregivers, and trustworthiness was confirmed by credibility and dependability. The quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics and multiple logistic regression; the qualitative phase applied content analysis. Both sets of results were then integrated and presented.ResultsQuantitative analysis showed that primary caregivers with full-time employment were 2.4 times more prepared than those without full-time employment. Furthermore, compared with those who perceived caregiving as a burden, primary caregivers who perceived caregiving as nonburdensome were 2.61 times more ready. The interviews identified five themes of readiness at home: (1) awareness and attitudes towards palliative care, (2) end-of-life caregiving skills at home and household readiness, (3) family commitment and shared responsibility in caregiving, (4) the need for assistance in emergency situations at home, and (5) family support after the patient’s death. The integration of the data was consistent across most of the themes, whereas theme 5 emerged uniquely from the qualitative results.ConclusionsThe results indicated that health professionals should increase their awareness of palliative services, support their acceptance of death, and engage in shared decision-making. Experiential training programs for caregivers, mobile consultation services, collaboration in the district health system to train caregivers, and the coordination of employment opportunities within communities by setting an appropriate rate for local caregivers, as well as establishing financial advice for families and implementing bereavement care programs, are essential for providing holistic support to families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02153-6.

Introduction

Palliative care (PC) is the active holistic care of individuals across all ages with serious health-related suffering due to severe illness, especially those near the end of life [1]. PC aims to improve patients’, families, and caregivers’ quality of life [2]. The WHO recommends that PC be integrated into health systems as an essential component of universal health coverage [1]. This concept was in alignment with the United Nations’ 17 Sustainable Development Goals regarding good health and well-being, ensuring that global access to a robust PC network is crucial for guaranteeing equitable health for individuals of all ages [3].

The growing proportion of older adults dying from noncancer conditions necessitates the expansion of palliative care services beyond cancer care [4]. Palliative care is needed for a wide range of diseases. The majority of adults in need of palliative care have chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases (38.5%), cancer (34%), chronic respiratory diseases (10.3%), AIDS (5.7%) and diabetes (4.6%). Many other conditions, including kidney failure, chronic liver disease, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, rheumatoid arthritis, neurological disease, dementia, congenital anomalies and drug-resistant tuberculosis, may require palliative care [1]. This information is consistent with the increasing older adult population, longer life expectancy, and changing patterns and durations of illness during old age [5]. Older adults generally prefer to remain in their homes and communities [6]. Hence, it is essential that individuals, families, and communities be prepared for future housing needs [7] and home-based palliative care (HBPC) [8].

HBPC refers to palliative care provided in home environments [9], which is provided by a multidisciplinary team trained in palliative care, including doctors, nurses, community health workers, and volunteers who need to link with the community and hospitals to deliver care focused on improving quality of life [10]. Thailand introduced the Palliative Care Policy in 2014, and PC services were integrated into the national health service plan. The Ministry of Public Health (MOPH) implements the National Palliative Care Program by supervising, monitoring, and evaluating its management through health inspections [11]. In 2024, the MOPH launched the Cheewabhibaln Palliative Care Centre to improve care for dependent and palliative patients. The aim is to provide holistic and continuous care, linked from hospitals to communities and patients’ homes [12]. As the WHO highlights, a well-functioning system requires integrated PC across all levels, with a referral system to ensure seamless support for patients and their families [10]. HBPC significantly improves the quality of life, increases the likelihood of patients dying at home, and reduces health care utilization, resulting in fewer hospital admissions, fewer emergency department visits, and shorter hospital stays [13]. Successful care delivery requires integrated multidisciplinary teams, effective symptom management, holistic care, and timely support from caregivers. However, barriers such as a lack of competence and the number of health workers in PC, caregiver burden, and a lack of psychosocial support remain significant [14, 15].

The World Health Organization (WHO) [1] has indicated that families must become progressively involved as care partners of their families. Healthcare provider teams, including physicians, nurses, and pharmacists, provide care to help and support PC patients at home with patients’ families. Primary caregivers who provide HBPC need fundamental nursing skills, including symptom observation, emergency management, and the ability to administer medications and provide pain relief, particularly with the safe use of oral morphine [16]. Consistent with HBPC medication practices in Thailand, the National Health Security Office (NHSO) allocates morphine for patients with advanced-stage cancer to support PC at home by following the clinical guidelines for morphine use [17].

Studies have shown that more than half of family caregivers suffer from depression [18]. When primary caregivers are confident in providing HBPC, both patients and caregivers can improve their quality of life and family well-being. Greater confidence enhances caregivers’ ability to manage symptoms effectively, leading to better patient comfort [19]. Moreover, confidence strengthens their preparedness to respond to emergencies and the end-of-life process, which is essential for sustaining care at home [14]. This increased ability also increases the likelihood that patients can remain at home and achieve their preferred place of death [20]. Primary caregivers involved in HBPC need support and must learn home care skills by means of a holistic approach [21]. Therefore, family caregivers’ readiness is an important factor in promoting HBPC.

Family caregivers’ readiness refers to the degree to which family members feel prepared, confident and capable of providing PC for patients at home, including (1) family maintenance and consensus, (2) home care skills and hospice preparation, (3) arrangements for sharing and rotation, and (4) timely emergency management and palliative care. Moreover, the Readiness for HBPC Scale (RHBPCS) for primary caregivers has been developed [16], and the factors associated with their preparedness for caring for paediatric patients receiving PC have been studied at Queen Sirikit National Institute of Child Health [22]. Several factors, such as participation in primary caregivers’ religious activities, caregiver stress management, perceived support agencies, agency assistance, good relationships between caregivers and patients, and family relationships, can influence HBPC readiness [10, 22, 23]. However, the readiness of primary caregivers remains underexplored. Mae Sot Hospital, Thailand, provides multidisciplinary palliative care (PC), addressing a growing demand for services. While community-based PC is integrated with hospital services and advanced care planning, research on the preparedness of primary caregivers for HBPC remains limited [24]. This study addresses the following research questions: (1) What is the level of readiness among primary caregivers? (2) What factors influence the level of readiness among primary caregivers? and (3) What is the understanding of primary caregivers regarding their readiness in providing PC at home? To address these questions, the study specifically sought to: (1) quantitatively investigate the level of readiness among primary caregivers and examine the factors influencing their readiness, (2) qualitatively explain the readiness among primary caregivers, and (3) integrate and interpret quantitative and qualitative data to draw meta inferences about the readiness among primary caregivers. The findings will be used to design targeted interventions, training programs, and policy recommendations that support primary caregivers and enhance PC services at home.

Methods

Study design

An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was employed based on the taxonomy of Creswell and Plano Clark [25]. This design begins with the quantitative phase, followed by the qualitative phase, to explain the quantitative findings and provide an in-depth understanding of a complex phenomenon.

Participants

The population comprised 335 primary caregivers who registered PC patients at Mae Sot Hospital, Thailand. The primary caregiver was defined as the individual who provided the greatest number of hours of caregiving per day and offered consistent care compared with others. The sample size was calculated using the proportion estimation formula for a known population in the n4Studies program. The estimated proportion (p) of caregivers with adequate readiness was set at 0.60, with a margin of error of 5% (0.05) and a significance level (α) of 0.05. The required sample size was 175.97, which was rounded to 180 participants. An additional 20% was added for nonresponse, resulting in 216 participants; the sample size was rounded to 220.

A simple random sampling method was used. The inclusion criteria were (1) being the primary caregiver of a registered palliative care patient at Mae Sot Hospital in 2023, (2) being aged 20 years or older, (3) being able to communicate in Thai, and (4) being willing to participate in the study. The exclusion criterion was being a primary caregiver who planned to relocate within the next six months. Participants who met the eligibility criteria received information about the study, were asked for their permission to include them in the study, received the consent form, and were invited to take part in the survey at home.

Data collection

The study was approved by the Naresuan University Research Ethics Board of Health (Ref. IRB No. P2-0073/2567). For the quantitative phase, permission from the Mae Sot District Public Health Office and Mae Sot Hospital was obtained before data collection. The list of PC participants from Mae Sot Hospital was obtained, after which the researcher coordinated with the director of subdistrict health-promoting hospitals to request the names of village health volunteers (VHVs); each VHV took care of approximately 10 households [26]. Because VHVs are local individuals who volunteer their time and efforts to support community health, they are uniquely positioned to accompany the researcher on home visits to primary caregivers. During these visits, the study objectives were explained, informed consent was obtained, and appointments were scheduled for data collection at home. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire for primary caregivers of patients receiving HBPC. The questionnaire consisted of two sections. Section 1 explored the demographic data of the primary caregivers and the characteristics of patients PC, utilizing the Barthel Activities of Daily Living (ADL) [27] and Palliative Performance Scale (PPS) scores [28]. Section 2 assessed readiness for HBPC using the Readiness for Home-Based Palliative Care Scale (RHBPCS). The RHBPCS is designed to assess the readiness of primary family caregivers across four domains: (1) family maintenance and consensus, (2) home care skills and hospice preparation, (3) arrangements for sharing and rotation, and (4) timely emergency management and palliative care. This instrument has undergone extensive psychometric testing and is recommended for assessing the readiness of primary caregivers for HBPC [16]. The scale consists of 15 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very low readiness) to 5 (very high readiness). [See the questionnaire in Supplementary file 1]. The total scores ranged from 15 to 75, with a cut-off score of 45 [29]. Scores < 45 indicated “not ready,” whereas scores ≥ 45 indicated “ready.” Content validity was evaluated by five experts, yielding an item–content validity index (I–CVI) of 1 [30]. A pilot test was conducted for Section 2 and 3, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients obtained were 0.86 and 0.91, respectively.

For the qualitative phase, the participants were primary caregivers of patients receiving PC who had participated in the quantitative survey. The qualitative semi-structured interview topic guide drew upon preliminary quantitative survey findings. This provided a deeper exploration of areas of importance, including experiences and challenges of readiness for palliative care, understanding of palliative care, essential components of HBPC, preparation of the primary caregiver, preparation of the home environment and essential equipment, and recommendations [See semi-structured interview guideline in Supplementary file 2]. We identified the questionnaire code. Based on the mean overall readiness scores obtained in the quantitative phase, participants for the qualitative phase were selected as extreme cases, meaning those whose scores fell within the upper and lower 10% of overall readiness. A semi-structured interview guide was developed, comprising four main areas: (1) experiences of readiness to provide palliative care, (2) essential components of HBPC (infrastructure, personnel, home-based equipment, and financial resources), (3) caregivers’ expectations regarding HBPC, and (4) recommendations for improving readiness to provide PC at home. The interviews were conducted in a neutral area at the participants’ homes. A total of 15 participants were recruited, and data saturation was achieved. All the interviews were audio-recorded, and field notes were taken with the participants’ permission. The interviews ranged from 50 to 65 min.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 20.0 software package. Descriptive statistics were employed for the characteristics of the patients receiving PC, the relationship of the primary caregiver with others and perceived burden, and the readiness for HBPC. The interpretation cut-off point for the readiness for HBPC was 45. The dependent variable was the readiness for HBPC, which was categorized as a categorical data item. The independent variables were sex, age, marital status, education, occupation, income source, income, anxiety about income, health insurance status, comorbidities, exercise status, the primary caregiver’s relationships with others, and perceived burden. Bivariate logistic regression was used to examine the relationship between the dependent variable and each independent variable. Multicollinearity was assessed as the tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF), which ranged from 0.529 to 0.937 and 1.07–1.89, respectively. Variables with a crude bivariable p value less than 0.25 were selected for inclusion in the multivariable model to adjust for confounders [31]. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to identify factors associated with the outcome variable, with statistical significance set at a p value < 0.05.

Qualitative analysis

For qualitative data analysis, the interviews were transcribed verbatim and transcripts were proofread by NK. The data were analysed manually using content analysis. Following an inductive approach, NK, RK, and KK independently reviewed the transcripts to enable the initial codes of the data in the field. The first meeting was held to verify the coding and to compare and polish through the discussions. Discrepancies were resolved until consensus was reached. The examples of inductive codes generated from the data included “understanding of palliative care,” “home equipment,” “caregiving skills,” “transportation for follow-up,” “financial strain,” “caregiver burden,” “emergency management,” and “bereavement care.” In the next step, the researchers independently organized the codes into themes and subthemes. A second meeting was organized to review and discuss the results with all the researchers. The analysis was performed in parallel with the data collection and replication from the interviews [32]. Trustworthiness was confirmed by the credibility that was reached through audio recording and verbatim transcription of all the interviews. This is to ensure that the findings represented the information drawn and that prolonged engagement was carried out during the interview at the appropriate time. Dependability was defined as consistency by code, and meaning saturation was used to identify similar meanings and interpretations [33].

Data integration

The linkage between the two phases was carried out by selecting key informants from the quantitative study to participate in the qualitative follow-up, and the quantitative results were used to guide qualitative data collection. Findings from the quantitative phase were used as key issues explored during the qualitative phase to verify and deepen the understanding of the results. In developing the qualitative data collection tools, particularly the semi-structured interview guidelines, the quantitative findings were integrated into the development of the questions. The final integration of results involved examining the consistency and inconsistency between the quantitative and qualitative findings to synthesize the overall knowledge generated by the study [34].

Results

Description of the primary caregiver’s readiness

Of the 220 caregivers approached at the hospital, a total of 198 primary caregivers participated, for a response rate of 90%. Among the participants, 77.78% (n = 154) were female, with an average age of 54.42 years (SD. = 14). The largest proportion of the participants were married (68.18%, n = 135). More than half (57.07% n = 113) had graduated from primary school. Half of the participants were self-employed, working in agriculture or having small grocery businesses (50.50%, n = 100). With respect to the main source of income, wages from employment accounted for 39.90% (n = 79), followed by financial support from family and relatives at 25.36% (n = 50). The median monthly income was 5,000 Baht (Minimum = 600 and maximum = 140,000 Baht). For household expenses, two-fifths of those participants reported mild concern about financial burdens (40.40% n = 80). Most participants had the Universal Health Coverage Scheme (70.20% n = 139). More than one-third of the participants had hypertension (38.16% n = 58 from 152). In terms of exercise, two-fifths of the participants exercised sometimes (2–3 days per week) (40.40%, n = 80). The majority of participants receiving PC at home had noncancer conditions (80.82%, n = 177). The caregivers had been providing care for an average of 6 years and 1 month, spending approximately 13.33 h per day on caregiving (SD = 8.81 h). Most caregivers lived with the patient (80.81%, n = 160), with an average household size of 2.88 people (SD = 1.94).

Most of the participants were family members, and 72.22% (n = 143) were children and siblings. The largest proportion of caregivers received social support from local organizations, including adult diapers, rotational assistance from village health volunteers (VHVs), and support from older adult clubs, accounting for 63.13% (n = 125). With respect to participation in religious activities, more than half reported occasionally joining (51.52%, n = 102). Most caregivers (87.88%, n = 174) recognized the availability of community and local organizational support, and the majority (85.86%, n = 170) had received assistance from these agencies, including home visits by healthcare providers, local administrative staff, and community volunteers (Table 1).

Factors associated with primary caregivers’ readiness

Multiple logistic regression analysis revealed that employed caregivers were 2.4 times more likely to be ready to provide patient care than unemployed caregivers (i.e., full-time primary caregivers) were (ORadj = 2.27, 95% CI = 1.18–4.34). With respect to the perceived caregiving burden at home, compared with those who perceived caregiving as a burden, caregivers who reported no burden were 2.61 times more likely to be ready to provide patient care (ORadj = 2.61, 95% CI = 1.30–5.24) (Table 4).

Results of the qualitative phase

In the qualitative phase, there were a total of 15 participants and saturation was attained. Five themes were described as a result of the analysis of the qualitative data: (1) awareness and attitudes towards palliative care, (2) end-of-life caregiving skills at home and household readiness, (3) family commitment and shared responsibility in caregiving, (4) need for assistance in emergency situations at home, and (5) family support after the patient’s death. The themes are presented below.

Awareness and attitudes towards palliative care

Patients and family caregivers accepted the illness, understood the disease and treatment plans, and received information from physicians and nurses. Treatments were symptom-based, with regular medical appointments. The primary caregiver acknowledged the patient’s preferences to die at home with the support system.

Awareness towards palliative care

Patients and families accepted the serious illness and recognized that palliative care improves quality of life rather than being curative. The participants preferred noninvasive care and decided to avoid unnecessary treatment-related suffering. Their decisions were guided by sharing information with family discussions and advice from health care professionals.

End-of-life caregiving skills at home and household readiness

HBPC requires the primary caregiver and household readiness to have adequate skills. Caregivers often lack confidence in managing deteriorating symptoms and need guidance from health care professionals. Primary caregivers adapted to caring for palliative patients and received support for home medical equipment. However, financial strain and transportation barriers hinder the continuity of care.

Confidence in managing end-of-life symptoms at home

Primary caregivers expressed uncertainty about caring for patients when symptoms deteriorate or when patients lose the ability to self-care and how to provide physical, emotional, and spiritual comfort to relieve suffering when caring for their loved ones. The decision about whether the patient should die at home or in the hospital should be discussed with the physician for clear instructions and support.

If she must stay at home, I want to know how to care for her.” (P1).“I worry that my mother’s condition will worsen. Doctors seem better at caring for her. I learned from them, but if her condition becomes very severe, I prefer that she stay in the hospital.” (P2).“I was able to care for my mother at home because the nurses taught me and let me practice before discharge, and the team continued supporting us at home. It made me feel confident.” (P3).“I talked to the palliative team about pain and eating problems. They explained how to use morphine, prepare supplemented food, and manage emotional distress, which helped me a lot.” (P8).

Household preparation, medical equipment, and transportation barriers

Most families prepared a space on the ground floor to avoid stairs and facilitate easier care. Essential equipment such as oxygen concentrators, medical beds, or suction machines was borrowed from community hospitals or local government organizations. However, some households struggled with travel to hospitals or electricity costs from oxygen use. Transporting patients to hospitals was a major barrier due to patient fragility, lack of transportation services, and caregiver workload. In some cases, families requested home consultations such as phone-based advice or medication refills without travel.“We borrowed the bed and oxygen machine from the hospital. After I started taking care of her full time, hospital admissions decreased from 4–5 times a month to once every four months.” (P3).“The oxygen is used all day and night; the electricity bill has increased a lot.” (P12).“If there were more home visits or teleconsultation options, it would be easier because transporting him is very difficult. It’s very hard to transfer him. I want someone to help take him to the hospital or check him at home instead.” (P11).

It’s possible, but not as comfortable as before.” (P3).“Since oxygen and medication are expensive, we must manage finances carefully. We’re lucky our child is a government employee, so some costs are reimbursed.” (P6).

Family commitment and shared responsibility in caregiving

Primary caregiving was provided mainly by close relatives at home. Care responsibilities were shared through rotation or support from neighbours and paid helpers when available. In addition, care for patients with dependency was provided by caregivers supported by caregivers under the national long‑term care fund.

Experience of caregiving

In every household, the primary caregiver was a family member, including sons, daughters, spouses, parents, or siblings. In the Thai context, which has extended families, children often return home to care for ageing or seriously ill parents to express their gratitude and family duty. In nuclear families, caregiving responsibilities typically fell to spouses or siblings-in-law. Families with higher incomes sometimes hired paid caregivers. Because they cared for someone they loved, families expressed a strong dedication to do their best.

“I care for my mother alone. If not me, who would do it? I quit my job because I feared she might choke or struggle to breathe.”(P3).“He has four children. I cook during the day because they’re all working. At night, his daughter stays with him. We don’t want him to suffer—we just want to care for him well.” (P11).“I hope that if I care for him well, my husband will be able to sit in a wheelchair again.” (P6).“We hired a caregiver to care for my mother because I must work during the day. My father is a senior and needs rest too. The caregiver supports us, and I care for my mother at night.” (P8).

Care rotation and shared responsibilities

Most caregivers were family members, often supported by relatives, neighbours, or, if affordable, hired caregivers. Care tasks included preparing meals, assisting with daily activities, providing companionship during the day, and supporting mobility or hygiene needs. When no paid caregiver was involved, family members alternated between daytime and nighttime care. The rotation depended on availability, earning responsibilities, and the need for caregivers to rest. For patients with high functional dependency (ADL < 11), community caregivers (CG) or local care workers provided home visits, but early-stage palliative patients (PPS > 50, ADL > 11) were not eligible for support and received only traditional home visits.“My husband and son help her with hospital visits and meals. She still does some things herself, but she tires easily.” (P1).“I work during the day and return to care for my mother at night. We hired a caregiver for daytime support.” (P8).“I care for my father every day, starting at 7 a.m. The activities are cooking, bathing, cleaning, feeding, preparing medicine, and helping with rest. My sister stays at night.” (P11).

Need for assistance in emergency situations at home

Caregivers of patients with high PPS scores often could not identify emergency symptoms. However, caregivers whose patients frequently visited hospitals were more familiar with urgent symptoms such as infection, respiratory difficulty, poor appetite, severe pain, or confusion. Caregivers’ confidence improved their skills when health care professionals visited them at home, demonstrated care techniques, and offered opportunities for return demonstrations. Providing caregivers with 24-hour contact information for emergency consultation was essential.

Family thoughts after the patient’s death

When patients are categorized by PPS score, they can be grouped as follows:

(1) PPS 80–100% (full ambulation & self-care), (2) 60–70% (unable to work, poor ambulation), (3) 40–50% (unable to work, mainly sits/lies), and (4) 20–30% (bed-bound, needs total care). Despite illness severity, caregivers think about their future after the patient dies. Most of the participants are of working age and temporarily take on the caregiving role, planning to return to work when their loved one dies. However, some caregivers quit their jobs to care for the patient full-time. Their priority is to provide the best care for their family member.

1.1 Awareness towards palliative care

Patients and families accept the serious illness and recognize that palliative care improves the quality of life rather than being curative. The participants prefer non-invasive care and decide to avoid unnecessary suffering treatment. Their decisions are guided by sharing information with family discussions and advice from health care professionals.

1.2 Attitudes towards palliative care

Patients and their families understood palliative care based on explanations and diagnoses provided by physicians and nurses. The participants had positive views about dying at home.

2) End-of-life caregiving skills at home and household readiness

HBPC requires adequate skills of the primary caregiver and household readiness. Caregivers often lack confidence in managing deteriorating symptoms and need guidance from health care professionals. Primary caregivers adapted to caring for palliative patients and received support for home medical equipment. However, financial strain and transportation barriers hinder the continuity of care.

2.1 Low confidence in managing end-of-life symptoms at home

Primary caregivers expressed uncertainty about caring for patients when symptoms deteriorate or when patients lose the ability to self-care and how to provide physical, emotional, and spiritual comfort to relieve suffering when caring for their loved one. The decision about whether the patient should die at home or in the hospital should be discussed with the physician for clear instructions and support.

2.2 Household preparation, medical equipment, and transportation barrier

Most families prepared a space on the ground floor to avoid stairs and facilitate easier care. Essential equipment such as oxygen concentrators, medical beds, or suction machines was borrowed from community hospitals or local government organizations. However, some households struggled with travel to hospitals or electricity costs from oxygen use. Transporting patients to hospitals was a major barrier due to patient fragility, lack of transportation services, and caregiver workload. In some cases, families requested home consultations such as phone-based advice or medication refills without travel.

2.3 Financial constrain

Financial burden varied by family economic status. Unemployed caregivers relied on pooled family income or savings. Costs related to non-covered medication, diapers, oxygen supplies, and supplemental nutrition were mentioned.

3) Arrangements for sharing and rotation

refers to the family’s shared role in supporting the patient’s daily comfort and dignity. It includes following the patient’s wishes for nutrition and routine activities, preparing culturally appropriate practices for home care and death, and managing expected signs at the end of life respectfully. Factors associated with primary caregiver’s readiness

- Primary caregivers with full-time employment were 2.4 times more likely to be prepared for caregiving compared to those without full-time work.

- Caregivers who perceived no caregiving burden were 2.61 times more likely to be prepared than those who perceived caregiving as burdensome.

3) Family commitment and shared responsibility in caregiving

Primary caregiving was mainly provided by close relatives at home. Care responsibilities were shared through rotation or support from neighbours and paid helpers when available. In addition, care for patients with dependency was provided by caregivers supported by caregivers under the national long‑term care fund.

3.1 Experience of caregiving

In every household, the primary caregiver was a family member, including sons, daughters, spouses, parents, or siblings. According to the Thai context, which has extended families, children often return home to care for ageing or seriously ill parents to express their gratitude and family duty. In nuclear families, caregiving typically fell to spouses or siblings-in-law. Families with higher incomes sometimes hired paid caregivers. Because they cared for someone they loved, families expressed strong dedication to do their best.

3.2 Care rotation and shared responsibilities

Most caregivers were family members, often supported by relatives, neighbours, or, if affordable, hired caregivers. Care tasks included preparing meals, assisting with daily activities, providing companionship during the day, and supporting mobility or hygiene needs. When no paid caregiver was involved, family members alternated care between daytime and nighttime. The rotation depended on availability, earning responsibilities, and the need for caregiver rest. For patients with high functional dependency (ADL < 11), community caregivers (CG) or local care workers provided home visits, but early-stage palliative patients (PPS > 50, ADL > 11) were not eligible for support and only received the traditional home visit.

4) Need for assistance in emergency situations at home

Caregivers of patients with high PPS scores often could not identify emergency symptoms. However, caregivers whose patients frequently visited hospitals were more familiar with urgent symptoms such as infection, respiratory difficulty, poor appetite, severe pain, or confusion. Caregiver confidence improved their skills when health care professionals visited them at home, demonstrated care techniques, and offered opportunities for return demonstrations. Providing caregivers with 24-hour contact information for emergency consultation was essential.

5) Family support after the patient’s death

When categorizing patients by PPS score, they can be grouped as: (1) PPS 80–100% (full ambulation & self-care), (2) 60–70% (unable to work, poor ambulation), (3) 40–50% (unable to work, mainly sits/lies), and (4) 20–30% (bed-bound, needs total care). Despite illness severity, caregivers think about their future after the patient dies. Most of the participants are in the working age and temporarily take on the caregiving role, planning to return to work when their loved one dies. However, some caregivers quit their jobs to care for the patient full-time. Their priority is to provide the best care for their family member.

Discussion

The mixed-methods study makes a significant contribution to the current literature by giving equal weight to both the quantitative (QUAN) and qualitative (QUAL) components [34]. This approach allows for a comprehensive understanding of primary caregivers’ readiness for HBPC, which is essential for supporting them as they navigate the trajectories of PC patients. A deeper understanding of readiness enables tailored preparation for caregivers and facilitates collaboration with local organizations to support PC patients and their families. Consequently, this design is suited for gaining an in-depth understanding of such a complex phenomenon [35, 36].

Most primary caregivers (78.79%) provided HBPC for non-cancer patients, consistent with previous findings in rural Thailand [37]. Caregiver readiness reached the highest mean score in family maintenance and consensus (mean = 4.15; SD = 0.60), reflecting that patients had established an Advance Care Plan (ACP) during outpatient palliative services at community hospitals. These findings are consistent with the established role of ACP as a patient-centered process that integrates the values and preferences of patients, families, and healthcare professionals into the decision-making. By addressing early PC needs and prioritizing non-invasive care. ACP significantly enhances the quality of end-of-life care. In addition, it serves as a tool for preparing families for grief and mitigating potential conflicts through clear, value-based treatment plans [38, 39].

Financial stability emerged as a determinant of caregiver readiness for HBPC. This supports that a salary provides the necessary financial security to cover medical expenses, essential services, transportation, and hired assistance, thereby reducing the caregiving burden. On the other hand, transitioning to full-time caregiving often leads to significant financial withdrawals and income reductions, aligning with previous studies indicating decreases of as high as 84% for patients and 35% for families [40–42]. In this study, 71.21% of caregivers are concerned about financial burdens, emphasising that readiness depends on financial stability, health status, and caregiving confidence [43]. This study suggested that local organizations should continue to support essential PC supplies such as diapers, beds, mattresses, and necessary medical devices. Communities should utilize local social capital, such as food and supply donations from temples and older adult clubs. For caregivers who left employment but still possess professional skills, job training and career opportunities should be coordinated with the local organization or the Department of Skill Development after the patient’s death to help caregivers sustain their livelihood.

The perceived burden of care significantly influences caregiver readiness. In this study, 89.90% of caregivers managed household tasks without difficulty, and the majority (72.22%) were family members. This perception of caregiving as a meaningful act rather than a burden is rooted in Thai cultural values like gratitude (katanyu), loving kindness, and merit-making. Although caregiver burden is multidimensional, burdens may persist in emotional domains or in the clinical management of complex symptoms inherent in palliative care. Evidence suggests that cultural context shapes the experience of burden and that increased preparedness can help reduce it [44]. While patient symptoms such as depression or anxiety can intensify the felt burden, these factors are often manageable [45]. Therefore, healthcare providers should assess both patients’ needs and caregiver burden to enable appropriate interventions.

Regarding the experience of primary caregiver readiness for HBPC, the integration of qualitative and quantitative data revealed alignment regarding family maintenance and consensus and awareness and attitudes towards palliative care. This readiness is supported by spiritual beliefs, particularly Buddhism, widely practised in this study. Buddhist teachings, specifically the Four Noble Truths and the Middle Path, help caregivers and patients cope with suffering. Belief in reincarnation, karma, and peaceful death leads to greater acceptance of mortality [46]. Compassionate care and emotional support further aid this acceptance, even in incurable cases [47]. Among older adult Buddhists, high levels of death preparedness (88.2%) have been observed, with spiritual maturity and attitudes toward death showing significant correlations with preparation (r = .36 and 0.34, p < .01) [48]. In this study, families shared similar care goals and shared responsibility, supported by medical guidance that clarified symptom-based care and end-of-life choices. Most agreed to noninvasive treatment and preferred dying at home.

The integration of qualitative and quantitative data revealed alignment between home care skills, end-of-life preparation, and end-of-life caregiving skills at home and household readiness. Caregivers emphasized the importance of home-based skills, the home environment, medical equipment, patient transport to hospitals, and financial adequacy. Many patients preferred to remain at home due to emotional comfort and familiarity. Environmental factors including privacy, home-like settings, sound, temperature, and light significantly impact symptom tolerance and emotional control at the end of life [49]. In this study, all homes had suitable ground-floor spaces for the PC patients. In addition, local organization supports the necessary medical equipment. Most caregivers (72.22%) were family members who managed personal care, medications, and symptoms like pain or dyspnoea, gaining confidence through training and support from home health care teams. Supporting caregivers in retaining employment provides mutual benefits for the patient, the caregiver, the economy, and the labor market [50]. Consequently, employers and policymakers should develop policies to support working caregivers [50]. Families essentially require early palliative guidance, clinical advice, emotional support, and grief counseling [51]. Addressing challenges such as skill deficits, insufficient support, and limited medication access is crucial [52]. This study suggests that healthcare providers should deliver early PC services, provide home-visit support, offer counselling to help caregivers build confidence, and support caregivers in maintaining their employment.

The integration of qualitative and quantitative data demonstrates a concordance regarding family commitment and shared responsibility in caregiving. This can be explained by the fact that, in this study, the primary caregivers were family members who provided care for their loved ones. Therefore, they did not perceive caregiving as a burden. Two types of primary caregivers were observed: (1) full-time caregivers who resigned from their jobs and had a full-time caregiver and (2) full-time caregivers who continued working but had a family member or a hired caregiver assist while they went to work. Despite these cultural strengths, caregivers emphasized the need for care rotation to prevent burnout. High burden levels and limited social resources often lead caregivers to seek temporary respite care [53–55]. Because social support is a factor in reducing caregiver burden [56], healthcare providers should offer counselling and collaborate with community groups or local organizations to strengthen social support and alleviate the caregiver burden.

Caregiver readiness to manage home emergencies showed consistency between quantitative and qualitative findings. Primary caregivers were provided with guidance on PC during home visits, which was supported by the LTC fund. Common physical symptoms requiring such preparation include pain, breathlessness, nausea, vomiting, and constipation, as well as psychosocial distress [57]. Early PC consultations help patients and families prepare for symptom progression and manage emotional distress as the illness advances [58]. Therefore, healthcare providers should be encouraged to offer regular counselling, inform families about common symptoms and emergency signs, and train primary caregivers to manage urgent symptoms at home.

Family support after the patient’s death emerges because caregiver readiness extends beyond the patient’s final moments and includes planning for life after loss. Preparing for post-bereavement support reduces stress and ensures long-term stability for caregivers, particularly those of working age who sacrificed employment for full-time care. Evidence suggests that caregivers with longer adaptation periods beyond six months can cope with complicated grief [59]. In addition, communication between healthcare professionals and families is vital, as it profoundly shapes the relatives’ perceptions of the care experience [60]. In addition, caregivers are concerned about financial security. Significant financial impacts are most noticeable among women and those under age 50, with practical changes often persisting more than three years post-bereavement [61]. To address these needs, healthcare providers should deliver ongoing information about illness progression and establish holistic bereavement programs. These services should include group support, grief education, and collaboration with local organizations to assist primary caregivers with future career planning.

Implications

This study contributes to the literature by identifying factors associated with the readiness for HBPC among primary caregivers and explaining the experiences of these outcomes. This study was conducted in the Thai context, which has implemented a community-based long-term care model to support dependent individuals and PC patients. This model includes all sectors, including population, community, healthcare providers, and local organizations, to work together. This study has implications for practice. The health sector should continue to promote ACP and provide continuing training for primary caregivers and community caregivers supported by the LTC fund using experience-based learning. Training should cover (1) disease progression and basic home care, (2) emergency symptom management at home, and (3) the learning needs of caregivers, along with access to remote consultation via mobile phones. Local organizations should continuously support essential PC equipment and offer financial planning assistance to families. In addition, health care and local organizations should collaborate with communities to employ local human resources by training community caregivers, creating employment opportunities, and developing bereavement care programs that help families manage grief and support caregivers in planning for their future, including developing skills that enable them to work after the patient’s death.

Study limitations

First, the cross-sectional design does not evaluate the changes over time. Additionally, as mixed-methods research requires significant methodological rigor, this study engaged experts with experience in both statistical analysis and qualitative inquiry to ensure overall technical strength. Second, this study was conducted in a rural area within one province, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. However, the results can be transferred to similar contexts and may be replicated in other regions across the country because the same PC service plan is implemented nationwide. Lastly, although the qualitative approach allowed for an in-depth understanding of caregiver readiness in HBPC, some researchers were healthcare providers, which may have introduced information bias due to power imbalances. To minimize this bias, data collection was carried out by a researcher who was not involved in providing PC services.

Future research on interventions to explore the effectiveness of experiential training, mobile consultations, and community-based employment support in strengthening caregiver readiness for HBPC is needed. In addition, the effectiveness of financial counselling and bereavement programs influences primary caregivers’ well-being and their ability to continue caregiving roles to ensure the readiness of the primary caregiver.

Conclusions

This study provides an empirical basis for understanding readiness for HBPC among primary caregivers and highlights the need for collaboration across populations, communities, health sectors, and local organizations. Specifically, health professionals should increase the readiness of primary caregivers, support their acceptance of death, and engage in shared decision making. To provide holistic family support, the recommendations include: experiential caregiver training, mobile consultation services, community-based employment support, financial counselling, and bereavement care programs to ensure that primary caregivers are ready to provide PC at home.
