Microbially enhanced dissolution of calcite in sinking marine particles
Authors: Benedict Borer, Adam V. Subhas, Matthew G. Hayden, Ryan J. Woosley, Andrew R. Babbin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe oceans regulate atmospheric CO2 over geologic time through transformations involving calcium carbonate. Observations have revealed that rapid dissolution must occur in the shallow ocean, but we lack a mechanistic understanding to constrain this important sink in the oceanic carbon budget. Here, we quantify how microbial activity in marine particles creates acidic microenvironments that enhance calcium carbonate dissolution, even in waters supersaturated with respect to the mineral. Our results suggest that a nuanced chemical landscape inside the marine particles drives the observed dissolution, with microbially enhanced rates exceeding abiotic dissolution more than 10-fold at the same bulk saturation. Our findings highlight the importance of microbial activity in marine particles for the ocean’s carbonate budget and its role in carbon storage.

The balance among production, dissolution, and burial of calcium carbonate (CaCO3) regulates the ocean’s ability to absorb atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2) over geological time scales (1). Biogenic CaCO3 produced in the sunlit ocean gets entrained in marine particles and enhances organic and inorganic carbon export due to its ballasting effect (2–4). Any calcium carbonate reaching sediments is buried and acts as a sink of alkalinity—reducing the capacity of seawater to buffer against changes in pH. This buried alkalinity is additionally a reservoir for buffering against long-term climate shifts on glacial timescales (5). In the open ocean, sinking CaCO3 that reaches a critical depth known as the saturation horizon is subject to thermodynamically driven dissolution due to decreasing temperature, and both increasing pressure and dissolved inorganic carbon (6). Yet, inference from excess alkalinity (7, 8), sediment traps (6, 9–13), meta-analysis (14), and modeling approaches (15–18) implies that a significant fraction of calcium carbonate exported from surface waters must dissolve in the shallow ocean far above the saturation horizon despite supersaturated bulk conditions. More than two decades ago, Milliman et al. postulated that acidic microenvironments, such as zooplankton guts and marine particles, may be hotspots for dissolution (19) and are collectively responsible for dissolving up to 80% of the biogenic CaCO3 in the upper 1,000 m. Since then, several studies have suggested a significant biological control on the pelagic water column CaCO3 dissolution (6, 7, 15–18, 20, 21). A recent study showed that up to 92% of ingested calcite dissolves in the acidic digestive tract of microzooplankton (21), with another showing that copepod grazing can also dissolve a significant fraction of ingested calcite (22). In contrast, the dissolution of CaCO3 inside sinking marine particles is poorly constrained despite their incredible abundance throughout the oceans, and a mechanistic understanding of particle-associated microbial metabolism and CaCO3 dissolution dynamics remains lacking.

The tendency of CaCO3 to dissolve is modeled by the saturation state of seawater Ω=[Ca2+][CO32-]/Ksp′. [Ca2+] and [CO32–] are typically taken as the calcium and carbonate ion concentrations in bulk water, respectively, and Ksp′—a function of temperature, pressure, and salinity (23, 24)—is the in-situ apparent solubility product of the calcium carbonate polymorph considered (e.g., high magnesium calcite, aragonite, or calcite). Whenever the product of calcium and carbonate ion concentrations exceeds the solubility product (i.e., Ω > 1), there is a thermodynamic tendency to precipitate the mineral, with the reverse (Ω < 1) indicating dissolution should occur. Calcium carbonate is supersaturated across the global marine surface, with Ω of the most stable polymorph, calcite, varying from approximately 2 in polar waters to 6 in the tropics (25). Saturation decreases with depth largely due to pressure effects. Microbial metabolic activity also impacts the saturation state by modifying the pH and/or the carbonate chemistry of seawater. Most CO2 released from microbial respiration equilibrates with the surrounding seawater (24), forming bicarbonate and releasing a proton (H+). This acidification shifts the relative composition of dissolved inorganic carbon compounds away from the carbonate anion, consequently reducing the saturation state Ω. In addition to pressure effects, the depth of the calcite saturation horizon (Ω = 1) is impacted by these biological processes and ranges from 4 km deep in the North Atlantic to 1 km in the subpolar North Pacific (17, 26). Notably less stable polymorphs such as aragonite and high magnesium calcite each have a shallower saturation horizon than calcite and will dissolve at higher carbonate ion concentrations.

At the scale of an individual marine particle, the elevated metabolic activity can alter the chemistry when compared to the bulk seawater due to diffusive limitations. Hyperlocalized accumulation or depletion occurs if the consumption or production of a chemical species exceeds the diffusive transport rate. For example, high respiration rates can deplete oxygen in marine particles (27, 28), creating microniches for anoxic metabolisms (29–34) all within a few hundred micrometers. High flow around sinking particles increases the diffusive exchange of solutes with the surrounding seawater (35), and the emerging biogeochemical landscape is thus sensitive to the sinking rate. Since calcium carbonate is an important ballast mineral that enhances the settling velocity of particles (4), there is a reciprocal feedback between the dissolution of calcite and the diffusive transport of solutes to and from the particle. Ultimately, the impact of microbial metabolism on the intraparticle pH and related saturation state proximal to the mineral surface is a function of the consumed carbon source, the diffusive exchange with the surrounding seawater, and the resulting metabolic pathway employed by the microorganisms (36–39).

Interrogating the impact of microbial metabolism on the hyperlocalized biogeochemistry of individual particles in situ is difficult due to their fragile nature. While several techniques have been employed, such as microelectrodes (27, 33, 40), particle image velocimetry (41, 42) coupled with oxygen nanosensors (43), or fluorescent in situ hybridization (44, 45), they typically impede longitudinal observation or the quantification of intraparticle gradients. Microfluidic devices containing marine particle analogs can circumvent this issue and resolve microbial growth dynamics (46), emerging oxygen gradients (47), and associated anoxic metabolisms (29, 30) at high resolution in both space and time. Here, we quantify oxygen concentrations and pH inside marine particle analogs embedded in microfluidic devices around which seawater 3.4× supersaturated with respect to calcite flows (Fig. 1) to estimate the intraparticle calcite saturation state. Our disk-shaped marine particle analogs, generated from agarose that permits direct control of their composition, shape, and microbial community, represent a simplified geometry of aggregated marine snow or fecal pellet cross-sections. The processes driving alterations to the microscale biogeochemistry (microbial self-engineering enabled by diffusive limitation) are a unifying feature across marine particles of any shape, provided sufficient microbial activity, and the processes demonstrated here are thus applicable to particles of all origins. We further measure the rate of CaCO3 dissolution using 13C-labeled calcite via the evolved inorganic carbon dissolved in the effluent. Calcite was chosen for experimentation as the most stable CaCO3 polymorph in the modern ocean. More soluble polymorphs entrained in marine particles, such as aragonite or high-magnesium calcite, would likely dissolve sooner than calcite. We further disentangle the influence of carbon utilization by providing either carbohydrates (glucose) or organic acids (citrate) and systematically investigate the effect of settling velocity by adjusting the flow of artificial seawater (ASW) through the microfluidic device.

Results

Bacterial Metabolism Shapes the pH Landscape at the Microscale Inside Particles.

The impact of microbial metabolism on the pH depends on the metabolic pathway and oxidized carbon source. In a first step, we grew the marine isolate Alteromonas macleodii on various carbon sources (carbohydrates and organic acids) in batch culture to assess how the microbial metabolism impacts the acid–base chemistry of the bulk seawater (Fig. 1C). Aerobic respiration of carbohydrates such as glucose typically lowers the pH following the paradigm of CO2 reaction with water to create carbonic acid (36). In contrast, the pH increases when respiring organic acids (37, 38). We subsequently embedded this bacterium together with calcite inside marine particle analogs to quantify whether localized but intense bacterial metabolism can facilitate calcium carbonate dissolution. To gauge the impact of respiration-driven acidification on the localized pH and saturation state, we measured the intraparticle pH and oxygen concentrations using noninvasive fluorescent probes for particles experiencing fluid flow equivalent to settling at 10 m d−1 (Methods). Based on our preliminary experiments in batch culture, we chose glucose as the sole carbon source that supported the strongest acidification of the seawater media. In contrast to sterile particles, the pH (calibrated to the total pH scale) decreased in the presence of microbial growth (Fig. 2A). From an initial pH of ~7.95, equivalent to the pH of the bulk experimental seawater, the pH inside particles containing bacteria rapidly decreased after approximately 8 h and stabilized at ~7.3 (Fig. 2B and Table 1, all data and variability provided in SI Appendix, Table S1). This reduction in pH is in line with previous measurements of localized acidification inside marine snow aggregates (40). In contrast, sterile particles remained at a pH of 7.95 ± 0.01 throughout the experiment. The heterogeneity of pH within the particles could not be resolved due to the opaque nature of the pH sensor spots. Using fluorescent oxygen nanoprobes, we concurrently measured the oxygen concentration in three separate particles of the same microfluidic device. Congruent with the observations of pH, oxygen was rapidly depleted from full air saturation in particles containing bacterial colonies, but remained saturated in sterile particles. The mean oxygen saturation across the particles diminished to ~50% after 24 h but had yet to reach a steady state, suggesting that the overall oxygenation of the particles would continue to decline. Interestingly, the temporal dynamics of acidification and deoxygenation are disconnected (Fig. 2C). Initially, pH declined rapidly during a period where deoxygenation of the particle remained limited. Once oxygen was consumed more quickly inside the particles, the pH stabilized. The observed variations in oxygen and pH dynamics must be driven by metabolic processes that shift the pH landscape inside the particles. Abiotic factors, such as the response time of the oxygen probes and pH sensor spots (instantaneous and approximately 60 s, respectively) or the hydration time scale of CO2 (~20 s), are negligible relative to the overall duration of the experiment.

Based on our measurements of pH inside the particle and the total alkalinity, we can calculate the saturation state for calcite (Fig. 2D, Table 1, and SI Appendix, Table S1). Due to the rapid acidification in particles containing bacteria, the mean intraparticle saturation state declined from ~3.4 (the value of the synthetic seawater that reflects surface conditions across much of the global oceans) and stabilized just below one, where dissolution becomes thermodynamically favored. In contrast, no change in saturation state was observed for sterile particles. Interestingly, bulk changes in DIC, pH, and TA from respiration-derived CO2 alone are not sufficient for particle-averaged undersaturation based on the stoichiometry of the respiration reaction (Fig. 2D and Eqs. 2 and 3 in Methods section). This suggests that other mechanisms, such as acidic intermediate metabolites released by the bacteria [e.g., via overflow metabolism (50)] or acid-producing pathways other than complete aerobic respiration of organic carbon to CO2, impact the pH and, thus, the saturation state.

Finally, we measured the dissolution rate of CaCO3 due to this respiration-driven undersaturation using 13C-labeled calcite (Fig. 2E). As expected, calcite dissolves more rapidly with a decrease in the degree of saturation. Interestingly, we observed calcite dissolution already after 12 h, where the mean intraparticle saturation states remained above 1. This suggests that heterogeneity within the particle (microscale pockets of localized acidity likely around bacterial colony growth) drove dissolution at even finer scales, whereas our reported saturation states reflect average values across the particle. Empirically, the dissolution rate is typically related to the saturation state as R=k(1-Ω)n where k is the dissolution rate constant of the calcium carbonate mineral polymorph, and n is a fit parameter that captures the nonlinearity between the dissolution rate R and the saturation state (48). However, since we capture particle-averaged saturation states that may differ from the saturation state at the mineral surface, we refrain from fitting a similar equation. Indeed, we postulate that the same abiotic dissolution equation applies, but the Ω needed to properly fit is that directly in contact with the mineral surface within the particle, not that of bulk seawater surrounding the particle. For this reason, compared to the literature data, our dissolution rates are in the same order of magnitude, although shifted to higher saturation states (Fig. 2E). For example, the dissolution rate of ~10−12 mol cm−2 s−1 we observe within marine snow after 24 h coincides with a mean particle saturation state of 0.91. In contrast, dissolution rates of the same magnitude were previously measured at saturation states between 0.7 and 0.8 in abiotic bulk dissolution experiments (49). At a corresponding mean saturation state, the particle rates are over an order of magnitude faster than those reported for inorganic dissolution experiments in bulk seawater. This shift in dissolution rate is driven by the hyperlocalized biogeochemistry altered by microorganisms operating in confinement. Contextualizing these experiments for the ocean, we note that aragonite or high magnesium calcite contained in a real seawater particle would dissolve preferentially before the calcite observed here, thus acting to stabilize calcite itself (51).

Carbon Type and Settling Velocity Influence CaCO3 Dissolution Rates in Particles.

We subsequently set out to disentangle the role of carbon source type and settling velocity on the calcite dissolution rate. To do so, we performed experiments with either glucose (glycolytic metabolism) or citrate (gluconeogenetic metabolism) as the sole carbon source at imposed fluid flow corresponding to sinking speeds of 5, 10, or 50 m d−1 that reflect the settling velocities of natural particles. Dissolution rates were calculated across the 48-hr experiment (Table 2, Fig. 3, and SI Appendix, Table S2). At 5 m d−1, a significant dissolution rate was measured for glucose as the carbon source after 12 h of incubation and persisted until the experiment’s conclusion. In contrast, no considerable dissolution was observed when citrate was the sole carbon source. This pattern changed at 10 m d−1, where both glucose and citrate as carbon sources resulted in enhanced calcite dissolution. At 50 m d−1, no significant dissolution was measured for either glucose or citrate. The wide range in dissolution rates at 50 m d−1 was not due to higher variability in observed δ13C in the effluent (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), but rather a result of the dissolution rate calculation where small changes in the δ13C signal become amplified due to the dilution effect at higher flow rates. Both the settling velocity and carbon type significantly influence the dissolution rate after 48 h (two-way ANOVA, P = 0.0005 and P = 0.0015, respectively).

Next, we calculated the percentage of the initial calcium carbonate that dissolved during the 48-h incubation by numerically integrating the dissolution rates across time (Fig. 3B). Significant dissolution only occurred at 5 m d−1 for glucose (one-sample t test, P = 0.0001) and at 10 m d−1 for both glucose and citrate (one-sample t test, P = 0.0192 and 0.0014, respectively). When comparing the two carbon sources within each settling velocity, there is a significant difference at 5 m d−1 (two-sample t test, P = 0.0011), but no difference for both 10 m d−1 (P = 0.2459) and 50 m d−1 (P = 0.8483). While the fraction of calcite that dissolved during the experiment at first seems inconsequential (~0.1% of the initial amount), they become substantial due to the long transfer times through the mesopelagic ocean during settling. At our imposed settling velocities of 5, 10, and 50 m d−1, a particle would take 200, 100, and 20 d to traverse from the surface ocean to the approximate 1,000 m depth of the thermocline distinguishing the deep ocean from the mesopelagic. Extrapolating our experimentally measured dissolution from 48 h to these time scales results in an average calcite dissolution of 15%, 14%, and 8% for glucose at 5 m d−1, glucose at 10 m d−1, and citrate at 10 m d−1, respectively (Fig. 3C, mean values provided in Table 2). These are conservative estimates since they are calculated based on the average dissolution across 48 h even though acceleration of dissolution can be expected from the initial condition as bacteria begin to colonize a sinking particle. Using the observed dissolution rates at the conclusion of the experiments, up to 35%, 65%, and 23% of the incorporated CaCO3 may dissolve for glucose at 5 m d−1, glucose at 10 m d−1, and citrate at 10 m d−1, respectively. Of importance to note is that more soluble calcium carbonate polymorphs (aragonite and high-magnesium calcite) would exhibit higher amounts of dissolution than observed for calcite. While there is much uncertainty when extrapolating an experiment to the ocean, our observations indicate that the intraparticle biogeochemistry that develops during respiration yields a significant loss of calcium carbonate at depths shallower than the saturation horizon. Furthermore, the compounding nature of respiration-driven and abiotic impacts on the saturation state inside particles, especially the decrease of the bulk saturation state due to the increasing pressure and DIC with depth, would enhance the decrease in saturation state with depth, effects that we did not consider when extrapolating our results.

Discussion

Dissolution Optimum Arises Due to Microscale Heterogeneity and Diffusive Limitations.

The notion that pelagic calcium carbonate is conserved in the surface ocean is a long-standing paradigm in oceanography (19). Yet, a number of recent studies have emphasized that a significant amount of CaCO3 must dissolve in the surface ocean (6, 7, 15–18, 20), and microenvironments such as zooplankton guts and marine snow particles have been postulated as possible hotspots for calcite dissolution (19). Here, we showed that localized acidification inside marine particles driven by microbial metabolism beyond just CO2 generation enhances the dissolution of calcite, the most stable polymorph of calcium carbonate.

A key observation is that the dissolution rate varies as a function of the settling velocity. We can explain this by considering how the localized flow around particles alters the exchange of nutrients and oxygen to and from the particle. Mass transfer can be simplified to diffusion since marine particles are generally viewed as gelatinous but impermeable aggregates (41). As such, nutrient and oxygen gradients (the difference between the concentration at the particle surface and the bulk divided by the boundary layer thickness) are the main drivers of the overall resource flux. Gradients may extend far into the surrounding liquid at low settling velocities and for suspended particles (Fig. 4A), diminishing the diffusive flux (35). When the settling velocity increases, the gradient field around the particle becomes increasingly asymmetric as it is warped around the particle and forms an elongated plume. This results in steep solute gradients, especially in front of and perpendicular to the particle settling axis, enhancing the diffusive exchange (Fig. 4B). We hypothesize that bacterial growth is hampered at low settling velocities (5 m d−1) due to inorganic nutrient and oxygen limitation inside the particle, resulting in an overall lower metabolic activity and associated dissolution of calcite (Fig. 3). In addition, slow exchange with the surrounding water increases carbonate ions due to the dissolving calcite, giving rise to a self-buffering system that hampers further dissolution. Indeed, in the real world, preferential dissolution by high-magnesium calcite and aragonite might help to preserve calcite in particles containing a mixture of polymorphs (51). At 10 m d−1, a higher diffusive flux permits more rapid growth, reducing the pH inside the particles, and promotes flushing of liberated calcium and carbonate ions from the particle, thereby enhancing the calcite dissolution rate. Finally, at even higher diffusive exchange with the surrounding fluid (50 m d−1), the supply of nutrients and oxygen to bacteria metabolizing inside the particles is considerable, but acidic waste products such as carbon dioxide are rapidly washed away, which limits local acidification. This hypothesis is substantiated by previous findings where a higher flow rate around a marine particle analog increases the microbial carrying capacity (47) or enhances the degradation rate because inhibitory waste products are flushed more rapidly (46). Additionally, elevated flow velocities can raise the pH inside biofilms (52), supporting the notion that higher flow rates restrict local acidification. As such, accounting for the nature of the marine snow particles becomes important. For instance, fecal pellets typically sink faster than marine snow aggregates, which alters the nutrient exchange with the surrounding fluid and impacts the final dissolution rate. In summary, we find that the dissolution rate is a nonlinear function of the settling velocity dictated by the relative rates of bacterial metabolic activity and solute exchange. The highest dissolution rates, therefore, occur at a delicate optimum where the solute exchange to and from the particle sustains rapid growth of the resident bacterial community but not so much as to reset localized acidification within the particle.

At the single particle level, a fabric of physical, chemical, and biological factors constrains the spatiotemporal development of metabolic, pH, and saturation landscapes. Following an initial rapid decline, the pH (and related saturation state) inside the particles gradually stabilizes, indicating that the system approaches a quasi-steady state (Fig. 2). In comparison, oxygen concentrations exhibit a more gradual decline over the course of the experiments that persists during the phase of pH stabilization. These observations suggest that the particle-averaged pH and oxygen consumption are only weakly coupled. We hypothesize that several concurrent factors, including the spatial distribution of the active microbial community within the particles, differences in the temporal diffusive dynamics of oxygen and components of the carbonate system, and the buffering by dissolving calcite, contribute to the observed patterns.

Initially, nutrient and oxygen limitations within the particles are negligible, allowing for spatially homogeneous aerobic respiration. Only once bacterial metabolism (consumption of oxygen or production of CO2) exceeds the diffusive transport can local accumulation or depletion occur. The diffusivity of oxygen (a measure of how quickly solutes spread) is double that of bicarbonate ions, the most dominant form of DIC and determinant of pH shifts during aerobic respiration (18). As such, a localized DIC accumulation and associated drop in pH can occur in the absence of oxygen drawdown if microbial metabolism exceeds the threshold for bicarbonate accumulation but oxygen demand remains lower than its diffusive supply. This initial decoupling is later obfuscated by continued bacterial growth and oxygen consumption as the experiment proceeds. Since respiration-derived CO2 cannot explain the magnitude of pH decline (Fig. 2A), other processes must contribute in tandem.

Emerging oxygen limitation within the particles restricts aerobic respiration to the particle periphery, where it intercepts the diffusive supply of oxygen and subsequently renders the particle partially anoxic (29, 30). While A. macleodii lacks the ability to respire in the absence of oxygen (e.g., via denitrification), overflow metabolism, defined as the seemingly wasteful strategy of using fermentation instead of more efficient respiration to optimize proteome allocation (50), may still occur. Organic acids, such as acetic, lactic, or pyruvic (with pKa around 4.8, 3.9, and 2.5, respectively), are common byproducts of overflow metabolism, even in oxygenated environments (53). This acid production has fundamental consequences in a spatial particle context. First, acidic byproducts can accumulate inside the particle early on, resulting in a rapid decline of pH despite moderate oxygen depletion. Second, these organic acids can be respired with residual oxygen that diffuses into the particle, resulting in a cross-feeding interaction between microbial cells all within hundreds of micrometers (54). Since bacterial growth on organic acids elevates the pH of the surrounding media (Fig. 1C), this spatially sequential metabolic activity can help explain not only the initial decoupling of pH and oxygen dynamics, but could also contribute toward a chemical equilibrium that stabilizes the pH toward later stages of the experiment by acting as a proton sink toward the core of the particle.

Finally, the dissolution of calcite inside the particle aids in buffering the pH, which stabilizes just below the saturation point of calcite (Fig. 1D). In our experiments, the observed dissolution of calcite cannot neutralize all of carbonic acid produced from aerobic respiration as calculated from the observed oxygen consumption dynamics and stoichiometry of oxygen and CO2 in aerobic respiration of glucose. However, to determine the required buffering inside the particle to stabilize the pH, spatially resolved maps of the metabolic activity and associated pH are required of future work able to reconcile such heterogeneity. Nevertheless, we expect that this localized cycling of calcium carbonate plays a governing role in setting the intraparticle carbonate chemistry and saturation landscape.

At even finer scales, our results further suggest that heterogeneity within the particles is the driving mechanism behind the dissolution of calcite. Previous studies have revealed steep oxygen and pH gradients inside marine aggregates (33, 40). Chemical gradients, specifically oxygen, are amplified in microbial colonies (at scales of ~20 to 50 µm) embedded within a marine particle due to the high respiration rate within the biofilm matrix (29, 30). Heterogeneity even emerges across a microcolony of bacteria due to growth and metabolism in confinement (29). By analogy, localized calcite dissolution hotspots can plausibly arise proximal to microbial activity hotspots inside the particles. While our measured dissolution rates are of similar magnitude to those previously determined dissolution rates as a function of the saturation state (Fig. 2E), our rates are offset to higher saturation conditions due to the nuances among bulk, particle-averaged, and mineral-adjacent saturation states. The final landscape of dissolution rates within a particle is determined by the trade-off between mass transfer limitations and the associated growth dynamics of microbial colonies, as well as the constraints imposed by mineral dissolution kinetics. Further work is needed to demonstrate how nutrient conditions and growth rates determine the intraparticle pH landscape, as well as novel methods (both experimental and computational) to concurrently capture the microscale pH, oxygen, and metabolic distributions within particles. This is essential for establishing a mechanistic understanding of biologically enhanced calcium carbonate dissolution within particles. The distinct differences in pH change (Fig. 2A) and dissolution rate (Fig. 3) between glucose and citrate metabolism highlight the unique role of carbon source on intraparticle pH and carbonate chemistry dynamics. The cycling of organic alkalinity—from, e.g., organic conjugate bases—within particle microenvironments thus may exert significant control on the pH of particles. In this fashion, the microscale phenomena documented here are similar to how organic compounds can contribute to alkalinity distributions in natural waters (55–57).

Extrapolation to the Water Column and Feedback of Dissolving Ballast.

Based on our measured dissolution rates and imposed settling velocities of 5 m d−1 and 10 m d−1, a conservative minimum estimate 10 to 20% of the calcite inside the particles may dissolve while settling from the surface ocean to 1,000 m (Fig. 3C). These estimates align with a recent assessment that up to 30% of the calcite exported in marine snow must dissolve in the mesopelagic (21) and are within the margin of previous total particulate inorganic carbon (PIC) dissolution estimates between 30% and 70% in the upper 1,000 m (6, 11, 16, 19). While these estimates generally exceed our prediction of 10 to 20%, they consider total PIC dissolution that includes more fragile polymorphs such as aragonite or high-magnesium calcites, while we focus on the most stable polymorph, calcite. Thus, if calcite is dissolving, we would expect an even greater fraction of aragonite and high-magnesium calcite to dissolve, potentially entirely before calcite, due to the differences in their solubility. However, extrapolating our results to the water column comes with some challenges. For instance, while we observe a gradual increase in dissolution rate for experiments containing glucose at 5 m d−1 and 10 m d−1, these rates may alter as biological growth reaches a steady state or microbial activity wanes. We further observe that the most significant decline in pH is not concurrent with the decrease in particle oxygenation (Fig. 2C). After an initial rapid decline, the pH (Fig. 2B) and associated saturation state (Fig. 2D) stabilize once oxygen saturation declines within the particle (Fig. 2B). These observations hint at the onset of anaerobic metabolisms that increase the pH slightly via a net consumption of protons (39) or stabilization via buffering by unflushed dissolution products. While particle deoxygenation and associated buffering from anoxic metabolisms may be most prevalent in large particles, changes in the bulk oxygenation experienced by particles sinking through an oxygen minimum zone or subjected to low-oxygen extremes such as those observed in the wake of phytoplankton blooms (58) can extend anaerobic processes across the particle size spectrum and preserve calcium carbonate transiting through the mesopelagic, enhancing the efficiency of the carbonate pump. Consequently, further research is required to understand how bulk dynamics during settling and compounding dissolution from various carbonate polymorphs collectively determine the fate of PIC in the water column.

Calcium carbonate inside marine particles is a critical ballasting material that determines the settling velocity by increasing the excess density of marine snow and enhancing the export of carbon to the deep ocean. We derive an idealized equation for the relative change in settling velocity (Urelative) based on Stokes’ law that incorporates terms for the dissolution of ballasting material inside the particle and a change in particle size due to remineralization (Eq. 1, derivation provided in Methods). Here, α represents the fraction of the original amount of ballasting material (in our case calcite) that has dissolved and γ the relative change in equivalent spherical diameter due to remineralization.[1]Urelative=1-αγ.

The predicted change in settling velocity as a function of dissolution and remineralization is shown in Fig. 4C. If all calcite dissolves while the particle size remains unchanged, the settling velocity approaches zero due to the absence of ballasting material. In contrast, if the calcite is preserved during particle remineralization (size reduction), the settling rate increases due to increased excess density. The maximum relative increase in settling velocity scales with the inverse of the initial volume fraction of ballasting material. In our analytical model, the change in settling velocity and related residence time in the water column is thus sensitive to the initial amount of calcite, the dissolution dynamics of calcite, and the remineralization rate of POC. We can estimate how these changes in settling velocity impact the transit time through the mesopelagic. If the dissolution occurs at a constant rate for most of the settling distance, the reduction in the settling velocity translates linearly to an increase in the residence time via the average settling velocity. As such, a dissolution of 20% in the upper 1,000 m of the ocean for a particle settling at 10 m d−1 translates to an increase in its residence time of 10% or ~10 d, assuming no considerable change in the particle size. Regardless of any change in particle size, the dissolution of calcium carbonate diminishes the excess density of the particle relative to seawater, slowing the particle and increasing its transit time to the deep ocean. We refrain from translating particle-associated calcite dissolution to PIC flux TA profiles, as this would require detailed information about size-resolved PIC concentrations, organic carbon lability, microbial activity, and related dissolution rates.

We have shown that microbial respiration inside marine particles can enhance calcium carbonate dissolution even in bulk supersaturated seawater, providing direct evidence for a previously hypothesized process. The magnitude of dissolution is determined by the settling velocity (and associated changes in mass transfer) and specific carbon metabolism driving respiration as CO2 evolution alone is insufficient to explain the acidification observed. Our observations further suggest that hotspots within the particle govern the overall dissolution rate, and more granular investigations are required in the future to distinguish chemistry across the particle landscape. Overall, we have shown that particles sinking at intermediate velocities, particularly those subjected to glycolytic metabolism, are most prone to hosting CaCO3 dissolution in shallow supersaturated waters. While we did not test the effect of particle size on this phenomenon, we hypothesize by extension of the velocity variation that a similar optimum of dissolution exists across the particle size distribution, where intermediately sized particles facilitate the most dissolution because of the contrasting balance between external nutrient supply and flushing of metabolic products. Further, the real ocean will be complicated by other, more soluble polymorphs of calcium carbonate that should dissolve even more readily than the calcite observed here. Our results provide a critical step to decipher the influence of microbially enhanced calcite dissolution in marine snow particles on the vertical transport of particulate inorganic and organic carbon, and the ocean’s ability to sequester carbon at the global scale.

Methods

Fabrication of Millifluidic Devices.

We used an established experimental system that mimics marine snow particles settling through the water column (29, 30, 47). Each microfluidic device contains a base layer, flow chamber, and cover layer. A 1-mm thick microscope slide (Vistavision Microscope Slide, VWR) was used as the base layer to permit direct visualization using an inverted microscope. The flow chamber was cut from Press-to-Seal double-sided adhesive silicone (0.8 mm, Electron Microscopy Sciences) using a Versa laser cutter/engraver (Universal Laser Systems) with parameters set for cutting 1 mm rubber silicone. The flow cell contains a 5-mm wide channel with 8-mm diameter circles spaced 10 mm apart (total of five, Fig. 1). The cover layer comprises a 1/8” acrylic sheet (Clear Cast Acrylic sheet, McMaster-Carr) with two 2.65-mm diameter circular holes for fluidic access. Viton tubing (1/8” OD, 1/16” ID, Black, McMaster-Carr) was inserted directly into the holes and cut flush at the base. Before the experiments, the Press-to-Seal flow chamber cell was attached to the base layer and cleaned rigorously with soap (Liquinox), isopropanol (90%), 0.1 mol L−1 of sodium hydroxide solution, and ultrapure, reverse osmosis water (MilliQ) to remove the laser-cutting residue. The partially assembled device was then dried using compressed air and exposed to a vacuum in a desiccator for 24 h.

Bacterial Strains, Culture Conditions, and Encapsulation in Marine Particle Analogs.

We used ASW to control chemical conditions for all experiments. The recipe is shown in SI Appendix, Table S3. For the specific batch of seawater used for the presented results, the total alkalinity was 2,205 ± 2 µmol kg−1 (measured using a custom total alkalinity titrator built by the lab of Andrew Dickson, UCSD-Scripps), and salinity was 35. At the temperature of the experiments (~20 °C), the saturation state for calcite is 3.4 (i.e., the ASW is more than three times supersaturated for calcite). We used a marine bacterial species, A. macleodii, isolated on Marine Broth 2216 agar plates at sea during a cruise in the Eastern Tropical North Pacific (Falkor cruise FK180624) (39) for all experiments. Isolates were identified using whole genome sequencing. In preparation for experiments, the strain was transferred from a −80 °C cryopreserved culture into a plastic culture tube containing 5 mL of ASW and 10% v/v Lennox lysogeny broth (5 g L−1 NaCl, BD Life Sciences, further abbreviated LB). The bacteria were grown overnight at 30 °C to an absorbance at 600 nm (OD600) of ~1. Subsequently, 50 µL of the culture was resuspended in a culture tube containing 5 mL ASW and 10% v/v LB and further incubated for ~4 h. Finally, we diluted the cells to an OD600 of 0.1 (approximately 107 cells mL−1) using ASW to ensure that the population was in the exponential phase before embedding in the marine particle analogs.

In parallel, we dissolved 15 µg of low-melt agarose (Agarose low gelling temperature, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1 mL of ASW in a block heater set to 70 °C. After 10 min, we set the block heater to 41 °C and added 5 mg of 100% 13C-labeled calcite (Ca13CO3, 99 atom% 13C, Sigma Aldrich). We chose stable isotope-labeled calcite since the dissolution of calcium carbonate in our marine particles would not translate to a measurable change of TA in the effluent (less than 0.002% change if all CaCO3 dissolved) and may be confounded by additional intermediate metabolic products exuded by the bacteria. The calcite was sieved and the fraction between 38 µm and 75 µm was used (most particles were ~70 µm in diameter as determined from microscopy). We added 10 µL of oxygen nano-sensor stock solution (Oxnano, PyroScience, 5 mg mL−1 dissolved in MilliQ) (29) for experiments that quantified localized oxygen concentrations inside the particles. Once the gel cooled to 41 °C, we added 10 µL of the previously prepared bacterial suspension to the agar for particles containing bacteria (resulting in a final concentration of ~105 cells mL−1) or 10 µL of ASW for sterile particles. After vortexing for ~10 s, we pipetted the molten agarose onto a sterilized microscope slide bordered by the same Press-to-Seal silicone, closed the gel-casting device with a second microscope slide, immediately transferred the assembly into a 50-mL centrifuge tube, and let it cool down on a rocking roller-mixer (GTRM-SF, Globe Scientific Inc.). This step was critical to ensure a homogeneous distribution of the calcite particles throughout the agarose. Using the same Press-to-Seal silicone when casting results in an equal thickness of the agarose gel and the flow chamber. When visualizing the intraparticle pH, the agarose was cast to a final thickness of ~850 µm to accommodate the pH sensor spot (described below). Once the agarose had solidified (15 min at room temperature, ~20 °C), we cut the marine particle analogs from the agar slab using 3-mm biopsy punches (Integra Miltex, Disposable Biopsy Punch, 3 mm). We then transferred them into the flow chamber before sealing the millifluidic device with the acrylic cover layer.

Millifluidic Incubation, Effluent Collection, and Image Acquisition.

Each experiment consisted of a reservoir filled with nutrient-enriched ASW, a millifluidic device with five particles (either inoculated or sterile), and a syringe pump (PHD ULTRA, Harvard Apparatus) drawing the ASW through the device and collecting the effluent for downstream analysis. We used 400 mL Schott glass bottles as reservoirs with caps containing four ¼-28 UNF threaded ports (Mobi-Cap for GL45 Threaded Bottles, Restek) to connect the 1/8” Viton tubing from the millifluidic devices. The ASW was enriched with trace elements as described in SI Appendix, Table S4 and either 500 µmol L-1 of carbon from glucose or citrate. We adjusted the partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2) inside the reservoirs to 500 ppm by bubbling a defined mixture of N2/O2 and of N2/CO2 through each reservoir using mass-flow controllers (M/MC series, Alicat Scientific). Finally, we mimicked the settling velocity within the millifluidic device by adjusting the flow rate using a syringe pump. The syringe pump pulled the amended ASW through the millifluidic devices and collected the effluent through a 0.2 µm syringe filter into syringes preloaded with 5 µL of saturated mercuric chloride solution to prevent further growth (10 mL or 50 mL, PlastiPak, BD). We imposed different settling velocities by adjusting the flow rate of the syringe pump calculated based on the geometry of the flow chamber (13.9, 27.8, and 138.9 µL min−1 for settling velocities of 5, 10, and 50 m d−1, respectively). The pumping rate changes the flow velocity of fluid around the particle and, as such, the imitated settling velocity of a particle in a Lagrangian framework. At each time point, 5 mL of the collected effluent was immediately transferred to borosilicate glass vials (5 mL total volume to minimize the headspace) with a screw cap and kept at 4 °C. All glass vials were previously washed with 1% hydrochloric acid and rinsed four times with MilliQ water before drying in an oven overnight. For each simulated settling velocity, we performed five inoculated experiments and three sterile controls, and collected samples every 6 h for 48 h. We further collected samples from the reservoirs at T = 0 h and T = 48 h to correct for any drift, but none was observed.

To obtain intraparticle oxygen concentrations and pH, we prepared particles containing oxygen nano-sensors (Oxnano, Pyroscience) and millifluidic devices with fluorescent pH sensor spots (PHSP5-PK8T, Pyroscience). Due to their small size, the oxygen nano-reporters are considered “real-time” sensors (i.e., instantaneous response to changing oxygen concentrations). The pH sensor spots have a response time of ~60 s (per manufacturer). Experiments (both inoculated and sterile) were mounted on a Nikon Ti2-E inverted microscope fitted with a SOLA SEii 365 Light Engine and Andor Zyla 4.2 sCMOS camera. Both for pH and oxygen visualization, we used a filter set for CY5 (ex 620/60 nm, em 700/75 nm, Chroma 49006). For oxygen concentrations, the particles were visualized at mid-depth with a Sola intensity of 40% and a shutter speed of 100 ms. For intraparticle pH, images were taken at the base of the opaque pH sensor spots with a Sola intensity of 40% and a shutter speed of 32 ms. Each particle was captured in 16-bit with a 10× objective (Plan Fluor DLL) as an automatically stitched 4 × 4 tile scan with 15% overlap. We used three particles in separate flow channels exposed to pH buffer media for calibration. The buffers were created by mixing 3-(N-Morpholino)propanesulfonic acid (MOPS acid, Sigma-Aldrich) and its conjugate base (MOPS sodium salt, Sigma-Aldrich) at defined ratios to a total concentration of 20 mmol L−1 in ASW. The pH of the final buffers on the total scale was measured using meta-cresol Purple (Sigma-Aldrich) following common practices (59) in a 1-cm cuvette. Calibration curves for all time points and an extended range of pH values are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2. To convert the acquired images of fluorescent intensity to oxygen saturation, we performed a two-point calibration where the initial image (T = 0 h) served as fully oxygenated conditions, and a separate image was taken for each experiment with sodium sulfide inside the flow chamber as an oxygen scavenger (Sigma-Aldrich, 10 g L−1) for the fully anoxic condition. Due to the nature of the pH sensor spots, we cannot resolve the intraparticle pH landscape. Patterns observed on the sensor spots stem from the adhesive films and stitching of the tile scan images.

Dissolved Carbonate System Measurements and Dissolution Rate Calculation.

Total DIC, δ13C of DIC, and total alkalinity were analyzed on the same vial collected from the microfluidic experiments. DIC and δ13C analyses were performed first, immediately after opening the vial. Once opened, the sample lines were inserted to the base of the vial and sealed with parafilm tape to limit gas exchange. DIC and δ13C of DIC were determined simultaneously using an Apollo AS-D1 analyzer connected to a Picarro G-2131i cavity ringdown system with CO2 extracted from a 1 mL sample volume by adding 0.5 mL of 3% phosphoric acid (60). A CO2 cutoff of 400 ppm was used to average the samples’ δ13C. Before each analysis, 1.1 mL of sample and 0.7 mL of acid are drawn into the sample syringe to flush out any remnants of previous samples from the system. After the flush, the 1 mL sample is drawn into the calibrated syringe and injected into the reaction chamber, where the resulting CO2 is carried by a zero-air stream to the Picarro G-2131i. Samples were run as single analyses due to volume limitation and calibrated twice daily against an in-house seawater standard that is intercalibrated against seawater Certified Reference Materials for DIC (Dickson batch #204), and known solid materials (NBS-19, IAEA-C2, and NBS-20) for δ13C.

Total alkalinity (TA) was determined using an open-system Gran titration on weighed 2.5-mL single samples using a Metrohm 805 Dosimat, with a 1 mL burette, and an 855 robotic Titrosampler. The 0.04 mol L−1 HCl titrant first acidifies the sample to pH 3.9 before continuing to pH 3.25, dosing at 0.02 mL increments. Before running, the weighed 2.5-mL sample is diluted to a total volume of 5 mL with deionized water to ensure sufficient volume for the titration. The samples are calibrated against in-house seawater standards that are run every 15 samples, to assess acid and electrode drift throughout the day. Total alkalinity was determined using a nonlinear least-squares method (59). Measurement accuracy is tied to our use of certified reference materials for standardizing DIC and TA measurements (Dickson seawater CRM Batch #207) and isotopic reference materials for δ13C (IAEA-C2, NBS-19, and TIRI-F). The precisions of each method, determined as the SD of standards run at three volumes (1, 2, and 3 mL) over multiple analytical sessions (n = 5), were 3.3 µmol kg−1 (DIC), 1.2 µmol kg−1 (TA), and 0.2‰ (δ13C-DIC).

Mass-Transfer Modeling Using COMSOL.

We used COMSOL v6.2 to calculate the mass transfer between a circular particle and the surrounding fluid as a function of the flow velocity. Particles with a diameter of 3 mm were located at the center of a 10-cm by 10-cm rectangle. A finite element approach based on a physically controlled adaptive grid with an average surface-interfacing mesh size of 20 µm was used to simulate flow (CFD module) and diffusion (chemical reaction engineering module) around the particles. Boundary conditions for the flow field were imposed by a mean flow velocity at the lower boundary and an outflow (0 pressure boundary) at the top. The oxygen concentration field is calculated by setting constant concentrations at the particle surface and at the domain boundaries (Dirichlet boundary conditions) with a concentration of 221 µmol kg−1 across all walls (saturated oxygen concentration in seawater at 20 °C) and 0 µmol kg−1 at the particle surface, and a diffusion coefficient simulating oxygen. Simulations were solved for a steady state with flow velocities from 0 m d−1 to 50 m d−1 at 1 m d−1 intervals. The total flux to the particle was calculated by integrating the flux across the circular particle boundary. The relative flux increase (Sherwood number) was then calculated by dividing the total flux by the flux for a suspended particle (i.e., flow velocity of 0).
