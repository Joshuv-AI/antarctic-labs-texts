Molecular and pathological screening of the current circulation of fowlpox and pigeon pox virus in backyard birds
Authors: Rania I Mohamed, Hanaa A Elsamadony, Rana A Alghamdi, Asmaa lbrahim Abdelaziz Zin Eldin, Ahmed EL-Shemy, Sameh Abdel-Moez Amer, Safia MA Bahshwan, Mohamed T El-Saadony, Hemat S El-Sayed, Khaled A El-Tarabily, Aalaa SA Saad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAvian pox is a disease that has devastating impacts on both poultry and wild bird species. Avian pox is caused by various strains of avipoxviruses (APV). Nevertheless, the virus has been detected in pigeons and chickens that are raised in backyard areas, leading to substantial financial damage for small-scale producers. There is a lack of comprehensive information regarding the transmission of APV among birds in the backyards and residential areas. Hence, the present investigation closely monitored and observed APV in domesticated birds residing in backyard areas, with the aim of impeding the transmission of the virus to nearby poultry farms. In 2023, a total of fifty backyard flocks were surveyed for the presence of avian pox disease. Sixteen backyards (14 pigeons and 2 chickens) exhibited warty nodular lesions on their heads and nonfeathered body parts. APV was confirmed in nodular lesions by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and genetic sequencing. All samples from the lesions showed successful amplification of the p4b locus (core protein p4b). Four confirmed samples were tested for pathogenicity on the chicken embryo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM). Histopathological examination revealed ballooning degeneration and numerous intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Bollinger bodies) in the ectoderm of the infected CAM. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the strains clustered into main clade A, with 11 in subclade A2 and 5 in subclade A1. Amino acid identity showed 100% similarity between the vaccine (fowlpox/VSVRI/Egypt) and some detected strains (PP537574 and PP537575). In addition, the PP537576.1 to PP537580.1 and PP537582.1 to PP537585.1 had 2-point mutations compared to the fowlpox/VSVRI/Egypt vaccine. The overall finding of low biosecurity levels in the investigated backyard birds emphasizes the significance of establishing sanitary measures and control vectors to reduce virus transmission routes and disease severity. In conclusion, it is necessary to emphasize the tracking of APV in backyard birds. Concurrently, we advised enhancing hygiene protocols, vector management, and subsequent vaccination to restrict the occurrence of APV outbreaks and prevent their transmission to neighboring poultry farms. Furthermore, it is crucial to incorporate molecular studies in order to enhance the vaccine seeds for disease management.Key words: Avian pox, avipoxvirus, backyards, molecular characterization, pathological change, pock lesion

INTRODUCTION

Avian pox is a disease affecting a wide variety of birds, including both domestic and wild birds. Reduced egg production, blindness, and increased mortality are common and serious consequences of the disease (Bassiouny et al., 2021). Avipoxvirus (APV) is a genus within the Poxviridae family that includes several species. The most common APV are pigeonpox virus (PPV), fowlpox virus (FPV), and canary poxvirus (OIE, 2018). APV are antigenically and immunologically distinct, which makes their identification difficult (Minhas et al., 2023).

PPV is one of the most contagious diseases affecting domestic and wild pigeons (Sudhakara and Sivajyothi, 2018). Similarly, FPV is a serious challenge for the poultry industry, with high economic losses due to increased mortality rates and reduced egg production in laying hens (Sarma et al., 2019). The primary transmission route of APV is direct contact via skin abrasions and mechanically by vectors such as mosquitoes. This route is seen in cutaneous form, while the diphtheric form is transmitted through inhalation or ingestion of the virus (Bertelloni et al., 2022). APV have a slow viral transmission rate, especially in multi-age poultry houses. In addition, transmission can occur quite quickly, especially when mosquito densities are high (Umar et al., 2021). The severity of the illness depends on the form of the disease (Giotis and Skinner, 2019).

The cutaneous form usually causes low to moderate mortality. While the diphtheric form is more deadly and causes high mortality rates, resulting in a negative economic impact on commercial poultry producers (Giotis and Skinner, 2019).

The virus invades pigeons and chickens through insect bites, contaminated food and water, and the inhalation of contaminated air through the aerosols of infected birds (Shalaby et al., 2021). After viral penetration and systemic infection, 2 forms appear in birds: cutaneous (dry form) and diphtheric (wet form) (Abd El Hafez et al., 2021; Bertelloni et al., 2022). The cutaneous form correlates with lesions, and grey-white nodules are found near the eyes, skin, beaks, and cloaca (Jan et al., 2017; Audarya et al., 2018). While the wet form in the upper digestive and upper respiratory tracts makes eating difficult and prevents drinking, it also leads to dehydration and death (Alehegn et al., 2014). Notably, the simultaneous presence of a mixed cutaneous and systemic form of FPV infection was recorded in young chickens (Akanbi et al., 2016).

Histopathological examination of the presence of intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies helps confirm APV in lesions from scabs that accumulate on the skin of birds or from virus-inoculated chicken embryo chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) (OIE, 2018). Conventional laboratory diagnostics for APV include histopathological examination, virus isolation by propagation on the CAM of embryonated chicken eggs, cell culture, and serological methods (Rahman et al., 2019; Abd El Hafez et al., 2021). In terms of sensitivity and rapid detection, molecular identification of APV using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has proven to be the most sensitive and rapid method for routine diagnosis compared with traditional diagnostic tests (van der Meer et al., 2022). In the same context, Taqman real-time PCR can also be useful for accurately identifying and differentiating different strains and genotypes of APV in different samples, including the blood of infected birds (Farias et al., 2010).

Recently, molecular techniques have developed rapid and sensitive systems that accurately detect viruses in clinical samples (Ramesh et al., 2018; Rahman et al., 2019; Abd El Hafez et al., 2021). The P4b core protein is widely used as a molecular diagnostic tool for detecting APV (Khaleefah et al., 2024). The PCR technique based on the amplification and sequencing of P4b is useful for the routine diagnosis of APV in infected samples (Ramzy et al., 2024). In addition, it helps differentiate FPV and PPV using real-time quantitative PCR (Faisal and Al-Azzawi, 2024). Likewise, molecular and sequencing tools can help in FPV and PPV detection and further characterization in various locations worldwide, including Egypt (Abd El Hafez et al., 2021; Jumaa, 2024). This provides valuable insights into the epidemiological situation of poxviruses and offers optimal opportunities for updating the seed pox vaccine (Lebdah et al., 2019).

The role of backyard chickens and pigeons in the spread of zoonotic viral diseases with fatal consequences and other viral diseases that pose a significant threat to the commercial poultry trade must be investigated (WOAH, 2021). Consequently, it is crucial to simultaneously report viral outbreaks in backyards while educating rural farmers about biosecurity measures and disease management (Fagrach et al., 2023). In the case of APV, hygiene measures, vector control, vaccination, and quarantine protocols need to be implemented to control its spread (Okwor and Chah, 2014). Likewise, the active involvement of stakeholders, policymakers, and government officials in engaging backyard farmers should enable them to find ways to improve rural farmers’ awareness of biosecurity measures and disease management and follow up their commitment to implement biosecurity measures in backyards (Correia-Gomes and Sparks, 2020).

It is important to raise chickens and pigeons in backyards as food sources for meat and eggs in rural areas (Lal et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). There is also, limited information about the spread of APV in birds in this sector, which can lead to significant economic losses, especially for small farmers (van der Meer et al., 2022). Therefore, effective tracking and control approaches are important for managing outbreaks and protecting pigeon and chicken populations from damage caused by FPV and PPV (Lebdah et al., 2019; Jumaa, 2024).

Backyard poultry are susceptible to viral infections, raising concerns regarding the potential risk of emerging viral outbreaks and transmission to surrounding farms. There is limited information available on backyard virus studies (Pervin et al., 2023).

The current study aims to investigate the prevalence of PPV and FPV in the Egyptian backyards of the Giza and Mansoura governorates during the summer months (from June to August) of 2023. Additionally, the present study aims to identify the molecular and pathological characteristics of the viruses and to determine the effectiveness of vaccines in protecting backyards from these diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical Statement

All bird sampling procedures in the present study were approved according to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee Agricultural Research Center, Egypt, with an approved permit number (ARC-AHRI- 6524).

Sampling

Fifty backyard flocks were examined for this particular study. A total of 35 pigeon backyards, consisting of both vaccinated and nonvaccinated birds, together with 15 chicken backyards consisting of nonvaccinated birds, were observed in the Giza and Mansoura governorates of Egypt throughout the summer months of June to August 2023. Approximately 16 backyards flocks showed nodular lesions indicating APV infection, with mortalities reaching up to 15% and 12% for pigeon and chicken backyard, respectively (Table 1). The lesions were observed in the 2023 summer season (June- August).

Samples were obtained from 5 diseased birds in each suspected backyard flock. These birds had warty, nodular lesions on their heads and nonfeathered body parts. The nodular lesions were carefully removed from the infected birds using sterile blades and placed in 2 Eppendorf tubes: 1 containing 10% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich Chemie GmbH, Taufkirchen, Germany) for the histopathological examination, and the other containing 0.5 mL of sterile viral transport medium (VTM) (AL270, HiViral™ Transport medium, HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India) supplemented with antibiotics (100 IU/mL penicillin and 1 mg/mL streptomycin) (Sigma-Aldrich) and antimycotics (amphotericin B) (Sigma-Aldrich) (Abd El Hafez et al., 2021). The collected samples from infected birds were pooled by backyard and then analyzed using molecular techniques.

Nucleic Acid Extraction and PCR Amplification of the P4b Gene From Examined Samples

DNA extraction from 16 backyard birds with nodular lesions (14 from pigeons and 2 from chickens) was performed using the QIAamp Minelute virus spin kit (57704, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) under the manufacturer's instructions. EconoTaq PLUS GREEN 2X Master Mix (30033-1, Lucigen Corporation, Middleton, WI) and a set of primers, APV4bF (CAGCAGGTGCTAAACAACAA) and APV4bR (CGGTAGCTTAACGCCGAATA) were used to amplify a 578-base pair (bp) fragment of the P4b core protein gene of APV.

According to Prukner-Radov̇ci´c et al. (2006), the thermal cycling conditions were 10 min of initial denaturation at 95°C, 30 cycles of denaturation (1 min at 95°C), 1 min of annealing at 55°C, and 1 min of extension at 72°C, followed by 10 min of final extension (72°C) using an automated thermal cycler (Gene-Amp PCR System 2700, Perkin-Elmer, Norwalk, CT).

Pathogenicity of FPV and PPV on Specific Pathogen Free-Embryonated Chicken Eggs (SPF-ECE)

The pathogenicity of the virus was determined randomly from 2 selected pigeon-detected strains, and the 2 confirmed chicken-detected strains, each performed according to the procedure established by the OIE (2018). Aliquots (0.2 mL) of prepared viruses were injected via the chicken embryo CAM route into 10-day-old SPF-ECE (purchased from Kom Oshim SPF farm in Fayum).

Mortality was monitored daily for 5 successive days after injection. The chicken embryo CAM was thoroughly examined to determine the focal white pock lesions development (Gilhare et al., 2015). Chicken embryo CAMs that appeared as lesions were collected in 10% formalin (Sigma-Aldrich) for the histopathological examination.

Histopathology

Nodular skin lesions from pigeons naturally infected by APV and pock lesions on the infected chicken embryo CAMs fixed in a 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma-Aldrich). They were then dehydrated, cleared in xylene (Sigma-Aldrich), and embedded in paraffin (GRM1137, Himedia).

Five-micron sections were then prepared using a microtome (Leica Camera AG, Wetzlar, Germany), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Sigma-Aldrich) according to Suvarna et al. (2018). An optical microscope (Olympus BH-2, Olympus Optical Co. Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) linked to a digital camera and software (Jenoptik ProgRes Camera, C12plus, Frankfurt, Germany) was used to examine the sections (Suvarna et al., 2018).

Sequencing of P4b Gene Fragment and Phylogenetic Analysis

The Wizard DNA Purification Kit (Promega-A1120, Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) was used to purify specific DNA bands. The PCR product was then sequenced using a Sanger 2-way sequencer (Macrogen, Inc., Seoul, South Korea).

The sequencing data was analyzed using NCBI's Blast tool and then compiled and edited using BioEdit version 7.1.5 (Hall, 1999). MEGA X software was then used to perform neighbor-joining phylogenetic analysis as part of the molecular-evolutionary-genetics-analysis (Tamura et al., 2021).

RESULTS

Clinical Manifestation and Gross Findings

Of the 50 backyards surveyed for pigeons and chickens in Giza and Mansoura governorates in Egypt, 14 of the backyards housing pigeons and 2 backyards housing chickens had sick birds with nodular lesions, with a mortality rate of up to 15% for pigeons and 12% for chickens. Nodular lesions were frequently observed in nonfeathered areas, particularly in the combs and wattles of chickens. In addition, nodular skin lesions were observed in the heads and peaks of the infected pigeons and squabs (Figures 1A and B).

Pigeon exhibiting natural infection with avipoxvirus. (A) Elevated cutaneous nodules on the skin of the pigeon's head (arrow). (B) Raised nodules on the commissure of the squabs' mouths (arrow). (C) Yellow to brown nodular skin lesions appeared on the pigeon's legs (arrow). (D) Large nodular yellowish cutaneous lesions under a pigeon's wing (arrow).

In addition, these elevated cutaneous lesions were observed on the legs, under the wings, and other nonfeathered skin regions (Figures 1C and D). Depression, emaciation, weight loss, and reduced egg production are all commonly associated with skin lesions. The prevalence of nodular lesions was higher in pigeons (28%) than in chickens (4%), indicating that PPV was more common than FPV in the examined samples. In addition, nodular lesions are commonly observed during the summer of 2023 (Figure 1).

PCR Amplification

All 16 samples from suspected nodular lesions detected in 50 backyards were found to be positive by PCR detection and showed successful amplification of 578 bp of the p4b gene for APV (n = 14 for pigeons and n = 2 for chickens). Ten positive molecularly detected samples from pigeon yards were vaccinated, whereas the 2 confirmed samples from chickens were from unvaccinated yards. The results showed the sensitivity of molecular tools in detecting APV in the examined samples.

Microscopic Examination of Infected Pigeons

The infected pigeons displayed different types of pox, both externally on the skin of the head (dry form) and internally in the pharynx, larynx, and nasal cavity (wet form). Phylogenetic Characterization and Mutation Analysis

In the present study, we performed partial sequencing of fpv for 14 PPV, and 2 FPV. All 16 sequences have been deposited in GenBank with the accession numbers (Table 2). These 16 sequences were aligned with 38 nucleotide sequences detected among different species of birds obtained from GenBank for phylogenetic analysis. Phylogenetic analysis revealed 5 main clades (A, B, C, D, and E) in the DNA tree of APV (Figure 6). The strains in the current study were clustered into main clade A, with 11 belonging to subclade A2 and 5 to subclade A1 (Figure 6).

DISCUSSION

Backyard livestock rearing continues to be a culturally significant activity in rural communities (Lal et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2022). They help alleviate food shortages by providing meat and eggs (Pires et al., 2019). Nevertheless, viral infections can hinder production and result in financial loss (Kannaki et al., 2021).

Pigeons face serious viral diseases that have been linked to fatal outbreaks and significant economic impacts on the pigeon industry worldwide (Santos et al., 2020). Among these, pigeon paramyxovirus 1 (PPMV-1) is one of the most dangerous diseases because its infection is associated with high mortality rates and possibly cross-species transmission capability, posing a zoonotic risk (Cui et al., 2023). PPV, pigeon circovirus, and avian influenza are other viral risks of concern that cause morbidity and mortality in pigeons (Islam et al., 2023; Martelli et al., 2023; Jumaa, 2024; Li et al., 2024).

APV are commonly found in backyard birds, particularly among younger pigeons and chickens (Sharma et al., 2019). About 28% of pigeon yards and 4% of chicken yards in the 2023 summer season showed scabs and nodular lesions in unfeather areas, indicating exposure to APV in Egypt (Lebdah et al., 2019). In Egypt, the high rate of APV infection occurs throughout the summer months. This is because the population of mosquitoes and other arthropods increases significantly during this time of the year. It has been observed that mosquitoes and other arthropods play a role in transmitting this viral infection in Egypt and other regions, as reported previously (Abdallah and Hassanin, 2013). They transmit APV from infected birds to healthy ones. Furthermore, mosquitoes can harbor the virus for about 7 months (Abdallah and Hassanin, 2013; Samuel et al., 2018). The positive backyards showed low biosecurity levels for mosquito populations, which are crucial in disease transmission and spread (Lawson et al., 2012).

The current study found nodular lesions characteristic of APV infection in ten vaccinated pigeon yards, while no vaccinated chickens showed these signs. This suggests that APV is circulating among birds, posing a threat to both pigeon and chicken populations, even in vaccinated yards (Lebdah et al., 2019). Grossly, visible pock lesions on chicken embryos CAM infected with APV were seen.

The virus induces characteristic large eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Bollinger bodies) in lesions in birds and APV-inoculated chicken embryos CAM. Growth factor-like genes (fpv80 and fpv211) may be responsible for hyperplasia in APV-infected tissues (Afonso et al., 2000). Pock lesions were opaque and varied in morphological findings, either color or size, as previously reported (Lebdah et al., 2019).

Histopathological examination of chicken embryos CAM infected by APV was similar to the findings of Offerman et al. (2013) that explained the hypertrophy and the appearance of many intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies in the ectoderm. All selected samples could produce visible pock lesions on chicken embryos CAM, which showed the sensitivity of molecular detection and was helpful in histopathological examination.

Clinically, the birds suffered from depression and anorexia. APV revealed characteristic cutaneous nodular skin lesions (cutaneous form). They appeared in the head, beaks, legs, feet, and in the feathered and unfeather parts of the wings of infected birds. These lesions are wart-like and differ in color from yellow to brown (Al-Ajeeli et al., 2015). In addition, the soft tissue layer under these cutaneous nodules was congested, as mentioned by Mori et al. (2005). The P4b core protein gene is a unique genetic marker for poxviruses (Sarker et al., 2021). The highly conserved nature of this gene allows the molecular diagnosis and distinguishing of poxviruses for different host species or even for various strains of the same species (Abdallah and Hassanin, 2013; Gyuranecz et al., 2013).

Phylogenetic analysis based on the P4b core protein gene of APV showed that the majority of strains detected in the current study clustered in clade A (fowlpox-like viruses), with 11 in subclade A2 and 5 in subclade A1. Specifically, 78.6% of PPV strains were clustered in subclade A2, and both chicken pox viruses were clustered in subclade A1. These phylogenetic analysis results represent subclade A1 for chicken-derived APV strains and subclade A2 for pigeon-derived APV strains (Gyuranecz et al., 2013). Similar clustering patterns are consistent with previous Egyptian studies on pigeon and chicken pox viruses (Abdallah and Hassanin, 2013; Lebdah et al., 2019).

The amino acid identity of 56.25% (9/16) of the strains studied here showed a close amino acid relationship of 97%-100% with the local Egyptian strains selected in the current study, especially those circulating in 2023. Similarly, previous studies in Egypt found high genetic similarity between the detected poxviruses and Egyptian strains available in GenBank (Abdallah and Hassanin, 2013; Lebdah et al., 2019; and Ramzy et al., 2024). In contrast, 43.75% (7/16) of the strains examined showed low homology in the amino acid identity, ranging from 56% to 93%. This variation demonstrated significant genetic differences in the detected strains and circulating APV and helped investigate the evolution of poxviruses in Egypt (Ramzy et al., 2024).

Comparison of amino acid identities with other selected APV and vaccines based on the P4b gene showed the tendency of acquisition point mutations in the p4b core protein amplicon was higher in pigeon strains, in contrast to chicken strains that did not have a mutation (Figure 8). Nine of the pigeon strains examined (Pigeonpox/p/2-1/Pigeon/Egypt to Pigeonpox/p/2-5/Pigeon/Egypt, and Pigeonpox/p/2-8/Pigeon/Egypt to Pigeonpox/p/2-11/Pigeon/Egypt) acquired 2 new point mutations. The point mutations identified in the present study may account for somewhat low genetic similarity between the studied strains and the (Fowlpox/VSVRI/Egypt/vaccine) accession number MN708968.1. 87.5% of the examined strains showed 59-91% genetic similarity to the selected local vaccine strain, consistent with the findings of Ramzy et al. (2024). Their research indicated differences in the amino acid sequences of their 2023 FPV and the local vaccine strain, suggesting antigenic variation between circulating FPV isolates and the vaccine strains. Furthermore, positive samples with mutations were detected in the vaccinated backyard. Studying these mutations can also help improve vaccines to provide better protection, control outbreaks, and reduce economic losses in pigeon and chicken flocks (Lebdah et al., 2019).

It is important to study and confirm the pathological characteristics through molecular detection to determine APV in pigeon and chicken populations. Additionally, the P4b can be used as a genetic marker to track mutations and study the evolution of poxviruses in backyard poultry farming in Egypt. Backyard farms could spread diseases to neighboring farms, so understanding the genetic markers is crucial for preventing disease outbreaks.

CONCLUSION

The findings reported in the current study elucidated the significance of closely monitoring APV in chicken and pigeon backyards in Egypt to manage disease outcomes and maintain production in this agricultural sector. Molecular analysis was confirmed to be a highly sensitive and rapid tool for the identification of APV in clinically positive samples. The identified strains shared a clustering pattern similar to that of the previously circulating APV virus in Egypt, as most of the strains that fell under this category showed high amino acid sequence similarity. Notably, 43.75% of the APV-positive samples from vaccinated and nonvaccinated backyards birds showed differences in amino acid sequences compared to the local vaccine strain. These findings underscored the necessity for additional investigation, such as whole-genome sequencing and the revision of vaccine seeds, to improve vaccine effectiveness. It is highly advisable to increase the knowledge of rural farms regarding the significance of implementing steps and demonstrating dedication to backyard vaccination programs. This is crucial for controlling the transmission of avian viruses within the agricultural sector and preventing the further spread of outbreaks to nearby farms.
