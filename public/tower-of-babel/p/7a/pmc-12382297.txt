Changes in intestinal bacterial characteristics during hospitalization in the NICU in very low birth weight infants
Authors: Junhua Wu, Ting Wang, Beirong Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiota plays a crucial role in neonatal defense against pathogens, immune training, nutrient absorption, and postnatal disease development.MethodsWe prospectively collected fecal samples from 58 very low birth weight infants and 34 preterm infants with birth weights > 1500 g during their first two postnatal months. Using 16SrRNA sequencing, we characterize the compositional dynamics of gut microbiota in very low birth weight infants during early postnatal development, and evaluate potential associations with clinical factors.ResultsIn the very low birth weight infants group (experimental group), a total of 45 bacterial phyla and 1,327 genera were identified. The top two dominant phyla were Proteobacteria (65.64%) and Firmicutes (29.64%), accounting for 95.28% collectively. Among the dominant phyla, the experimental group showed significantly higher levels of Bacteroidetes (P < 0.001) and Firmicutes (P = 0.018) compared to the control group, whereas Acidobacteria (P < 0.001), Proteobacteria (P < 0.001), Fusobacteria (P = 0.001), Chloroflexi (P = 0.018), and Patescibacteria (P = 0.006) were significantly lower. The top 10 dominant genera represented 91.9% of the total. At the genus level, the experimental group had significantly higher abundances of Klebsiella (P = 0.016), Streptococcus (P < 0.001), Corynebacterium_1 (P < 0.001), Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 (P = 0.018), Clostridioides (P = 0.013), Staphylococcus (P < 0.001), and Lactobacillus (P = 0.001), while Escherichia_Shigella, Sphingomonas (P = 0.013), Veillonella (P = 0.037), Methylobacterium (P = 0.021), and Enterobacter (P = 0.029) were significantly lower compared to controls. Additionally, during the first 28 days after birth, the control group exhibited higher relative abundance of Bifidobacterium than the experimental group. Compared to preterm infants with birth weight > 1500 g, very low birth weight infants demonstrated more active intestinal metabolism. Within the very low birth weight infants, probiotic use, delivery mode, and premature rupture of membranes significantly influenced the relative abundances of Streptococcus, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, and Pseudomonas, respectively.ConclusionsVery low birth weight infants exhibit distinct gut microbiota characteristics during their first two months of life. Factors including probiotic using, delivery mode, and premature rupture of membranes significantly influence specific bacterial populations. The gut microbiome represents a crucial influencing the health outcomes of very low birth weight infants.Keywords: Very low birth weight infants, Intestinal, Bacteria, 16SrRNA genes

Introduction

The gut microbiota has the richest microbial community, with 10 times more cells and 150 times more genes than human cells. After birth, neonatal gut bacteria begin to colonize, and they are inextricably linked to defense against pathogens, immune training, nutrient absorption, and disease development.

Intestinal bacteria can promote host intestinal defense by reducing apoptotic signaling in immature intestinal epithelial cells, blocking inflammatory signaling, and promoting barrier function maturation [1]. They are also essential for the host synthesis of multivitamins such as vitamin K, vitamin B12, biotin, thiamin, riboflavin and pyridoxine [2–4]. The gut microbiota is also strongly associated with growth and development, with some animal studies finding that mice transplanted with the gut microbiota of 6-month-old infants grew faster than mice transplanted with the gut microbiota of 18-month-old infants [5]. Gut microbiota is also involved in neurological formation and development [6]. It is associated with late-onset sepsis (LOS) [7], necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) [8, 9], Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia [10], and may even influence the development of conditions such as allergic diseases [11], metabolic disease [12, 13], autism spectrum and cognitive disorders, and many other diseases [14–17].

The composition of the infant microbiota is more variable and unstable over time compared to that in adults. As a special group of preterm infants, very low birth weight infants (VLBWI), i.e., preterm infants with a birth weight of less than 1,500 g, face more uncertainties. These include life in incubators, a higher rate of cesarean sections and antibiotic use, inability to breastfeed, complex nutritional supplementation, and parenteral nutrition. A follow-up study of postnatal gut microbiome analysis and neurodevelopmental assessment in VLBWI found that the gut microbiome plays a role in early cognitive and behavioral development in newborns [18]. However, there are fewer existing studies on VLBWI gut bacteria due to the more difficult sample collection and limited research tools.

In this study, we investigated the composition characteristics of intestinal bacteria in 58 VLBWIs (very low birth weight infants) and 34 preterm infants with birth weights > 1500 g by performing sequencing and bioinformatic analyses on fecal samples collected postnatally on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 60 (for VLBWIs only). We further analyzed the influence of clinical factors as probiotics usage, main feeding pattern, delivery mode, PROM (premature rupture of membranes), mechanical ventilation, mild asphyxia, and antibiotics using on gut microbiota composition.

Materials and methods

Participants

VLBWI admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of Ningbo Women and Children’s Hospital, China between January 2021 and April 2022, for hospitalization after birth, were included in the experimental group. Preterm infants admitted to the NICU with a birth weight greater than 1500 g were included in the control group. Exclusion criteria included preterm infants born with severe asphyxia (APGAR score ≤ 3 points), congenital anomalies, inherited metabolic disorders, or those in the presence of shock or multi-organ failure. The study subjects in this experiment were administered probiotics as BIFICO (Combined Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Enterococcus, Live, Powder) orally. The diagnostic criteria for mild asphyxia were an Apgar score of ≤ 7 at 1 or 5 min and an umbilical artery blood pH < 7.20 [19].

All subjects obtained informed consent from their guardians and signed an informed consent form. The trial complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Women and Children’s Hospital of Ningbo University (Approval No: [2019] Ethics Approval No. 42).

Samples collection

Fecal samples were collected from study subjects on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 24, 28, and 60 (experimental group only) using a special fecal box, and stored in a freezer at -80 °C immediately after collection. Collection was stopped for subjects who were discharged from the hospital or died during the observation period.

DNA extraction, amplification and sequencing

The DNA of the samples was extracted using QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen) and the DNA was quantified by Nanodrop. The V3-V4 region of the qualified DNA product was amplified with primers (338 F: ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA,806R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) designed for the conserved region. The variable region of the rRNA gene was amplified using TransStart® FastPfu DNA Polymerase (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). The amplification products were recovered and purified by VAHTSDNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), stained with Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit and then quantified in a Microplate reader (BioTek, FLx800). Sequencing libraries were prepared using Illumina’s TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit (San Diego, California, America). The libraries underwent quality control on Agilent Bioanalyzer (Palo Alto, San Francisco, America) in the gradient, then sequenced.

Bioinformatic analysis

The raw data obtained from the Illumina platform turned into effective Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) after removing the primer sequence, trimming, merging, and filtering. Species annotation was performed using the Naive Bayes classifier, and the species were finally classified into 6 hierarchical levels: kingdom, phylum, order, family, genus, and species. Chao1 index, observed species index, Shannon index, and Simpson index were calculated, separately for each sample, by QIIME2 software to reflect the sample alpha diversity. Analyses of principal coordinates (PCoA) and Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) used the R vegan package. Functional prediction analysis was performed using PICRUSt2 v2.5.0 (2022) gene prediction software.

Statistical analyses

The comparative analysis of baseline characteristics between the two groups was performed using Student’s *t*-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test (χ² test) for categorical variables.

Microbial alpha diversity was compared using the Mann-Whitney U test (Rank Sum Test), while comparisons across multiple time points were conducted using a randomized complete block design (RCBD). Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0.2. The significance level (α) and confidence interval (CI) were set at 0.05 and 95%, respectively. Alpha diversity comparisons between groups were assessed using the Student’s *t*-test, whereas beta diversity was evaluated through PERMANOVA to assess the overlap of centroids between the two groups.

There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender, primary feeding mode, mode of birth, or premature rupture of membranes (PROM). However, there were significant differences in the use of probiotics, mechanical ventilation, mild asphyxia, and gestational age.

All valid sequences were clustered into ASVs at 100% similarity, and the abundance of ASVs was counted for each sample. A total of 27,202 ASVs were detected across the two groups of samples. Unique ASVs accounted for 58.66% (15956 out of 27202) in the experimental group and 26.76% (7278 out of 27202) in the control group. Distribution of intestinal dominant bacteria in two groups

At the phylum level, a total of 45 phyla were detected in the samples of the experimental group. The top five phyla in terms of relative abundance, in order, were Proteobacteria (65.64%), Firmicutes (29.64%), Actinobacteria (1.78%), Tenericutes (1.61%), and Bacteroidetes (0.84%). These top five phyla accounted for 99.51% of all detected phyla.

A total of 45 phyla were detected in the control group. The top five phyla in terms of relative abundance, in order, were Proteobacteria (50.51%), Firmicutes (46.14%), Actinobacteria (1.96%), Bacteroidetes (1.18%), and Tenericutes (0.11%) (Fig. 5).

Composition of the top 5 dominant phyla in two groups

The top two dominant phyla in both the experimental and control groups were. Proteobacteria and Firmicutes, collectively accounting for > 95% of the total bacterial composition.

At the genus level, a total of 1327 bacterial genera were detected in the experimental group, and the top 10 bacterial genera in terms of relative abundance in descending order were, Pseudomonas (39.77%), Enterococcus (16.60%), Klebsiella (9.34%), Staphylococcus (8.96%), Escherichia_Shigella (8.13%), unclassified Enterobacteriaceae(3.66%), Ureaplasma(1.6%) Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (1.34%), Sphingomonas(1.42%), Streptococcus (1.18%), totaling 91.90% of the detected genera. Clostridium sensu stricto 1 represents a phylogenetically distinct clade within the Firmicutes phylum, Clostridia class, comprising strictly anaerobic bacteria. The Latin designation “sensu stricto” (abbreviated as s.s.) denotes a narrowly defined taxonomic grouping, distinguishing it from broadly classified Clostridium species. The numerical identifier “1” specifically demarcates this particular phylogenetic branch based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing analysis.

A total of 1397 bacterial genera were detected in the control group, and the top 10 bacterial genera of relative abundance in descending order were, Pseudomonas(33.21%), Enterococcus(22.68%), Staphylococcus(10.19%), Clostridium sensu stricto 1(6.41%). Klebsiella (5.36%), Escherichia_Shigella (4.46%), Streptococcus (3.36%), unclassified Enterobacteriaceae (2.32%), Sphingomonas (1.24%), Bifidobacterium (1.23%), totaling 90.45% of the detected genera.

Of the top 10 genera in both groups, Ureaplasma was unique to the experimental group, Bifidobacterium was unique to the control group. In both groups, the first, second and ninth ranked bacteria were the same, respectively Pseudomonas, Enterococcus, and Sphingomonas (Fig. 6). Pseudomonas was the most abundant genus in both study groups, with no significant differences in relative abundance between the two groups.

Discussion

Our study analyzed the bacterial structure of intestinal fecal specimens from low-birth-weight preterm infants and preterm infants with birth weights greater than 1500 g who were admitted to a separate ward of the NICU. The Shannon index of intestinal bacteria in the VLBWI group showed an increasing trend over time, which is similar to the findings of a previous study that found the diversity of VLBWI fecal samples was higher at 5–6 weeks postnatally compared to 2–3 weeks [20]. However, Patel AL et al. collected and sequenced VLBWI anal swabs and found that the Shannon index was lowest at birth, increased at 2 weeks, and declined rapidly at 3–5 weeks (p < 0.01) [21]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in sample sources.

In our study, the top 5 phyla in VLBWI were Proteobacteria (65.64%), Firmicutes (29.64%), Actinobacteria (1.78%), Tenericutes (1.61%), and Bacteroidetes (0.84%). Initially, Proteobacteria dominated the VLBWI gut bacteria, with a gradual decrease in relative abundance over time, reaching a minimum at day 14, and then gradually increasing. Firmicutes showed the opposite trend, reaching a maximum at day 14. This contrasts with previous studies on preterm infants. Sanz Y et al. found that Firmicutes (63.4%) was the most abundant phylum in fetal stool samples, followed by Proteobacteria (27.7%), which rose to become the dominant phylum by the third week (57.6%) [22]. However, it has been suggested that a gradual decline in Bacilli (Firmicutes), a gradual rise in Gammaproteobacteria (Proteobacteria), and the maintenance of a certain abundance, followed by an abundance of Clostridia (Firmicutes), might indicate the maturation of the intestinal bacteria [23, 24], which is similar to the change in relative abundance of dominant genera after day 14 in this study. When analyzing the gut microbiota of newborns, the impact of regional differences cannot be ignored. A study on gut microbiota performance in healthy infants found that different regions showed different species. China was “P-type” (abundant in Proteobacteria), with Escherichia_Shigella and Klebsiella being the dominant genera [25]. We hypothesize that geographic differences may be one of the reasons for the difference in results between our study and previous studies.

The top 10 genera in the VLBWI were Pseudomonas (39.77%), Enterococcus (16.60%), Klebsiella (9.34%), Staphylococcus (8.96%), Escherichia_Shigella (8.13%), unclassified Enterobacteriaceae (3.66%), Ureaplasma (1.6%), Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (1.34%), Sphingomonas (1.42%), and Streptococcus (1.18%). The composition of the dominant genera was similar to the results obtained in previous studies of the intestinal bacteria of VLBWI, although the order of relative abundance differed [20, 21, 26]. Our study revealed that Pseudomonas was the most abundant genus in both the experimental and control groups. This finding is consistent with the work of Lee et al., who demonstrated that preterm infants exhibit significant gut dysbiosis in the early postnatal weeks compared to healthy term infants, characterized by overgrowth of Proteobacteria (including Pseudomonas and Enterobacter) and depletion of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium [27]. Notably, Pseudomonas infection has been associated with the highest mortality rates in LOS cohorts [28]. Our study demonstrated a significantly higher relative abundance of Bifidobacterium in the control group compared to the experimental group during the first 28 postnatal days, suggesting a positive correlation between Bifidobacterium colonization and advancing gestational age. This finding is consistent with that of Fangfang Li et al., whose study showed that the proportion of bifidobacteria (such as Bifidobacterium breve and B. longum) in the gut microbiota of preterm infants increased significantly from 13.6 to 44.2% around one month after birth [29]. Similar observations were made in Indian VLBW infants (< 28 weeks GA), where reduced B. breve abundance was associated with extreme prematurity [30]. A study evaluating the effects of probiotics on the growth and development of VLBW infants found that bifidobacteria significantly accelerated weight, length, and head circumference growth, with this effect being more pronounced in infants exposed to antibiotics after birth [31]. Beyond promoting growth, another critical benefit of bifidobacteria is their significant advantage in reducing the incidence of NEC in preterm infants [32]. Infants with late-onset sepsis exhibited reduced levels of bifidobacteria [33]. Bifidobacteria competitively inhibit the colonization of pathogenic bacteria, produce short-chain fatty acids (such as butyrate) to promote gut health, and modulate immune responses to reduce inflammation, thereby preventing NEC [34]. Additionally, bifidobacteria can elevate certain amino acid levels in the gut of preterm infants, reduce serum TLR4/NF-κB expression, decrease the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α), alleviate systemic inflammatory responses, and ultimately significantly reduce the incidence of feeding intolerance [35, 36]. In extremely preterm infants, colonization by bifidobacteria is associated with a lower risk of late-onset sepsis (LOS), likely achieved by suppressing the overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria [37].

A study of the intestinal bacteria of preterm infants with apnea found that preterm infants had a significant increase in the relative abundance of Klebsiella and Streptococcus [18]. In contrast, the relative abundance of these genera was significantly higher in the VLBWI group than in the control group in our study, which might be related to the higher frequency of apnea in VLBWI. Klebsiella can colonize the intestines of both healthy and sick preterm infants without established genetic or phenotypic correlates [38]. Multiple studies have identified Klebsiella as one of the common pathogens causing sepsis in VLBWI [33, 39]. In addition to Klebsiella, other common intestinal colonizers such as Escherichia coli and Serratia marcescens are also implicated in late-onset sepsis (LOS), suggesting that LOS originates from the intestinal tract rather than cutaneous migration [33]. Previous studies have also suggested that the elevated relative abundance of Klebsiella may be associated with the occurrence of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) [8, 40].

In our study, the alpha diversity of intestinal bacteria was lower in the small-for-gestational-age group compared to the large-for-gestational-age group, although there was no significant difference in the top 10 dominant genera. Chernikova et al. [41], found significantly higher alpha diversity of gut bacteria in very preterm infants (born before 28 weeks) compared to older preterm infants, a difference that persisted until 6 weeks after birth [41]. These differences may be attributed to prolonged hospitalization and increased exposure to antibiotics and other medical interventions in younger gestational age preterm infants. This is supported by the lower Simpson, Shannon, and Pielou’s evenness index of gut bacteria observed in VLBWI receiving mechanical ventilation in our study.

Very low birth weight infants (VLBWI) born by vaginal delivery are initially exposed to bacteria from the mother’s vagina and feces, whereas those born by cesarean section are initially exposed to bacteria from the hospital environment, healthcare workers, and the mother’s skin [40]. The results of this study indicate that the diversity of species and relative abundance of intestinal bacteria were lower in the vaginal delivery group compared to the cesarean delivery group. Similar to our findings, Ho TTB found that diversity was higher in VLBWIs born by cesarean delivery [42]. This result is different with the mainstream view regarding the influence of delivery mode on gut microbial composition. Research on gut microbiota in 46 preterm infants (median gestational age 27 weeks) showed no significant difference in microbial diversity between cesarean and vaginally delivered groups. Stewart CJ et al. suggested this might be attributed to dominant factors in the NICU environment, including the universal use of antibiotics after birth and the characteristic absence of Bacteroides colonization in preterm infants [43]. Numerous other studies support the view that delivery mode has minimal impact on gut microbiota in preterm infants [22, 43–45].

In this study, the relative abundance of Pseudomonas was significantly lower in the group with premature rupture of membranes (PROM) compared to the group without PROM. There is evidence indicating that even in cases of intact membranes, preterm infants may experience intrauterine bacterial invasion, with the amniotic fluid of laboring fetuses potentially being colonized by ascending vaginal microorganisms and from the placental microbiota [33].

In our study, very low birth weight infants (VLBWIs) who were predominantly breastfed were categorized into the breastfed group, while those primarily formula-fed were categorized into the formula-fed group based on their primary mode of feeding. The primary mode of feeding did not significantly affect the intestinal bacteria of VLBWIs in our study. However, Saleem et al. found that breastfed VLBWIs exhibited increased fecal microbial diversity and higher levels of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides [46]. Exclusive breastfeeding is associated with accelerated maturation of intestinal barrier function, and initiation of breastfeeding immediately after birth facilitates intestinal barrier maturation in both term and preterm infants [46].

Research has found that NEC-afflicted infants exhibit lower microbial diversity [47]. A recent study revealed significantly increased Streptococcus abundance in the intestines of preterm infants with apnea [18], while multiple studies have confirmed Streptococcus as a major pathogen causing LOS in preterm infants [33, 48, 49]. In our current study, probiotic supplementation in VLBW infants was associated with both increased gut microbiota diversity and reduced relative abundance of Streptococcus, providing further evidence that probiotic use may help steer preterm infant gut microbiota toward healthier outcomes.

However, there are several limitations to consider. Firstly, research has shown that infants from different geographical regions, exhibit greater diversity in gut bacteria [50]. This suggests that intestinal bacteria in newborns may vary significantly based on geographic location. Our study, conducted solely at Ningbo Women’s and Children’s Hospital NICU, may not fully encapsulate and represent the intestinal microbiota characteristics of all VLBWIs. We aim to conduct a multicenter study in the future to achieve more comprehensive and representative results. Secondly, this study utilized fecal specimens as samples. While this method provides practicality and avoids the invasiveness of procedures like biopsies or tissue scraping, it may not fully capture the intricacies of host-microbe interactions occurring on the intestinal epithelial surface. Future studies could explore more detailed sampling methods such as mucus biofilm sampling or tissue biopsies to gain deeper insights. In addition, we will explore the impact of more diverse clinical factors, such as maternal GBS colonization [61] maternal infection status, and prophylactic antibiotic treatment, on the intestinal microbiome. Looking forward, the specific gut microbiota of VLBWIs in the NICU holds potential as a source of biomarkers for identifying infants at risk of diseases or growth disorders, and as a therapeutic target for promoting growth. In the future studies by our group, we plan to expand the sample size and conduct multicenter studies. Further follow-up assessments will be performed on the VLBWIs included in this study to evaluate additional growth and developmental parameters. Furthermore, we will systematically collect and analyze compositional differences between the gut microbiota of VLBWIs and term infants to more precisely delineate the unique microbial signatures characteristic of VLBWIs. This will allow us to investigate potential associations between early-life gut microbiota characteristics and subsequent growth trajectories/disease susceptibility.

Conclusion

In summary, our study describes the characteristics and changes in gut bacteria at 2 months postnatal in VLBWIs hospitalized in the NICU, and further analyzes the impact of clinical factors. We found that the most abundant bacterial genus in the intestines of VLBWIs was Pseudomonas, and the relative abundance of each dominant genus changed over time. In terms of distribution, the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium in the gut of VLBWIs was relatively low, while exhibiting higher intestinal metabolic activity. Probiotics administration, delivery mode, and PROM significantly influenced the gut microbiota composition of VLBWIs. Our study findings provide objective data support for microbiota research in VLBWIs, and reveal previously uncharacterized relationships between clinical factors and gut microbial dynamics.
