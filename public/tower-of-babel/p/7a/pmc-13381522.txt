Development and characterization of nanocellulose sheets from rice husk through sequential pretreatment and hydrolysis
Authors: Maida Salah Ud Din, Muhammad Naveed, Hotaf Hassan Makki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanocellulose has emerged as a promising biomaterial owing to its exceptional mechanical strength, biodegradability, and wide industrial applicability. Rice husk, an abundant agro-waste rich in cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, represents an economical and sustainable feedstock for nanocellulose production. Rice husk was subjected to sequential pretreatment processes including hot water retting, caustic soda retting, acetic acid retting, alkaline treatment, and bleaching to progressively remove hemicellulose, lignin, and other impurities. The purified cellulose was further hydrolyzed with sulfuric acid to obtain nanocellulose, followed by repeated washing, centrifugation at 4000 rpm, and drying at 60 °C. The synthesized material was characterized using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Pretreatment steps effectively delignified rice husk and enriched the cellulose fraction, as evident from the progressive changes in fiber morphology. Sulfuric acid hydrolysis produced nanoscale cellulose fibrils, which aggregated into thin sheet-like structures upon drying. SEM micrographs revealed a dense fibrillar network with diameters ranging from 80 to 100 nm, while FTIR confirmed the retention of characteristic cellulose functional groups. EDX analysis showed elemental composition consistent with pure cellulose (C 54.52 wt%, O 45.48 wt%), verifying the high chemical purity of the synthesized nanocellulose. Furthermore, moisture uptake analysis revealed that nanocellulose exhibited significantly lower hygroscopicity compared with cellulose, confirming its superior dimensional stability under humid conditions. This study demonstrates a sustainable and efficient approach for converting rice husk into nanocellulose sheets through chemical pretreatment and controlled hydrolysis. The resulting nanocellulose possesses high purity and well-defined nanoscale morphology, highlighting its potential for applications in biopolymers, packaging, environmental remediation, and advanced material engineering.

Introduction

Nanocellulose, a derivative of cellulose with remarkable properties, has garnered the attention of scientists across the globe in the past few decades1. It is a well-known fact that rice is one of the most widely cultivated crops globally, so its agricultural residues (husk and straw) can effectively produce nanocellulose inexpensively2. Using non-biodegradable waste, value-added products with great industrial applications can be produced. Rice husk, an abundant agricultural byproduct and waste product, has excellent potential to be transformed into sustainable value-added products3. Millions of tons of rice husk are produced all over the globe every year, which challenges effective waste management and environmental sustainability4. While rice husk has classically been considered a waste product, its high cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, and silica make it a promising feedstock for the manufacturing of nanocellulose5.

Nanocellulose, a cellulose derivative on a nanoscale, possesses immense mechanical strength. Due to its renewability, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, it can be used across various fields, including medicine, pharmaceuticals, and electronics6. Alongside these, it can also help in environmental protection. The rise in the global population means a more significant requirement for sustainable resources7. The production of Nanocellulose from rice husk starts with the pre-treatment of lignin and hemicellulose removal, followed by the hydrolysis step, which extracts cellulose at the nanoscale8.

This approach helps reduce environmental pollution caused by agricultural waste and supports circular bioeconomy strategies. In addition, using Nanocellulose in these advanced materials has been shown to improve the functional properties of composites, greatly enhance drug delivery systems, and replace green polymers instead of synthetic composites9. The high availability of cellulose-containing rice biomass makes the cultivation of nanocellulose economical in rice-producing countries. Nanocellulose is an environmentally friendly material because it comes from renewable sources. With biodegradability, the environmental impacts are lower than traditional plastics10. Nanocellulose has the highest tensile strength and stiffness of any known substance, augmenting the mechanical strength of bioplastics and composites11.

The high surface area associated with Nanocellulose improves its interactions with other materials, making it a superior filler for polymer matrices12. Nanocellulose is an excellent barrier to oxygen and moisture, making it ideal for use in food packaging13. While it is highly durable, Nanocellulose is also lightweight, making it useful in industries such as aerospace and automotive6. Nanocellulose possesses good thermal stability, enabling it to be used in various applications where temperatures can be sensitive14.

A low density combined with remarkably vast surface area, high mechanical strength, and excellent biodegradability make Nanocellulose very suitable for use in the production of bioplastics and other industrial parts. In the paper and packaging sector, Nanocellulose improves the toughness and quality of the products while retaining biodegradability12, thus replacing non-biodegradable materials. It is added to cement composites as an admixture in building and construction to improve mechanical properties and perpetuate carbon reduction for better green construction7. The other industry that would benefit from the potential of Nanocellulose is the textile industry, where fabrics made from Nanocellulose are lightweight, strong, and possess excellent moisture management properties13. Moreover, in the medical field, Nanocellulose is essential for drug delivery systems, wound dressings, and tissue engineering as it’s biocompatible and has a high surface area that permits controlled drug delivery and advanced healing processes9.

Likewise, it is advantageous in the automotive and aerospace industries because its lightweight and high strength-to-weight ratio composites enhance fuel economy and reduce emissions10. Additionally, Nanocellulose is used in flexible electronics and conductive films because of its properties, which encourage the development of new-generation devices11. It has been commonly used in purifying water because of its capacity to remove contaminants14.

The production of Nanocellulose from rice biomass offers a sustainable pathway to address two pressing global challenges: dealing with agricultural waste and combating the environmental damage caused by plastic waste. Due to its exceptional qualities, rice biomass nanocellulose is an essential, versatile material for bioplastic and many other industrial applications3.

Various methods have been reported for extracting nanocellulose from lignocellulosic biomass, typically involving direct alkali treatment followed by acid hydrolysis. However, incomplete removal of non-cellulosic components can limit the efficiency of fibrillation and the quality of the resulting nanocellulose. In the present study, a sequential pretreatment strategy consisting of hot water retting, caustic soda treatment, acetic acid delignification, alkaline treatment, and bleaching was employed to progressively remove hemicellulose, lignin, and other impurities prior to hydrolysis. This stepwise approach improves cellulose accessibility and facilitates the formation of nanocellulose fibrils that can assemble into dense sheets upon drying, which are promising for future biodegradable plastic and polymer composite applications.

The use of Nanocellulose in bioplastics greatly enhances their mechanical and barrier properties while aiding in establishing a circular economy12. Additionally, Nanocellulose’s use in various fields such as packaging, construction, and medicine emphasizes its promise to transform material science, thus making rice nanocellulose production a significant advancement towards sustainable development13. This development makes strides towards building a greener future. The research aims to enhance the production methods of Nanocellulose extracted from rice husk and its nanocellulose properties, processing strategies, and extraction optimizations8. This study seeks to utilize non-utilized resources to produce greener materials, reduce the agricultural waste problem, and promote sustainable development4.

Materials and methods

Cellulose extraction

Pretreatment of rice husk

The pretreatment of rice husk is an important step to breakdown the lignin structure that allows the extraction of cellulose. This procedure includes subsequent chemical treatments including hot water retting, caustic soda retting and acetic acid retting are. These pretreatment steps are described below.

Hot water retting

Hot water retting process was performed in order to remove impurities, waxes, and loose hemicellulose from the rice husk. 10 g of rice husks was added to distilled water in the amount of 100 ml which resulted in a solid/ liquid ratio of 1:10. It was then heated to a temperature of 100 °C, while being stirred for a period of 30 min. This procedure assists in the principal decomposition of some parts of the bound matrix, improving non-cellulosic component chemical treatment efficacy subsequently. After the heating process the mixture was cooled, washed through Whatman filter paper, and underwent thorough washing with distilled water in order to eliminate any soluble matter. The rice husk was then stored at room temperature to dry before it was processed further15.

Caustic soda retting

Caustic soda retting was performed to dissolve hemicellulose and sever the crystalline bonds of lignin to harvest cellulose. The NaOH 4% pretreatment was performed in a solid to liquid ratio of 1:10 using rice husk. The solution was maintained at 90 °C for 1 h while stirring to achieve adequate chemical distribution. Sodium Hydroxide reacts with hemicellulose and lignin by cleaving the ester and ether bonds resulting in more accessible cellulose16. After the treatment, the mixture was cooled down. The mixture was then filtered and washed with distilled water until the wash water reached a neutral pH (~ 7).

Acetic acid retting

In order to increase cellulose purity, acetic acid retting was conducted to further degrade lignin. 50% acetic acid was added to the previously treated NaOH treated rice husk with 1:5. In order to facilitate lignin removal, the mixture was heated while constantly stirred to 80 °C over a period of three hours. Acetic acid serves as a delignifying agent which removes residual lignin while preserving the structure of cellulose. Following the reaction, the sample was cooled, and in order to remove the residual acid, it was filtered and washed with distilled water.

Alkaline treatment

To ensure complete removal of non-cellulosic components, the pretreated rice husk was subjected to an alkaline treatment using 4% NaOH.The sample was added to a conical flask containing the alkali solution and set to a boil for 2 h while continuously stirring. The boiling process increases the penetration of NaOH into the rice husk fibers, breaking down any remaining hemicellulose and disrupting the crystalline structure of lignin. The residue was thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove excess alkali.

Bleaching process

The bleaching step followed to further improve the purity of the cellulose extracted, as well as to remove remaining lignin. The solid residue that came from alkaline treatment was treated with a mixture of acetic acid and 1.7% aqueous chlorite buffer. In addition, the mixture was stirred continuously and heated for 4 h at 100–130 °C to remove the remaining impurities. The acid environment helped to maintain cellulose while degrading lignin. The reaction was followed by cooling down the mixture which was then filtered to obtain purified cellulose. The cellulose undergoes multiple washes with distilled water followed by neutral drying at room temperature as well as oven drying at 50–60 °C. The purified cellulose was collected and stored for further characterization and analysis17.

Nanocellulose synthesis

Following pretreatment, bleaching, and purification, the extracted cellulose was subjected to controlled acid hydrolysis for nanocellulose synthesis. The purified cellulose fibers were treated with concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) under continuous stirring to hydrolyze the amorphous regions of cellulose. After the reaction, the suspension was repeatedly washed with distilled water to neutralize the medium and remove residual acid. The neutralized suspension was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min, and this washing–centrifugation cycle was repeated three times to ensure complete removal of unreacted components. The final pellet containing nanocellulose was carefully collected and dried in a hot air oven at 60 °C until constant weight was achieved. The dried product was stored in airtight containers for further analysis and characterization.

Characterization of nanocellulose

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to analyze the surface chemistry of the nanocellulose samples. The study was conducted on a Shimadzu IR Tracer model, which features a diamond ATR module, enabling on-the-spot spectral sampling. Before analysis, the nanocellulose suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min to harvest the solid portion, which was dried at room temperature. The spectra were measured from 4000 to 400 cm¹ using an infrared spectrometer. The profiling analysis enabled the identification of functional groups within the cellulose framework, indicating the presence of vibrational changes associated with hydroxide, alkyl, and ether groups18. The resulting spectrum provided molecular-level confirmation of the cellulose backbone and its structural integrity following the nanoscale modification process.

Scanning electron microscopy

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to examine the surface morphology and structural characteristics of the synthesized nanocellulose. A thin layer of dried nanocellulose was carefully mounted on carbon adhesive tape, which was then fixed onto an aluminum stub. To enhance the surface conductivity of the sputter-coated sample and reduce any imaging charging artifacts, a uniform gold layer was sputter-coated onto the sample. A Nova Nano SEM was used for SEM analysis and was operated at an accelerating voltage of 12.50 kV. The system was equipped with a Through-Lens Detector (TLD), a Secondary Electron (SE) detector, and a Low Vacuum Detector, which enable imaging at high resolution and permit the capture of nanoscale surface features19. The micrographs of nanocellulose were captured at resolutions of 500X, 1000X, 2000X, and 3000X.

Energy-dispersive X-ray analysis

Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) Spectroscopy was used to examine the composition of the synthesized nanocellulose. The analysis was carried out in parallel with scanning electron microscopy (SEM), which involves bombarding the surface of a sample with a concentrated electron beam. This excites the atoms to emit X-rays, which are characteristic of the atoms in the sample. The emitted X-rays, after undergoing detection, were used to assess the sample in terms of its elemental composition. The procedure enabled the confirmation of the absence of other elements and also helped in assessing whether other foreign elements were unintentionally added during the nanocellulose synthesis process.

Nanocellulose fibril diameter distribution analysis

The diameter distribution of nanocellulose fibrils was determined from scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images using digital image analysis. High-resolution SEM micrographs obtained at magnifications of 2000× and 3000× were used for the measurements. The images were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) after calibration with the SEM scale bar to convert pixel dimensions into nanometers.

For each sample, at least 50 individual nanocellulose fibrils were randomly selected from different regions of the micrographs to ensure representative sampling. The fibril diameter was measured by drawing a perpendicular line across the visible width of each fibril. The collected measurements were used to calculate the average fibril diameter and standard deviation. To evaluate the fibril size distribution, the measured diameters were grouped into defined size intervals and the frequency percentage for each interval was calculated. The distribution of fibril diameters was then presented as a histogram, illustrating the variation in nanocellulose fibril sizes within the sample.

Frequency (%) = Number of fibrils in a size interval / Total number of fibrils measured ​×100.

Moisture uptake test

The water absorption activity of cellulose and nanocellulose was evaluated through a gravimetric moisture uptake test using a saturated sodium chloride solution. Samples were first dried in an oven at 50 °C for 24 h and cooled in a desiccator to obtain constant dry weights (Wd). Dried cellulose and nanocellulose samples were then placed separately in airtight beakers containing 75% NaCl solution to provide a controlled humidity environment. Weights of the samples were recorded at the start of the experiment and subsequently after 3, 6, and 12 h of exposure. Moisture uptake was calculated using the formula:

Moisture Uptake (%) = (Ws​−Wd​) / Wd ​×100.

Where Ws is the sample weight after exposure to humidity and Wd is the initial dry weight.

Results

Cellulose extraction

Pretreatment of rice husk

The initial pretreatment effectively disrupted the rigid lignocellulosic matrix, loosening the husk structure and enhancing accessibility of cellulose (Fig. 1A). Visible reduction in surface impurities and partial degradation of hemicellulose and lignin were observed, providing a cleaner substrate for subsequent chemical treatments.

Hot water retting

Hot water retting successfully removed loosely bound waxes, soluble hemicellulose, and surface impurities (Fig. 1B). The process resulted in partial fibrillation and softening of the rice husk, evident from the cleaner fiber bundles that facilitated improved penetration of subsequent chemical reagents.

Caustic soda retting

Treatment with 4% NaOH solution (Fig. 1D) significantly dissolved hemicellulose and disrupted lignin linkages, exposing cellulose microfibrils. The fibers became more distinct, with reduced compactness, indicating effective alkali penetration and enhanced cellulose availability (Fig. 1C).

Acetic acid retting

Acetic acid treatment efficiently delignified the husk by solubilizing residual lignin, resulting in a lighter fiber color and smoother texture. This step improved cellulose purity while maintaining structural integrity, preparing the fibers for further alkaline refinement.

Alkaline treatment

The additional alkaline step further removed residual non-cellulosic components, yielding fibers with improved fibrillation and reduced matrix rigidity. The cellulose fraction appeared more pronounced and uniform, confirming efficient purification before bleaching.

Bleaching process

The bleaching stage produced highly purified cellulose by eliminating remaining lignin and pigments, resulting in a white, fibrous product. The fibers exhibited uniform texture and enhanced brightness, demonstrating successful preparation of cellulose suitable for nanocellulose extraction.

Nanocellulose synthesis

The sulfuric acid hydrolysis successfully converted purified cellulose into nanocellulose which is shown in Fig. 2, evident from the significant reduction in fiber dimensions and formation of uniform nanoscale structures. Upon drying, the nanocellulose formed a thin, flexible sheet-like structure, confirming successful synthesis. This sheet morphology reflects the nanoscale fibrillation and aggregation of cellulose fibers, providing a suitable material for advanced applications in nanocomposites and biopolymer matrices.

Characterization of nanocellulose

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The FTIR spectrum of the rice husk-derived nanocellulose (Fig. 3) confirms the successful isolation of high-purity cellulose fibrils. The dominant broad band at 3340–3400 cm− 1 is characteristic of O-H stretching vibrations, indicating the preservation of the hydrogen-bonded network of cellulose. A significant diagnostic feature is the absence of peaks at 1730 cm− 1 and 1510 cm− 1, which are typically associated with the C = O stretching of hemicellulose and the aromatic C = C vibrations of lignin, respectively. This confirms that the sequential pretreatment process effectively removed non-cellulosic matrix components. The peak at 1160 cm− 1 confirms the retention of beta-1,4-glycosidic linkages, proving that the structural integrity of the cellulose backbone remained intact throughout the acid hydrolysis process see Table 1.

Scanning electron microscopy

The scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis gave insight into the structure and surface morphology of the produced nanocellulose. The micrographs taken at 500, 1000, 2000, and 3000x magnifications clearly show a fibrous morphology composed of slender, elongated fibrils arranged in parallel or overlapping layers. At lower magnifications of 500x and 1000x, the surface exhibited rough, compact bundles with visible striations and grooves, which suggests some degree of fibrillation of the cellulose fibers. At higher magnifications of 2000x and 3000x, the fibrillar network became more defined, revealing nanoscale features and surface texture typical of cellulose fibers that have been mechanically or chemically processed. The nanofibers had diameters ranging from 80 to 100 nm, demonstrating successful reduction to the nanocellulose scale. The observed structure suggests a high aspect ratio that is tightly packed, which is beneficial for various applications. Overall, the results obtained from SEM indicate the successful formation of a dense and anisotropic nanocellulose network with preserved structural continuity and nanoscale dimensions and results are shown in Fig. 4.

Energy-dispersive X-ray analysis

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy was used to analyze the elemental composition of the synthesized nanocellulose sample and evaluate its purity. The EDX spectrum in Fig. 5 showed identifiable peaks for the elements carbon (C) and oxygen (O), which are the two major elements and given in Table 2 found in cellulose. Quantitative analysis revealed that carbon comprised 54.52 wt% and oxygen 45.48 wt%, corresponding to atomic percentages of 47.36% and 52.64%, respectively. These values are consistent with the empirical formula of cellulose (C₆H₁₀O₅)n, confirming the successful isolation of cellulose in nanoscale form, free from contamination from extraneous elements. The synthesized nanocellulose’s chemical purity is further verified by the absence of peaks for metals and other non-cellulosic compounds. This elemental profile confirms that the sample retained its organic nature and was not compromised during the extraction or processing steps. The EDX spectrum of the synthesized nanocellulose (Fig. 5) showed dominant peaks corresponding to carbon and oxygen, which are the principal elements of cellulose. Importantly, no distinct peaks corresponding to silicon, commonly present in rice husk silica, were observed. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the spectrum is dominated by sharp peaks for Carbon and Oxygen, consistent with the polysaccharide structure of cellulose. Critically, the baseline remains completely flat at 2.3 keV, confirming the absolute absence of residual sulfur from the acid hydrolysis step. This absence of sulfur, combined with the lack of silicon peaks commonly associated with rice husk, underscores the efficacy of the sequential pretreatment and subsequent washing-centrifugation cycles in achieving high chemical purity.

Moisture uptake test

The gravimetric analysis showed that both cellulose and nanocellulose absorbed moisture over time and experiment shown in Fig. 7; however, cellulose consistently exhibited higher uptake than nanocellulose. For cellulose, the initial dry weight was 0.149 g, which increased to 0.157 g after 3 h, 0.164 g after 6 h, and 0.171 g after 12 h. In comparison, nanocellulose sample initially weighed 0.099 g and showed a gradual increase in weight during moisture absorption, reaching 0.102 g after 3 h, 0.104 g after 6 h, and 0.106 g after 12 h. These results, when expressed as percentage moisture uptake, indicate that cellulose gained approximately 5.37% moisture at 3 h, 10.07% at 6 h, and 14.76% at 12 h, while nanocellulose absorbed only 3.03%, 5.05%, and 7.07% at the same intervals. A line graph plotting moisture uptake percentage against time in Fig. 8 clearly illustrates the difference in sorption behavior, with cellulose showing a steeper and more progressive increase compared to the relatively flatter curve of nanocellulose. Discussion

The extraction of nanocellulose from rice husk offers an environmentally friendly and cost-effective pathway for valorizing agricultural residues. Rice husk is generated in millions of tons annually and is particularly rich in cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, making it a promising feedstock for nanocellulose production4. In our study, sequential pretreatment involving hot water, alkali, acetic acid, and bleaching effectively removed non-cellulosic fractions such as lignin, hemicellulose, waxes, and silica. This stepwise approach ensured gradual purification while minimizing cellulose degradation, consistent with earlier reports demonstrating that optimized pretreatment strategies improve the accessibility and purity of cellulose8.

The sequential pretreatment protocol employed in this study consisting of hot water retting, caustic soda treatment, acetic acid delignification, alkaline treatment, and bleaching differs fundamentally from existing extraction methods. While conventional protocols often rely on direct alkali treatment followed by energy-intensive mechanical processes such as high-pressure homogenization or TEMPO-mediated oxidation, our method utilizes a stepwise chemical refinement. This specific sequence progressively removes non-cellulosic components (lignin, hemicellulose, and waxes) in a controlled manner, which improves cellulose accessibility and prevents the degradation that often occurs during harsh, single-step acid treatments.

Furthermore, unlike existing methods that typically produce nanocellulose in powder or suspension form, this sequential protocol facilitates the formation of continuous, self-standing nanocellulose sheets upon drying. The high degree of purification achieved through our sequential approach enhances interfibrillar hydrogen bonding during the drying phase, leading to a unique aggregation mechanism that results in a dense, moisture-resistant fibrillar network. This methodology offers a relatively simple, cost-effective pathway using commonly available reagents, making it highly suitable for sustainable industrial-scale production without requiring specialized high-energy equipment.

Subsequent sulfuric acid hydrolysis successfully converted the purified cellulose into nanoscale fibrils. The process eliminated amorphous regions of cellulose and yielded a suspension that, upon repeated washing and centrifugation, produced a clean nanocellulose fraction. Upon drying at 60 °C, the nanocellulose aggregated into a continuous sheet-like structure, an outcome that reflects strong inter-fibrillar hydrogen bonding. While prior studies typically report nanocrystals or nanofibrils in powder or suspension form20, the self-standing sheet produced here highlights a novel morphological outcome that could be directly utilized in applications such as biodegradable films, wound dressings, and barrier membranes.

Characterization confirmed the nanoscale nature and purity of the product. SEM imaging revealed slender fibrils with diameters of 80–100 nm, consistent with the nanoscale dimensions of cellulose fibrils reported in similar studies8. FTIR analysis demonstrated the retention of characteristic cellulose functional groups while confirming the removal of lignin and hemicellulose. EDX analysis revealed an elemental composition dominated by carbon and oxygen, matching the theoretical stoichiometry of cellulose. Together, these results indicate that the sequential pretreatment followed by sulfuric acid hydrolysis yields nanocellulose of high chemical purity and structural integrity.

The lower moisture uptake observed for nanocellulose compared with cellulose may be attributed to the formation of a densely packed nanocellulose sheet during drying, where strong interfibrillar hydrogen bonding results in a compact fibrillar network with reduced porosity. This structural densification limits the accessibility of hydroxyl groups to water molecules, thereby decreasing moisture adsorption despite the inherently hydrophilic nature of nanocellulose. In addition, the formation of a relatively compact nanocellulose sheet was intentionally favored in this study considering its potential application in biodegradable plastic composites, where reduced hygroscopicity is advantageous for improving dimensional stability and performance of the resulting materials.

Previous studies have reported nanocellulose extraction from rice husk using methods such as high-pressure homogenization, TEMPO-mediated oxidation, or direct acid hydrolysis. These approaches typically produce nanocrystals or nanofibrils in powder or suspension form. In contrast, the present study employs a sequential pretreatment strategy combining hot water retting, alkaline treatment, acetic acid delignification, and bleaching prior to sulfuric acid hydrolysis. This stepwise approach enhances the removal of lignin and hemicellulose, improving cellulose accessibility. Furthermore, the synthesized nanocellulose forms a continuous sheet-like structure upon drying, which has potential applications in biodegradable films and bioplastic reinforcement materials.

Similar strategies have been reported in natural fiber–based polymer composites, where surface modification and incorporation of nanofillers significantly improve the mechanical performance and structural stability of the resulting materials. For example, Nair et al. demonstrated that natural fiber waste combined with nanoclay fillers can enhance the mechanical properties of polymer matrix composites, highlighting the potential of bio-based materials for sustainable composite development21.

Compared with more complex processes such as high-pressure homogenization or TEMPO-mediated oxidation, the present method is relatively simple, requiring only commonly available reagents and equipment8. The novelty of this work lies in the demonstration that carefully sequenced pretreatment steps not only enhance cellulose accessibility but also enable the formation of continuous nanocellulose sheets upon drying. Such sheet morphology has not been widely reported for rice husk-derived nanocellulose and provides new opportunities for direct use in advanced material applications. The moisture uptake test further validated the improved stability of nanocellulose over cellulose. While cellulose absorbed progressively higher amounts of moisture during exposure, nanocellulose showed only minimal increases, indicating its reduced hygroscopic nature. This property enhances its suitability for applications such as bioplastics and packaging where moisture resistance is essential.

Quantitatively, the nanocellulose fibrils synthesized in this study exhibited diameters ranging from 80 to 100 nm. While these dimensions are within the nanoscale regime, they are notably larger than those reported by Samsalee et al.3, who achieved diameters of 10–12 nm using high-pressure homogenization. Similarly, the peroxyformic acid method employed by Vu et al.20 yielded thinner structures with diameters of 11.7–48.3 nm.

The comparatively larger diameter in the present work is attributed to the absence of high-energy mechanical fibrillation or ultrasonication steps, which are typically required to break down microfibril bundles into individual elementary fibrils. This research results align more closely with Iorfa et al.8, who reported diameter ranges of 50–150 nm using similar chemical-only hydrolysis.

Despite the larger diameter, recent research approach achieved a higher degree of purification, evidenced by the C: O ratio (54.52:45.48 wt%) which closely matches the theoretical stoichiometry of pure cellulose. Furthermore, unlike the powders or suspensions reported by Samsalee and Vu, these fibrils aggregated into a continuous, self-standing sheet upon drying. This unique morphology explains the significantly lower moisture uptake (7.07% at 12 h) compared to standard cellulose (14.77%), confirming a more compact and dimensionally stable network suitable for industrial bioplastic applications.

The nanoscale dimensions obtained in this study are comparable with previously reported rice husk-derived nanocellulose, although variations in fibril size are commonly observed depending on extraction conditions and mechanical treatments. For example, nanocellulose produced through high-pressure homogenization has been reported to exhibit diameters of approximately 10–12 nm4, with lengths of several hundred nanometers. Other studies have reported cellulose nanowhiskers from rice husk with diameters ranging from 11.7 to 48.3 nm20, depending on the rice husk variety and hydrolysis conditions. In comparison, the fibril diameters obtained in the present study (80–100 nm) are slightly larger, which may be attributed to the absence of high-energy mechanical fibrillation or ultrasonication steps. Nevertheless, the observed morphology still confirms successful fibrillation of cellulose into nanoscale structures suitable for applications in bio-based composite materials.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. Sulfuric acid hydrolysis requires careful handling and waste treatment, and repeated washing increases water use. Furthermore, while morphological and compositional purity have been established, additional characterization such as crystallinity index, thermal stability, and mechanical strength would strengthen the understanding of performance in targeted applications. Despite these limitations, the work demonstrates a reproducible and sustainable approach to convert low-value rice husk into high-value nanocellulose materials with unique structural characteristics.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study successfully demonstrated the conversion of rice husk, an abundant agro-waste, into high-purity nanocellulose through sequential pretreatment and sulfuric acid hydrolysis. The process effectively removed hemicellulose, lignin, and other non-cellulosic fractions, yielding nanoscale fibrils with diameters of 80–100 nm and a continuous sheet-like morphology. Characterization through FTIR, SEM, and EDX confirmed the structural integrity, nanoscale features, and chemical purity of the material, while the moisture uptake test highlighted its improved dimensional stability compared to cellulose. These findings establish rice husk-derived nanocellulose as a sustainable, eco-friendly, and versatile biomaterial with promising applications in biopolymers, packaging, medicine, and advanced material engineering.
