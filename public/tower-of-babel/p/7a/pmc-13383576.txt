Robot-navigated sacroiliac joint fusion as salvage surgery for pelvic fixation failure in adult spinal deformity surgery: patient series
Authors: Arnav Sharma, Muhammad M. Abd-El-Barr, Peter Passias, Christopher I. Shaffrey, Brett Rocos, Parastou Fatemi Quist, William Richardson, Khoi D. Than
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPelvic fixation failure (PFF) remains prevalent in adult spinal deformity correction, with failure rates up to 35%. Current revision surgeries are highly invasive and morbid, requiring extensive incisions and recovery times. Sacroiliac joint fusions (SIJFs) may offer a salvage option for PFF. Robotic navigation has previously been explored as a feasible method for primary SIJF. In this study, the authors aim to establish the viability of robot-navigated SIJF as a salvage surgery for PFF.OBSERVATIONSThis study examined that data of patients who underwent previous deformity correction of at least four levels, experienced symptomatic PFF with radiographic evidence, and underwent robot-navigated SIJF as salvage. Eight patients were identified. The mean operative time was 45.1 ± 6.4 minutes and the mean blood loss was 7.5 ± 3.8 mL. The mean postoperative self-reported pain improvement was 48.1% ± 41.6% at 1.5 months (p = 0.029), 53.6% ± 41.3% at 3 months (p = 0.007), and 58.6% ± 40.4% at latest follow-up (p = 0.002). All patients with multiple follow-ups reported at least 50% improvement during the postoperative course. Pelvic fixation remains a crucial technique in anchoring extensive hardware constructs in adult spinal deformity (ASD) surgery. Pelvic fixation increases stability across the lumbosacral joint and improves rates of lumbosacral fusion success.1–3 Yet, pelvic fixation failure (PFF) remains a prominent barrier in achieving optimal postoperative ­outcomes. PFF includes rod fracture, pseudarthrosis, hardware ­loosening, dislodged set caps, or sacroiliac (SI) fracture. The two most common current approaches to pelvic fixation are traditional direct iliac screw (IS) placement or S2-alar-iliac (S2AI) screws, which directly cross the SI joint. Although the literature describes mild discrepancies in PFF rates between the two techniques, both demonstrate high failure rates, with studies finding rates as high as 34.3% in IS and 35% in S2AI.1,4

In cases of minimally symptomatic PFF, patients have the option of conservative management through radiographic monitoring and pain control. However, in cases of major PFF requiring surgical intervention, revision surgeries tend to be invasive and highly morbid. Concurrently, revision surgery is explicitly identified as a risk factor for repeated PFF.4 Although navigation-assisted SI fixation techniques have been described, a well-defined minimally invasive salvage strategy specifically for symptomatic PFF after long-segment ASD surgery remains poorly characterized in the literature.

One solution may be found in the emerging field of robotic spine surgery. Wang et al. described a novel robot-navigated approach to SI joint fusion (SIJF), with results demonstrating a reproducible ambulatory surgery that yielded minimal complications and significant improvement in postoperative pain.5 Although SIJF is traditionally ­indicated in degenerative SI joint pathology, our observation leads us to believe that its scope of usage can be broadened to PFF.

In this case series, we aim to establish the baseline viability of robot-navigated SIJF as a salvage surgery for PFF, thereby providing a minimally invasive yet effective alternative to traditionally morbid revision surgeries.

Study Description

Design and Patient Selection

This study was a retrospective case series with the goal of evaluating clinical outcomes of robot-navigated SIJF as a salvage surgery for PFF. Operative case logs of a single surgeon were reviewed from January 2022 to December 2025. Patient inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) previous significant multilevel (defined as four or more levels) spinal fusion with posterior instrumentation and anchoring pelvic fixation; 2) radiographic evidence of distal pelvic fixation abnormality, including peri-hardware lucency suggestive of loosening, hardware migration, rod fracture at the lumbosacral junction, sacral fracture adjacent to instrumentation, or progressive distal junctional instability (Fig. 1); 3) concordant symptomatic clinical presentation characterized by new or progressive low back pain localizing to the distal lumbosacral/SI region, often unilateral or asymmetric, with transient improvement on diagnostic/therapeutic steroid injection of the SI joint; and 4) treatment with robot-navigated SIJF. Alternative pain generators, such as lumbosacral pseudarthrosis, adjacent segment pathology, and hip-related pathology, were evaluated by review of clinical examination and available imaging. Patients were only included when the treating surgeon determined that symptoms were most consistent with symptomatic PFF and that revision with SIJF was indicated.

Follow-up visits were scheduled for 1.5, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Patients reported self-estimated percent improvement from baseline at each follow-up, with missing data supplemented via telephone follow-up.

Demographic variables collected included age, sex, BMI, type of previous fixation, days between previous fixation and SIJF, type of instrumentation in previous fixation, levels of previous fixation, and reason for revision. Perioperative variables collected included laterality of SIJF, estimated blood loss, length of surgery, PACU-to-discharge time, total length of patient stay, and postoperative complications. Postoperative clinical and radiographic outcomes collected included patient percent improvement in pain, radiographic evidence on CT of SI joint fusion, and occurrence of 30- or 90-day readmissions. Postoperative CT imaging performed ≥ 9 months following SIJF was reviewed to assess for adequate fusion across the SI joint; fusion was specifically defined as visible bridging bone across the SI joint on multiplanar review without radiographic evidence of implant loosening, hardware failure, or persistent lucent cleft across the treated joint.

Statistical Analysis

Given the small sample size, analysis was primarily descriptive. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Postoperative self-estimated percent improvement in pain was summarized at each available follow-up time point.

Limited paired comparisons between preoperative baseline and postoperative outcomes at 1.5 months, 3 months, and the latest ­follow-up were performed using paired Student’s t-tests at a significance of α = 0.05. For time points with very small sample sizes (6-month and 12-month follow-ups), outcomes are reported at the individual patient level.

Technical Note on Screw Trajectory Planning

A unique challenge of SIJF as a salvage surgery is the consideration of prior hardware in screw trajectory planning. Optimal screw placement involves three screws spanning the articular portion of the SI joint. Although three screws remain ideal in SIJF with preexisting hardware, spatial and anatomical constraints occasionally restrict the surgeon to two screws. Nonetheless, successful outcomes can be achieved with two screws.

A preoperative CT scan of the patient’s lumbar spine and pelvis is uploaded into proprietary planning software, through which a synthetic 3D model is created. The 3D model is rotated toward the operative side of interest. A true lateral view is obtained, requiring superimposed alignment of the alar lines and sciatic notches. On this view, boundaries for screw placement are encompassed by a triangle, with the borders being the alar line, anterior aspect of the sacrum, and posterior aspect of the sacrum (Fig. 2A). Initial screw placement is done in this true lateral view with respect to the boundaries, taking care to avoid preexisting hardware. Generally, two screws can fit superior to prior pelvic fixation hardware, and one can fit inferior. Occasional anatomical and spatial limitations, however, may only allow for two screws to fit (Fig. 2A). FIG. 2.Preoperative planning for a 50-year-old patient undergoing left-sided SIJF. Once sagittal alignment on the synthetic model is found, attention is then turned toward the software’s CT-guided trajectory mapping in the axial and coronal planes (Fig. 2B). In these planes, screw alignment and length are optimized to achieve maximum purchase across the articulation of the SI joint while ensuring the screw remains within the desired anatomical boundaries. The superior-most screw is aimed cranial to the S1 foramen, toward the S1 pedicle. The second screw is aimed toward the S1 foramen, with special care given to not contact the foramen itself. The caudal-most screw is aimed inferior to the S1 foramen. As screws are adjusted in the software, they are further confirmed to not breech anteriorly or into the S1 foramen using the synthetic model’s pelvic inlet and outlet views, respectively; these projections approximate fluoroscopic inlet and outlet views used intraoperatively to assess anterior-posterior depth and foraminal safety. Pelvic inlet views are taken with the virtual projection tilted 30° caudally, allowing for visualization of the pelvic brim and confirmation of depth and anterior/posterior alignment of the screw (Fig. 2C). Pelvic outlet views are taken with the virtual projection tilted 30° cranially, allowing for circular projection of sacral foramina (Fig. 2D).

After screw trajectories are grossly planned, minor adjustments are made to ensure screws follow the natural curve of the sacral spine on the lateral view and are parallel on the pelvic outlet view. The safety of screw lengths is reconfirmed to ensure the S1 foramen is not breeched (Fig. 2E).

At this stage, preoperative planning is complete. A comparison can be noted between the planned trajectory and final postoperative screw alignment in an example case (Fig. 2F).

Demographics and perioperative metrics

Values are presented as number of patients (%) or mean ± SD.

All patients’ primary presenting symptom was low back pain. Seven patients identified the location of this pain as paramedian localizing to the SI joint; 1 patient identified midline pain. Pain was unilateral or dominantly asymmetric in 7 of 8 patients. All patients identified worsening pain associated with activity. Three patients noted accompanying radiculopathy with their back pain. All patients had attempted medications, steroid injection, and physical therapy preoperatively.

During preoperative screw planning, 5 (62.5%) patients’ anatomical dimensions were able to support three planned screw trajectories. In the remaining 3 (37.5%) patients, two screws were planned.

Four patients underwent left-sided SIJF, and 4 patients underwent right-sided SIJF. The mean estimated blood loss was 7.5 ± 3.8 mL (5 mL in 5 patients). The mean length of surgery was 45.1 ± 6.4 ­minutes, the mean PACU-to-discharge time was 169.9 ± 84.1 minutes, and the mean total length of stay was 385.8 ± 87.4 minutes. All patients were discharged the same day as their surgery. No complications were noted.

The mean time of the latest follow-up was 17.9 ± 16.8 months. The median latest follow-up was at 14 months (IQR 4.5–27 months) with a range of 1.5–46 months. Six (75%) patients had at least 5 months of follow-up data; 2 had surgery within 6 months of data extraction. Four (50%) patients had more than 1 year of follow-up data.

Self-estimated percent improvement in pain demonstrated ­significance compared to preoperative baseline at 1.5-month follow-up at 48.1% ± 41.6% (p = 0.029), 3-month follow-up at 53.6% ± 41.3% (p = 0.007), and latest follow-up at 58.6% ± 40.4% (p = 0.002) (Table 2). Discussion

Observations

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and outcomes of robot-navigated SIJF as a salvage for PFF in ASD surgery. SIJF has a successful track record in treating degenerative SI joint pathology. A prospective clinical trial with 2 years of follow-up found that minimally invasive SIJF yielded significant improvement in 6-month pain scores compared to nonsurgical management; these improvements were further sustained through the 2-year follow-up interval.6 Dengler et al. similarly identified minimally invasive SIJF as offering superior pain improvement compared to conservative management in patients with low back pain of SI joint etiology.7 Despite its efficacy, SIJF remains unexplored as a salvage strategy for PFF in ASD.

The need for a minimally invasive salvage option remains substantial due to a high basal rate of PFF regardless of screw type. Current literature identifies a wide range of PFF rates in both IS and S2AI screw placement.8 A 2013 study identified a PFF rate of 34.3% with ISs, with 11.9% of patients having a major PFF (i.e., rod breakage or removal);4 a 2014 study found rates of 35% in S2AI screws and 12% in ISs;1 and a 2023 study noted a PFF rate of 4.3%.9 Eastlack et al., in a 2022 retrospective multicenter cohort study with 418 patients (the largest thus far in the literature), found an overall PFF rate of 23.74%.10

Current revision surgeries for PFF entail either direct replacement of the offending instrumentation or extensive additional hardware implantation, often in the form of 4-rod constructs requiring large incisions, long hospital stays, and substantial recovery times. These current approaches also lead to high rates of downstream revision. In a 13-center retrospective review of acute PFFs in adult spinal fusions, Martin et al. found a 16% rate of required second revisions following initial posterior instrumentation revision and pelvic screw replacement.11 Furthermore, the study found that secondary revision rates dropped when a 4-rod construct was used connecting to at least 4 pelvic fixation points; correspondingly, all 16% of the patients requiring secondary revisions required second revised constructs with at least 4 rods. This reliance on extensive secondary constructs underscores the need for less morbid revision strategies. Robot-navigated SIJF may fulfill this role in selected patients with symptomatic PFF. Neuromodulatory strategies such as spinal cord stimulation may be considered in selected patients with persistent postoperative pain without a clear structural pain generator; however, in this cohort, symptoms localized to the lumbosacral/SI region with concurrent radiographic abnormalities, favoring structural salvage.

In our study, all 8 patients meeting inclusion criteria had prior S2AI fixations that failed due to mechanical complications relating to the screw or screw cap. Overall, 87.5% of revisions were due to loosened S2AI screws; this finding aligns with those of Eastlack et al., who reported a high baseline screw-loosening rate of 21.37% in S2AI screws as well as significantly higher relative rates of screw loosening in S2AI screws compared to ISs (p = 0.001).10

Importantly, radiographic S2AI screw loosening alone does not necessarily represent clinically meaningful failure, as some degree of lucency may be encountered without symptoms. In this series, loosening was considered pathological only when accompanied by a respective mechanical pain localizing to the SI region and judged to represent distal construct failure requiring revision. As such, our findings should not be interpreted to suggest that all S2AI screw loosening warrants intervention, but rather that symptomatic loosening may identify a subset of patients in whom SIJF serves as a targeted salvage strategy.

Perioperative findings highlighted the minimally invasive aspect offered by robot-navigated SIJF. Smith et al. identified a mean blood loss of 288 ± 182 mL in open SIJF, which was significantly higher than their collected estimated blood loss of 33 ± 27 mL for nonrobotic minimally invasive SIJF.12 Our data reported lower blood loss (7.5 ± 3.8 mL) than both previously reported open and minimally invasive SIJF. This value was consistent with that of Wang et al., who found robotic SIJF to yield a mean blood loss of 21.0 ± 16.7 mL.5

Similar trends were noted across length of surgery, where our data demonstrated a mean operative length of 45.1 ± 6.4 minutes and mean total length of stay of 385.8 ± 87.4 minutes, whereas corresponding values for minimally invasive SIJF in Smith et al. demonstrated 70 ± 24 minutes and 1.3 ± 0.5 days, respectively.12 In comparison, open SIJF metrics were 163 ± 25 minutes and 5.1 ± 1.9 days, respectively. Therefore, our findings demonstrate lower blood loss, mean operative time, and mean hospital stay time compared to previous works examining perioperative metrics in minimally invasive SIJF.

We found no postoperative complications, 30-day readmissions, or 90-day readmissions, highlighting relative baseline safety. Statistically significant self-reported scores in pain reduction were found at the ­1.5-month follow-up (p = 0.029), 3-month follow-up (p = 0.007), and latest follow-up (p = 0.002).

We were unable to derive any further analytical metrics from our 6- and 12-month follow-up data, as only 3 and 2 patients’ data were available to us at the respective time points. Yet, our patient-level pain values at those time points still highlight the positive trend in pain improvement consistent across other temporal benchmarks. Notably, despite inconsistent overlapping temporal trends in recovery kinetics, there was significant improvement noted for nearly all patients at the most recent follow-up. This finding seems to indicate substantial success rates on a short-term level and significant promise with long-term outcomes, consistent with previously described literature.

Lessons

Given its technical feasibility, favorable perioperative profile, and consistent pain improvement, minimally invasive robot-navigated SIJF should be considered a viable salvage surgery for PFF. Rather than pursuing extensive posterior revision with hardware replacement or multirod construct augmentation, this approach yields a minimally invasive alternative in managing PFF by directly addressing ­lumbosacral-pelvic instability through arthrodesis.

This strategy reconceptualizes PFF not solely as an instrumentation problem, but as a mechanical failure at the SI joint interface. By achieving fusion across the SI articulation, SIJF may restore load transfer stability while avoiding the morbidity associated with large posterior revisions. In appropriately selected patients, particularly those with symptomatic S2AI screw loosening, robotic SIJF may provide meaningful pain relief with minimal blood loss, short operative times, and same-day discharge.

Preoperative trajectory planning is critical in the setting of prior instrumentation. Safe and effective screw placement requires careful 3D planning to avoid existing hardware and sacral foramina while maximizing purchase across the SI joint. Robotic navigation facilitates this precision and may expand the feasibility of this approach in anatomically constrained revision settings.

This study had several limitations. First, as a retrospective case series of 8 patients treated by a single surgeon, the sample size is small and limits statistical power as well as generalizability; as such, comparative analyses should be interpreted cautiously. The absence of a control group precludes direct comparison with traditional revision strategies. Additionally, there is an inability to perform meaningful subgroup analyses (e.g., which PFF mechanisms are best served with SIJF). Second, pain outcomes were based on patient self-reported percentage improvement relative to preoperative baseline, not through formal validated outcome measures. Validated patient-reported outcome instruments such as the visual analog scale/numeric rating scale, Oswestry Disability Index, or Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System were not consistently available across the retrospective follow-up period and therefore could not be included in analysis; pain was instead captured as patient self-estimated improvement relative to baseline, which is subjective and introduces recall bias while limiting comparison with prior SIJF literature. Third, radiographic follow-up was limited, with postoperative CT imaging beyond 9 months available in just 3 patients. While all scans showed arthrodesis, longer-term imaging is needed to confirm durability.

Despite these limitations, our study highlights tremendous feasibility for this novel indication for robot-navigated SIJF, supporting strong short-term clinical efficacy. While longer-term durability remains to be established, preliminary long-term data are promising. These findings suggest that robot-navigated SIJF may represent a possible minimally invasive paradigm shift in the management algorithm for selected cases of PFF. Future prospective comparative studies are needed to determine which patterns of failure are most amenable to this approach and to evaluate long-term outcomes and construct stability.
