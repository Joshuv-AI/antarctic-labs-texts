Prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and public health risk assessment of zoonotic bacterial pathogens in raw cattle meat from three zonal cities in Tigray, Ethiopia
Authors: Merhawit Reda, Teka Mebrahtu, Muuz Gebru, Mebrahtu Weldegebriel, Mebrahtu Berhe, Haftay Abraha, Kedir Seid, Gidey Ambaye, Abrha Bsrat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEthiopia harbors a large cattle population that significantly contributes to the national economy through meat and dairy production. However, bacterial contamination of raw meat and antimicrobial resistance present serious public health challenges. This study aimed to isolate zoonotic bacterial pathogens from raw cattle meat and assess their antibiotic resistance profiles in the urban centers of Mekelle, Maichew, and Humera in Tigray, Ethiopia. A cross-sectional survey was conducted from October 2019 to June 2020, collecting one hundred thirty-two (132) raw meat samples from three abattoirs. Bacterial isolation and identification were performed using conventional microbiological methods, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing followed Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. Additionally, a questionnaire survey assessed knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to rational drug use among livestock owners and veterinary professionals. Result showed bacterial contamination in 95.45% of samples, with frequent presence of Escherichia coli (38.64%), Salmonella spp. (63.64%), Staphylococcus aureus (65.15%), and Campylobacter spp. (43.94%). Resistance was notably high to some antibiotics such as Vancomycin (100%) and Erythromycin (78.26%), which are not commonly used in veterinary practice in the region, while resistance to commonly used antibiotics like Amoxicillin was moderate (47.83%). The survey revealed relatively good knowledge of drug use among professionals (79.17%) but poor practices among livestock owners (56.16%). These findings highlight significant bacterial contamination likely due to poor hygiene in abattoirs and handling, along with concerns about antimicrobial resistance potentially linked to unregulated veterinary antibiotic use. This study underscores the need for improved meat hygiene measures, strengthened veterinary drug regulation, and targeted awareness campaigns to promote rational antimicrobial use and enhance food safety, thereby mitigating public health risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04469-4.Keywords: Abattoir, Antimicrobial susceptibility testing bacterial isolates, Food borne diseases, Food safety

Introduction

Ethiopia has an estimated cattle population of approximately 59.5 million, contributing significantly to the country’s agricultural economy [1]. Annually, these cattle produce over 3.6 million tons of milk and around one million tons of meat, making animal-derived foods essential for nutrition and economic sustenance [2]. However, foodborne diseases remain a major public health concern worldwide, affecting both developed and developing nations due to inadequate hygiene and poor food management practices [3].

Despite efforts to ensure meat hygiene, microbial contamination can occur at various stages of processing, from farm to consumption [4–6]. Pathogenic bacteria that enter the food chain at multiple points, posing a significant health risk, frequently cause food borne diseases. These diseases, many of which are zoonotic, can be transmitted between animals and humans through direct or indirect contact [7]. The burden of food borne illnesses is particularly severe in developing nations, where over a billion cases of gastroenteritis and approximately five million deaths occur annually due to contaminated food [8].

The disparity in food borne disease prevalence between developed and developing nations highlights the critical need for improved food safety and hygiene practices in developing nations. Africa, in particular, faces significant challenges due to weak food monitoring systems, inadequate food safety laws, economic constraints in acquiring safety equipment, poor infrastructure, and a general lack of awareness and training among food handlers [9]. Common food borne pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus, and Campylobacter spp. are frequently isolated from contaminated meat and dairy products, contributing to severe public health concerns [10]. These pathogens persist in natural environments, raw animal products, and food processing facilities, thriving even under unfavorable conditions such as low nutrient availability and high environmental stress [11]. Among these, Salmonella species are of particular concern due to their global impact on public health [12]. Annually, about 1.3 billion cases of acute non-typhoidal gastroenteritis and three million deaths are attributed to Salmonella infections worldwide. In the United States alone, approximately 1.4 million cases occur each year, resulting in 17,000 hospitalizations and 585 deaths. Additionally, Salmonella accounts for a significant proportion of foodborne illnesses in the European Union, contributing to 95% of reported cases [7].

Similarly, Staphylococcus aureus is a zoonotic pathogen commonly found in animal products and human carriers. It can cause a range of illnesses, from minor skin infections to severe conditions such as pneumonia, bacteremia, meningitis, and food poisoning [5, 13, 14]. Escherichia coli, another well-known pathogen, naturally exists in the gastrointestinal tracts of humans and animals but includes strains like E. coli O157:H7, which are highly virulent and responsible for severe foodborne outbreaks [15]. Campylobacter spp., particularly C. jejuni and C. coli, are also significant contributors to global foodborne diseases, with approximately 98% of reported cases linked to gastrointestinal infections [16, 17]. These pathogens pose a substantial health and economic burden, particularly in low-income countries where proper food safety interventions are limited.

In Ethiopia, the consumption of raw meat is a common cultural practice, increasing the risk of foodborne illnesses [4, 18]. The excessive use of antibiotics in animals contributes to the selection of resistant bacterial strains, which can be transferred to humans through the food chain [19, 20]. The misuse of antibiotics in Ethiopian veterinary practices is attributed to factors such as incorrect dosages, inaccurate diagnoses, lack of standardized veterinary treatment guidelines, and the frequent use of first-line antibiotics [21]. These practices have contributed to the rise of multidrug-resistant bacteria, posing a serious threat to public health. Common antibiotics used in Ethiopian veterinary medicine include drugs for both therapeutic and prophylactic purposes, but resistance to these agents has compromised treatment efficacy [22].

Research conducted in different parts of Ethiopia has documented the presence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria in diverse food sources. For instance, Salmonella isolates from apparently healthy slaughtered cattle have demonstrated antimicrobial resistance patterns [23–26], while E. coli has been detected in raw meat samples from Addis Ababa and Bishoftu [27]. Furthermore, the prevalence of E. coli has been reported in raw meat obtained from abattoirs in Dire Dawa City [28] and from apparently healthy cattle slaughtered at Haramaya University [29]. Campylobacter spp. has also been identified in a variety of food products across several regions [30–33], and Staphylococcus spp. has been isolated from different food sources in Ethiopia [4, 29, 34].

In Ethiopia, and particularly in the Tigray region, food borne diseases and antimicrobial resistance remain critical public health concerns. These challenges are exacerbated by weak food safety practices, limited regulatory enforcement, and the widespread consumption of raw or undercooked meat, which heightens the risk of exposure to pathogenic bacteria. Addressing this issue requires systematic detection and control measures. Therefore, the present study was undertaken with three primary aims: to isolate major bacterial pathogens from raw cattle meat in Tigray, to evaluate the antimicrobial resistance patterns of the identified isolates, and to investigate the perceptions and practices of both the community and field animal health professionals regarding the rational use of veterinary drugs.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was carried out in three locations within the Tigray region of Ethiopia: Maichew, Mekelle, and Humera. Maichew, located at an altitude of 2,432 m.a.s.l., has a mixed farming economy and an estimated cattle population of 77,000. Its municipal abattoir slaughters about five cattle daily, with numbers tripling during holidays. Cattle are sourced from local markets and nearby towns, including Ofla, Adishiwo, and Raya-Azebo [35].

Mekelle, the capital of Tigray region, sits at 2,300 m.a.s.l. and has a human population of over 400,000. The city’s economy includes trade and small enterprises. Its abattoir primarily receives cattle from Western, Southern, and Central Tigray, processing animals from Humera, Sheraro, and Raya-Azebo [36].

Humera, in Western Tigray region, has a hot climate with temperatures ranging from 27 °C to 45 °C. The city is home to about 129,343 cattle, including local and Begait breeds. Its municipal abattoir processes around eight cattle per day, doubling during festivals. Cattle originate from Humera’s farms and nearby areas like Adebay and Maykadra [37].

Study population

A simple random sampling method was used to select the study zones, with zonal cities chosen through a lottery-based approach. Municipal abattoirs in the selected zones were visited during three purposively selected Orthodox Christian holidays, and slaughtered animals were sampled using a simple random sampling technique. Additionally, a questionnaire survey was conducted through personal interviews to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of livestock owners who came to the abattoir during the sampling days. Socio-demographic, hygiene, and sanitation practice data were collected from the individuals working in the butcher shops and from the study participants in the butchery houses and animal health professionals (including veterinarians in government clinics, private veterinary pharmacies, and clinics) in the three study areas were interviewed regarding the rational use, handling, and utilization of veterinary drugs.

Sample size, sample collection, transport, and processing

There are seven zonal abattoirs in the Tigray region of Ethiopia. Out of these, three were intentionally selected based on their agroecological zones and accessibility: Humera (lowland), Mekelle (midland), and Maichew (highland). A total of one hundred thirty-two (132) raw red cattle meat samples were aseptically collected from 1,320 cattle slaughtered at these abattoirs. Specifically, 60 samples were taken from Abergele Export Abattoir in Mekelle (out of 600 cattle), 31 from Humera Municipal Abattoir (out of 310 cattle), and 41 from Maichew Municipal Abattoir (out of 410 cattle). The number of samples from each site was determined proportionally to the facility’s daily slaughter capacity, with every tenth animal selected for sampling. Sections of meat samples were aseptically collected and placed in separate sterile plastic bags to prevent spilling and cross-contamination. Using aseptic procedures, trained study assistants took the samples and transported them to the Veterinary Microbiology Laboratory of the College of Veterinary Sciences, Mekelle University, using an icebox and stored them at 4 °C until the performance of laboratory experiments. Upon arrival, specimens were inoculated onto different types of mediums based on the bacterial species [38].

Bacteriological isolation and identification of pathogenic

Salmonella species were isolated and identified according to the technique recommended by the International Organization for Standardization. Detection of Salmonella species was performed using the standard guidelines from ISO 6579: 2002. This isolation and identification procedure involves principal stages including pre-enrichment, selective enrichment, selective plating, and confirmation using biochemical test [39].

Pre-enrichment

Raw meat specimens were pre-enriched with an inappropriate amount of buffered peptone water and lactose broth in a (1:9) ratio, or twenty-five grams of raw meat was placed in 225 ml of peptone water or lactose broth to produce high resuscitation rates for bacteria and promote intense growth. The sample mixture was shaken for approximately 2 min and was incubated at 37 ± 1 °C for 24 h [40].

Selective enrichment

Selenite F broth was used for the selective enrichment purpose. About 1 ml of the pre-enriched broth was transferred into a tube containing 10 mL of Selenite F broth and was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h [40].

Isolation of Salmonella species. Xylose lysine deoxycholate (XLD) agar, MacConkey agar, Salmonella and Shigella agar (SSA), and bismuth sulfite (BS) agar plates were used for plating out and identification. A loop full of inoculums from Selenite broth cultures was inoculated into XLD, BS, SSA, and MacConkey agar plates and was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the plates were examined for the presence of typical and suspect colonies. Typical colonies of Salmonella grown on XLD agar have a black center and a light transparent zone of reddish color due to the color change of the media, while H2S-negative variants grown on XLD agar are pink with a darker pink center. On BS agar, Salmonella colonies are brown, grey, or black, sometimes with a metallic sheen. Typical colonies of Salmonella on SSA are with a black center (spot black at the center), 1 mm to 2 mm in diameter, and cause the color of the medium to change to a typical color. Suspected colonies were selected from the selective plating media, streaked onto the surface of pre-dried nutrient agar plates, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h [40].

Escherichia coli

Isolation of E. coli was conducted following standard procedure. Upon arrival at the laboratory, all pre-enriched buffered peptone water broth raw meat samples were subsequently inoculated onto MacConkey agar and were incubated at 37 °C overnight. Bacterial growth was subjected to a lactose fermenter and a non-lactose fermenter, and the lactose fermenter colony was sub-cultured to Eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Colonies showing typical dark red to purple red with metallic sheen were taken as E. coli isolates [41].

Staphylococcus aureus

Isolation of S. aureus was conducted following the standard procedure. Upon arrival at the laboratory, Mannitol salt agar (HI Media Laboratories, Pvt. Mumbai, India) was used and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the presence of S. aureus was confirmed by golden yellowish colonies, Gram stain, and a biochemical test using a slide coagulase test [42, 43].

Campylobacter species

Approximately 10 g of raw meat sample was aseptically collected using sterile forceps and scissors and placed into 90 ml of buffered peptone water in a sterile plastic bag and homogenized for 1 min using a stomacher and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h in the microaerophilic atmosphere (gas mix of 5% O2, 10% CO2, and 85% N2). Then a 0.1 ml of the enriched sample was streaked onto Karmali Campylobacter Agar Base (HiMedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India) (Blood free Campylobacter selective agar base medium containing Campylobacter selective supplement comprising cefoperazone, amphotericin B (CCDA selective supplement SR0155E)) [44] and kept in a gas jar containing Campylobacter gas pack systems to maintain the microaerophilic condition and was incubated at a temperature of 37 °C for 48 h.

Biochemical tests

Identification of Salmonella species and E. coli was done using different biochemical tests, including catalase, triple sugar iron (TSI) agar, Methyl red (MR), urease, SIM agar (sulphide, indole, and motility), and citrate tests. Colonies that showed red slant with yellow butt and H2S production, Indole negative, methyl red positive, citrate positive, and urease negative were confirmed as Salmonella species, whereas colonies that showed yellow slant and acid butt with no hydrogen sulfide, Indole positive, motile, methyl red positive, citrate negative, and urease negative were confirmed as E. coli [41]. The S. aureus was positive for Gram stain, coagulase, and catalase tests. Moreover, the three species undergo oxidative fermentation. S. aureus bacteria were found to be brown pigment producers, while the other S. aureus did not produce the expected pigment. Campylobacter species. The colonies were provisionally identified based on staining reaction with Gram’s stain, cellular morphology [45], catalase test, and oxidase test [46], and growth appearance on 5% sheep blood agar (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hampshire, England) at 37 °C after 24 h [47–55].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The modified Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion technique was used to perform an antimicrobial susceptibility test. The pure colonies of Salmonella species, E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Campylobacter species were tested in separate Mueller-Hinton agar. With a sterile wire loop, five colonies of similar morphological type were transferred to a tube containing 3–5 ml normal saline solution (NSS). Salmonella species, E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Campylobacter species were tested for susceptibility to the following 16 antibiotics (OXOID, England): amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (30 µg), gentamycin (10 µg), kanamycin (30 µg), nalidixic acid (30 µg), nitrofurantoin (30 µg), streptomycin (10 µg), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and using the disk diffusion method according to guidelines set by the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [56]. Salmonella species, E. coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Campylobacter species separate suspension was prepared and matched with the McFarland standard (0.5) and was seeded using an applicator cotton swab to the Muller-Hinton agar, and the paper impregnated antibiotic disks within 15 min. The petri-dish was incubated at 37 °C for 16–18 h. After 18 h incubation, each plate was examined, and the diameters of the complete inhibition zones were noted and measured using callipers and classified as sensitive, intermediate, and resistant by the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [56].

Antimicrobial susceptibility test was performed for all S. aureus isolates according to the criteria of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [42]. For the susceptibility test, a pure culture of all identified E. coli was taken from BUG media and transferred to a tube containing 5 ml of sterile normal saline and mixed gently to make a homogenous suspension, which was adjusted to a turbidity equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard as measured by a turbidity meter. The bacterial suspension was inoculated onto Muller–Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK) with the sterile swab to cover the whole surface of the agar. The inoculated plates were left at room temperature to dry. The plates were prepared as per the manufacturer’s instructions and checked for sterility before inoculation by incubating the plates overnight at 37 °C. Before using the antimicrobial disks, they were kept at room temperature for one hour and then dispensed on the surface of the media. Following this, the plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. based on the criteria of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [43]. Thus, antimicrobials used in this study were: nalidixic acid (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), vancomycin (30 µg), clindamycin (2 µg), trimethoprim (25 µg), penicillin G (10 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (20/10µg), amikacin (30 µg), cephalothin (30 µg), co-trimoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (25 µg), streptomycin (10 µg), kanamycin (30 µg), Chloroamphenicol (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg) (Oxoid, UK), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), nitrofurantoine (300 µg), norfloxacin (10 µg), and ceftriaxone (30 µg) (Oxoid, UK). The diameters of the zone of inhibition around the disks were measured to the nearest millimeter using calibrated rulers, and the isolates were classified as susceptible, intermediate, and resistant according to the interpretative standards of Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [56–59].

Questionnaire survey

A semi-structured questionnaire was employed to evaluate the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) of participants regarding rational drug handling and usage. The participants included animal health professionals and animal owners who brought their animal to the abattoir during sampling day involved in drug management. The questions were initially prepared in English and then translated into Tigrigna. Knowledge and practice were categorized into good, moderate, and poor based on Modified Bloom’s cut-off points of 80–100%, 60–79%, and below 50%, respectively. Additionally, the mean score was used to classify attitudes as favorable if the score was above the mean and unfavorable if below, according to the Likert scale cut-off points.

Data management and analysis

Data from the questionnaire survey and laboratory investigations were first cleaned, coded, and validated in Microsoft Excel, and then exported to STATA version 12 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the prevalence of bacterial isolates. Logistic regression modeling was applied to identify factors associated with bacterial contamination, with potential confounders controlled during the analysis. Chi-square (χ²) tests were employed to assess associations between categorical variables after verifying that the assumptions of the test were met. Statistical significance was determined using p-values (< 0.05), and inferential analyses were also conducted to evaluate knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) of respondents in relation to antimicrobial use and meat safety.

Discussion

This study assessed bacterial contamination of raw cattle meat from three abattoirs in Tigray, Ethiopia, identifying four major zoonotic pathogens: Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., Staphylococcus aureus, and Campylobacter spp. The overall contamination rate was high (95.45%), with a significant proportion (80.95%) of samples harboring mixed infections. These findings underscore substantial public health risks, particularly in Ethiopia where consumption of raw or undercooked meat is culturally common.

The prevalence of E. coli (38.64%) detected in this study is consistent (40% and 30.97%) with some reports from Ethiopia [29, 60], but notably higher than a finding [27] from the country’s capital city, Addis Ababa and its nearby city Bishoftu (21.6%), which was conducted using a molecular technique. s, this variability likely reflects differences in the level of public awareness, hygiene standards at abattoirs [61], animal health status, and sampling methodologies. Poor sanitation during slaughtering and handling processes is a plausible contributor to such contamination. The presence of E. coli, especially pathogenic strains such as O157:H7, poses significant risks of severe gastrointestinal illnesses.

Staphylococcus aureus was detected in 65.15% of the meat samples. This level of contamination in lines with a study finding Iran [62] but relatively higher than several reports from Ethiopia [3, 4], and Saudi Arab [63]. Since S. aureus is a zoonotic bacterium, the high level of contamination poses a significant public health risk, particularly when such meat is consumed raw or undercooked. The high contamination levels may reflect poor hygiene and sanitation in meat production at the abattoirs, or it could indicate that animals are being slaughtered without proper ante-mortem examinations or pathogen screenings, such as for S. aureus. S. aureus also has shown statistically significant higher prevalence in Maichew and Humera compared to Mekelle. These differences may reflect inconsistencies in abattoir infrastructure, hygiene compliance, and worker practices. S. aureus contamination poses risks of toxin-mediated food poisoning, further amplifying public health concerns.

Salmonella spp. Contamination was prevalent (63.64%), exceeding levels reported from several findings reported from Ethiopia [8, 64–67]. The high prevalence may be attributable to inadequate ante-mortem screening of animals, unhygienic slaughter practices, and cross-contamination within abattoirs. Given Salmonella’s known global burden as a leading foodborne pathogen causing substantial morbidity and mortality, these findings highlight urgent needs to improve meat safety. Of the meat samples were found to be contaminated with Salmonella. This high prevalence indicates that the meat from the three abattoirs is heavily contaminated with Salmonella, which could lead to a significant risk of foodborne illness in humans following consumption. The high level of Salmonella contamination may be due to poor hygienic and sanitary practices in the abattoirs or the slaughter of animals infected with Salmonella without proper screening. However, the relatively higher prevalence (70%) of Salmonella detected in fresh meat from retail shops in Bahir Dar city (Northwest Ethiopia) [24] may be attributed to variations in sample size and lab techniques, hygiene, sanitation practices, type of the abattoirs,

A higher prevalence (43.94%) of Campylobacter species that aligns with global reports [30, 68–70] implicating this pathogen in gastrointestinal infections, particularly in vulnerable population such as children and immunocompromised individuals. The presence of Campylobacter bacteria may indicate poor hygiene and sanitation practices at the abattoirs. As, contamination could occur during manual skinning, evisceration, carcass washing, or if infected animals are slaughtered.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed widespread multidrug resistance among all isolates, excluding Campylobacter spp., align with several study findings for E. coli [28, 71, 72], Salmonella spp [24–26, 66, 74]., and S. aurus [34, 73], with variable level of resistance towards different antimicrobials. High levels of resistance to Vancomycin and Erythromycin were observed, although these antibiotics are not commonly used in veterinary medicine in the study areas, suggesting environmental or human-associated selective pressures. Resistance to veterinary-utilized drugs such as Amoxicillin and Tetracycline was also substantial, raising concerns about therapeutic efficacy. These patterns likely arise from inappropriate drug use, including uncontrolled distribution, lack of standardized treatment protocols, and inadequate professional oversight.

The KAP survey elucidated important differences between animal health professionals and livestock owners. While veterinary professionals demonstrated relatively good knowledge (79.17%) and practices (87.5%) concerning rational drug use, approximately half exhibited unfavorable attitudes, possibly linked to systemic challenges such as limited drug access or training opportunities. Conversely, livestock owners exhibited good knowledge and attitudes but poor practices (56.16%), approximately half exhibited unfavorable attitudes, possibly linked to systemic challenges such as limited drug access or training opportunities.

This integrated assessment reveals the complex interplay among microbial contamination, antimicrobial resistance, and human behavioral factors in sustaining food safety and public health risks, emphasizing the need for a One Health approach that connects veterinary care, food safety, and community education.

Limitations of the study

The study has limitations, including reliance on conventional bacteriological and biochemical methods without molecular confirmation, which may compromise the specificity and sensitivity of pathogen detection; its cross-sectional design and sampling timeframe may not reflect seasonal variations; and potential biases in self-reported knowledge, attitudes, and practices data, such as social desirability bias, may affect findings. Future research should employ molecular diagnostics for precise pathogen and resistance gene identification and conduct longitudinal studies to track trends over time.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study identified four major zoonotic bacterial pathogens, Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus, and Campylobacter spp. in raw red meat collected from municipal abattoirs in Maichew, Mekelle, and Humera, showing a high prevalence of contamination that poses serious public health risks, particularly in communities where raw or undercooked meat is commonly consumed. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed widespread multidrug resistance, with notable resistance to several antibiotics frequently used in veterinary practice, underscoring the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in the region. The findings suggest that these high contamination rates are linked to poor hygiene, sanitation, and biosecurity practices at abattoirs, as well as possible infections in slaughtered animals, while the development of AMR is likely driven by improper and unregulated veterinary drug use, weak enforcement of antimicrobial stewardship, and limited awareness among livestock owners and some veterinary practitioners. Despite limitations such as a relatively small sample size, reliance on conventional bacteriological and biochemical methods without molecular confirmation, and the cross-sectional design that restricts causal inferences and may be influenced by seasonal variations, the study emphasizes the urgent need for interventions. The key recommendations are to improve abattoir hygiene through HACCP-based protocols, strengthen regulation of veterinary antimicrobial use, establish treatment guidelines and diagnostic support for rational drug use, provide ongoing training and awareness for veterinarians and livestock owners, and promote research using molecular methods and long-term studies to track pathogens, resistance genes, and intervention outcomes within a One Health framework in Ethiopia, especially in Tigray.
