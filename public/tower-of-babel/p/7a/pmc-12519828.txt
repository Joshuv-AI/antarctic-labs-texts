Nanotechnology-driven biofortification of Fe, Zn, and Se in edible plants
Authors: Luana Vanessa Peretti Minello, Suelen Goettems Kuntzler, Emílio Berghahn, Liane Terezinha Dorneles, Felipe Klein Ricachenevsky, Raul Antonio Sperotto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIron (Fe) and zinc (Zn) are essential micronutrients for both plants and humans, whereas selenium (Se) is essential for human health and beneficial for plants. Nevertheless, deficiencies in these elements remain a global concern. Nanoparticle (NP)-mediated biofortification of edible plants offers a sustainable and effective strategy to enhance crop nutritional value. This review synthesizes current knowledge on (i) NP types and formulations, highlighting how particle size, composition, and surface properties influence nutrient delivery; (ii) application methods, including soil amendments, foliar spraying, and seed nanopriming, emphasizing their efficiency and limitations; and (iii) mechanisms of NP uptake, translocation, and accumulation in edible tissues, identifying factors that affect bioavailability. Compared with conventional fertilization, NP-based approaches deliver micronutrients more effectively, promote better plant growth, and enhance nutritional content. We also discuss key research gaps, environmental and economic challenges, and regulatory considerations that need to be addressed to ensure safe and scalable implementation. By overcoming these barriers, NP-mediated biofortification can significantly contribute to combating micronutrient deficiencies and improving global food security.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Environmental safety, Fortification, Malnutrition, Micronutrients, Nanofertilization, Sustainability

Introduction

Micronutrients are essential vitamins and minerals required in small amounts for both plants and humans. Despite their low concentrations, they play key roles in many physiological processes and development [1, 2]. Micronutrient deficiencies can lead to malnutrition, also known as hidden hunger, which occurs when people consume diets high in calories but poor in vitamins and minerals [3, 4]. Such micronutrient deficiencies affect approximately one-third of the global population, posing significant challenges to economic development [5, 6]. The problem is most severe in low- and middle-income countries, where poverty forces reliance on cereal-based staples that provide calories but lack essential nutrients due to nutrient-poor soils [2, 4]. Micronutrient deficiencies arise not only from nutrient-poor soils but also from factors such as moisture, temperature, texture, and pH [7]. Deficient soils reduce plant growth and seed quality [1] and increase susceptibility to abiotic and biotic stresses [8, 9]. Addressing these deficiencies in staple crops is a global challenge, as improving crop nutrition is essential for food security and aligns with the United Nations sustainable development goal 2, which aims to eradicate hunger, improve nutrition, and foster sustainable agriculture [3].

Over the past few decades, efforts to increase food production for a growing global population have mainly focused on maximizing crop yields. Although crop productivity has increased, nutrient density has declined [4, 10]. Several studies now provide strong quantitative evidence of an inverse relationship between crop yield and nutrient density, known as the dilution effect. Landmark rice and wheat cultivars have shown significant reductions in micronutrient concentrations alongside yield gains [11]. Similar patterns have been observed under elevated atmospheric CO2, where mineral concentrations in staple crops decline by ~ 8% on average [12]. Furthermore, high-yielding cultivars often accumulate more carbohydrates at the expense of proteins and other nutrients, exacerbating the problem of hidden hunger [13, 14]. This burden is not evenly distributed. According to the World Health Organization, more than 2 billion people suffer from micronutrient deficiencies, especially women of reproductive age, young children, and populations in low- and middle-income countries [15, 16]. Iron (Fe) deficiency anemia affects nearly 30% of women of childbearing age globally, while zinc (Zn) deficiency is most common in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [17]. Selenium (Se) intake also varies widely, with dietary insufficiency reported in several parts of Europe and Asia [18]. These disparities show that nutritional vulnerability is strongly influenced by age, sex, and geography, reinforcing the need for targeted solutions to address hidden hunger.

Intervention approaches to improve micronutrient intake include supplementation, biofortification, and dietary diversification, with outcomes depending on local conditions and available resources [3, 19]. Biofortification offers a sustainable, cost-effective strategy by increasing vitamins and minerals in staple crops, especially in communities that depend on them [4, 10]. It can be achieved through agronomic practices, conventional breeding, and transgenic approaches [2, 4]. Among these, agronomic biofortification stands out as the most straightforward, using soil or foliar fertilization and seed priming to enhance nutrient solubilization, mobilization, and uptake, thereby improving the nutritional value of crops for better human health [1, 4, 7, 10, 20–22].

Nanoparticles (NPs) have been increasingly explored for crop biofortification, taking advantage of nanoscale properties such as small particle size (< 100 nm), large surface area, and unique structures [2, 23, 24]. A wide range of nanomaterials have been applied in agriculture, including carbon nanotubes, metal NPs (e.g., Al, Cu, Ag, Au), and metal oxide NPs (e.g., Fe3O4, ZnO, Ce2O3, TiO2) [1, 4, 20, 23, 25]. Nanofertilizers (NFs) offer target specificity, slow-release kinetics, and long persistence in soil, which reduce nutrient losses through leaching or runoff, lower application frequency, and mitigate environmental impacts [20, 26]. These characteristics improve nutrient solubilization, uptake, and targeted delivery to edible tissues, making NFs more effective than conventional or chelated fertilizers.

Although several reviews have examined the role of NPs in agriculture, most have focused on general applications, environmental impacts, or toxicity, with limited attention to NP-mediated biofortification [20, 27]. In contrast, this review provides a targeted synthesis across the three nutritionally most relevant micronutrients (Fe, Zn, and Se). We analyze how NP formulations, shape, and application methods influence uptake, translocation, and accumulation of micronutrients in plants. We also summarize recent advances in enhancing Fe, Zn, and Se levels in edible crops. Beyond agronomic outcomes, we integrate evidence on dietary contributions relative to reference daily intake (RDI), discuss safety thresholds and ecological considerations, and evaluate the scalability of NP-based strategies. By combining these aspects, our review offers a cross-comparative perspective that highlights both the opportunities and limitations of nanobiofortification to address global Fe, Zn, and Se deficiencies.

Different NP formulations and shapes

NFs can be categorized into three main groups: (i) nanoscale nutrients, in which macro- or micronutrients are reduced to the nanoscale forming NPs; (ii) nano additives, which combine conventional fertilizers with NPs to improve nutrient efficiency; and (iii) nano coatings, which encapsulate fertilizers within NPs to control nutrient release [28, 29]. These NPs have unique physicochemical properties that influence the reactivity, mobility, and availability of nutrients in agricultural systems. They can be synthesized through chemical, physical, or biological methods, depending on the desired properties, cost, and scalability [30]. NPs for biofortification are formulated from a variety of organic and inorganic materials, including emulsions, metals, metal oxides, dendrimers, polymers, liposomes, and quantum dots. Each formulation offers specific advantages for enhancing nutrient uptake and reducing environmental impacts [31, 32].

Several studies have explored the potential of NPs in promoting plant growth and protection [33–35]. This review focuses on the key features and differences among polymer-, metal-, and metal oxide-based NPs, with emphasis on their applications in Fe, Zn, and Se crop biofortification. Inorganic NPs, such as those made from metals and metal oxides, typically from 1 to 40 nm in size, significantly influence absorption, translocation, and accumulation in plant tissues. For example, metal-based NPs smaller than 50 nm readily enter leaves through stomata, while uptake decreases with larger particles [36]. NP characteristics such as size, charge, and composition govern absorption and transport in both roots and leaves [37]. Consistently, ZnO-NPs of 10–30 nm achieved higher translocation factors and penetrated leaves more effectively than larger particles, supporting the preference for smaller NP formulations [38].

NPs can have various shapes, including spherical, rod-shaped, irregular, and sheet-like structures [39]. Metallic NPs containing essential micronutrients (e.g., Ag, Cu, Fe, and Zn) are chemically stable, making them effective under diverse environmental conditions. Their surfaces have charged atoms, facilitating Coulombic interactions and quantum confinement effects [40]. Metallic NPs can also be functionalized with other elements or surface-modified to change their texture (from smooth to rough) or to introduce specific properties such as fluorescence and magnetism. Such modifications enhance key properties, including photocatalytic activity, solubility, and dispersion [41]. Metal oxide NPs also have surface charges, with metal ions (positive) and oxygen ions (negative) forming strong ionic bonds through electrostatic attraction [42]. Compared to metallic NPs, metal oxide NPs often show higher reactivity and greater nutrient-use efficiency [41]. An additional benefit of metallic NPs is their antimicrobial activity, helping suppress plant pathogens and thereby contributing to improved plant health [43].

Polymeric NPs, particularly those derived from natural polymers such as chitosan, cellulose, sodium alginate, starch, and lignin, are promising to stimulate plant growth and improve nutrient assimilation [26]. Their structures vary with the synthesis method, forming capsules, micelles, liposomes, or more complex systems such as porous and core-shell architectures. These NPs can encapsulate bioactive compounds, protecting them from stress and degradation, significantly improving fertilizer efficiency [44]. Derived from renewable sources, polymeric NPs are biodegradable into CO2, H2O, and inorganic compounds [45], aligning with green chemistry principles and making them a sustainable option for agriculture. Polymeric NPs can also coat macronutrients such as nitrogen (N), potassium (K), and phosphorus (P), reducing fertilizer loss, improving soil water retention, and increasing nutrient use efficiency [26]. Surface functionalization with ligands further increases affinity for plant tissues, optimizing nutrient delivery efficiency and biofortification outcomes. Additional agronomic benefits include protective films from cellulose [46] and the biocompatibility, low cost, and uniform surface of lignin-based NPs [47]. Altogether, polymeric NPs combine high bioavailability, efficient nutrient delivery, and environmental safety, supporting their potential for sustainable crop biofortification [44].

Foliar spraying

Leaves are vital plant organs that regulate gas exchange and help maintain metabolic homeostasis. They also serve as protective barriers against foreign molecules, pathogens, and pests [49]. Various agricultural inputs can be applied via foliar spraying, but their efficiency depends on their interaction with leaf surface structures, which differ among plant species [50]. Conventional foliar formulations often rely on metallic inorganic salts and chelated metal ions. These compounds typically have a low affinity for leaf surfaces due to their large size and charge, resulting in poor adhesion and reduced cuticular absorption [51, 52]. As a result, much of the applied solution is easily leached by rain, contributing to soil contamination, environmental pollution, and increased costs due to repeated applications. By controlling particle size, surface chemistry, and reactivity, NFs can deliver nutrients and minerals with high precision and efficiency [53]. They can achieve absorption rates of up to ~ 90% [50], which enhances nutrient uptake, crop yields, and biofortification efficiency while reducing input costs and improving plant health.

The sorption and mobility of NPs in plants depend on several factors, including particle shape, size, affinity, surface charge, concentration, exposure time, application method, plant species, and zeta potential [54]. Zeta potential, in particular, influences NP passage across lipid layers and cell walls, enabling transport through membranes into organelles [55]. After foliar spraying (Fig. 1a), NPs can enter the mesophyll cells via hydrophilic (stomata, necrotic areas, hydathodes, and trichomes) or lipophilic routes (diffusion through cuticle cracks and pores) [52, 56], reflecting both polar and apolar absorption mechanisms. Effective absorption of nanoencapsulated atrazine (NC + ATZ) formulated with poly(ε-caprolactone) has been demonstrated through the hydathodes of Brassica juncea leaves [57]. The hydrophilic interaction between the nanocarrier and hydathodes enhances absorption. Once inside the leaf, nanoatrazine penetrated the mesophyll and was transported through the vascular system to the chloroplasts, where it triggered herbicidal activity. This highlights how hydrophilic NPs can target delivery through foliar pathways. Mechanistic studies show that micronutrient NPs enter leaves through pathways similar to those of other foliar-applied compounds. In wheat, ZnO NPs penetrate the epidermis via stomata, move through the apoplast, and reach mesophyll cells [58]. In maize, Fe3O4, CuO, and ZnO NPs are absorbed and translocated, with element-specific patterns of distribution [59]. In addition, plant-microbe interactions also influence ZnO-NP foliar uptake, which decreased when stomatal function is impaired [60].

NP size strongly influences foliar uptake: particles < 4.8 nm can penetrate the waxy cuticle, while larger ones (>5 nm) may require physiological changes such as altered lipid layer fluidity caused by climate change or pest attacks [61]. Foliar wettability and stomatal absorption can be improved by adding biobased surfactants to NP formulations [62], which lower surface tension. Metals and metal oxides also adhere more strongly than ionic forms due to their surface charges and size [63]. NP properties can be tailored during synthesis, with functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl, amine, and carboxyl) enhancing adhesion through covalent, hydrogen, and electrostatic bonding [64]. For example, phenolic compounds on green (organic) NPs promote hydrogen bonding between their -OH groups and lipids in the cuticle [65]. Coating NPs with biomolecules such as carbohydrates, amino acids, and peptides also promotes targeted delivery to plant organs [66]. These interactions ultimately influence NP dissolution, transport, and biofortification efficiency.

A meta-analysis has shown that spraying plants with Fe oxide NPs (50 ppm FeO4-NPs) significantly improved growth, highlighting their potential in nanofertilization [67]. While lipid-based and liposomal NPs demonstrated high absorption efficiency, current methods remain limited in accurately distinguishing NP adsorption and absorption in leaves [68]. This represents an important gap in advancing nanostructured agroinput development. Another underexplored factor is the phyllosphere, which is the community of microorganisms inhabiting the leaf surface. These microbes can secrete signaling molecules or other types of exudates that either promote NP dissolution and facilitate the absorption of metal ions or form protective biofilms that block external molecules from entering the leaf [56]. For example, certain bacterial strains in the phyllosphere produce organic acids and siderophores that chelate metals, accelerating the dissolution of metal-oxide NPs into plant-available ionic forms [69]. Conversely, microbial biofilms rich in extracellular polymeric substances may immobilize or physically trap NPs on the leaf surface, limiting their penetration into plant tissues [70]. In addition, enzymatic activity at the leaf-microbe interface may also modify NP surface chemistry, influencing their aggregation, charge, or reactivity [71]. However, little is known about how these interactions affect NP delivery and, consequently, the efficiency of biofortification, highlighting the need for further studies to clarify the phyllosphere-NP-plant interface.

Root absorption

NPs are primarily absorbed by roots through two root pathways (Fig. 1c): the apoplast, where water and solutes move freely along cell walls; and the symplast, where molecules pass from cell to cell via plasmodesmata [56]. Root cap cells and mucilage can adsorb and accumulate NPs regardless of charge [72], but uptake is selective: negatively charged NPs are more easily absorbed and translocated via the apoplast, while positively charged NPs stimulate mucilage production and become trapped within it.

Root NP absorption is influenced by root morphology, surface properties, pore size, root exudates, rhizosphere interactions, soil type and physicochemical properties, plant developmental stage, and species [61]. The fibrous and fasciculated root systems of monocots may offer greater resistance to NP absorption through the epidermis and apoplast. In contrast, eudicots typically have taproots that often exhibit higher NP absorption partly due to their higher cation-binding capacity [73]. Although the root epidermis protects the plant against external agents, it also serves as an entry point for small NPs applied to soil or hydroponic systems [56]. However, the direct absorption mechanisms of NPs through the root epidermis remain poorly understood. Anatomical barriers such as the Casparian strip and endodermis limit NP vascular transport, though small fissures or malformations at lateral root junctions can bypass these barriers and allow apoplastic entry [61].

The absorption route of ZnO-NPs in corn roots was elucidated through the combination of microscopy and spectrometry [74]. In hydroponic culture containing 2–100 mg L−1 ZnO-NPs, root metabolism dissolved the particles, releasing Zn²⁺ ions that were absorbed mainly as Zn phosphate, limiting long-distance transport. A small fraction of intact ZnO-NPs was detected in the root cortex, likely entering through fissures at lateral root junctions. In contrast, carbon-based NPs may enter roots directly via endocytosis, although this pathway remains poorly characterized [36].

Root exudates, especially those released from plant-microorganism interactions, can modify NP surface charge and form nano-crowns that influence absorption, bioavailability, and vascular transport [75]. Their composition varies with plant developmental stage, releasing compounds that alter NP size, charge, and chemical speciation. These changes are particularly relevant for metal-based NPs, affecting uptake, mobility, and overall biofortification effectiveness [67].

Soil type and physicochemical properties also determine NP bioavailability to roots. Clay-rich soils tend to retain NPs by forming aggregates, which limit their mobility and reduce absorption [76]. Soil texture, organic matter content, pH, and cation exchange capacity directly affect NP surface charge behavior and therefore must be considered when selecting and applying nano-inputs [75]. In addition to these abiotic factors, soil microorganisms play a central role in modifying NP availability. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi enhance NP dissolution and mobilization by increasing the root surface area and releasing organic acids, which improve micronutrient acquisition [77]. Similarly, rhizobia and other root-associated bacteria produce siderophores and extracellular enzymes that modify NP surface chemistry, accelerating metal ion release and altering speciation [78]. Depending on the microbial community composition and soil conditions, these microbial processes can either increase or decrease NP bioavailability, but their role in biofortification remains largely unexplored. Despite the importance of the plant-NP-soil interaction, few studies have investigated its physiological effects under field conditions. Most available data come from controlled environments using hydroponic systems or soil amended for experimental consistency. This indicates a research gap and the need for more studies to evaluate NP performance and behavior in diverse soil types in natural environments.

Root-based NP application is effective but generally less efficient than foliar application. Foliar absorption is faster through the stomata and the cuticle, whereas root absorption is slower due to the root epidermis [79]. Additionally, nano-inputs tend to have higher bioavailability in leaves, as rhizosphere interactions and soil conditions can limit NP absorption [80]. Comparative studies confirm this difference: in tomato, foliar Fe-NPs improved fruit yield and nutrient content more effectively than soil applications under Cd stress conditions [81]; and in chickpea, foliar ZnO- and Fe2O3-NPs produced similar or higher grain Zn and Fe concentrations than equivalent soil treatments [82]. These findings help explain why most biofortification studies favor foliar application as a more efficient and reliable route.

Seed absorption (nanopriming)

Nanopriming is a pre-sowing treatment in which seeds are soaked in NP solutions, dried, and then planted (Fig. 1b) [83]. Compared to conventional germination methods, nanopriming improves seed germination, early growth, water uptake, carbon assimilation, yield, and product quality [61]. Additionally, it can increase plant resilience to metabolic stress caused by climate change. This lasting protective effect, known as “priming memory” or “protective memory”, arises from latent defense mechanisms that remain in standby after drying and can be reactivated under stress [84–86]. Despite its potential, the physiological and molecular basis of nanopriming, especially its epigenetic and transgenerational effects, remains poorly understood.

Nanopriming accelerates the release of seed dormancy by regulating antioxidant capacity, enzymatic activity, and hormonal balance [87]. NPs interact with key phytohormones during germination, particularly the abscisic acid (ABA) and gibberellins (GA) balance, which is further influenced by seed coat phenolics that promote germination and nutrient mobilization to the embryo [88]. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), produced by the endosperm and transported to the seed coat, promotes GA synthesis to break dormancy [89] and also enhances NP adhesion, absorption, and transport within seeds [90]. Organic NPs are quickly recognized by seed tissues, triggering the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that signal GA biosynthesis and α-amylase activity, mobilizing stored reserves and initiating germination-related signaling cascades [91].

Seed priming with Fe3O4-NPs has also been shown to promote NP adhesion to seed surfaces and subsequent uptake. The improved physiological performance in wheat and increased leaf Fe content suggest effective Fe3O4-NPs uptake during germination [92]. Nanopriming with silver NPs enhanced the viability of aged rice seeds and subsequent seedling development [93]. Similar benefits were observed in onions (Allium cepa L.) [94] and maize [95], where ZnO-NP penetrated the seed coat and enhanced water absorption.

Nanopriming can activate aquaporin genes, often in response to ROS and phosphorylation of aquaporin proteins [85]. In rapeseed (Brassica napus L.), Se-nanoprimed seeds absorbed water more rapidly and upregulated BnPIP1-1 and BnPIP2-2 aquaporin genes [96]. Changes in water potential and free radical accumulation in cotyledons indicated that NPs crossed the seed coat and triggered metabolic activity. Once inside, NPs can move systemically by diffusion, bulk flow, and translocation through phloem, plasmodesmata, and aquaporins, mechanisms that will be discussed in detail later. Nanopriming enhances water uptake, promotes uniform germination, and improves seedling establishment and plant resilience, but it also carries risks.

High NP concentrations or unsuitable NP types have been linked to reduced germination rates, chromosomal abnormalities, and abnormal seedling morphology [85, 97]. Effective concentrations are generally in the low mg L⁻¹ range, although thresholds vary with NP type and plant species. For example, AgNPs at 10–20 mg L⁻¹ enhanced rice germination and seedling vigor, but >40 mg L⁻¹ delayed germination and caused abnormal root development [97]. Similarly, ZnO-NPs at 25–50 mg L⁻¹ improved maize germination and water uptake, while concentrations >100 mg L⁻¹ induced oxidative stress and reduced growth [85]. Overall, most studies indicate that concentrations below 50 mg L⁻¹ stimulate germination, while higher doses may induce toxicity, highlighting the need to optimize NP size, type, and concentration for safe and effective biofortification.

NP translocation and accumulation in plants

Translocation of NPs within plants

After foliar absorption through the cuticle and stomata (Fig. 1c) or uptake by roots (Fig. 1d), NPs are translocated through mesophyll cells via the symplastic or apoplastic pathways until they reach the vascular system [52]. Long-distance translocation of NPs occurs in two distinct ways: (1) bottom-up (from roots to shoots) via the xylem; and (2) top-down (from leaves to roots) via the phloem (Fig. 1e) [56]. The direction and efficiency of translocation depend on NP size, surface properties, application method, and plant physiology [36]. The plant vascular system is non-circulatory, meaning that molecules do not cycle continuously [56]. Thus, NPs applied via foliar spraying are primarily translocated through the phloem, while root-applied NPs move upward through the xylem. Reverse movement across vascular routes is generally limited, although some exceptions involving redistribution have been observed. NP mobility within plant tissues is influenced by Van der Waals forces, Brownian motion (diffusion), gravity, and double-layer forces [98]. In a hydroponic system, CeO2-NPs (200-2,000 mg L⁻¹) were absorbed by cucumber roots, biotransformed into CeO₂, transported to the shoots via the xylem, and remobilized to roots through the phloem, where they accumulated. These processes were confirmed using advanced microscopy and spectrometry [99].

NP transport via the phloem is also influenced by the solution pH and the presence of other ions or compounds [36]. One critical factor is the electrical charge of the phloem, which affects systemic NP translocation following foliar application [100]. The transport of polymeric nanocarriers (NC) with different surface charges was compared in a hydroponic system using a dicot (tomato) and a monocot (wheat) [101]. Anionic NCs were more efficiently transported in tomatoes (21%) compared to wheat (8%). Cationic NCs were only transported in tomatoes (13%) and not detected in wheat. These differences may result from the retention of positively charged NCs in the leaf epidermis, which limits apoplastic transport and reduces their ability to enter the phloem [102]. For NPs to reach the phloem, they must first cross the cell wall and be internalized into mesophyll cells [66]. Carbon-dot-based NCs coated with guiding peptides, sucrose, or amino acids can enhance the transport of chemicals by facilitating bio-recognition, improving delivery to chloroplasts and phloem tissues [55]. This strategy also improves the efficiency of transporting minerals such as N, P, and K to target plant tissues and holds promise for making biofortification more precise and effective [52].

The lipid exchange envelope penetration model [103] explains NP absorption and translocation, identifying particle size and zeta potential as critical parameters determining their efficiency. Experimental results in tomato plants support this model [100]. The authors found that NPs with higher surface charge (zeta potential < −10 and > +10 mV) have greater phloem mobility, whereas NPs with a neutral charge (zeta potential between −10 and +10 mV) move more slowly through plant tissues. NPs with negative zeta potential were efficiently translocated across plant organs, while positively charged NPs tended to accumulate at the application site, limiting systemic movement [104]. This behavior was further demonstrated in hydroponic experiments where CeO-NPs (~ 4 nm) were applied to various crops [105]. In tomatoes and lettuce, neutrally or negatively charged NPs were translocated to shoots, while positively charged NPs accumulated in roots. In contrast, corn and rice showed reduced NP mobility, suggesting species-specific differences. These results reinforce the idea that positively charged NPs may adhere to root mucilage, restricting their transport and potentially limiting their effectiveness in biofortification strategies.

Accumulation of NPs in different plant tissues

NP accumulation and metabolic transformation in plants remain elusive mainly due to methodological limitations. The most widely accepted hypothesis is that NPs undergo biotransformation and accumulate in organelles such as vacuoles, plastids, the nucleus, the Golgi apparatus, and the endoplasmic reticulum [106]. Microscopy studies in maize, cotton, and rice confirm their presence in these compartments [107–109]. However, these findings are mostly qualitative, indicating the need for quantitative analyses on NP distribution, concentration, and chemical state. NP internalization into vacuoles or the cytosol can occur via passive diffusion (especially when membrane integrity is compromised) or active transport, involving membrane proteins such as aquaporins, as well as endocytosis clathrin-mediated or not [110].

ZnO-NPs of varying sizes (10–380 nm) accumulated in seeds and roots of several plant species, including maize, wheat, and soybeans [98]. In maize, the main sites of NP deposition were the epidermis, cortex, root tip cells, and vascular tissues [74]. Similar patterns occur with Ag-NPs: in peanuts grown in sandy soil, doses of 50−2,000 mg kg−1 were absorbed by roots, translocated to stems and leaves, and subsequently remobilized to grains, following a xylem-phloem-sink organ transport model [73]. Accumulation was dose-dependent and negatively affected productivity. Low Ag-NPs doses (0.5–100 ng mL−1) in onion seeds also caused Ag ion accumulation, leading to genotoxicity, oxidative stress, and morphoanatomical changes in seedlings [73]. CuO-NPs applied foliarly (100-1,000 mg L−1) accumulated in lettuce leaves, were transported to roots, and remobilized to sink organs such as young leaves as Cu ions [111]. Although the exact sites of NP biotransformation were not identified, dissolution likely occurred shortly after application in roots or leaves, where chemical catalysis and metabolic activity enhance NP mobility [112].

Following NP absorption and transport via xylem or phloem, plants may generate ROS [75]. Besides acting as signaling molecules, ROS also interact with surface charges and ions on NPs, catalyzing reactions that lead to biotransformation. This cascade induces the expression of genes involved in defense responses and phytohormone synthesis, ultimately affecting hormonal homeostasis. NPs may also improve nutrient and water absorption in plants, influencing primary metabolism and synthesis of carbohydrates, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, and minerals [67]. In parallel, they may enhance both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems. However, metallic or metal oxide NPs (including Fe-NPs and ZnO-NPs) can accumulate in edible tissues (e.g., fruits, leaves, and seeds) and exert toxic effects, interfering with plant growth and reducing productivity [113]. In contrast, organic NPs such as carbon dots are more easily metabolized and biotransformed through natural physiological pathways [114]. These reactions contribute to carbon fixation, photoassimilate production, and nutritional enhancement in edible parts, supporting biofortification efforts without causing phytotoxicity or environmental risk [115]. In peppers and eggplants treated with graphene NPs (0.1–0.3 g L⁻¹), increased secondary metabolite synthesis and productivity were observed [116]. Foliar spraying did not induce cytotoxicity but instead promoted more efficient metabolic regulation in both species.

Despite some contradictory findings in the literature, the application of CuO and ZnO-NPs has also shown positive effects largely due to their ability to undergo biotransformation [117]. These NPs are generally unstable at low pH, dissolving shortly after absorption. Once inside plant tissues, they are often converted into other ionic forms and subsequently remobilized to sink organs like roots, flowers, fruits, and seeds [118]. The fate of these particles varies depending on the target organ. NPs may aggregate, dissolve, or become coated with proteins forming nano-coronas, which further influence their mobility, bioavailability, and physiological effects [52].

An interesting aspect of nanomaterial use in agriculture is their ability to promote the accumulation of other minerals of interest (biofortification) in the edible parts of cultivated plants, without necessarily increasing the content of the element used in the NP itself [119]. For instance, application of ZnO-NPs (25 mg kg−1) in Medicago polymorpha L. significantly increased the levels of macro- (P, K, Mg, Ca) and micronutrients (Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, Mn) in tissues [120]. Similarly, CuO-NPs (20 mg) improved the levels of K, Na, S, Ag, Cd, Mg, Fe, Ni, Cu, and Zn in lettuce leaves, with foliar and soil application routes showing distinct effects [121]. Although some treatments increased the accumulation of elements that may be toxic at high levels, the measured concentrations remained within safe intake limits. In another case, cucumbers grown in soil treated with CuO-NPs showed increased levels of K, P, Mn, and Zn [122].

The above-mentioned examples show that the transport of NPs within plants is strongly influenced by plant species, application methods, and nanomaterial properties (e.g., size, surface charge, and morphology) [79]. The processes of NP absorption, translocation, and accumulation are complex and shaped by multiple factors. A better understanding of these mechanisms is essential to optimize the use of nanoinputs in agriculture, maximizing their benefits while minimizing potential risks associated with bioaccumulation, particularly of metallic particles. Importantly, the ability of nanoinputs to biofortify edible plant parts offers a promising strategy to address both climate resilience and the global challenge of hidden hunger (Fig. 1f). With a clear understanding of the key advantages of NPs, optimal application methods, and the mechanisms driving their translocation and accumulation in plants, we now turn to the strategies employing this technology for the biofortification of crops with Fe, Zn, and Se.

Comparative phytoavailability of bulk and nano-form fertilizers

The phytoavailability of nutrients strongly depends on the physical and chemical properties of fertilizers. Conventional bulk fertilizers often show low solubility, rapid fixation in soils, and low uptake efficiency, limiting their effectiveness in biofortification programs [78, 123]. In contrast, NPs enhance nutrient solubility and mobility in the rhizosphere, enabling closer interaction with root surfaces and more efficient transport through apoplastic and symplastic pathways than bulk fertilizers [54].

Several studies have demonstrated that NP-based formulations improve nutrient uptake compared to bulk forms. Fe2O3 and ZnO NPs provide greater bioavailability and accumulation of Fe and Zn in edible tissues than bulk oxide forms, due to their enhanced dissolution and reduced precipitation in soils [124, 125]. Similarly, Se NPs display higher uptake and translocation than bulk Se compounds such as selenite or selenate, resulting in more effective biofortification [126, 127]. Collectively, these findings suggest that NFs offer clear advantages over bulk fertilizers in nutrient phytoavailability, reinforcing their potential for sustainable biofortification.

An important mechanistic question is whether the observed benefits of NPs are truly nano-specific or mainly due to ionic species released during dissolution. Dissolution kinetics, which vary with particle size, coating, and composition, directly influence nutrient solubility, uptake, and accumulation in edible tissues. Recent studies emphasize the need to compare NP formulations with equivalent ionic species to distinguish ion-driven from NP-specific effects [128, 129].

Fe biofortification strategies using NPs

Fe deficiency is one of the most prevalent nutritional deficiencies worldwide, affecting billions of people, particularly in developing countries. It is a significant public health issue and the leading cause of Fe deficiency anemia, a condition characterized by fatigue, weakness, and impaired cognitive function. In animals, Fe deficiency leads to poor growth, a weakened immune system, and increased susceptibility to infections [130]. Fe plays a central role in metabolic processes, including oxygen transport, energy production, and DNA synthesis, as it is a key component of hemoglobin and myoglobin [131]. The recommended dietary allowance for Fe varies by age and gender but is approximately 8 mg day−1 for men and 18 mg day−1 for women [132]. Rich dietary sources of Fe include red meat, poultry, seafood, beans, and fortified cereals [133].

Fe availability in soil is influenced by pH, organic matter content, moisture, and oxygen levels. While Fe is abundant in the earth’s crust, its low solubility in alkaline soils often limits its bioavailability to plants [134]. Consequently, Fe deficiency can lead to chlorosis, reduced photosynthesis, and ultimately lower crop yield and quality [135]. Fe is essential for plant metabolism, contributing to chlorophyll synthesis, electron transport during photosynthesis, and N fixation for legumes [136]. Ensuring adequate soil Fe availability is therefore critical for crop productivity and nutritional quality.

Various biofortification methods have been explored to increase Fe content in crops, including soil application of Fe fertilizers, foliar spraying, and genetic engineering [137]. Among these, Fe-NPs have emerged as a promising approach, combining high reactivity with efficient enhancement of plant Fe uptake [138]. However, several challenges remain. Soil properties and environmental factors can affect the bioavailability of Fe-NPs, and excessive application may pose toxicity risks to plants and ecosystems. Additionally, concerns about NP safety and limited public acceptance of genetically modified crops hinder the widespread adoption of these technologies [139]. Future research should aim to optimize Fe biofortification strategies, balancing efficacy with environmental safety and public acceptance to ensure sustainable improvements in crop nutrition.

Table 1 summarizes NP-based strategies for Fe biofortification in edible plants. Based on the relative increase in Fe concentration compared to control plants, we highlight four strategies: (1) soil application of citrate-coated CoFe2O4-NPs (68 mg kg−1) raised Fe in wheat seeds by 150% [140]; (2) seed priming with Fe3O4-NPs (20 mg L−1) increased wheat seed Fe by 106% [141]; (3) seed priming with Fe3O4-NPs (150 mg L−1) raised rice seed Fe by 156% [142]; and (4) foliar spraying of nano-chelated Fe fertilizer (2.5 g L−1) increased rice seed Fe by 145% [143]. These results indicate that the efficiency of Fe biofortification does not depend solely on the application method or NP type. All three methods (soil application, seed priming, and foliar spraying) were effective, as were different NP formulations. Current evidence suggests that Fe biofortification with NPs may be more effective in cereals than in other crops: rice and wheat often show increases above 100%, whereas vegetables such as cucumber, spinach, and tomato rarely exceed 75% (Table 1; Fig. 2). However, this pattern is based on a few studies, and more comparisons across crop types are needed to confirm it. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that plant species strongly influence biofortification outcomes.

Side-by-side testing is essential to determine the best combination of NP type, dosage, application method, plant cultivar, and growth conditions for effective Fe biofortification [144]. Equally important is the need for studies to systematically compare the efficiency of metal-based NPs with conventional Fe fertilizers, such as salt-based or chelated forms. However, many existing studies rely solely on untreated control plants (without mineral supplementation) as the reference group. This practice limits the ability to attribute observed improvements specifically to the nanoscale formulation. Without proper comparisons, it remains unclear whether the benefits result from the presence of the metal itself or its nanostructured form.

For each study, we calculated the percentage of the Fe RDI [132] provided by consuming 100 g of the biofortified product. Among the 12 strategies reviewed (Table 1), none achieved 100% of the highest Fe RDI (18 mg for women). Two strategies in wheat [140] and rice seeds [145] reached 83% and 97%, respectively, of the women’s RDI, while one using spinach exceeded the lower RDI for men (8 mg), reaching 106% [146]. However, most strategies delivered less than 50% of the men’s RDI [141, 143, 147–150], indicating limited nutritional impact (Table 1). These findings highlight the importance of selecting plant species or cultivars with naturally high Fe concentrations, preferentially popular, high-yielding varieties, to maximize the effectiveness of NP-based Fe biofortification [151, 152]. Figure 2 provides an overview of the main NP-based strategies used to biofortify Fe in edible plants.

While NP-based approaches can substantially increase Fe concentrations in edible plant parts, it is equally important to verify that this Fe remains bioavailable after processing and digestion. Human feeding trials with FePO4-NPs have shown relative Fe bioavailability of up to 72% compared to conventional FeSO4, even when delivered with meals [153]. In vitro digestion and Caco-2 cell models demonstrate that processing and the food matrix significantly affect Fe bioaccessibility, with some vegetables retaining high Fe uptake efficiencies post-digestion [154]. These findings highlight the need to confirm that Fe accumulated via NP-based biofortification remains nutritionally available under real dietary conditions and emphasize the importance of targeted bioavailability studies.

Zn biofortification strategies using NPs

Zinc (Zn) deficiency is common, especially in developing countries, and affects vulnerable groups such as children, the elderly, lactating women, and individuals with chronic diseases [23]. Symptoms include hypogonadism, anorexia, persistent diarrhea, infertility, stunted growth, skin lesions, alopecia, and impaired immunity [155]. Emerging evidence suggests that Zn supplementation may reduce COVID-19 mortality [156]. As an essential trace element, Zn serves as a cofactor or structural component for enzymes across all six main classes (oxidoreductases, hydrolases, transferases, isomerases, lyases, and ligases) [157]. The RDI for Zn is approximately 11 mg day−1 for men and 8 mg day−1 for women [158], with major dietary sources including red meat, fish, oysters, legumes, dairy products, and whole grains (cereals and legumes) [159]. In animals, Zn deficiency also impairs growth, reproduction, and productivity, leading to substantial economic losses in livestock [23].

Zn is also essential for plant metabolism, and its low availability reduces crop productivity and nutritional quality, particularly in cereal grains [160]. About 50% of cereal-growing soils, especially calcareous soils, have low Zn availability [161]. Zn plays a fundamental role in photosynthesis, respiration, protein synthesis, hormone regulation, chlorophyll and carbohydrate synthesis, water absorption, and tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses [162–164]. Widespread Zn depletion is further driven by leaching and yield-focused agricultural practices. This highlights the urgent need for innovative strategies to enhance food nutraceutical value, reduce inputs, and improve nutrient delivery [23]. Agronomic biofortification using nanomaterials offers a promising solution to increase Zn in edible crops, improving human and animal nutrition and helping combat hidden hunger.

A comprehensive understanding of micronutrient-plant interactions is essential for successful biofortification [165]. In wheat, Zn from ZnSO4 dissolves in soil, is absorbed as Zn2+ by the root epidermis, and translocated via the xylem to leaves through selective active transport [161]. However, uptake is often limited by low soil Zn, moisture, pH, and competing elements such as P, carbonates, and Fe oxides. Although ZnSO4 is the most commonly used Zn fertilizer, its bioavailability is restricted by soil-induced biotransformation [166]. Moreover, repeated fertilizer applications increase production costs, promote salt accumulation, reduce soil fertility, and cause nutrient leaching [139]. In contrast, NP-based fertilizers provide high sorption capacity, controlled release, and efficient tissue penetration [167], enabling more targeted delivery and potentially improving Zn bioavailability and uptake in plants.

Table 2 summarizes NP-based strategies for Zn biofortification in edible plants. Research on Zn far exceeds that on Fe and Se, reflecting the global prevalence of Zn deficiency that affects approximately 20% of the population [168] and ranking as the fifth leading health risk in developing countries and eleventh worldwide [169]. Of 48 studies reviewed, 17 (35%) reported at least 100% increase in Zn concentration compared to control plants. The following approaches stand out: (1) foliar ZnO-NPs (100 ppm) raised Zn in tomato fruits by 43,080% [170]; (2) foliar ZnO-NPs (100 mg L⁻¹) increased Zn in grapevine berries by 400% [171]; (3) wheat seed coating with ZnO nanofiber (3.0%) + 1% urea raised Zn by 433% [172]; (4) foliar Zn-doped amorphous calcium phosphate NPs (100 ppm) increased Zn in tomato fruits by 352% [173]; (5) soil-applied ZnO-NPs (1,000 mg kg−1) raised wheat seeds by 330% [174]; (6) soil-applied ZnO-NPs (225 mg kg⁻¹) increased Zn in beetroots by 227% and green peas by 150% [175]; (7) soil-applied ZnO-NPs (800 mg kg−1) raised Zn in potato tubers by 149% [176]; and (8) foliar spinel nanocomposite (nMnZnFe2O4 − 50 mg L−1) increased Zn in rice seeds by 140% [145]. Interestingly, Zn biofortification using NPs appears to be more effective in fruits than in cereals, likely due to species-specific physiological traits and variations in agronomic and environmental conditions [177–179]. In cereals, anatomical barriers, particularly those restricting Zn loading and distribution within the grain, remain major bottlenecks [180, 181].

For each study, we also calculated the contribution of 100 g of the biofortified product to the Zn RDI, using reference values of 11 mg for men and 8 mg for women [158]. As shown in Table 2, only one strategy in spinach leaves [182] exceeded 100% of the RDI for men, while seven others, including wheat seeds [129, 174, 183], green bean pods [184], pinto bean seeds [185], lettuce leaves [186], and green peas [175]; reached at least 50%. When considering the lowest Zn RDI of 8 mg for women, only one approach surpassed the requirement, providing 112% [186]. In contrast, 27 out of 49 strategies failed to deliver even 50% of the lowest Zn RDI. Notably, leafy vegetables like spinach and lettuce [182, 186] contributed the most, reinforcing their potential for Zn biofortification. These findings reveal the need to enhance Zn loading in cereal grains, which feed much of the world’s population [187]. This would depend on identifying key genetic, physiological, and environmental factors that regulate Zn uptake, transport, and accumulation in grains. Figure 2 provides an overview of the main NP-based strategies used to biofortify Zn in edible plants.

Zn-based NFs can substantially increase Zn concentrations in edible tissues, but excessive Zn inputs may antagonize other micronutrients, particularly Fe, Cu, and Mn [188, 189]. These interactions, driven by competition for transporters and binding sites in roots and vascular tissues, have been associated with reduced accumulation of these elements in grains and leaves [190]. Therefore, future Zn biofortification strategies must carefully balance Zn application rates against the risk of secondary micronutrient deficiencies to improve Zn nutrition without disrupting overall mineral balance.

Se biofortification strategies using NPs

Se is also essential for human and animal health, primarily as a component of selenoproteins involved in antioxidant defense, thyroid hormone metabolism, and reproductive function [101]. The RDI for adults is about 55 µg [191] and dietary sources include Brazil nuts, seafood, meat, and eggs [192]. Se deficiency is a global health concern, particularly in regions with Se-deficient soils, which lead to low Se levels in locally produced foods. In humans and animals, insufficient Se intake is associated with compromised immune function and increased susceptibility to infections, potentially increasing the severity of diseases such as COVID-19 [193], besides cardiovascular diseases and thyroid dysfunction [194]. Severe deficiencies can result in diseases such as Keshan disease (cardiomyopathy) and Kashin-Beck disease, which affects joints and bones [195].

Although Se is not considered an essential nutrient for plants [196], its presence can offer beneficial effects such as enhancing resistance to oxidative stress and improving tolerance to drought and salinity [197]. Plants can absorb and accumulate Se in bioavailable forms, serving as a key dietary source of this nutrient for animals and humans. However, Se availability in soils is highly variable and depends on pH, organic matter content, and redox conditions [198]. Se concentrations are generally low in acidic, highly weathered soils, especially in regions with high rainfall or intense agricultural activities that can accelerate Se leaching and depletion [199].

Several strategies have been developed to increase Se content in crops [200]. Soil application, typically using selenate or selenite, is common but its effectiveness can be limited by soil composition, leaching, and Se mobility. Se spraying directly onto leaves can be more effective under certain conditions but involves higher costs and greater labor requirements [201]. Genetic biofortification, through breeding or engineering plants to enhance Se uptake, translocation, and storage, is still in its early stages and faces regulatory and consumer acceptance challenges [202]. While these methods can help combat Se deficiency, they must be carefully managed to avoid Se toxicity that can harm plant health and pose risks to animal and human consumers.

Table 3 summarizes NP-based strategies to biofortify Se in edible plants, most of which achieved substantial increases in Se concentration. Seven strategies stood out for achieving the highest Se increases compared with control plants: (1) foliar Se-NPs (100 mg L⁻¹) raised fava bean Se by 7,395% [203]; (2) soil Se-NPs (100 mg kg⁻¹) increased wheat seed Se by 5,080% [204]; (3) foliar Bio-NP-Se (120 mg L⁻¹) raised radish Se by 3,786% [205]; (4) foliar Se-NPs (10 mg L⁻¹) increased tomato fruits Se by 3,400% [206]; 5/6) foliar Se-NPs (100 µmol L⁻¹ and 80 g ha⁻¹) increased rice seed Se by 2,509% and 2,500%, respectively [207, 208]; and 7) foliar Bio-NP-Se (20 mg L⁻¹) raised rice seed Se by 1,575% [209]. Notably, some strategies using Bio-NP-Se or Se-NPs also enriched edible tissues where Se was undetectable in controls, such as eggplant [210] and tomato fruits [206, 211] (Table 3). Of the 13 studies, only two used soil Se-NPs in wheat and lettuce [204, 212], with one achieving excellent Se enrichment in wheat seeds. Thus, while most evidence comes from foliar spraying, we cannot yet conclude that it is more effective than soil application.

Beyond total Se concentration, the chemical form (speciation) of Se in edible tissues is a key determinant of nutritional value and safety. Plants can convert inorganic Se forms (selenate, selenite, or Se-NPs) into organic species, mainly selenocysteine (SeCys) and selenomethionine (SeMet), with SeMet being the most bioavailable for humans [213, 214]. SeMet can be non-specifically incorporated into proteins in place of methionine, serving as a stable storage pool of Se, whereas SeCys is more metabolically active but less stable [215]. Evidence suggests that NP-based Se delivery increases the proportion of organic Se compounds [216], indicating effective conversion into bioavailable forms. However, more systematic studies are needed to confirm whether Se-NPs consistently increase organic Se accumulation across different plant species and growing conditions.

For each study, we also calculated the percentage of the Se RDI that 100 g of the biofortified product could provide [191]. Among the 13 strategies listed in Table 3, seven achieved more than 100% of the Se RDI: [207–209] for rice seeds, [203] for fava bean seeds, [206] for tomato fruits, [204] for wheat seeds, [217] for coffee beans, and [218] for common bean seeds. In contrast, four strategies ([212] for lettuce leaves, [210] for eggplant fruits, [211] for tomato fruits, and [114] for celery) provided less than 50% of the Se RDI (Table 3). As previously discussed, achieving full Se RDI coverage using NPs depends not only on the application method or NP type but also on the plant species and their natural Se content. For example, foliar spraying of Se-NPs (5 mg L⁻¹) on celery increased Se concentration by 400% but supplied only 18% of the Se RDI [114]. This highlights the importance of selecting plant species or cultivars that naturally accumulate Se more efficiently to ensure nutritional impact. While NP-based approaches can substantially increase Se concentrations in edible tissues, they raise important safety concerns. Se has a narrow margin between essentiality and toxicity, with an RDI of ~ 55 µg day−1 for adults and a tolerable upper intake level (UL) of ~ 400 µg day−1 [191]. Chronic intake above this threshold can cause selenosis, manifested as gastrointestinal disturbances, nail and hair brittleness, and, in severe cases, neurological damage [219]. For example, foliar application of Se-NPs (100 µmol L⁻¹) in rice [207] increased grain Se to >500 µg per 100 g, thereby exceeding the UL. This illustrates that while Se-NP biofortification is effective against deficiency, application rates must be carefully optimized to avoid toxicity.

Approaches for the future

Opportunities for further research and innovation in NP-mediated biofortification

NP-mediated biofortification has emerged as a promising strategy to combat global micronutrient deficiencies and enhance crop yields. By taking advantage of the unique properties of NPs, such as their nanoscale size, high surface area-to-volume ratio, and tailored surface chemistry, researchers have enhanced the nutrient content and bioavailability of essential minerals in crops [20, 25]. Despite recent progress, further research is needed to optimize NP formulations, refine application methods, and ensure both environmental and food safety (summarized in Fig. 3). NP-mediated biofortification holds great potential, but regulatory and safety challenges remain. Current regulations for agricultural NP use are limited and vary by region, while potential risks to human health, non-target organisms, and ecosystems are still under investigation. Comprehensive risk assessment, including studies on NP accumulation in edible tissues, long-term environmental impacts, and safe application protocols, is essential before widespread implementation. Addressing these considerations is critical to ensure that NP-based biofortification is both effective and safe.

Key opportunities for innovation in NP-mediated biofortification of crops. Created with BioRender.com

Continued innovation in this field is essential to fully realize the potential of nanotechnology in sustainable agriculture and global nutrition. Therefore, we highlight four major areas that deserve special attention:

1) NP design, synthesis, and nutrient release dynamics: advancing the design and synthesis of NPs tailored for specific nutrient delivery is critical for improving plant biofortification. Optimizing NP size, shape, composition, and surface chemistry can enhance nutrient uptake while minimizing potential phytotoxicity. Equally important is understanding the mechanisms governing nutrient loading onto NPs and their release kinetics in the soil or plant tissues. Exploring novel encapsulation techniques and environmentally responsive nanomaterials may allow for targeted, controlled nutrient release based on plant requirements and surrounding environmental conditions. Recent findings indicate that biomaterials, including polylactic acid, polyhydroxyalkanoates polymers, alginate, liposomes, carbon nanotubes, and chitosan, serve as effective carriers [220–226], supporting controlled delivery while also minimizing ecological risks [20].

2) Interaction with plant physiology: exploring how Fe, Zn, and Se-based NPs interact with plant physiology at the molecular, cellular, and whole-plant levels is essential to elucidate uptake mechanisms, translocation pathways, and metabolic responses. Advanced imaging, omics technologies, and bioanalytical tools offer valuable insights to unravel these complex interactions and guide the design of more effective biofortification strategies. Recent studies using transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic approaches, combined with physiological analyses, have provided insights into how plants respond to NP exposure and micronutrient enrichment [227–231]. Advanced real-time imaging techniques such as synchrotron-based X-ray fluorescence (XRF) microscopy and laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) allow in vivo tracking of NP uptake, translocation, and transformation in plant tissues [232]. These high-resolution approaches complement omics analyses by providing spatially resolved data on nutrient distribution and storage, thereby enhancing our mechanistic understanding of NP-plant interactions. These findings deepen our understanding of the soil-plant-NP-micronutrient interface. For a comprehensive review of NP interactions with signaling pathways and nutrient homeostasis, see [25].

3) Environmental impacts and safety: assessing the environmental fate, bioavailability, and potential ecological risks of applying NPs in agriculture is essential for ensuring sustainable and safe use. Studies should examine NP persistence, mobility, and interactions with soil microbiota and ecosystems to inform comprehensive risk assessments and regulatory guidelines. While nanobiofortification may reduce agrochemical dependency and promote resource efficiency (e.g., lower fertilizer and water use, shorter crop cycles), it also raises concerns. Unintended effects, including nanomaterial accumulation, phytotoxicity, disruption of soil microbial communities (e.g., rhizobia and mycorrhiza), and toxicity to non-target organisms via ROS overproduction, must be thoroughly addressed [20, 25, 233]. Integrating life cycle assessment can support the evaluation of environmental sustainability and guide responsible NP use in agriculture [234].

4) Field trials and scaling up: large-scale field trials in diverse agroecosystems and geographical regions are important to validate the efficacy, feasibility, and agronomic performance of NP-based plant biofortification strategies. These trials should include comparisons with conventional fertilizers to evaluate relative benefits in terms of yield, nutritional quality, environmental impact, and cost-effectiveness for both crop producers and consumers [229]. While replicability remains a challenge in field trials, conducting realistic experiments is critical for translating laboratory findings into viable agricultural practices. Additionally, scaling up NP production and assessing economic viability are key steps toward commercialization and adoption by farmers.

Challenges and opportunities for scaling up NP-based biofortification strategies

While the benefits of NP-based biofortification are well documented, challenges remain in scaling up nanomaterial production for agriculture and improving global nutrition [235, 236]. Figure 4 summarizes the main limitations and opportunities for large-scale implementation. A major challenge is reproducibility, including size uniformity, shape, and distribution of NPs. Although top-down and bottom-up synthesis methods are established, few studies have optimized production parameters, increasing variation in large-scale manufacturing [237, 238]. Other obstacles include quality control, NP aggregation, contamination, degradation, stability, low yields, and high production costs, which further reduce efficiency and discourage industry investment [239].

From laboratory to industrial scale: limitations and opportunities in NP production for agricultural biofortification. Created with BioRender.com

At the same time, these challenges create opportunities for innovation. Green synthesis, which uses plant extracts, microorganisms, natural materials, or organic waste as reducing and stabilizing agents, offers a safer and more sustainable alternative to chemical synthesis. Green-synthesized NPs may also show improved biocompatibility though direct evidence for Fe, Zn, and Se biofortification remains limited. Preliminary results indicate that green NPs enhance nutrient uptake and accumulation in edible crops, offering a potential safer, more sustainable, and cost-effective alternative for large-scale production [240]. Standardizing synthesis methods and automating production could further improve reproducibility, precision, and output.

Scaling up NP production also enables the development of specialized equipment, such as high-throughput nanoformulators, advanced foliar sprayers, seed-coating machinery, and automated nanopriming systems. Progress will require multidisciplinary collaboration between process engineers and plant scientists to establish industrial-scale supply chains that can support sustainable biofortification [241].

Conclusion

This review highlights the potential of NP-based biofortification to combat micronutrient deficiencies and improve global food security. While significant progress has been made, key challenges remain, particularly the need for large-scale field validation, standardized NP synthesis and characterization protocols, and cost-effective, reproducible production methods. Equally important are comprehensive safety assessments addressing NP accumulation, persistence, and ecological impacts. Despite these challenges, opportunities to enhance scalability and promote sustainable agriculture are substantial. Advances in nanotechnology hold great promise for developing effective, sustainable biofortification strategies, but long-term field studies are urgently needed to evaluate NP persistence, environmental fate, and potential ecological impacts, ensuring that agronomic gains do not compromise environmental safety.
