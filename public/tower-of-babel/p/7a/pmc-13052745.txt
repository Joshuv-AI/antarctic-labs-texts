Modifying continuity of care indices for adults with organ failure nearing the end of life: a retrospective population-based study in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Shuaib Hafid, Aaron Jones, Sarina Isenberg, Anastasia Gayowsky, Aria Wills, Aleisha Fernandes, Kieran Quinn, Erin Gallagher, Amy T Hsu, Colleen Webber, Peter Tanuseputro, Michelle Howard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo develop and describe the performance of two modified relational continuity of care indices for adults with organ failure nearing the end of life, addressing limitations of existing indices that penalise multispecialty physician care.DesignPopulation-based retrospective study.SettingLinked routinely collected health administrative databases in Ontario, Canada.ParticipantsAdults aged ≥19 years who died between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2022, with kidney failure on dialysis, advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and/or heart failure.Primary and secondary outcome measuresWe adapted the Usual Provider of Care (UPC) and Bice-Boxerman Continuity of Care (BB) indices to avoid penalising multispecialty physician care, resulting in the UPC-Team and BB-Team indices. Indices were calculated for the last 2 years of life (truncating the last month) using outpatient physician visits. Correlation coefficients were produced between the unmodified indices, the modified indices and outpatient healthcare utilisation.ResultsThe cohort included 199 035 individuals, with a median age of 79 years (P25, P75: 70, 86); 55.5% were male. The median modified continuity scores were higher than the unmodified scores (UPC=0.44 (0.32, 0.61), UPC-Team=0.79 (0.67, 0.89); BB=0.24 (0.15, 0.40), BB-Team=0.75 (0.52, 0.95)). The modified indices shared weaker correlations with outpatient healthcare use (ie, number of visits or specialists involved), compared with the unmodified indices.ConclusionsThe UPC-Team and BB-Team indices do not penalise patients for receiving multispecialty physician care, compared with the unmodified UPC and BB indices. The modified indices demonstrated more consistency in measuring continuity across patients with varying degrees of medical complexity and less correlation with outpatient healthcare use. Use of these indices is suggested to capture different aspects of relational continuity in the context of multispecialty care nearing the end of life.Keywords: End stage renal failure; Heart failure; Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; Quality in health care

Introduction

Continuity of care (COC) is a routinely measured indicator of quality of care in health services research, and often defined as ‘the degree to which a series of discrete healthcare events is experienced as coherent and connected and consistent with the patient’s medical needs and personal context’.1 COC can encompass three different domains, such as informational, management and relational continuity.1 Informational continuity measures the degree of information sharing between care providers regarding, but not limited to, the patients’ medical complexity and their preferences and values. Management continuity, which is relevant for increasingly medically complex patient populations, includes shared care models that ensure consistent or complementary care provision across different providers. Lastly, relational continuity is intrinsic to measuring the longitudinal relationship between a care provider and a patient, which may result in a degree of care predictability within the patient-provider care dyad.2 A meta-summary of 33 qualitative studies exploring patients’ perceptions of COC identified that the majority of studies highlighted the importance of longitudinal relationships with clinicians for establishing trusting care partnerships to discuss sensitive healthcare issues.3 Further, there is widespread evidence demonstrating the positive associations between relational continuity and decreased acute care utilisation and reduced mortality.4,6

There is an impetus for having a relational continuity measure which can be applied to routinely collected health administrative data for the purpose of measuring care quality as informational and management continuity cannot accurately be captured using structured health administrative data. Two commonly used relational COC indices in health services research include the Usual Provider of Care (UPC) and the Bice-Boxerman COC (BB) indices. These two indices quantify patterns of visit concentration and are commonly used as proxies for relational continuity in studies using health administrative data, under the assumption that repeated contact with the same physician(s) reflects ongoing therapeutic relationships. The UPC index is a density measure which calculates the concentration of care with the usual provider among all providers during a specified interval,7 while the BB index measures the dispersion of care across different providers.8 UPC scores typically decrease with the involvement of additional providers, as the index is the fraction of visits to the designated usual provider out of all provider visits (including the visits to each additional provider). The BB index would also yield lower scores with each additional care provider involved, as the patients’ visits would further spread out among the providers. Further, the UPC index was originally applied to a paediatric patient population and a previously published systematic review5 and rapid review9 identified that both indices have been typically applied across diverse non-end-of-life patient populations.

Our previous study measuring outpatient physician care patterns among a general decedent population identified family physicians were responsible for the majority of outpatient care, yet there were also four unique specialties (outside of family medicine) involved on average during the last year of life.10 In a subsequent study using the same cohort, we identified that COC (measured using the UPC, BB and Sequential continuity indices) was generally very low during the last year of life, after adjusting for the patients’ clinical characteristics and healthcare utilisation.11 Patients navigating the end of life are medically complex12 as they have multiple comorbidities contributing to their progression,10 which may require the appropriate involvement of several specialists treating their respective conditions whether for curative or comfort care. However, increasing the number of specialists involved naturally lowers continuity scores as more unique physicians are involved in a patients care. This also creates a correlation between use of medical specialist care and continuity that may lead to confounding and spurious epidemiological relationships. This scenario highlights how measuring continuity using traditional indices may not be appropriate for patients nearing the end of life as they penalise patients for receiving multispecialty physician care,13 suggesting that team-based care is poor quality.

There is increasing literature demonstrating the benefits of multispecialty care for managing complex organ failure conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure (HF) and kidney failure. For instance, integrating specialised teams (including cardiologists, primary care physicians and allied health professionals) has been associated with decreased hospital readmissions, shorter hospital stays and improved quality of life.14 15 Similarly, multiprovider teams yield decreased mortality rates, decreased temporary catheterisation rates, fewer hospitalisations and slower illness progression—especially for patients with later disease stages.16,18 Collectively, these findings highlight the value of collaboration between multiple care providers in enhancing clinical outcomes and patient-centred care for individuals with organ failure. Therefore, the objective of this study is to develop and describe the performance of two modified indices that consider the need for multispecialty physician care during the end of life to represent a nuanced, comprehensive reflection of relational COC for this unique care stage, given the challenges and limitations of traditional visit-concentration indices as proxies for relational continuity.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using linked population-level health administrative data from Ontario, Canada (online supplemental appendix 1). Administrative data holdings are linked using unique encoded identifiers and analysed at ICES (formerly known as the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences). ICES is an independent, non-profit research institute whose legal status under Ontario’s health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyse healthcare and demographic data for health system evaluation and improvement.

Sample population

The sample included adults (aged 19 or older) who died between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2022, with prevalent kidney failure on dialysis (KF-D) at any point before death, and advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (ACOPD) and/or HF at 2 years before death. KF-D prevalence was defined using the validated definition to capture chronic kidney disease (ie, two subsequent estimated glomerula filtration rate scores less than 30 in the Ontario Lab Instrument System) and any record of long-term maintenance dialysis initiation (both haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis) in the Canadian Organ Replacement Register.19 We adapted a sensitive validated case definition for COPD to capture ACOPD,20 by requiring individuals to have at least one hospitalisation for a COPD-related condition or the receipt of long-term oxygen therapy at least 2 years before death.21 HF prevalence was defined using a previously validated case definition, where prevalence was defined as having an inpatient or outpatient visit with a HF diagnosis and a following record across all care settings with a HF diagnosis within 1 year.22 We modified the HF diagnosis by not requiring individuals to be older than 65 years of age as our cohort consisted of decedents. Otherwise, individuals were excluded if they were 105 years of age or older at death, if they were ineligible for the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) during the last 2 years of life, and if they resided in a long-term care home during the last 2 years of life. Patients were categorised into mutually exclusive organ failure subgroups, including ACOPD only, HF only, ACOPD and HF only, and any KF-D, for the purpose of comparing differences between specialties that differ in their billing practices (ie, consultation-based billing in respirology and cardiology vs management-based billing in dialysis units)

Cohort characteristics

Individuals’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were captured at 2 years before death, unless otherwise specified. We used the Registered Persons Database to capture individuals’ age and sex at death, and Statistics Canada’s Postal Code Conversion File+to capture their rurality and neighbourhood-level income status. Individuals’ comorbidity status was captured using the Johns Hopkins Adjusted Clinical Group System Aggregated Diagnosis Groups (ADG), Version 10.23 The system categorises all assigned International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) / International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) diagnosis codes into 32 functional ADGs, based on the certainty of diagnosis, duration, severity and aetiology of the condition, as well as the odds of needing specialist services.24

COC measures: concerns and modifications

The UPC index ranges from 0 when all visits are with unique physicians to 1 when all visits are with the usual physician.7 The usual physician may be specified a priori or identified as the physician with the greatest number of visits in a specified timeframe. The BB index measures the degree of dispersion across visits to all physicians during a specified interval.8 The BB index ranges from 0 to 1 where 1 occurs when all visits are to the same provider.

As traditionally measured, individuals seeing multiple specialties would have lower continuity scores, which would suggest poorer quality of care and penalise multispecialty models of care. An ideal adapted index would reach 1 when all visits are within the team and 0 when all visits are outside. To accomplish this, we modified the UPC index by measuring the proportion of visits to a team of usual physicians instead of an individual physician, creating the modified UPC-Team index. Like the unmodified UPC, the UPC-Team ranges from 0 to 1 for ease of comparison and interpretation. Individuals receive a score of 1 when all their visits are with the team, and a score of 0 when all their visits are with uniquely different physicians. Ultimately, the UPC-Team index shifts the focus away from individual physicians to multispecialty models of care.

Online supplemental appendix 2 includes practical examples comparing the unmodified and modified indices under high and low specialty concentration scenarios.

We calculated the unmodified and modified continuity indices for using outpatient visits for each patient during their last 2 years of life (truncating the last 30 days of life). We used this approach to define the end-of-life period as it ensures consistency with the observation period for the index scores in addition to the last month of life being associated with increased acute care use.26,28 Outpatient visits were captured using unique physician billing claims from the OHIP database where the location codes were ‘Office’, ‘Home’ or ‘Phone’. For the UPC, the usual provider was identified as the family physician with the highest number of visits during the time.

Healthcare utilisation indicators

We measured the total number of outpatient visits, the number of outpatient visits to family physicians, the number of outpatient visits with specialists, and the total number of unique specialists visited during the last 2 years of life, truncating the last 30 days of life to ensure temporal consistency with the calculated continuity indices.

Analyses

Continuous variables were reported with means and SDs or medians and 25th percentiles and 75th percentiles if non-normally distributed, while categorical and ordinal variables were reported as counts and percentages. We conducted Kruskal-Wallis tests and produced eta-squared effect sizes to assess differences in outpatient healthcare indicators and COC scores, across the organ-failure subgroups. We also conducted distributional comparisons between the unmodified and modified indices by producing histograms of the score distributions using violin plots, which were reported for the total cohort and per organ failure subgroup. We also measured the correlation between the unmodified, modified indices and outpatient healthcare utilisation indicators by producing Pearson correlation matrices. Lastly, we also produced scatter plots and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) scores between the unmodified and modified indices as a supplementary analysis to assess the degree of alignment between the versions. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 (two-tailed) and all analyses were completed using R Studio V.0.98.1091.

Results

A total of 199 035 eligible individuals were included in the analysis after applying the exclusion criteria (figure 1). The median (P25, P75) age of the sample at 2 years before death was 79 (70, 86), 55.5% was male, and the median number of ADGs at 2 years before death was 13 (11, 16) (table 1). 12.8% of the cohort had ACOPD only, 64.0% had HF only, 16.3% had ACOPD and HF without KF-D, and 6.8% had KF-D. Individuals with KF-D were younger, comprised more males and had a greater number of comorbidities compared with individuals without KF-D, while cancer prevalence was highest among patients with ACOPD. Characteristics were significantly different across organ failure subgroups (p<0.001) due to the large sample size but were modest in magnitude of effect (effect sizes ranging from 0.023 to 0.144).

Additionally, the BB and UPC index scores were almost perfectly correlated (Pearson’s correlation: 0.96) and the modified versions remained strongly positively correlated with one another (Pearson’s correlation: 0.86) (online supplemental appendix 4). The BB and UPC index scores shared weak to moderate correlations with outpatient healthcare utilisation indicators, such as the number of outpatient visits (BB: −0.17; UPC: −0.22), the number of visits with the family physician (BB: 0.15; UPC: 0.14), the number of visits with specialists (BB: −0.43; UPC: −0.49) and the total number of unique specialists visited (BB: −0.52; UPC: −0.54). The BB-Team and UPC-Team indices shared weaker correlations with these outpatient healthcare utilisation indicators or ceased to be correlated entirely. These patterns remained consistent when stratified by the organ failure subgroups as well. Lastly, the ICC scores between the unmodified and modified indices reported moderate alignment, suggesting that the underlying concept of relational continuity is still being measured (UPC and UPC-TEAM, ICC (95% CI): 0.67 (0.67 to 0.67; BB and BB-Team–ICC (95% CI): 0.71 (0.71 to 0.72) (online supplemental appendix 5). Most of the points cluster in the top left quadrant and along the diagonal, suggesting that the indices track in the same direction with the modified indices systematically scoring higher or assigning ‘perfect’ continuity (ie, UPC-Team and BB-Team scores of 1.0).

Discussion

In this study of patients who died with organ failure conditions in Ontario, Canada, we created and assessed the performance of two modified COC indices for the end-of-life context compared with existing unmodified indices. The modified UPC-Team and BB-Team indices reported substantially higher scores during the last 2 years of life, compared with their unmodified variants which penalised individuals for having multiple physician specialties involved during the end of life, resulting in lower COC scores.11

Patients with organ failure conditions, such as ACOPD, HF and KF-D often have multiple comorbidities which require multispecialty physician care. For instance, a recent Delphi study of Canadian respirologists, cardiologists and family physicians identified strong consensus belief in optimising multidisciplinary care for COPD patients with comorbid cardiac conditions to improve healthcare resource utilisation and clinical outcomes (ie, acute care visits).29 Similarly, a Cochrane review identified that integrated disease management (including involvement of general practitioners and COPD specialists) of COPD has identified significant benefits, including improved quality of life and decreased COPD-related acute care utilisation.30 Moreover, a meta-analysis identified that multicomponent integrated care for HF (involving general practitioners and HF specialists) is associated with decreased HF-related hospitalisations and improved mortality compared with the standard of care,31 while a systematic review of multidisciplinary care for chronic kidney disease identified that multidisciplinary care (involving kidney specialists, surgeons and general practitioners) was associated with slower disease progression and improved mortality as well.32 The unmodified indices were not created to account for the appropriate involvement of multiple medical specialists and therefore may inappropriately suggest poorer care quality at the end of life. Ultimately, continuity, specifically informational, can remain high among multispecialty care models so long as the physicians maintain transparent communication regarding the patients’ health status and preferences.33

The unmodified indices were moderately correlated with outpatient healthcare utilisation measures (ie, number of visits, number of unique specialists involved), while the modified indices were either weakly correlated or not correlated with the outpatient care measures. This reduced correlation suggests that the UPC-Team and BB-Team may be capturing other variables at play nearing the end of life, instead of overall outpatient care utilisation. This may be more appropriate for multi-specialty models of care, where the number of physicians involved should not matter if the physicians are appropriately involved. COC measures should reflect positive aspects of care and should not be conflated with high care use, which is often considered to be negative. Next, healthcare utilisation is influenced greatly by multiple extraneous factors, such as how medically complex a patient is. For example, patients with more comorbidities (ie, patients with KF-D or patients with ACOPD and HF) are expected to have more encounters and specialists involved. Continuity indices should distinguish between measuring continuity while mitigating the confounding of other factors on resource utilisation. The UPC-Team and BB-Team indices produced more consistent scores across all organ failure populations, compared with their unmodified versions. The consistency in measurement is ideal as it demonstrates that the modified indices are less sensitive to clinical differences across end-of-life populations. For example, patients with KF-D were the most medically complex and had the highest number of specialists involved during the end of life, compared with the other organ failure populations. Despite these significant differences, the modified indices reported much more comparable scores between the organ failure subgroups compared with the differences across the unmodified indices. As a result, the modified indices may also be utilised to measure the relational end-of-life continuity of other medically complex patients, such as patients with terminal illnesses like cancer.

Additionally, the modified UPC-Team index is less sensitive to different billing practices across specialties (ie, specialties that predominantly bill management fee codes for a specified time period instead of individual visits or services). For example, nephrologists typically use weekly management fee codes for patients that receive long-term maintenance dialysis in Ontario, in lieu of billing for each individual visit.19 If the management fee code represents at least one outpatient visit with their nephrologist, a patient may have 52 outpatient visits with their nephrologist. As a result, the non-modified UPC for patients using this definition would be significantly lower as the denominator (ie, the total number of outpatient visits) increases greatly while the numerator (ie, the total number of outpatient visits with the usual family physician) would remain the same. Instead, the modified UPC-Team variable is less sensitive to specialties that predominantly bill management fee codes, as those specialists would be considered as part of the usual physician team. Overall, the modified UPC-Team does not penalise differences in patient populations with varying degrees of medical complexity and among specialties with management-focused billing practices, which in turn increases the utility of the modified index as a system-level quality of care indicator for the end of life. Moreover, the original indices were not developed for the end-of-life period,7 or for measuring multispecialty physician care.8 Apart from our previous studies,11 most applications of both indices have generally been applied to non-end-of-life populations,5 9 34 disease-specific patient populations9 or older patients with multiple chronic conditions.35 Moreover, patients at the end of life will have different goals of care which may not prioritise intensive treatments that extend life, compared with the other patient populations these indices have been typically applied to. Lastly, patients experience a fair degree of volatility at the end of life, partly due to their medical complexity, which requires flexible clinical approaches that empower multispecialty physician care when desired. Therefore, we also need a longitudinal continuity measure that affords the same degree of flexibility for team-based models of care at the end of life.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, this is the first presentation of a relational continuity index modified specifically for the end-of-life context. Second, we leveraged population-level health administrative data, maximising the study sample size and increasing the external validity of our findings. As a result, the study findings may also be relevant and replicable in other jurisdictions that provide comprehensive health services to their patients. This study also has limitations. First, this study is descriptive in nature and does not explore associations between the modified indices and key end-of-life healthcare outcomes such as location of death. Second, we were unable to determine the patients’ primary cause of death due to a multi-year delay in cause of death data availability. As a result, our findings are relevant to patients with prevalent organ failure conditions at the end of life. Third, our analytic cohort was limited to individuals who did not spend any time in long-term care homes, as these individuals experience different outpatient care patterns compared with patients’ residing in the community. Fourth, we cannot assess the quality of multispecialty care or the degree of physician variation within each team. Lastly, we are unable to capture the degree of relational continuity with non-physician care providers (ie, nurse practitioners) by using physician billing claims data as we do not have access to data relating to outpatient care provided by non-physician providers in Ontario, Canada.

Conclusions

We presented and discussed the performance of two modified relational continuity indices for the end-of-life context that do not penalise medically complex patients receiving multispecialty physician care. Having multiple specialties involved does not inherently mean that patients’ experience poorer continuity or experience poorer quality of care, and COC indices should reflect this. Importantly, the modified COC indices we presented may capture different continuity patterns as most patients experience multiple comorbidities during the end of life, in addition to other medically complex patient populations such as older adults. While the modified indices behave differently than the unmodified indices, further research should be conducted to measure the associations between the UPC-Team and BB-Team and routinely measured end-of-life healthcare quality outcomes such as acute care utilisation during the last month of life and place of death. More importantly, future research should also validate these measures against patient preferences or experiences of terminally ill individuals or bereaving caregivers and their perceptions of continuity to ensure accurate alignment with the end-of-life period.
