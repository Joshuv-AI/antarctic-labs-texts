Minimally invasive surgery for remnant gastric cancer: a single-center retrospective study of treatment outcomes in Japan
Authors: Kenichi Iwasaki, Edward Barroga, Yoichiro Kaneko, Shohei Kondo, Toru Sakurai, Erika Yamada, Masaya Enomoto, Yota Shimoda, Kenta Kasahara, Hiroaki Osakabe, Junichi Mazaki, Hiroshi Kuwabara, Junya Oguma, Hiroyuki Koga, Akishige Kanazawa, Yuichi Nakagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSurgical resection of remnant gastric cancer (RGC) is technically challenging and difficult. Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has been adopted for various procedures, but reports of MIS for RGC remain limited. Herein, we report the surgical techniques and short-term outcomes of MIS for RGC.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study involving 61 consecutive RGC patients who underwent open or minimally invasive (laparoscopic or robotic) total gastrectomy for potentially curable RGC at our institution (January 1999–August 2025). A propensity score-matched cohort was used for an exploratory evaluation of the safety and efficacy of MIS for RGC, focusing on feasibility rather than superiority over open surgery.ResultsOf these 61 patients, 53 underwent open surgery and eight underwent MIS gastrectomy for RGC. The median age was 70 years. The cohort comprised 53 men and eight women. The initial procedure was open surgery in 58 patients and MIS in three patients. Propensity score matching showed significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss in the MIS group compared with the open surgery group (39 mL vs. 576 mL, p < 0.05), and significantly longer operation time in the MIS group (352 minutes vs. 297 minutes, p < 0.05). Postoperative hospital stay was significantly shorter in the MIS group (12 days vs. 17 days, p < 0.05). The incidence of postoperative pancreatic fistula was lower in the MIS group, although not significantly different (0% vs. 16.7%).ConclusionMIS, including robotic gastrectomy, is feasible and safe for RGC, with potential perioperative benefits requiring multicenter validation.Keywords: Remnant gastric cancer, Minimally invasive surgery, Robotic gastrectomy

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men and the fifth among women globally [1]. Remnant gastric cancer (RGC) encompasses gastric cancer that arises from the remnant stomach in cases of either benign or malignant gastrectomy [2]. Despite RGC being reported as a relatively rare disease with an incidence rate of 1%–5% of all gastric cancer cases [3–5], the incidence of RGC is expected to increase with the aging population, innovations in diagnostic techniques and routine endoscopic surveillance, and advancements in surgical techniques [6].

Recent advancements in chemotherapy and radiotherapy have improved the treatment options for gastric cancer. However, gastrectomy with appropriate lymph node dissection remains the standard approach for resectable gastric cancer and resectable RGC [7]. Gastrectomy is more challenging for RGC than for primary gastric cancer because of potential extensive adhesions to adjacent organs, alterations in anatomical structures, and modifications in lymphatic flow [8,9].

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) including laparoscopic and robotic approaches has steadily gained attraction in various surgical fields over the years. As a curative treatment method for primary gastric cancer, MIS has improved pain, reduced blood loss, and shortened the length of hospital stay [10–12]. However, the number of reports published on MIS for RGC remains limited. This scarcity is attributed to not only the inherent rarity of RGC itself but also the considerable technical challenges associated with performing MIS in this context. As a result, the adoption of MIS for RGC has been relatively slow, and robust data on its safety, feasibility, and long-term outcomes are still lacking in the literature.

This highlights the need for further clinical experience and multicenter collaboration to establish a more comprehensive evidence base. To date, only a few studies have evaluated and reported on the safety and feasibility of MIS specifically for RGC [12,13]. Thus, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the safety, feasibility, and short-term postoperative outcomes of MIS for RGC.

METHODS

Patients

We assessed a total of 61 consecutive patients who were pathologically diagnosed with RGC in this retrospective cohort study. All the patients underwent open or minimally invasive total gastrectomy (i.e., laparoscopic or robotic) for potentially curable RGC between January 1999 and August 2025 at our institution. Patients included in this study were those who had undergone surgical resection with curative intent for histologically confirmed adenocarcinoma arising in the remnant stomach. Eligibility criteria required complete clinical, radiological, and pathological data indicating the absence of distant metastases at the time of surgery. Patients with evidence of unresectable disease, distant metastasis, or who underwent palliative or non-curative procedures were excluded from the analysis to ensure a homogenous study cohort focused exclusively on curative treatment outcomes. The patients were divided into two groups according to the surgical approach. The flow diagram of the patients who were initially diagnosed with RGC (n = 107) to the final study population (n = 61) is shown in Fig. 1. Surgical procedures

Curative R0 total gastrectomy with sufficient lymphadenectomy was routinely performed in all the patients following the Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines [15]. After an upward retraction of the gastric remnant, the common hepatic artery, celiac trunk, and splenic artery were exposed for lymph node dissection. A Roux-en-Y esophagojejunostomy or jejunojejunostomy was performed for reconstruction followed by total gastrectomy completion. During robotic surgery, adhesiolysis was routinely performed using a double-bipolar technique in soft coagulation mode. This technique facilitated controlled dissection of intra-abdominal adhesions while maintaining reliable hemostasis, including cases with severe adhesions, thereby eliminating the need for ultrasonically activated shears.

Definition and assessment of outcomes

Postoperative complications were defined and classified according to the Clavien-Dindo classification system. For this study, only complications of grade III or higher that occurred within 30 days after surgery were included in the analysis, in order to focus on clinically significant events that required pharmacological, surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention. Intraoperative blood loss was estimated using a combination of standard surgical methods. Specifically, the total volume collected in the suction canister was recorded, from which the volume of irrigation fluid used during the procedure was subtracted. In addition, the amount of blood absorbed by surgical gauze was visually assessed and incorporated into the final estimation based on institutional guidelines routinely used in our operating rooms. Although the values are presented in milliliters, it should be emphasized that these figures represent estimated approximations derived from intraoperative records and are not precise volumetric measurements.

Follow-up protocol

All patients were followed postoperatively through routine outpatient visits at our institution. In the early postoperative period, clinical evaluations, blood tests, and imaging studies (e.g., plain chest and abdominal X-ray, abdominal computed tomography scans) were performed as needed to monitor recovery and detect any complications. Follow-up visits were scheduled at regular intervals (typically at the first month and every 3–6 months for the first 5 years) according to institutional protocol, regardless of the study’s primary focus on short-term outcomes. Postoperative complications were assessed and recorded within 30 days of surgery or during the hospital stay, whichever was longer, in accordance with Clavien-Dindo classification. For the purposes of this study, data related to short-term surgical outcomes, including perioperative morbidity, mortality, and hospital stay, were collected from the medical records and verified during the follow-up visits.

Indocyanine green fluorescence procedures

In the MIS group (n = 8), intraoperative blood flow evaluation was performed using indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging in all cases at the time of anastomosis. This assessment allowed for real-time confirmation of both vascular perfusion and tissue viability at the anastomotic site (Fig. 2). To ensure consistency across cases, we adopted a standardized protocol for ICG administration and imaging. Specifically, one vial of ICG (25 mg) was dissolved in 10 mL of sterile normal saline, and a dose of 2 to 3 mL was administered intravenously immediately after completion of the gastrectomy and prior to the anastomosis. Near-infrared fluorescence imaging was then utilized to visualize perfusion of gastric or intestinal stumps, allowing surgeons to verify adequate blood flow before proceeding with the anastomosis.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics and surgery-associated outcomes of the patients who underwent open or MIS total gastrectomy were compared using the Fisher exact test or Pearson chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables. To minimize the influence of potential confounding variables and ensure a balanced comparison between groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed. A logistic regression model was used to estimate propensity scores for each patient based on a comprehensive set of clinically relevant covariates. These covariates included age, sex, body mass index, indication for the initial gastrectomy (malignant vs. benign disease), surgical approach of the initial procedure (open surgery vs. MIS), reconstruction method used in the initial gastrectomy (Billroth I, Billroth II, or Roux-en-Y following distal gastrectomy [DG]; and non-DG), and the clinical TNM stage. Patients were matched in a 1:1 ratio using the nearest-neighbor method without replacement, within a caliper width equal to 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. This approach was chosen to reduce selection bias and simulate the conditions of a randomized controlled trial as closely as possible within the limitations of a retrospective design. PSM was used as an exploratory approach to reduce selection bias and improve comparability between the MIS and open surgery groups. Given the rarity of RGC and the limited number of MIS cases, the analysis was not designed to support definitive causal inference, but rather to generate hypotheses regarding the feasibility and short-term outcomes of MIS. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of <0.05. All statistical analyses, including propensity score generation and matching, were conducted using IBM SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

We assessed a total of 61 patients, composed of 53 patients who underwent open surgery and eight patients who underwent MIS gastrectomy for RGC. The clinicopathological characteristics of the 61 patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the cohort was 70 years (range, 39–84 years) and consisted of 53 men and eight women. The initial surgery was open surgery in 58 patients and MIS in three patients. Among the initially resected lesions, 11 were benign and 50 were malignant. Surgical procedures included DG in 56 patients and proximal gastrectomy (PG) in five patients. Reconstruction methods included Billroth I in 40 patients, Billroth II in nine patients, and Roux-en-Y in seven patients post-DG. A comparative analysis of baseline clinicopathological characteristics between the MIS group and the open surgery group showed no statistically significant differences across most variables. However, the proportion of male patients was significantly higher in the MIS group than in the open surgery group, representing the only notable imbalance between the two cohorts. For PG, one patient underwent esophagojejunostomy and four had jejunal interposition. The median interval from initial surgery to RGC diagnosis was 10 years (range, 1–58 years). The surgical approaches to RGC included open surgery in 53 patients and MIS in eight patients (three laparoscopic and five robotic).

Following PSM, perioperative outcomes were compared between the MIS group and the open surgery group within the matched cohort. The MIS group showed significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss compared with the open surgery group (39 mL vs. 576 mL, p < 0.05). However, the operation time was significantly longer in the MIS group (352 minutes vs. 297 minutes, p < 0.05). The length of postoperative hospital stay was significantly shorter in the MIS group than in the open surgery group (12 days vs. 17 days, p < 0.05). In terms of postoperative complications, the overall complication rate in the matched cohort was 16.7% in the open surgery group, whereas no complications were observed in the MIS group; however, this difference was not significant (p = 0.391). Notably, one patient (16.7%) in the open surgery group developed a clinically significant pancreatic fistula, whereas no such events occurred in the MIS group, although this difference was also not significant (p = 0.391). There were no anastomosis-related complications in either group. DISCUSSION

The increasing cases of RGC reflect broader trends in gastric cancer management, including improved survival rates after initial gastric resection. As surgical techniques advance and the patient population ages, it is anticipated that the incidence of RGC will continue to increase [16,17]. The present study contributes to the understanding of MIS for RGC, demonstrating that although technically challenging, MIS can provide substantial benefits over traditional open surgical approaches.

RGC presents unique challenges primarily because of the changes in anatomy resulting from previous surgeries, which often lead to significant adhesions. These factors complicate the surgical approach and can increase operative times [18]. The high incidence of complications related to adhesions, such as bowel obstruction or intra-abdominal infections, necessitates careful preoperative assessment and the use of advanced imaging techniques [19]. Several studies have suggested that the surgical approach should be tailored to the individual patient, considering factors such as prior surgical history, extent of the disease, and presence of comorbidities [20].

Although MIS techniques, including laparoscopic and robotic surgery, have been shown to reduce recovery times and postoperative complications in various surgical fields, their application to RGC remains limited [21]. The steep learning curve associated with these advanced MIS techniques further emphasizes the need for experienced surgical teams who are familiar with the nuances of RGC management [22].

The present findings indicate that, despite a longer operative time, MIS significantly reduces intraoperative blood loss and postoperative hospital stays. The median intraoperative blood loss of 15 mL in the MIS group was significantly lower than that in the open surgery group. This significant reduction in blood loss remained consistent even after PSM. This reduction is likely attributable to the magnified visual field characteristic of laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgeries, as well as the ability to perform precise surgical maneuvers using a variety of advanced surgical devices. Such minimization of blood loss is one of the key reasons why laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgeries are regarded as “minimally invasive procedures.”

Conversely, our findings demonstrated that the operative time was significantly longer in the MIS group than in the open surgery group, not only in the overall cohort but also in the propensity score-matched analysis. Several factors may account for this result. First, MIS, particularly in the context of RGC, is technically more demanding than conventional open surgery owing to the presence of adhesions, altered anatomy from previous gastrectomy, and limited working space. Second, the inherent complexity of RGC surgery—often requiring meticulous dissection around previous anastomoses and adhesions—further contributes to the prolonged operative time. Additionally, in robotic-assisted procedures, which comprised a portion of the MIS group, the number of robotic arms that can be utilized is typically fewer than the number of instruments available in open or even standard laparoscopic surgery. As these robotic procedures are frequently performed as solo surgeries without the aid of a surgical assistant, this limitation may further extend the operative duration. These factors collectively highlight the technical challenges and learning curve associated with performing MIS for RGC. Although no significant difference was observed in the overall complication rate and after propensity score-matched analysis, the incidence of postoperative pancreatic fistula was significantly lower in the MIS patients than in the open surgery patients. These advantages are crucial, particularly in the elderly population, where the risk of complications from larger surgical interventions is higher [23]. Additionally, MIS can minimize postoperative pain, leading to improved recovery trajectories. The MIS cohort comprised both laparoscopic and robotic procedures, which differ in technical characteristics and instrumentation. However, the limited number of cases precluded a meaningful comparison between these two approaches, and this heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting the results. Although an exploratory comparison between laparoscopic and robotic surgery was performed (Table 4), the small sample size precludes any definitive conclusions regarding superiority. In the literature, MIS has been shown to have comparable or superior outcomes in terms of short-term morbidity and recovery [24]. The reported benefits of MIS align with studies highlighting its role in enhancing patient quality of life. For instance, a meta-analysis of laparoscopic approaches for gastric cancer suggested that patients experienced faster return to normal activities and less postoperative discomfort [25]. Moreover, the cosmetic benefits of smaller incisions may also contribute to enhanced patient satisfaction. In addition to these advantages, advancements in imaging techniques and intraoperative navigation may further enhance the safety and efficacy of MIS for RGC, which are encouraging prospects for surgeons. These technologies can provide real-time insights into the anatomical changes caused by previous surgeries, potentially reducing the incidence of complications.

The ICG fluorescence method is a technique that captures near-infrared light emitted by ICG injected into the body. It has been applied to evaluate arterial blood flow in the gastrointestinal tract and liver, tumor localization in the liver, and lymphatic flow assessment [26,27]. This evaluation is also possible for MIS in technically demanding procedures such as completion gastrectomy. A notable aspect of our report is the extremely limited number of studies on robot-assisted surgery for RGC. Given the anticipated increase in the adoption of robot-assisted procedures in the future, our inclusion of treatment outcomes for robot-assisted surgery in this study is of significant value. Although intraoperative blood loss was estimated using a standardized institutional method, some degree of subjectivity is unavoidable, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting the results. Despite the promising short-term outcomes, the long-term efficacy of MIS in RGC is not yet well established, and ongoing research is needed to assess survival rates and recurrence patterns [28]. Recent studies have indicated that the long-term outcomes of RGC patients may vary significantly based on the initial surgical factors, tumor biology, and the timing of the secondary surgery [29]. Aoyama et al. [30] reported that MIS for RGC demonstrates favorable short and long-term outcomes compared with open surgery and may serve as a promising option for curative resection. Similarly, Umeki et al. [31] concluded that laparoscopic gastrectomy for RGC is a safe and feasible surgical approach, showing good short- and long-term prognoses. However, it is important to note that both studies focused solely on conventional laparoscopic procedures, and robot-assisted surgery was not included within their MIS groups. The present study incorporates cases of robot-assisted gastrectomy, albeit a limited number, which adds a novel dimension to the existing literature. Given the increasing adoption of robotic technology in gastrointestinal surgery, the present findings provide valuable insights into the role and potential advantages of robot-assisted surgery in the treatment of RGC. Future investigations should focus on a multicenter approach to gather comprehensive data regarding long-term survival and recurrence rates in patients undergoing robotic gastrectomy for RGC.

Future studies with MIS cohorts are needed for more definitive conclusions; however, increasing case numbers is inherently challenging given the rarity of RGC. To improve baseline comparability between the MIS and open surgery groups, we performed a propensity score-matched analysis to enable a more balanced comparison between the MIS group and the open surgery group. Although PSM reduces selection bias, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. In addition, because this study spanned an extended period during which surgical techniques, perioperative management, and surgical technology evolved substantially, most MIS procedures were performed in the later era. Therefore, improvements in perioperative outcomes may partly reflect temporal advancements rather than the surgical approach itself, and this potential era effect could not be completely eliminated. Moreover, the small MIS sample size limits the robustness of alternative adjustment methods, such as inverse probability of treatment weighting, which may be sensitive to extreme weights when applied to small treatment groups. Accordingly, the matched-cohort findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. In this context, the PSM analysis primarily provides preliminary insights into the feasibility and short-term outcomes of MIS for RGC rather than definitive evidence of superiority over open surgery. Despite these limitations, the matched comparison offers valuable initial information and underscores the need for larger multicenter studies to validate these observations.

ICG fluorescence imaging for intraoperative blood flow evaluation was used only in the MIS group and may have affected postoperative outcomes, particularly anastomosis-related complications. As the cohort spans cases from 1999 and ICG perfusion assessment was widely adopted only from the mid-2010s, its routine use was not feasible in earlier cases. This difference reflects the study’s long-time frame and retrospective design. Thus, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution and validated in future studies with standardized use of perfusion assessment. Additionally, the MIS findings should be regarded as preliminary rather than definitive evidence of superiority.

MIS, including robotic gastrectomy, appears to be a feasible and safe approach for RGC, and may provide advantages of reduced blood loss and shorter hospital stay, while maintaining postoperative pancreatic fistula rates comparable to those of open surgery. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these preliminary findings and provide more definitive evidence.
