The effect of ascorbic acid supplementation on plasma leptin and adiponectin levels: Systematic review of in vivo and in vitro studies
Authors: Mohammad Gholizadeh, Zahra Noushzadeh, Samira Movahed, Baharak Aghapour, Ahmadreza Kolahi, Parvin Mirmiran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeptin plays a key role in regulating energy balance and lipid reserves. Leptin has a broad spectrum of regulatory actions. Adiponectin, an adipokine released by adipocytes, is recognized as an important factor in maintaining balance of blood glucose levels, lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity. Recent studies have investigated the role of Ascorbic acid (AA) in the management of metabolic syndrome or the effect of high dose AA supplementation on serum AA, leptin and cortisol parameters.PurposeThe purpose of the current systematic review was to identify associations between AA supplementation and plasma leptin and adiponectin level in vivo and in vitro setting.MethodsComprehensive systematic search was performed in the PubMed/Medline, SCOPUS, and Web of Science databases up to 14 May 2026. A total of 948 studies were initially searched, 187 from PubMed, 204 from the Web of Science, and 557 from Scopus databases. After excluding the duplicates, the remaining 435 articles were screened by reviewing the title and abstract, and 411 unrelated articles were excluded. Of the remaining 24 articles, 9 articles met our inclusion/exclusion.ResultsThe results obtained from the in vivo studies show that AA supplementation positively influences the secretion of adiponectin hormone from adipose tissue cells. Moreover, in vivo studies reveal an inverse relationship between AA concentration and leptin secretion, suggesting that AA supplementation may reduce leptin levels. However, human studies present conflicting evidence regarding the effect of AA on leptin levels.ConclusionThis systematic review demonstrates a positive relationship between AA and adiponectin and inverse relationship with leptin.

Introduction

Obesity is characterized as a “low-grade inflammatory disease” caused by elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), and IL-6. As antioxidant enzyme activity and serum antioxidant levels decline in obese individuals, further research is being conducted to explore the impact of antioxidant vitamins on weight loss. Studies showed ascorbic acid (AA) could decrease systematic inflammation by blocking the CRP and TNF.1, 2 Throughout this review, 'ascorbic acid (AA)' refers to L-ascorbic acid and its physiologically active ascorbate form, synonymous with vitamin C in nutritional contexts. In addition, adipose tissue in obese individuals secretes substances such as adiponectin and leptin, which are related to improving insulin sensitivity, and control food intake and energy consumption, respectively.3 Leptin is a hormone mainly secreted by fat tissue and lower amounts from other tissues like the stomach, placenta and mammary gland. Leptin plays a key role in regulating energy balance and lipid reserves. Leptin has a broad spectrum of regulatory actions, including metabolic programming effects that development and function of the adipose organ that are related to its role in energy homeostasis4; Fig. 1. Adiponectin, an adipokine released by adipocytes, is recognized as an important factor in maintaining balance of blood glucose levels, lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity. This is achieved through its ability to reduce inflammation, and act as an anti-fibrotic and antioxidant. AA is a water-soluble non-enzymatic antioxidant that is biosynthetically derived from glucose and exhibits structural similarities, such as a shared carbon skeleton and functional groups (e.g., enediol), enabling competition for cellular transporters like GLUT1 and GLUT4.6 AA helps to eliminate reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, and contributes to the reduction oxidative stress both in vivo and in vitro settings.7, 8 AA is not produced naturally in the human body, so the diet is the only source of this essential nutrient.7 Reduced levels of antioxidants have been observed in obese individuals across different age groups globally. Lower concentrations of AA were inversely correlated with obesity and body fat mass.9 AA helps protect against oxidative stress, prevents non-enzymatic glycosylation of proteins, and improves arterial dilation through its impact on nitric oxide release.10

Recent studies have investigated the role of AA in the management of metabolic syndrome11 its potential effects on serum AA, leptin, and cortisol parameters.12 Emerging evidence suggests that AA may influence adipokine regulation through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, including the attenuation of oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators that affect adipocyte function.13 In addition, AA may modulate glucose transport pathways, which could indirectly alter leptin secretion.14 However, existing evidence remains fragmented across in vivo and in vitro studies, and no previous systematic review has comprehensively evaluated the association between AA supplementation and both leptin and adiponectin levels simultaneously. Therefore, the present systematic review aimed to synthesize the available evidence on the effects of AA supplementation on plasma leptin and adiponectin levels in in vivo and in vitro settings.

Method and materials

This systematic review was performed based on Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.15 PROSPERO registration number is CRD42024585557.

Search strategy

A comprehensive systematic search was performed in the PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science databases up to May 14, 2026. Studies were collected using the following search strategy: “vitamin C supplementation” OR “vitamin C” OR “ascorbic acid” OR “ascorbate” OR “VC” OR “Ascorbic Acid” AND Adiponectin OR ACDC OR ADPN OR APM1 OR APM-1 OR GBP28 OR ACRP30 OR ADIPOQ OR adiponectin OR “Obese Protein” OR “Obese Gene Product” OR “Adipocyte Complement-Related Protein 30-kDa” OR “ACRP30 Protein” OR “Adipose Most Abundant Gene Transcript 1” OR adipokine OR adipocytokine OR Adipokines OR “Leptin” OR “LEP” OR “leptin” OR “ob gene product” OR “ob protein” OR “obese gene product” OR “obese proteinsis”.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria for selection of the studies

All randomized controlled trials with in-vitro and in-vivo design, investigating the effect of AA supplementation on substances including leptin and adiponectin were included. Observational studies (cross-sectional, case-control, and cohort studies), reviews, letters, conference abstracts or case-reports, supplementation combined with another ingredient, and studies with inadequate information were excluded. Moreover, the references of included studies and related review were evaluated (Ref-to-ref search) to avoid missing eligible studies. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus between the two reviewers; if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (MG or ZN) made the final decision.

Data extraction

After meeting the inclusion criteria, data extraction from primary articles was performed independently by two reviewers (MG and BA), and following information was abstracted: 1) author’s name, 2) year of publication and the study location, 3) gender, 4) mean age, 5) duration of intervention, dosage of AA supplementation and baseline of plasma AA, 6) study participants in intervention and control groups, 7) measurement location; and 8) mean ± standard deviation and/or changes of the outcomes including leptin and adiponectin measures before and after intervention in both intervention and placebo groups.

Quality assessment

Cochrane risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool was used to evaluate randomized controlled trials (RCTs). This assessment considered the following domains: randomization process (random allocation and allocation concealment), baseline characteristics similarity, awareness of participants and researchers regarding intervention assignment, deviations from intended interventions, availability of outcome data, handling of missing data, awareness of outcome assessors, objectivity of outcome measurements, and the selection of reported results. Each domain was rated as low, moderate, high, or unclear risk of bias. An overall risk of bias was assigned based on the collective evaluation of these domains.

QUIN quality assessment

For in vitro and experimental studies, the QUIN Quality Assessment framework was utilized. This tool evaluated aspects such as the clarity of the research question, description of experimental conditions, control adequacy, detailed reporting of cell lines and culture conditions, treatment descriptions, measurement method validation, use of appropriate statistical methods, presentation of results, discussion quality, and the reporting of conflicts of interest. Each domain was rated, and a total quality score out of 11 was calculated to provide a comprehensive measure of study quality.

Results

Search results and characteristics of studies

The systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus yielded 948 studies (187, 204, and 557, respectively). After removing duplicates, 435 articles underwent title and abstract screening, of which 411 unrelated studies were excluded. Full-text reviews of 24 articles identified 9 eligible studies; 15 were excluded due to combined vitamin C supplementation (n = 4), unreported leptin/adiponectin outcomes (n = 9), or observational designs (n = 2) (Fig. 3). The studies comprised 3 human randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (157 participants; mixed-sex cohorts) and 6 in vitro experiments (Table 1). Characteristics of reviewed in vivo studies.

In vivo studies

Results from in vitro studies demonstrated that AA supplementation positively influences adiponectin secretion from adipose tissue cells. For example, Roes et al. [1] supplemented human Simpson–Golabi–Behmel syndrome (SGBS) adipocytes with AA and found that it significantly increased the secretion of high-molecular-weight (HMW) adiponectin (1.7-fold) without altering total adiponectin secretion or adiponectin expression. Similarly, Gu et al. [2] reported that AA significantly enhanced the secretion of total and HMW adiponectin from tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)-treated HepG2 cells. Furthermore, Lou et al. [3] observed increased HMW adiponectin secretion following AA supplementation in primary human preadipocytes and adipocytes treated with pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α and palmitic acid (PA) to simulate an obesogenic environment.

Effects on leptin gene expression and secretion

The impact of AA supplementation on leptin gene expression and secretion from adipose tissue cells was assessed in three in vitro studies, with varying results. Garcia-Diaz et al. [4] analyzed the effects of AA supplementation on epididymal primary rat adipocytes and observed a concentration-dependent decrease in leptin secretion, particularly in insulin-stimulated cells. However, another study by Garcia-Diaz et al. [5] found no effect of AA supplementation on leptin mRNA expression or secretion in 3T3-L1 mouse preadipocytes. The effects of dehydroascorbic acid (DHAA) supplementation on leptin resistance were also investigated. Thon et al. [6] studied DHAA in human neuroblastoma (SH-SY5Y) cells stably transfected with the long form of the leptin receptor (SH-SY5Y-ObRb). They reported that DHAA upregulated ER stress-related genes and inhibited leptin-induced STAT3 phosphorylation (Table 1).

In vitro studies

The findings from human studies on the effect of AA supplementation on leptin levels are inconsistent. In a study by Mohammadi et al. [7], individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) were given a daily supplement of 500 mg AA for eight weeks. Compared with the placebo group, the AA group showed a significant increase in leptin levels, even after adjusting for age and body mass index (BMI). However, another study found contradictory results, reporting that oral administration of 1000 mg/day of AA for four weeks reduced leptin levels in patients with T2DM [8]. The effect of AA supplementation on serum adiponectin levels was investigated by Johnston et al. [9]. In this study, 20 obese men and women followed an energy-restricted diet low in AA for eight weeks and were randomly assigned to receive either a placebo or 500 mg/day of AA. Plasma adiponectin levels increased by 13% from baseline in both groups by week 8. Quality assessment

Cochrane risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias evaluation for the included randomized controlled trials revealed a generally low risk of bias across most domains. For example, Johnston (2007) demonstrated low risk across all assessed domains, except for allocation concealment, which was categorized as ``unknown risk.'' Similarly, Mohammadi (2023) showed a low risk for all domains except allocation concealment, which was also rated as ``unknown risk.'' In contrast, Ashworth (2019) exhibited moderate risk for the domain assessing participant awareness and an ``unknown risk'' for researcher awareness. These findings suggest that while most studies were robust in design, certain areas, particularly allocation concealment and researcher awareness, could be improved.

QUIN quality assessment

The QUIN Quality Assessment revealed that the included in vitro and experimental studies were of high quality, with total scores ranging from 8 to 11. Notably, Xinqian Gu (2019) achieved a perfect score of 11, reflecting low risk across all domains. Other studies, such as those by Xiaoqin Luo (2022) and Garcia-Diaz (2011, 2010), achieved scores of 10, demonstrating minor limitations in specific areas, such as variability in results and presentation. In contrast, Thon (2016) scored 8, primarily due to issues related to replicates and results presentation. Overall, the assessments indicate that the studies generally adhered to rigorous experimental and reporting standards.

Discussion

This systematic review evaluates the relationship between AA supplementation and the plasma levels of leptin and adiponectin. Findings from in vivo studies indicate a positive correlation between AA administration and the secretion of the adiponectin hormone from adipose tissue cells. In human trials, AA supplementation was found to elevate plasma adiponectin’s levels; in addition, it wasn’t observed to have a significant efficacy on the circulating levels of adiponectin. Because only one study has investigated this aspect, conclusive results remain limited, and further research is required. Moreover, in vivo studies reveal an inverse relationship between AA concentration and leptin secretion, suggesting that AA supplementation may reduce leptin levels. However, human studies present conflicting evidence regarding the effect of AA on leptin levels. Specifically, a group receiving 500 mg of vitamin C showed a significant increase in leptin levels, while a dosage of 1000 mg resulted in decreased leptin levels. Therefore, while supplementation at doses of 1,000 mg and above may positively influence leptin reduction, the limited number of studies in this domain precludes definitive conclusions, highlighting the need for additional research.

Leptin is a peptide hormone synthesized by adipose tissue and is encoded by the obese (ob) gene and it functions by binding to leptin receptors (LR) located on the surface of various cells.16 The primary action of leptin occurs in the brain, where it plays a crucial role in regulating appetite by activating satiety centers, potentially mitigating obesity.17 The principal signaling mechanism associated with LR is the JAK-STAT pathway. Leptin binds to the OB-R receptor, a type I cytokine receptor that lacks autophosphorylation and intrinsic kinase activity, requiring JAK as an auxiliary kinase to initiate signaling. Upon binding leptin, JAKs activate and phosphorylate each other and the receptor's intracellular tail, creating docking sites for STAT proteins. This process activates the JAK/STAT pathway, including protein tyrosine kinase and MAPK pathways, allowing STATs to interact with DNA and regulate gene expression. As adipose tissue diminishes, the production of leptin decreases, leading to reduced levels crossing the blood-brain barrier.16, 18 The central nervous system (CNS) perceives this decline in leptin as an indication of energy deficiency, initiating a series of physiological responses aimed at managing the stress associated with starvation.16 Conversely, as food consumption rises and adipose tissue levels increase, there is a corresponding elevation in leptin production and its release into the bloodstream.16 This rise in leptin levels inhibits the body's starvation response, thereby facilitating decreased food intake and enhanced energy expenditure to address the existing energy surplus.16 Neurons located in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) that express LepRb exhibit a direct response to leptin, leading to a reduction in feeding.19 Adiponectin is a protein specifically secreted by adipose tissue, with its mRNA being highly expressed in adipocytes.20 Interestingly, despite its secretion from adipose tissue, the levels of adiponectin in circulation tend to decline as central adiposity increases.20 Conversely, elevated levels of adiponectin are observed in individuals with greater amounts of adiposity in the lower extremities and trunk.20 Furthermore, adiponectin facilitates the uptake and oxidation of fatty acids in skeletal muscle by activating AMPK and PPARα, which serve as critical regulators in the management of energy metabolism21 which leads to inhibition of acetyl-CoA carboxylase, lowering malonyl-CoA levels and allowing fatty acids to enter mitochondria for β-oxidation. This promotes fat breakdown, prevents fat buildup in muscle and liver, and helps improve insulin sensitivity.22

The Influence of AA on Glucose Metabolism and Leptin Suppression Research indicates that AA competes with glucose for transport via GLUT 1-3-4 carriers.1 A study conducted on mice models demonstrated alterations in the IRS-1/JNK signaling pathways1 also suggests that AA exerts an inhibitory effect on leptin secretion, and its supplementation is associated with modest weight reduction.1 Obesity appears to significantly impact the body's AA levels. Individuals weighing over 91 kg exhibit markedly lower plasma concentrations of AA compared to those weighing less than 72 kg. Additionally, plasma AA levels have been shown to have an inverse relationship with both body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference in adult populations.23

The research conducted by Fischer et al. demonstrated that the oral intake of alpha-tocopherol or AA can lead to a reduction in serum leptin levels among individuals with diabetes.17 Additionally, Vincent et al. found that a regimen of daily antioxidants (comprising 800 IU of vitamin E, 500 mg of AA, and 10 mg of beta-carotene over a period of eight weeks) resulted in elevated plasma adiponectin levels in overweight young adults which this increase was linked to a decrease in oxidative stress and the inflammatory cytokine IL-6.24 Furthermore, a study involving 118 sedentary, nonsmoking adults revealed an inverse relationship between plasma AA levels and markers of adiposity, particularly among women; however, AA supplementation did not affect the circulating levels of adiponectin.21 Garcia et al. documented notable impacts of insulin and AA administration on leptin secretion. They found that escalating levels of AA led to a dose-dependent reduction in both basal and insulin-induced leptin secretion. Conversely, regarding adiponectin, although a significant overall effect was identified, no discernible differences were noted among the various experimental groups.2 In a cross-sectional study conducted by Garcia et al. in 2012, it was found that among women with obesity, higher levels of leptin were correlated with reduced concentrations of AA when analyzed according to BMI, percentage of body fat, and waist circumference.25 Additionally, a 2010 animal study indicated that AA led to a significant, concentration-dependent reduction in leptin secretion, particularly in cells stimulated by insulin.2 Furthermore, an eight-week randomized controlled trial investigating AA supplementation in patients with type 2 diabetes revealed a notable increase in leptin levels within the AA group.26

A notable finding of this review is the discrepancy between experimental and clinical evidence. In vitro studies consistently showed beneficial effects of AA on adiponectin secretion and, in some models, reduced leptin production. These findings may reflect tightly controlled experimental conditions in which oxidative and inflammatory pathways are directly manipulated. In contrast, human studies demonstrated inconsistent effects, particularly for leptin, likely due to substantial heterogeneity in participant characteristics, baseline metabolic status, supplementation dose, intervention duration, and coexisting dietary factors. Patients with obesity or type 2 diabetes may also exhibit altered vitamin C metabolism and adipokine responsiveness, which may partly explain the inconsistent clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the number of eligible studies was limited, particularly clinical trials in humans, which restricts the generalizability of the conclusions. Second, substantial methodological heterogeneity existed across studies in terms of experimental models, AA dosage, duration of intervention, and outcome assessment methods. Third, combining in vitro and human studies in a single qualitative synthesis may introduce challenges in directly comparing mechanistic and clinical outcomes. Finally, due to the limited number and variability of studies, quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible, and the conclusions are based solely on qualitative evidence.

Conclusion

In in vivo studies presented that there is a positive association between AA treatment and the secretion of adiponectin hormones and in a human study vitamin C supplementation increased Plasma adiponectin. In in vivo studies, vitamin C supplementation decreases the leptin level and in human studies the impact of vitamin C on leptin levels are conflicting. Due to the limitation in the number of studies conducted in human studies, we cannot draw a definite conclusion, and more studies are needed.
