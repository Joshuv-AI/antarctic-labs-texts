Effects of a 4-week neuromuscular balance training program on knee proprioception, functional performance, and landing errors in national-level female wrestlers: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ebrar Atak, Erdi Kayabınar, Büşra Kayabınar, Zeynep İclal Sağ, İbrahim Şahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuromuscular and balance-based training programs have been shown to reduce lower extremity injury risk by improving biomechanical and sensorimotor control. However, randomized controlled trials examining the effects of short-term interventions on landing mechanics (LESS-RT) and related sensorimotor outcomes in national-level female wrestlers are limited.MethodsThis parallel-group randomized controlled trial was conducted in a supervised training environment integrated with regular wrestling practice. Twenty-two female wrestlers were randomly assigned to an intervention group (n = 11) or a control group (n = 11). The intervention consisted of a 4-week neuromuscular balance-based training program. Landing mechanics were assessed using LESS-RT. Secondary outcomes included knee proprioception via joint position reproduction, force sense error using a Pressure Biofeedback Unit, static balance (Single-Leg Stance Test), and functional performance (Single-Leg Hop Test Battery).ResultsThe intervention group demonstrated significant improvements in landing mechanics, with LESS-RT decreasing from 13.54 ± 1.29 to 9.18 ± 0.98 (p < < 0.001). Improvements were also observed in knee proprioception, static balance, and single-leg functional performance. Between-group comparisons favoured the intervention group across all outcome measures.ConclusionA short-term neuromuscular balance training program leads to meaningful improvements in landing mechanics and associated sensorimotor functions, suggesting potential for reducing knee injury risk in female wrestlers.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT07504666, registered on March 18, 2026.

Introduction

Wrestling is a contact sport that demands elevated amounts of muscle strength, endurance, agility and coordination [1]. Competitive wrestling practiced within official sport settings has a high injury incidence, and knee ligament injuries, particularly anterior cruciate ligament lesions, constitute a significant clinical burden [2]. Over the past 10 years, there has been a substantial increase in female athlete participation for this sport; therefore, training volume, competition frequency, and contact intensity have also increased [3, 4]. The athlete-exposure (AE) concept has been widely used by many authors in their descriptive epidemiological analyses of sports-related injuries and is a common measure of exposure expressed as an individual athlete taking part in 1 training session or competition, allowing to make comparisons between different types of sports. Existing data indicate that wrestling injury incidence has been approximated at between 16.3 and 69.5 per 1000 AE based on the study design, and this rate is greater than many contact sports [5, 6].

Imaging-based studies at the Olympic level show that those with injury to ligament tissues around knee joint are most common in wrestling, and anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and medial collateral ligament (MCL) injuries are some of the most commonly noted pathologies [6]. Thus, physical features including lower limb alignment, intercondylar notch geometry and anatomical laxity; in addition to variations in sex hormones and neuromuscular control, contribute a multidimensional susceptibility profile that predisposes female athletes to knee injury [7–10]. Some of the main mechanisms that increase ligament loading can be recognized as biomechanical patterns like dynamic knee valgus, lack of trunk control, and failure to apply proper landing strategies [11, 12]. In that sense, neuromuscular and balance-based training strategies may decrease injury susceptibility through enhanced proprioceptive sensitivity and postural control as well as better landing mechanics; A more than 50% reduction in injury incidents has been observed in certain sports. The direct evidence regarding the effectiveness of ACL prevention programs in wrestling and other combat sports is still nascent, but core components from established neuromuscular programs (e.g., balance, proprioception/trunk control/hip control/lower-extremity alignment/landing technique) generally addressing similar modifiable factors are relevant to wrestling [3, 13–15]. Similar to wrestling, judo, Greco-Roman wrestling and taekwondo athletes have reported functional improvements from a short-term balance and neuromuscular intervention [1, 4, 16–18] with common tasks in wrestling including continuous single-leg support, rapid directional changes reacted to through balance perturbations or induced by the sport (i.e. high knee-loading demands). Consequently, while biomechanics and clinical reasoning suggest the practical utility of such strategies to female wrestlers, the impact on this population awaits investigation.

However, there is limited evidence on randomized controlled trials that assess the effects of modifiable short-term neuromuscular-balance-focused interventions and their relationship with Landing Error Scoring System–Real Time (LESS-RT), proprioception, force perception, balance and indices of functional performance in national-level female wrestlers.

Evidence from short-term balance, proprioceptive and neuromuscular training studies in combat and other high-demand pivoting sports suggests that sensorimotor and postural-control adaptations can occur within a brief training period of time. A study conducted in young judoist showed that a four-week supervised slackline-based balance training program added to conventional judo practice improved postural control and jumping performance [16]. Likewise, a four-week core-specific training program was successfully incorporated into the normal training regimen of junior Greco-Roman wrestlers and enhanced wrestling-specific performance measures [17]. Proprioceptive training improves balance ability in taekwondo athletes with a 6-week proprioceptive training program [18] or a 6-week neuromuscular exercise program shown to enhance static and dynamic postural stability as well as functional movement outcomes in female taekwondo athletes. In female handball players, significant improvements in balance after six weeks of training with a neuromuscular warm-up program were reported [19]. Consequently, short-term neuromuscular and proprioceptive protocols seem appropriate tools to assess immediate sensorimotor changes in athletic populations subjected to high lower-extremity loading demands.

The Single-Leg Hop Test Battery similarly provides a functional measure of unilateral lower-extremity performance in athletic populations. Hop tests, therefore, require a combination of explosive power and dynamic balance as well as neuromuscular coordination involving propulsion, landing control, and postural stability during sport-like tasks — not only lower-extremity strength. There have been many previous studies which revealed that sprint and agility were found to be associated with horizontal single-leg hop tests in female athletes, confirming their relevance as measures of functional neuromuscular performance [20]. Single-leg hop tests and limb symmetry index values have been used to assess functional lower-extremity performance and bilateral symmetry in the combat-sport population, specifically young elite judokas [21]. Moreover, performance-based measures like the single-leg hop for distance have been described as clinical and sport-specific evaluations of lower-extremity function [22] whereas repeated hop tasks like the triple hop may provide emergent information because they require successive unilateral propulsion and landing control [23]. The investigation of the single leg hop performance in the current study allows for multi-faceted assessment of functional neuromuscular adaptations in high-level female wrestlers.

The aim of this study was to provide quantitative evidence to the field-based screening and intervention literature by examining the effects of a neuromuscular- and balance-based exercise program on national female wrestlers, primarily in terms of LESS-RT score, as well as associated sensorimotor and functional risk markers.

We hypothesized that the four-week neuromuscular balance-based training program would produce greater improvements in landing mechanics, knee proprioception, force sense accuracy, static balance, and single-leg hop performance compared with regular wrestling training alone.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a two-arm, parallel-group, pretest–posttest randomized controlled intervention trial performed to assess the efficacy of a neuromuscular–balance-based exercise program on landing mechanics and sensorimotor performance in national-level female wrestlers.

The main outcome measure was the LESS-RT total score. Secondary outcomes were defined as Joint Position Sense (JPS), Force Sense Error Test (FSET), Single Leg Stance Test (SLST) and the Single-Leg Hop Test battery (SLHT).

The study adhered to the principles of Declaration of Helsinki and was granted ethical approval (Decision No: 2025/221) by Yalova University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee. Informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from the participants. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07504666). Registered on March 18, 2026.

Participants

Using LESS score data from a previous study in female athletes, an a priori power analysis was calculated using G*Power software. Assuming a medium effect size (0.57), α of 0.05, and a power of 80%, at least 26 participants (13 in each group) were needed [24]. We aimed to recruit approximately 20% more participants than the calculated sample size, in order to allow for potential attrition, based on average dropout rates obtained from randomized controlled exercise studies.

Accordingly, the study was voluntarily participated by 32 national-level female wrestlers aged between 16 and 23 years with minimum five years of licensed wrestling experience and routine training practice. National-level wrestlers described athletes who competed at the national level and were participating in official events within two seasons prior. Athletes were included if they had at least 5 years of licensed wrestling experience, were currently participating in regular training, had not undergone knee surgery or sustained a major lower-extremity injury during the previous 6 months, had no acute pain or injury affecting test performance, were available to attend the assessment and exercise sessions on a regular basis, and provided consent to complete all follow-up evaluations. Baseline characteristics included smoking status and previous injury history. Smoking status was recorded because cigarette smoking has been associated with exercise-related musculoskeletal injury and may impair tissue healing through adverse effects on bone healing, tissue repair, fibroblast activity, and immune responses [25].

Computer-assisted block randomization was used to assign participants into an intervention (n = 16) and control (n = 16) groups. Randomization was conducted by an independent investigator and group assignments were concealed in opaque, sealed envelopes. Of the 32 randomized national-level female wrestlers, 22 (intervention group: n = 11; control group: n = 11) completed the study. Reasons for withdrawal included lack of adherence to the exercise protocol during the intervention phase, refusal to attend the follow-up evaluation, sports injury during the follow-up phase, inability to participate in the follow-up evaluation and illness. The groups did not differ significantly in regard to completion rates. Participant flow is reported in the CONSORT flowchart (Fig. 1). Participant recruitment and data collection were conducted between June 2025 and September 2025.

Blinding

As participants could not remain blinded to group allocation due to the characteristics of the exercise intervention (as well as the physiotherapist supervising training sessions), no blinding took place for either participants or researchers. Outcome assessors and statistical analysts remained masked throughout the trial. Video recordings were evaluated by five physiotherapists responsible for LESS-RT scoring, who graded the video independently regarding participants’ group assignments. Furthermore, to mitigate assessment and analysis bias, all statistical analyses were performed using coded group data.

Assessment protocol

Conversely, all measurements were performed under standard environmental conditions and using the same setup. Pre-test measures were applied the morning after a day when the athletes had not trained. The post-tests were conducted the day after the first off-day, after completing four weeks of intervention (12 sessions in total), at the same time and using the same protocol.

Joint Position Sense (JPS) test

The active repositioning method was used to assess the positional aspect of knee proprioception. Eye-closed sitting position was used for testing participants. The knee joint was passively moved from the starting position to target flexion angle, that position held for 5 s, and then returning to starting position. The angle was actively reproduced by the participant. The absolute angular error (°) was calculated as the difference between the target angle and the reproduced angle and used in the analyses [26].

Force Sense Error Test (FSET)

Force sense was assessed using ipsilateral remembered method. Participants were evaluated seated; a pressure biofeedback unit (Stabilizer™, Chattanooga) was positioned under the knee and baseline pressure adjusted to 20 mmHg. The highest pressure value reached during maximal isometric quadriceps contraction was determined and 50% and 65% of it were calculated as the target pressure. All subjects were asked to close their eyes and reproduce the target pressure values, with the absolute difference between the target and produced pressure used as an error value. In the analyses, the average of multiple repetitions was used [27].

Single Leg Stance Test (SLST)

Static balance performance was evaluated barefoot in a single-leg stance on the dominant and non-dominant extremities. Measurements were taken based on two conditions: eyes open and eyes closed, the time until first loss of balance was measured with a stopwatch and maximum time was limited to 60 s. Averages of three repetitions for each condition were calculated [28]. The individual composite SLST score was consequently computed as the arithmetic mean of individual average eye-open and eye-closed times, in order to normalize variability between sensory conditions and obtain a holistic assessment of postural control.

Single-Leg Hop Test Battery (SLHT)

For the lower-extremity functional performance, a Single-Leg Hop Test Battery including single hop for distance, triple hop for distance, crossover triple hop for distance and 6 m timed hop tests with previously validated protocols were bilaterally used [20, 21, 29]. Single Hop for Distance: Participants performed one maximal forward hop on the tested limb and landed back on that same limb. In the triple hop for distance, participants completed three maximal forward hops sequentially on the same limb, and the total distance hopped was recorded. Crossover triple hop for distance, participants performed three consecutive forward hops while crossing over a marked area (15-cm-wide) in alternating legs and the total distance covered was measured. In the 6 m timed hop participants hopped as fast as possible along a straight, flat surface on a previously injured limb (provided they had one) over a distance of 6 m.

Prior to the recorded assessment, each participant completed one familiarization trial for each test. Next, a recorded performance trial was performed for each lower extremity. The distances for the single, triple and crossover triple hop were expressed in centimeters (cm) while time to complete the 6 m timed hop was measured in seconds (s). For the statistical analyses, they used the result from the recorded performance trial.

Landing Error Scoring System - Real Time (LESS-RT)

The Turkish-validated real-time version of the Landing Error Scoring System (LESS-RT), developed by Padua et al., was used to assess landing mechanics in relation to lower extremity injury. and whose original validity study was published in 2009 [29–31] Participants dropped from a 30 cm high platform, landing at half their height (approximately), followed by an immediate vertical jump of maximal effort. There were three valid trials collected for each subject. Five independent physiotherapists blind to group allocation analyzed images acquired with 2 digital cameras (frontal and sagittal planes). Before the measurement, the evaluators conducted a brief calibration session.

LESS-RT is comprised of 17 error criteria concerning lower extremity alignment, trunk control, symmetry and landing quality. In the case of the first 15 criteria, if an error was observed in at least two out of three trials, 1 point was awarded. For the last two criteria, landing quality was classified as soft, moderate or stiff; the former earned 1 point and the latter 2 points. Then we summed the points of all items to calculate the total score. The higher the score means worse landing mechanics and the increased injury risk [29].

Exercise intervention

The intervention group performed a neuromuscular-balance-based program based on knee joint stabilization, proprioceptive integration and landing control 3 days/week for four weeks during approximately 30 min/session. The four-week period was chosen in an effort to create a concise, realistic and real-world implementable training block that could be added in largely un-altered form into athletes’ regular wrestling practice without removing much from their programming. This selection was based on prior short-duration research indicating that combat-sport settings such as judo and wrestling are susceptible to four-week training blocks, and reviewing the literature regarding balance-proprioceptive-neuromuscular interventions indicated that four- to six-week functional balance postural control and performance changes could be produced [16–19]. Therefore, the current time period was deemed suitable for investigating early sensorimotor and motor-control adaptations (e.g., proprioceptive accuracy, postural stability, force sense, landing-control strategies) rather than hypertrophic or direct injury-incidence outcomes.

The program content was based on protocols with demonstrated effectiveness [14, 15, 32, 33]. Intervention consisted of eight exercises targeting lower extremity strength, proprioception, balance, and landing control, including single-leg stance, mini squat, dynamic lunge, lateral step movements, wall sit, and plyometric tasks (drop jump, single-leg hop, and double-leg broad jump). Exercises were performed in 2 sets of 5 repetitions or 30–60 s per set, depending on the exercise type, with emphasis on controlled movement and proper knee alignment.

We adapted the FIFA 11+ “Strength & Balance” module to include single-leg stance and squat exercises performed on a balance pad [32]. The static and dynamic balance exercises were made with eyes closed and integrated Knäkontroll method to improve proprioceptive and vestibular feedbacks [34, 35]. Open- and closed-kinetic-chain exercises were provided using TheraBand resistance promoting quadriceps–hamstring co-activation and frontal plane stability, in accordance with the PEP (Prevent Injury and Enhance Performance) protocol [14]. Landing, and lateral movements mini-trampoline exercises were modified from Myklebust protocol [33]. Low-level reactive balance components was also added to the program [34].

All exercises were explained in detail (individually) with demonstration by a physiotherapist prior to the intervention. Patients practiced the exercises under the supervision of a physiotherapist until correct performance was verified. To aid in proper execution, an illustrated exercise booklet was subsequently provided. Thereafter, participants completed the program on their own and adherence was assessed via regular telephone follow-up to ensure they had carried out the prescribed exercise sessions.

Control group

The control group performed regular wrestling training without additional intervention. Timing of testing and conditions during assessment were the same as for the intervention group.

Adverse events and harms

Participants were monitored throughout the intervention period for any adverse events, injuries, pain exacerbations, or exercise-related complications. Attendance and tolerance to the exercise sessions were also recorded. No adverse events or intervention-related injuries were reported during the study period.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of continuous variables was evaluated by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuos normally distributed data were shown as mean ± standard deviation and non–normally distributed data were presented as median (minimum–maximum).

Within each group, differences between pre-test (BT) and post-test (AT) measures were assessed using paired samples t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as appropriate. Comparisons between groups were performed using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for BT and AT values. Furthermore, change scores (Δ = AT − BT) used to compare between group were computed using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests.

The prespecified primary end point was the total LESS-RT score. Secondary outcome analyses were deemed exploratory. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Only participants who completed post-intervention assessments were included in the final analyses; no imputation procedure was performed for missing data.

Results

The mean age of the Athletes included in the study was 19.81 ± 1.83 years for intervention group and 19.18 ± 1.66 years for control group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (all p > 0.05) regarding these variables: age, body mass index, and wrestling experience. Limb dominance distribution was symmetrical across groups, with a right-sided predominance. None of the participants smoked, and there were no differences in past injuries. Discussion

The current study showed that a 4-week neuromuscular balance intervention was effective in inducing significant improvements in landing mechanics, as measured by reduced LESS-RT, and marked and maintained improvements in knee proprioception, static balance, and functional performance parameters among national-level female wrestlers. The significant decline in LESS-RT scores also underscores that maladaptive biomechanical patterns are modifiable even after a relatively brief intervention, reflecting retained sensorimotor plasticity among highly trained athletes.

Overall, the findings of the current investigation align with existing literature reporting on the effects of neuromuscular interventions on injury-related outcomes in a female athlete population. Structured neuromuscular training demonstrates a significantly decreased risk of anterior cruciate ligament injuries, as assessed in the meta-analysis by Sugimoto et al. [13]. Directly monitoring injury incidence was outside the scope of the present study; however, improved proprioceptive accuracy and decreased LESS-RT error scores suggest that biomechanical and sensorimotor markers related to injury risk can be modified. Such a perspective is further corroborated by reports highlighting the usefulness of field-based screening tests for tracking risk profiles [36–38].

Content for the intervention was derived from core components of FIFA 11+, Knäkontroll, and PEP protocols consisting of dynamic balance, hip stabilization, plyometric control, and motor learning processes [14, 15, 32, 33]. Accordingly, the multi-component intervention used here addressed balance, strength and landing control simultaneously. As reported by Su et al. (2025) have shown that excitability of different sensorimotor systems can be tested simultaneously, and training multiple physiologic pathways can induce more robust adaptations than training a single-axis intervention [3].

The propensity of female athletes to suffer knee injuries is well described, being attributed to the interplay of anatomical, hormonal and neuromuscular factors [8, 10]. Furthermore, it has been reported that functional recovery was more inconsistent in females than males after surgery [39]. More recently, it has been suggested that the relationship between phases of the menstrual cycle and laxity of connective tissues may potentially contribute to mechanical stability during various periods [7]. Although the present study did not standardize menstrual cycle phase and therefore took individual variation in biological fluctuations into account as a possible confound that cannot be entirely removed from the interpretation of improving parameters, our findings of post-intervention sensorimotor improvements suggest that neuromuscular control encompasses a component that can be trained above and beyond biological predictors. Studies highlighting the contribution of access to training environments, load consistency and developmental opportunities on conditioned systems have further substantiated this view [4, 10].

Improvements in proprioceptive performance can be explained by the combined contribution of more efficient integration of peripheral afferent input and adaptations in central motor planning processes. More precise integration of sensory inputs from joint capsule, muscle-tendon, and skin receptors can improve the accuracy of joint position perception. Exercise surroundings in which visual feedback is constrained, and postural challenges are elevated might facilitate more economical error-correction strategies. Similar neuromuscular remodelling, described in athletes following ACL reconstruction, provides evidence that the sensorimotor system is amenable to intervention across clinical contexts [40].

Observations made regarding landing mechanics support the injury model proposed by Hewett et al. (2005), stressing frontal plane control of the knee and stability of the trunk, and are in line with modern biomechanical constructs that emphasize the interaction between neuromuscular control and loading patterns [12, 41]. Interventions directed at the coordinated activation of the hip musculature, hamstrings, and trunk stabilizers could improve proximal control leading to improved loading patterns transferred to the knee joint. Furthermore, this supports our mechanism with empirical evidence that the addition of strengthening, feedback and coordinative control components improves effectiveness [13].

The observed improvements in static balance and functional performance are likely due to coordinative reorganization rather than muscular hypertrophy, given the short duration of the intervention. Research highlighting task specificity in motor adaptation supports the view that these gains reflect a targeted reorganization of responses to specific sensorimotor demands, rather than a broad improvement in overall performance [42]. Additionally, studies have shown that programs designed to reduce injury risk markers can be implemented without compromising performance in elite athletes across various sports [43].

LESS-RT is preferred due to its greater feasibility for field implementation and its superior capacity for standardization. The reliability and validity of its Turkish version have been established [31], and previous studies have reported high inter-rater dependability [44]. Furthermore, the concurrent administration of additional balance and hop tests with LESS-based assessments increases sensitivity to various sensorimotor components [36, 38]. Moreover, the predictive value of LESS-RT scores for injury likelihood in university-level female athletes provides additional clinical context for the observed improvements [37].

Pragmatically, the recommended 4-week neuromuscular balance program can be easily incorporated into regular wrestling pre-competition warm-up routines, using minimal equipment such as balance pad and TheraBand, and taking between 30 min to complete per session. This is consistent with current approaches to injury prevention that emphasise feasibility and require coach-led implementation without interfering with sport-specific training [45]. Consequently, sports rehabilitation professionals and coaches may want to integrate this protocol into strength training programs as a time-efficient method of addressing sensorimotor and biomechanical knee injury risk markers in female wrestlers.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. While the homogeneous group of 22 national-level athletes strengthens the internal validity, its generalizability is limited to other performance levels and different disciplines, as well as other age groups. The intervention was carried out for four weeks (12 sessions), and the program was independently continued by athletes, which potentially limited the implementation of essential motor learning aspects such as disturbance and verbal reinforcement. This might, in turn, restrict the possibility of reaching conclusions about long-term retention of the noted adaptations. In addition, because the control group did not perform a time-matched alternative activity, the intervention group had a greater total training volume. This difference may have contributed to the observed between-group effects and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Injury incidence was not directly recorded, and risk profiles were indirectly assessed through functional screening tests and performance-based measures. As such, the results should not be interpreted as a decrease in the incidence of injury but rather, changes to markers linked to the risk of injury. In addition, the measurement battery was restricted to functional tests without assessments of muscle strength, electromyographic activity, joint moments or three-dimensional kinematic analyses. As a consequence, the underlying neurophysiological and biomechanical mechanisms could not be directly investigated.

The menstrual cycle phase has lacked standardization, limiting the ability to isolate hormonal fluctuations on neuromuscular responses. Further, while two-dimensional video-based landing analysis can again offer practical advantages in field scenarios, this approach has an inherent dimensional constraint and hence less spatial accuracy versus a three-dimensional motion analysis system. Lastly, the testing protocols were administered under controlled conditions and may not be representative of wrestling-specific factors (i.e., contact, reactive perturbations, and competition dynamics).

Conclusion

A short-term neuromuscular and balance-based intervention significantly improved knee proprioception, postural stability, functional performance, and landing mechanics in national-level female wrestlers. A particularly notable finding was a significant decrease in LESS-RT scores, indicating that faulty landing patterns can be improved.

These results indicate that a structured neuromuscular training protocol can improve biomechanical and functional performance parameters related to mechanical loading patterns at the knee joint. In this sense, the proposed intervention model allows for creating a structure that can be incorporated into preventive physiotherapeutic strategies to address intermediate risk markers rather than directly addressing injury incidence.

In conclusion, neuromuscular and balance-based interventions appear to improve biomechanical and functional performance parameters relevant to knee health in national-level female athletes. But more research, with longer follow-up periods and prospective designs with direct injury endpoints, is needed to determine whether these effects translate into meaningful reductions in clinically relevant injury risk.
