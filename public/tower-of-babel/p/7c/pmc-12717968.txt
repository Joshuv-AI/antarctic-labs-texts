Environmental Microbial Cues Alter Embryonic Development and Stress Responses in Vertebrates: Insights From the Zebrafish (Danio rerio) Model
Authors: Emily M. Green, Akila Harishchandra, Colin R. Lickwar, Yeon Ji Kim, John F. Rawls, Richard T. Di Giulio, Nishad Jayasundara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHost‐microbiome interactions shape key physiological processes, including bioenergetics, neurodevelopment and xenobiotic metabolism, and strongly influence the ecological fitness of the host. However, our understanding of host‐microbiome interactions is primarily derived from post‐embryonic free‐living life stages, when the organism is in direct contact with microbes. Whether environmental microbial communities influence embryogenesis—particularly in oviparous organisms where embryos are encased in a protective chorion—remains unknown. Using zebrafish ( Danio rerio ) embryos reared germ‐free or conventionalized with a defined microbial community at 6 and 24 h post‐fertilisation, we demonstrate that environmental microbiota influence embryonic development prior to hatching, altering transcriptomic, proteomic and metabolomic pathways linked to energy metabolism, neurodevelopment and xenobiotic responses, including cytochrome P4501A (cyp1a) activation. Furthermore, embryos exposed to benzo(a)pyrene, a CYP1A‐activator, exhibited microbiome‐dependent changes in embryonic mitochondrial function and larval behaviour, revealing persistent developmental effects. These findings challenge the long‐held assumption that embryonic development is insulated from microbial influence. Instead, our results reveal that host–microbe interactions begin earlier than previously recognised, with implications for developmental plasticity, xenobiotic stress responses and environmental sensitivity likely affecting the ecological fitness of the host. Our results advance understanding of molecular adaptation to microbial environments and provide a foundation for investigating how microbiota shape the developmental origins of host resilience and vulnerability in microbially‐dynamic natural habitats.

Introduction

Animal evolution occurred in an environment abundant with microorganisms, resulting in the emergence of symbiotic relationships that shaped evolutionary trajectories (McFall‐Ngai et al. 2013; Walsh and Guillemin 2022; Bosch and McFall‐Ngai 2021). The microbiome, an ecosystem teeming with microorganisms, is critical in maintaining normal vertebrate physiology including immune function, digestion, behaviour, energy homeostasis and xenobiotic metabolism (McFall‐Ngai et al. 2013; Bertotto et al. 2020; Catron, Gaballah, and Tal 2019; Davis et al. 2016; Heijtz et al. 2011; Phelps et al. 2017; Sampson and Mazmanian 2015; Venema 2010; Bruckner et al. 2022; Gomez De Agüero 2016; Macpherson et al. 2017). Furthermore, the microbiome can influence vertebrate development during post‐embryonic stages (McFall‐Ngai et al. 2013; Walsh and Guillemin 2022; Bosch and McFall‐Ngai 2021; Gilbert et al. 2015). However, there is a significant gap in our understanding of host‐microbe relationships during embryogenesis, a developmental stage most susceptible to environmental challenges, including exposure to environmental pollutants (Hamdoun and Epel 2007; Wilson 1973).

Egg‐laying oviparous organisms develop surrounded by a diverse microbial environment, although conventional knowledge is that vertebrate embryos are shielded from physical contact with microbial cells until hatching (Phelps et al. 2017; Pham et al. 2008). For example, zebrafish ( Danio rerio ) embryogenesis occurs in a three‐layer acellular chorion perforated with small pore canals, spanning 0.5–0.78 μm in diameter, that are essential for oxygen and nutrient exchange, while restricting entry of larger objects such as microbial cells (Phelps et al. 2017; Pham et al. 2008; Chen et al. 2020; Rawson et al. 2000). Although some microorganisms are smaller than the chorionic pore size, penetration is further believed to be hindered by the chorion's negative charge and the low diffusion coefficient of the inner chorionic fluid (Lee et al. 2007; Hart and Donovan 1983). Therefore, embryos lack direct microbial contact, though surrounding bacterial diversity still affects hatching, survival and susceptibility to pathogens during embryonic development (Martín‐Vivaldi et al. 2014; Clark and Wedekind 2011; Wilkins et al. 2016). Although some vertebrates recruit microbes to the surface of the embryo for protection (Phelps et al. 2017; Hamdoun and Epel 2007; Flórez et al. 2015), the influence of microbial communities on embryogenesis remains understudied. In mammals, the maternal microbiome influences fetal health and pre‐ and post‐natal immunity (Gomez De Agüero 2016; Macpherson et al. 2017; Ganal‐Vonarburg et al. 2020; Vuong et al. 2020; Younge et al. 2019). However, the mechanisms by which microbes impact in utero development remain poorly understood due to the difficulty in studying these interactions in mammalian models.

Using the experimental tractability of zebrafish, we tested the hypothesis that the aquatic microbiome communicates with the developing embryo across the chorion and alters the developmental trajectory of the host. We compared germ‐free (GF) zebrafish embryos to embryos reared GF until colonisation with a microbial community at 6 h post‐fertilisation (hpf; CV6; shield stage) or 24 hpf (CV24; Prim‐5 stage) and assessed transcriptomic, proteomic and metabolomic changes in whole embryos at 32 hpf (Prim‐15 stage) (Kimmel et al. 1995). This is a critical window of zebrafish development when organ systems, including the nervous system, musculature, vasculature, kidneys and heart, establish functional roles (Smith and Kimelman 2020) and energy production dynamics are in flux (Stackley et al. 2011; Arribat et al. 2019). Furthermore, given that embryonic development is highly sensitive to external environmental perturbations (Hamdoun and Epel 2007; Wilson 1973), including toxic chemicals (Bertotto et al. 2020; Tartaglione et al. 2023), and the well‐established role of the microbiome in xenobiotic metabolism (Catron, Gaballah, and Tal 2019; Catron, Swank, et al. 2019; Weitekamp et al. 2019; Stagaman et al. 2024), we investigated the impact of the surrounding microbial community on physiological responses to xenobiotics.

As a model compound, we selected benzo(a)pyrene (BaP), a well‐studied aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) agonist and cytochrome P450 1A (CYP1A) inducer (Bugiak and Weber 2009; Incardona et al. 2011; Fang et al. 2015). BaP is known to elicit toxic effects on mitochondrial function and neurodevelopment (Hawkey et al. 2022), making it an established model for evaluating host responses to xenobiotics, although it is unknown whether microbial‐directed effects may alter xenobiotic responses during embryogenesis. Therefore, we quantified embryonic mitochondrial function and larval behaviour following BaP exposure and revealed that exposure to a microbial community is critical for embryonic development and developmental sensitivity to xenobiotics. These early‐life microbial interactions may have lasting consequences for the ecological fitness of the animal host by shaping organismal resilience to environmental stressors encountered in natural habitats.

Results

The Aquatic Microbial Community Drives Transcription in the Developing Embryo

We compared transcriptomic changes between CV6, CV24 and GF embryos to determine the influence of the aquatic microbiome on host gene expression during embryonic development (Figure 1A). All groups were treated to remove the microbial community at 6 hpf and one group was maintained under germ‐free (GF) conditions. The CV6 group was aqueously conventionalized with a microbial community from aquarium water immediately following GF derivation at 6 hpf and served as the control. Since the first 24 h of zebrafish embryonic development are critical for organogenesis and energy production (Smith and Kimelman 2020), we included a CV24 group by conventionalizing GF embryos at 24 hpf to identify whether the presence of a microbiome during these first 24 h was critical for development. Conventionalization media contained a diverse composition of bacteria, dominated by Proteobacteria and Fusobacteriota, as well as taxa known to have conserved abundance in the zebrafish intestinal tract across various laboratory aquaculture facilities (Roeselers et al. 2011). Our experiment illustrates increased relative abundance of Proteobacteria throughout the duration of colonisation in our experiment (CV6; Figure S1).

At 32 hpf, embryos were randomly selected for RNA sequencing (n = 4, 15 embryos per sample). Differential gene expression of pairwise comparisons using DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2019) revealed 3221 significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs; adjusted [adj.] p‐value < 0.05, log2Fold Change>|0.5|; Supporting Information S1). A principal component analysis (PCA) of normalised transcripts illustrated both microbial colonisation status and conventionalization timing affect transcriptional profiles of the developing embryo. Two principal components (PC) were extracted, with PC1 and PC2 describing 33% and 31% of the variance, respectively, representing a combined total of 64% variance (Figure 1B).

Among significant DEGs, 1599 were differentially expressed in GF and 541 were differentially expressed in CV24, with respect to CV6 embryos. The greatest number of DEGs, 2284, were differentially enriched in GF with respect to CV24 embryos (Figure 1C). Among all pairwise comparisons, the GF versus CV6 comparison shared the most DEGs with the GF versus CV24 comparison (Figure 1D), suggesting shared differences were driven by microbial colonisation. More DEGs were upregulated in GF compared to both CV6 or CV24 embryos, and more DEGs were downregulated in CV24 embryos compared to CV6 embryos (Figure S2). We suggest that the observed upregulated gene expression in GF embryos may reflect either a baseline upregulation that is suppressed or modified upon microbial colonisation, or a compensatory response aimed at sustaining normal biological processes in the absence of microbial cues.

To determine tissue specificity of overall gene expression across treatments, we compared our dataset to 31 tissue‐enriched gene lists from a published embryonic single‐cell transcriptomic dataset (Farnsworth et al. 2020) and assigned each tissue a rank based on significance (mroast; FDR adj. p‐value < 0.05). Differential enrichment was most strongly represented in the liver and myeloid lineage in GF with respect to CV6, the central nervous system and placode in CV24 with respect to CV6, and the pharyngeal endoderm and lens placode in GF with respect to CV24 (Figure S3), suggesting the microbial environment targets a broad range of tissue types critical to healthy embryonic development.

We then used Metascape (Zhou et al. 2019) to identify enriched biological process gene ontology (GO) terms across pairwise comparisons (Figure 1E). This revealed GO terms involved in mitochondrial and cellular energy metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, neurodevelopment, immune function, apoptotic signalling, protein translation, cell adhesion and signalling and cellular response to stress, among others (Figure 1E, Supporting Information S2).

The Aquatic Microbiome Influences Embryonic Energy Metabolism and Neurodevelopmental Gene Expression

To identify interactions between conventionalization status and timing of conventionalization, we performed a likelihood ratio test (LRT) (Love et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2019), which identified a total of 1769 significant DEGs (FDR adj. p‐value < 0.05, log2Fold Change>|0.5|; Figure S4, Supporting Information S3). To identify pathways most significantly impacted, DEGs with a log2Fold Change>|0.64| were subset for further analysis, which represented the trends observed among all significant LRT DEGs (Figure S5). Four distinct gene expression patterns were identified across treatments. LRT patterns 1 and 2, comprising 93% of LRT DEGs, consisted of 306 DEGs downregulated and 621 DEGs upregulated in GF embryos compared to groups with a microbiome (Figure 2, Supporting Information S4). LRT patterns 3 and 4 contained 73 DEGs, 17 upregulated and 56 downregulated, in both GF and CV24 with respect to CV6 embryos (Figure 2, Supporting Information S4), suggesting these genes were sensitive to the time at which microbial colonisation occurred.

Dots represent the mean transcript abundance across replicates, scaled by Z‐score. Data are visualised as boxplots of the mean and interquartile range.

Since energy metabolism and neurodevelopment were abundant contributors to enriched GO terms across pairwise comparisons, we further investigated these processes by identifying enriched pathways within LRT patterns. Among significant pathways (p‐value < 0.05) related to energy metabolism or neurodevelopment, we identified 134 genes involved in energy metabolism (Figure 3A) and 43 genes involved in neurodevelopment (Figure 3B).

Heatmaps of normalised transcript counts for LRT DEGs involved in (A) energy metabolism and (B) neurodevelopment. Energy metabolism genes are further categorised by function (carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function) and membership is annotated on the y‐axis. Transcript counts for individual genes are scaled by z‐score across 4 sequenced samples per treatment. Gene lists and normalised counts for each heat map are in Supporting Information S6.

Of the 134 energy metabolism genes, 44 were attributed to carbohydrate metabolism, 68 to lipid metabolism and 22 to mitochondrial function. While these pathways are intrinsically connected, gene function was determined by membership to the assigned significant GO term. Uniquely, all 68 DEGs involved in lipid metabolism were upregulated in GF embryos compared to embryos with a microbiome (Figure 3A). These genes were involved in lipid transport and homeostasis, fatty acid oxidation, cholesterol metabolism, alcohol metabolism and steroid metabolism and signalling (Supporting Information S5 and S6). Genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism and mitochondrial function were distributed across gene expression trends, suggesting these genes were dependent on both colonisation status and the timing of colonisation. Specifically, genes involved in ADP/ATP metabolism and AMPK activity were upregulated in GF embryos, while genes involved in carbohydrate metabolism and TORC1 signalling were downregulated in GF embryos. Genes involved in mitochondrial transmembrane transport displayed both upregulation and downregulation in GF embryos (Supporting Information S5 and S6). LRT DEGs associated with mitochondrial respiration, mitochondrial membrane permeabilization, ATP/ADP/AMP metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, mitochondrial protein localization and responses to reactive oxygen species were differentially expressed in both GF and CV24 embryos compared to CV6 embryos (Supporting Information S5 and S6).

Among the 43 genes involved in neurodevelopment, hierarchical clustering revealed approximately half were upregulated and the other half were downregulated in GF embryos, while CV6 and CV24 embryos generally showed comparable transcript counts (Figure 3B). Specifically, neurodevelopment LRT DEGs were involved in calcium control of neurotransmitter release and synaptic transmission, retinal and photoreceptor cell development, glial cell and neuron development and swimming behaviour, among several others (Supporting Information S5 and S6).

Among other processes, we identified 33 immune‐related DEGs across treatments (Figure S6), most of which were upregulated under GF conditions. Given the known role of the maternal microbiome on prenatal and postnatal immunity (Gomez De Agüero 2016; Macpherson et al. 2017), this further supports our hypothesis that the surrounding microbial environment shapes the development of the embryo.

The Aquatic Microbiome Impacts Embryonic Protein and Metabolite Abundance

An additional subset of CV6, CV24 and GF embryos were randomly selected for protein quantification by LC–MS/MS (n = 4, 15 embryos per sample). Differential protein analysis using DEP (Zhang et al. 2018) revealed 1146 differentially abundant proteins (DAPs; p‐value < 0.05, log Fold Change>|0.5|) across all pairwise comparisons (Supporting Information S7). 492 differed in GF with respect to CV6 embryos, 469 differed in GF with respect to CV24 embryos, and 575 differed in CV24 with respect to CV6 embryos, which was the greatest of all comparisons (Figure 4A). This may either be reflective of a transition state of CV24 embryos as they respond to newly introduced microbial cues and re‐establish homeostasis or a lag between microbial‐induced transcriptional changes and subsequent translational responses. A PCA revealed 21.3% of variance in PC3 and PC4 was explained by microbial colonisation status (Figure S7). Significant DAPs were compared to all significant LRT DEGs, revealing 54 DAPs with differentially expressed corresponding transcripts (Supporting Information S8).

The DAVID Functional Annotation Tool (Huang et al. 2009; Sherman et al. 2022) was used to identify enriched pathways (p‐value < 0.05), revealing 140 DAPs involved in energy metabolism (Figure 4B) and 56 DAPs involved in neurodevelopment (Figure 4C). GF embryos differed in oxidative phosphorylation and electron transport chain proteins and CV24 embryos revealed broader differences in mitochondrial membrane translocation, protein translation, oxidative phosphorylation, the electron transport chain, redox cycling, mTOR signalling and lipid metabolism compared to CV6 embryos. Furthermore, ATPase activity, mitochondrial translation and phospholipid metabolism differed in GF embryos compared to CV24 embryos (Figure 4B, Supporting Information S9). Many of the DAPs identified were involved in similar biological functions to LRT DEGs, highlighting energy metabolism as a critical embryonic process influenced by the surrounding microbial environment.

The 56 DAPs enriched in neurodevelopmental pathways also mirrored functions observed in LRT DEGs. GF embryos revealed differences in postsynaptic and axonogenesis‐related proteins with respect to CV6 embryos and proteins involved in neural crest cell differentiation and calcium signalling with respect to CV24 embryos. CV24 embryos differed in additional proteins involved in axonogenesis, calcium signalling and neural crest cell development with respect to CV6 embryos (Figure 4C, Supporting Information S9), highlighting the critical importance of the microbiome during the first 24 h of development.

To determine whether the aquatic microbial environment elicited embryonic metabolome changes, an additional subset of CV6, CV24 and GF embryos were randomly selected for targeted metabolomics using the MxP Quant 500 kit (Biocrates Innsbruck, Austria) (n = 4, 15 embryos per sample). Differential analysis revealed 33 metabolites significantly differed in concentration (ANOVA, Tukey's HSD; p‐value < 0.05, log2Fold Change>|0.5|) across all pairwise comparisons. 31 metabolites differed in GF with respect to CV6, 2 differed in CV24 with respect to CV6, and 1 differed in GF with respect to CV24 (Figure S8A, Supporting Information S10). Overall, GF embryos displayed increased concentrations for all significant metabolites detected (Figure S8B), which were involved in energy metabolism and neurodevelopmental pathways. For example, GF embryos had increased lipid concentrations, including triacylglycerols, diacylglycerols and ceramides, relative to CV6 embryos (Figure S8C), confirming transcriptional upregulation of lipid metabolism (Figure 3). Furthermore, GF embryos had increased concentrations of GABA (Figure S8C) and amino acid glutathione constituents (Figure S8D), consistent with gene expression data (Figure 3).

The Aquatic Microbiome Impacts Bioenergetic and Behavioural Responses to an Embryonic Xenobiotic Exposure

Transcriptomic results revealed differential expression of genes involved in cellular responses to cyclic organic compounds, including several xenobiotic metabolism genes (Figure S9A). The single most differentially expressed gene across all treatments was cyp1a, which was downregulated under GF conditions (Figure 5A, Figure S2). We used quantitative PCR to confirm cyp1a expression was maintained in the presence of a microbial community, regardless of the timing of conventionalization (Figure S10). Cytochrome P450s play a vital role in the oxidative metabolism of xenobiotics and are transcriptionally activated by the AhR (Guengerich 2008). While CYP1A protein abundance was differentially regulated (Figure 5B), we observed a discordant trend in protein and gene expression data. GF embryos also displayed increased aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (arnt) expression (Figure S9A), which is required for the translocation of AhR to the nucleus to induce gene transcription, including cyp1a. This may be a compensatory mechanism in response to reduced expression of cyp1a among GF embryos. In addition to AhR‐directed phase I metabolism, the pregnane X receptor (PXR) regulates genes involved in both phase I and II metabolism of xenobiotics (Rakateli et al. 2023). PXR (nr1i2) increased in GF embryos compared to embryos with a microbiome (Figure S9B), suggesting alternative xenobiotic metabolism pathways GF embryos may utilize to compensate when cyp1a expression is reduced in the absence of microbial cues.

We used benzo(a)pyrene (BaP), a well‐studied AhR agonist and CYP1A inducer (Bugiak and Weber 2009; Incardona et al. 2011; Fang et al. 2015) to determine whether microbial‐driven differences in cyp1a expression influenced physiological responses to xenobiotic exposure. To test this, we assessed the effects of the microbiome on bioenergetic and behavioural responses following exposure to BaP. Assessment of embryonic mitochondrial respiration at 32 hpf illustrated that basal oxygen consumption rates (OCR) decreased in CV24 embryos compared to CV6 and GF embryos, while no changes in maximal OCR were observed in the absence of a BaP exposure (Figure 5C). However, in the presence of 10 μM BaP exposure, both basal and maximal respiration decreased in CV24 embryos (Figure 5C), while spare capacity and mitochondrial abundance remained unchanged (Figures S11 and S12). Non‐mitochondrial OCR significantly increased in GF embryos compared to CV24 embryos in the absence of a BaP exposure and increased in GF embryos compared to CV6 embryos in the presence of BaP (Figure 5C).

To assess whether the environmental microbiome during embryogenesis differentially influenced larval behavioural response to BaP exposure, we measured total distance moved during alternating light and dark cycles at 5 days post‐fertilisation (dpf). Larvae raised under GF conditions increased total distance travelled (hyperactivity) compared to CV6 larvae. However, CV24 larvae were not statistically different from CV6 or GF larvae and exhibited minor hyperactivity compared to CV6 larvae (Figure 5D, Figure S13A). With an embryonic 10 μM BaP exposure, total distance moved in both the GF and CV24 groups significantly increased compared to CV6 larvae, suggesting the timing of conventionalization is critical for the behavioural response to BaP (Figure 5D). Notably, GF larvae experienced hyperactivity in both the light and dark phases, while CV24 larvae only exhibited hyperactivity in the dark in response to a 10 μM BaP exposure (Figure S13B). In summary, we found the aquatic embryonic microbiome impacted embryonic bioenergetics and later‐life larval behaviour, effects that were pronounced in response to a xenobiotic exposure.

Discussion

The Aquatic Microbial Environment Affects Embryonic Development

Contrary to the notion that zebrafish embryos are shielded from the aquatic microbial environment (Phelps et al. 2017; Pham et al. 2008), our molecular data show a distinct interaction between the surrounding microbial environment and embryogenesis. Gnotobiotic studies commonly conventionalize zebrafish around 72 hpf, after hatching (Pham et al. 2008). However, our study emphasizes the timing of conventionalization as an important consideration when designing gnotobiotic studies, as the embryo undergoes critical developmental processes sensitive to microbial cues from the aquatic microbial environment.

We demonstrate the presence of a microbiome is critical for optimal transcriptomic and translational patterning during embryonic development. Furthermore, our LRT analysis identified a small subset of genes (~7%) dysregulated in both CV24 and GF embryos relative to CV6 embryos that receive microbial cues during key developmental windows. During the first 24 h of zebrafish development, embryos experience a decline in total mitochondria from which all mitochondria will replicate during subsequent biogenesis (Otten et al. 2016) and switch from glycolysis to mitochondrial respiration as the major source of energy production (Stackley et al. 2011; Arribat et al. 2019). Efficient energy metabolism is critical during the early stages of embryogenesis as organogenesis, including the development of the nervous system, is highly energy intensive (Bifari et al. 2020; Ribeiro et al. 2020). Therefore, our findings highlight the importance in developmental timing of microbial cues from the surrounding environment, especially during the first 24 h of embryonic development. However, further studies are needed to determine the drivers of these gene expression patterns and whether gene expression is more dependent on the developmental timing of microbial introduction or the temporal duration of exposure to a microbial community.

The Aquatic Microbiome Impacts Embryonic Energy Metabolism

The gut microbiome is known to influence metabolic homeostasis during larval development in zebrafish (Rawls et al. 2004; Massaquoi et al. 2023; Sheng et al. 2018), although our findings are among the first to show microbial‐directed effects on energy metabolism begin during embryogenesis. Previous studies during post‐embryonic development show microbial products influence mitochondrial respiration, biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, the TCA cycle, ATP production, proton leak and AMPK activity (Ribeiro et al. 2020; Clark and Mach 2017; Donohoe et al. 2011; Frye et al. 2016; Rose et al. 2018). Our results showed significant treatment‐specific differences in embryonic expression of genes, proteins and metabolites involved in mitochondrial function (e.g., TCA cycle, ATP synthesis, oxidative stress). Our transcriptomic results further revealed a significant increase in AMPK (prkaa2) in GF embryos compared to embryos with a microbiome, suggesting GF embryos may enhance glucose uptake, mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation and respiratory capacity through AMPK‐dependent mechanisms (Clark and Mach 2017; Mollica et al. 2017). We suggest this may be a compensatory response, where GF embryos upregulate energy metabolism processes in an attempt to offset the reduced efficiency of energy production in the absence of a microbiome, although further studies are needed to fully test this hypothesis. While mitochondrial content remained consistent across treatments, we observed a significant decrease in bnip3lb, a gene involved in mitophagy regulation, in GF and CV24 embryos. Therefore, the absence of a surrounding microbial environment during the first 24 h of embryogenesis may reduce mitophagy, which may be critical for maintaining mitochondrial efficiency and sustaining sufficient energy production (Pickles et al. 2018; Wrighton et al. 2021).

In concordance with molecular data, we observed a significant decrease in basal mitochondrial respiration in embryos that did not receive a microbiome until later in embryonic development (CV24). This suggests that the surrounding microbial environment may be critical during the first 24 h of embryonic development in establishing mitochondrial function and energy production efficiency, although whether embryos recover from this state requires further investigation. However, it is unclear why mitochondrial respiration was not affected in GF embryos. While GF embryos increased non‐mitochondrial respiration rates, it is possible GF embryos reach metabolic homeostasis or shift energy substrates, possibly aided by upregulation of AMPK‐directed pathways, in the absence of microbial‐directed cues. In contrast, CV24 embryos received microbial‐directed cues, although developmentally later than CV6 embryos, and may adjust mitochondrial respiration efficiency following those cues. These results highlight the importance of the surrounding microbial environment in shaping embryonic energy production, which may have lasting consequences for developmental trajectories.

The observed uniform upregulation of DEGs involved in lipid metabolism and increase in lipid metabolites in GF embryos may also be a compensatory attempt to increase energy metabolism in the absence of microbial‐directed cues. We show GF embryos significantly increased expression of genes involved in fatty acid synthesis and oxidation, cholesterol synthesis and lipid absorption/transport, notably nr1i2 (PXR) and apoa1. Induction of apoa1 via PXR is linked to lipid transport and homeostasis (Rakateli et al. 2023; Bachmann et al. 2004; Nakamura et al. 2007), and may explain the increased abundance of lipid metabolites in GF embryos. GF studies during post‐embryonic development suggest cholesterol synthesis and fatty acid oxidation increase under GF conditions (Rawls et al. 2004; Sheng et al. 2018; Brown et al. 2023; Lickwar et al. 2022), while fatty acid synthesis, absorption, transport and accumulation decrease under GF conditions (Sheng et al. 2018; Clark and Mach 2017; Brown et al. 2023; Lickwar et al. 2022; Semova et al. 2012). It is possible that in the absence of a microbiome during early development, lipid synthesis is initially upregulated, as we see at 32 hpf, and subsequently downregulated as energy storage becomes depleted. These findings further suggest the environmental microbiome plays a role in balancing lipid metabolism during embryogenesis. The absence of these normal microbial cues may predispose embryos to shifts in energy allocation dynamics, which may have consequences for growth, development and long‐term metabolic health.

The Aquatic Microbiome Impacts Embryonic Neurodevelopment

The gut‐brain axis is well characterized during post‐embryonic development, with microbial signals influencing neurodevelopmental processes such as calcium and neurotransmitter signalling, synaptic plasticity, visual development, axonogenesis and HPA/HPI axis activation (Davis et al. 2016; Heijtz et al. 2011; Sampson and Mazmanian 2015; Vuong et al. 2020; Massaquoi et al. 2023; MacFabe 2012; Martin et al. 2018; Silva et al. 2020; Kaur et al. 2023; Rea et al. 2022). Our findings extend this model to suggest microbial influence begins as early as embryonic development. Vuong et al. (2020) demonstrated the maternal microbiome modulates fetal neurodevelopment, including reductions in axonogenesis and GABA receptor genes, resulting in post‐natal behavioural effects in mice (Vuong et al. 2020). Similarly, our study observed significant differential regulation of molecules involved in axonogenesis, including gabra1 and gabrg2 expression, GABA concentrations and p21 protein (Cdc42/Rac)‐activated kinase 1 and 2a (PAK1 and PAK2a) abundance. In addition to our finding that genes associated with the central nervous system were most significantly enriched among CV24 compared to CV6 embryos, our data emphasizes that the effect of the microbiome on neurodevelopment commences prenatally during embryogenesis, which could have developmental consequences for brain development, neuron formation and neurotransmitter physiology.

Findings from Rea et al. (2022) suggest zebrafish neurodevelopment is delayed under GF conditions, marked by dysregulated eye and lens development, Wnt signalling and neuron and glial organisation (Rea et al. 2022). Our results confirm disruption of eye and lens development and neurogenesis, although the specific DEGs involved in these processes differed, which may be attributed to differences in collection timing or methods employed in analysis. While dysregulated Wnt signalling pathways were not significant in our LRT analysis, Wnt signalling was identified as one of the most significantly enriched GO terms among the GF compared to CV24 embryos. Taken together, these findings suggest the microbiome plays a critical role in regulating embryonic Wnt‐directed neurodevelopment, including axon formation, migration and synapse development (Mulligan and Cheyette 2012), potentially shaping neurobehavioural outcomes later in life.

Our larval behavioural studies complement observed embryonic transcriptional and translational differences. While larval behavioural changes are in concordance with previous studies (Davis et al. 2016; Heijtz et al. 2011; Phelps et al. 2017; Catron, Swank, et al. 2019; Stagaman et al. 2024), we additionally demonstrated a time‐dependent impact of conventionalization, as CV24 displays mild hyperactivity, but not to the extent of GF embryos. Interestingly, GABA concentrations increased in GF embryos at 32 hpf. Excitatory‐inhibitory balances play a key role in regulating critical periods of neuronal plasticity in the visual cortex (Levelt and Hübener 2012) and increases in GABA have been linked to impairment of axonogenesis (Vuong et al. 2020; Wu et al. 2012). Therefore, a disruption in these balances during embryogenesis may ultimately contribute to difficulties in controlling behavioural impulses, explaining the observed hyperactivity we see in GF larvae. Collectively, these studies support a role of the environmental microbial community in modulating neurodevelopment beginning during embryogenesis, with persistent effects on later life larval behaviour.

The Aquatic Microbiome Impacts Embryonic Responses to Xenobiotic Exposure

Zebrafish embryos can metabolise xenobiotics even before the liver is completely formed during embryogenesis (Nawaji et al. 2020). We propose that microbial‐driven regulation of xenobiotic metabolism genes, including several CYPs, may underlie this embryonic response to chemicals. Previous studies show microbial metabolites activate AhR, inducing transcription of cyp1a (Ma et al. 2020; Modoux et al. 2022). We observed cyp1a was the most downregulated gene in the absence of a microbiome. However, the discordance in cyp1a gene expression with protein abundance warrants further studies and may be attributed to differences in post‐translational modifications of CYP1A across treatments.

Furthermore, in the absence of a microbial community, we observed an increase in nr1i2 (PXR), along with a corresponding increase of phase II metabolism genes, including glutathione S‐transferases (gstt2, gsta.2), as well as glutathione metabolomic constituents. While GF embryos were observed to have downregulated expression of cyp1a, these results suggest GF embryos may utilize alternative xenobiotic metabolism pathways in the absence of microbial cues. Similar to the AhR, PXR is activated by xenobiotics, dietary compounds and microbial metabolites (Venkatesh et al. 2014), therefore transcriptional upregulation in GF embryos remains unexplained, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms. However, these findings clearly highlight an integral role of the microbiome in shaping xenobiotic metabolism and detoxification pathways during early development, which may influence developmental responses to environmental chemicals and ecological susceptibility.

Our results also demonstrated impacts of the aquatic microbial environment on host physiology in response to an embryonic xenobiotic exposure. While our results showed that basal mitochondrial respiration decreased in embryos lacking a microbiome until 24 hpf, this difference was pronounced upon exposure to BaP, as CV24 embryos showed an overall reduction of both basal and maximal mitochondrial respiration.

In terms of swimming behaviour, while only GF larvae experienced significant hyperactivity in the absence of BaP exposure, a time‐dependent impact of conventionalization became evident upon exposure to BaP. When exposed to BaP, both GF and CV24 larvae displayed hyperactivity compared to CV6 embryos conventionalized earlier during development. While BaP is previously reported to affect post‐embryonic zebrafish bioenergetics and larval behaviour (Hawkey et al. 2022), our study further emphasised that the presence and timing of microbial colonisation during embryogenesis are significant for regulating mitochondrial function and neurobehavioural outcomes when challenged with xenobiotic exposure, underscoring the microbiome as a key determinant of developmental resilience to environmental stressors.

Conclusion

The current data clearly demonstrate host‐microbiome interactions commence as early as embryogenesis, shaping the trajectory of organismal development, particularly in relation to key processes including embryonic energy metabolism, neurodevelopment and xenobiotic responses. This challenges the current notion that oviparous embryos develop completely shielded from the microbial environment until after hatching and compels a re‐evaluation of germ‐free methodologies in developmental biology studies. While we acknowledge that germ‐free states are not natural in environmental contexts, these approaches provide a powerful framework to tease apart developmental time points and processes that are microbe‐dependent, and therefore potentially sensitive to dysbiotic states. While the chorion provides physical protection against microbes and other environmental stressors, our data reveal this barrier is not impervious to microbial communication from the surrounding environment. These early interactions suggest that microbial signals from diverse aquatic habitats may influence developmental plasticity, physiological performance and ultimately the ecological fitness of oviparous organisms. Furthermore, our findings contribute to a broader understanding of how environmental microbial biodiversity shapes molecular adaptation during the earliest and most vulnerable stages of vertebrate development. Future studies will identify specific microbial‐derived molecules that mediate communication across the chorion and determine whether microbes or their products alter the permeability of these membranes. This will establish a foundation for examining how surrounding microbiomes modulate the developmental origins of disease and influence in utero and in ovo responses to chemical exposures in natural systems.

Methods

Germ‐Free Derivation and Conventionalization

All procedures were approved by the Duke University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol A069‐22‐04). Adult zebrafish were spawned for 1 h (3 females, 2 males per breeder tank) and embryos were collected in sterile antibiotic solution containing 250 ng/mL amphotericin B, 10 μg/mL kanamycin and 100 μg/mL ampicillin in 0.2‐μm sterile filtered 30% Danieau's medium (1.06 g/L NaCl, 16.3 mg/L KCl, 15 mg/L MgSO4, 31 mg/L Ca(NO3)2, 375 mg/L HEPES, pH 7.2). Germ‐free (GF) derivation was performed at 6 hpf as previously described (Pham et al. 2008) and detailed in the Supporting Information. Groups of 25 GF embryos were reared at 28°C in 25 mL of sterile 30% Danieau's in 50 mL 25‐cm2 cell culture flasks (Greiner Bio‐One, Monroe, NC). Conventionalized (CV) embryos simultaneously underwent GF treatment and were subsequently conventionalized by performing an 80% water change and replacing 50% of sterile Danieau's medium with AHAB system water at one of two timepoints: immediately following derivation at 6 hpf and at 24 hpf, to generate treatments CV6 and CV24, respectively. To assess microbial community structure of conventionalization media, 1 mL of this water before conventionalization at 6 hpf and at endpoint collection (32 hpf) from each of the CV6 and CV24 flasks was used for sequencing of the V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene as detailed in the Supporting Information. All CV and GF groups were maintained under consistent conditions.

RNA Extraction, Library Preparation, Paired‐End Sequencing and Processing

Fifteen embryos from each treatment (CV6, CV24 or GF) were randomly selected at 32 hpf, pooled and flash frozen (experiments repeated four times). RNA was extracted from each pooled embryo sample using RNeasy QIAGEN Mini Kit (74104, QIAGEN Inc.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Whole‐transcriptome library preparation and paired‐end sequencing were performed by Duke University Sequencing and Genomic Technologies and processed as previously described (Pertea et al. 2016) and in the Supporting Information. Extracted RNA was also used for cDNA synthesis and quantitative RT PCR (qRT‐PCR) to confirm relative expression of cyp1a, described in the Supporting Information.

Pairwise Differential Gene Expression Analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using R (v4.3.2) (R Core Team 2021) and Bioconductor (v3.18) packages DESeq2 (v1.42.0) (Love et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2019), DEGreport (v1.38.5) (Pantano 2023), and org.Dr.eg.db (v3.18.0) (Carlson 2019). Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wald test with an FDR adjusted (adj.) p‐value < 0.05 and a log2Fold Change>|0.5| (Supporting Information S1). A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed among all significant pairwise DEGs and two principal components (PCs) extracted. Transcript counts were normalised with a regularised log transformation and graphed with plotPCA function of DESeq2 and ggplot2 (v3.5.1) (Wickham 2016). Venn diagrams were created using CRAN package ggVennDiagram (v1.2.3) (Gao et al. 2021) and volcano plots were created using Bioconductor (v3.18) package EnhancedVolcano (v1.20.0) (Blighe et al. 2018). Individual DEGs of interest were graphed using ggplot2 (v3.5.1) (Wickham 2016). A tissue‐specific gene set enrichment was performed as previously described (Babich et al. 2024) and detailed in the Supporting Information to compare differential expression to 31 tissue‐enriched gene sets from a published embryonic single‐cell transcriptomic dataset (Farnsworth et al. 2020).

Metascape was used to screen pairwise lists of DEGs using default parameters in the custom analysis and identify significantly enriched biological process gene ontology (GO) terms (Zhou et al. 2019). GO terms with a p‐value < 0.01, a minimum count of 3, and an enrichment factor > 1.5 were considered significantly enriched. Metascape was used to generate a Circos plot to identify overlaps between DEGs (Krzywinski et al. 2009) and group enriched GO terms into clusters (based on a Kappa score > 0.3) (Cohen 1960). Top terms were graphed in a heatmap and all enriched GO terms are included in Supporting Information S2.

Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) of Gene Expression

Significant DEGs were identified by DESeq2 (v1.42.0) (Love et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2019) LRT function (FDR adj. p‐value < 0.05, log2Fold Change>|0.5|), log‐transformed and the degPatterns function of the DEGreport package (v1.38.5) (Pantano 2023) was used to identify gene expression trends across treatment groups using default parameters. The enrichGO function of the ClusterProfiler package (v4.11.0) (Wu et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2012) was used to identify enriched biological process pathways using default parameters within each LRT pattern (Supporting Information S5). Significant LRT DEG transcript counts were normalised using a variance stabilising transformation, scaled by Z‐score and graphed using CRAN package pheatmap (v1.0.12) (Kolde 2018).

Proteomic Sample Preparation and Differential Protein Abundance Analysis

Fifteen embryos from each treatment (CV6, CV24, or GF) at 32 hpf were randomly selected, pooled and flash frozen (experiments repeated four times). Sample preparation and quantitative LC–MS/MS analysis was performed by Duke University Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility (detailed in the Supporting Information). Differential protein abundance was determined using R (v4.3.2) (R Core Team 2021) and the Bioconductor (v3.18) package DEP (v1.26.0) (Zhang et al. 2018). Data were filtered as described in the Supporting Information. Pairwise comparisons were performed using linear models and empirical Bayes statistics and significant differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) were identified (p‐value < 0.05, log Fold Change>|0.5|; Supporting Information S7). A Venn diagram was graphed using the CRAN package ggVennDiagram (v1.2.3) (Gao et al. 2021). A PCA was performed, four PCs extracted and graphed using the plot_PCA function of DEP and ggplot2 (v3.5.1) (Wickham 2016). The DAVID Functional Annotation Tool (Huang et al. 2009; Sherman et al. 2022) was used to identify significantly enriched biological process pathways (p‐value < 0.05) using default parameters. Raw counts for DAPs involved in energy metabolism and neurodevelopment were scaled by Z‐score and graphed using the CRAN package pheatmap (v1.0.12) (Kolde 2018). Individual DAPs of interest were graphed using ggplot2 (v3.5.1) (Wickham 2016).

Metabolomic Sample Preparation and Analysis of Differential Metabolites

Fifteen embryos from each treatment (CV6, CV24 or GF) at 32 hpf were randomly selected, pooled and flash frozen (experiments repeated four times). Homogenization, quantitative UHPLC separation and semi‐quantitative flow injection analysis‐tandem mass spectrometry (FIA‐MS/MS) were performed by Duke University Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility (detailed in the Supporting Information). Data were filtered as described in the Supporting Information and log2 transformed. Log2 transformed concentrations were scaled by Z‐score and graphed using CRAN package pheatmap (v1.0.12) (Kolde 2018). The log2 Fold Change for each pairwise comparison was calculated. Analytes with a p‐value < 0.05 and a log2FoldChange > |0.5| were considered significant (ANOVA, Tukey's HSD, Supporting Information S10) and graphed using CRAN package ggVennDiagram (v1.2.3) (Gao et al. 2021) and ggplot2 (v3.5.1) (Wickham 2016).

Xenobiotic Exposure

CV6, CV24 and GF embryos at 24 hpf were aqueously exposed to 10 μM benzo(a)pyrene (Sigma‐Aldrich, CAS 50‐32‐8) in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma‐Aldrich, CAS 67‐68‐5) or a 0.1% DMSO vehicle control and raised in exposure media until endpoint collection.

Mitochondrial Function Assay

Mitochondrial respiration rates were measured using the Seahorse BioAnalyzer XFe96 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) according to a previously published protocol (Stackley et al. 2011; Babich et al. 2020). Briefly, basal, maximal (using FCCP; Sigma‐Aldrich, CAS 370–86‐5), non‐mitochondrial (using sodium azide, Sigma‐Aldrich, CAS 26628‐22‐8), and spare mitochondrial capacity (maximal—basal) oxygen consumption rates (OCR; pmol/O2/min) were measured per individual embryo. Embryo OCR values were normalised to the average of CV6 embryos within each experiment and graphed using GraphPad Prism 10 (v10.1.0; GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA). Two one‐way ANOVAs, separated by BaP dose, with Tukey post hoc tests, were performed using JMP Pro (v16.2.0) (JMP 2021) to determine significance (p‐value < 0.05). Detailed methods for mitochondrial function and copy number assays are in the Supporting Information.

Larval Behavioural Assay

Behavioural responses were measured at 5 dpf using DanioVision (Noldus, Wageningen, The Netherlands) as described previously (Hawkey et al. 2022) and detailed in the Supporting Information. Briefly, total distance travelled by individual larvae was recorded across a 10‐min habituation period in the dark and 2 sequential cycles of 10‐min in the light followed by 10‐min in the dark (50 min per assay). Movement was measured in mm/min and graphed using GraphPad Prism 10 (v10.1.0, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA) and R package ggplot2 (v3.5.1) (Wickham 2016). Significance was determined using a two‐way ANOVA with a Tukey post hoc test (p‐value < 0.05) in JMP Pro (v16.2.0) (JMP 2021).
