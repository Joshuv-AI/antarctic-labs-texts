Regulatory roles of microRNAs in signaling pathways and their biomarker potential in prostate cancer
Authors: Hossein Kalarestaghi, Negin Amani, Hamed Shoorei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PCa) is considered as one of the most common malignancies in men. Despite substantial socioeconomic investment in surgical and clinical treatment strategies, the five-year survival rate for patients with metastatic PCa remains approximately 30%. Therefore, the need for further investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying PCa development and progression is urgent. In this regard, signaling pathways, including TGF-β, Notch, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin, and NF-κB, are involved in PCa pathogenesis through the regulation of gene expression, cell proliferation and differentiation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix synthesis and degradation. During carcinogenesis, genes are abnormaly expressed, hence, targeting the signaling networks that regulate these genes represents an important therapeutic strategy. On the other hand, miRNAs, which are closely associated with multiple signaling pathways, has been linked to PCa development and progression. In particular, miR-let-7c is downregulated, whereas miR-199a-5p and miR-150-5p are upregulated in PCa specimens. This review highlights miRNAs as regulators of signaling pathways in PCa and their clinical relevance, offering potential therapeutic insights.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04236-z.Keywords: Prostate cancer, MicroRNA, Cancer-related signaling pathways, Carcinogenesis

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) predominantly affects men aged 45–60 years and, in Western countries, contributes to cancer-related mortality [1]. The diagnosis of PCa is typically achieved through several methods, including prostate biopsy and analysis, routine clinical evaluations, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), digital rectal examination, and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing [2]. In 1986, the introduction of PSA testing led to a marked increase in the incidence of PCa; however, in recent years, this trend has reversed as routine PSA testing has declined. The reduced acceptance of PSA testing is largely due to its substantial rate of false positives and the resulting overdiagnosis, with estimates indicating that 20% to 50% of men diagnosed may undergo unnecessary treatment [3].

PCa risk factors include a family history of the disease, ethnicity, increasing age, obesity, and other environmental factors. Despite the identification of these factors, reliable tools for predicting tumor progression, stratifying risk, and assessing therapeutic response remain limited [4]. The growing need for accurate biomarkers underscores the potential of miRNAs as valuable tools for screening, monitoring, prognostic evaluation, and assessing therapeutic responses in PCa [5]. In the context of PCa, analyses of miRNA profiles from tumor and normal tissues have revealed inconsistent expression patterns, including sporadic overexpression alongside an overall trend of downregulation. MicroRNAs are small, non-coding RNA molecules, typically fewer than 22 nucleotides in length, that regulate multiple mRNAs and are evolutionarily conserved [6].

These miRNAs play a critical regulatory role, influencing approximately 1–5% of the human genome and at least 30% of protein-coding genes. They exert their effects by suppressing target gene expression post-transcriptionally, either through the degradation of target mRNA or by inhibiting its translation into functional proteins [7]. In cancer, microRNAs are critical regulators of essential cellular processes, including programmed cell death, cell proliferation, differentiation, and carcinogenesis [8]. Altered miRNA expression is strongly associated with the invasion and development of various human malignancies. Notably, approximately 50% of known miRNA genes are located within genomic regions associated with cancer. These dysregulated miRNAs contribute to PCa pathogenesis by downregulating tumor suppressor genes and upregulating oncogenes [9].

MiRNAs

MiRNAs, typically 20–24 nucleotides in length, are small non-coding RNA molecules. They play important roles in numerous biochemical processes in humans and other multicellular organisms. These miRNAs significantly influence cancer-related processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, cell cycle regulation, programmed cell death, migration, and metabolism. They primarily function by inhibiting gene expression at the post-transcriptional or translational stages [10]. First identified in 1993 by Ambros and colleagues in Caenorhabditis elegans, miRNAs have since been shown to be abundantly expressed in viruses, plants, and animals [11]. In many types of cancer, miRNAs are dysregulated through transcriptional, epigenetic, genetic, or processing mechanisms. This dysregulation has led to the suggestion that either restoring or inhibiting specific miRNAs may offer therapeutic advantages [12].

Extensive research has shown that miRNAs are intricately involved in nearly all aspects of cancer biology, including tumor cell growth, invasion, migration, interactions within the tumor microenvironment, angiogenesis (the formation of new blood vessels), tumor immunology, and resistance to treatment [13]. Circulating miRNAs, known for their remarkable stability in bodily fluids, have emerged as reliable non-invasive biomarkers for cancer detection. To develop new diagnostic tools and miRNA-based therapeutic strategies, it is necessary to fully understand the molecular mechanisms by which miRNAs contribute to tumor metastasis. With more than a thousand miRNAs identified, a single miRNA can regulate hundreds or even thousands of mRNAs, whereas several miRNAs may simultaneously target a single mRNA. The miRNA biogenesis pathway is regarded as a critical component of gene regulatory networks [14].

MiRNAs and their biogenesis

MiRNA biogenesis is initiated by transcription as an independent unit, generating primary miRNA transcripts (pri-miRNAs) through the activity of RNA polymerase II or III. Pri-miRNAs contain long, flexible terminal loops of at least 10 base pairs, along with extended single-stranded regions that can reach up to 1 kilobase. These transcripts also possess a 5’ cap and a polyadenylated 3’ tail [15]. Following transcription, pri-miRNAs are recognized and processed by the microprocessor complex, composed of Drosha and DGCR8, which produces precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs) of approximately 60–70 nucleotides. The pre-miRNAs are subsequently exported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm by exportin 5 (XPO5), which also protects them from degradation throughout the transport process [16].

In the cytoplasm, pre-miRNAs undergo additional processing by the RNase III enzyme Dicer, which binds to their 3′ overhang through its PAZ domain and cleaves them using two catalytic domains. This step produces mature miRNAs with an average length of about 22 nucleotides. Then, miRNAs are matured and incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to perform their regulatory functions. The RISC consists of Dicer, PACT, TRBP, Argonaute-2 protein (Ago2), and GW182. Once loaded into RISC, miRNAs bind to the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) of their target genes. Depending on the degree of complementarity between the miRNA and its target, this binding either represses translation when partial, or results in degradation of the target mRNA when complete (Fig. 1) [17, 18].

miRNA Biogenesis Pathways: RNA polymerase II or III transcribes pri-miRNA, which is cleaved in the nucleus by Drosha-DGCR8. The resulting pre-miRNA is exported to the cytoplasm via the exportin-5-Ran-GTP complex and subsequently processed into mature miRNA

Notably, miRNAs often exhibit imperfect complementarity with their targets, enabling them to regulate multiple genes simultaneously. Research on miRNAs has expanded substantially in recent years. Numerous studies have clarified the complex relationship between dysregulated miRNAs and cancer development. As a result, miRNAs are now regarded as promising targets for therapeutic development [19].

miRNAs in cancer

Studies using microarray expression data from various cancer tissues and cell types have shown that aberrant miRNA expression is a prevalent phenomenon in cancer. The roles and functional significance of miRNAs have been documented in multiple cancer types, including breast, colon, stomach, lung, thyroid, and PCa [20, 21]. MiRNAs can function as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors. As oncogenes, they inhibit tumor suppressor genes, thereby promoting tumor development. Conversely, as tumor suppressors, miRNAs downregulate oncogenes, thereby inhibiting cancer progression [22]. Tumor suppressor miRNAs, which are typically downregulated in cancer cells, inhibit the expression of proto-oncogenes and block tumor initiation or progression. For instance, the let-7 family has been identified as a tumor suppressor miRNA family [23]. In contrast, oncomiRs are miRNAs that promote tumor formation by targeting tumor suppressor genes and are often overexpressed or amplified in cancer cells, thus supporting tumorigenesis. Notably, miR-221 and miR-222 function as oncogenes by inhibiting the tumor suppressor gene p27Kip [24].

An increasing body of research has focused on the role of circulating miRNAs in blood as potential biomarkers for PCa. Numerous circulating miRNAs have been proposed as promising non-invasive biomarkers for PCa. For example, Dong and colleagues demonstrated that the miRNA let-7a binds to and downregulates CCND2 and E2F2, inducing cell cycle arrest at the G1/S phase in PC3 and LNCaP cell lines. It was further confirmed that let-7a can suppress prostate tumor progression in vivo using xenograft models [25]. Several miRNAs, such as miR-375, have been found to be dysregulated in the biological fluids of PCa patients compared with healthy individuals [26]. In addition, miRNAs, such as miR-141, showed differential expression levels in various stages of PCa and in response to treatment [27]. It has been suggested that miRNAs have great potential as non-invasive biomarkers for PCa. However, identifying reliable biomarkers for specific urological malignancies, such as PCa, remains challenging due to conflicting results in previous studies. Building on these discoveries, miRNA-based therapeutic strategies are being developed to effectively target cancer progression.

TGF-β signaling pathway

Transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), an important cytokine, influences multiple cellular processes, including growth regulation, differentiation, and transcriptional control [32]. Members of the TGF-β superfamily are also essential in the pathophysiology of multiple chronic diseases. In early-stage cancer, TGF-β typically exhibits tumor-suppressive properties and acts as a potent inhibitor of epithelial cell proliferation [33]. Conversely, in advanced stages, TGF-β promotes tumor growth by inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which facilitates tumor metastasis [34].

The canonical TGF-β signaling pathway is initiated when a ligand binds to a dimer of type II transmembrane receptors. This interaction triggers a signaling cascade in which type II receptors phosphorylate type I receptors, leading to the formation of a heterotetrameric complex. Phosphorylation of type I receptors displaces the inhibitory FKBP12 protein, thereby allowing intracellular proteins, particularly SMADs, to engage [35]. Upon activation of the type I receptor (TGFBR1), SMAD2/3 becomes phosphorylated. These phosphorylated SMADs subsequently form a complex with SMAD4 and translocate to the nucleus, where they orchestrate the transcriptional regulation of TGF-β target genes. The SARA protein is essential for recruiting SMAD2/3 to TGF-β receptors, thereby enhancing signaling fidelity [35, 36].

In addition to the classical SMAD-dependent pathways, TGF-β signaling can activate non-canonical pathways, including IκB kinases (IKKs), mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI-3k), Akt, and Rho family GTPases. These non-canonical pathways can interact with SMAD proteins or independently trigger signaling cascades that significantly affect gene expression (Fig. 2) [37, 38].

MiRNAs, TGF-β signaling, and PCa

It has been elucidated that alterations in miRNA expression in PCa tissues and urinary exosomes highlight their potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [39]. For example, reduced expression of miR-133b in PCa and its associated bone metastases correlates with upregulation of TGFBR1 and TGFBR2, thereby enhancing TGF-β signaling and, consequently, promoting PCa cell migration and invasion. Notably, restoration of miR-133b expression in mouse models has been demonstrated to be capable of inhibiting the development of PCa bone metastasis [40].

Similarly, miR-505-3p has emerged as a critical regulator of PCa progression. Reduced expression of miR-505-3p affects SMAD2/3, thereby facilitating cancer cell migration and invasion. Furthermore, lower levels of miR-505-3p in PCa bone metastases exhibit a negative correlation with serum PSA levels, Gleason grade, and bone metastasis-free survival in patients [41]. A study showed that overexpression of miR-1260b inhibited SMAD4 and promoted PCa growth, whereas genistein-induced downregulation of miR-120b increased SMAD4 and sFRP1 expression, thereby inducing cell death [42]. MiR-133b inhibits PCa progression by downregulating TGFβR1 and TGFβR2. Reduced levels of miR-133b stimulate TGF-β signaling, promoting PCa progression [43]. Several miRNAs downregulate TGFRβR2, thereby promoting PCa progression. For instance, miR-21 downregulates TGFRβR2 and promotes PCa through a positive feedback loop with AR [44].

Another notable miRNA, miR-301a, has been identified to inhibit SMAD4 expression, thereby promoting proliferation of PCa cells and tumor formation in vivo, as demonstrated by Li and colleagues [45]. Furthermore, miRNAs modulate SMAD2 expression; for example, overexpression of miR-486-5p in PCa has been shown to suppress SMAD2 expression, enhancing cellular migration and proliferation. Correspondingly, in vivo studies have indicated that inhibition of miR-486-5p effectively reduces tumor growth [46].

Furthermore, SMAD4 expression is regulated by miR-183. A study by Ueno and colleagues demonstrated that miR-183 reduces cell proliferation and motility in PCa cells and significantly suppresses prostate tumor growth in vivo. Their study identified Dkk-3 and SMAD4 as potential targets of miR-183 [47]. Taken together, these findings support miRNAs as potential novel biomarkers for PCa and suggest that targeted inhibition of specific miRNAs may offer therapeutic benefits in treating this malignancy.

Notch signaling pathway

The Notch signaling pathway is pivotal for regulating an array of cellular functions, including proliferation, cell death, and differentiation, with its effects being context-dependent, thereby promoting or inhibiting these processes as dictated by the specific biological milieu. Notch signaling is critical for regulating cell-fate decisions during development, maintaining homeostasis, and mediating pathological conditions, underscoring its role in modulating cellular behavior [48, 49].

Notch signaling is activated through direct interactions between neighboring cells. During this process, a transmembrane ligand on one cell binds to the Notch receptor on an adjacent cell. Notch receptors (Notch1, Notch2, Notch3, and Notch4) are synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), processed in the Golgi apparatus, and then transported to the cell surface. These receptors interact with ligands, such as Jagged-1, Jagged-2, and Delta-like ligands (DLL-1, 3, and 4) on neighboring cells [48, 50]. Ligand binding triggers a two-step cleavage process: first, the receptor is cleaved by ADAM metalloproteases, followed by γ-secretase-mediated cleavage, which releases the intracellular domain of the Notch receptor (NICD). Upon release, NICD translocates to the nucleus, where it assembles with other proteins to form a transcriptional complex that regulates target gene expression. Furthermore, NICD can directly activate genes, such as PI3K and Akt, through non-canonical signaling pathways, thereby expanding the functional scope of Notch signaling (Fig. 3) [51].

This signaling in oncogenesis has garnered considerable attention in cancer research, given its conserved role in regulating fundamental biological processes. Dysregulation of this pathway has been observed in a spectrum of malignancies, such as PCa. Alterations in the expression of Notch components have been consistently documented in PCa cell lines, murine models, and human PCa specimens, manifesting at both mRNA and protein levels. Functional studies manipulating Notch pathway components further indicate a dual role, as Notch signaling can exhibit both tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting effects, contingent on the cellular context [52]. Emerging evidence positions miRNAs as crucial regulators of the Notch signaling pathway in the context of PCa [53]. For instance, the overexpression of miR-34a has been shown to downregulate both AR and Notch1, thereby inhibiting PCa cell proliferation through modulation of the Notch pathway [54]. Similarly, research conducted by Sun et al. demonstrated that miR-139 diminishes cyclin D1 and MMP-7 and MMP-9 expression, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation via targeted downregulation of Notch1 in PCa cells [55].

Bauer et al. showed that in vitro overexpression of miR-449a silenced NOTCH pathway genes, including NOTCH1 and HES1. In PCa cells, inhibition of NOTCH signaling or HES1 knockdown reduced oncogenic traits, indicating a pro-tumorigenic role for NOTCH in prostate cancer [56]. Moreover, several miRNAs have been identified as significant modulators of Notch signaling in PCa. Notably, enforced expression of miR-628 can inhibit cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and spheroid formation while concurrently diminishing stemness-associated markers. Mechanistically, miR-628 binds to the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of Jagged1 (JAG1), leading to downregulation of Notch signaling. This interaction decreases the expression of transcription factors Snail and Slug, thereby limiting the EMT and invasiveness in PC3 cells [57]. These findings clearly show that miRNAs can effectively modulate the Notch signaling pathway, highlighting their potential as therapeutic targets for PCa treatment.

PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway

The phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway is fundamental to regulating a vast array of cellular processes, including growth, metabolism, and survival in both normal and pathological contexts [58]. This signaling plays a crucial role in mediating cellular responses to mitogenic and oncogenic stimuli, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of a diverse spectrum of cancers [59].

This pathway is activated upon binding of ligands, such as insulin, hormones, and growth factors, to G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) or receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs). This interaction triggers phosphorylation of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), which subsequently recruits Akt to the plasma membrane. Then, mTORC2 and PDK1 phosphorylate Akt, enabling it to regulate multiple downstream targets, including components of the mTOR complex that control protein synthesis and cellular growth. Concurrently, other Akt substrates, such as GSK-3 and FoxO1, are inhibited, promoting cellular proliferation and survival [60]. Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), as a tumor suppressor, serves as a critical negative regulator of this pathway by dephosphorylating PIP3 back to PIP2, thereby attenuating signaling (Fig. 4) [61].

Interaction between MiRNAs and PI3K/AKT/mTOR Signaling Pathway in PCa: Activated PI3K converts PIP2 into PIP3, which is negatively regulated by PTEN through dephosphorylation. Activated Akt regulates downstream proteins, including mTOR, Bcl-2 family members, and glycogen synthase kinase 3, thereby promoting various cellular processes

Mutations and aberrant regulation of key genes within the PAM pathway are frequently observed across various malignancies. For instance, PI3K overexpression is observed in ovarian and cervical carcinomas [62, 63], while mutations within this pathway are prevalent in breast, glioblastoma, and gastric cancers [64–66]. In the context of PCa, the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway is substantially implicated, with recent studies elucidating its interactions with miRNAs. In PCa tissues, miR-133a-3p expression is significantly diminished, especially in samples with bone metastases. Decreased expression of miR-133a-3p is associated with advanced clinicopathological characteristics and a reduction in bone metastasis-free survival. Research has shown that miR-133a-3p can reduce bone metastasis in PCa by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt signaling cascade through direct interactions with cytokine receptors, including MET, EGFR, IGF1R, and FGFR1 [67].

Conversely, a study conducted by Gao et al. elucidated that miR-146b is upregulated in PCa tissues. Overexpression of miR-146b enhances PCa cell proliferation by promoting autophagy and suppressing PTEN via the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. In contrast, downregulation of miR-146b inhibits cellular proliferation, indicating its potential as a therapeutic target [68]. Indeed, miR-1 is downregulated in PCa cells and has been demonstrated to diminish cell survival and proliferation through modulating the c-Met/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. Likewise, the tumor suppressor miRNA, miR-99b, is frequently downregulated and influences differentiation, proliferation, invasion, and migration in PCa [69]. Additional miRNAs, including miR-128, miR-26a, and miR-381, have been demonstrated to significantly influence the advancement of PCa through the PI3K/Akt/mTOR Signaling pathway [70]. These findings collectively illuminate the critical role of miRNAs in the regulation of this signaling pathway, underscoring their potential as therapeutic targets in the context of PCa. Ongoing research is anticipated to further elucidate miRNA-mediated mechanisms and their implications in the progression of this malignancy.

Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway

This pathway is a crucial signaling network that impacts a myriad of biological processes, such as tissue homeostasis, embryonic development, and disease progression [71]. This pathway is divided into two main branches: the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which is β–catenin–dependent, and the non-canonical pathway, which is β–catenin–independent. Wnt dysregulation has been observed in a wide range of pathologies, including cardiovascular, pulmonary, hepatic, and neurological disorders [72, 73]. Furthermore, its abnormal activation has been associated with several malignancies, including adrenocortical carcinoma, ovarian cancer, and PCa [74, 75].

In the canonical Wnt pathway, Wnt ligands bind to Frizzled receptors and the LRP5/6 co-receptors (low-density lipoprotein receptor–related proteins), initiating a complex signaling cascade [76]. Notably, these receptors are of considerable physiological importance in oncological contexts. For instance, LRP5 has been linked to conditions such as osteoporosis, osteosarcoma, and PCa, while LRP6, which shares structural similarities with LRP5, has been observed in lung, breast, and liver tumors (Fig. 5) [77]. Recent investigations have illuminated the intricate interplay between Wnt signaling and miRNAs, underscoring their potential as therapeutic targets [78]. For example, miR-34a has been demonstrated to inhibit Wnt/β-catenin signaling in breast cancer [79], while miR-101 appears to regulate this pathway, functioning as a tumor suppressor [80]. Additionally, miR-130a-3p has been shown to mitigate colorectal cancer proliferation by targeting Wnt1 and its downstream effectors, such as cyclin D1 [81].

The TCF/LEF complex then enhances transcription of key genes, including Snail and Twist, promoting EMT. In the right panel, absence of Wnt ligands allows GSK-3β to phosphorylate β-catenin within a destruction complex containing CKIα and Axin, leading to β-catenin degradation through the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway

In PCa, studies suggest that miRNAs exert significant regulatory roles in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. In this context, Ming et al. reported that miR-95-3p acts as a protumorigenic factor in PCa cells by enhancing EMT and Wnt/β-catenin signaling [82]. Furthermore, miR-182 has been shown to facilitate PCa progression by targeting negative regulators of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, including glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β), Axin, adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), and casein kinase 1 (CK1) [83]. On the other hand, several miRNAs have been identified that suppress tumor growth by modulating the aforementioned pathway. For instance, miR-320 downregulates this signaling pathway, leading to reduced prostate stem-like tumor-initiating cells and drug resistance, while concurrently reversing EMT-induced tumorigenesis [84]. Similarly, miR-138 has been found to inhibit metastasis by upregulating E-cadherin and downregulating vimentin expression, thereby attenuating Wnt/β-catenin signaling [85]. Reports indicate an inverse association between FZD7 and miR-613 expression in PCa tissues. FZD7, a receptor for Wnt ligands, can be inhibited by miR-613 in PCa cells. Consequently, upregulation of miR-613 decreases the proliferation and invasion of PCa cells by suppressing FZD7/Wnt signaling [86].

In summary, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a critical role in PCa progression. The interplay between this pathway and miRNAs provides a promising avenue for developing novel therapeutic strategies. Further clarification of miRNAs’ specific regulatory functions in Wnt signaling could lead to significant improvements in clinical outcomes for PCa patients and other malignancies with dysregulated Wnt signaling.

NF-κB signaling pathway

This pathway is an essential modulator of immunological and inflammatory responses. This family of transcription factors is pivotal in both innate and adaptive immune responses, serving as a crucial regulator of inflammatory processes [87, 88]. It includes five proteins—p50, p52, and p65 (known as NF-κB1, NF-κB2, and RelA, respectively), as well as RelB and c-Rel—which can form various heterodimeric and homodimeric complexes [89].

NF-κB activation occurs through two main pathways: the classical (canonical) and the alternative (non-canonical). The first is triggered by ligands such as growth factors, antigens, and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1/6. In contrast, the second is activated by specific members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) family, including CD40 ligand (CD40L), receptor activator of nuclear factor Kappa-B ligand (RANKL), and B cell-activating factor (BAFF) [90]. These two pathways lead to the phosphorylation and ubiquitination of IκB proteins, enabling active NF-κB dimers to translocate into the nucleus and regulate the transcription of target genes. The NF-κB signaling pathway plays a significant role in inflammation, proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis (Fig. 6) [91].

MiRNAs in NF-κB Signaling Pathway: TNF-α, IL-7, and EGF receptors (left panel), as well as BAFF receptor and CD40 (right panel), can be activated or repressed by miRNAs. The expression of tumor-associated genes can be either suppressed or promoted through NF-κB activation

Dysregulation of NF-κB is also linked to various cancers, with miRNAs serving as key regulators of NF-κB signaling in PCa. For instance, Gu et al. demonstrated that miR-191 suppresses angiogenesis by activating NF-κB signaling, which in turn upregulates p21 and downregulates matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP-1), thereby inhibiting endothelial cell proliferation and migration [92]. Conversely, tumor-associated angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis can be driven by miR-526b and miR-655 via activation of the NF-κB pathway [93]. Other miRNAs may act as suppressors or oncogenes in PCa. For instance, miR-520c and miR-373 promote metastasis and invasion by activating MMP-9 and enhancing the Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK signaling cascade, which leads to NF-κB activation [94]. Conversely, miR-372 targets p65, repressing its transcriptional activity and inducing G0/G1 cell cycle arrest in PCa cells, highlighting its potential as a tumor suppressor [95].

Additionally, miR-96-5p has been shown to downregulate N-myc downstream-regulated gene 1 (NDRG1), thereby activating NF-κB and promoting EMT, migration, and metastasis in PCa [96]. These findings underscore the critical importance of targeting NF-κB signaling, along with its regulatory miRNAs, as potential therapeutic strategies for PCa. Future research should aim to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these interactions and their clinical implications for targeting aberrant NF-κB activity in PCa.

Role of MiRNAs in tumor microenvironment

The tumor microenvironment (TME) has been identified as a key component in cancer biology that contributes to treatment resistance [97]. Tumor cells and surrounding immune cells can communicate via miRNAs. Alterations in miRNA expression within tumor cells can influence the immune microenvironment [98]. In this regard, miRNAs control the production of chemokines or cytokines, thereby affecting the recruitment and proliferation of immune cells. Exosomes represent a key mechanism for miRNA transport and can modulate the immune microenvironment by delivering tumor-derived miRNAs to immune cells [99].

Exosomal MiRNAs

Exosomes, membrane-bound extracellular vesicles (EVs), are present in all body fluids and include tumor-derived exosomes (TEX), which are released by a large number of cells. In the plasma of patients with various types of cancer, TEX participates in transferring diverse molecular cargo, including prostaglandin E2, TGF-β1, IL-10, PD-L1, TRAIL, FasL, and CD39/73, ultimately facilitating robust intercellular communication [100]. Moreover, exosomes deliver diverse genetic cargo, including miRNAs, RNA, and/or DNA, thereby facilitating effective intercellular communication [101].

Immune regulation

The release of tumor-derived exosomes can inhibit dendritic cell (DC) differentiation and alter their function, turning them from antigen-presenting cells into negative regulators of immune responses [31]. The expression of TLR4, TNF-α, and IL-12 in dendritic cells (DCs) can be inhibited by exposure to pancreatic tumor-derived exosomes (TEX) carrying miR-203, leading to impaired cellular immunity [102]. It has been stated that a number of miRNAs, such as exosomal miR-105, miR-125b, miR-130b, miR-141, miR-155, miR-375, and miR-409 via targeting CDK6 and small heterodimer partner (SHP) can promote mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET), AR activation, and EMT formation in cancer cells [31, 103–106]. Moreover, it has been reported that two distinct proteins, Integrin Subunit Beta 1 (ITGB1) and Integrin Subunit Alpha 3 (ITGA3), found in exosomes collected from PC3 and LNCaP cells, play a significant role in the tumor migration and invasion [107].

Macrophage polarization

Macrophage polarization, which plays a significant role in immune tolerance and maintenance of physiological homeostasis, is regulated by miRNAs [108]. In tumors, for example, miR-21, miR-29a, let-7b-5p, and miR-101-3p can modulate tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) through a number of signaling pathways, such as SOCS/JAK-STAT and NF-κB, leading to the production of TNF-α, IL-6/8, IL-8, and CD80, thereby promoting cancer progression and inflammatory responses [109–112].

NK and T cell regulation by MiRNAs

Tumor-derived miRNAs can also modulate NK cells and T lymphocytes to produce antitumor cytokines, such as IL-4, IFN-γ, IL-13, and perforin [113]. Tumor microvesicles, through secretion of miR-214, can reduce PTEN expression while promoting IL-10 production and differentiation of Tregs in CD4 + T cells [114]. Overall, in the TME, miRNA regulation of immune cells reveals mechanisms underlying tumor progression and may suggest potential targets for tumor cure.

Androgen-regulated MiRNAs in PCa

By binding to specific targets and modulating diverse cancer-associated gene networks, miRNAs represent powerful tools for developing novel therapeutic strategies to address PCa [115].

Androgen ablation therapy has been found to be highly effective in treating advanced PCa. Furthermore, miR-133a-5p has been identified as a suppressor of AR-positive PCa cell proliferation by blocking the FUS/AR interaction, thereby increasing sensitivity to androgen ablation therapy [116]. Notably, AR is crucial to PCa pathogenesis, with its expression observed in the majority of both primary and metastatic cases [29]. MiR-31 directly suppresses AR expression by binding to the coding area of AR mRNA. Consequently, miR-31 directly represses genes associated with cell cycle control. Notably, miR-31 can be inhibited by AR as a transcriptional target, establishing a negative regulatory loop between miR-31 and AR [117].

According to Coarfa et al., the expression of 12 miRNAs is decreased in metastatic PCa, including miR-1, miR-31, miR-4-1−5p, miR-133a/b, miR-135a, miR-143-3p, miR-145-3p, miR-205, miR-221-3p, miR-221-5p, and miR-222-3p [118]. They also discovered that substantial direct binding of AR was seen around the miR-135a locus and that androgen strongly induces miR-135a. This modulation contributes to the increase in AR in CRPC, as miR-135a activates AR signaling and helps restore AR expression under androgen-depleted conditions [119].

MiRNAs associated with cell migration, invasion, and EMT

EMT has been shown to correlate with the advancement of PCa, including tumor metastasis, drug resistance, recurrence, and the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [120]. Research indicates that changes in the expression of EMT-associated genes result not only from genetic abnormalities but also from intricate interactions between miRNAs and linked pathways [121]. It was discovered that miR-205 participates in EMT and is downmodulated in PCa. Through the suppression of ZEB1 and ZEB2 and the resulting elevated E-cadherin levels, it functioned as an EMT suppressor linked to metastasis [122].

It is now known that p53 can regulate miR-145. The absence of miR-143/145 can modulate Golgi membrane protein 1, thereby inducing PCa cell migration and invasion [123]. Expression analysis indicated that miR-466 was downregulated in PCa compared to normal tissues. It has been reported that in vitro upregulation of miR-466 in metastatic PCa cells reduces oncogenic characteristics—such as cell proliferation, migration, and invasion—and enhances cell cycle arrest and tumor cell death compared to control treatments [124]. Zhou et al. demonstrated that engrailed homeobox 2 (EN2) is inversely regulated by miR-605, and that downregulation of miR-605 promotes the proliferation and invasion of PCa cells by upregulating EN2, ultimately advancing PCa [125].

Cell cycle and PCa

Cell division comprises four distinct phases: G1, S, G2, and M, corresponding to cell growth, DNA synthesis, preparation for mitosis, and mitosis, respectively [126]. Checkpoints at both transitions, G1/S and G2/M, are essential for proper progression and preventing DNA damage. Dysregulation of the cell cycle is a hallmark of cancer, often resulting in uncontrolled cellular proliferation [127].

Cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) serve as crucial regulators of the cell cycle. Specifically, CDK1 regulates G2/M progression, CDK2 controls S phase and DNA replication, and CDK4/6 manage the G1/S transition [126]. Dysregulation of these CDKs has been implicated in various malignancies, including PCa [128].

Recent investigations have identified miRNAs as key modulators of the cell cycle in cancer. For example, miR-449a inhibits PCa progression by downregulating cyclin D1 and preventing the phosphorylation of retinoblastoma (Rb) protein, effectively blocking the G1/S transition [129]. Additionally, miR-139 enhances p21 expression and downregulates cyclin D1, thereby reducing proliferation and metastasis by obstructing the G2 phase of the cell cycle [130]. Moreover, miR-107 and miR-186 impede the G1/S transition by targeting CDK6, thereby suppressing PCa cell proliferation [131]. In contrast, miR-221/222 functions as an oncogene by inhibiting apoptotic factors such as caspase-10, which subsequently promotes PCa growth (Fig. 7) [132]. In conclusion, miRNAs play critical roles in the regulation of the cell cycle, and their dysregulation significantly contributes to the progression of PCa. Therefore, targeting miRNAs implicated in cell cycle regulation in PCa represents a potential therapeutic intervention.

Future perspectives

Prostate cancer remains a major clinical challenge due to its high incidence, heterogeneity, and limitations of current diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. The limited accuracy of conventional screening methods highlights the need for reliable non-invasive biomarkers. MicroRNAs are key regulators of prostate cancer–associated signaling pathways and show strong potential as diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic tools. Restoration of tumor-suppressive miRNAs, such as miR-1, miR-34a, miR-29b, miR-99b-5p, and miR-34b/c, along with inhibition of oncogenic miRNAs, including miR-146b, miR-216a, miR-200c, miR-21, miR-30e, and miR-210-3p, may represent effective strategies to limit PCa progression.

Future studies should focus on elucidating miRNA–signaling pathway interactions, optimizing miRNA-based therapeutic delivery, such as including nanoparticle- and exosome-based platforms, and validating miRNA biomarkers in large-scale clinical trials to facilitate clinical translation.
