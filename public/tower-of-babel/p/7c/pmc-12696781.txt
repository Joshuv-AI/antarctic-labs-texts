Synthesis, Photophysical Properties, and Practical Hg(II)‐Sensing Applications of a Rigidified Phenolo‐Merocyanine Dye
Authors: Kévin Renault, Zuzana Pohancenikova, Maël Croizier, Vincent Gaumerd, Manhattan Lebrun, Michel Chalot, Anthony Romieu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmong the strategies available to improve photophysical properties of organic fluorophores intended for imaging and sensing, structural modifications allowing for scaffold rigidification are particularly effective for enhancing yellow emission and photostability. In order to develop novel high‐performance phenolic fluorophores, we have explored the synthesis of a conformationally restrained analog of flexible Brooker's merocyanine. We had the idea of applying a rigidification approach previously devised for cyanine dyes. Thus, the implementation of an acid‐catalyzed domino double cyclization process allowed the synthesis of fluorescent phenol KR306. A comprehensive photophysical study has highlighted its outstanding optical properties in neutral aq. buffered conditions (a brightness of 71,500 M−1 cm−1 at pH 7.5 for a yellow emission centered at ca. 560 nm). The hydroxyl group of KR306 can be modified to regulate its fluorescence properties. This feature has been demonstrated through the preparation of chromo‐fluorogenic thiophosphinate and thiophosphonate esters suitable for mercuric cations Hg(II) sensing in neat aq. buffers. The validation of these chemodosimeters has highlighted superior sensing performances of thiophosphinate derivative 5. Furthermore, its ability to be activated by microorganisms in kefir grains doped with Hg(II) ions, was confirmed by simply observing a color change by the naked eye.Keywords: conformational restriction, hydroxyl group protection‐deprotection, mercuric cation, merocyanine dye, reaction‐based fluorescent probe, thiophosphinate ester

Introduction

In the field of fluorescence molecular sensing, the advent of reaction‐based approaches, typically involving the protection‐deprotection of an optically tunable functional group, was made possible by advances in the chemistry and photophysics of fluorescent anilines and phenols, as first highlighted by the Ma group [1] and has since been well‐illustrated by other reviews covering more recent achievements [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The vast majority of these fluorogenic dyes belong to common fluorophore classes such as (aza)‐Bodipy, coumarin, cyanine, N‐alkyl‐1,8‐naphthalimide, phenoxazine and xanthene dyes, or related hybrid structures. Due to their ability to undergo facile deprotonation through intra‐ or intermolecular proton transfer in either the electronic ground or excited state, to yield an emissive conjugated base, phenolic fluorophores have come to be considered as essential reporters in a myriad of reaction‐based fluorescent probes modulated by ESIPT and/or ICT photo‐processes [10]. In addition to well‐known and popular fluorescein, 7‐hydroxycoumarin (also known as umbelliferone), 2‐(2'‐hydroxyphenyl)benzoxazole (HBO), and resorufin dyes (“native” forms and derivatives) [10], a set of more sophisticated hydroxy‐containing cyanine (i.e., QCy7 scaffold) [11, 12] and hemicyanine hybrid scaffolds [13, 14, 15] (especially those fused with dicyanoisophorone (DCI) [16, 17] or dicyanomethylene‐4H‐pyran (DCM) [18, 19] moiety as acceptor unit) have emerged during the past decade with the aim of red‐shifting and tuning spectral features within the first near‐infrared window (NIR‐I, 650–900 nm) (Figure 1). The recent availability of these long‐wavelength fluorogenic dyes has promoted the facile construction of activatable fluorescent probes suitable for “turn‐on” biosensing/bioimaging and/or molecular theranostics (i.e., fluorogenic prodrugs) in living systems [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. However, their large molecular size, linked to extended π‐conjugated systems, and the flexibility of their polymethine chain or para‐hydroxystyryl arm may adversely affect the performances of the corresponding probes, especially (1) their ability to recognize and react with the targeted analyte (e.g., enzymatic biomarkers), (2) their physicochemical properties (e.g., poor aq. solubility and aggregation propensity), and/or (3) the fluorescence brightness of the released phenolate.

One possible way to overcome these limitations, is to devise structurally compact phenolic fluorophores, retaining both valuable spectral features and the fluorogenic reactivity. We thus envisioned a backbone‐rigidification strategy [20, 21] readily applied to the indolenium analog of Brooker's merocyanine 1 (popularized by Eastman‐Kodak company in the 1950s) [22, 23] to yield a phenolo‐merocyanine named KR306 (Figure 1).

To the best of our knowledge, a few fluorogenic dyes based on the same molecular design have already been reported in the literature [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. The three published examples reflect the sole contribution of the Kim group with compounds named NCC1, CCNa1, and HCC1 (Figure 2) [29, 30, 31, 32]. Interestingly, primary aniline NCC1 was successfully used in the preparation of azo‐based type‐I photosensitizers ACC1 and ACC2, suitable for two‐photon photodynamic therapy (2P‐PDT) [29, 30]; its N‐functionalization and subsequent covalent conjugation to N‐(2‐methoxyphenyl)‐N'‐(4‐amino‐2‐methoxyphenyl)‐1,7‐diaza‐15‐crown‐5 led to a red‐emissive 2P fluorescent probe for sensing mitochondrial sodium ions influx in live cells and mouse brain tissue [31]. Lastly, naphthol‐based fluorophore HCC1 was identified as an effective NIR‐I ratiometric 2P fluorescent probe for pH measurements in human stomach cancer tissue [32]. Surprisingly, despite these promising applications, fluorogenic dyes NCC1 and HCC1 were not used in the design of reaction‐based fluorescent probes. To both fill this gap and achieve midterm objectives of a research program devoted to the development of a rapid onsite detection method for toxic mercuric cations (Hg(II) cations), we have recently considered the use of KR306 in activity‐based sensing via Hg(II)‐induced deprotection of its hydroxyl group capped with a thiophosphinate or a thiophosphonate moiety [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39].

Structures of rigidified merocyanine dyes and related 2PA photosensitizers and fluorescent chemosensor for Na+ cations, reported by the Kim group [29, 30, 31, 32]. [2PA = two‐photon absorption, X− = counter‐ion not specified].

Herein, we report a concise and efficient synthetic route to this novel conformationally restrained phenolo‐merocyanine dye. A comprehensive photophysical study was next conducted to highlight valuable spectral features of KR306 in aq. physiological conditions. The practical utility of this unprecedented fluorogenic phenol was also explored through the design of two original reaction‐based fluorescent probes responsive to toxic Hg(II) cations. Gratifyingly, good sensing performances of a thiophosphinate‐based chemodosimeter have facilitated the discovery of a novel living system suitable for the real‐time visualization of its selective activation without resorting to sophisticated optical instrumentation. These are kefir grains, which are found to be resistant to high contents of heavy metal cations, including Hg(II) [40], and thus recently assessed as a valuable biosorbent for the removal of these pollutants from contaminated matrices [41].

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Rigidified Phenolo‐Merocyanine KR306

Inspired by the seminal works of Amersham Biosciences company (conducted in close collaboration with the Waggoner group) [42, 43], and more recent achievements from the Brown and Schnermann groups [44, 45, 46], on the design and synthesis of conformationally restrained polymethine cyanine dyes, particularly to improve their fluorescence brightness, we devised a three‐step synthesis whose key transformation is an acid‐catalyzed tandem cyclization of a bifunctional merocyanine bearing a masked aldehyde and a further ortho‐phenol group (Scheme 1). Indeed, biphasic aq. acidic conditions were found to be effective in readily converting 3 into KR306 through this domino reaction. As depicted in Figure 3, two distinct mechanisms can be proposed even though the first one described is much more likely, especially in acidic conditions, and already assumed for the synthesis of related conformationally restrained chromophores [28, 44, 45, 46]: (1) the common intermediate formed by Prins cyclization underwent dehydration and subsequent intramolecular oxa‐Michael addition of pendant phenol, or (2) prototropy and cyclodehydration by intramolecular nucleophilic substitution of pendant phenol, to form 2H‐benzopyran ring fused to the remaining tricyclic part of fluorophore scaffold. First, commercial 2,3,3‐trimethyl‐3H‐indole was quaternized with 2‐(2‐bromoethyl)‐1,3‐dioxolane in dry MeCN under reflux [24]. The resulting indolenium salt 2 was isolated in a moderate yield (50%) by simple trituration in cooled Et2O. Further attempts aimed at improving the yield have involved pre‐generation of more reactive 2‐(2‐iodoethyl)‐1,3‐dioxolane through Finkelstein reaction (i.e., treatment with KI (1.5 equiv.) in dry MeCN at 50°C for 1 h), and purification of 2 by column chromatography over deactivated silica gel, but led to nothing. The indolenium salt 2 was next reacted with 2,4‐dihydroxybenzaldehyde in dry EtOH at room temperature for 17 h to give conventional flexible merocyanine dye 3 in a quantitative manner. It was also possible to shorten the time of this Knoevenagel reaction to 4 h by refluxing heating, but under these harsher conditions, a less clear process leading to a more complex mixture was revealed by RP‐HPLC‐MS monitoring. In view of the good purity of the crude merocyanine 3 upon its recovery by simple evaporation of EtOH and drying under vacuum, it was directly engaged in the final domino double cyclization reaction. Thus, prolonged treatment of 3 with aq. 50% H2SO4 in CHCl3 (1:41, v/v) at room temperature resulted in the formation of its rigidified analog with a high conversion rate. KR306 was isolated in a good yield by column chromatography over silica gel (yield 64% over two steps for synthesis conducted on the gram scale). Optimal purity (> 95%) required for photophysical characterization was readily achieved by further purification by semi‐preparative RP‐HPLC. KR306 was then recovered as red amorphous powder (TFA salt, TFA mass content = 33% (1.4 molecules), determined by ionic chromatography). Its structure was unambiguously confirmed by ESI‐HRMS and detailed 1H, 13C, and 19F NMR spectroscopic analyses. [1H‐1H] COSY and [1H‐13C] HSQC 2D NMR experiments were used to completely assign all signals observed in 1H and 13C spectra (Supporting Information: Section S4). The high purity of KR306 (> 97% whatever the wavelength channel used for UV‐Vis detection) was confirmed by RP‐HPLC‐based analytical control.

Photophysical Properties of Rigidified Phenolo‐Merocyanine KR306

Since sensing applications envisioned for KR306 will take advantage of its ability to display a dramatic bathochromic shift of absorption/emission maxima upon phenol deprotonation, the photophysical properties of this fluorophore were preferentially determined in neutral aq. buffers (phosphate and HEPES buffers, pH 7.3 and 7.5, respectively). However, to establish a correlation between the results obtained from fluorescence assays conducted under buffered neutral conditions and those collected through RP‐HPLC‐fluorescence analyses (involving the use of an acidic mobile phase), and related to fluorogenic activation of Hg(II)‐sensitive probes 5 and 6 (vide infra), spectral characterization of KR306 was also achieved in aq. 0.1% formic acid (FA, pH 2.2). At physiological pH, whatever the buffer used, we first noted the absence of mirror likeness between the absorption and fluorescence emission bands due to the narrowing of this latter one. This is a typical feature of negative solvatochromic merocyanines for which the excited state S1 is less polar than the ground state S0 [50]. On the basis of the Dähne's scheme of the three limiting resonance structures of merocyanines (Figure 5), we assumed a significant contribution from the zwitterionic polyene structure A3 in the ground state S0 of KR306, and an enhanced contribution of the ideal polymethine structure A2 to its electronic structure in the fluorescence state S1. From the UV‐Vis spectra displayed in Figure 4, it can be seen that both shape and position of absorption curve of KR306 were altered compared to those observed with flexible parent merocyanine 1 (less broad Δλ 1/2max = 65 nm vs. 130 nm and red‐shift of 22 nm). KR306 exhibited maximum absorption/emission at 542 and 562 nm, respectively. This result indicated that conformational restriction was helpful for the red shift of the dye wavelength.

Dähne representation of the three limiting structures of merocyanine KR306.

Furthermore, the narrowing of the [1] ππ* electronic band of KR306 could be interpreted as the higher prevalence of its phenolate form as supported by the decrease of the phenol ground‐state pKa value determined by drawing pH‐dependent absorption curves (see Figures S15 and S16 for raw and fitted curves, pKa = 6.9 (KR306) vs. 7.2 (1)). This value is also lower than those reported in the literature for popular long‐wavelength phenolic fluorophores DCI‐OH (pKa = 7.9) [16] and DCM‐OH (pKa = 7.1) [48], and therefore unlike these compounds, only phenolate form is present at pH 7.4. This valuable attribute also distinguishes it from HCC1 (pKa = 7.5) [32], whose far‐red emissive deprotonated state was mainly observed as pH > 8. For these three fluorescent phenols already published, the presence of a more extended π‐conjugated system leads to red‐shifted absorption/emission maxima wavelengths compared to those of KR306, but brightness values of DCI‐OH and DCM‐OH are much more modest, partly due to the molecular flexibility of para‐hydroxystyryl arm known to promote non‐radiative decay (Table 1, entries 12–15). Indeed, spectacular molar extinction coefficients and fluorescence quantum yields were obtained for KR306 in neutral aq. buffers compared to the limited absorptivity and lack of emissive ability observed for the flexible molecule 1. Non‐radiative deactivation processes were less favored owing to the rigidity present in the conformationally restrained merocyanine core, as also reflected by a relatively long fluorescence lifetime of 3 ns for the excited state S1. We noted both a perfect aq. solubility of KR306, whatever the concentration range used for these spectral measurements (0.1–25 µM), and no formation of non‐emissive H‐type aggregates as evidenced by the perfect matching between the absorption and excitation spectra. The effects of acidic pH on the spectral properties of KR306 were also measured. At pH 2.2, phenol form exhibited an absorption maximum at 464 nm (hypsochromic shift of 78 nm) and an emission maximum at 562 nm with a lower quantum yield of 42%. These changes in spectral properties are associated with modulations in the intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) behavior of KR306 caused by the lower electron‐donating ability of the protonated form of its phenol group. In view of the main features observed for the emission curve: wide (Δλ 1/2max = 91 nm vs. 45 nm in neutral aq. buffers), symmetric, bell‐shaped and lack of distinct fine structure, we assumed an electronic structure nearing the zwitterionic polyene structure A3 for the fluorescence state S1 [50], arising from the photo‐excited deprotonation of the phenol moiety. The spectral behavior of this phenol form may support the hypothesis of a possible fluorescence quenching of KR306 by masking its hydroxyl group with a protecting group or substrate directly [5] or through a self‐immolative linker [51]. This opens the way to the facile construction of analyte‐responsive chemodosimeters suitable for practical sensing applications as illustrated below with fluorogenic thiophosphinate or thiophosphonate esters reactive towards Hg(II) cations.

Rational Design, Synthesis, and Photophysical Properties of Hg(II)‐Responsive Chemodosimeters Based on Phenol KR306

Heavy metal pollution (in particular cadmium (Cd), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb)) remains a major environmental concern. Since the early 2000s, global and collective awareness has led both to the ratification of international conventions (e.g., the Minamata Convention on Mercury, adopted in October 2013) [52] and the implementation of proactive policies to reduce the release of these metals arising from specific industrial facilities and activities (e.g., artisanal and small‐scale mining operations, stationary combustion of coal, …). Furthermore, stringent permissible limits for drinking water have been established (e.g., for Hg, 10 nM (2 ppb) and 30 nM (6 ppb) according to US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) [53] and World Health Organization (WHO) [54], respectively). However, the persistent presence of these pollutants (particularly in their most toxic cationic forms) in soil, ground, and surface waters has a lasting impact on ecosystems and food chains, finally leading to serious effects on human health. In the case of mercury, clinical manifestations of acute and chronic toxicity of its most hazardous forms (i.e., Hg(II) and MeHg(I) cations) include genotoxicity, immunogenicity, pregnancy and reproductive system damage, cancer progression, cardiotoxicity, and neurological, pulmonary, and renal diseases [55]. Considering this worrying health situation, many efforts have been devoted to the development and practical deployment of different decontamination methods applicable on‐ or offsite (e.g., soil excavation followed by washing or stabilization processes, or thermal treatment to recover elemental Hg(0) in liquid form; phyto‐ and bioremediation techniques [56]). The availability of reliable and sensitive methods for detecting Hg(II) and MeHg(I) species greatly facilitates the evaluation of the effectiveness of these detoxification procedures. In such a context of environmental analysis, fluorescence detection techniques often offer numerous advantages over other spectroscopic/spectrometric methods that are equally sensitive but not easy to deploy directly onsite (e.g., surface‐enhanced Raman spectroscopy, inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy [ICP‐OES] or mass spectrometry [ICP‐MS]) [57]. These include rapid response, the possibility of real‐time in situ detection, and practical implementation with cheap, safe and easy‐to‐use miniaturized portable fluorometers. In addition, the use of a molecular system rationally designed to act as an effective and selective recognition and signaling element for the analyte to be detected will produce an intense and easily measurable fluorescence signal. In some cases, this “turn‐on” fluorogenic detection can be accompanied by the appearance (or at least, a drastic change) of a color (“naked eye” chromogenic response). Not surprisingly, recent literature is full of examples of fluorescent probes designed for environmental detection or in vivo analysis of Hg(II) cations [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 58]. Depending on the nature of the interaction between the probe and Hg(II) cation, a distinction is made between fluorescent chemosensors (involving weak and potentially reversible metal‐ligand interactions) and fluorescent chemodosimeters, for which a specific, non‐reversible chemical reaction readily occurs between the probe and Hg(II) cation [59]. The latter approach often produces better results in terms of selectivity and sensitivity, as the chemical reaction triggered by a soft Lewis acid is highly specific towards other analytes (e.g., other metal cations) present in matrices to be analyzed. Most of the chemical transformations implemented in these fluorescent dosimeters exploit the high affinity of Hg(II) cation (“soft sphere” according to HSAB theory) for sulfur. For instance, Hg(II) is capable of promoting the deprotection of thioacetals/thioketals or desulfurization of thiocarbonyl (C = S) groups in aq. media at physiological pH, thereby altering the electronic distribution of the probes and enhancing their fluorescence properties [60]. The main inherent difficulty in this reaction‐based strategy is to obtain a totally “quenched” and, if possible, colorless probe in solution prior to the reaction with Hg(II) cations, so as to maximize the signal‐to‐noise ratio and hence the amplitude of the chromo/fluorogenic response.

In order to apply the hydroxyl group protection‐deprotection strategy [5] to rigidified phenolo‐merocyanine KR306, with the aim of obtaining an efficient “turn‐on” chemodosimeter for the detection of Hg(II), we have selected the diphenylphosphinothioyl moiety as the masking group of its phenol [61, 62]. This choice was guided by some positive results from several published studies showing, in particular, the ability of Hg(II) cations to promote hydrolysis of fluorogenic dimethyl‐ or diphenylthiophosphinate aryl esters in pure neutral aq. buffers or mixed with an organic co‐solvent (Table 2) [63, 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70]. As summarized in Scheme 2, the esterification of KR306 with diphenylthiophosphinic chloride 4 (this latter one being freshly prepared through a reaction between chlorodiphenylphosphine and elemental sulfur S8 conducted at 130°C and under neat conditions, and easily monitored by 31P NMR) [71], was readily achieved in dry MeCN and in the presence of TEA. The resulting probe 5 was easily purified by semi‐preparative RP‐HPLC and recovered as a TFA salt with a very good yield (88%). Having confirmed its structure by detailed NMR (1H, 13C, 19F and 31P) and ESI‐HRMS spectroscopic analyses (see section S4), spectral features were examined in aq. buffers previously used for the photophysical characterization of KR306 (Table 1, Figures 6 and S7–S14). Whatever the medium used, a relatively strong absorption centered at 440–441 nm (hypsochromic shift of ca. 100 nm compared to KR306) was observed in the UV‐Vis spectrum, and a weak emission at 567–568 nm was revealed upon excitation at 480 nm.

Compared to the strong emissive ability of KR306, this fluorescence quenching could be attributed to a decrease of ICT character linked to the introduction of a medium electron‐withdrawing group (‐Ph2P(S)) onto the phenol moiety, thus preventing the formation of strong electron‐donating phenolate species in solution. However, accurate determination of relative fluorescence quantum yields under the conditions used for KR306 (i.e., same excitation wavelength and R6G as a standard), revealed incomplete quenching of 5 that might impact its sensing performance. In order to try to fix this, we came up with the idea of implementing a further quenching event based on homo‐FRET (self‐quenching) between two identical molecules of capped phenolo‐merocyanine located in close proximity to each other [72]. Furthermore, we assumed that the virtual release of two fluorophore units upon reaction of one molecule of probe with a single Hg(II) cation could lead to signal amplification for better detection sensitivity. The commercial availability of phenylthiophosphinic dichloride has enabled the facile one‐step synthesis of diaryl thiophosphonate ester 6 under conditions similar to those used for preparing 5 (Scheme 2), and its purification was achieved by semi‐preparative RP‐HPLC (TFA salt recovered with a satisfying yield of 68%). Its structure was unambiguously confirmed by ESI‐HRMS and detailed 1H, 13C, 19F and 31P NMR spectroscopic analyses (see Supporting Information). During the RP‐HPLC purification, a minor amount of mono‐aryl thiophosphonate ester 7 was also recovered (yield of 22%) and only characterized by ESI‐HRMS. Somewhat disappointing, the photophysical characterization of this phenolo‐merocyanine dimer revealed a spectral behavior quite similar to that of its parent monomeric analog 5 (especially residual fluorescence quantum yields of the same order of magnitude), and no significant change in the shape of its absorption/emission bands was noted. No homo‐FRET (or self‐quenching) process seems to occur within this dimeric photoactive scaffold. One possible explanation could be the mutual repulsion of sterically hindered gem‐dimethyl groups of indolenium moieties thus preventing the two molecules of KR306 from being close together for efficient energy transfer between them. Next, both chemodosimeters 5 and 6 were thus evaluated without distinction for their sensing performances towards Hg(II).

Hg(II) Sensing Performances of Fluorogenic (Di)aryl Thiophosphinate and Thiophosphonate Esters 5 and 6

All fluorogenic Hg(II) assays and blank experiments were achieved through time‐course measurements supplemented by fluorescence emission spectra recording before and after these kinetics. Typically, a 1.0 µM solution of 5 in PB (pH 7.5) or HEPES buffer (pH 7.2) was incubated with 2.5 equiv. of HgCl2 at 25°C, and the evolution of the fluorescence signal was monitored using the set Ex/Em parameters (λ ex/em = 540/560 nm) well suited for detecting the released phenolate form of KR306. As shown in Figure 7A, a steady and moderately rapid increase of fluorescence emission at 560 nm was observed and reached a high‐intensity plateau within 30 min for activation conducted in PB. A much slower increase of yellow‐green fluorescence was obtained when HEPES buffer was used as solvent. To interpret this marked difference in reactivity towards this aryl thiophosphinate ester, we hypothesized nucleophilic assistance of H2PO4 − and HPO42− anions (major species in PB at pH 7.5) that would greatly promote the release of KR306 (phenolate form) through the formation of mixed anhydride 8, acting as a driving force for the ester hydrolysis reaction. Such participation of buffer molecules did not appear to be favored with 2‐[4‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)piperazin‐1‐yl]ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES molecule) despite the presence of a nucleophilic primary alcohol within its structure. The reaction manifold is discussed in more detail in the section dedicated to deciphering the sensing mechanism 9.

After 1 h of reaction in PB, both shape and intensity of the emission curve overlapped well with the fluorescence spectrum of a 1.0 µM solution of the authentic sample of KR306, also prepared in PB (Figure 7B). This indicates that this fluorogenic protection‐deprotection strategy mediated by a stoichiometric amount of Hg(II) is a quantitative and fully water‐compatible process. Interestingly, this probe activation was also reflected in a marked color change from light yellow to pink that is characteristic of the phenolate form of rigidified merocyanine KR306 (Figure 7). Owing to the high brightness of this fluorophore, “naked eye” chromogenic response was possible even at a low concentration of 1.0 µM. Furthermore, in the absence of this Lewis acid analyte, the diphenylthiophosphinate linkage of 5 was highly resistant to hydrolysis, as confirmed by blank experiments that did not produce noticeable fluorogenic “turn‐on” response after 1 h incubation. After these promising initial results, we then focused on determining the sensing performances of 5, including selectivity towards other thiophilic metal cations, and accurate determination of the limit of detection (LOD). A series of additional fluorescence assays with the following metal cations: Ag(I), As(III), Cd(II), Cu(II), Cu(I) and Pb(II), was considered and carried out in the same way as mentioned above. Only Ag(I), Cd(II) and Cu(I) caused a very slight increase in fluorescence emission at 560 nm, but that was negligible compared to the amplitude of “turn‐on” fluorescence response produced by Hg(II) (Figure S60). These results confirmed the excellent selectivity of 5 for sensing Hg(II) cations. The other important challenge associated with the upcoming deployment of this sulfur‐based fluorescent probe in environmental analysis applications was the accurate evaluation of its LOD using a robust and reliable methodology applicable to a wide range of fluorometers. Ideally, a value close to or lower than permissible limits for drinking water, advocated by the US EPA and WHO (vide supra), is expected. A comprehensive description of our methodology employed for LOD determination is available in the Supporting Information (Section S3). From time‐course fluorescence curves established by incubating 5 with different concentrations of Hg(II), we managed to obtain a linear correlation by plotting emission intensity at 560 nm (value after 10 min of incubation with Hg(II) ions) against analyte concentration in the range of 100 nM to 750 nM (Figures S17–S18). Depending on the chosen definition of Sd in the formula typically used for detection limit calculation (i.e., residual standard deviation of the calibration function or the intercept of the calibration function), LOD was determined to be 75 nM (15 ppb) and 52 nM (10.4 ppb), respectively. These values are of the same order as those reported for the sole published diphenylthiophosphinate‐based chemodosimeter characterized in neat aq. buffer and derived from N‐butyl‐4‐hydroxy‐1,8‐naphthalimide (Table 2, entry 2). We next studied the ability of diaryl thiophosphonate ester 6 to also act as an effective fluorescent chemodosimeter for the detection of Hg(II) in pure aq. buffers. Much more disappointing results were obtained. Time‐course fluorescence assays compiled in Figure 8A clearly reveal some limitations of this fluorogenic dimeric system: higher fluorescence background, higher propensity to undergo hydrolysis in buffer alone, slower kinetics of activation compared to 5, and poor selectivity towards Cu(I)/Cu(II) cations. Curiously, slightly better results in terms of activation kinetics were obtained when HEPES buffer was used as solvent. As claimed during the design phase of this fluorogenic diaryl thiophosphonate ester, a 2 h incubation with HgCl2 (2.5 equiv.) in HEPES buffer led to the release of the two units of KR306, as evidenced by the level of fluorescence emission intensity at 560 nm that has been reached, approximately twice the intensity obtained with a 1.0 µM solution of KR306 in HEPES buffer (Figure 8B). We did not explore in depth the sensing performances of 6 because its moderate hydrolytic stability is a major obstacle to determining an accurate and relevant LOD value.

Verification of the Sensing Mechanisms of 5 and 6

To both confirm the proposed sensing mechanism and highlight potential side‐reactions (Figure 9), all solutions resulting from fluorescence assays were next subjected to RP‐HPLC analyses involving either fluorescence detection (see Figures S19–S31 and S39–S50 for the corresponding elution profiles related to validation of probes 5 and 6, respectively) or ESI‐MS detection (see Figures S32–S38 and S51–S57 for the corresponding elution profiles and mass spectra related to validation of probes 5 and 6, respectively). Since these RP‐HPLC analyses were performed using acidic eluents (pH 2.1), the most suited fluorescence detection channel was λ ex/em = 440/560 nm (see Table 1 for the spectral features of KR306 and Hg(II)‐responsive probes 5 and 6). In the case of 5, successful activation of this probe by Hg(II) cation was clearly confirmed by the visualization of released phenolo‐merocyanine KR306 as a single and intense peak at t R = 3.5 nm on RP‐HPLC elution profiles of the reaction mixture (fluorescence channels: λ ex/em = 440/560 nm and 540/560 nm). The overwhelming presence of the starting probe in samples corresponding to blank experiments (i.e., 1 h incubation in buffer alone) and assays conducted with other metal cations was reflected by the presence of a major peak at t R = 4.8 min. These observations confirmed the main conclusions drawn from fluorescence assays and have been finally validated by RP‐HPLC‐MS analyses. Indeed, after 1 h of incubation with Hg(II), the sole peak observed on the Vis channel (λ = 440 nm) at t R = 3.1 min was assigned to merocyanine KR306 (m/z = 318.2 [M]+, calcd for C21H20NO2 + 318.1). A more in‐depth analysis of total ion chromatogram (TIC channel) also revealed the presence of a minor compound at t R = 2.9 min (m/z = 334.2 [M]+) whose structure might be assigned to hydroxylated analog of KR306 (a chemical structure related to a catechol or an hemiacetal analog formed through a photochemical process, was proposed but without any certainty, see Supporting Information). Since it was a somewhat anecdotal observation, we hypothesized that KR306 and related probes are very slightly prone to visible‐light photooxidation. In order to confirm the assumed sensing mechanism leading to the release of diphenylphosphinic acid (Ph2P(O)OH, calcd mass for C12H11O2P 218.0), several further RP‐HPLC‐MS analyses (mainly in the selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode) of the crude reaction mixtures were also achieved. We used an authentic sample of diphenylphosphinic acid (commercial source) as a reference. Thus, by selecting the proper SIM+ channel (m/z = 219.0 ± 0.5), the presence of diphenylphosphinic acid (t R = 2.8 min) was confirmed only in the mixtures corresponding to incubation of 5 with HgCl2. However, we noted a lower intensity of SIM peak (ca. 5.1 E+4) compared to that obtained with commercial reference (ca. 4.2 E+5), for the same injected amount (20 pmol). Our hypothesis was the formation of mixed anhydride derivative 8 by reaction of aryl diphenylphosphinate ester with phosphate ions of PB. However, we failed to detect this latter compound through ESI‐MS analyses (both full‐scan and SIM modes). In view of its assumed high polarity, an early eluted peak whose ionization in the ESI source was inhibited by non‐volatile phosphate salts of the buffer could be a plausible explanation. The same analytical methodology applied to samples from fluorescence assays conducted with thiophosphonate ester 6 also provided interesting information about the behavior of this probe in aq. buffers. Indeed, RP‐HPLC elution profiles (either fluorescence or ESI‐MS detection) of blank experiments (i.e., HEPES buffer, 2 h incubation in the absence of Hg(II) analyte) revealed the presence of several peaks. In the case of RP‐HPLC‐fluorescence analysis, t R = 3.5 min and 4.3 min, respectively assigned to a mixture of co‐eluted merocyanine KR306 and monoaryl thiophosphonate ester 7, and starting probe 6. In the case of RP‐HPLC‐MS analysis: t R = 3.2 min assigned to merocyanine KR306 (m/z = 318.2 [M]+, calcd for C21H20NO2 + 318.1), t R = 3.5 min assigned to 7 (m/z = 474.2 [M]+, calcd for C27H25NO3PS+ 474.1), t R = 4.1 min assigned to 6 (m/z = 387.3 [M]2+, calcd for C48H43N2O4PS2+ 774.3), and a very minor peak at t R = 3.9 min assigned to mono‐hydroxylated analog of 6 (m/z = 395.3 [M]2+, calcd for C48H43N2O5PS2+ 790.3). These observations confirmed the poor aq. stability of diaryl thiophosphonate ester 6 mentioned above, even if its resistance to hydrolysis was found to be better in PB than in HEPES buffer (hydrolysis rate of 25% vs. 55% after 2 h, see Figures S42 and S44). The counterpart of this higher stability is a dramatically slower reaction with Hg(II) analyte obtained in PB. This overall behavior is problematic when considering practical sensing applications for 6. Finally, to gain insights about the activation mechanism of 6 (through its reaction with Hg(II) or water), we tried to detect the released phenylphosphonic acid (PhP(O)(OH)2, calcd mass for C6H6O3P− 157.0) by performing further RP‐HPLC‐MS (mainly in the SIM mode (ESI‐)) and using a commercial sample as reference. Unfortunately, for the same reasons as those mentioned for mixed anhydride 8, we failed to detect unambiguously this low molecular weight highly polar compound in reaction mixtures from fluorescence assays, same goes for mixed anhydride 9. In view of the promising sensing performances of aryl thiophosphinate ester 5, we completed this study with some experiments aimed at demonstrating both robustness and practical applicability of this Hg(II)‐responsive chemodosimeter (vide infra).

Application of Hg(II)‐Responsive Chemodosimeter 5 to a Real Water Sample and Bacterial Biosorbents (Kefir Grains)

First, we conducted a fluorescence assay of 5 in a real water sample (pH 6.5). The latter was collected in a pond located at “Parc des Alliaires” (city of Vieux‐Charmont, Doubs department, France), a former industrial wasteland recently rehabilitated as a “living lab” to carry out experiments in phytomanagement [73, 74]. Since the lack of a mercury contamination for this pond water is well established, the collected sample was solely doped with HgCl2 (2.5 µM) without further treatments. As shown in Figure 10A, in the absence of Hg(II) analyte, 5 was stable under the measurement conditions and did not show any fluorogenic “turn‐on” response after 1 h of incubation. Upon doping of this real water sample, signaling of Hg(II) ions by 5 was effective and illustrated by a gradual increase of fluorescence emission at 560 nm. Interestingly, a plateau of comparable intensity to that obtained with fluorescence assays conducted in PB (Figure 7), indicating a complete signaling, was reached but only after 2 h. This slowing of activation kinetics could be explained by the lower pH of real water sample and its low phosphate ion content, both affecting the rate constant for Hg(II)‐mediated hydrolysis of thiophosphinate ester linkage. Despite much less stability in aq. buffers, we were pleasantly surprised to find that fluorogenic diaryl phosphonate ester 6 was also effective for detecting Hg(II) in this real water sample (Figure 10B). Indeed, the lower pH value than that of PB or HEPES buffer (pH 6.5 vs. pH 7.4–7.5), was a positive feature for reducing non‐specific hydrolysis while not negatively impacting Hg(II)‐mediated fluorogenic activation, even if this latter one was far from complete. Value of emission intensity at 560 nm reached after 2 h of incubation in Hg(II)‐doped water, was not twice that obtained with 5. Finally, for both chemodosimeters, Hg(II)‐mediated release of phenolo‐merocyanine KR306 in real water sample, was also confirmed through a remarkable “naked eye” chromogenic response (Figures S58–S59).

In the context of heavy metal biosorption by microorganisms [75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80], several studies have highlighted the ability of bacteria (e.g., lactic acid bacteria, LAB) and yeast found in kefir grains, to achieve effective sorption of heavy metals mainly through physical/chemical interactions with bacteria cell walls [41]. In the more specific case of mercury, tolerance of Penicillium sp. isolated from kefir grains, towards this heavy metal was demonstrated by de Oliveira et al. [40]. Indeed, when Hg(NO3)2 was added to the culture medium at a concentration of 175 mg/L, only 17% growth inhibition of this microorganism occurred. This growth was completely stopped only when the concentration of Hg(II) reached 200 mg/L. These valuable findings led us to explore the potential of kefir grains as a novel biological platform for validating the analytical capacity of fluorescent chemodosimeters such as 5. Tracking the probe's fate within these transparent microorganisms was considered by directly observing the color change of grains with the naked eye, and by visualizing their fluorescence under UV‐A illumination. By analogy with zebrafish (Danio rerio) [81, 82], transparency should serve as a crucial advantage in the investigation of imaging capability of such novel reaction‐based fluorescent probes. From a practical point of view, Petri dishes containing 5 to 10 kefir grains, were flooded with PB solution of probe 5 (10 µM, Petri dishes #1 and #4) or merocyanine KR306 (10 µM, Petri dish #3) and incubated at 23°C for 1 h. After removal of PB solutions, Petri dishes were again flooded with fresh PB solution alone (Petri dishes #1 and #4) or containing HgCl2 (100 µM, Petri dish #3) and photographs were regularly taken over a period of 40 min (Figure 11). The color change from light yellow to pink of the signaling kefir grains (Petri dish#3, kefir grains sequentially treated with probe 5 + HgCl2) was evident and increasingly noticeable over time. This seemed to confirm both penetration and selective activation of 5 by Hg(II) ions. Conversely, under illumination with a UV lamp (365 nm) typically used for TLC analysis, no clear distinction between the two Petri dishes containing treated and untreated kefir grains respectively, could not be made. Indeed, intense yellow fluorescence was observed in all Petri dishes, which was consistent with the spectral features of released phenolo‐merocyanine KR306 and starting probe 5 determined in PB (see entries 1 and 6 or Table 1). Indeed, the fluorescence quantum yield of 5 was divided by three compared to that of KR306, but its molar extinction coefficient at 365 nm is 2.5 fold higher (11,850 M−1 cm−1 vs. 4450 M−1 cm−1) thus leading to brightness values of roughly the same order of magnitude. This easy‐to‐implement experiment demonstrated the good permeability of both positively‐charged 5 and zwitterionic form of KR306 (predominant at pH 7.5 due to phenol deprotonation) that were easily introduced into kefir grains by simply adding the corresponding dye/probe to the incubation medium. The incomplete quenching of 5 has prevented the achievement of spectacular results in terms of selective fluorogenic “turn‐on” response, but this unprecedented probe validation approach was the first exploratory step towards a more widespread use of kefir grains as a valuable model alive organism for toxicology and environmental pollution research.

Conclusion

In summary, we have studied a structural rigidification strategy based on recent achievements in cyanine chemistry to convert indolenium analog of Brooker's merocyanine into high‐performance yellow‐emissive phenolic fluorophore KR306. The facile implementation of a Brønsted acid catalyzed domino double cyclization reaction has enabled to devise an efficient and rapid synthetic route to this rigidified phenolo‐merocyanine (overall yield 32% over three steps). Its outstanding spectral properties in neutral aq. buffers, in particular owing to its relatively acidic hydroxyl group (ground state pKa = 6.9) and conformational restriction of the native para‐hydroxystyryl side‐chain (brightness = 71,500 M−1 cm−1 at pH 7.5 for a fluorescence emission centered at ca. 560 nm), have been highlighted thanks to a comprehensive photophysical study. The fluorogenic behavior of KR306 was demonstrated through the reversible masking of its hydroxyl group, in the context of reaction‐based fluorescence “turn‐on” detection of heavy metal cation Hg(II). With the aim of monitoring Hg(II) pollution in environmental matrices, we have studied in detail both photophysics and reactivity in aq. media of chromo‐fluorogenic thiophosphinate and thiophosphonate esters 5 and 6 with this soft Lewis acid. Fluorescence‐based assays completed with RP‐HPLC‐fluorescence and RP‐HPLC‐MS analyses have highlighted some positive features of the reaction‐based sensing strategy involving the use of thiophosphinate probe 5, defined as follows: selective signaling of Hg(II) ions through a univocal and quantitative hydrolysis reaction promoted by sulfur‐Hg(II) binding event; this latter process being promoted by phosphate ions. Despite its incomplete fluorescence quenching, 5 exhibited good sensing performances (great hydrolytic stability, perfect selectivity towards other thiophilic metal cations, and LOD within the range 52–75 nM (10–15 ppb)) which enabled its deployment in more practical experiments. Thus, we disclosed chromo‐fluorogenic signaling behavior of 5 and 6 in a real water sample dopped with Hg(II) ions, and more original but still in an exploratory manner, in living micro‐organisms containing a variety of bacterial and fungal species (kefir grains) resistant to high concentrations of heavy metal cations such as Hg(II), for 5. This latter medium was thus identified as a novel potential biological platform for validating small molecule fluorescent probes. The present study contributes to expand the range of high‐performance phenolic fluorophores suitable for the facile design of analyte‐responsive fluorescent probes based on the protection‐deprotection strategy [5].

Instruments and Methods

Freeze‐drying operations were performed with a Christ Alpha 2–4 LD plus apparatus. Typically, a 10−5 M solution of sample (sample preparation: stock solution in DMSO was diluted by a factor of 21 in H2O‐MeCN (1:1, v/v)) was then analyzed via the RP‐HPLC‐HRMS.

Syntheses

Rigidified Phenolo‐Merocyanine KR306

To a solution of 1‐[2‐(1,3‐dioxolan‐2‐yl)ethyl]‐2,3,3‐trimethyl‐3H‐indolium bromide 2 (4.97 g, 12.8 mmol, 1 equiv.) is dry EtOH (238 mL) was added 2,4‐dihydroxybenzaldehyde (1.77 g, 12.8 mmol, 1 equiv.). The resulting reaction mixture was stirred at RT for 17 h.
