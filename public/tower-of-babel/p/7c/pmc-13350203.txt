Routine intraoperative infection testing in presumed aseptic hip and knee revision: a matched cohort retrospective study
Authors: Mattia Loppini, Caterina Rocchi, Marco Di Maio, Katia Chiappetta, Alberto Bulgarelli, Guido Grappiolo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction Periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) complicate 1–2% of primary and up to 4% of revision arthroplasties, and their diagnosis remains challenging due to the lack of a diagnostic gold standard. Differentiating PJI from aseptic failure is particularly difficult in low-grade or biofilm-related infections. Routine intraoperative microbiological screening is not recommended in presumed aseptic revisions. This study aimed to evaluate the association between routine intraoperative microbiological screening and implant survival in presumed aseptic revision arthroplasty. A secondary objective was to identify preoperative predictors of unexpected PJIs. MethodsRetrospectively collected data of patients undergoing presumed aseptic THA and TKA revisions between 2013 and 2019 were included. Two matched cohorts were compared: a screened group (2016–2019) undergoing routine intraoperative microbiological sampling, and an unscreened one (2013–2015) without systematic screening. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing assessed overall and infection-free survival. Univariate conditional logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses evaluated potential associations between preoperative serum markers and unexpected PJI. Results A total of 559 patients were included, of whom 295 underwent screening and 264 did not. Unexpected infections occurred in 20.3% (n = 60) of screened patients. No significant differences in implant survival were observed between groups. Although overall and infection-free survival were numerically higher in the screened cohort, these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.168 and p = 0.118, respectively). Stratified analyses by joint and failure mechanism showed comparable findings. C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate were associated with unexpected PJI (p = 0.004 and p = 0.046), but ROC analysis suggested limited discrimination of individual markers, whereas a combined serum model improved diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.833).ConclusionsRoutine intraoperative microbiological screening in presumed aseptic revision arthroplasty was not associated with a significant improvement in implant survival. Future studies should investigate whether selective screening improves outcomes. Level III retrospective cohort study.

Introduction

In a society with an increasingly older population, the global demand for primary and revision arthroplasty continues to rise [1]. Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) affects 1–2% of procedures but its true incidence is likely underestimated [2].

If on one hand, diagnosis is difficult due to the absence of a universally accepted definition, the fact that cultures are often negative and clinical presentation subtle further complicates the matter [3–6].

To date, diagnosis of PJI relies on a combination of clinical, radiological, and laboratory findings. While positive joint aspirate cultures remain the gold standard, growing attention has shifted toward metagenomic next-generation sequencing for their rapidity and accuracy [7–9]. At the same time, several biomarkers are under investigation, having shown elevated concentrations in the presence of PJI [10–12]. Despite recent advances, differential diagnosis of PJI remains challenging as no reliable immunological profile is able to distinguish between low-grade infection and aseptic failure [13, 14].

Routine intraoperative microbiological screening is not recommended in presumed aseptic revision arthroplasties [15, 16]. However, among presumed aseptic revision patients, up to 9.2% present with at least one intraoperative unexpected positive culture (UPC) [17]. The prognosis for this category of patients remains controversial. Indeed, some studies highlight how the discovery of a single UPC does not necessarily cause subsequent development of a PJI caused by the same microorganism. This is particularly true for single positive cultures involving skin commensals, which are often resulting from contamination rather than a true infection [18]. Conversely, other authors have documented increased odds of all-causes revision in patients with UPCs [19, 20]. The number of positive samples, the virulence of the isolated organism, and patient-related factors such as immunocompromised status likely influence prognosis, yet no universally accepted thresholds or standardized management strategy currently exists. As a result, post-operative management is heterogeneous, ranging from observation to prolonged antibiotic regimens [21–23].

The underexplored clinical relevance of unexpected PJIs in presumed aseptic revisions raises questions concerning routine screening utility, highlighting the dual risk of underdiagnosis and overtreatment.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the association between routine intraoperative microbiological screening and implant survival in presumed aseptic revision arthroplasty in a real-world clinical setting, by comparing the outcomes of hip and knee revision arthroplasty between two matched patient populations. The secondary objective was to identify potential predictors of unexpected PJI.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective matched cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center between 2013 and 2019, employing medical file records of patients included in a registry of orthopedic surgical procedures. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of our institution (Approval number 618/17) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Persons unable to give informed consent were excluded. Two distinct patient cohorts were included. First, a screened cohort, composed of patients who underwent presumed aseptic partial or total hip arthroplasty (THA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA) revisions between April 2016 and November 2019, during which an intraoperative microbiological screening protocol was routinely implemented; second, an unscreened cohort, which included patients who underwent similar procedures between January 2013 and December 2015, for whom no systematic intraoperative screening was performed.

Patients within the unscreened cohort were matched at the cohort level with those in the screened group for sex, age at intervention, diagnosis, and type of surgical procedure. Aseptic revisions were defined as revisions classified as “infection unlikely” according to the European Bones and Joints Infection Society (EBJIS) 2021 criteria [24]. Exclusion criteria included previous history of infection in the index joint, pre-operative diagnosis of PJI, intraoperative spacer positioning, revision surgery for spacer removal and reimplantation, and antibiotics use within two weeks prior to surgery. The absence of a systematic intraoperative screening for PJI within the unscreened cohort does not mean that cultures were not taken in cases of strong clinical suspicion: cultures were sent whenever concern of infection arose during surgeries. However, cases requiring conversion to staged procedures with spacer implantation were excluded from the analysis.

Surgical technique and microbiological samples analysis

All the included procedures were performed by the same team of experienced orthopedic surgeons, employing a posterolateral approach with patients in lateral decubitus for the hip, and through a medial parapatellar approach for the knee.

Pre-operative assessment included physical examination, laboratory tests including CRP and ESR, and plain radiographs including anterior-posterior (AP) view of the pelvis and axial view of the hip for THA, AP and lateral view of the knee, axial views of patella, and full-length weight-bearing view of bilateral lower extremities for TKA. Bone scintigraphy, CT scan, or MRI was performed according to surgeon preference. Preoperative joint aspiration was not routinely performed, as all cases were classified as ‘infection unlikely’ based on clinical assessment, normal or minimally elevated inflammatory markers, and the absence of specific signs suggestive of PJI. In this context, aspiration was not considered mandatory.

Intraoperative testing was conducted for all the patients within the screened cohort, with synovial fluid collected before capsulotomy to prevent any contamination. During surgery, from five up to seven intra-operative periprosthetic tissue samples were obtained for microbiological analysis. Finally, the removed prosthesis was sent for sonication.

After surgery, patients in both cohorts received a standard perioperative prophylactic or empiric antibiotic treatment with vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam, vancomycin and ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin, or cefazolin until microbiological results were available. The antibiotic regimen was decided based on patients’ risk factors or intolerance to penicillin. Antibiotic therapy was discontinued after seven days in both screened and unscreened patients. In cases of UPC, treatment was prolonged and tailored according to pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. While preliminary results were generally available within the first seven days, full culture incubation, particularly for anaerobes and fungi, could extend to two weeks. In cases of late culture positivity, targeted therapy was initiated when the result became available. For culture-negative unexpected PJIs, a standardized empiric antibiotic regimen was continued for a total duration of three months.

Diagnosis of unexpected PJI was made in the absence of visible purulent material and assessing microbiological and laboratory criteria, including the following: leukocyte count > 2000/µl or > 70% granulocytes (PMN) in synovial fluid, > 50 CFU/ml in sonication fluid, positive synovial culture, two or more positive tissue cultures, or only one positive tissue culture for high virulent pathogens (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus), as per the definition criteria of the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) [25], the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) [26], and the 2021 criteria of the EBJIS [24]. Synovial leukocyte and PMN thresholds were based on values commonly used in clinical practice during the study period, consistent with MSIS-era ranges, and therefore do not strictly correspond to IDSA or later EBJIS criteria. The definition of failure of treatment was performed with Delphi-based consensus, and success criteria at one year follow-up were the following: (I) the eradication of infection, characterized by a healed wound without a fistula, drainage, or pain and without recurrence caused by the same organism, (II) no subsequent surgery for persistent or peri-operative infection, (III) no mortality related to the infection, and (IV) no requirement for long term (> 6 months) antibiotic suppression treatment.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed employing Python 3.9 (Python Software Foundation), resorting to NumPy and Pandas extensions for descriptive analysis, and lifelines package for survival analysis. Graphs were generated with GraphPad PRISM 10. Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05 (two-tailed). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test p > 0.05), or as median with interquartile range (IQR) when otherwise. Categorical variables were summarized as absolute values with percentages. Group comparisons were conducted by employing the Chi-square (χ²) test, or the Fisher’s exact test when sparse data were encountered. Baseline balance between cohorts was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values < 0.1 indicating adequate balance.

The average follow-up length was 135 months (95% CI: 131–140) for the unscreened and 98 months (95%CI: 95–100) for the screened group. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for the entire cohort, and stratified by joint (hip versus knee), outcome type, and patient group (screened vs. unscreened). Median and mean survival times with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were obtained via non-parametric bootstrap resampling (200 iterations). Differences between survival functions were evaluated with a two-sided log-rank test.

To identify potential preoperative predictors of cultures positivity, univariate conditional logistic regression models were fitted for each preoperative variable, including age at surgery, hemoglobin concentration, C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals and two-tailed p-values are reported.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the predictive performance of individual preoperative serum markers and of the combined multivariable serum model. Areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated to quantify overall diagnostic accuracy. Optimal sensitivity and specificity were determined using the Youden index. For the multivariable analysis, a logistic regression model incorporating CRP, ESR, peripheral leukocyte count, hemoglobin, and platelet count was constructed, and predicted probabilities were used to generate ROC curves.

Internal validation of the multivariable serum model was performed using 2,000 bootstrap resamples to assess performance stability. Bootstrap-corrected AUC and percentile-based 95% CI were calculated.

Results

A total of 559 revisions met the inclusion criteria, 295 within the screened and 264 within the unscreened group. Propensity score matching achieved a satisfactory balance on age (67.3 ± 10.5 vs. 67.5 ± 11.5 years; SMD = 0.02) and sex (39.4% vs. 37.6% males; SMD = 0.04). The only notable imbalance concerned joint type: knees accounted for 11.7% of unscreened cases, but for only 2% of screened cases (SMD = 0.35). All the potential biological predictors of unexpected PJI (CRP, ESR, hemoglobin, leukocytes, electrolytes, creatinine, etc.) were well matched, with each falling within the SMD < 0.1 threshold. As such, both cohorts can be considered comparable with respect to the matched variables, and any observed difference is unlikely to have been driven by baseline demographics or laboratory bias. Patients’ demographics are reported in Table 1.

Implant event-free survival was compared between the screened (n = 295) and unscreened (n = 264) cohorts. Kaplan-Meier analysis suggested early numerical separation between cohorts; however, differences did not reach statistical significance (log-rank χ² = 1.90, p = 0.168). At one month, survival was 99.7% in the screened group and 97.3% in the unscreened group, and remained above 95% in both cohorts throughout follow-up. Widening confidence intervals over time reflected decreasing numbers at risk. Similarly, infection-free survival did not differ significantly between groups (log-rank χ² = 2.44, p = 0.118). Infection-related failures occurred in 10 of 295 screened patients and 17 of 264 unscreened patients. At 12 months, survival exceeded 97% in both cohorts and remained above 96% at final follow-up. Survival analyses stratified by group and infection are presented in Fig. 1. Discrepancies in outcomes between screened and unscreened cohorts are shown in Table 2.

Among the 295 screened revisions, 60 (20.3%) presented unexpected PJI despite the absence of clinical suspicion. Of 60 unexpected PJIs, 28 (46.6%) met ≥ 2 concordant cultures criteria. Criteria for the diagnosis of unexpected PJI are displayed in Table 3.

Univariate conditional logistic regression was performed on pre-operative variables, identifying two statistically relevant preoperative biomarkers: CRP (OR: 1.04, p = 0.004) and ESR (OR: 1.02, p = 0.046). Furthermore, lower hemoglobin showed a borderline association with cultures positivity (OR: 0.85, p = 0.049), while BMI and diabetes were non-significant. The role of preoperative biomarkers as predictors of unexpected PJI is presented in Table 4.

ROC analysis suggested limited discrimination for individual serum PJI markers, with CRP showing moderate accuracy and ESR, leukocyte count, hemoglobin, and platelet count performing variably. In contrast, the multivariable serum model integrating all five preoperative parameters yielded substantially improved diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.833). At the Youden-optimized probability threshold, sensitivity reached 89.5% with a specificity of 75.4%, indicating a favorable balance between rule-out and rule-in capability. Bootstrap validation supported stable discrimination, with a corrected AUC of 0.786 (95% CI 0.751–0.832). ROC analysis is presented in Fig. 2.

Discussion

The main finding of the present study is that routine intraoperative microbiological screening in presumed aseptic revision arthroplasty was not associated with improvement in implant survival or reduce infection-related failures (p = 0.118). In contrast, preoperative inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP and ESR emerged as significant predictors for detection of unexpected PJIs, with moderate predictive power (p = 0.004 and p = 0.046, respectively).

Propensity score matching achieved satisfactory results within the two cohorts, except concerning joint type (knees: 11.7% in the unscreened vs. 2% in the screened group; SMD = 0.35). The observed disparity likely reflects temporal heterogeneity in revision types rather than a systemic bias.

Based on the intraoperative samples analyzed, 20.3% (n = 60) of the presumed aseptic patients in the screened group were diagnosed with unexpected infections, exceeding most previously reported rates in the literature (ranging between 9.2–14%) [17, 27, 28]. This result should be interpreted cautiously, as culture-based screening has a limited discriminative power in distinguishing between infection by virulent pathogens and contaminants. Furthermore, the lack of preoperative routine aspiration due to absence of clinical suspicion of PJI may have contributed to underdiagnosis of low-grade infections and could partially explain the relatively high rate of unexpected PJI observed.

Despite the non-negligible incidence of unexpected PJIs, no statistical significance in implant or infection-free survival was observed between the screened and unscreened cohorts (p = 0.168 and p = 0.118, respectively). These trends persisted when stratifying by joint type or failure mechanism.

The comparable inter-group survival outcomes indicate no substantial advantage in routine intraoperative screening, which may instead expose patients to unnecessary overtreatment and contribute to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) development. Although some authors reported that a single UPC might lead to higher risk of re-revision for PJI (RR: 2.63; 95% CI: 1.16–5.96), other studies have proven that THA and TKA revisions with UPCs are not significantly associated with higher failure rates at 5 years [19, 29]. Furthermore, diagnostic discrepancies due to variations in microbiological processing between laboratories may lead to inconsistent UPC identification [30]. These uncertainties in microbiological interpretation highlight the importance of contextualizing laboratory findings within broader clinical and epidemiological trends. Indeed, the clinical interpretation of UPCs should be framed within the context of a global rise in AMR, along with interregional variability in antimicrobial stewardship protocols and pathogen-related susceptibility patterns. Given the evolving resistance patterns of coagulase-negative staphylococci and other low-virulence organisms, prognostic relevance of UPCs may evolve over time, underscoring the need for dynamic screening guidelines for presumed aseptic cases, adapting to the evolving microbiological landscapes and resistance profiles [31, 32].

The only significant predictors of unexpected PJIs were CRP and ESR, showing moderate predictive power (p = 0.004 and p = 0.046, respectively). Yu et al. found that combination markers of CRP ∧ ESR could efficiently predict PJI (sensitivity: 78.3%, specificity: 88.8%, positive predictive value: 70.0%, negative predictive value: 92.5%) [33]. Furthermore, Parvizi et al. reported that patients with UPCs had a higher median CRP level than culture-negative cases (2.1 vs. 1.2 mg/dL, p = 0.03), while Hughes et al. found that a CRP threshold of 7.1 mg/L predicted infection and UPCs (80% sensitivity, 88% specificity, p < 0.001) [34, 35]. However, CRP does not constitute an appropriate rule-out test for PJI, and lower median levels have been described in infection by Propionibacterium spp. (5.4 mg/L), CN Staphylococci (12.2 mg/L), and Enterococcus faecalis compared with Staphylococcus aureus (194 mg/L) and streptococcal infections (89.3 mg/L, p < 0.001) [36]. Furthermore, our ROC analysis underscores that while individual hematologic inflammatory markers provide only partial information, their combined evaluation enhances diagnostic precision in the preoperative assessment of suspected PJI, with high sensitivity and specificity (89.5% and 75.4%, AUC = 0.833).

Our findings align with the body of literature questioning the benefit of routine intraoperative screening in presumed aseptic revision arthroplasty. Although early reports emphasized the role of unexpected PJI, their detection does not necessarily translate into improved prevention or survival. Moreover, their identification may expose patients to overtreatment, leading to increased hospital length of stay, higher healthcare costs, and reduced quality of life due to medication-related side effects, in absence of clear benefits from extended antibiotic prophylaxis [37–39].

A major strength of this study lies in its matched cohort design, the large sample size and the long-term follow-up period, allowing for a strong control of all variables, particularly in demographic and surgical factors.

Nonetheless, several limitations are present. First, the study could not distinguish with certainty the effect of intraoperative screening from the effect of subsequent treatment. The initial empiric antibiotic strategy may have attenuated the clinical effect associated with positive cultures. As a result, the study compares two management strategies (screening plus treatment vs. no systematic screening), rather than the isolated diagnostic value of screening itself. Therefore, the absence of a difference in outcomes cannot be interpreted as evidence that screening lacks clinical utility. Additionally, the use of broad-spectrum postoperative antibiotic regimens may have attenuated early infection expression and reduced observable differences between groups, potentially masking any benefit of intraoperative screening. Although matching was performed, a residual imbalance in joint distribution persisted. While this may introduce confounding, stratified analyses did not demonstrate differential effects according to joint type. The temporally separated cohort design (2013–2015 vs. 2016–2019) also represents a limitation, as changes in pathogen epidemiology and AMR patterns over time may have influenced detection rates and outcomes, despite consistent surgical and laboratory protocols. A further limitation is the relatively low number of infection-related events, which may result in limited statistical power and an increased risk of Type II error. Second, while the microbiological definitions used in the screened group adhered to MSIS, EBJIS, and IDSA guidelines, intraoperative culture interpretation remains subject to diagnostic uncertainty, with possible contamination or misclassification. The EBJIS criteria were applied retrospectively, and not all required variables could be collected, potentially leading to incomplete classification. Third, the single-center design limits external validity, as local epidemiological characteristics and resistance patterns may differ from those of other institutions. Lastly, the retrospective observational nature of the study limits causal inference and may introduce information bias. Taken together, these limitations restrict causal inference, and the findings should be interpreted with caution and viewed as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

In this retrospective matched cohort study, routine intraoperative microbiological screening in presumed aseptic revision arthroplasty was not associated with a statistically significant improvement in implant survival. However, given the influence of postoperative antibiotic strategies and the inability to isolate the independent effect of screening, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further multicenter studies are required to clarify the clinical utility of routine intraoperative screening under different postoperative management protocols.
