Modeling transient mixed flows in sewer systems with data fusion via physics-informed machine learning
Authors: Shixun Li, Wenchong Tian, Hexiang Yan, Wei Zeng, Tao Tao, Kunlun Xin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A comprehensive PINN-based model is proposed for simulating and inverting transient mixed flow in UDS.•The α factor is conducted to seamlessly link water hammer equation and open-channel equation.•TMF-PINN(Lite) is more effective to capture the trends of pressure and velocity at unmonitored locations.•TMF-PINN is more sensitive for complex dynamic processes featuring high-frequency.

Introduction

Transient mixed flows are common but complex phenomena in urban drainage system, and can cause hydraulic failures such as the collapse of infrastructure, the dislodgement of manhole covers, geysers, and basement inundation (Bousso et al., 2013). Within sewer networks, when discharge into the system surpasses pipe capacity, pressurized flow ensues, leading to transient mixed flows transitioning from free surface to pressurized flow conditions. A well-designed urban stormwater model, incorporating precise modeling of mixed flows, can swiftly facilitate the evacuation of stormwater and mitigate street flooding (Teng et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018; Song et al., 2024).

In the last decades, several methods have been proposed to handle the transitioning free-surface and pressurized flow condition, which can be categorized based on the solution strategy, i.e. the shock-fitting method (Wiggert, 1972; Fuamba, 2002) or the shock-capturing method (Cunge and Wegner, 1964; Vasconcelos et al., 2006).

The extension of free-surface Saint-Venant equations to encompass pressurized flow, thus capturing the interface, has rendered shock-capturing methods more appealing compared to shock-fitting approach (Z. Chen et al., 2021). Among these methods, the Preissmann slot method (PSM) stands out for its simplicity in model implementation, notably featured in the EPA Storm Water Management Model (SWMM) (USEPA, 2022). Recent advancements include the modified PSM proposed by León et al. (2009), introducing a gradual transition between the pipe and the slot to enhance accuracy and robustness, and the Negative PSM conceptualized by Kerger et al. (2011a), incorporating a negative slot to simulate subatmospheric pressurized flow. Furthermore, Maranzoni et al. (2015) extended the 1D PSM to 2D to accommodate pressurized flow beneath bridges, while Sharior et al. (2023) introduced the dynamic PSM, a nondimensional adaptation of the classical PSM employing a defined Preissmann number to mitigate instabilities and shocks. Additionally, Vasconcelos et al. (2006) devised a two-component pressure approach assuming elastic pipe walls, resembling to PSM (Sanders and Bradford, 2011) and Negative PSM (Kerger et al., 2011). In this way, various unsteady flows can be simulated effectively, including free-surface flow, mixed flow, pressurized and subatmospheric pressurized flow.

However, traditional modeling methods for transient mixed flows encounter certain challenges as detailed below:1)Data Assimilation: The Internet of Things (IoT) sensing techniques in urban water systems enable real-time data collection, which accumulates over time but is difficult to couple with numerical simulation (Duan et al., 2024). The general approach is to use the data for calibration rather than simulation (Zhou, et al., 2022a; 2022b; 2023).2)Equation Discovery: Present mixed flow models often rely on assumed parameters, such as low wave speeds or neglect of gas-water interactions; however, this can lead to non-physical numerical oscillations when applied to actual physical characteristics (Bousso et al., 2013). To accurately describe complex physical process, it is typically necessary to perform mathematical derivations or adjust parameters based on different assumptions, in order to obtain more precise equations (Sharior et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2022). These uncertain characteristics, expressed in the form of equations, can be learned in collections of measured numeric data (Todorovski, 2017).3)Computational Efficiency: Mainstream grid-based methods, such as Finite Volume Method (FVM), rely on spatiotemporal resolution to ensure accuracy and stability, but they are often limited by grid boundaries during simulation (Yang et al., 2024). Some mesh-free methods, such as Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH), are often accompanied by the problem of long simulation time in the simulation process (Song et al., 2024, 2023). Surrogate models, as fast alternatives, offer significant potential for accelerating computational speed (Garzón et al., 2022).

Currently, there is extensive research on calculating hydrodynamic problems through data-driven methods, which may effectively address the above issues. Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINN) is a semi-supervised learning method that couples measured data with physics laws and applies deep neural networks (DNN) to solve PDE systems (Raissi et al., 2019). Because of its great potential in solving PDEs to describe the unrepresented physics, PINN has been used in data assimilation (Feng et al., 2023; Sliwinski and Rigas, 2023), equation discovery (X. Chen et al., 2021) and swift surrogate model (Z. Chen et al., 2021; Donnelly et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2023).

Recent advancements in the application of PINNs in the hydraulics and hydrology domain have sparked considerable research interest. Waqar et al. (2022) and Ye et al. (2022) have each proposed methodologies using PINNs to resolve water hammer equations within a reservoir-pipe-valve system, paving the way for modeling wave propagation using PINNs. Following this work, Ye et al. (2024) integrated the complex topology of pipe networks into PINNs by incorporating a versatile elastic water column model suitable for various network configurations. In a study by Feng et al. (2023) the capabilities of PINNs were extended to analyze water height and flow velocity within a comprehensive river model, employing one-dimensional Saint-Venant equations to enhance coastal process simulations. Furthermore, Donnelly et al. (2023) innovatively applied PINNs in surrogate modeling for hydrodynamic simulators by solving shallow water equations. This work significantly contributes to the development of fast and accurate flood prediction models, which is a critical requirement in facing increasing flood risks exacerbated by climate change.

Although the PINNs have demonstrated their potential capability for predicting the wave propagation process in pipeline systems or river models, it has never been applied to complex flow regimes such as the transient mixed flow, which is a complicate phenomenon in urban drainage systems characterized by frequent transitions between the free surface and pressurized flow and vice versa. In the evolving landscape of related research (Yang et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2023), it is becoming evident that PINNs should be regarded not merely as a novel network architecture but more so as a paradigm that integrates the residuals of differential equations into the neural network's loss function. Therefore, this realization leads to the development of the Physics-Informed Neural Network for Transient Mixed Flow (TMF-PINN).

This study aims to provide a novel PINN-based model to investigate and potentially rectify three challenges encountered in the traditional numerical simulation of transient mixed flows. The proposed model integrates experimental data, simulation results and PDEs into its loss function, facilitating the resolution of data assimilation issues. Then, a status factor (α), is introduced to seamlessly link open channel and pressurized flow dynamics, adaptively adjusting actual physical parameters. Based on this, Fourier feature extraction and a quadratic neural network are employed to capture high-frequency dynamics. Finally, through the analysis of an analytical case, a physical experiment case, and a numerical case, it has been demonstrated that our method can effectively address the aforementioned issues.

Numerical studies

Model set-up and hyperparameters selection

A looped sewer network case presented first by Ji (1998) and further tested by Zhang et al. (2021) was used to test the proposed methods. As depicted in Fig. 1(a), the looped pipeline system is composed of six links (pipes) and six nodes. Links are represented by L, while nodes are denoted by N. Nodes N2 and N3 are subjected to variable water level boundary conditions, which can be regarded as river nodes; and nodes N1 and N5 are subject to variable flowrate boundary conditions, with the specifics illustrated in Fig. 1(b). The details of the links and nodes are provided in Fig. 1(c). At the initial condition, the water depths in all pipes are set to 0 m. This case was simulated using the TFM-PINN model, as well as with the FVM and the Storm Water Management Model (SWMM). However, SWMM only provides flowrate and water height in the middle of each pipe. The FVM, widely utilized in CFD modeling (León et al., 2006; S. Chen et al., 2021), offers a more detailed description of the flow results. Consequently, the numerical results are derived from the FVM based on the PSM model using the HLL solver, with a spatial step length of 1m, a Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) number of 0.4, the Preissmman slot widths of 0.01*Diameter, and a wave speed ranging between 20–25m/s, which also is regarded as the test dataset of TMF-PINN. Unlike the FVM, the parameters for the TMF-PINN are primarily determined by the neural network's hyperparameters. Following a sensitivity analysis in supplementary information (SI), the neural network parameters for water level and flowrate were selected to be 6 layers with 64 neurons each, while the parameters for α were chosen as 4 layers with 32 neurons each. The wave speed was set between 1000–1200m/s, depending on the diameter of the pipe. Additional details are provided in Fig. 1(c).

Our computations suggest a slight delay in the flood arrival times compared to the SWMM model predictions. On a setup featuring an Intel i5–12600K CPU and an Nvidia 3090 GPU, the FVM took 11.9 h to simulate the flow in the network containing six pipelines with a spatial step size of 1m and CFL number of 0.4. In contrast, TMF-PINN completed the training in just 39 min, while TMF-PINN(Lite) finished in approximately 21 min. Despite the significant speed improvement over FVM, the developed method still requires considerable time for entire training. While the fine-tuned TMF-PINN can make predictions in just a few seconds, re-training is necessary whenever the boundary conditions change. Thus, these models are better suited for offline analysis tasks.

Additionally, under the PSM framework, the wave speed in the FVM was at 25m/s, whereas TMF-PINN can achieve a wave speed of 1200m/s, mirroring the actual wave speed in pressurized pipeline more accurately. Looking ahead, employing a CFD-based methodology to swiftly generate mechanistic data on a coarse grid, augmented by actual experimental data for deep learning-based method training, presents a promising avenue. This strategy significantly enhances computational efficiency, aligns model parameters more closely with reality, and elevates the precision of forecasts.

The factor α is directly related to the water level. To avoid the reuse of data, we embedded h, rather than the α valve, as prior knowledge only in the loss function, as seen in Eqs. (6) and (7). The results of the proposed model are shown in Fig. 5(b). To maximize the generalization ability of TMF-PINN, the α factor tends towards a fixed value. Although this approach achieves optimal simulation results, it lacks certain interpretability.

In our proposed model, we attempted to incorporate the value of factor α as hard constraints into the loss function, as shown in Eq. (2). Under the same hyperparameters and iteration conditions, TMF-PINN is able to invert the α results from FVM in the same wave speed as FVM, as seen in Fig. 5(c). In cases with actual wave speeds, only part of the pipeline exhibited a gradual transition, which also corresponds to the transition between free surface flow and hydraulic transient flow, as seen in Fig. 5(d). This also indicates that under high wave speed conditions, it will not only result numerical oscillation in the accuracy of traditional FVM methods, but also introduce instability to data-driven models.

Errors comparison

To assess the effectiveness of the TMF-PINN model, we compared three evaluation metrics, including Mean Square Error (MSE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Kling-Gupta Efficiency (KGE) (Gupta and Kling, 2011), against those from the TMF-PINN(Lite) and standard PINN models, using identical hyperparameters and model parameters. It is evident that the α block significantly enhanced the performance of the standard PINN, enabling it to achieve relatively robust results. Although both TMF-PINN(Lite) and TMF-PINN demonstrated excellent fitting performance, each model has its own strengths and weaknesses. TMF-PINN(Lite) offers relatively rapid training times but faces difficulties in modelling high-frequency flow dynamics. In contrast, TMF-PINN employs advanced techniques to modify both the inputs and structure of neural networks, including Fourier feature extraction and a Quadratic neural network, as detailed in 4.2.3, 4.2.4. While these methods result in slower training times, they significantly enhance network's capability in capturing high-frequency wave oscillations. However, this advantage is offset by a decrease in numerical accuracy for the low-frequency flow dynamics. Overall, the TMF-PINN and its Lite version surpass standard PINN in terms of both MSE and RMSE, showcasing considerable accuracy and computational efficiency in simulating mixed flow hydraulic models for urban drainage networks.

In addition, to evaluate the capacity of TMF-PINN model in simulating pressurized flow and negative pressures in pipeline systems, we included an analytical solution case featuring a model of water hammer caused by a sudden change in inflow. Furthermore, a laboratory case was designed to simulate a simplified stormwater well-pipe-stormwater well system transitions from non-full to surcharged and eventually to full-pipe flow, demonstrating the TMF-PINN model's capability to effectively simulate surcharged flow. Both cases are included in the SI, and the origin of data used in each case is provided in Figure S7 in the SI.

Conclusions

This work proposed a novel and general PINN-based model to reconstruct complex dynamic processes in urban drainage systems, including water hammer, open channel flow and transient mixed flow. Comparing to both traditional CFD-based and data-driven solvers, our proposed model, TMF-PINN, effectively reduces the complexity associated with traditional mechanistic approaches and lowers the computational costs. When dealing with high-frequency flow dynamics, Fourier features extraction and quadratic neural networks module can be incorporated into the proposed TMF-PINN model, involving a slight trade-off in accuracy for enhanced performance in high-frequency conditions.

In the realm of data-driven solutions, this work pioneered the integration of water hammer and Saint-Venant equations through the α factor, enabling the proposed model to be applicable to various flow regimes, including both non-full and full pipe flows. To demonstrate the effectiveness and precision of the proposed model, three cases have been conducted, including one water hammer scenario with negative pressures, one experimental transient mixed flow scenario and one SWMM scenario have been conducted.

The findings from this investigation reveal that PINN-based solvers, representing a novel framework in data-driven methodologies, can effectively combine data from diverse sources, including experimental, mechanistic, and simulation sources. Offering comparable computational accuracy, these solvers significantly trim down computational demands while preserving the integrity of model parameters. Therefore, the proposed TMF-PINN model can effectively address three major issues associated with traditional methods to a certain extent.

However, like their CFD-based counterparts, PINN-based models require recalibration for distinct initial and boundary conditions, with their stability intimately tied to data precision. In the quest for versatile urban flood simulation models, PINN-based solutions emerge as a promising alternative, suggesting that for broader application, a data-driven model balancing efficiency, accuracy, and stability is preferable.

Loss function: the α factor

Transient Mixed Flow refers to a hybrid flow regime that occurs between open-channel flow and fully pressurized pipe flow. For the complex circular cross-section transient mixed flow, this paper defines a novel factor to formally unify the water hammer and the de Saint-Venant equations and embeds it into the PDE-Loss of PINN, which is given in the following equations:(11)F1=∂u^∂t+u^∂u^∂x+g∂h^∂x+α·f|u^|u^2D+(1−α)·g(Sf−S0)(12)F2=∂h^∂t+u^∂h^∂x+α·a2g∂u^∂x+(1−α)·AB∂u^∂xwhere α is the control factor, acting as the outputs of the extra neural network activated by sigmoid, which limiting the range of α from 0 to 1, as shown in Fig. 1b. When α=1, Eqs. (11) and (12) can be converted to the water hammer equations, namely Eqs. (3); when α=0, it represents the open-channel flow equations, equivalent to Eqs. (4); and when α∈(0,1), it represents the intermediate state between these two extremes.

In order to validate the effectiveness of the α factor, we trained these cases using TMF-PINN without Fourier feature embedding and quadratic neural networks, termed TMF-PINN(Lite). This neural network's initial layer can be written in the following form, respectively:(13)HTMF−PINN(Lite)=ϕ(W1x+b1)

Here, ϕ is the activation function, W1 and b1 denote the weight matrix and bias vector for the first hidden layer.

Fourier feature embedding

While PINNs have shown significant potential, recent research has uncovered certain limitations, particularly when dealing with PDEs that exhibit high-frequency or multiscale characteristics (Fuks and Tchelepi, 2020; Krishnapriyan et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2023) To address these challenges, a transformation of the PINN inputs into a multiscale feature space is suggested, using approaches such as the sinusoidal (Cheng Wong et al., 2024) or Fourier feature mappings (Tancik et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022).

Following the method introduced by (Wang et al., 2021) and (Feng et al., 2023), we incorporate a multiscale Fourier feature framework ahead of the fully connected neural network, where the Fourier feature embeddings are applied to the input coordinates as follows:(14)γ(v)=[cos(2πBv)sin(2πBv)]Where γ represents the Fourier feature mapping function, with v being the input coordinates expressed as ν=[x,t]T. The matrix B corresponds to the frequency of the multiscale Fourier features within the mapping. Entries within B are sampled from a Gaussian distribution, N(0,s). The scaling parameter s may be adaptively optimized during the PINN training process, or it can be predetermined based on the existing knowledge of the boundary conditions. Utilizing the Fourier feature embeddings transforms the neural network's initial layer to the following form:(15)H1=ϕ(W1·γ(v)+b1)where ϕ, W1 and b1 are the same as Eqs (13).

In this work, the concurrent output of water depth, flow velocity, and α is imperative. Water depth and flow velocity constitute the key outcomes, while α is a crucial variable that characterizes the state of the flow status within the pipeline, with its output bounded between 0 and 1. To facilitate this, we introduce a hybrid mapping approach. This method utilizes the Fourier feature mapping to process water depths and flow velocities, and employs a sinusoidal mapping activation function for α, structured as follows:(16)NN(h,u):H1=ϕtanh(W1·γ(v)+b1)(17)NN(α):H1=ϕsin(W1·v+b1),Hn=ϕsigmoid(Wn·Hαn−1+bn),n=2,…where NN(h,u,α) represents neural networks for output water level, flow velocity and α, respectively; the n represents the current layer of the hidden layers.

Quadratic Neural Networks

A standard DNN layer is defined asHDNN=σ(Wx+b), where Wx+b are tunable parameters and σ adds non-linearity. The linear component is Wx+b, necessitating multiple wide layers for complex patterns (Fan et al., 2020). As proposed by Bu & Karpatne (2021), quadratic residual terms at every layer of neural network contribute to additional nonlinearity.(18)H1=ϕ(Wn1x∘Wn2x︸1+Wn1x+bn)

The Hadamard product is represented by "∘", and Term 1 is the quadratic residual component (Qres). For scenarios where linear decision boundaries adequately model the complexity at hand, this quadratic neural network can simplify to a standard DNN by adjusting Wn2 to zero. Conversely, for more challenging problems requiring greater functional capacity than what DNNs offer, Qres activates the quadratic residual to enhance non-linearity without necessitating deeper or wider networks.

Finally, in this work, the neural network's layers can be written into the following form:(19)NN(h,u):Hn=ϕtanh((Wn1·γ(v))∘(Wn2·γ(v))+Wn1·γ(v)+bn),n=1,…(20)NN(α):H1=ϕsin(W1·v+b1),Hn=ϕsigmoid(Wn·Hαn−1+bn),n=2,…where the n represents the current layer of the hidden layers.

Evaluation Metrics

To assess the effectiveness of the TMF-PINN model, we have applied two evaluation metrics, including MSE and RMSE for each pipe. Since each model will have results output from six pipelines, we employed KGE to reduce the dimensionality of the error values from each pipeline, as shown in the following formula:(21)KGE=1−(rKGE−1)2+(αKGE−1)2+(βKGE−1)2where rKGE is Pearson correlation coefficient, αKGE is a term representing the variability of prediction errors, βKGE is the bias term. The terms αKGE and βKGE are defined as follows:(22)α=σsσo,β=μsμowhere φ = {s, o}, index s refers simulated time series, and index o represents observed time series, commonly used in hydrological sciences; μφ is the mean of errors, which is ∑i=1NφiN,σφ2 is the variance of errors, which is ∑i=1N(φi−μφ)2N. The KGE metric combines three statistical components: the correlation coefficient, variability ratio, and bias ratio, into a single measure of model performance. The KGE ranges from negative infinity to 1, where a value of 1 signifies perfect agreement between predictions by PINN and simulations by FVM in proposed model.

Validation data: Numerical simulation based on finite-volume strategies with pressimann slot method

For transient mixed flow, the most common method in research is still Finite-Volume Method based on the Pressimann slot method (PSM). The PSM assumes that the closed conduit can be regarded as the open channel with one narrow slot inserted into the pipe crown, thereby using the form of the one-dimensional Saint-Venant equations (SVE) to describe the phenomenon of transient mixed flow (Cunge and Wegner, 1964). The slot width can be determined based on the wave velocity a, which is defined as:(23)Ts=gAca2where Ts is the slot width, Ac is the cross-section area of the pipeline.

For circular pipes, the transient mixed flow can be described using a hyperbolic conservation law derived from the 1D Saint-Venant equations, which involve the conserved variables flow area A and flowrate q (Sturm, 2021; S. Chen et al., 2021). It is noted that the flow area A and pressure term Ifor non-full pipe flows are the same as open channel flow (Eqs.4). But when the water height is higher than the pipe crown, the B (Eqs.4) is the same as Ts (Eqs.23). This indicates that the cross-section transitions into a composite shape with a slender rectangle positioned directly above a circular shape.

In order to improve computational accuracy and efficiency, this work used the MUSCL scheme to reconstruct central variables and the HLL scheme to calculate fluxes. The HLL has a simple but efficient form when solving the Riemann approximation problem with dry cells compared to other schemes. The MUSCL method can improve the spatial accuracy of numerical solutions to second-order, and more details are referred in Toro (2001).

The handling of nodes within pipe networks presents a stark contrast to their counterparts in open channel systems. Unlike the latter, where nodes often lack precise geometric boundaries, nodes in pipe networks—typically manifesting as rain grates or manholes—are characterized by defined geometric forms (Schmitt et al., 2004). Here, the approach used in SWMM (Rossman, 2006) and many other models is adopted: the junction is assumed to be momentum less so only a continuity equation is solved (Sanders and Bradford, 2011).
