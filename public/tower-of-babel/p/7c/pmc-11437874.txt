Therapeutic single-cell landscape: methotrexate exacerbates interstitial lung disease by compromising the stemness of alveolar epithelial cells under systemic inflammation
Authors: Sung Hae Chang, Seyoung Jung, Jeong Jun Chae, Jeong Yeon Kim, Seon Uk Kim, Ji Yong Choi, Hye-Jeong Han, Hyun Taek Kim, Hak-Jae Kim, Hyun Je Kim, Woong Yang Park, Jeffrey A Sparks, Eun Young Lee, Jeong Seok Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundInterstitial lung disease (ILD) poses a serious threat in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA). However, the impact of cornerstone drugs, including methotrexate (MTX) and TNF inhibitor, on RA-associated ILD (RA-ILD) remains controversial.MethodsUsing an SKG mouse model and single-cell transcriptomics, we investigated the effects of MTX and TNF blockade on ILD.FindingsOur study revealed that MTX exacerbates pulmonary inflammation by promoting immune cell infiltration, Th17 activation, and fibrosis. In contrast, TNF inhibitor ameliorates these features and inhibits ILD progression. Analysis of data from a human RA-ILD cohort revealed that patients with ILD progression had persistently higher systemic inflammation than those without progression, particularly among the subgroup undergoing MTX treatment.InterpretationThese findings highlight the need for personalized therapeutic approaches in RA-ILD, given the divergent outcomes of MTX and TNF inhibitor.FundingThis work was funded by GENINUS Inc., and the National Research Foundation of Korea, and Seoul National University Hospital.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease, Methotrexate, Tumor necrosis factor inhibitor, SKG mouse, Macrophage differentiation, Th17 cell activation, Fibrosis

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Rheumatoid arthritis-associated interstitial lung disease (RA-ILD) is a serious extra-articular manifestation characterized by progressive lung fibrosis and considerable morbidity and mortality. The management of RA-ILD remains challenging, as there is insufficient evidence to guide the use of disease-modifying drugs and biologics.

Methotrexate (MTX) and tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) are pivotal in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), yet their impact on RA-ILD remains a topic of ongoing debate. MTX, despite concerns about pulmonary toxicity, has recent data suggesting it may not increase ILD risk and might even protect against it, although historical and observational reports imply a potential for lung injury. Similarly, TNFi has improved RA outcomes by reducing inflammation and joint damage, but its impact on the lung is mixed, with studies reporting both exacerbation of ILD and stabilization or improvement in lung function in RA-ILD patients.

Epidemiologic studies have provided valuable information on the connections between MTX, TNFi, and lung disease in RA patients; however, few mechanistic studies have investigated the specific effects of these treatments on the lungs. MTX has been demonstrated to promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition in human alveolar adenocarcinoma cell lines, thus playing a role in fibrosis development. Tumor necrosis factor, secreted by macrophages and epithelial cells in response to pulmonary irritants in both humans and experimental animals, is recognized for its substantial contribution to tissue remodeling and pulmonary fibrosis. However, the precise mechanisms through which MTX and TNFi influence the lung environment with systemic inflammation, such as in RA-ILD, have not yet been thoroughly studied, especially at the microenvironmental level.

Added value of this study

The study examined the effects of MTX and TNFi on early-stage lung inflammation in SKG mice, an experimental animal model of RA-ILD. MTX induced significant pneumonitis, whereas TNFi did not, despite both MTX and TNFi treatments effectively alleviating joint inflammation. Within the lung microenvironment, MTX treatment led to an increase in the proportion of alveolar epithelial cells exhibiting compromised stemness while promoting a pro-inflammatory environment through the facilitation of monocyte-to-alveolar macrophage (AM) differentiation. MTX treatment also enhanced the activation of Th17 cells and increased the proportion of myofibroblasts expressing genes targeted by antifibrotic agents. In contrast, TNFi treatment ameliorated these features, inhibiting ILD progression.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our research sheds light on the complex microenvironmental mechanisms underlying divergent outcomes of MTX and TNFi in patients with RA-ILD, highlighting the importance of personalized treatment strategies in managing RA-ILD. In real world clinical practice, the concurrent use of various medications and individual susceptibility leads to diverse therapeutic responses and long-term outcomes of MTX or TNFi treatment in RA-ILD patients. Nevertheless, our current animal study could provide important insights into understanding data from human patients, particularly by elucidating how MTX provokes unexpected pathologic responses under systemic inflammation. Furthermore, the current study results could provide biological support that TNFi treatment without MTX might benefit patients with RA-ILD.

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) constitutes a complex spectrum of pulmonary disorders characterized by inflammation and fibrosis within the lung interstitium.1,2 In rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, ILD can manifest as a progressive and potentially life-threatening condition.3,4 However, conclusive outcomes in research on RA-associated ILD (RA-ILD) are challenging, even in prospective cohort studies, due to the disease's insidious onset and slow progression, heterogeneous clinical manifestations, and the complexities associated with therapeutic interventions for RA. Understanding the pathophysiology and clinical implications of RA-ILD is crucial, as they influence therapeutic decisions and patient outcomes.5

Methotrexate (MTX), a cornerstone of RA treatment, is the most commonly used disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) and effectively suppresses joint inflammation. MTX is associated with a spectrum of adverse pulmonary events, including pneumonitis.6, 7, 8 However, conflicting reports suggest its safety in certain RA populations.9, 10, 11 Studies in mouse models have shown that MTX treatment induces lung fibrosis and apoptosis.12, 13, 14 Similarly, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibition, while effective at managing RA symptoms, raises questions regarding its impact on ILD.15,16 Individuals suffering from RA-ILD seldom participate in clinical trials with biological agents, such as TNF inhibitors, leading to a shortage of safety data in this context.17 Given that screening for the presence of ILD is not always recommended when initiating drug therapy for RA, studies conducted on real-world patients may inherently carry significant biases.18,19 Therefore, translational research into pathophysiology is needed to bridge this knowledge gap.

The pathogenesis of ILD can be divided into two main components, inflammation and fibrosis. These processes often interact, sometimes exacerbating tissue damage and organ failure, but they may also ameliorate each other.20,21 Notably, the interplay among immune cells, including lung epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages, influences the differentiation of each cell population, occasionally revealing unique cell subsets observed only in disease models. The SKG mouse model is an experimental animal model affecting multiple organs that is widely employed in research related to autoimmune diseases.22, 23, 24, 25, 26 This model is characterized by specific mutations in ZAP70 that influence the development and function of T cells, playing a pivotal role in triggering autoimmune responses.22,27 SKG mice manifest inflammatory arthritis and ILD simultaneously, providing an ideal context for investigating RA-ILD.

Recent advancements in single-cell transcriptomics have revolutionized the ability to investigate complex diseases at the cellular level.28 We leverage this technology to dissect the early effects of MTX and TNF blockade on the single-cell landscape of ILD in the context of RA. By scrutinizing the molecular signatures and cellular interactions, we aimed to provide insights into the underlying biology driving divergent outcomes that are observed in clinical practice.

Methods

Ethics

All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Soonchunhyang University Research Institute (IACUC No: SCH20-0037, SCH19-0061, SCH21-0045).

Mice

SKG mice (CLEA Japan, IMSR_JCL:JCL:MOD-0003) aged 7–12 weeks were used in the experiments. All mice were kept in specific pathogen-free conditions within the Gyerim Experimental Animal Resource (GEAR) Center.

Induction of arthritis and pneumonitis in murine disease model and treatment

At 8–10 weeks of age, male SKG mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of zymosan from Saccharomyces cerevisiae (7.5 mg per 20 g; MilliporeSigma, Z4250) to induce arthritis and pneumonitis. We then gave them twice-weekly intraperitoneal injections of PBS, MTX (7.5 mg/kg; MilliporeSigma, M9929), or TNFα inhibitor (100 μg/kg; R&D Systems, MAB4101). Each group consisted of four mice, with each group maintained in separate cages and housed in the same room. Under uniform environmental conditions, each animal in both the placebo and treatment groups was administered injections of the same volume, using the same technique. The treatment schedule is summarized in Fig. 1a.

Lung histology

The left lung of each injected mouse was inflated with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The right lung was resected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Immunofluorescent staining

Each paraffin-embedded lung section was sliced into 5 μm sections and deparaffinated. Briefly, each slide was rinsed twice with PBS for 5–10 min at room temperature, incubated with preheated heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) buffer for 15–30 min at 95–99 °C, then treated with cold PBS for 20 min on ice. For membrane permeabilization, the samples were treated with PBST (PBS with 0.1% Triton X100) for 10 min at room temperature, then incubated with 2–5% serum in PBST for 30 min to block nonspecific antigen–antibody reaction. Next, the samples were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibody. The following antibodies were used for immunofluorescence: Alpha-smooth muscle actin-cy3/α-SMA (MilliporeSigma, C6198), CXCL15 (Abcam, ab197016), and Isolectin B4, FITC-conjugate/IsoB4 (MilliporeSigma, L2895).

Joint assessment

Arthritis scores were assessed weekly by visual inspection and scored as follows: 0, no evidence of erythema or swelling; 1, erythema and mild swelling confined to the tarsals or ankle joint; 2, erythema and mild swelling extending from the ankle to the tarsals; 3, erythema and moderate swelling extending from the ankle to metatarsal joints; 4, erythema and severe swelling encompassing the ankle, foot, and digits, or ankylosis of the limb. Mean scores were calculated for both the forepaws and hindpaws.

Single-nucleus preparation from frozen mouse lung

Nuclei were prepared from frozen lung tissue under ribonuclease-free conditions using a method adapted from an existing protocol. Briefly, samples were cut into approximately 7-mm pieces, injected with 1 ml of ice-cold Nuclei EZ Lysis buffer (MilliporeSigma, NUC101) supplemented with a protease (Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd, 589279100) and ribonuclease (RNAse; Promega Corp., N2615) inhibitor (250 units/ml RNasin® Plus; Promega Corp., N2615) using a 26-gauge needle, minced into 1 to 2-mm pieces with scissors in a weigh boat with 1 ml additional supplemented lysis buffer, and then transferred to a gentleMACS™ C tube (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-093-237). The gentleMACS lung1 and lung 2 programs were run in sequence, with the latter stopped after 20 s. The foam was spun down for 10 s using a short spin. The suspension was passed through a 40-μm cell strainer and washed with 4 °C cold PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Nuclei were pelleted at 500×g and resuspended in 1X PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.5 units/μl RNasin Plus before counting them by hemocytometer and diluting to 1000 nuclei/μl. For 10X Chromium, 10,000 nuclei were loaded per lane. The 10x Chromium libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer's protocol (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) using 3’ V3.1 kits and submitted for sequencing through the Geninus Inc. Laboratory, Seoul, Korea, on a NovaSeq S1 flow cell to a depth of 50,000 reads/cell.

Library preparation and sequencing

Raw sequencing data were processed using the zUMIs pipeline, removing low-quality barcodes and then mapping the remaining barcodes to the mouse genome (mm10) using Cell Ranger v4 (10x Genomics). Expression matrices containing intronic, exonic, and intronic + exonic reads were generated for snRNA seq data.

Batch correction and clustering of scRNA-seq data

Additional filtering was performed in Seurat 4 with R 4.3.0.1 (Satija Lab, New York, NY, USA). We filtered cells using UMI counts, expressed gene counts, and mitochondria percentage. After filtering, we normalized the data using the SCTransform function in each dataset. Integration of samples was achieved using the top 3000 variable genes from the SelectIntegrationFeatures function. After the PrepSCTIntegration function was used to prepare for integration, anchors were found by the FindIntegrationAnchors function to transform the data in a shared space. Finally, we constructed a batch effects-corrected transformed “SCT” data matrix using the IntegrateData function.

For clustering, PCA (RunPCA function) was carried out for dimensional reduction of the transformed “SCT” data matrix. UMAP and neighbors were determined with the top 50 principal components (PCs) using the RunUMAP and FindNeighbors functions. The cells then underwent unsupervised clustering according to the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph (FindClusters function, resolution = 0.5) and were visualized by UMAP using the top 50 PCs. For sub-clustering analysis of AECs, mono/macrophages, T/NK cells, and fibroblasts, each count matrix of the subcluster was normalized and scaled using the same procedure above and underwent unsupervised clustering using PCs as described above (AECs: PCs = 21, resolution = 0.8; mono/macrophages: PCs = 30, resolution = 0.4; T/NK cells: PCs = 30, resolution = 1.4; fibroblasts: PCs = 20, resolution = 0.2).

Characterization of each subcluster of scRNA-seq data

To identify marker genes, the DEGs in each cluster relative to the other clusters were selected based on the Wilcoxon rank sum test using the FindAllMarkers function (default parameter). To describe the characteristics of each subcluster, we then performed gene set enrichment analysis by calculating the gene set module score (AddModule-Score in the Seurat package), combined score (enrichR), and enrichment score (GSEA v4.2.3, Broad Institute, CA, USA) using publicly available gene sets, including the Gene Ontology Biological Process databases (GO.BP), IREA, KEGG, and HALLMARK pathway.

Pseudo-time and interactome analysis of scRNA-seq data

To investigate the dynamic changes in the mono/macrophage subsets, we exported cells from the monocyte/macrophage subset for monocle3's standard analysis process (monocle3, v1.3.1). CellDataSet objects were built based on the normalized count (transformed “RNA” data matrix) using the as cell_data_set function. Subsequently, the learn_graph function and orderCells function (default option) were used to generate the trajectories of mono/macrophage subset from our scRNA-seq data. We then performed downstream analysis of specific branches from trajectories using the plot_cells function. In fibroblast subsets, we exported cells from the fibroblast subset for monocle's standard analysis process (monocle2, v2.22.0). CellDataSet objects were built based on the normalized count (transformed “RNA” data matrix) using the newCellDataSet function. The size factors and dispersion of gene expression were estimated. The dimension of the normalized data was reduced using the reduceDimension function (method = DDRTree), followed by the orderCells function to generate the trajectories of fibroblast subsets.

Clustering gene module in condition and cell type specific DEGs

To identify the dynamic change of gene expressions between conditions, the DEGs in specific cell type cluster or condition relative to the other clusters were selected based on the Wilcoxon rank sum test using the FindAllMarkers function (default parameter) and FindMarkers function (default parameter). Average expression value of DEGs in each condition that satisfied the following criteria (p < 0.05, logFC >0.25) were calculated using AverageExpression function in the Seurat package (default parameter). K-means clustering was performed on DEGs using kmeans function in the stats package (centers = 9).

In vitro assay for Th17 cell differentiation

To investigate the effects of MTX in Th17 cell differentiation, 0.5 × 106 A549 cells (ATCC, CVCL_0023) were incubated in 16-well with or without 1 μM MTX (Supelco, PHR1396). Th17 cell differentiation was achieved by coculturing 0.5 × 106 PBMCs between 0.4 μm transwell plates either in absence or presence of MTX treated A549 cells, further stimulated with 1:16 Human T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Gibco, 11131D). For intracellular cytokine staining, the cells were further treated with 50 ng/ml PMA and 500 ng/ml Ionomycin, followed by 0.4 μl GolgiPlug™ Protein Transport Inhibitor (Containing Brefeldin A) (BD sciences, 555029) and GolgiStop™ Protein Transport Inhibitor (Containing Monensin) (BD sciences, 554724) for 6 h after polarization.

Flow cytometry

PBMCs were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies, for 15 min at room temperature. The Live/Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen, L34972) was used to exclude dead cells. For intracellular staining, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Kit (Invitrogen, 00-5523-00) for 15 min. Next, antibodies to label cytoplasmic proteins were added for another 15-min incubation. Flow cytometry was performed using an FACSymphony™ A5 Cell Analyzer (BD Bioscience), and the data were analyzed with FlowJo software version 10.4 (Treestar). The available flow cytometry plots and paper citations using the antibody clone were carefully reviewed prior to purchase to determine experimental suitability. Prior to experiment, each antibody was serially diluted and used to stain PBMCs to determine the optimal dilution and confirm that the proportion of stained cells was consistent with published data. Following directly conjugated antibodies were used to identify cell markers of human CD4+ T cells (clone, manufacturer, and RRID): mouse anti-human CD4-BV510 (SK3, BD sciences, AB_2744424), mouse anti-human CD3-BV605 (UCHT1, BD sciences, AB_2740919), mouse anti-human IL-17A-BV-711 (BL168, Biolegend, AB_2563888), mouse anti-human CD14-PE-TR (61D3, eBioscience, AB_2574534), mouse anti-human CD19-PE-TR (HIB19, eBioscience, AB_2574538), rat anti-human IL-4-APC (MP4-25D2, Biolegend, AB_315131), mouse anti-human IFN- γ-APC-R700 (B27, BD sciences, AB_2739031), mouse anti-human CD45RA-APC/Cyanine7 (HI100, Biolegend, AB_10708880).

The RA-ILD cohort data

We analyzed data from the Korean Rheumatoid Arthritis Interstitial Lung Disease (KORAIL) cohort, a multicenter prospective longitudinal observational study from six tertiary hospitals in Korea. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the ethics committees of all six participating centers, with all participants providing written informed consent (Supplementary Table S6). All procedures followed the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Participants aged 18 years and above diagnosed with RA according to the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) criteria, coupled with ILD identified through chest computed tomography (CT) scans were recruited at an outpatient rheumatology clinic, spanning from January 2015 to July 2018.29 Since this cohort was an observational study aligned with routine clinical practice, we allowed a ±3-month window period around the established follow-up schedule. The follow-up period extended for three years (date of last follow-up: September 2021). Additional information about the KORAIL cohort can be found in the preceding publication.30, 31, 32

Among 168 participants enrolled in the cohort, we excluded participants incompatible with ILD by the second review after the cohort completion. We defined the methotrexate treatment group as participants treated with methotrexate for at least 24 weeks before enrollment or during the follow-up periods. Progression was defined as any exacerbation of the visual CT score compared to the baseline during the follow-up period. The baseline characteristics of the participants are indicated in Supplementary Table S7.

Continuous variables were compared between the two treatment groups (MTX users vs. non-MTX users) at enrollment using either a student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. For categorical variables, a chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was used. Linear mixed effects models (lme) were used to analyze the longitudinal data with continuous outcomes including ESR over time, accounting for both fixed and random effects. The fixed effects included treatment group (MTX use vs non-MTX user), visit, and their interaction, while the random effects accounted for subject-specific variability. Analyses were performed using the ‘nlme’ package in R version 4.3.2.

Statistical analysis

The respective sample sizes were established based on internal pilot studies and prior research.33,34 Statistical analysis was conducted using Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software) and R (4.2.1 version). Statistical analysis was performed using the two-sided Mann–Whitney U test, unpaired T test, Spearman rank correlation, one-way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis test and Friedman test with Dunn's multiple comparisons. p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Role of funders

The funders had no role in study design, data collection and analyses, interpretation or writing of this manuscript.

Results

MTX exacerbates pulmonary inflammation in the SKG mouse ILD model

SKG mice received intraperitoneal injections of zymosan A to induce RA-ILD. To investigate the effect of DMARDs in the lungs of the RA-ILD animal model, we treated the mice with PBS, MTX, or TNF inhibitor intraperitoneally for 12 weeks (Fig. 1a). These mice were further divided into four groups: SKG mice with no stimulation (n = 4; negative control [CTL]), SKG mice with zymosan A stimulation (n = 4; positive CTL), SKG mice with zymosan A stimulation and MTX treatment (n = 4; MTX-treated), and SKG mice with zymosan A stimulation and TNFα inhibitor treatment (n = 4; TNFi-treated). Twelve weeks after zymosan A injection, the mice were sacrificed, and lung samples collected for further analysis (Fig. 1a). Arthritis scores were checked from 7 weeks post zymosan A injection. Joint swelling was observed in the positive CTL group, whereas the SKG mice treated with MTX or TNF-a inhibitor had lower arthritis scores (Supplementary Fig. S1a). Histopathological analysis by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed an initiation of pulmonary inflammation and leukocyte aggregation in the positive CTL group, and these features were exacerbated in the MTX-treated group and alleviated in the TNFi-treated group (Fig. 1b).

Immune landscape of lung cells

Using the 10x Genomics platform, we performed single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of extracted nuclei from lung tissues in 16 SKG mice, acquiring a total of 59,077 cells after filtering the data. Unsupervised clustering of single-cell data with a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) resulted in 27 clusters after dimensional reduction using the Seurat package. By classifying cell types using previous reports on single-nucleus cells from mouse lungs,35 we annotated cell sub-types with representative marker genes (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table S1). These clusters and annotated cell types were unbiased according to experimental batches from scRNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. S1b).

In downstream analysis, we categorized the identified cell sub-types into four major cell types: epithelial, immune, endothelial, and fibroblasts. To analyze dynamic changes in the inflammatory lung microenvironment, we examined the quantitative changes in the major cell types in different groups (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. S1c). We observed a significant increase in immune cells and decrease in epithelial cells in MTX-treated mice compared to the negative CTL group (Fig. 1d).

To analyze changes in the lung microenvironment by treatment in detail, we further dissected each major cell type into minor subclusters (Supplementary Fig. S1c–f) and investigated the proportional and qualitative changes in each cell type compared to the negative CTL group. In epithelial cells, we observed a significant decrease (p < 0.05) in ciliated cells in the MTX-treated group (Fig. 1e). In immune cells, the proportion of alveolar macrophages and T cells increased in the MTX-treated groups, whereas B cells were decreased in all groups (Fig. 1f). Most of the upregulated genes compared to the negative CTL group originated from alveolar macrophages in all three conditions (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. S1g). Overall, although MTX and TNF blockade alleviated arthritic features in SKG mice, MTX exacerbated pulmonary inflammation with increased immune cell infiltration, comprising mostly T cells and lung macrophages.

MTX impairs alveolar epithelial cell stemness and induces an innate immune response

As they composed the major population of epithelial cells in previous data, we analyzed alveolar epithelial cells (AECs). When we performed a further sub-clustering analysis of AECs, including type 1 (AT1) and type 2 (AT2) cells (n = 15,153), we identified seven different cell types: resting AT2, primed AT2, damage-associated transient progenitors (DATPs), other AT2, mitochondria (MT)-high AT2, AT1, and MT-high AT1 (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table S2). Primed AT2 cells and DATPs were classified by upregulated gene expression of Lrg1, Lcn2, Hif1a, and Krt8, as well as inflammatory cytokines Il33 and Cxcl15 (Fig. 2b). In the MTX-treated group, we observed relatively increased proportion of primed AT2 cells and DATPs (Fig. 2c). Pseudo-time analysis revealed skewness of DATPs towards AT1 cells in the MTX-treated group, which were previously reported in IL-1β-treated lung organoids (Supplementary Fig. S2a).36 By performing enrichment analysis on a BMI1-knockout mouse gene set exhibiting depressed self-renewal in lung cells,37 we discovered specific enrichment in cluster-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in primed AT2 cells (Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) = 2.224; FDR q-value < 1e-3) and DATPs (NES = 1.770; FDR q-value < 1e-3), but not in resting AT2 cells (NES = 1.019; FDR q-value = 0.430) (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. S2b). The MTX-specific module score was specifically upregulated in the MTX-treated group for primed AT2 cells and DATPs (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. S2c).

To characterize the subtypes of AT2 cells in detail, we analyzed the transcriptomic changes between the different DMARD treatment groups. Genes significantly upregulated in primed AT2 cells and DATPs from the MTX-treated group were analyzed in terms of Gene Ontology (GO) biological pathways. Leukocyte aggregation, Positive regulation of endocytosis, Intrinsic apoptosis, Cytokine-mediated signaling pathway, and Negative regulation of epithelial cell proliferation were enriched in GO terms (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. S2d). We obtained additional evidence of downregulated cell stemness in MTX-treated AT2 cells by examining the gene expression of Tfcp2l1, which encodes a transcription factor for AT2 cell self-renewal and restriction of the inflammatory program.38 Tfcp2l1 expression was decreased in AT2 cells from the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. S2e).

To confirm whether the damaged cell stemness and inflammatory features of primed AT2 cells and DATPs were MTX-specific, we performed K-means clustering in condition- and cell type-specific DEGs from primed AT2 cells and DATPs (Supplementary Fig. S2f). The DEGs were clustered into nine modules, and each module was further analyzed in terms of GO biological pathways. Module 2 (n = 81), which was composed of genes upregulated in both the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups, was related to cytokine signaling by interferon and TNF-α, as well as the inflammatory response (Fig. 2g and h). Module 4 (n = 96), which was specifically upregulated in MTX-treated mice, was enriched with GO terms Inflammatory responses, Leukocyte aggregation, and Positive regulation of apoptotic signaling pathway. Among upregulated genes in Module 4, Cxcl15, which encodes a key molecule of neutrophil chemotaxis, was prominently upregulated in AT2 cells and correlated with an increased proportion of neutrophils in the MTX-treated groups (Fig. 2i). Expression of CXCL15 protein in AT2 cells was confirmed by immunofluorescent staining of MTX-treated lung tissue (Fig. 2j). Overall, MTX treatment led to an increased proportion of stemness-damaged primed AT2 cells and DATPs, followed by upregulation of inflammatory genes, which may contribute to the recruitment of immune cells, including neutrophils.

Divergent effects of MTX and TNF inhibition on monocyte-to-alveolar macrophage differentiation via aberrant pro-inflammatory pathways

To uncover treatment-specific transcriptional signatures in alveolar macrophages, we subclustered monocytes and macrophages (n = 11,342), revealing nine different cell types based on representative marker gene expression: classical monocyte, non-classical monocyte, MHCII-high monocyte, interstitial macrophage (IM), monocyte-derived alveolar macrophage (AM), Kcnip4+ tissue-resident AM, Adipor2+ tissue-resident AM, proliferating AM, MT-high AM, and undefined sub-cluster (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S3). Interestingly, monocyte-derived AMs specifically expressed inflammatory cytokine gene Il1b and interferon-responsive genes Hif1a and Irf1 with profibrogenic genes Inhba, Mmp12, and Gpnmb (Fig. 3b). We observed an increase in the proportions of MHCII-high monocytes and monocyte-derived AMs in the MTX-treated group, which were significantly reduced in the TNFi-treated group (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. S3a). In contrast, Kcnip4+ tissue-resident AMs were relatively decreased in the MTX-treated group and showed an increase in the TNFi-treated group. By performing immune response enrichment analysis (IREA),39 we discovered that TNF-α was the main contributor to pro-inflammatory monocyte differentiation, further detailing the contrary effect of MTX treatment and TNF blockade (Fig. 3d and e).

In principal components analysis (PCA), we found distinct transcriptomic features of monocyte-derived AMs compared to other tissue-resident AM clusters (Fig. 3f). To further investigate the effect of DMARD treatment on the differentiation and activation of monocytes and infiltrated macrophages, we performed a differentiation pseudo-time ordering analysis. We discovered diverging trajectories, with MHCII-high monocytes being the bifurcation point: one trajectory directly towards monocyte-derived AMs and the other transiting interstitial macrophages (Fig. 3g). Notably, the trajectory from MHCII-high monocytes to monocyte-derived macrophages was only specific in the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups, not in the negative CTL and TNFi-treated groups. We validated these changes in the cell trajectories by checking the monocyte-derived AM signatures of MHCII-high monocytes given different treatments. MHCII-high monocytes in the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups had significantly skewed toward monocyte-derived AMs, which is shown as upregulated marker gene expression of monocyte-derived AM, but this was suppressed by TNF blockade (Fig. 3h).

Analyzing treatment-specific DEGs in monocyte-derived AMs, the MTX-treated group had significantly upregulation of inflammatory signature Nos2, as well as cytokine-related genes Il1b, Il12a, and Cxcl16 (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. S3b and c). Upregulation of interferon-responsive genes Hif1a, Irf1, and Stat1 was observed in the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups (Fig. 3i). Because the expression of these genes was significantly upregulated in monocyte-derived AMs compared to other myeloid cell types, we further validated the average gene expression of each gene after different treatments. Expression of Il1a, Il1b, Il12a, and Cxcl16 in MHCII-high monocytes and monocyte-derived AMs were specifically upregulated in the MTX-treated group but significantly downregulated in the TNFi-treated group (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. S3d and e). Interestingly, the proportion of Adipor2+ tissue-resident AMs was increased in the TNFi-treated group, followed by upregulation of cell cycle-related genes (Cdk6, Cdk14) (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. S3f).

Overall, pulmonary inflammation induced by zymosan stimulation and MTX treatment led to the infiltration of MHCII-high monocytes, which further differentiated into monocyte-derived AMs with pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrogenic features. MTX treatment upregulated interferon-responsive genes, as well as Il1b and Cxcl16 in MHCII-high monocytes and monocyte-derived AMs, which was further attributed to the differentiation of AT2 and immune cells by boosting the inflammatory microenvironment in the lungs. Conversely, TNF blockade significantly suppressed the initiation of ILD by inhibiting TNF-α-mediated monocyte-derived AM differentiation and activation, which was followed by an increased proportion of tissue-resident AMs, which may contribute to maintenance of immune homeostasis.

MTX augments differentiation and activation of lung tissue-resident CD4+ T cells into Th17 through enhanced TCR stimulation and NF-κB signaling

Next, we studied lymphocyte-specific features that dynamically changed during zymosan-induced pneumonitis, as the proportion of T cells was increased in the MTX-treated group. We sub-clustered T cells and NK cells (n = 6958) into 12 cell types and found activated Th17 cells, which had upregulated gene expression of effector features (Il17a, Furin, Bhlhe40, Tnf, Rora) described in recent studies40,41 (Fig. 4a and b and Supplementary Table S4). The CD4+ Tem subcluster encompassed T helper subtype (Th1, Th2, Th17) features, with Th1 and Th17-skewness observed in MTX-treated mice (Fig. 4c). The proportion of CD56high NK and activated pulmonary MAIT cells were increased in the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. S4a).

Unlike Th17 cells, activated Th17 cells were specifically increased in both the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups compared to the TNFi-treated group (Fig. 4d and e and Supplementary Fig. S4a). Because Th17 and activated Th17 cells had different quantitative changes with treatment, we examined whether the functional gene expression was also altered. We performed K-means clustering of cell type-specific and group-specific DEGs in Th17 and activated Th17 cells (Fig. 4f). DEGs were clustered into nine modules, and we identified gene modules specifically upregulated in both the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups (Module 5) and only in the MTX-treated groups (Module 4). Module 4 (n = 80), which was specifically upregulated in MTX-treated mice, was comprised of genes related to Th17 cell effectorness (Bhlhe40, Furin, Tnf), as well as NF-κB signaling pathways (Il1r1, Nfkbia, Tnf). Comparing Modules 4 and 5 with the GO analysis by KEGG and MSigDB Hallmark, Th17 cell differentiation was enriched in both the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups, whereas Inflammatory response and TCR receptor signaling pathway were specifically associated with MTX treatment (Fig. 4g). In accordance with these findings, the NF-κB signaling pathway was significantly upregulated in the activated Th17 and activated pulmonary MAIT subcluster (Fig. 4h).

We further investigated the main contributors to the induction of activated Th17 cells as a pathological Th17 state. The NF-κB signaling pathway can be activated by TNF receptor 1, T cell receptor, IL-1 receptor 1, and Toll-like receptor.42 Furin can enhance the TCR stimulation, which leads to enhanced activity of transcription factors (AP-1, NFAT, and NF-κB) in activated or memory T cells.43 Gene expression of Furin was significantly upregulated in MTX-treated groups and downregulated in the TNFi-treated group (Fig. 4i). Il1r1 and Cxcr6 are markers of pathologic Th17 cells.40 Because expression of IL-1β and CXCL16 was specifically enriched in MHCII-high monocytes and monocyte-derived AMs, we further investigated the cell interactions between these myeloid cells and T cells. Encoding the receptors for these cytokines, Il1r1 and Cxcr6 were enriched in activated Th17 and pulmonary MAIT cells, especially in the MTX-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. S4b–d). Cxcl16 expression in both MHCII-high monocytes and monocyte-derived AMs significantly correlated with Cxcr6 expression in activated Th17 cells, indicating that MHCII-high monocytes and monocyte-derived AMs are the main contributors to activated Th17 cells (Fig. 4j).

To investigate how MTX-treated inflammatory lung microenvironment contributes to differentiation and activation of Th17, we performed in vitro experiments consisting in coculturing MTX-treated A549 cells (a cell line model for AT2 cells), with human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) between 0.4 μm transwell plate. We discovered significant increased frequency of Th17 cells as well as IFN-γ+ Polyfunctional Th17 cells, which was further suppressed by TNFi treatment (Fig. 4k–m and Supplementary Fig. S4e).

The proportion of activated Th17 cells was specifically increased in MTX-treated groups and expressed Cxcr6 and Furin, which are well-known features of pathological Th17 found in other autoimmune diseases.40 The pro-inflammatory features of activated Th17 highly correlated with upregulated inflammatory cytokine expression in MHCII-high monocytes and monocyte-derived AMs, as well as Furin expression, which may be induced by TCR activation.

TNF inhibition diminishes myofibroblasts and inhibits fibrosis

We performed an immunofluorescence assay of lung tissue from SKG mice. Immunofluorescent staining of α-SMA and IsoB4 revealed myofibroblast and immune cell aggregation at foci in MTX-treated lung tissue (Fig. 5a and b), delineating the profibrogenic role of MTX in chronic lung inflammation.

To further investigate the characteristics of fibroblasts and transcriptomic changes affected by MTX, we subclustered fibroblasts (n = 2197) into three subclusters: fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table S5). Among these, the myofibroblast subcluster exhibited extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis genes Col1a1 and Col1a2 (Fig. 5d). Consistent with the immunofluorescent staining, the proportion of myofibroblasts was significantly upregulated in the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups but decreased in the TNFi-treated group (Fig. 5e). Antifibrotic drugs such as pirfenidone and nintedanib, targeting fibroblasts, have been developed and are utilized in the treatment of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), showing promise for the treatment of RA-ILD.44 Expression of the target genes of pirfenidone and nintedanib was also upregulated in myofibroblasts (Fig. 5f). Trajectory analysis revealed a transition of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, with increased gene expression of Col1a1 and Col1a2 (Fig. 5g). Although MTX treatment upregulated pathogenic ECM synthesis genes Col1a2, Col3a1, and Col5a2 in myofibroblasts, genes related to basal membrane synthesis (Col4a1, Col4a2) were downregulated in the MTX-treated groups (Fig. 5h). Overall, MTX treatment increased differentiation into myofibroblasts, followed by upregulated expression of ECM synthesis genes, further aggravating the progression of ILD and fostering pulmonary fibrosis in SKG mice.

As we observed distinctive pulmonary fibrosis in the lungs of MTX-treated mice, we focused on profibrogenic changes in the transcriptome across different cell types. In AT2 cells, the fibrogenic AT2 cell signature was significantly upregulated in the MTX-treated group in resting AT2 cells, primed AT2 cells, and DATPs, in accordance with downregulated expression of Cdc42 (Supplementary Fig. S5a and b).45 Dysregulated club cells, defined by the upregulation of SCGB3A1 and MUC5B, also contributed to lung fibrosis.46, 47, 48 As a marker of dysregulated club cells, MHC-related genes and Scgb3a1 were also upregulated in MTX-treated mice (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. S5c).49

Next, we performed sub-cluster analysis of endothelial cells and identified four subtypes (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. S1d). Transcriptomic expression of Foxf1, a gene related to activation of fibroblast and immune cell migration, was downregulated in endothelial cells from the positive CTL and MTX-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. S5d). Similarly, MTX-treated endothelial cells were enriched with genes related to chemokines for immune cell recruitment (Icam1, Sell, Ccl9) and profibrotic features (Fn1, Spp1) (Fig. 5j). In macrophages, genes upregulated in Fab5+ fibrogenic macrophages from human IPF patients were also upregulated in monocyte-derived AMs (Fig. 5k). We further analyzed an independent scRNA-seq dataset from the lungs of human IPF patients and observed shared profibrogenic features of monocyte-derived AMs in our single-cell data (Supplementary Fig. S5e–g).50 Thus, there is a resemblance between the profibrotic microenvironment observed in the lungs of IPF patients and the aspects of macrophages in both the positive CTL of the SKG model and the MTX-treated group.

These results illustrate the discursive effects of MTX in lung fibrosis across different cell types, further explaining the mechanism of transition from chronic inflammation to fibrosis and the potential effects of DMARDs in the progression of RA-ILD.

A trend of increased systemic inflammation in ILD progressors with MTX treatment in the RA-ILD cohort

Next, we investigated if lung fibrosis progression was associated with inflammation in patients treated with MTX using an RA-ILD cohort. The Korean Rheumatoid Arthritis associated Interstitial Lung disease cohort is a prospective observational cohort established to address the natural course of RA-ILD. Detailed information about this cohort is described in preceding research31,32,51 and methods section (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7). CT images from three representative cases with significant ILD progression are shown in Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. S6a and b. Among MTX users, the patients who experienced any radiologic progression of ILD (i.e., ILD progressor) tended to have persistently higher serum C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations and erythrocyte sedimentation rates (ESR) than those without progression (i.e., ILD non-progressors; Fig. 6b). Interestingly, ILD progressors among MTX users tended to have lower clinical activity of arthritis, as measured by the tender joint count (TJC) and swollen joint count (SJC), than non-progressors (Fig. 6c). This pattern was not observed in patients not using MTX (Supplementary Fig. S6c and d). Therefore, though MTX has a beneficial effect in suppressing joint inflammation in patients with RA-ILD, there may be no such effect on, or even a potential worsening of, systemic inflammation including ILD.

Discussion

Our findings illustrate inflammatory microenvironments in the lungs and the impact of MTX and TNF inhibitor in a mouse model of RA-ILD. The SKG mouse is an optimal model for investigating systemic inflammation involving the joints and lungs simultaneously.24,33 In the positive CTL group, we found an increase in immune cell infiltration in the lungs, including MHCII-high monocytes and their differentiation into monocyte-derived AMs, as well as activation of Th17 cells, which further suggests how ILD is first initiated in RA patients. In addition, we demonstrated an interplay among damaged AECs, monocyte-derived macrophages, and activated Th17 cells, which contribute to the generation of an inflammatory cascade in MTX-treated mice. In contrast, TNFi successfully suppressed these inflammatory cascades.

Alveolar macrophages are tissue-resident macrophages important for lung immune homeostasis.28,52 Monocyte-derived AMs, which are derived from recruited monocytes and more immunoreactive than tissue-resident AMs,53 had inflammatory (Il1b) and interferon-responsive features (Hif1a, Irf1) with upregulated profibrogenic genes, including Inhba, Mmp12, and Gpnmb. In addition, monocyte-derived AMs expressed Cxcl16, the ligand of which, CXCR6, is important in many autoimmune diseases. These activated macrophages contribute to lung inflammation by activating T cells, including Th17 cells. T cells are master regulators in lung host defense and mediators of pulmonary inflammation.54 Furin, a T cell-expressed proprotein convertase, has been known to play a dual role in both naïve and memory T cells.55 While maintaining peripheral tolerance in naïve T cells, Furin can enhance TCR stimulation, which leads to enhanced activity of transcription factors (AP-1, NFAT, and NF-κB) in activated or memory T cells.43 In the end, macrophages and T cells share the influence of the same inflammatory microenvironment. Particularly, the differentiation of macrophages affects the subtype of memory T cells, exacerbating the inflammatory cascade.

The crosstalk between inflammation and stem cells plays a central role in maintaining tissue integrity through regeneration following tissue damage.56 A recent study utilizing organoids and a mouse model elucidated that inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, induce a reduction in the stemness of AECs during the differentiation of AT2 cells to AT1.36 In our SKG mouse model, we used zymosan A as a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), which provoked systemic inflammation and primed the tissue regeneration. However, in the presence of additional exposure to MTX, impaired stemness prevents normal regeneration, sustaining a pro-inflammatory environment. This led to accelerated monocyte differentiation into tissue macrophages, exacerbating immune cell infiltration and tissue damage.

The precise mechanism by which MTX suppresses inflammation, aside from its action in inhibiting folate metabolism, has not been fully elucidated. Due to concerns about the potential direct induction of pneumonitis or exacerbation of inflammation by MTX, caution is advised in prescribing it for patients with confirmed ILD, especially in the context of RA.6,7,57 Unlike its anti-inflammatory role in T cells or fibroblasts, MTX can also induce apoptosis and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 or TNF, in monocytes.58 However, the majority of clinical studies have reported no significant correlation between MTX use and pneumonitis or acute exacerbation of ILD.59,60 Given that MTX-mediated lung injury is rare, initiation of lung inflammation and progression into ILD might be due to a mutual interplay between RA-mediated systemic inflammation and continued use of MTX rather than MTX toxicity itself. We administered MTX from the onset of active lung inflammation in the SKG mice, which may represent early pathological features that are challenging to observe in actual RA-ILD patients. In addition, the MTX dose and folate supplementation may not mirror the typical conditions for humans. Mechanisms underlying the impact of MTX on established ILD with repeated lung inflammation and fibrosis require another experimental study. Nevertheless, the results of this study indicate that MTX may accelerate lung damage, especially when systemic inflammation is present. Indeed, MTX, which is effective in controlling T cell-mediated joint inflammation, may exhibit altered effects in the lung. In vitro experiments with A549 cells have shown that alveolar epithelial cells exposed to MTX can interact differently with other cells in the lung inflammatory microenvironment. In light of the above, extra caution and careful monitoring may be warranted when administering MTX to patients with RAILD in the presence of active systemic as well as active pulmonary inflammation.

TNF inhibition significantly downregulated inflammation-related monocyte-derived AM differentiation and restored the tissue-resident AM population. Although the TNFi-treated group had an increased proportion of Th17 cells, this result did not progress into activation of pathological Th17 cells, unlike MTX treatment. Immune signature analysis revealed that MHCII-high monocytes and monocyte-derived AMs significantly correlated with the TNF response. In addition to immune cells, epithelial cells and fibroblasts in the TNFi-treated group consistently exhibited transcriptomic features associated with reduced inflammation. Our results suggest that TNF inhibition is safe to use in RA patients with early phase of ILD by controlling inflammation both in the joint and the lung and preventing transition to fibrosis. However, in clinical data, the effect of TNFi on the lung is still undetermined-there are several reports where TNFi exacerbated ILD while several reports including the recent meta-analysis suggested even stabilization of ILD in patients with RAILD.15,61,62 Notably, although further studies were not conducted due to emergence of more effective drug and paradigm shift from inflammatory disease to epithelial cell disease, the exploratory, placebo-controlled trial revealed the tolerability and safety of TNFi even among patients with IPF.63 Altogether, the findings of this study suggest that TNFi treatment, especially without MTX, may offer biological support for being beneficial to patients with RA-ILD by potentially not exacerbating lung damage within a microenvironmental context.

In conclusion, this explorative study contributes to our understanding of the early pathophysiology of ILD that emerges during exposure to various drugs in the context of RA treatment, including MTX and TNFi. Biologically, we proposed how impaired epithelial cell stemness and the interaction of immune cells might contribute to the exacerbation of pathological inflammation. The next steps involve longitudinal observation of an RA cohort over an extended period of time, collecting biospecimens and clinical information concurrently. This research approach is essential for gaining insights into the dynamic changes and factors influencing ILD progression in RA patients over time.
