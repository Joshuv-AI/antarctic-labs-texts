Site-specific PEGylation of proteins: Insights into structural and functional changes
Authors: Ming Ma, Jinwei Di, Chengcai Wang, Yanwei Xie, Fengqian Cui, Yanan Zhai, Shanwei Zhu, Jing Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is a polymer with different molecular weights formed by the polymerization of ethylene oxide monomers. Due to its ability to significantly reduce the immunogenicity of protein drugs and extend their half-life, as well as its high safety profile, it has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as the gold standard for delivering protein drugs. Although numerous PEGylated protein drugs have been marketed, the focus of research on PEGylated protein drugs has primarily been on reducing immunogenicity and extending in vivo circulation half-life. However, the understanding of how PEG modification affects the structure and function of proteins is still limited, with “activity” often being the sole criterion for evaluating changes in protein structure and function. A deeper exploration of the existence and interaction between PEG chains and proteins is of great significance for understanding the properties exhibited by PEGylated proteins. This review summarizes strategies for PEG site-specific modification of proteins, the interaction states between PEG and proteins, and the effects of PEG chains on protein spatial structure, solubility, activity, and thermal stability. It also summarizes the characterization techniques for PEG–protein interactions, aiming to predict or explain the functions of PEGylated protein drugs through the analysis of PEG–protein interactions.

Introduction

Biologics are a class of therapeutic or preventive drugs produced using biotechnology, characterized by high specificity, broad therapeutic range, and significant efficacy1, 2, 3. As an important component of the pharmaceutical field, biologics have witnessed rapid development and garnered widespread attention in recent years. Peptide and protein drugs are a crucial part of biologics4, 5, 6. These drugs provide a wealth of active factors and hormones for clinical treatment, such as colony-stimulating factor, growth hormone, and hepatitis B vaccine7, 8, 9. The synthesis of these drugs has greatly met the current clinical needs and has been widely used and applied in treatments, achieving good therapeutic effects. Based on the broad application prospects of these drugs, an increasing number of peptide and protein drugs are being developed10, 11, 12. To deliver protein drugs into the human body, two main issues must be addressed. First, the inherent immunogenicity of proteins can lead to strong immune responses when administered into the body13,14. Second, the short biological half-life of the protein limits its potential therapeutic application15, 16, 17. The immunogenicity of proteins can cause them to be recognized and cleared by phagocytic cells. Protein chains have multiple sites recognized by proteolytic enzymes18, making them susceptible to degradation by these enzymes during circulation, thereby losing their original activity and function19,20. The molecular weight of proteins is often less than the renal clearance threshold (Proteins with an approximate molecular weight of 60 kDa are the upper limit of renal clearance)21, leading to their direct excretion from the body during circulation, reducing their circulation time. These two issues of protein have driven the evolution and development of drug delivery technologies.

To address the inherent challenges of protein drug delivery, researchers have adopted strategies to “mask” the protein surface and increase the molecular weight of proteins to ensure that protein drugs can function safely in the body22,23. Zwitterionic polymers are repeating unit polymers containing oppositely charged groups. These oppositely charged groups are uniformly distributed across the polymer so that the molecule exhibits an overall neutral charge through ionic solvation, which has a strong hydration effect24. Some studies have employed zwitterionic materials for protein delivery24,25, which can significantly reduce the immunogenicity of proteins while extending their half-lives without significantly affecting protein activity26. On the other hand, PASylation is a novel approach to extend the plasma half-life of small molecular proteins27,28. PASylation technology is based on the genetic fusion of biopharmaceuticals with a hydrophilic random coil sequence of proline (Pro), alanine (Ala), and serine (Ser) amino acids29. For instance, PASylation of Certolizumab Fab’ has been shown to significantly improve its in vivo characteristics30. Additionally, fusion protein technology and other methods can also be employed for the delivery of proteins31,32. Among these delivery strategies, polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification is the most extensively studied and applied method for clinical translation33,34. PEG is a polymer with different molecular weights formed by the polymerization of ethylene oxide monomers and has been approved by the FDA as a pharmaceutical excipient due to its excellent safety profile35,36. PEG monomer units can form hydrogen bonds with water, leading to an overall increase in hydrophilicity and solubility, providing a “water shielding” effect that reduces the immunogenicity of protein drugs and extends their circulation half-life37,38. Although recent studies have focused on the immunogenicity of PEG itself39, 40, 41, this does not affect its status as one of the most promising strategies for the delivery of protein drugs. Since the first PEGylated protein drug, Adagen42, was marketed in 1990, over 20 PEGylated protein drugs have been approved by the FDA for clinical use, including human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, interferon, and urate oxidase43, 44, 45. These drugs not only achieve good therapeutic effects but also reduce the frequency of administration, greatly improving patient compliance46,47.

Reducing immunogenicity and extending circulation half-life are the ultimate outcomes of PEGylated protein drugs, reflecting the overall interaction between PEG and proteins. Understanding how PEGylation affects the structure and function of proteins is crucial for the properties exhibited by the modified products in vivo, yet this aspect is often overlooked once the desired effects of long circulation and low immunogenicity are achieved. In this article, we focus on site-specific PEGylation strategies, primarily discussing how PEG chain modification influences the structure and function of proteins, and summarize the characterization techniques used for these modifications.

Site-specific PEGylation strategies

PEGylation can be categorized into site-specific and non-site-specific approaches48. Initially, non-site-specific PEGylation was employed due to its ease of preparation. However, this method introduces complex effects on protein structure and function, particularly adverse impacts on the homogeneity and activity of the modified products49. Nevertheless, non-site-specific PEGylation remains one of the methods of choice for reducing the immunogenicity of highly immunogenic proteins due to its excellent ability to mask protein immunogenic epitopes. On the other hand, there has been a growing focus on exploring site-specific PEGylation strategies in recent years, as shown in Fig. 1, aiming to produce PEGylated protein formulations with well-defined structures and enhanced in vivo functionality50,51. Site-specific PEGylation represents the developmental direction for PEGylated drugs. By pre-designed selection of appropriate PEGylation sites, ideal PEGylated products can be obtained. This approach facilitates the maintenance of product homogeneity and enables a more straightforward analysis of the effects of PEGylation on protein activity. Site-specific modification can be achieved through covalent attachment to specific amino acid residues, such as lysine or cysteine, allowing for highly selective modification. This strategy not only improves the retention of biological activity but also enhances batch-to-batch reproducibility.Figure 1Schematic diagram of site-specific PEGylation strategies.Figure 1

N-terminal PEGylation

The first attempt to carry out N-terminal PEGylation of proteins was reported by Kinstler et al.52,53 in 1996, and a single N-terminal modified recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (Filgrastim, r-metHuG-CSF) was successfully prepared, and later PEG-G-CSF became the first FDA-approved N-terminal PEGylated protein drug43,54. Amine groups are often considered ideal sites for chemical modification. The protein surface is abundant in amine functional groups, including the side-chain amines of lysine and the N-terminal α-amines55. Lysine accounts for approximately 5.9% of the protein surface, while the N-terminal α-amine is unique within the protein sequence. Therefore, from the perspective of specificity, the N-terminal α-amine is more amenable to site-specific modification56,57. This can be achieved by adjusting the pH of the reaction solution and using PEG-aldehyde with low reactivity for modification, thereby minimizing the modification of lysine side-chain amines58,59. The pKa of the N-terminal α-amine, which is between 6 and 8, is lower than that of the side-chain amine, which is around 10.5. Under weak acidic conditions, although both the N-terminal and side-chain amines are protonated, the N-terminal amine is more likely to deprotonate to form a free amine due to its lower pKa, while the side-chain amine remains in a protonated form under the same pH conditions, and the protonated amine is less reactive with aldehyde groups. Therefore, by controlling the reaction pH, the N-terminal amino group can react with the aldehyde group to form Schiff bases. The Schiff base is reduced to an amine by sodium cyanoborohydride, thereby ensuring a stable linkage. However, although N-terminal PEGylation has good specificity, its modification efficiency is low, generally not higher than 50%.

Dong et al.60 successfully modified octreotide using PEG-aldehyde, demonstrating specific N-terminal modification. The modified product not only retained the original protein activity but also exhibited a circular dichroism (CD) spectrum that closely matched that of the native octreotide. Furthermore, PEGylation using a 5 kDa PEG chain showed more significant improvements in pharmacokinetics compared to a 2 kDa chain. Luo et al.61 performed site-specific N-terminal modification of β-lactoglobulin, examining the effects of different reaction conditions on PEGylation. They used cation exchange chromatography to separate and purify PEGylated products with different extents of modification, thus confirming the PEGylation site and characterizing the conformational changes pre- and post-modification. The study found that the optimal yield of mono-PEGylated product was achieved with a β-lactoglobulin: PEG ratio of 1:20. Cation exchange chromatography effectively separated mono-PEGylated, di-PEGylated, and unmodified proteins, confirming the successful attachment of PEG to the N-terminus. N-terminal PEGylation had no significant impact on the secondary structure of β-lactoglobulin, and the decrease in antigenicity was attributed to the shielding effect of the PEG chain.

Sun et al.62 conjugated the N-terminus of interferon (IFN) with pyridine-2,6-dicarboxaldehyde and then linked it to poly (oligo (ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate) (POEGMA). The resulting POEGMA–IFN conjugate exhibited an 8.1-fold increase in in vitro biological activity compared to the FDA-approved PEGylated interferon drug (Pegasys®), with similar half-lives (POEGMA-IFN 53.1 h versus Pegasys® 55.0 h). This method is suitable for proteins with active sites distant from the N-terminus, and it is characterized by its efficiency, mild reaction conditions (25 °C and near-neutral pH), high specificity, and the preservation of good activity in the modified product. Zhang et al.63 PEGylated the N-terminus of Interleukin-2 (IL-2), obtaining a mono-PEGylated product that extended the half-life of the L72-FSY mutant. PEGylation prevented rapid in vivo clearance of L72-FSY, increased the opportunity for covalent binding of IL-2 to IL-2Rα, and thereby enhanced the ability of IL-2 to preferentially activate treg cells in a stronger and more sustained manner. The PEGylated product also exhibited a superior treg-selective activation capability when compared to the unmodified form.

Cysteine PEGylation

Cysteine is present in most proteins, and its reactive thiol group makes it unique among all potential modification site64,65. The thiol group exhibits strong nucleophilicity and a low redox potential, allowing it to react with maleimide groups through a Michael addition reaction to form a thioether bond66. Cimzia is an example of an approved thiol-selective PEGylated biotherapeutic. This reaction is a cornerstone in bioconjugation chemistry, rendering maleimide-containing PEG chains an effective tool for site-specific protein modification67,68. However, maleimide can also react with protein amines under alkaline conditions, necessitating careful control of the pH of the reaction buffer. Moreover, cysteine is often located within the protein structure, resulting in low direct modification yields. To address this, small amounts of guanidine or urea can be added to induce mild protein denaturation, exposing cysteine residues for modification. Subsequent dialysis can then remove the denaturants and restore the native protein structure69. On the other hand, cysteine residues are mostly present in proteins in the form of disulfide bonds, thus limiting the modification of cysteine residues so mild disruption of the disulfide bonds can provide more sites for modification of cysteine residues, but disruption of the disulfide bonds may also affect the activity of the protein itself.

Qi et al.70 coupled six staphylokinase (SAK) molecules to an 8-arm PEG through maleimide-thiol specific reactions, creating 6SAK-PEG, and compared its in vitro activity and antigenicity with 2SAK-PEG and SAK-PEG. The results showed that 6SAK-PEG had lower thermal stability than 2SAK-PEG and SAK-PEG. However, 6SAK-PEG exhibited the strongest in vitro activity and induced the highest IgG antibody titers. The authors attributed these findings to the number of SAK proteins linked to PEG.

Chris E et al.71 introduced a new cysteine residue on the α-subunit surface of hemoglobin, distant from the active site, and used maleimide-PEG to modify the mutant. This approach achieved an 80% efficiency in producing a uniform PEGylated product without affecting the protein's function. Compared to mono-sulfone-PEG-modified mutants, which had higher PEGylation yields and enhanced resistance to glutathione reduction, maleimide-PEG-modified products exhibited minimal side effects. Although cysteine offers high specificity, its chemical linkage is less stable due to the retro-Michael reaction, which belongs to a class of elimination reactions in organic chemistry in which the Michael addition product decomposes under specific conditions, regenerating the original α, β-unsaturated carbonyl compound and nucleophilic reagent72. Some studies have demonstrated that maleimide-thiol modification products can undergo retro-Michael addition in vivo, leading to cross-linking with endogenous thiol-containing substances (e.g., albumin Cys34 and glutathione). This process may cause PEG chain detachment from the protein surface and consequently accelerate drug clearance and reduce therapeutic efficacy71,73. Huang et al.74 investigated the stability of the maleimide-thiol linkage and found that applying moderate tensile force through single-molecule force spectroscopy could stabilize the structure. Mechanical forces of appropriate magnitude can preferentially break the C–N bond, thereby stabilising the C–S bond, and thus hydrolysis of the succinimide adduct makes the Michael addition irreversible, thus maintaining the stability of the conjugate. In addition to this, the selection of cysteine reaction sites with different solvent accessibility and local charge (high solution accessibility and local positive charge) promotes ring-opening hydrolysis of succinimide adduct73, which can also be achieved by prolonged alkaline solution treatment of succinimide adduct (which may affect the protein activity)75, and ring-opening hydrolysis is now an effective means of counteracting the Retro-Michael reaction and endogenous thiol exchange. At the same time, more and more novel maleimide scaffolds are being developed76.

Willis et al.77 addressed thrombosis caused by antiphospholipid syndrome by modifying the N-terminal domain I (DI) of beta-2-glycoprotein I with cysteine. The resulting PEG-DI effectively inhibited thrombus formation in model mice, reduced tissue factor expression in the aorta, and showed no toxic effects, offering a potential new approach against APS. However, PEGylation did not significantly improve the half-life of DI, and further pharmacokinetic studies are needed.

Orthogonal click PEGylation

The 2022 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded for research on “click chemistry and bioorthogonal chemistry”, which has also brought new directions for the covalent connection of PEG with proteins78,79. Click chemistry primarily utilizes the chemical reaction between alkynes and azides to generate a stable five-membered ring80. This reaction has a high yield, strong specificity, and can occur in aqueous solutions, thus offering broad applicability. Bioorthogonal chemistry refers to reactions that occur within a biological system without interfering with the normal chemical reactions of the organism. The PEG modification site in orthogonal click chemistry is determined by the position of the unnatural amino acid (UAA) doped into the protein. The UAA generally corresponds to a termination codon, such as the amber codon TAG, to avoid competition with the natural amino acid. It is first necessary to design or select UAAs with click chemistry groups (azide groups, alkyne groups, etc.) Next, a genetic code expansion system is constructed to modify the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (aaRS) and the tRNA so that they can recognise a specific termination codon (e.g., TAG), and a plasmid of the modified aaRS and the tRNA is introduced into the host cell. Subsequently, in the DNA sequence of the target protein, the site to be inserted with UAA is replaced with a termination codon (e.g., TAG), and the cells are cultured in medium containing UAA, which is inserted into the target site by the modified aaRS/tRNA system, and the success of the successful doping of UAA can be examined by means of mass spectrometry and fluorescent probes. Specific modification of UAA-containing proteins can be achieved by using PEG with complementary click chemistry groups79.

Zhou et al.81 developed a convenient method for PEG site-specific modification based on this technology. Initially, non-natural amino acids containing azide groups are inserted using genetic code expansion techniques. Subsequently, a copper-free click reaction is employed to connect PEG-4-dibenzocyclooctyne with the azide-containing non-natural amino acids, forming a stable triazole, thereby achieving site-specific modification.

Wu et al.82 utilized a copper-free click reaction to perform controlled multi-PEGylation of human growth hormone (hGH) and compared its properties, including activity, pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and immunogenicity, with those of singly PEGylated hGH. The results indicated that site-specifically multi-PEGylated hGH, compared to singly PEGylated hGH, showed comparable in vitro activity but superior in vivo pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics, and immunogenicity. This is attributed to the better shielding effect on the hGH surface provided by multiple short PEG chains, as opposed to a single long PEG chain, under the condition of the same total PEG molecular weight. Moreover, the selected multiple modification sites are all distant from the protein's active center, thus not likely to affect the original protein activity.

Wang et al.83 developed an efficient and convenient “one-pot” method to address the issue of grafting two or more functional groups onto a protein (strain-promoted azide-alkyne cycloaddition). By installing noncanonical amino acids containing mutually orthogonal azide and tetrazine ‘‘click” handles, PEG, fluorescent groups, and other drugs can be simultaneously connected to the protein to exert their functions. This method not only expands the therapeutic scope of proteins but also provides new treatment approaches. Moreover, this controllable multi-PEGylation is promising for application to other proteins.

Despite the good site-specific modification effects of orthogonal click chemistry, there are still many issues that need to be addressed, including the expression efficiency of non-natural amino acids in proteins, the selection of modification sites, and the purification efficiency.

Enzyme-catalyzed PEGylation

Enzyme-catalyzed PEGylation is a commonly used site-selective method for modifying complex structural proteins. The principle behind this approach is based on many post-translational modifications that are employed in natural biological activities to control and regulate protein activity and interactions. The major enzymes involved include transglutaminases, sialyltransferases, and galactose oxidase. Glutamine transaminase (TGase) is present in both mammals and microorganisms, catalyzing the transfer of acyl residues to primary amines, cross-linking the γ-carboxamide group of glutamines with the ε-amino group of lysine on proteins. Therefore, under the mediation of TGase, PEG-NH2 can specifically couple with glutamine residues84,85. The most widely used TGase is derived from Streptomyces mobaraense (M-TGase) 86. It has been reported in the literature that, compared with mammalian-derived G-TGase, microbial M-TGase exhibits broader substrate catalytic capabilities. Moreover, M-TGase tends to catalyze the glutamine residues of the native protein without the need for additional fusion protein tags87. Generally, M-TGase has only one glutamine residue substrate, and M-TGase is more likely to react with glutamine residues in conformationally flexible or solvent-exposed regions88. However, when multiple reactive glutamine residues are present on a protein surface, site-specific modification mediated by M-TGase can be disrupted. Mero et al.89 investigated the modification of salmon calcitonin (sCT) and human growth hormone (hGH) to explore how to control reaction conditions to achieve site-specific modification when multiple glutamine residues are present. The results showed that adding a co-solvent (a mixture of organic and aqueous phases in different proportions) to the reaction mixture affected the secondary structure of the target protein and the activity of M-TGase. The organic solvent reduced the flexibility around some glutamine residues, thereby fine-tuning the specificity of M-TGase. This also demonstrated that M-TGase still has catalytic ability and better specificity even in the presence of organic solvents.

Based on the catalytic mechanism of TGase, it is possible to achieve specific modification of lysine amino groups on proteins if PEG is conjugated to a substrate structure similar to that of TGase. Mero et al.90 synthesized a specific acyl donor PEG by linking the substrate molecule dipeptide carbobenzoxy-l-glutaminyl-glycine (ZQG) of M-TGase to PEG. Under the mediation of M-TGase, lysine residues on G-CSF were modified, and the PEG–G-CSF conjugate was successfully prepared in high yield, with PEG selectively coupling to Lys41. Compared with the product PEG-Q135-G-CSF, in which PEG-NH2 was used to modify Gln135 under the mediation of M-TGase, the secondary and tertiary structures of the two conjugates are similar, and they exhibit comparable abilities to extend the half-life. Spolaore et al.91 reported a method for TGase-mediated, site-specific PEGylation of interferon α (IFNα). Under TGase catalysis, two PEG mono-modified products can be obtained. If the protein reacts in the presence of an amino-containing ligand, modification occurs at Gln101. If a glutamine-containing molecule is present, the reaction occurs at the Lys164 site. These sites are modified due to their location in highly flexible protein regions, and both PEG mono-modified products have high purification efficiency, yield, and activity.

Carbohydrates are ubiquitous in nature and are closely related to various life activities. They can regulate protein folding, secretion, recognition, and other physiological processes. Glycans can specifically bind to certain amino acids under the action of glycosyltransferases. Based on this characteristic, a modification technology has been developed using glycosyltransferases to connect PEG-sialic acid to glycoproteins92. Some proteins are directly glycosylated when expressed in mammalian cells, but not when expressed in vectors such as Escherichia coli. These non-glycosylated proteins have many potential natural glycosylation sites and typically have a short half-life, making them suitable for glycosylation PEG modification to extend their in vivo circulation half-life93.

Henrik et al.94 used the natural N-glycan of coagulation factor FIX as a modification site for PEGylation. The results showed that the PEGylated product retained between 73% and 100% of the specific activity in plasma and whole blood and showed comparable efficacy to the unmodified protein rFIX in acute bleeding experiments in mice with hemophilia B. In dogs with hemophilia B, the half-life of the PEGylated product was 113 h, compared to only 16 h for rFIX, significantly extending the duration of drug action in the body.

Existence states of PEG-modified proteins

After PEGylation, proteins can exhibit different existence states, with PEG chains either covering the protein surface or freely oscillating in solution. These distinct states significantly impact protein function. PEG chains covering the protein surface can effectively counteract immune system recognition and protease hydrolysis, while those freely oscillating in solution can effectively increase the hydrodynamic volume, reducing renal clearance. Unfavorable interactions can cause PEG chains to remain in the aqueous solution, away from the protein surface, forming a dumbbell-like conformation. On the other hand, due to strong attractive forces between PEG and the protein, if the entropic loss due to reduced PEG flexibility can be overcome, PEG can envelop the protein, forming a core–shell conformation95.

Fig. 295 illustrates the possible conformations of proteins modified with PEG of different molecular weights. Aravinda et al. used PEG of various molecular weights (2, 5, 10, and 20 kDa) to modify the N-terminus of bovine serum albumin (BSA) and investigated the interactions between PEG and BSA, as well as the existence states of PEG chains, through molecular dynamics studies. The results showed that when the PEG molecular weight was 2 kDa, the Rg value between the centers of the PEG chain and the protein was 53.6 Å, significantly greater than the sum of the Rg values of free PEG and free BSA (42.9 Å; PEG2kDa + BSA = 15.4 + 27.5 Å), indicating a dumbbell structure for PEG at this molecular weight. When the PEG molecular weight was 5 and 10 kDa, the Rg values of the modified products were 44.4 Å and 41.0 Å, respectively, greater than the Rg values of free PEG but less than the sum of the Rg values of free PEG and free BSA (49.1 Å for PEG 5 kDa + BSA = 21.6 + 27.5 Å and 56.1 Å for PEG 10 kDa + BSA = 28.6 + 27.5 Å), indicating partial coverage of the protein surface. When the PEG molecular weight was 20 kDa, the distance between the two was 39.2 Å, less than the sum of the Rg values of free PEG and free BSA (77.2 Å; PEG 20 kDa + BSA = 49.7 + 27.5 Å), indicating that most of the PEG chain covered the protein surface, presenting a wrapped conjugate conformation. Additionally, calculations of local PEG density on the BSA protein surface also showed that as the molecular weight of the PEG chain increased, the PEG coverage on the protein surface increased accordingly. Therefore, the existence state of PEG-modified proteins in solution is closely related to their own molecular weight. Similarly, another study demonstrated that the state of existence of PEG is closely related to its molecular weight. Coeur et al.96 employed small-angle neutron scattering to examine the effects of increasing PEG molecular weight on the activity of modified proteins and the efficiency of modification. Two PEG chains were specifically conjugated to two cysteine sites. The results indicated that the conformation of PEG is a function of its length. When the PEG molecular weight is below 10 kDa, no significant difference in conformation was observed between free PEG and grafted PEG, both of which exhibited a “dumbbell” shape. When the molecular weight exceeded 10 kDa, the radius of gyration of the grafted PEG was significantly smaller than that of free PEG, indicating that the grafted PEG chains tend to collapse onto the protein surface, potentially forming a “shroud” state. Impact of site-specific PEGylation on protein structure and function

The structure and function of proteins undergo significant changes upon conjugation with PEG. PEGylation is a technique that involves covalently attaching PEG molecules to proteins, with the core mechanism being the alteration of protein molecular weight and spatial conformation, thereby affecting their solubility, stability, and pharmacokinetic properties. Site-specific PEGylation, compared to random PEGylation, offers more controllable effects on the structure and properties of the product. This is because site-specific PEGylation can selectively attach PEG to specific amino acid residues, thereby avoiding multiple-site modification and the formation of isomers. This selective modification not only better preserves the biological activity of the protein but also endows it with new physicochemical properties. Impact on secondary structure

The secondary structure of a protein refers to the local folding patterns of the polypeptide chain in the protein molecule. Predicting and analyzing the secondary structure of PEGylated proteins helps understand the interactions between PEG and proteins, as well as the functional changes proteins undergo. Ernesto et al.99 reported the PEGylation of Collinein-1 and recombinant Collinein-1 (rCollinein-1) and assessed its effects on the secondary structures of these two proteins. Upon modification of cysteine residues with maleimide-methoxy PEG (mPEG-MAL), the maximum absorption wavelengths of both Collinein-1 and rCollinein-1 shifted. The secondary structure of rCollinein-1 changed more significantly compared to Collinein-1 with PEGylation, reflected in the fact that the alpha-helices increased from 2% to 15%, and beta sheets decreased from 31% to 22%. This indicates that PEGylation has different effects on the secondary structures of recombinant and native proteins.

During the construction of Gal3C[T243C]-PEG, Amanda et al.97 determined its melting temperature (Tm) using CD spectroscopy and elucidated the thermal unfolding pathway of the protein from the perspective of secondary structure. As shown in Fig. 597, PEGylated Gal3C[T243C] (Gal3C[T243C]-PEG) and Gal3C[T243C] exhibit two completely different thermal unfolding pathways. Gal3C[T243C]-PEG shows an intermediate transition state-like form as the temperature increases, and compared to the unmodified Gal3C[T243C], when the temperature is above Tm1, Gal3C[T243C]-PEG still retains a similar β-sheet content. Additionally, the authors mixed free PEG without reactive groups with Gal3C[T243C] for CD measurements, and the results showed that the temperature-dependent CD data were essentially consistent with those of single Gal3C[T243C]. Although the above studies have shown that PEGylation has an impact on the secondary structure of proteins, in most cases, PEGylation does not significantly alter the secondary structure of proteins100. Therefore, the effect of PEGylation on the secondary structure of proteins is not absolute, and the extent of the impact may depend on the location of the modification site, as well as the disruption of hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, and hydrophobic interactions in the local region of the original protein after modification.

Impact on tertiary structure

The tertiary structure of a protein is not only the foundation for its biological function but also a crucial key to understanding how proteins execute their complex tasks at the molecular level. Gaining insights into the tertiary structure of proteins is extremely important for drug design, disease treatment, and biological research. Qi et al.70 utilized the specific reaction between thiol groups and maleimide to conjugate multiple Staphylokinase (SAK) molecules with octa-arm PEG, aiming to enhance the bioactivity and half-life of SAK. Since the near-UV CD spectrum of SAK is generated by the environmental Phe, Trp, and Tyr residues, near-UV CD spectroscopy (250–320 nm) was used to determine changes in the tertiary structure of SAK. Compared to unmodified SAK, the octa-arm PEG-conjugated product showed higher CD spectra at 290 and 305 nm (Trp) and between 250 and 265 nm (Phe), indicating that the octa-arm PEG conjugation slightly affected the tertiary structure of SAK.

To further characterize the tertiary structure, intrinsic fluorescence analysis was employed. The intrinsic fluorescence of proteins, primarily originating from Trp and Tyr residues, provided valuable insights. The results showed that the maximum absorption wavelength remained at 362 nm both before and after PEGylation. However, the octa-arm PEG-conjugated SAK exhibited a slightly stronger fluorescence intensity compared to the unmodified SAK. This indicates that the octa-arm PEG conjugation may have slightly perturbed the local environment of Trp and Tyr residues within SAK.

The modification of lysine amino groups on traditional protein surfaces has been considered “random” for a long time. However, different lysine sites exhibit varying reactivity with PEG, and it is possible to prepare “site-specific” PEGylated products by controlling reaction conditions. Leran et al.101 proposed a reaction model that depends on the tertiary structure of proteins to simulate the rate of PEGylation of amine groups on the protein surface. Then also introduced the concept of “PEG chain coverage” as a visible indicator to assess the shielding effect of PEG chains. By employing linear, structure-based, and machine-learned models, they achieved a comprehensive understanding of how exposed surface areas, pKa, and surface charges influence each amine reactive site. The simulation validation results for Somavert®, Pegasys®, and phenylalanine ammonia lyase showed a good correlation between the predicted degree of PEGylation and the experimentally determined degree of modification. This model can optimize the yield and specificity of PEGylated products, enabling the preparation of target PEGylated products in a highly efficient and convenient manner.

The purpose of PEGylating proteins is generally to improve the immunogenicity and half-life of the proteins while retaining their original functional activities. Since the tertiary structure of a protein is closely related to its function, similar to the secondary structure, the tertiary structure of PEGylated proteins usually does not undergo significant changes.

Impact on active structural domains

PEGylation can affect the overall structure of proteins, and it may also alter the active domains of proteins during this process, thereby affecting the binding and dissociation of proteins with their interacting molecules. On the other hand, PEGylation induces a physical shielding effect on the protein surface, which can interfere with the binding of interacting molecules to the active domains.

Pegilodecakin is a recombinant IL-10 protein modified at the N-terminus with PEG-aldehyde of molecular weights 12 or 20 kDa. Compared to the unmodified protein, it exhibits higher inhibitory activity against all serum inflammatory cytokines after 20 h, significantly upregulating TNFα, IL-12p40, and IL-6102. Batugal et al.103 investigated the impact of PEGylation at different sites on the activity of lysostaphin by inserting non-natural amino acids at various positions and conducting orthogonal click chemistry for site-specific PEG modification. The lysostaphin variants N319F∗ and G273F∗ were obtained using copper-free click cycloaddition. The N319F∗ mutation is located far from the active domain, whereas the G273F∗ mutation is situated within the active residues. The activity of the modified products was evaluated by turbidity reduction and minimum inhibitory concentration. The results indicated that the activities of both PEGylated mutants were diminished relative to the native protein, with the activity loss of G273F∗-PEG1k being more significant than that of N319F∗-PEG1k. This could be attributed to the fact that residue 273 is located in a loop near the active site, where PEGylation may impose a shielding effect on the active site.

To investigate the interfacial interactions between prothrombin and pseutarin C, Üstok et al.104 mutated the residues on the A1, A2, and A3 domains of factor V from pseutarin C to Cys, and modified them with PEG-maleimide. They examined the effects of different PEG modification sites on the initial docking mode of prothrombin. The PEGylation of residues within the expected domains significantly diminished the rate of thrombin generation. In contrast, PEGylation of residues outside these domains had no impact. At the same time, PEGylation on the A1-loop also reduced the rate of thrombin generation, indicating that the amino acid residues on the A1-loop were indispensable for thrombin formation.

Impact on solubility

PEG is inherently highly polar and exhibits excellent solubility in both water and organic solvents, making it a common choice as a solubility enhancer for improving the solubility of hydrophobic drugs105. During the PEGylation process of proteins, the concentrations of buffer salts, PEG, and protein are crucial for maintaining the stability of the reaction system. Salts and PEG often act as protein precipitants. In the solution, they absorb water molecules around them, reducing the solvation of proteins by water molecules and thereby causing precipitation.

To investigate the effects of PEG molecular weight, buffer salt concentration, PEG concentration, and protein concentration on the stability of the solution system during PEGylation, Ahlem et al.106 used hen egg-white lysozyme (HEWL) as the study protein and examined the solution states under 90 different conditions (Fig. 6)106. The authors categorized the solution states into five classes: homogeneous, nanoparticles, microparticles, soluble aggregates, and precipitation. The results showed that when both mPEG-OH and protein concentrations were low, the solution remained in a homogeneous state. Most samples after reaction exhibited a nanoparticle state (140–250 nm), with no significant correlation to phosphate salt and mPEG-OH concentrations. Under conditions of high protein, salt, and mPEG-OH concentrations, microparticles and soluble aggregates were mainly observed, the latter primarily occurring when the concentration of mPEG-OH (>5 kDa) exceeded 10%. Among the four factors, PEG molecular weight had the most significant impact on the solution state. Impact on thermal stability

The thermal stability of a protein reflects its ability to retain its tertiary structure under high-temperature conditions. This can be evaluated through techniques such as thermogravimetry, determination of thermal denaturation temperatures, and assessment of protein aggregation and precipitation107. Grigoletto et al.108 employed PEG chains with different reactive functional groups at both ends to simultaneously perform enzymatic and N-terminal modification of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), resulting in the preparation of a circular conjugate cPEG-Nter/K41-G-CSF with dual-site PEGylation, as well as the enzymatic mono-PEGylated product PEG-K41-G-CSF and the N-terminal mono-modified product PEG-Nter-G-CSF. Compared with G-CSF, the Tm of cPEG-Nter/K41-G-CSF and PEG-Nter-G-CSF increased by 1.3 and 1.9 °C, respectively, whereas that of PEG-K41-G-CSF decreased slightly by 0.6 °C. The reversibility of thermal unfolding was investigated after the samples were heated at 90 °C and then cooled to 25 °C. The peaks at 208 and 222 nm of G-CSF disappeared completely after heating and cooling, indicating the loss of α-helical structure and protein denaturation. In contrast, all PEGylated products retained a structure similar to that of the native protein. This may be attributed to the reversibility of partial thermal denaturation in PEGylated products.

Liu et al.109 modified alpha1-antitrypsin (ATT) using two modification sites, the thiol group and the N-terminal amine, to investigate the impact of PEGylation and different modification sites on the thermal stability of the native protein. The apparent mid-transition temperatures (at 324 nm) of ATT and PEG-ATT were close (57.6 ± 0.3 °C). Both ATT and PEG-ATT lost activity after heat treatment. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and size exclusion chromatography (SEC) analyses indicated that the thermal denaturation of both was related to sample aggregation, which occurred after thermal denaturation, likely triggered by the denatured samples. Moreover, PEGylation products of ATT at different sites exhibited varying thermal stabilities. SEC results showed that after heating at 70 °C for 1 h, the proportion of aggregates in the ATT protein was 52.7%, in the N-terminal amine 2-arm 40 kDa PEG-modified product was 25.7%, and in the thiol 2-arm 40 kDa PEG-modified product was only 13.5%. This suggests that PEGylation of ATT can resist protein aggregation, and the thiol PEG-modified product has a stronger anti-aggregation ability than the N-terminal amine PEG-modified product.

Impact on immunogenicity

Hydrophilic PEGylation on the protein surface can form a hydration layer and a dynamic physical barrier, resisting protein adsorption while protecting immunogenic sites on the protein from recognition by the human immune system, thereby endowing the modified product with a “stealth” characteristic110,111. Considering that low-molecular-weight PEG is more effective than high-molecular-weight PEG in reducing the immunogenicity of modified proteins, Tong et al.107 conjugated urokinase with 5 kDa mPEG-SPA. Through optimization of the modification conditions and purification by molecular sieving, a mono-PEGylated uricase was prepared. When unmodified uricase was used as the coating antigen, no positive antibodies were detected in the PEG-uricase group, indicating that PEGylation can completely mask the intrinsic immunogenicity of uricase. Meanwhile, when PEG-uricase was used as the coating antigen, positive antibodies were detected in the PEG-uricase group. This suggests that the positive IgG and IgM antibody responses were generated by the mPEG fragment, rather than by uricase itself.

In recent years, the increasing prevalence of PEGylated drugs has led to the detection of anti-PEG antibodies in both experimental animals and humans112,113. The production of these antibodies is likely associated with prior exposure to PEGylated drugs or PEG additives, such as those found in cosmetics and over-the-counter medications. The binding of anti-PEG antibodies to PEG chains can further trigger complement activation, thereby accelerating the clearance of PEGylated drugs38,114,115. Li et al.116 evaluated the immunogenicity and toxicokinetics of N-terminally mono-PEGylated recombinant human interleukin-11 (rhIL11) in cynomolgus monkeys. The antibody determination results revealed that when monkeys were administered a low dose of PEG-IL11, some developed low-titer anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) that lacked neutralizing activity. These antibodies became undetectable after the recovery period, indicating a weak immunogenic response. Conversely, when the dose of PEG-IL11 was increased, the positivity rate and titer of ADAs were elevated, though they diminished during the recovery period, suggesting a moderate level of immunogenicity. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that despite repeated injections of PEG-IL11 inducing ADAs, there was no significant impact on body weight, clinical signs, or the function of major organs.

Neulasta® is a linear 20 kDa PEGylated product with site-specific modification at the N-terminus of human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (PEG-G-CSF), which was approved for clinical use in 2002 for the treatment of chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. To further investigate its safety profile, E. Elsadek et al.117 examined whether PEG-G-CSF could induce anti-PEG antibodies in mice. They first discovered that repeated administration of PEG-G-CSF in mice led to accelerated clearance, which may also compromise the clinical efficacy of Neulasta® when administered consecutively in humans. The anti-PEG IgM antibodies induced by PEG-OVA could affect the pharmacokinetics of multiple doses of PEG-G-CSF through complement activation, indicating that the clinical efficacy of PEG-G-CSF would be significantly reduced if patients had pre-existing anti-PEG antibodies from other PEGylated products during clinical use. Additionally, the authors compared the anti-PEG IgM responses elicited by intravenous and subcutaneous administration of PEG-G-CSF. The results showed that intravenous injection induced a stronger anti-PEG IgM response. Therefore, considering both compliance and safety, subcutaneous injection is a more suitable route of administration for PEG-G-CSF.

Impact on biological half-life

PEGylation on the protein surface can increase protein solubility and hydrodynamic volume, thereby reducing glomerular filtration in the body and extending the residence time of PEGylated protein drugs in vivo118,119. Additionally, PEG chains can physically shield antibodies and proteolytic enzymes, reducing the immunogenicity and clearance rate of proteins in the body120. These factors collectively contribute to the long-circulating effect of PEGylated proteins in the body121.

Diaz-San et al.122 conducted pharmacokinetic studies in pigs to investigate the blood half-life of PEGylated drugs using PEGylated porcine IFNα through intravenous (iv) or intramuscular (im) injection. The authors administered 25 μg/kg (iv), 25 μg/kg (im), and 50 μg/kg (im) PEGylated recombinant porcine IFNα (PEGpoIFNα) to three groups of animals and monitored blood drug concentrations up to 240 h post-administration. The iv results showed that the blood drug concentration reached a peak at 30 min, with an average half-life of 59.7 h. The time to peak concentration for im administration was significantly longer than for iv administration, and even with an im dose twice that of iv, the blood drug concentration did not reach the same level as iv. Despite this, the half-lives for both administration methods were similar, and the drug concentrations were comparable 72 h post-administration.

PEGylation not only extends the half-life of protein drugs in circulation but also has a long-circulating effect in the lungs. Mahri et al.123 focused on recombinant human DNase I (rhDNase, Pulmozyme®) as the subject of their study. They extended the half-life of rhDNase by PEGylating its N-terminus, aiming to achieve a long-term effect of reducing mucus concentration. This is the only inhaled protein drug approved for local pulmonary administration. Compared to rhDNase, the transport of PEG-rhDNase across the Calu-3 monolayer to the basolateral side was lower, possibly because PEGylation hindered the protein's cellular vesicular uptake or trans-tight junction absorption. The activity of rhDNase and PEG-rhDNase was retained on the apical side but was mostly lost on the basolateral side, indicating that both proteins underwent degradation during transport. Moreover, macrophage uptake of PEG-rhDNase was less than that of rhDNase, and the diffusion of PEG-rhDNase in tracheal mucus and sputum was slower. These combined factors led to a significantly extended half-life of PEG-rhDNase in the lungs.

Factors affecting the structure and function of proteins during PEGylation

The structure and properties of PEGylated proteins are closely related to the decisions made during the modification process, such as different PEGylation sites and molecular weights of PEG. These factors can lead to distinct interactions between PEG and proteins, thereby affecting the biological activity and spatial conformation of the proteins. Therefore, understanding the factors that influence the structure and function of PEGylated proteins is highly instructive for obtaining modified products with the desired functionality. Molecular weight and terminal groups of PEG

During protein folding, hydrophobic groups are typically sequestered within the protein's interior, while hydrophilic groups are often exposed on the exterior to enhance protein solubility in water. PEG chains themselves are hydrophilic, but the hydrophobicity of PEG terminal groups can vary. The hydrophobic interactions between PEG and proteins are crucial for the spatial conformation of the final product after modification. Bekale et al.124 selected human serum albumin (HSA) and BSA, which differ in hydrophobicity, to study the effects of different PEG molecular weights and PEG-anthracene on the interaction between PEG and proteins. By measuring the binding constants of PEG–protein complexes, it was found that for BSA, the affinity order was PEG-6000 > PEG-anthracene > PEG-3000, and for HSA, the affinity order was PEG-6000 > PEG-3000 > PEG-anthracene. This indicates that high molecular weight PEG has a stronger adsorption capacity for both proteins. The binding ability of low molecular weight PEG to proteins is related to their hydrophobicity. PEG-anthracene (with two hydrophobic terminal groups) tends to bind more readily with the more hydrophobic BSA, while PEG-3000 tends to bind more readily with the more hydrophilic HSA. This suggests that hydrophobic interactions are dominant in the former case, while hydrogen bonding is more important in the latter.

The terminal groups of PEG are the parts that directly connect to proteins, and the differences in terminal groups may affect the interactions between the PEG chain and the protein. Xue et al125. employed phenyl, propyl, and amyl moieties as linkers to perform PEGylation at the C-terminus of SAK. The results indicated that, compared with the flexible propyl and amyl groups, the rigid and hydrophobic phenyl linker induces a more compact PEG conformation, which can extensively shield most of the protein structural domains near the C-terminus of SAK, including antigenic epitopes and hydrolytic sites, without significantly affecting the active domains of SAK itself. This dense PEG conformation is also more conducive to maintaining the bioactivity of PEGylated SAK, increasing its plasma half-life, and reducing the proteolytic sensitivity and immunogenicity of PEGylated SAK.

Structure and polydispersity of PEG

PEG is a high molecular weight polymer, which inherently has a certain degree of dispersity. This characteristic leads to differences between batches of PEGylated products and presents challenges in their purification and characterization. To address the issues of polydispersity and non-biodegradability of PEG chains, Wang et al.126 incorporated polyamide as a component in the polymerization of PEG chains, constructing monodisperse and biodegradable PEG modifiers. Compared to traditional PEG chains, these novel PEG modification chains feature embedded amide bonds that can form better hydrogen bonds with the modified proteins, providing a degradable “stealth cloak” for the proteins.

Dynamic light scattering results indicated that although the molecular weight of the polyamide PEG chain used was 1.14 times that of conventional PEG, the final particle size of albumin modified with polyamide PEG chain was only 1.02 or 1.06 times that of albumin modified with conventional PEG. This suggests that proteins modified with polyamide PEG chains may adopt a more compact conformation, which results from the hydrogen bonds formed between the amide groups and the protein.

Burggraef et al.127 synthesized a defined molecular weight, linear 5 kDa mPEG active ester using an efficient and scalable method based on an iterative liquid-phase approach. They then performed PEGylation of BSA using both the defined molecular weight mPEG and a commercially available polydisperse mPEG. The PEGylated products were characterized using various techniques. The results indicated that the PEGylated products using the defined molecular weight PEG were easier to precisely separate and determine the molecular weight. Moreover, since the PEG molecular weight was known, the modification sites of PEG could be inferred by calculating the molecular weights of the trypsin-digested fragments, thereby clarifying the regions of interaction between PEG and the protein. This application of defined molecular weight PEG is beneficial for enhancing the quality of PEGylated protein drugs.

PEGylation sites

The PEGylation site plays a crucial role in the properties of the modified product. For instance, different modification sites have varying effects on the shielding of protein antigenic epitopes and enzymatic cleavage sites. This, to some extent, determines the differences in immune responses and stability exhibited by PEGylated proteins after entering the body. Moreover, the relative distance between the modification site and the active site is closely related to the activity displayed by the modified product128.

Cooper et al.129 introduced new thiol-reactive sites away from the heme group and α/β aggregates to preserve the normal function of hemoglobin. Two mutants βCys93Ala/αAla19Cys and βCys93Ala/βAla13Cys were created. Analyses using gel electrophoresis, size-exclusion chromatography, and mass spectrometry revealed that both mutants yielded highly PEGylated products. The PEGylated products of both mutants exhibited oxygen affinity and cooperativity of oxygen binding comparable to those before modification. However, the PEGylation efficiency was higher for αAla19Cys, and the modified product reduced auto-oxidation and heme release rates, demonstrating potential for future clinical applications.

Wu et al.82 employed copper-free click chemistry to break the traditional single-site PEGylation mindset and investigated PEGylation at multiple specific sites. SDS-PAGE results showed that even when using PEG of the same molecular weight, modification at different sites led to differences in apparent molecular weight. This discrepancy may stem from the distinct interactions between PEG and proteins at various modification sites. Additionally, the authors found that when the PEG molecular weight was below 20 kDa, the clearance rate of di-PEGylated hGH was 2 to 3 times slower than that of the mono-PEGylated product with the same total size, with comparable half-lives. Notably, the half-life and mean residence time of hGH with two 20 kDa PEG chains were 2 times longer than those of hGH modified with a single 40 kDa PEG chain. This indicates that under the same molecular weight conditions, di-PEGylated isoforms generally outperform mono-PEGylated isoforms, especially when high molecular weight PEG is used. The enhanced performance may be attributed to the broader protective coverage provided by multi-site PEGylation, which more effectively shields the protein from enzymatic degradation in the body.

Techniques for characterizing the structural and functional changes of proteins after PEGylation

Rational characterization methods are crucial for exploring the effects of PEGylation on protein structure and function. In-depth analysis of the structure and interactions of PEGylated proteins can provide rational explanations for the properties exhibited by the modified products, thereby offering theoretical foundations for the design and optimization of PEGylated products. Commonly used characterization methods encompass a variety of advanced techniques, which can analyze the changes in protein structure and properties after PEGylation from different perspectives. The integrated application of multiple techniques helps to comprehensively understand the impact of PEGylation on proteins. Circular dichroism

CD is a spectroscopic technique used to infer the configuration and conformation of chiral molecules. It can be measured in solution, which closely mimics the physiological state of proteins, making it one of the most commonly used technologies for protein structure characterization130,131. Santos et al.132 performed PEGylation on the N-terminus of catalase and evaluated the impact of PEGylation on the protein's spatial structure using far-UV and near-UV CD spectroscopy. The results indicated that the PEGylation and purification processes did not affect the secondary and tertiary folding of catalase. The CD spectra of PEGylated catalase were identical in shape to those of the native enzyme, exhibiting two negative peaks at 222 and 208 nm, and a positive peak at 198 nm, which are characteristic of the typical α-helical structure.

Similarly, they conducted CD spectroscopy analysis on PEGylated Cytochrome c (Cyt-c). The results showed that Cyt-c maintained the characteristics of α-helices after PEGylation, with no significant spectral changes observed133. Li et al.134 conducted CD studies on Phycocyanin and found that it exhibited typical α-helical structures with maximum negative absorption peaks at 208 and 222 nm. After PEGylation, the negative peak value at 222 nm shifted, indicating the formation of protein secondary structural elements, which may result from the conjugation of PEG promoting protein secondary structure folding. Additionally, the ratio of the peak values at 222 and 208 nm decreased after PEGylation, suggesting a slight dissociation of the protein's tertiary structure, with the α-helix transforming from a coiled helix to a single-stranded helix.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy refers to the spectrum formed by the fluorescence emitted from the inherent fluorescent substances within a biological organism when excited by light of specific wavelengths. Its advantage lies in the ability to generate fluorescence through excitation light without the need for the addition of external dyes and fluorescent probes. The endogenous fluorescence is primarily produced by tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine. When the protein structure changes, the positions of the amino acids also change, leading to alterations in endogenous fluorescence.

Nikravesh et al.135 conducted intrinsic fluorescence measurements on G-CSF, PEG-G-CSF, and Albumin binding domain (ABD)-modified G-CSF. The results showed that after excitation and emission of the tryptophan residues (Trp59 and Trp119) in the G-CSF protein at 295 nm and 300 to 450 nm, all three exhibited similar maximum peaks in their fluorescence spectra at 350 nm. This confirmed that the structure of the G-CSF molecule remained unchanged after PEGylation and partial fusion with the ABD.

Luo et al.136 employed intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy to investigate the effects of PEGylation on the tertiary structure of β-lactoglobulin (β-LG). The results indicated that PEGylation and 25% ethanol had a slight impact on the tertiary structure of β-LG. Under excitation at 280 nm, the emission fluorescence intensities of PEGylated β-LG and β-LG treated with 25% ethanol decreased from 6361 (control) to 5159 and 5665, respectively, while the maximum emission wavelength remained unchanged. The intrinsic fluorescence of β-LG is primarily excited by Trp residues. The authors suggested that the decrease in fluorescence intensity was attributed to two main factors. On one hand, the addition of 25% ethanol during the PEGylation process led to a loosening of the β-LG structure, exposing the Trp residues. The poor hydrophobicity of the external solvent resulted in a decrease in fluorescence intensity. On the other hand, the spatial shielding effect produced by the PEG chains also had a significant influence on the changes in fluorescence intensity.

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a powerful analytical technique based on the resonance phenomenon of atomic nuclei in an external magnetic field, widely used for protein structure analysis137,138. NMR can provide both static structural information of proteins, such as determining the positions of atoms in the main chain and side chains, and dynamic information, such as changes in chemical shifts and relaxation times of protein atoms after PEGylation. These changes can reflect the impact of PEGylation on protein structure.

Ravera et al.139 addressed the challenge of characterizing PEGylated proteins using conventional NMR hydrogen spectroscopy. They transformed PEGylated proteins into a pelleted state (obtained by freeze-drying and subsequent rehydration), which allowed the use of Solid-State NMR to evaluate the impact of PEGylation on the native conformation of protein structures at the atomic level. Solid-State NMR analyses of PEGylated products and unmodified proteins of human CuII-ZnII-Superoxide Dismutase (SOD), human H Ferritin (hHF), and E. coli Lasparaginase II (ANSII) showed that the spectra largely overlapped. This was the first demonstration that PEGylation had minimal impact on the structure and assembly of these three proteins, with some chemical shift perturbations occurring primarily in surface residues, particularly near lysine residues. The fine spectra and the inherent high packing factor of protein particles also made three-dimensional spectra for assignment possible. This convenient method holds strong appeal for the design and monitoring of PEGylated protein drugs in the industry.

Hodgson et al.140 used NMR spectroscopy to evaluate the higher-order structure of three PEGylated protein therapeutics, Neulasta®, Pegasys®, and PEG-Intron®. These PEGylated products have molecular weights ranging from 32 to 60 kDa. Data collection was performed within the temperature range of 25 to 50 °C to enhance the tumbling of the PEGylated proteins, with the optimal temperature being 40 °C. At higher temperatures (45 to 50 °C), the resonances showed signs of line broadening, leading to a degradation in spectral quality, which was associated with the PEGylation. The NMR spectra were found to be highly overlapping before and after PEGylation, indicating that PEGylation did not alter the higher-order structure of these proteins.

Pritzlaff et al.97 discovered that PEGylation did not alter the overall folding of Gal3C. However, through heteronuclear single quantum coherence spectra, they observed changes in the chemical shifts or line widths of approximately 12 amide groups. These changes were primarily located near the PEGylation sites. This perturbation indicates that PEGylation on Gal3C does not result in a random helical structure but rather attaches to the protein surface within a specific region. Additionally, to investigate the duration of non-covalent interactions between Gal3C[T243C] and PEG, the authors analyzed hydrogen–deuterium exchange (HDX) NMR data of Gal3C[T243C]-PEG at 30 °C. Upon addition of 2H2O, a significant reduction in amide signals was observed in the Gal3C[T243C]-PEG sample, indicating that most amide groups were fully solvent-accessible. Moreover, the solvent-accessible regions of Gal3C[T243C]-PEG overlapped extensively with amino acid regions perturbed by PEGylation. This observation suggests that the transient nature of non-covalent interactions between Gal3C[T243C] and PEG fails to prevent bulk solvent molecules from accessing the protein surface. The interactions between the protein and PEG chains appear to be transient rather than sustained, which may further elucidate the formation of a hydration shell around PEGylated protein.

Differential scanning calorimetry

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) is a valuable technique for investigating the thermal behavior of proteins, particularly the heat absorbed or released during structural changes. DSC can precisely determine the onset temperature of protein denaturation, known as the melting point of the protein141,142. When PEG is conjugated to proteins, the DSC curves can be compared to evaluate how PEG affects the thermal stability of the protein. For instance, studies have shown that PEGylation can either enhance or reduce the thermal stability of proteins, depending on factors such as the PEGylation site and the protein's inherent properties143,144.

Plesner et al.145 utilized DSC to analyze the effects of six linear PEGs on the modification of BSA. The results indicated that PEGylation altered the apparent unfolding temperature (Tmax) of the protein. The Tmax of native BSA was 82.5 ± 0.7 °C, and the Tmax of all PEGylated BSA was approximately 2 °C lower than that before modification. This decrease in Tmax was independent of the molecular weight of the PEG used. In addition, they modified recombinant human factor VIIa with linear 10 kDa and branched 40 kDa PEG. DSC measurements indicated that glycosylation delayed heat-induced aggregation and slightly increased the protein's unfolding temperature (from 57.7 ± 0.2 °C to 59.0 ± 0.2 °C). Moreover, this change in thermal stability was also proven to be independent of the molecular weight and size of PEG146. DSC can also be used for the comparison of originator and generic biopharmaceuticals. For instance, by employing DSC to analyze the thermal stability of the generic Pegfilgrastim-cbqv/CHS-1701 (Udenyca®) and the originator pegfilgrastim (Neulasta®), it was found that the thermal unfolding curves of the two were highly similar, with only minor differences in PEG molecular weight and PEGylation variants147.

Small-angle scattering

Small-angle scattering is an experimental technique used to characterize the multi-scale structural features of materials from the nanometer to the micrometer level, typically including small-angle neutron scattering, small-angle light scattering, and small-angle X-ray diffraction. Small-angle scattering offers several advantages in structural studies, such as easy sample preparation, non-destructive nature, and no limitations on molecular weight and concentration148,149.

Baranova et al.150 investigated the effects of PEG molecular weight and concentration on the spatial structure of human hemoglobin (Hb) under neutral pH conditions. They used small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) combined with size exclusion chromatography to characterize the influence of PEG present in the solution on the Hb structure. The results showed that Hb exists in a tetrameric state under neutral pH conditions, and the protein model constructed by SAXS closely resembles the known crystal structure of metHb in the protein database. PEG-600 can act as a stabilizer for Hb without significantly interfering with its spatial structure at concentrations up to 10% (w/v). When 5% (w/v) of PEG-2000 and PEG-4000 were used, there was a slight decrease in the maximum particle dimension and a minor reduction in the radius of gyration (Rg), indicating that the dehydration effect of higher molecular weight PEGs can disrupt the hydration shell on the protein surface. Although this study effectively simulated the effects of PEG molecular weight and concentration on protein structure, the PEG in the aforementioned study was not covalently linked to the protein surface, making it difficult to analyze the local conformational effects of PEG on the protein.

He et al.151 aimed to investigate the conformational issues of proteins after PEGylation by combining small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) with SAXS. They used human galectin-2 (hGal-2) and 5 kDa PEG as the research subjects to explore the complementary structural information after their conjugation. The SAXS signal was generated by the protein, while the SANS signal originated from both the protein and PEG. The SAXS results indicated that the radius of gyration (Rg = 21.5 Å) of the modified product was independent of concentration, and the dimeric structure and ellipsoidal shape of the protein remained unchanged after PEGylation. In contrast, the SANS results showed that the Rg value of the modified product was concentration-dependent. When the concentration of the modified product increased from 2 to 14 mg/mL, its Rg decreased from 31.5 to 26 Å. Therefore, the authors concluded that the conformation of PEG after attachment to the protein surface is closely related to the concentration of the modified product. In addition, SANS can be combined with DLS and rheological methods to investigate the influence of the geometric shape of PEG on the conjugated configuration of the modified products152.

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-ToF MS) is a soft ionization mass spectrometry technique primarily used for the analysis of large molecules such as proteins, peptides, nucleic acids, and polymers. The core mechanism involves laser excitation of matrix molecules, which gently ionizes the sample molecules. Subsequently, the ions are accelerated in a high-voltage electric field, travel through a field-free drift tube, and reach the detector. The mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) is calculated based on the differences in ion flight times and is then converted into a mass spectrum. Due to its ability to efficiently and gently analyze complex biomacromolecules, MALDI-ToF MS has become an indispensable tool in the fields of life sciences and materials science.

MALDI-ToF MS has unique application value and significant advantages in the characterization of PEGylated proteins, such as confirming whether PEG has been successfully conjugated to the protein surface and identifying whether the product is a mono-PEGylated species97. For example, the molecular weight of the exendin-4 analog (Ex4C) protein is 4289.4785 Da. After modification with PEG20kDa-MAL (molecular weight 21,655.0378 Da), MALDI-ToF MS indicates that the molecular weight of PEG20kDa-Ex4C is 25,944.0313 Da, demonstrating that exactly one PEG molecule has been successfully attached to the Ex4C protein surface153. Due to the inherent polydispersity of PEG, which means that the molecular weight of PEG is distributed over a range, the purification and characterization of PEGylated proteins become more challenging. This is manifested in MALDI-ToF MS as a broad mass range for PEGylated proteins. Burggraef et al127. synthesized a monodisperse (non-polydisperse) 5 kDa methoxyPEG-OH (mPEG-OH) and activated it. Subsequently, they used the activated PEG and commercial PEG to modify bovine serum albumin (BSA), respectively. The results showed that compared to the polydisperse BSA products modified with commercial PEG, MALDI-ToF MS indicated that the BSA (66.5 kDa) modified with the synthesized PEG (5 kDa) exhibited a single peak at 71.6 kDa, proving that it was mono-PEGylated. In addition, MALDI-ToF MS also has the advantages of low sample consumption and a broad detection range, which generally covers large-molecular-weight PEGylated proteins.

Liquid chromatography peptide mapping

Liquid chromatography peptide mapping is a protein analysis technique based on the combination of liquid chromatography (LC) and mass spectrometry (MS). Its core concept involves enzymatic digestion of proteins into peptides, followed by chromatographic separation and MS detection to generate a “peptide map” that comprehensively characterizes the composition and modification information of proteins. After PEGylation of proteins, the peptides containing the modification sites will exhibit altered molecular weights upon enzymatic hydrolysis. By comparing the peptide maps of PEGylated and unmodified proteins, the modification sites can be identified.

Saha-Shah et al.154 developed a cleavable PEG linker. This PEG chain, when conjugated to the surface lysine residues of Interferon α-2b (IFN) under the catalysis of TGase, can be cleaved at arginine or glutamine residues by trypsin or other hydrolytic enzymes. This process leaves a short amino acid chain attached to the PEGylation site on the protein, which can be detected in the peptide map data, thereby identifying the PEGylation site. Compared with previous studies, this research, for the first time, discovered that K134 of IFN can undergo a small amount of PEGylation under TGase catalysis. This method simplifies sample processing steps and ensures the stability of PEG conjugation.

Qin et al.155 addressed the issue of multiple PEGylation sites on recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) and the resulting high heterogeneity of the modified products. They first separated mono-PEGylated rhGH with different modification sites using ion exchange chromatography with a pH gradient elution. Subsequently, they analyzed five different mono-PEGylated rhGH samples using MALDI-TOF trypsin peptide mapping. By comparing the hydrolysis maps with those of unmodified rhGH, they identified the missing peptide segments, thereby inferring the PEGylation sites. The five single modification sites were determined to be the N-terminal amine, lys140, Lys145, Lys158, and Lys38.

Liquid chromatography peptide mapping is the most commonly used technique for identifying PEGylation sites after protein PEGylation, providing detailed information on protein fragments. However, challenges remain in identifying PEGylation sites due to incomplete enzymatic digestion, complex data requiring specialized software analysis, and issues with MS resolution.

Surface plasmon resonance

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) is a real-time, label-free analytical technique based on an optical phenomenon, widely used for the study of molecular interactions. The primary principle is that when incident light strikes a metal film at a specific angle, it excites the collective oscillation of free electrons on the metal surface (surface plasmon wave). The resonance condition is highly sensitive to the refractive index of the surrounding medium. Consequently, when molecules bind or dissociate, changes in the refractive index of the medium cause detectable shifts in the resonance angle or light intensity, thereby allowing real-time, label-free quantification of molecular interactions. This method is useful for investigating the effects of PEGylation on the binding capacity of proteins and their targets156,157.

Kong et al.158 examined the PEGylated products of IL-2 mutants compared to the binding capacity of IL-2 with two receptor subunits, IL-2Rα and IL-2Rβ. The SPR results indicated that, compared to IL-2, the PEGylated products showed virtually no binding with IL-2Rα and only a 6.1-fold decrease in binding capacity with IL-2Rβ. This alteration in affinity may be related to the non-specific steric hindrance caused by the PEG chains.

Wu et al.82 utilized SPR technology to compare the in vitro activity of hGH modified with PEG of different molecular weights and at different sites. The results demonstrated that the affinity of hGH for its substrate decreased with increasing PEG molecular weight, especially when modified with PEG of 40 kDa. The same trend was observed at different PEGylation sites. Additionally, when the molecular weight of PEG on the hGH surface was constant, it was found that hGH modified with multiple PEG had similar or slightly reduced affinity compared to hGH modified with a single PEG. Therefore, the effect of PEGylation on protein activity is not only related to the molecular weight of PEG itself, but also significantly influenced by the number of PEG chains.

In addition, due to the technical advantages of SPR in detecting antibody levels in animal sera, SPR can also be used to evaluate the immunogenicity of PEG chains themselves in PEGylated proteins. Li et al.159 modified the surface of a gold chip with a PEG self-assembled monolayer and subsequently used SPR to measure serum antibodies in mice immunized with PEGylated keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH). When the serum passed over the chip, if specific anti-PEG antibodies were present, they would bind to the chip. Subsequent treatment with a secondary antibody could then amplify and detect the signal. The results showed that all mice treated with PEG-KLH exhibited high levels of anti-IgG and anti-IgM antibodies in their sera, demonstrating the immunogenicity risk of PEG itself, which was consistent with the results obtained by ELISA.

Summary and prospects

To this day, PEGylated protein drugs remain one of the most promising strategies for successful clinical translation. Although PEGylated protein drugs have achieved great success in the human body, researchers’ focus is no longer solely on the external manifestations of “low immunogenicity and long circulating half-life” displayed by PEGylated proteins. Instead, there is a growing interest in understanding how to appropriately PEGylate proteins and how PEGylation affects the structure and function of proteins. Understanding the interaction between proteins and PEG is crucial for achieving the desired effects of PEGylation.

This review summarizes the current mainstream strategies for site-specific PEGylation of proteins. Starting from these strategies, it analyzes the existence state of PEGylated proteins in solution, the impact of PEGylation on protein structure and function, the factors affecting the properties of the modified products, and the commonly used characterization techniques for PEGylated proteins. We hope this review can explain the functions presented by the final modified products from the perspective of the interaction between PEG and proteins.

“Site-specific PEGylation” is actually a broader concept that needs to be understood from two aspects. On the one hand, “site-specific” does not mean that PEGylation can occur at any amino acid residue on the protein. The commonly used PEGylation strategies often involve the reaction of PEG with an active group and specific amino acid residues on the protein that have reactive groups. Therefore, the location of PEGylation is determined by the position of the reactive amino acid residues on the protein surface. On the other hand, to achieve PEGylation at any desired site on the protein surface, the corresponding site can be mutated to an amino acid with a reactive group (such as cysteine, azidohomoalanine, homopropargylglycine, etc.). This approach enables PEGylation at the “specific site”. For the current PEGylation strategies, the essence of “site-specific” modification is actually “residue-specific”.

Conventional PEGylation relies on the natural amino acids on the protein surface (such as the amino group of lysine and the thiol group of cysteine). These natural amino acids are widely distributed and may occur in multiple locations, which can lead to random modification and strong product heterogeneity. The integration of non-natural amino acids via genetic code expansion is a milestone in achieving true site-specific modification in protein PEGylation. Non-natural amino acids can be precisely inserted at specific sites on the protein, thereby preventing the active sites from being masked after modification and significantly enhancing the uniformity of the modified products. The chemical groups carried by non-natural amino acids, such as azides (cyclooctyne) and ketones (hydrazide), can be selectively coupled to functionalized PEGs through click chemistry under mild reaction conditions (ambient temperature, neutral pH). This approach avoids protein denaturation caused by traditional chemical cross-linking. Moreover, since the chemical groups of non-natural amino acids do not exist in natural proteins, non-specific binding is minimized, thereby enhancing coupling efficiency and product purity. Orthogonal click chemistry overcomes the limitations of conventional PEGylation methods that are influenced by natural amino acids. It makes PEGylation more precise and efficient, providing a new tool for the functional customization and performance optimization of protein-based drugs.

The functional properties of PEG-modified proteins are predominantly governed by three critical parameters in a synergistic manner: (1) spatial distribution of modification sites: targeted conjugation at specific functional domains enables strategic shielding effects. For instance, modifications near immunogenic regions can effectively mask antigenic epitopes, while proximity to active sites requires careful avoidance of steric hindrance. (2) PEGylation degree (valency): the number of covalently attached PEG chains per protein molecule directly determines surface coverage density. Moderate valency enhances immune shielding and prolongs in vivo half-life, whereas excessive modification may induce conformational perturbations leading to functional impairment. (3) PEG molecular weight: chain length interacts dynamically with valency to collectively regulate spatial shielding efficacy. Significantly, these parameters exhibit marked coupling effects—the type and quantity of available modification sites fundamentally dictate the maximum achievable valency, while site selection is constrained by PEG reactivity and target-specific binding capacity. Therefore, rational design of PEGylated proteins necessitates precise structural characterization of the target protein combined with multi-parametric optimization to establish robust structure–function relationship models.

Despite the fact that it has been 30 years since the first PEGylated protein drug was marketed, the design and analysis of PEGylated protein drugs have always been a challenge. From the protein itself, there are a large number of amino acids on its surface available to conjugate with PEG. The abundance of candidates brings the trouble of choice. The mainstream idea at present is to carry out PEGylation away from the active site. It is still an issue how to control the optimal distance from the modified site to the active center, which is limited by the amino acids capable of reacting with PEG and difficult to be arbitrarily chosen. With the development of the non-canonical amino acids technique and orthogonal click chemistry, site-specific PEGylation of proteins at any desired position is becoming increasingly feasible.

Protein activity loss after PEGylation appeared in many research and this loss was commonly attributed to the interference of PEG with the binding of substrates to the active site. These conclusions are typically derived from inferences based on experimental results, rather than direct observation. It is still difficult to acquire the reaction process information between PEGylated proteins and substrates based on existing technologies. The appropriate characterization methods to understand the reaction process will be powerful tools for the design of PEGylated proteins.

PEG itself is a highly polydisperse polymer. Although it falls within a certain polydispersity coefficient range, strictly speaking, PEG monomer-modified products are actually mixtures of different molecular weights. This may lead to batch-to-batch differences in PEGylated proteins and difficulties in purifying the modified products. For most characterization methods, it is impossible to distinguish between these polydisperse PEG-modified protein products. However, PEG-modified proteins with different molecular weights may exhibit different pharmacokinetic behaviors in the body. Some studies have focused on the synthesis of PEG with a single molecular weight and its modification of proteins, but the maximum molecular weight achieved is only 5 kDa. It is necessary to prepare PEG with higher and single molecular weights.

While existing characterization techniques can characterize PEG-modified protein products from different perspectives and yield relatively rich information, such as spatial structure and thermal stability, these methods still face several challenges. On one hand, the information they provide is often limited in scope. For example, CD can only offer rough information about the two-dimensional structure (such as changes in α-helices), and intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy is restricted to providing information about the positions of a few specific amino acids. These methods struggle to deliver a comprehensive overview of protein changes. On the other hand, techniques like nuclear magnetic resonance and small-angle scattering have relatively low resolution and involve complex data processing. Especially for the detailed characterization of high molecular weight proteins, this presents a significant challenge. Therefore, exploring a combination of multiple methods is essential for characterizing PEGylated proteins, and there is a need to explore new characterization techniques to break through the limitations of current approaches.

In summary, site-specific PEGylation of proteins represents the future direction for the development of PEGylated protein drugs. The selection of the modification method and site should be guided by the impact of PEGylation on protein structure and function, aiming to produce PEG-modified products with optimal characteristics. As technology continues to advance, there is a growing need for more intuitive and visual characterization methods. These methods will enable us to accurately investigate the changes that occur when PEG is connected to proteins, thereby facilitating the translation of more PEGylated proteins into clinical applications.
