Efficacy of cerebellar cathodal transcranial direct current stimulation for post-stroke aphasia: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiaoyi Xu, Shuping Chen, Zifan Li, Lijie Zhang, Tianjin Pan, Siying Long, Tingting Yang, Meng Li, Qianru Li, Na Chen, Shangjie Chen, Qing Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Cerebellar cathodal tDCS combined with speech therapy improved language scores in post-stroke aphasia.•fNIRS showed decreased right and increased left hemisphere connectivity, indicating functional reorganization.•Findings highlight the cerebellum as a promising neuromodulation target for aphasia rehabilitation.Keywords: Transcranial direct current stimulation, Cerebellum, Post-stroke aphasia, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy technology, Functional connection

Introduction

Aphasia is one of the most debilitating consequences of stroke, affecting more than one third of stroke survivors(Breitenstein et al. 2017) (Palmer et al. 2019). This disorder affects not only the comprehension and production of language, but also reading and writing skills(Brady et al. 2016) (K.-P. Li et al. 2023), leading to a significant reduction in quality of life and social engagement. Consequently, aphasia poses a significant challenge to rehabilitation. Currently, the rehabilitation of post-stroke aphasia relies predominantly on conventional speech therapy methods, which include impairment-based approaches such as phonological, lexical-semantic, and syntactic training, as well as functional communication therapies like PACE (Promoting Aphasics’s Communicative Effectiveness)(Fridriksson and Hillis 2021). However, conventional SLT presents several limitations. First, therapy intensity and dosage often fall short of the levels demonstrated to be most effective—high-frequency, high-dose therapy is associated with better outcomes, yet such delivery is difficult to achieve in standard clinical settings(Stahl et al. 2018). Second, traditional approaches require sustained motivation and cognitive engagement from patients, which can be challenging, particularly in the chronic stage or in individuals with attention or mood disturbances(Pulvermüller et al. 2001). Lastly, the generalization of gains from therapy to everyday communication remains limited, and progress tends to plateau over time, especially in chronic aphasia(Cherney et al. 2008). These limitations highlight the need for innovative and supplementary interventions. Therefore, enhancing the efficacy of aphasia rehabilitation remains a key challenge in the field.

In recent years, non-invasive methods of neuromodulation have attracted considerable interest for their potential to promote neuroplasticity, offering advantages such as being non-invasive, safe and portable. Among these techniques, transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) has shown potential for improving the outcome of speech and language therapy in people with aphasia(Marangolo 2020). Most of the existing research has focused on anodal stimulation targeting regions in the left hemisphere, in line with conventional models of aphasia recovery. However, patients with chronic post-stroke aphasia often have extensive areas of encephalomalacia, which may hinder the efficacy of stimulation on tissue close to the target lesion(Datta et al. 2011). In addition, applying stimulation to affected brain regions may result in unpredictable effects(Datta et al. 2011), and recent studies have also highlighted the impact of right cerebellar damage on language-related tasks(Turkeltaub et al. 2016). Research in functional neuroimaging has consistently revealed activation in the right posterolateral cerebellum during language tasks, implying that the cerebellum is vital for language processing(Baillieux et al. 2010) (Stoodley and Schmahmann 2009). Furthermore, there is proof that cerebellar tDCS can boost language fluency in healthy individuals and change cerebellar-brain connectivity, particularly in the left-dominant language network(Turkeltaub et al. 2016). While cerebellar tDCS has been examined for tasks like verb generation(Pope and Chris Miall (2012)) (Marangolo et al. 2018), picture naming(Sebastian et al. 2020), and spelling(Sebastian et al. 2016), a recent study reported no significant language processing improvements in post-stroke aphasia patients following application of right cerebellar tDCS in conjunction with speech therapy, either immediately after treatment or at a three-month follow-up(DeMarco et al. 2021). These findings prompt significant inquiries regarding the possible effectiveness of cerebellar tDCS in aphasia rehabilitation after a stroke.

There is still a lack of understanding regarding the neural mechanisms of cerebellar tDCS in post-stroke aphasia. A randomized, double-blind study by Sebastian et al(Sebastian et al. 2020) involved 24 patients with aphasia to compare the effects of cathodic cerebellar tDCS, anodal tDCS, and a placebo treatment. The results showed that the group that received cathodic cerebellar tDCS showed significantly greater improvements in naming ability than both the anodal and sham stimulation groups. Moreover, some scientists propose that cerebellar tDCS mainly influences by altering the polarization of Purkinje cells, which subsequently changes the activity of the deep cerebellar nuclei and impacts the distal plasticity of cortical areas(van Dun et al. 2016) (Grimaldi et al. 2016). Studies reveal that anodal stimulation increases Purkinje cell activity, enhancing the inhibitory effect on the facilitatory pathway from the cerebellar nuclei to the cortex. Alternatively, cathodal stimulation generates the opposite outcome by releasing Purkinje cells from inhibition, which then activates the left prefrontal cortex(Pope and Chris Miall (2012)) (Pope 2015). However, this study did not assess changes in brain network activity. On the other hand, research conducted on healthy adults using fMRI indicated that cerebellar tDCS changed the connectivity between the cerebellum and the language network located in the left hemisphere(Turkeltaub et al. 2016). This suggests that cerebellar tDCS may affect neural pathways associated with language processing. To improve recovery in patients with post-stroke aphasia, it is essential to assess cortical activity and understand how cerebellar tDCS affects neural function. However, there is currently no direct evidence that cathodic cerebellar tDCS can specifically modulate cortical activation or functional connectivity in patients with post-stroke aphasia.

fNIRS, or functional near-infrared spectroscopy, is a new optical neuroimaging technique that facilitates real-time observation of haemodynamic changes in the brain by measuring oxyhaemoglobin (HbO) and deoxyhaemoglobin (HbR) concentrations. This approach provides a direct indication of cortical activation, providing valuable insight into brain regions associated with different cognitive processes(Yamanaka et al. 2014). Compared to other neuroimaging techniques, fNIRS has several advantages, including portability, ease of use, low susceptibility to noise, and cost-effectiveness(Balardin et al. 2017). These features position fNIRS as a promising tool for the safe and non-invasive assessment of cortical activation patterns in individuals with neurological disorders. Research involving fNIRS in the context of stroke-related aphasia has demonstrated atypical brain activation and functional connectivity in patients, both at rest and during language tasks, showing that fNIRS is a beneficial technique for detecting brain activity changes(Meier et al. 2023) (Gilmore et al. 2021) (H. Li et al. 2022). With the help of fNIRS, two studies examined how brain functional connectivity in stroke aphasia patients differed after rTMS treatment compared to initial values(Hara et al. 2017) (Chang et al. 2022). As of now, this procedure has not been applied to evaluate changes in cortical activation and functional connectivity post-cerebellar tDCS treatment compared to baseline values.

In conclusion, there is controversy over the clinical effectiveness of cerebellar tDCS in aphasia treatment, and the underlying neural mechanisms are not well understood. The purpose of this study is to explore if integrating cerebellar tDCS with standard speech therapy have an effect on improving the language performance in individuals with post-stroke aphasia and alter their brain functional connection by using fNIRS. The investigation will also look into how tDCS regulates cortical network activity in stroke patients, with the goal of offering a theoretical framework for using cerebellar tDCS in treating aphasia caused by stroke.

Subjects and methods

Experimental design

Our study was a randomized, parallel-group, sham-controlled, single-blind, single-center clinical trial designed to examine the potential of cerebellar cathodic transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) in enhancing the effectiveness of intensive therapy in patients with chronic post-stroke aphasia and word finding difficulties. Every participant underwent standardized naming therapy while receiving either cathodal tDCS or a sham tDCS. The total score of the Chinese Standard Aphasia Scale (CRRCAE) served as the primary outcome measure, and secondary outcomes included changes in brain activation during a naming task, assessed via fNIRS. The assessment of outcomes occurred at two intervals: before the intervention and directly following a 10-day treatment period. Fig. 1 depicts the study design. This investigation was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: 2500107492) and sanctioned by the Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital, Southern Medical University, located in Guangzhou, China. Participants were given compensation for their involvement, and the study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Test flow chart.

Participants

Sixteen patients with post-stroke aphasia after a single hemorrhagic or ischaemic stroke of the left hemisphere were recruited from the Rehabilitation Department of Zhujiang Hospital.

The inclusion criteria were: 1) diagnosis of left hemisphere haemorrhagic or ischaemic stroke confirmed by CT or MRI; 2) age between 18 and 80 years with right hand dominance; 3) diagnosis of post-stroke aphasia based on established clinical criteria; 4) time since stroke onset between 3 and 12 months; 5) stable vital signs and active cooperation with treatment; and 6) voluntary participation in the study with signed informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: 1) right cerebellar lesions; 2) presence of intracranial metal implants; 3) history of seizures within the last 12 months; 4) sensitive scalp; 5) history of mental illness or other neurological disorders; 6) severe visual or hearing impairment; and 7) inability to complete the assessment. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their enrollment in the study.

A total of 16 participants with aphasia were included, with 8 in the experimental group and 6 in the control group. Excluded from the study were two patients due to changes in their medical condition.

Treatment

Simple randomization was adopted. A random number table was generated using SPSS, and participants were assigned random numbers accordingly. Subsequently, cases were ranked, and participants were randomly allocated into the intervention group and the control group based on the system-generated randomization. The specific intervention procedures employed in this study were as follows:

(1) Cathodic cerebellar tDCS: The cathodic electrode for the tDCS device (Henan Xiangyu Medical Equipment Co. LTD.) was placed on the right cerebellar cortex, approximately 1 cm below the occipital tuberosity and 4 cm laterally, corresponding to the scalp projection of the seventh cerebellar lobule. Placed on the patient's right shoulder, the anodal electrode delivered stimulation at a 2 mA current for 20 min daily across 10 days(Rosso et al. 2014), as depicted in Fig. 2.

Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Schematic. (A) electrode placement: cathodic electrode was placed on the right cerebellar cortex (1 cm below the occipital tuberosity and 4 cm laterally); anodal electrode was placed on the patient's right deltoid; (B) stimulation of transcranial direct current stimulation instrument: The stimulation current intensity is set to 2 mA, and the duration of stimulation is set to 20 min.

(2) Sham stimulation: The electrode configuration for the sham group mirrored that of the experimental cohort. However, in this case the current was gradually increased to 2 mA and then decreased to zero over a period of 30 s. The device was removed after a total of 20 min(Fridriksson et al. 2018). This group followed the same procedure as the experimental group, but the stimulation was only active for 30 s before being switched off. Sham stimulation was also given for 20 min daily for 10 days(Balardin et al. 2017).

(3) Conventional speech therapy: An individualised rehabilitation plan was developed for each patient, taking into account the results of their speech function assessments. Speech-language pathologists conducted individual one-on-one therapy sessions that included exercises targeting respiratory control, vital capacity, sound production, listening comprehension, naming tasks, and reading. Each session lasted 30 min and took place daily for a total of 10 days.

The test group received both cathodic cerebellar tDCS and conventional speech therapy, whereas the control group was given sham stimulation in conjunction with conventional speech therapy. Following each stimulation session, patients were inquired about any sensations, including itching or tingling. No adverse reactions were reported in either group during or after the treatment period.

Outcome measures

In this research, CRRCAE served as the main outcome measure, with fNIRS employed as a secondary outcome to evaluate alterations in brain activation during a naming task. The CRRCAE is a specialised instrument based on advanced theoretical frameworks for the assessment of aphasia, specifically tailored to the linguistic characteristics of the Chinese language, including its grammar and vocabulary. The CRRCAE is widely used in the clinical assessment and treatment of Chinese-speaking individuals with aphasia and has demonstrated high reliability and sensitivity, with its composite score showing robust validity, making it an effective tool for assessing the severity of aphasia(Meier et al. 2023). The assessment includes nine dimensions: 1) listening; 2) retelling; 3) speaking; 4) reading; 5) writing; 6) copying; 7) describing; 8) dictating; 9) calculating. Each dimension has 30 subtests. All dimensions except Calculation use a scoring system ranging from 1 (indicating severe aphasia) to 6 (indicating no aphasia), with lower scores reflecting greater severity of aphasia.

fNIRS was used to assess brain region activation and functional connectivity during the naming task. The fNIRS system used in this study consisted of a fibre-optic probe, a head cap and the associated software. A total of 55 channel light source-detector pairs were arranged to cover both the frontal and parietal lobes, allowing comprehensive synchronous measurements across all channels. The system used two specific wavelengths of light − 690 nm and 830 nm − emitted from fibre-optic probes that penetrate the scalp to access the cerebral cortex. Detectors, positioned 30 mm from the probes, picked up signals from both the scalp and underlying cortical regions. The light sources and detectors were attached to the head cap according to the international 10–20 system for electrode placement(Grimaldi et al. 2016). The probes were colour coded red and blue, with each light source/detector pair constituting a separate measurement channel. The correlation between these channels and the Brodmann areas (BA) of the brain was established using a probabilistic registration method. Based on the coordinate data obtained from the fNIRS measurements, four regions of interest (ROIs) were identified for analysis:the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) that contains Broca's area, the supramarginal gyrus (SMG), along with the supplementary motor area (SMA). The exact arrangement of the near-infrared device, probes and measurement channels is shown in Fig. 3, Fig. 3, while the ROIs are shown in Fig. 3C.

Schematic diagram of near-infrared channel distribution (A) the distribution diagram of fNIRS device and probe and measuring channel; (B) head cap and displayed channel; (C) the relationship diagram of fNIRS acquisition head cap and ROI.

Before the test, participants were given a full explanation of the purpose and procedure of the naming task. To reduce potential data errors and minimise fatigue, participants were given a 10-minute rest period before the test began. After participants completed the naming task, the collected data were exported for further analysis. During the fNIRS measurements, participants engaged in a block design paradigm, alternating between picture naming and rest periods. The picture naming task consisted of a total of 48 pictures, with each block organised as follows: an experimental block of 30 s and a control block of 20 s. Each type of block was repeated eight times, resulting in a total task duration of 6.5 to 7 min per session. As shown in Fig. 4, during the experimental blocks, participants were shown six pictures (e.g. a fan) and were asked to verbally name each object within a four-second timeframe. In the control blocks, participants focused on a fixed black dot on the screen. Participants' verbal responses during the picture naming task were recorded for later analysis. To reduce potential bias, a standardised set of 48 pictures was used, consisting of 24 noun pictures (e.g. balloons) and 24 verb pictures (e.g. drinking water).

Statistical analysis

Initially, measurement data were evaluated for normality. Data that satisfied the criteria of normal distribution and equal variance are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons within the same group, paired sample t-tests were used, whereas independent sample t-tests were used for comparisons between different groups. One-way ANOVA was additionally applied to assess the behavioral outcomes intergroup differences separately at baseline and post-treatment. To control for baseline differences, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with baseline values as covariates for post-treatment efficacy comparisons. In analyzing categorical data, the chi-squared test was used to compare frequencies, while ordinal data was assessed using the rank-sum test. For multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using Bonferroni correction. A two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 was used, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

All fNIRS data were analysed using Nirspark 1.8.1 software (NirSmart, Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co. Ltd.). Data processing included the following steps 1) Conversion to optical density: The raw fNIRS data were first converted to optical density; 2) Motion artefact correction: The optical density data were adjusted to account for motion artefacts; 3) Filtering: Instrumental or physiological noise was eliminated using a high-pass filter at 0.01 Hz and a low-pass filter at 0.2 Hz; 4) Concentration change calculation: Using the modified Beer-Lambert law, changes in optical density were transformed into concentration changes for both HbO (oxygenated haemoglobin) and HbR (deoxygenated haemoglobin); 5) The estimation of the hemodynamic response function (HRF) was performed using the general linear model (GLM), followed by the calculation of task-related β-values. This analysis highlights the β-value as a measure of brain activation in certain regions, emphasizing the HbO signal for its generally better signal-to-noise ratio over the HbR signal. Once pre-processing was completed, Pearson correlation analysis was utilized to determine the correlation coefficients between the regions of interest (ROIs). These coefficients were then converted into normally distributed Z-values through Fisher's Z-transformation. Within-group functional connectivity strength was assessed using a paired-samples t-test, and between-group comparisons were made using an independent-samples t-test, with a significance level of P < 0.05. In functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was additionally applied.

Discussion

This study investigated cerebral cortex activation using functional near–infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) in patients with post–stroke aphasia following 10 consecutive days of cerebellar transcranial direct current stimulation (ctDCS). Aphasia severity and treatment-related changes in language performance were assessed using the Chinese Rehabilitation Research Center Aphasia Examination (CRRCAE). The innovative aspects include targeting the right cerebellum—a region structurally spared in most supratentorial strokes—as a single, consistent neuromodulation site across heterogeneous cortical lesion profiles, to engage cerebro–cerebellar language networks directly. Unlike previous cathodal tDCS studies that primarily reported behavioral improvements in naming or fluency, this trial uniquely couples 10 consecutive days of stimulation with objective neurovascular imaging via fNIRS, enabling real–time mapping of cortical hemodynamic responses and neural activation changes. This integration of neuroimaging with standardized CRRCAE scoring facilitates a mechanistic understanding of how cerebellar stimulation modulates both language function and cortical dynamics, thereby representing a significant advance in non- invasive neuromodulation research for aphasia.

The results showed that language performance improved significantly after 10 consecutive days of tDCS (sham) stimulation combined with conventional speech therapy (P < 0.05). Before undergoing treatment, both groups had similar CRRCAE scores with no statistical difference (P > 0.05), and their language performance was also comparable (P > 0.05). It has been suggested that Purkinje cells in the cerebellar cortex inhibit excitation in language areas via the dentate nucleus-ventrolateral thalamic-left prefrontal cortex pathway. The inhibitory effect of cathodal cerebellar tDCS reduces the influence of Purkinje cells on cerebellar nuclei, thereby freeing cognitive resources in the cerebral cortex(Pope and Chris Miall (2012)) (Pope and Chris Miall (2014)) (Galea et al. 2009). However, the results of this study contrast with those of Sebastian et al(Sebastian et al. 2020), who demonstrated that 15 sessions of cathodal tDCS delivered to the cerebellum, combined with computerised aphasia therapy, improved language outcomes. Similarly, research by Marangolo et al(Marangolo et al. 2016) and Pope et al(Pope and Chris Miall (2012)) also showed that cathodal cerebellar tDCS can improve verb generation. There are several potential reasons for these discrepancies. First, this study had a smaller sample size and participants had different types of aphasia with significant individual differences in their CRRCAE scores, which may have influenced the results. Also, although the CRRCAE scale showed high sensitivity and reliability, its correlation with the Western Aphasia Battery (WAB) was mainly limited to the spontaneous speech and naming ability subtests, excluding other aspects such as retelling and listening comprehension. Given these factors, it is not possible to conclude that cerebellar tDCS cannot improve language performance in patients with post-stroke aphasia. Additional studies with increased sample sizes are essential to comprehensively examine the effects of cerebellar tDCS on language recovery.

We used a 55-channel fNIRS spectrometer to acquire signals from the frontal and parietal lobes. Our analysis revealed significant changes in functional connectivity between the SMA and several other brain regions before and after treatment. Although traditionally associated with motor control, recent evidence suggests that the SMA is involved in language networks. Anatomically, the SMA is considered critical for language recovery in aphasic patients, as it is connected to IFG or Broca's area via the arcuate fasciculus − an essential pathway for language processing(Catani et al. 2012). The anterior SMA is involved in speech preparation, initiation of vocal movements, word selection, and the generation and sequencing of speech plans, earning the name 'speech initiation zone. However, the posterior SMA is thought to be involved in word pronunciation, initiation, timing control and speech monitoring(Hertrich, Dietrich, and Ackermann 2016). Some researches also proposed that the anterior SMA coordinated mechanisms and controls contralateral inhibition(Hertrich, Dietrich, and Ackermann 2016). Research exploring cathodal tDCS targeting the right Broca's area in healthy adults, Rosso et al(Rosso et al. 2014) found that following the intervention, there was an enhancement in picture naming speed and in the functional connectivity between the right SMA and right IFG, suggesting that the SMA may play a role in speech monitoring. This suggests that the SMA may mitigate the inhibitory effect of the right IFG on the left IFG. Another perspective on the role of the SMA in language processing comes from the embodied cognition framework, which proposes that the SMA is involved in action generation and may therefore play a role in the production of action-related words(Zhang, Sun, and Wang 2018) (Postle et al. 2008) (Postle et al. 2008). In this view, patients may modify the functional connectivity of their SMA during tasks such as verb naming, reflecting actions commonly performed in everyday life (e.g., drinking water, kicking a soccer ball). In support of this theory, Postle et al(Postle et al. 2008), using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), observed that action-related words evoke greater SMA activation compared to other lexical categories, whereas Turkeltaub et al(Turkeltaub et al. 2016) (D’Mello, Turkeltaub, and Stoodley 2017) argued that cerebellar tDCS over the posterolateral cerebellum primarily alters functional connectivity within the brain-cerebellum network without affecting the somatomotor network. A possible reason of this discrepancy could be methodological differences: both studies collected resting-state fMRI data from healthy adults, whereas patients with post-stroke aphasia often have limb movement impairments, leading to greater activation of motor areas during language tasks(Gili et al. 2017). Furthermore, the verbal tasks used in these studies (e.g. verbal fluency and semantic prediction) differ from tasks such as verb naming, which are more likely to engage motor-related brain regions(Postle et al. 2008).

Based on functional connectivity analysis, we found increased connectivity between the right prefrontal cortex and the SMG, the ipsilateral SMA, the contralateral SMA, and between the LSMA and the LSMG. We also found increased functional connectivity between the LSMA and the left posterior central gyrus. Our findings are consistent with previous reports that aphasic patients have shown increased activity in the right hemisphere and reduced activity in the left hemisphere, particularly in language processing regions. According to the inhibition theory of interhemispheric interaction, increased activation of language areas in the right hemisphere may increase the inhibitory output to the affected left hemisphere, thus impeding the functional reorganisation of the damaged brain. Consequently, reduced right hemisphere activation and increased activation of left language-related regions may facilitate the improvement of language function in aphasic patients. The outcomes of our study support previous research findings that rehabilitation for post-stroke patients with motor aphasia leads to improved functional connectivity among the left middle frontal gyrus, left prefrontal cortex, and left precentral regions. Simultaneously, we noticed decrease functional connectivity between the left middle frontal gyrus and various regions in the right hemisphere, including the right superior temporal gyrus, right middle temporal gyrus, right insula and right superior frontal gyrus. Bai Guangtao et al found that compared with the sham stimulation group, low-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation improved the listening comprehension function of patients with post-stroke non-fluent aphasia by inhibiting the activation of multiple brain regions in the right parietal lobe by using resting-state fMRI(Gan et al. 2024). Similarly, in the present study, by applying cathodal tDCS stimulation to the cerebellum, we observed decreased right frontoparietal connectivity and increased left frontoparietal connectivity. According to these results, the cerebellum might improve speech performance by modulating the functional connectivity in the cerebral cortex's language-related areas.

The neural mechanisms by which the cerebellum regulates language remain elusive. One hypothesis is that the cerebellum influences language performance by modulating language-related areas in the cerebral cortex. In addition, the cerebellum may influence language through its broader regulation of cognitive functions. An anatomical study using neurotropic viruses revealed cerebellar connectivity with prefrontal, posterior parietal and cortical motor areas, with projections through the ventral thalamus to the contralateral hemisphere of the brain(Strick, Dum, and Fiez 2009). Furthermore, research involving tDCS applied to the right lateral cerebellum has demonstrated that repeated sessions of cathodal cerebellar tDCS can lead to cortical excitation, which in turn aids in activating remaining language regions in the left hemisphere(Strick, Dum, and Fiez 2009). Nonetheless, only a limited number of studies have explored how cathodal tDCS affects the connectivity between the cerebellum and the cerebral cortex(B et al. 2024).

In the present study, we found that cathodal tDCS reduced connectivity between the LSMA and the LSMG, and between the RDLPFC and both the RSMA and the RSMG. These findings suggest that cathodal tDCS may enhance compensatory functional connectivity in the right hemisphere, potentially strengthening language-related areas in the dominant hemisphere. However, our analysis was limited by the number and location of the headcap channels, which restricted our focus to functional connectivity within the bilateral SMG, bilateral DLPFC, bilateral IFG and bilateral SMA. As a result, we were unable to detect functional connectivity in regions such as the inferior temporal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, superior temporal gyrus, angular gyrus and middle frontal gyrus. Furthermore, because fNIRS can only detect changes in HbO in the cerebral cortex, this study could not directly assess functional connectivity between the cerebellum and the cerebral cortex. To further elucidate the influence of cathodal cerebellar tDCS on language-related regions in the cerebral cortex, future studies with larger sample sizes are needed.

Conclusion

After treatment, the improvement in language scores was greater in the experimental group than in the control group. Furthermore, the treatment group showed a reduction in functional connectivity as detected by fNIRS, particularly in areas such as the RDLPFC, RSMA and the RSMG after intervention. By inhibiting specific areas, cathodal tDCS may induce adaptive changes in functional connectivity, potentially enhancing the language network in the dominant hemisphere through right-hemisphere compensation. Thus, our study suggests that transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) applied to the cerebellum may improve language performance in patients with post-stroke aphasia by modulating brain functional connectivity.
