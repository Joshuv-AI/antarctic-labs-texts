Comparative response of root-associated soil bacterial communities to phytophthora blight in healthy versus diseased Panax notoginseng plants
Authors: Shuang Ma, Zhongjian Chen, Xiong Gao, Mengmeng Cheng, Yong Wang, Yuqin Sun, Tie Zhang, Teng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhytophthora blight of Panax notoginseng caused by the fungal pathogen Phytophthora cactorum is a typical soilborne disease, that severely devastates the P. notoginseng industry, and soil bacterial diversity is closely related to the development of Phytophthora blight. A systematic investigation into the abundance of Phytophthora spp. as well as the structural and functional characteristics of the soil bacterial community was carried out using quantitative PCR, high-throughput sequencing of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene, and KEGG pathway analysis. Results showed Phytophthora spp. was most abundant in the rhizosphere soil of diseased plants (RSD) (9.3017 copies ng⁻¹ DNA) and least in the rhizosphere soil of healthy plants (RSH) (0.0169 copies ng⁻¹ DNA). For bacterial α-diversity, ACE and Chao1 indices of diseased soils (RSD and root-zone soil of diseased plants, ZSD) were significantly higher than healthy soils (RSH and root-zone soil of healthy plants, ZSH) and furrow soil (FS) (p < 0.05), with no difference in Shannon index (p > 0.05). β-diversity analyses revealed distinct clustering of the same soil type and inter-group compositional differences. Dominant taxa analysis showed higher Actinomycetota and Bacteroidota in healthy soils, while Verrucomicrobiota, Planctomycetota, and Ramlibacter were more abundant in diseased soils. KEGG functional analysis indicated that the abundances of the “Signal transduction” and “Membrane transport” pathways in the RSD group were higher than those in other groups, while the abundances of the “Metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides” and “Biosynthesis of other secondary metabolism” pathways in the RSD group were lower than those in healthy soils. This suggests that the lack of microorganisms synthesizing anti-oomycete secondary metabolites may contribute to the enrichment of pathogens in the RSD group. This study reveals the association mechanisms between the abundance of soil pathogens, bacterial community structure, and functions in P. notoginseng soils, providing a theoretical basis for the prevention and control of soil-borne diseases and the regulation of soil microecology in P. notoginseng cultivation.Keywords: Panax notoginseng, Phytophthora Blight, Soil bacterial community, Phytophthora cactorumSubject terms: Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

At present, the wild population of Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F. H. Chen has become globally extinct, and its resource supply relies entirely on artificial cultivation. It is worth noting that this species shows extreme sensitivity to habitat conditions. In China, its suitable cultivation regions are mainly restricted to southwestern areas, including Yunnan, Guangxi, and Guizhou provinces. Yunnan Province, as the most optimal and concentrated production area, contributes more than 98% to both the national cultivated area and yield of P. notoginseng. As a local authentic medicinal herb in Yunnan, it accounts for over 50% of the total output value of the province’s traditional Chinese medicine industry1. Specifically, Wenshan Prefecture in Yunnan is recognized as the genuine producing area, where the climatic and soil conditions are optimally suited for the cultivation of P. notoginseng2,3. In September 2024, severe Phytophthora blight occurred in all P. notoginseng fields in the surrounding areas of Wenshan Prefecture, Yunnan Province, which has caused huge losses to the P. notoginseng industry. P. cactorum is one of the main pathogenic oomycete in the cultivation of P. notoginseng. The blight caused by it often leads to the wilting of the whole P. notoginseng plant and the rot of its root system. Once infected, the whole plant can die within 3–5 days. Moreover, the pathogenic oomycete can spread rapidly through soil and rainwater. Once it breaks out in the field, the incidence rate can reach more than 50% in a short period, and even the entire crop in severely affected plots can be lost. Due to the fact that the cultivation cycle of P. notoginseng is as long as 3 years or more, the pathogenic oomycete is easy to accumulate in the soil, making it prone to contiguous spread once the disease occurs. Currently, there is a lack of effective control measures.

Plant diseases often arise following shifts in the condition of microbial communities, which in turn disrupt their homeostatic balance4. The rhizosphere, a key area around plant roots, carries out crucial roles like supporting plant health via complex interactions between microbial groups and the plant itself5. Nevertheless, the population traits of the plant microbiome can exert a notable impact on these interactive dynamics, with such impacts being either advantageous or detrimental. Previous studies have demonstrated that the occurrence of crop diseases is often accompanied by a significant imbalance in the structure of rhizobacterial communities, such as a reduction in the abundance of beneficial bacteria and the dominance of pathogenic bacteria6,7. Therefore, deciphering the response characteristics of rhizobacterial communities in P. notoginseng following the onset of Phytophthora blight can provide important clues for revealing the microecological mechanisms underlying disease occurrence. Despite the significant threat of Phytophthora blight to the P. notoginseng industry, current research has predominantly focused on aspects such as pathogen identification and screening of chemical agents, with insufficient attention paid to the dynamic changes in rhizosphere microbial communities and their driving mechanisms during disease occurrence. Previous studies on the rhizosphere microorganisms of P. notoginseng have mostly focused on continuous cropping obstacles, while there have been no reports on the response characteristics of rhizosphere bacterial communities during the occurrence of Phytophthora blight caused by P. cactorum8,9. Therefore, this study conducts a comparative analysis of the differences in rhizosphere bacterial communities between healthy and diseased P. notoginseng, which is conducive to clarifying the association between the imbalance of microbial community structure and disease occurrence, and provides a scientific basis for the development of Phytophthora blight prevention and control strategies based on microecological regulation.

Materials and methods

Soil samples collection

Soil samples were collected in September 2024 from a Panax notoginseng cultivation base (104°18’ E, 23°39’ N) owned by Huaxin Company in Wenshan Prefecture, Yunnan Province, China. The soil type at the experimental site is lateritic red earth, which is referred to as such in the Chinese Soil Taxonomy and correlates closely with Rhodic Ferralsols in the FAO-WRB system. On this soil, P. notoginseng had been continuously cultivated for three years over an area exceeding two hectares. At the time of sampling, the incidence of Phytophthora blight in this field reached as high as 80%. Samples were collected from five distinct soil compartments: rhizospheric soil of healthy plants (RSH), root-zone soil of healthy plants (ZSH), rhizospheric soil of diseased plants (RSD), root-zone soil of diseased plants (ZSD), and furrow soil (FS). Rhizospheric soil refers to the soil that stays attached to fine roots after being shaken gently, and it is collected by means of soft brushing10. Each root-zone soil sample was collected with a sterile shovel from a depth of 20 cm around the rhizosphere of each plant. In this planting base, a total of 15 soil samples were collected and divided into five groups: rhizosphere soil of healthy plants, root zone soil of healthy plants, rhizosphere soil of diseased plants, root zone soil of diseased plants, and furrow soil, with three biological replicates per group. For rhizosphere and root-zone soils, samples were taken from three independent plants within the same field plot, and the selected plants were adjacent to each other. Furrow soil (FS) samples were collected from the inter-row furrows at a consistent distance (approximately 50 cm) from the nearest plant row, and this distance was maintained for areas adjacent to both healthy and diseased plants. All samples were subjected to subsequent sequencing analysis. The collected soil samples were sieved through a 4-mm mesh sieve and then stored in a -80 °C.

Pathogen isolation and morphological characterization

Diseased P. notoginseng plants showing typical Phytophthora blight symptoms were collected from the same field described in Sect. 2.1. Stem segments were excised from the junction of diseased and healthy areas using a sterile scalpel and surface-sterilized by immersion in 75% ethanol for 30 s followed by 0.1% mercuric chloride (HgCl₂) for 30 s, then rinsed three times with sterile distilled water. After sterilization, the segments were cut into small pieces (approximately 5 mm×5 mm), blotted dry on sterile filter paper, and placed onto potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium. Plates were incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 4 days and examined daily for mycelial growth. Hyphal tips from emerging colonies were transferred to fresh PDA plates to obtain pure cultures. The isolates were cultured on PDA at 25 °C in the dark for 7 days, after which microscopic features, including hyphae and sporangia, were observed under a light microscope (Leica IC50, Germany).

Pathogen quantification

Total DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of each soil sample using the E.Z.N.A™ Mag-Bind Soil DNA Kit (Omega, Norcross, GA, USA). The relative abundances of Phytophthora spp. in all samples were determined in triplicate with a LightCycler480 Real-Time Quantitative PCR Instrument (Roche, Rotkreuz, Switzerland), following the method described by Yang et al11. with the following modification: the annealing temperature was set to 62°C. A pair of Phytophthora genus-specific primers (Yph1F: 5’-CGACCATKGGTGTGGACTTT-3′ and Yph2R: 5’-ACGTTCTCMCAGGCGTATCT − 3′) was used in this assay, which amplifies an approximately 450 bp fragment of a Phytophthora gene12. A plasmid standard containing the target region was constructed using the Hieff Clone™ Zero TOPO-TA Cloning Kit-HB180280 (Shanghai Yisheng Biotechnology Co., LTD, China) and cloned into Escherichia coli TOP10. The copy numbers of the specific gene were calculated based on standard curves. The abundance of Phytophthora spp. was expressed as copies per ng DNA (copies ng⁻¹ DNA).

Illumina MiSeq sequencing and analysis

The structure of the soil samples’ bacterial community was sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq system (Illumina MiSeq, USA). To identify bacterial communities that are strongly linked to the development of Phytophthora blight in soil, we conducted high-throughput amplicon sequencing on the gathered soil samples. The bacteria-specific primers 341 F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′) were used to amplify the hypervariable region V3-V4 of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene13. Prior to sequencing, the DNA concentration of each PCR product was measured with a Qubit® 4.0 Green double-stranded DNA assay, and quality control was performed using a bioanalyzer (Agilent 2100, USA). In the course of PCR amplification, distinct Barcode sequences were added to the amplified gene fragments in each sample, acting as sample identifiers. In bacterial amplicon sequencing, the average sequencing data volume per sample is 114.36 MB.

Raw sequencing data were processed according to the standard pipeline of Sangon Biotech. Briefly, primer and adapter sequences were removed using cutadapt (https://cutadapt.readthedocs.io). Paired-end reads were merged using PEAR (https://cme.h-its.org/exelixis/web/software/pear) based on overlap. Quality filtering was performed with PRINSEQ (http://prinseq.sourceforge.net) to remove low-quality sequences. Clean reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold using Usearch (v11.0.667, https://www.drive5.com/usearch). Chimeric sequences were removed during the clustering process. Singletons were removed prior to downstream analysis. To account for uneven sequencing depths, rarefaction was applied by randomly subsampling each sample to 50,000 sequences per sample before calculating α-diversity indices. Taxonomic assignment of OTUs was then performed against the RDP 16 S rRNA database using the RDP classifier (v2.12, https://sourceforge.net/projects/rdp-classifier).

Sequence data analysis was performed using QIIME2 and R packages (version 3.6.0). The α-diversity indices (including ACE, Chao1, and Shannon indices), which quantify OTU richness, were all calculated using Mothur software (version 1.43.0). To examine the structural differences in bacterial communities among samples, β-diversity analysis was performed using the Bray-Curtis metric along with principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed with an LDA score threshold > 2 to detect taxa with differential abundances between groups. Meanwhile, statistical analysis of taxonomic and functional profiles (STAMP) was applied to identify variations in the abundances of taxa across groups. PICRUSt2 (v2.5.2, https://github.com/picrust/picrust2) was employed to predict bacterial functions in soil samples, including those from healthy and diseased P. notoginseng plants and furrow soil. The accuracy of the functional predictions was assessed by the nearest sequenced taxon index (NSTI), which was calculated using default parameters. Hierarchical clustering analysis was performed to compare the similarity of bacterial community composition among different soil samples (RSH, ZSH, RSD, ZSD, and FS). The clustering was based on the relative abundance of OTUs at the genus level. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was used as the distance metric, and complete linkage was employed as the linkage method for hierarchical clustering. The dendrogram was visualized using the ape package (https://cran.r-project.org/package=ape).

Statistical analyses

Experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard error, derived from a minimum of three biological replicates. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (v19.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (with a 5% significance level) was applied to identify significant differences in Phytophthora spp. abundance, α-diversity indices (ACE, Chao1, and Shannon).

For linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), the default parameters were used: the Kruskal–Wallis test (α = 0.05) was first employed to detect differentially abundant taxa among groups, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests (α = 0.05) to check consistency across subgroups. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was then performed to estimate the effect size of each differentially abundant feature, with an LDA score threshold of 2.0. Statistical analysis of taxonomic and functional profiles (STAMP) was performed using Welch’s t-test for two-group comparisons, and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to adjust for multiple testing.

Results

Morphological characterization and isolation rates of the pathogen

The disease symptoms of Phytophthora blight were observed in September 2024 in Wenshan Prefecture, Yunnan Province. The fruits of P. notoginseng start to turn black. Subsequently, the junction between the fruits and stems loses water and turns black, showing a “neck-wilting” symptom(Fig. 1A). The leaves wither, and eventually the entire plant wilts(Fig. 1B). Six strains of P. cactorum were isolated from infected stems or diseased soil. After 7 d of growth on PDA at 25℃, the colony of the strains remained white, it assumes a shape like a white lotus flower(Fig. 1C). The hyphae are non-septate and branched, and oval or lemon-shaped sporangia can form directly within the hyphae(Fig. 1D).

Quantitative analysis of Phytophthora spp. in soil

Soil samples were collected from P. notoginseng fields and categorized into five types: rhizosphere soil of healthy plants (RSH), root-zone soil of healthy plants (ZSH), rhizosphere soil of diseased plants (RSD), root-zone soil of diseased plants (ZSD), and furrow soil (FS). The abundance of Phytophthora spp. in each soil sample was quantified using Phytophthora genus-specific primers(Fig. 2). The highest content of Phytophthora spp. in soil samples was found in the rhizosphere soil of diseased plants, reaching 9.3017 copies ng⁻¹ DNA, which was significantly higher than that in other soil samples (p < 0.05). The lowest abundance was observed in the rhizosphere soil of healthy plants (RSH), with a value of 0.0169 copies ng⁻¹ DNA, showing no significant difference from other soil samples (p > 0.05).

The letter represents a significant difference (Tukey HSD test, p < 0.05). Bacterial microbiome data acquisition and analysis

Following quality assessment of the raw sequence libraries, analysis using Illumina-based next-generation sequencing was conducted to detect the V3-V4 regions of bacterial 16 S rRNA genes. In bacterial datasets, trimmed sequences had lengths spanning 400 bp to 500 bp. After undergoing homogenization, 1,481,290 high-quality bacterial 16 S rRNA sequences were grouped into 9,915 unique bacterial Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs). These data have been deposited in NCBI with the accession number PRJNA1308182.

For the ACE and Chao1 indices, diseased soil samples (RSD, ZSD) presented relatively high values. The RSD sample’s ACE and Chao1 were significantly higher than those of healthy soil samples (RSH, ZSH) and the furrow soil sample (FS) (p < 0.05, distinguished by letters). Concerning bacterial sequences, in line with the higher ACE and Chao1 in diseased samples, diseased soil samples showed higher OTUs compared to healthy ones, as reflected by the overall diversity trends.

To investigate the β-diversity of bacterial communities in healthy, diseased, and furrow soils of P. notoginseng, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed (Fig. 3). PC1 and PC2 accounted for 24.35% and 14.92% of the variations in the bacterial communities, respectively. Specifically, Soil samples associated with healthy plants cluster more closely with one another than do soil samples from diseased plants, indicating that disease strongly shapes microbiome composition. This effect is observed not only in the soil closely associated with the root (the rhizosphere) but also in more distal soil compartments that are not directly associated with the root root zone.

Distributions of bacterial communities

A genus-level composite heatmap integrated with hierarchical clustering was generated to assess the taxonomic composition and potential clustering patterns of bacterial communities in P. notoginseng soil samples (Fig. 4). Through hierarchical clustering, it was shown that samples within the same soil type tended to cluster together, which reflects distinct bacterial community structures among different soil groups. The color-coded taxonomic bars further highlighted the differential abundance patterns of key taxa (Sphingomonas, Gemmatimonas), with specific genera exhibiting preferential enrichment in certain soil types. This analysis underscores the pronounced influence of soil type on shaping the bacterial community composition in P. notoginseng soil samples, providing a fundamental perspective on microbial assemblages linked to soil microenvironments.

Comparison of bacterial community characteristics

The differences in microbial communities among various soil types of P. notoginseng were further analyzed using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to identify differentially abundant taxa. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the LDA score was used to quantify the discriminative power of bacterial taxa across groups (RSD, RSH, ZSH, FS, ZSD). Taxa with higher LDA scores (e.g., g_Chthoniobacter, g_Gemmatimonas in FS; g_Ellin6067 in RSH; p_Acidobacteriota in ZSH) exhibited stronger discriminatory ability, suggesting their potential association with specific soil types. Notably, the taxa enriched in RSD (such as o_Pseudomonadales, g_norank_Tepidisphaerales, g_Sphingobium, and g_Rhizobium) and those enriched in ZSD (such as c_Blastocatellia, g_AKYG587, and g_norank_RCP2.54) were distinct from those in other groups and had relatively high LDA scores. These results indicate that the bacterial community structure differs among soil types, and the identified taxa may reflect the differentiation of soil microecology in P. notoginseng fields.

LDA score distribution of microbial taxa across different P. notoginseng soil samples.

Through analyzing the total relative abundances of individual bacterial genera and phyla in all samples, the top 25 dominant genera and phyla were recognized (Fig. 6). At the phylum level, Planctomycetota, Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, Actinomycetota and Gemmatimonadota were dominant phyla in the ZSD and ZSH soil samples (Fig. 6A). In the two groups of rhizosphere soil samples of P. notoginseng, the dominant bacterial phyla with differences are distinct from those in the root zone soil. The differential dominant bacterial phyla in the RSD and RSH soil samples are Acidobacteriota, Planctomycetota, Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Actinomycetota (Fig. 6B). Notably, the abundances of Verrucomicrobiota and Planctomycetota in the two healthy soil samples (ZSH and RSH) are lower than those in the diseased soil samples; the abundances of Actinomycetota and Bacteroidota are higher than those in the diseased soil samples, and Acidobacteriota is more abundant in the healthy rhizosphere soil. The differential dominant bacterial genera in the root zone soil were Sphingobium, Nitrospira, Ramlibacter, RB41, norank_Saccharimonadales, and norank_Rickettsiellaceae (Fig. 6C). The abundances of norank_Saccharimonadales and norank_Rickettsiellaceae in the root zone soil of healthy P. notoginseng plants were higher than those in the root zone soil of diseased plants. The differential dominant bacterial genera in the rhizosphere soil were TRA3-20, Ellin6067, Ramlibacter, UTCFX1, Paludibaculum, and norank_Elusimicrobiota (Fig. 6D). The abundances of TRA3-20, Ellin6067, Paludibaculum, and norank_Elusimicrobiota in the rhizosphere soil of healthy P. notoginseng plants were higher than those in the rhizosphere soil of diseased plants. It is worth noting that, in both root zone soil and rhizosphere soil, Ramlibacter exhibited a higher abundance in the diseased soil compared to that of healthy soil.

Function of soil bacterial community

In this study, PICRUSt2 was used to predict the functional profiles of microbial communities in different groups (RSD, ZSD, RSH, ZSH, FS) based on 16 S rRNA sequencing data. Based on functional classification, the KEGG pathways14 were divided into 6 functional modules and 34 subcategories (Fig. 7, KEGG database, https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html). In the Cellular Processes module, the Cell motility pathway shows differences in abundance among the soil samples, suggesting that functions related to microbial cellular activities exhibit community specificity. In the Environmental Information Processing module, the abundances of the “Signal transduction” and “Membrane transport” pathways in the RSD group were higher than those in the other soil sample groups. In the Genetic Information Processing module, the variation range of abundance among different soil sample groups was small, and no obvious inter-group differences were observed. The abundance of the Human Diseases functional module in P. notoginseng soil samples was low, suggesting that human-pathogenic bacteria are not prevalent in these soils, which is consistent with the safety requirements for P. notoginseng as a medicinal herb. In the Metabolism module, the relative abundances of the “Metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides” and “Biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites” pathways in the RSD soil samples (with relatively high Phytophthora spp. abundance) tended to be lower than those in the healthy soil samples. The RSD group had fewer microorganisms capable of synthesizing anti-oomycete secondary metabolites, which may contribute to its higher abundance of Phytophthora spp. In the Organismal Systems module, the pathway abundance was low, suggesting that the bacterial communities in the P. notoginseng Root-Associated Soils have a weak potential role in organismal system-related functions.

Discussion

Soil-borne diseases triggered by P. cactorum heavily endanger the cultivation of P. notoginseng, a valuable herb in traditional Chinese medicine. The soil microorganism, acting as a core regulator of soil health, is pivotal for mediating plant-pathogen interactions15. In this study, researchers systematically examined Phytophthora abundance, bacterial community composition, and functional features in five different soil types associated with P. notoginseng. The findings revealed critical links between soil microbial communities and pathogen enrichment, providing novel insights into the ecological mechanisms driving P. notoginseng soil-borne diseases and potential strategies for microecological regulation.

The quantitative analysis of Phytophthora spp. revealed significant variations in its abundance across soil types, with the highest level in RSD (rhizosphere soil of diseased plants, 9.3017 copies ng⁻¹ DNA) and the lowest in RSH (rhizosphere soil of healthy plants, 0.0169 copies ng⁻¹ DNA). Under continuous cropping, medicinal plants produce secondary metabolites as a physiological response to abiotic stress. Although the accumulation of these compounds in soil may result in autotoxic effects, disrupt the structure of rhizosphere microbial communities, and ultimately promote the proliferation of pathogenic microorganisms16. A growing body of evidence indicates that under early-stage stress, plants can also recruit beneficial microbes by releasing specific signaling molecules—such as terpenoids and flavonoids—thereby facilitating the establishment of a protective microbial barrier, which represents an important adaptive strategy for plants under stress17. Thus, the tripartite interactions among plants, the rhizosphere microbiome, and pathogens can be viewed as a dynamic equilibrium, wherein plants continuously balance between stress-induced susceptibility and active defense. The ultimate trajectory of this microecological process is shaped by multiple factors, including the concentration and composition of root exudates, the responsiveness of the microbial community, and the prevailing environmental conditions. Specifically for RSD, the high abundance of Phytophthora spp. may be due to the weakened defense capacity of diseased P. notoginseng roots, which reduces the secretion of anti-oomycete substances and creates a pathogen-friendly rhizosphere microenvironment. In contrast, healthy roots (RSH) may release specific metabolites that inhibit Phytophthora proliferation, resulting in the lowest pathogen abundance. Notably, the abundance of Phytophthora spp. in furrow soil (FS) was intermediate between diseased and healthy soils. This may be because FS is not directly influenced by root exudates, resulting in a more stable microbial community structure. This further confirms that the rhizosphere microhabitat is the core zone regulating Phytophthora abundance in P. notoginseng fields.

For α-diversity, the ACE and Chao1 indices of diseased soils (RSD, ZSD) were significantly higher than those of healthy soils (RSH, ZSH) and FS (p < 0.05), while the Shannon index showed no inter-group difference (p > 0.05). However, the unchanged Shannon index suggests that even though the number of species increases, the relative abundance of dominant taxa remains balanced, implying that the bacterial community maintains basic functional stability despite pathogen disturbance. β-diversity analyses consistently showed that bacterial communities of the same soil type clustered distinctly, while different soil types exhibited distinct compositional differences. The clear clustering of samples of the same type further indicates that soil health status is closely related to the bacterial community composition18.

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) identified differentially abundant taxa between soil types. Among these, several phyla and genera exhibited pronounced differences in relative abundance between healthy and diseased soils. Healthy soils (RSH, ZSH) were enriched in Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and genera such as Ellin6067, TRA3-20, and Paludibaculum. These taxa are well-known for their roles in pathogen suppression. Actinomycetota is a major producer of anti-oomycete secondary metabolites, and its higher abundance in healthy soils may enhance the synthesis of terpenoids and polyketides, thereby potentially inhibiting Phytophthora19. Bacteroidota participates in the decomposition of complex organic matter, which may compete with Phytophthora for nutrients and reduce pathogen proliferation20,21. The role of the genus Ellin6067 in agricultural soils has not been reported yet. Its high abundance in RSH likely contributes to the lowest Phytophthora level. In contrast, diseased soils (RSD, ZSD) had higher abundances of Verrucomicrobiota, Planctomycetota, and Ramlibacter. Verrucomicrobiota and Planctomycetota are often associated with nutrient-poor or disturbed environments, and their enrichment may indicate soil degradation caused by Phytophthora infection22,23. Notably, Ramlibacter was more abundant in diseased rhizosphere /root-zone soils than in healthy soils. This genus has been linked to the degradation of plant-derived compounds, and its overgrowth may reflect the increased availability of decaying root material in diseased soils, further promoting Phytophthora colonization24.

KEGG pathway analysis revealed functional differences among groups that may help explain the high Phytophthora abundance in RSD. In the Metabolism module, RSD had lower abundances of “Metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides” and “Biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites” than healthy soils. The reduced abundance of these pathways in RSD suggests a depletion of microorganisms capable of producing anti-oomycete substances, which may contribute to the proliferation of Phytophthora. The Genetic Information Processing module showed minimal inter-group variation, indicating that core life processes are highly conserved across bacterial communities, ensuring basic microbial function regardless of soil health25. Additionally, the low abundance of pathways categorized under “Human Diseases” suggests that human-pathogenic bacteria are not prevalent in these soils, which is consistent with the safety requirements for P. notoginseng as a medicinal herb. This observation aligns with the quality standards for traditional Chinese medicinal materials and supports the ecological safety of P. notoginseng cultivation.

This study provides correlative evidence between bacterial communities and Phytophthora abundance, but several limitations should be acknowledged. It lacks direct functional verification; future studies should isolate candidate beneficial taxa and conduct experiments to confirm their ability to suppress Phytophthora. Future research could explore the development of microecological regulators targeting taxa such as Actinomycetota and Ellin6067 to restore functional balance and potentially control Phytophthora blight of P. notoginseng.

In conclusion, the abundance of Phytophthora spp. in P. notoginseng soils is closely linked to bacterial community structure and function. Soil type (diseased/healthy, rhizosphere/root-zone) is associated with distinct bacterial community clustering, with healthy soils enriched in taxa (Actinomycetota, Ellin6067) that may contribute to pathogen suppression. RSD exhibited reduced abundance of pathways related to anti-oomycete secondary metabolism, which may create a favorable environment for Phytophthora enrichment. The low abundance of human disease-related pathways is consistent with the safety requirements of P. notoginseng as a medicinal herb. These findings highlight the potential of targeting soil microbial communities for the ecological management of P. notoginseng soil-borne diseases.
