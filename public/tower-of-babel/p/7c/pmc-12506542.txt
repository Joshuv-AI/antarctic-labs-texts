Persistent hematuria postkidney transplant due to needle biopsy at the time of kidney retrieval: Successful management with selective renal artery embolization
Authors: Haytham Araibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Persistent hematuria following kidney transplantation is an uncommon yet potentially serious complication with a wide differential, including vascular injury from biopsy procedures performed either before or after implantation. We present a case of persistent hematuria in a kidney transplant recipient, ultimately traced to a pseudoaneurysm resulting from a needle biopsy performed during donor kidney retrieval. Despite initial conservative measures, bleeding persisted and required selective renal artery embolization (RAE), which resulted in complete resolution and preservation of graft function. This report underscores the importance of early recognition, targeted imaging, and timely intervention in preventing graft loss and optimizing patient outcomes.

Introduction

Hematuria is a relatively common finding after kidney transplantation, with reported incidence ranging from 12% to 15% in the early postoperative period [1]. Most cases are transient and self-limiting—often related to urinary catheterization, mucosal irritation, or minor surgical trauma. However, persistent hematuria requires prompt evaluation to avoid missing potentially serious complications, including urinary tract infection (UTI), acute rejection, calculi, malignancy, surgical site hemorrhage, and vascular injury [1,2].

One important but underrecognized cause of persistent hematuria is vascular injury due to renal biopsy. Such injuries—pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, or hematoma—are well documented following postimplantation renal allograft biopsies, with reported vascular complication rates of less than 2% [2,3]. By contrast, vascular injuries resulting from retrieval-time biopsies—performed on donor kidneys before implantation—are rarely reported.

Many transplant centers perform “Quality in Organ Donation” (QUOD) biopsies during procurement to assess histopathology and for research purposes [4]. Although generally considered safe, retrieval biopsies can cause parenchymal or vascular injury, which may remain silent until the graft is reperfused in the recipient [5]. In this setting, the delayed onset of symptoms can obscure the link between biopsy and post-transplant bleeding.

The diagnosis of biopsy-related vascular injury is best achieved through contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or digital subtraction angiography (DSA) [6,7]. Conservative measures may be attempted initially, but persistent bleeding often requires selective renal artery embolization (RAE), a minimally invasive technique with success rates up to 90% in transplant recipients [[8], [9], [10]]. Superselective embolization—targeting only the culprit branch—maximizes parenchymal preservation [11].

We report a unique case of persistent hematuria in a kidney transplant recipient due to a pseudoaneurysm from a retrieval-time QUOD biopsy, successfully managed with superselective RAE. This case emphasizes the need for vigilance for preimplantation vascular injuries and demonstrates an effective management approach.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old man with end-stage renal disease secondary to type 2 diabetes mellitus and long-standing hypertension underwent a preemptive deceased donor kidney transplant. His past medical history was significant for type 2 diabetes mellitus of 15 years’ duration, managed with insulin, hypertension for 20 years on triple therapy, and dyslipidemia treated with statins. He had no history of coagulopathy, malignancy, kidney stones, or prior urological surgery. Family history was negative for renal disease and bleeding disorders.

The donor was a 56-year-old man with controlled hypertension and no significant renal history. At organ retrieval, a QUOD biopsy was taken from the upper pole cortex to assess organ quality. The procurement procedure was otherwise uneventful. The graft was implanted in the recipient’s right iliac fossa with an end-to-side anastomosis of the renal artery to the external iliac artery and the renal vein to the external iliac vein, followed by ureteroneocystostomy. Warm ischemia time was 29 minutes and cold ischemia time 12 hours, with immediate graft reperfusion and urine output.

On postoperative day (POD) 1, the patient developed gross, painless hematuria with clot retention. At that time, his vital signs were stable: blood pressure 138/82 mmHg, heart rate 78 bpm, temperature 36.8°C, and oxygen saturation 98% on room air. Continuous bladder irrigation was initiated. Laboratory studies showed a hemoglobin level of 11.2 g/dL (reference 13.5–17.5), creatinine 1.3 mg/dL (0.7–1.3), platelets 215 × 10⁹/L (150–400), INR 1.0 (0.8–1.2), aPTT 32 seconds (25–35), and fibrinogen 320 mg/dL (200–400). Urinalysis demonstrated numerous red blood cells per high-power field with negative leukocyte esterase and nitrites.

On POD 3, ultrasound of the transplanted kidney demonstrated a well-perfused graft in the right iliac fossa measuring 11.1 cm, with a small hypoechoic perinephric collection (2.4 × 1.4 cm), no hydronephrosis, and good Doppler flow (Fig. 1). On POD 5, cystoscopy revealed a normal urethra, a mildly enlarged prostate, catheter-related mucosal changes, and approximately 200 mL of clot in the bladder, which was evacuated. Active bleeding was seen at the ureteroneocystostomy site. Discussion

Pseudoaneurysm after renal biopsy is an uncommon but well-recognized complication, with reported incidence after postimplantation biopsies of 0.2%-2% [2,3]. It results from arterial wall injury with subsequent blood extravasation contained by surrounding tissue [12]. The vast majority of such reports involve biopsies performed on functioning grafts; complications from retrieval-phase biopsies are rarely described [4,5].

In our case, the pseudoaneurysm likely formed at the time of donor biopsy, but clinical manifestation occurred only after graft implantation and reperfusion in the recipient. This delayed presentation complicates diagnosis, as post-transplant hematuria is more often attributed to infection, rejection, or surgical trauma [1,13].

Diagnosis: Persistent hematuria unresponsive to bladder irrigation should prompt vascular imaging once infection, rejection, and coagulopathy are excluded [6,14]. CT angiography is highly sensitive for pseudoaneurysm, while DSA allows both diagnosis and treatment [7].

Management: RAE is first-line therapy for transplant biopsy-related pseudoaneurysms, with technical success rates of 85%-90% and low complication rates [8,9,15]. Superselective embolization—used here—minimizes loss of functioning renal tissue [10,11]. Comparable cases by Antonopoulos et al. [16] and Sharma et al. [17] report excellent outcomes with preserved graft function, underscoring the efficacy of this approach.

Conclusion

Persistent hematuria in a transplant recipient can rarely result from vascular injury during donor organ retrieval. When conservative measures fail, selective renal artery embolization provides a safe and effective treatment that preserves graft viability. Awareness of this possibility can prevent diagnostic delay and optimize patient outcomes.
