Ideas matter: An analysis of the effects of framing on health system strengthening in Zimbabwe
Authors: Alison T. Mhazo, Charles C. Maponga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealth system strengthening (HSS) has been a well-endorsed concept globally since the 2000s. However, few studies have utilized theory to examine its evolution at the country level and potential implications for policy. This study used framing theory to examine changes in the portrayal of HSS in Zimbabwe from the mid-2000s to the period post-2020.MethodsWe employed a framing analysis methodology encompassing documentary review and key informant interviews.ResultsFour main frames were identified, namely HSS as (i) a donor imperative, (ii) a pathway to resolve crisis, (iii) a strategy for achieving long-term stability and (iv) a foundational investment for a resilient health system. HSS as a remedy for a crisis frame has been the most influential, but the greater involvement of donors in crisis response may have entrenched the mis-framings of HSS as a donor imperative. Donors have shifted from outlining strategies to assigning responsibility for HSS, with framing for more governmental funding. The government’s response to those framing shifts has been more symbolic rather than substantive, undermining health system performance. Recently, donors have started to reduce their funding to align with framing towards more governmental responsibility, resulting in health system shocks in critical areas such as human resources.ConclusionsThe vulnerability and emotional frames that attracted donor funding during peak crisis have lost potency over time. Shifts in framing require proactive action, as policy underreaction can lead to exacerbated problems, missed opportunities for improvement and a loss of public trust in government effectiveness and responsiveness. Nascent, overly futuristic framings such as “resilience” need to be interpreted with caution, as they may cloud the reality that HSS is simply sufficient investment in basic functions. HSS needs to be reframed as a routine, country-owned strategy aimed at improving health system performance rather than a crisis response shaped by donor interventions. Zimbabwe has a rich history of effectively framing events to design and implement pragmatic, domestic -led policies towards HSS, and such latent potential should be tapped into to steer progressive reforms.

Background

The WHO defines health system strengthening (HSS) as “any array of initiatives that improves one or more of the functions of the health systems and that leads to better health through improvements in access, coverage, quality or efficiency” [1]. Since the 2000s, HSS has been a focal priority for both global agencies [2–5] and low- to middle-income countries (LMICs) [6, 7]. However, a central concern has been that the dominant interests of global health institutions may lead to distortions in health priorities in LMICs and inadvertently weaken health systems they purport to strengthen [8–10]. Thus, debates about HSS are often rooted in rational choice theory, which prioritizes interest as the driving force of human action and a determinant of choices. Although the role of institutions and interests [11] in HSS cannot be denied, what is often glossed over is the role of ideas. This is rather a surprising oversight, considering that ideas are central variables in policy-making [12] and that HSS itself is guided by an array of divergent ideational frameworks [13, 14]. HSS is also accompanied by incessant calls for evidence (ideas) to inform approaches [15–18].

Ideational approaches emphasize the role of meaning in shaping choices and thus consider choices to be a result of attempts by actors to understand and interpret the world or framing. Ideas are powerful because they can act as structures, to the extent that the ways in which issues are understood are often taken for granted or as a given. In that context, discussions are not referenced to the ‘first principles'; instead, issues are discussed in reference to debates that have been won and may not seem worth revisiting [19]. Second, ideas provide a common understanding to various actors and therefore provide an opportunity for collective action to address situations. Framing has been shown to be highly consequential in shaping global health policies in general [20]. For example, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) policies have been profoundly changed by the incorporation of human rights and security threat frames [21]. Similarly, framing has been shown to influence how globally conceived norms become policy at the country level [22].

When groups of people who share the same ideas about what they believe is important, such as campaigning for change by protesting, advocating or making speeches, it is called a social movement [23]. Thus, a defining feature of social movements is the consolidation of concerns or shared grievances [24] among diverse actors and the ensuing collective effort to realize social change. Although the term “social movement” is typically used to describe the activities of people who are outside formal institutions, in this study, we use it in relation to the activities of actors within and outside the government as they endeavour to shape HSS. The alignment of HSS with social movements can be traced to the origin of the concept itself. The WHO’s landmark report entitled “Everybody’s business: strengthening health systems to improve health outcomes: WHO’s framework for action” [25] highlighted a serious concern regarding weak health systems (shared grievances) and provided ideas on how various actors should act to strengthen health systems (collective action). That grievance, and the need for concerted action has generally been maintained over the years [26, 27].

Framing theory posits that, regardless of the strength of an idea, prior understandings and perspectives among individuals and institutions are bound to change over time, also known as frame transformation [28, 29]. For example, after many years, with a semblance of settled debates on HSS, the cataclysmic nature of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic fostered a rethink on what HSS exactly entails [30], akin to a ‘revisit to the first principles'. Although HSS has been a dominant idea in Zimbabwe since the mid-2000s, few studies have specifically examined how the framing of the idea has transformed over time and its implications for policy. This paper is divided into the following sections: (i) conceptual framework, (ii) methods, (iii) results, (iv) discussion and (v) conclusion.

Conceptual framework

In their working paper published in 1981, Gamson and Lasch explored the dynamics of political culture in relation to social welfare policy, particularly focusing on US welfare reforms introduced during the 1960s [31]. The premise of the paper is that political discourse surrounding welfare issues draws on a catalogue of available idea elements, and makes use of a variety of symbolic devices to express these ideas. The political discourse on welfare policies is anchored on an interpretive package, which refers to a framework that organizes and presents information about a specific issue or event in a way that shapes how it is understood by the audience. Interpretive packages can be supported or propagated by various organizational sponsors, including political actors, media outlets and social movements, who seek to promote specific narratives or perspective. The portrayal and interpretation of issues is shaped by framing devices, which refers to theoretical approaches that can be used to understand how people think and feel about issues and how they respond to them. Framing devices consist of metaphors, exemplars, catchphrases, depictions and visual images [31]. The devices that provide justifications or reasons for a general position are roots, consequences and appeals to principle. Framework from Gamson and Lasch

Although Gamson and Lasch present welfare policies in the context of governmental programs aimed at protecting the needy, in this study we considered HSS as a social welfare policy that is concerned with improving health outcomes amongst the population. We considered the Gamson and Lasch framework to be suitable for analysing HSS in Zimbabwe for a variety of reasons. Broadly, as mentioned earlier, HSS is largely ideational, which is the analytical basis of the framework. As an idea, HSS is characterized by framings, reasoning and justifications, which aligns with interpretive packages.

Methods

Research design

We conducted qualitative research to analyse the evolution of HSS in Zimbabwe.

Methodology

We conducted a framing analysis via the methodological approach suggested by Linström and Marais [32]. The methodology consists of the following steps: choosing a medium/topic, determining a time frame, drawing a sample, identifying a unit of analysis, selecting a framing typology, operating definitions and identifying the frames.

Choosing a medium

This involves selecting the source of frames. The main documents selected are national health strategies, Global Fund funding requests (formerly proposals) and documents that guide multi-donor pooled funds. These documents were purposively selected on the basis of their authoritative nature in guiding HSS in Zimbabwe. The National Health Strategy is considered an overall blueprint for the health sector, including HSS. Global Fund funding and multi-donor pooled funding strategies were selected because they have contained a standing HSS component since the late 2000s and the early 2010s, respectively. Time frame, sample and unit of analysis

We reviewed HSS-related documents produced from the mid-2000s to 2023. The mid-2000s was chosen as the starting point because it coincides with the period during which the WHO formally introduced the term “HSS” as a guiding principle for health sector development. However, key informants were given the flexibility to share insights into their own experiences prior to the period. Importantly, the reviewed documents highlighted HSS-laden aspects for the period before the mid-2000s, although they were not formally analysed as part of the study. Regarding the unit of analysis, we adopted the guidance that, in written content, “the unit of analysis might be a single word or symbol, a theme, or an entire article or story”. The unit of analysis in our study was HSS.

Framing analysis

Selection of a frame typology

The selection of frames was performed inductively, where frames identified as the research progressed.

Identification of frames

The identification of frames involved analyses of relevant text for “symbolic devices” or “signature elements” that are located within the relevant documents. This was done in three phases. The first phase involved general multiple readings of the documents while taking descriptive notes about the content. The phrase “health system strengthening” and its variants, such as strong health systems or strengthened health systems, were manually searched. The whole section or relevant text was read. We also read the executive summary or country context at the beginning of each document to understand the environment under which HSS was framed. Phase 2 involved a second reading to identify the overall frame in relation to HSS. This involved searching for metaphors, exemplars, catchphrases, depictions and recurring themes. Phase 3 involved in-depth interpretation of the articles to analyse the position that defined HSS through reasoning and justification. This involved an interpretive analysis of the roots, consequences and appeals to principles in relation to HSS. Data from documents were triangulated by insights from key informant interviews.

Key informant interviews

Key informant interviews were conducted between August and October 2022 as part of a larger HSS study in Zimbabwe. National-level key informants (KIs) who were involved or were previously involved in the health sector in Zimbabwe were identified through (1) document review and (2) purposive selection based on authors’ knowledge and experience with the Zimbabwean health sector. A pre-defined sample size for the KIs was not determined. Instead, a concept that is commonly used in qualitative inquiry called saturation was used to assess sample adequacy [33]. Saturation refers to the point where new data collection ceases because no new themes, insights or patterns emerge, indicating a comprehensive understanding of the research topic. Profile of key informants

One author interviewed 17 individuals. All interviews – lasting between 56 and 90 min – were conducted through Zoom®. Informed consent was obtained from all the KIs before the interview. The questions ranged from their understanding of HSS, the reasons it emerged in Zimbabwe, changes in ideas and approaches over time, the actors involved, lessons from COVID-19 and perceptions of how HSS can be improved in the future. Identifying information has been completely masked throughout the manuscript to adhere to strict confidentiality as requested by the key informants. With the KI’s additional expressed consent, the interviews were recorded for transcribing purposes.

Data transcribing

Each pre-recorded oral Zoom® interview was retrieved and converted verbatim into an electronic record of written text. The written text was transferred to Microsoft Word®.

Interview data analysis

The authors separately read the Microsoft Word® transcripts. A Microsoft Word® data charting matrix was used to capture the HSS frames, justifications and reasonings that emerged from each interview. The authors separately analysed the interview output and met through Zoom to triangulate the notes. Areas of discordance were resolved through a focused discussion during the meeting.

Results

We identified four interpretive packages associated with HSS as it evolved from the late 2000s to date, namely, (i) HSS as a donor imperative, (ii) HSS as a pathway to remedy a crisis, (iii) HSS as a pathway for long-term health system development and (iv) HSS as a pathway to build a health sector that can withstand large-scale shocks. HSS as a donor imperative

In line with framing under social movements, by the late 2000s, there was widespread concern among stakeholders (grievance) about the adverse impact of weakened health systems on the performance of HIV/AIDS programs supported by donors, particularly the Global Fund [34]. Although “strengthening the health system” was mentioned in the first ever Global Fund grant proposal for Zimbabwe in 2002 [35], we found that the term HSS was explicitly mentioned for the first time in the country’s Global Fund round 5 proposal in 2005 [36]. However, the framing of HSS under Global Fund proposals differed substantially at the launch of the grants in the early 2000s compared with the late 2000s. At the inception of Global Fund grants, strengthening the health system was framed mainly in relation to creating an enabling environment for the scale-up of specific services for HIV/AIDS, TB and malaria. Moreover, strengthening the health system was framed as one of the “additional outcomes” of Global Fund proposals. However, by 2007, the core framing of HSS had shifted to portray the issue as a remedy for dealing with weaknesses in the health system in which TB, HIV/AIDS and malaria services were delivered [34]. In other words, weak health systems were framed as a major threat to achieving the goals of the Global Fund. The shift in the core framing for HSS had several effects. The first is related to the metaphors and catchphrases associated with HSS. Whereas the initial metaphor portrayed HSS as an “additional outcome”, it had transformed to a “cross-cutting strategy” by the late 2000s. The metaphorical shift signified that HSS was no longer considered an offshoot of Global Fund investments but rather an issue that demanded a systematic approach. This framing transformation meant that the Global Fund was now concerned with specific interventions to address health system weaknesses. To demonstrate that HSS had emerged as a priority, and to ensure the successful implementation of related interventions, an assessment of stakeholders’ knowledge of HSS was conducted as part of the preparation for the Global Fund round 8 proposal. The proposal was the first to frame the issue of HSS in a more comprehensive way, with a clear framework accompanied by relevant justifications and reasoning.

However, certain problems, particularly human resource (HR) shortages, were depicted as more damaging to Global Fund programs than others were, often framed with catchphrases to portray intense severity, such as “crisis” [34]. Several examples were provided to demonstrate how interventions aimed at HR capacity had helped to scale up antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation in public-sector hospitals. Low salaries resulting from a reduction in the budgetary allocation for health were identified as the root of the human resource for health (HRH) crisis, and the consequences are framed beyond the Global Fund programs.“Currently (2007) an estimated 80% of the average 140 new medical graduates leave within the first three years. The human resource crisis, if not addressed, may negate investments in the overall health system, including the fight against HIV and AIDS, TB and Malaria” [34]

“Currently (2007) an estimated 80% of the average 140 new medical graduates leave within the first three years. The human resource crisis, if not addressed, may negate investments in the overall health system, including the fight against HIV and AIDS, TB and Malaria” [34]

The importance of HRH and the implications of the problems associated with the area were also elaborated upon by one key informant (KI):“Without a system that is centering around one of the most important pillars, which is your human resources, you can forget about any meaningful performance of any health programs. Because the human resource is the backbone, and the health sector is labor intensive.”

“Without a system that is centering around one of the most important pillars, which is your human resources, you can forget about any meaningful performance of any health programs. Because the human resource is the backbone, and the health sector is labor intensive.”

As a result of strategic framing, justification and reasoning, HSS emerged as the key area for Global Fund investments in 2007, with a request of over 80 million US dollars (USD) for HSS over 5 years [34]. The request targeted three priority areas as follows: an emergency salary augmentation of USD 68 million to retain and attract critical health workers; USD 9 million for community health systems; and USD 4 million for information systems. Although donor support for HRH was timely in resolving the prevailing crisis, KIs raised the concern that those interventions could not be sustained because the government did not honour its commitment to absorb some of the HRH costs.“In terms of sustainability, there was an agreement that the Global Fund would fund this program in the retention scheme but would then reduce the amount of money they put in by a quarter every year. Therefore, the government would take over in four years fully whatever funding the Global Fund was taking away from human resources. However, this failed, it never truly came to be because the government failed to match what the Global Fund was paying.”

Therefore, the government would take over in four years fully whatever funding the Global Fund was taking away from human resources. HSS as a pathway to resolve crisis

The near collapse of the health sector that peaked in 2008 transformed the framing of HSS as a solution for addressing the sector-wide crisis. The Zimbabwe National Health Strategy (2009–2013) contained a specific section on HSS, with clearly laid out strategies modelled along the WHO’s health system block [37]. The core framing during the period was that HSS provides a conducive environment for the implementation of interventions aimed at resolving the prevailing health system crisis. The position was that, since the health system collapsed, it was futile to implement vertical programmes targeting specific diseases without addressing underlying causes. The cholera outbreak of 2008 and the rapid deterioration in maternal and neonatal and child health (MNCH) indicators were provided as examples to show the extent of the crisis. The strategy clearly described the problems for each block in terms of current status together with historical trends. At the same time, HSS interventions were proposed not only to resolve the crisis but also to aid recovery to historical standards. The roots of the crisis are traced to severe and escalating economic challenges, which resulted in a sharp decrease in funding for social services. However, the consequences of not implementing HSS are framed as catastrophic:“If the overall health system is not ‘strengthened’, there will be a complete collapse across all six blocks.”

“If the overall health system is not ‘strengthened’, there will be a complete collapse across all six blocks.”

The framing of the weak health system as a crisis that could lead to the total collapse of the health sector and the dire consequences that could result if action was not taken portrayed the country as highly vulnerable. This framing provided an appeal to principles for HSS as a moral imperative where joint efforts among the government and external partners were needed to assist the country in “lifting off the ground” [37]. Thus, aided by improvements in the political environment, a multi-donor trust fund, which is typical of fragile and vulnerable contexts known as the Health Transition Fund (HTF) was created. The mechanism was conceived as the major vehicle to implement HSS interventions aimed at aiding post-crisis recovery and preventing a collapse of the health sector. However, the donor imperative had an effect on how HSS was framed. Although the whole health system had collapsed, donors identified high maternal and child mortality as a major problem which HSS interventions under HTF would help address. In the HTF document, Zimbabwe’s exceptional health system performance in the 1980s (framed as one of the best in Africa) and positive maternal health indices during the same period were provided as examples to demonstrate the country’s inherent capacity to rebound. The catchphrase was “transition”, implying that the intended purpose of the mechanism was to bridge the country from a crisis to a more stable situation. There was notable frame alignment between the government and donors based on the problems to which HSS had to respond to. However, the consequences for weak health systems were focused primarily on MNCH indicators under the HTF, with a moral appeal to improve the circumstances of vulnerable populations. Nevertheless, the thematic areas under HTF, such as HRH, health financing and medicines and commodities, sought to address some of the underlying drivers for the weak health system. The architects of HTF generally regarded it as a highly effective mechanism for HSS, which aligned with the sentiments of the KIs for this study.“The intention of the Health Transition Fund was very good because the focus was on family health. Maternal and neonatal outcomes were very poor. Therefore, the HTF came to address this issue in particular. However, they supported the health system at large, although it was a focus on maternal and children’s health.”

“The intention of the Health Transition Fund was very good because the focus was on family health. Maternal and neonatal outcomes were very poor. Therefore, the HTF came to address this issue in particular. However, they supported the health system at large, although it was a focus on maternal and children’s health.”

However, the end-of-HTF evaluation report identified the non-sustainability of HSS interventions as a major shortfall under the mechanism [38]. In an apparent contradiction to the core frame, the report cited the lack of a “transition strategy” to the government and limited local ownership as the root causes of non-sustainability. The report further highlighted that the gains achieved under HTF would be lost in the short term unless external funding was maintained (consequences).

HSS as a pathway for long-term health system development

The post-2015 period coincided with the framing of HSS as a pathway to long-term health system development. The main position amongst external agencies was that more than 5 years after the crisis, the country had stabilized and was in a much better position to address the underlying drivers of health system weakness. The National Health Strategy (2016–2020) highlighted that, over the period 2009 to 2015, the health system had strengthened as a result of increased government expenditure and external assistance. Thus, the catchphrase was to “sustain the gains” achieved during the period 2009–2015. The impacts on HRH and commodity supply were singled out as examples of the gains achieved. However, unlike the 2009–2015 National Health Strategy, which clearly presented HSS as a standalone pillar, the 2016–2020 National Health Strategy outlined HSS-related issues under an all-encompassing enabling environment section [39]. Similarly, HSS was not explicitly framed according to the health system blocks and the synopsis of health system problems, and how HSS was expected to be a solution was not as clearly articulated as in the previous National Health Strategy. The roots and consequences largely remained the same; however, the appeals to principle shifted from HSS as a moral imperative to address a humanitarian emergency towards a strategy to ensure long-term stability in the health sector. In tandem, the donors shifted from the Health Transition Fund to the Health Development Fund (HDF). The core frame under the HDF was that, as the country shifted from a crisis mode, donor financing mechanisms towards HSS should also shift. Of note is that, under HDF, the donors had started to reframe their approach to progressively shift from supporting recurrent costs towards addressing the structural drivers of health system performance. However, the donors acknowledged that, although the crisis had stabilized, the roots for the weak health system had largely been unaddressed, framing the health system as fragile:“The health system remains fragile due to low and uncertain budget allocation from the government, and continued HSS investments are required to avoid a further decline.” [38]

“The health system remains fragile due to low and uncertain budget allocation from the government, and continued HSS investments are required to avoid a further decline.” [38]

In that vein, donor funding under HDF was justified to avoid a return to crisis. Under the HDF, HSS interventions were maintained in critical areas such as HRH and health commodities, which helped to further stabilize the health system. However, similar to HTF, the midterm HDF evaluation report highlighted unsustainability as one of the major gaps for the mechanism [40]. As remarked by one KI, unsustainability was mostly felt in relation to changes in or withdrawal from HR support:“There is a lot of demotivation even with the village health workers, there is demotivation even at head office, because there was staff that was retained by HDF and the demotivation also resulted in poor outcomes and people continuously getting out of the system…. the attrition for nurses was going to as far as 30% to 40%”.

“There is a lot of demotivation even with the village health workers, there is demotivation even at head office, because there was staff that was retained by HDF and the demotivation also resulted in poor outcomes and people continuously getting out of the system…. the attrition for nurses was going to as far as 30% to 40%”.

During the same period, the catchphrase under Global Fund grants shifted from HSS to “resilient and sustainable systems for health” (RSSH) [41]. A core framing persisted that portrayed Global Fund programmes as at risk if the health system environment in which they we implemented remained weak. Although improvements in priority areas such as HRH were noted compared with the pre-crisis period, the health system was depicted as weak enough to justify investments in RSSH. At the same time, the bulk of the Global Fund budget was allocated for the procurement of health products, leaving a large budget shortfall for RSSH. Despite these shortfalls, the number of health workers supported under the Global Fund increased from 24,000 to 25,000, alongside continued funding for supply chain systems, health information, community and laboratory systems.

HSS as a pathway to withstand future shocks

The current period (post-2020) is characterized by framings that portray HSS as a pathway to building a health system that can handle external shocks. Thus, while HSS had been framed in relation to avoiding crises that had been experienced in the past, the post-COVID-19 era framed HSS as a pathway to building a health sector with the ability to handle unknown shocks that could emerge in the future [42, 43]. The overall position is that a strengthened health system is not only capable of providing services under routine conditions but also endowed with the capacity for public health emergency preparedness and response. Thus, the catchphrase “resilient” is invoked as an aspirational depiction of a health system that will withstand future epidemics and pandemics. The National Health Strategy (2021–2025) frames the COVID-19 pandemic as an event that reveals gaps in the health system and is focused on addressing those gaps. Under donor-funded programs, the successor to the HDF is named the Health Resilience Fund(HRF). The Fund, with a budget of approximately USD 90 million, a significant reduction from USD 287 million under the HDF; also conjoins HSS with a new pillar: global health security. Thus, a new framing emerged that links donor support for HSS as a way of helping the country to handle future threats. The Global Fund grant cycle 7 (GC7) application also underscores that RSSH should be linked with specific programmatic challenges and/or priorities for TB, HIV and malaria while contributing to pandemic preparedness [44]. The shift in core framing had a notable influence on how HSS itself is understood as an idea. Whereas previous shifts in framing have strongly influenced HSS approaches, the COVID-19 pandemic has challenged the HSS idea itself. The statement below from the UK, the largest contributor to the HRF, gives credence to those potential shifts:“In the last 10 years, Zimbabwe has seen some significant improvements in maternal and child mortality and wider health outcomes, in part owing to the UK’s longstanding support in this sector. However, this progress has masked serious vulnerabilities in the health system” [43]

“In the last 10 years, Zimbabwe has seen some significant improvements in maternal and child mortality and wider health outcomes, in part owing to the UK’s longstanding support in this sector. However, this progress has masked serious vulnerabilities in the health system” [43]

The framing of HSS in relation to health security was also considered an opportunity to stimulate domestic ownership for HSS and re-examine the role of external assistance. As remarked by one KI:“If you do not take your own national health security seriously, no one will come and sort it out for you…. and no one will save you”.

“If you do not take your own national health security seriously, no one will come and sort it out for you…. and no one will save you”.

However, the pandemic stimulated interest in donors to support broader aspects of the health system. For example, the Global Fund’s COVID-19 response mechanism prioritized HSS areas that had not been traditionally funded through agencies, such as critical and emergency care [45]. Nevertheless, some KIs maintained that such interest should not be conflated with a permanent shift in approach, since it was essentially geared to protect TB, HIV/AIDS and malaria programs. As a result, KIs were less enthusiastic about relying on shifts in donor priorities as a vehicle for HSS, underscoring HSS as the responsibility of the government. They also challenged framings that illuminate a lack of financial resources as the root of the weak health system. Instead, the most recurring sentiment was that “Zimbabwe is rich”, with persistent reframing of the weak health system as a result of non-concerted policy action.

COVID-19 has also accentuated some problems, aiding their appeal as focal priorities for HSS. The Zimbabwe National Health Strategy (2021–2025) identifies strengthening of the laboratory system and surveillance as a key priority for pandemic preparedness and response capacity [46]. This favourable attention is mirrored in the Global Fund GC7 grant , where the country proposed to invest close to USD 4 million of its total grant allocation towards laboratory systems as matching funding under the RSSH innovation fund [44]. However, the GC7 grant and HRF show some disinvestments or shifts in the donor position on HSS. For example, HRH is categorized as scale-down activity for RSSH under the GC7 grant. To that end, the retention scheme targeted close to 7000 staff under GC7 [44]; a significant reduction from the over 25,000 supported in the previous grant [47]. Unlike the large-scale HR support that had characterized previous Global Fund grants, the narrower scope under GC7 was justified on the basis of mitigating high attrition amongst critical health workers only. Similarly, there is scaling down for operational and HR support for the distribution of health commodities, which is framed as a cost-saving shift. In tandem, the HRF shifted the strategy from directly supporting HR retention towards supporting remote and online capacity-building approaches.

Discussion

This study analysed the historical trajectory of the idea of HSS in Zimbabwe for more than 20 years using framing theory; an approach that has been rarely used. This approach is potentially enriching since historical analysis of policies spanning longer time horizons provides nuances into the drivers of policy reform and insights into what may be possible or not in the face of shifting circumstances [48]. This study generated new analytical insights in three areas that can be explored further. Theoretically, it has demonstrated that HSS is amenable to analytical approaches that are rooted in social science theory. This is important, considering that there is undertheorization on the study of health systems in general [49, 50], which generates relatively limited insights into how and why policies are developed and implemented effectively over time, particularly in LMICs [51]. By using framing theory, we have attempted to unpack how and why certain historical interactions led to particular positions on HSS as opposed to mere descriptions of what happened with HSS. The historical lens has also helped to generate new insights into how different contexts favour or constrain HSS in Zimbabwe. That may be useful within the context of learning health systems, which asserts that a system that does not learn from history is condemned to repeat past mistakes [52].

This study highlights the intersection of interests and grievance interpretation in shaping HSS. Consistent with the emerging grievance that weak health systems were a threat to programs funded by global health institutions in the mid-2000s [53], the framing of HSS as a remedy for weak health systems that undermined Global Fund programs was highly consequential in attracting the attention of the agency to the issue in Zimbabwe. In alignment with frame amplification, which involves accenting and highlighting some issues as being more salient than others [23], health system weaknesses that directly threaten Global Fund programs, such as HR, were prominently highlighted, resulting in enhanced attractiveness for funding. In the late 2000s, the grievances shifted towards the impact of weak health systems on maternal and child health. Thus, similar to the Global Fund, the consortium of donors for HTF was instrumental in framing the impact of weak health systems on MNCH as an appeal to action for HSS. This aligns with framing theory, which posits that actors do not automatically pursue their objective material interests but must come to define those interests [54]. In terms of public policy response to problems, HSS was largely introduced under compartmentalized micromanagement, a common approach in which large problems are analysed in small pieces rather than in their totality as complex systems [55]. Under that approach, the intention is to identify specific issues that can be carefully described and more easily managed as opposed to dealing with complex wide-ranging problems that can be intimidating and difficult to grasp. That may help to explain that, despite incessant calls for external agencies to support health systems more broadly, the funding has been heavily tilted towards selected diseases, even in instances where such funding is labelled as HSS [30].

This study has potential policy implications from a framing perspective. First, over the years, Zimbabwe has relied excessively on frames induced by turbulent crises or large-scale crises to mobilize action on HSS. Turbulent crisis has been powerful in mobilizing external funding for HSS since it is normally portrayed as disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations, which invokes moralization frames [20] that drive humanitarian action [56]. However, there has been a crisis with slow and cumulative effects or a creeping crisis [55] over time, which redirected the responsibility of action towards the government. This suggests that, while the grievances over weak health systems remained over time, later framings shifted from defining what needed to be strengthened and why (content of HSS) towards who is responsible for acting and how HSS should be organized (actors, institutions and governance ecosystem for HSS) [57]. However, typical of creeping crises, the policy agenda has failed to accommodate the necessary preventive or preparatory work to handle changes in donor funding. Thus, the policy response from the government can be described as largely symbolic where an issue is framed as a problem without commensurate action to resolve it or a placebo policy [55]. This may explain the health system shocks experienced as erstwhile donors implement sudden shifts in HSS support [30], as has been the case in other countries [58]. From a framing perspective, limited governmental funding alongside overreliance on donors may be emblematic of the relative influence of the initial framing of HSS as a donor imperative, which aligns with the theory of path dependence [59]. The theory of path dependence describes how past events or decisions can constrain future outcomes, meaning that the path a system takes can influence its future trajectory, even if it becomes clear that the initial decisions or choices are no longer tenable. This study has important conceptual and instrumental implications for how HSS should be viewed in contemporary times. Conceptually, HSS itself is under scrutiny, including calls for internal reframing from influential frame sponsors globally. In 2020, the Technical Review Panel of the Global Fund raised concerns that the agency’s past and current RSSH investments continue to primarily inject short-term inputs rather than strengthen health systems [60], alongside explicit recommendations to shift from supporting to strengthening health systems – a shift that was recently endorsed by the Global Fund board [61]. An empirical analysis of recent Global Fund grants also raised the same concern and issued similar recommendations [62]. While in the past such framing has been largely symbolic, the current era suggests substantive action to match those shifts in framing. This may help to explain the substantial reductions in the level of HR support under the Global Fund, HDF and HRF. For a country that relies on the Global Fund for input costs framed as HSS, such as salaries and distribution costs for health products, the country needs to take immediate steps to mitigate the consequences of the emerging shifts. Evidence suggests that domestic actors can proactively act to support smooth transitions [63].

Instrumentally, political leaders and policy-makers should face the reality that, after 20 years of substantial external support to ameliorate turbulent crises [30], those conditions are no longer there to sustain donor interest in HSS. This is in line with framing theory, which posits that, in social movements, grievances are a variable, not a constant [54]. The first step is to reframe HSS as a routine, country-owned strategy to improve health system performance rather than a reactive response to a crisis that is steered by donors. This framing is not new to Zimbabwe, as the country has demonstrated the capacity to break from ideas that predispose it to donor funding as a driver of HSS. For example, in the early 2000s, the HIV/AIDS pandemic was framed as a matter of national security that could not be left to the whims of donors, which led to the creation of the National AIDS Trust, a global exemplar for domestic financing for HIV/AIDS [64]. A similar reframing recurred during the COVID-19 pandemic, leading to another example of domestic financing for vaccine procurement [65].

Emerging catchphrases, such as “resilience”, should also be interpreted with caution, as this may lead to the idea that building a resilient health system involves groundbreaking or futuristic actions when what is needed are radical reforms to fund existing ideas [66]. As underscored in recent critiques, the normative use of the term “resilience” can present a vague metric by which to measure the success of the health system [67]. Notably, there is nothing magical about the concept of health system resilience, as it requires routine funding to strengthen basic health system dimensions such as the workforce, financing, supply chains, data systems and governance [66]. In that sense, resilience should be viewed as a process, not an end state [68]. For example, Zimbabwe’s resilient health system of the 1980s was largely attributed to routine investments in basic functions as part of primary health care [69].

Limitations of the study

This study has limitations worth noting. First, we focused primarily on the influence of framing on HSS. However, every shift in HSS approaches cannot be attributed to changes in framing. Nevertheless, the use of framing theory was helpful in presenting the ideational factors that influence HSS. Further research is needed to understand how other policy determinants, such as power, institutions and interests, can intersect with framing. The second limitation is that studies that explicitly evaluate HSS interventions are not readily available. As a result, the study focused only on the framing of HSS by the government through national health strategies and documents from the Global Fund and pooled donor mechanisms. While these documents are authoritative, they mainly represent the official position of the government and may not provide objective criticism of HSS. To mitigate bias, key informant interviews were conducted to obtain diverse and potentially neutral viewpoints. Further studies are needed to review specific HSS interventions in detail, particularly in areas that have been targeted for HSS for a long time, such as HR and commodity supply chains.

Conclusion

Framing affects how HSS is understood and portrayed in Zimbabwe and the resulting policy positions. Frames that emphasized HSS as a pathway to address or recover from health crises were more powerful in mobilizing action from diverse stakeholders, including external funders. However, overreliance on those frames undermined HSS in Zimbabwe since it favoured policy responses that focused on coping strategies to mitigate and stabilize problems rather than resolving. The shift in emphasis from supporting to strengthening the health system within donor financing mechanisms represents fundamental reframing of donor contributions to health systems, which must be taken seriously by national authorities. This historical analysis of the idea of HSS in Zimbabwe over 20 years contributes knowledge on the contextual drivers of policy reform and provides insights on what may be possible or not in contemporary times.
