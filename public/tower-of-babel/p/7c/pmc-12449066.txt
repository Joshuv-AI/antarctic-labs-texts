Mechanistic and evolutionary insights into a family of aminoacyl-tRNA deacylases that protects against canavanine toxicity
Authors: Juan S Maldonado, Stephanie Sepulveda, Sharva Karthikeyan, Karina T Shirakawa, Ivian Merced, Alexander A Radecki, Jordan Douglas, Wolfgang Peti, Rebecca Page, Oscar Vargas-Rodriguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAminoacyl-tRNA deacylases safeguard the accurate translation of the genetic code by hydrolyzing incorrectly synthesized aminoacyl-tRNAs. Canavanyl-tRNA deacylase (CtdA) was recently shown to protect cells against the toxicity of canavanine (Can), a nonproteinogenic amino acid synthesized and accumulated by leguminous plants. In most organisms, Can is ligated to tRNAArg, causing translation of arginine codons with Can. CtdA prevents Can toxicity by hydrolyzing canavanyl-tRNAArg. Here, we investigated the function, structure, substrate specificity, phylogenetic distribution, and evolution of CtdA. We show that CtdA is essential for preventing Can cytotoxicity in Salmonella enterica, and its heterologous expression can also protect Escherichia coli. By determining the structure of CtdA, we identified its putative binding pocket and residues that modulate enzymatic activity and specificity. We also found that CtdA displays robust specificity for the canavanyl moiety, a feature that contributes to maintaining arginyl-tRNAArg levels unaffected. Finally, we showed that despite their structural homology, CtdA and the aminoacyl-tRNA hydrolytic domain of phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase are functionally and evolutionarily divergent. Collectively, these results substantially expand our understanding of the CtdA family, providing new insights into its structure, function, and evolution. This work also highlights the diverse mechanisms, unique to each organism, that ensure faithful translation of the genetic code.

Introduction

Plants are known to produce and accumulate diverse nonproteinogenic amino acids that share structural and chemical similarities with genetically encoded amino acids. These nonproteinogenic amino acids are often used as chemical defenses against herbivores, pests, or pathogens due to their toxic effects on other organisms [1–3]. The toxicity of plant-sourced nonproteinogenic amino acids frequently stems from their capacity to infiltrate protein synthesis by impersonating genetically encoded amino acids [2, 4–7]. A prominent example is canavanine (Can), an arginine (Arg) analog produced by several legumes, including Jack bean and alfalfa [8]. Ingestion or exposure to Can harms different species, including animals and bacteria [9]. The toxicity is generally caused by the inability of the translation machinery to differentiate between Arg and Can effectively, leading to the replacement of Arg residues with Can in the proteome (Fig. 1A). Can’s distinct chemical characteristics relative to Arg, including a lower isoelectric and pKa of the guanidine group, destabilize protein function, resulting in cellular dysregulation [9].

The entry point of Can into proteins is via tRNA aminoacylation, where arginyl-tRNA synthetase (ArgRS), the enzyme responsible for ligating Arg to tRNAArg, fails to reject Can. Consequently, in vulnerable organisms, Can is attached to tRNAArg, and the resulting Can-tRNAArg product is used by the ribosome to translate Arg codons with Can (Fig. 1A) [9]. To avoid self-harm, Can-producing plants have evolved ArgRSs that effectively discriminate against Can, lowering or preventing mistranslation [10]. Interestingly, Can-discriminating ArgRSs have also been found in insects that feed on seeds of the Can-producer plant Dioclea megacarpa [11, 12]. Conversely, some bacteria have developed an alternative resistance mechanism involving the enzymatic degradation of Can to homoserine and hydroxyguanidine or guanidine [13, 14].

Recently, a new mode of protection against Can was discovered in the bacterium Pseudomonas canavaninivorans. This organism can use Can as a primary source of nitrogen and carbon [15]. Although P.canavaninivorans ArgRS accepts Can, accumulation of Can-tRNAArg is prevented by a canavanyl-tRNAArg deacylase (CtdA), which removes Can from the tRNA (Fig. 1A) [16]. The discovery of CtdA, a single-domain enzyme, revealed the diverse mechanisms that enable adaptation to Can-rich environments. However, despite the significance of the CtdA discovery, existing knowledge of this enzyme family is limited.

In this study, we investigated the evolution, phylogenetic distribution, structure, substrate specificity, and function of the CtdA family. Using diverse bioinformatic approaches, we define the phylogenetic distribution and evolution of CtdA and its functional relationship with the editing domain of phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase (PheRS). We found that CtdA is widely present in bacterial genomes and encoded in some archaea and lower eukaryotic species, making it one of the few examples of free-standing aa-tRNA deacylase families found in all three domains of life. Phylogenetic studies uncovered a complex evolutionary history of CtdA, likely emerging in bacteria and later sporadically transferring to archaea and eukaryotes. We also demonstrated that the bacterial pathogen Salmonella enterica, one of the leading causes of food poisoning globally, encodes a functional CtdA that protects it against Can toxicity. Notably, biochemical assays show that CtdA evolved a robust specificity for Can-tRNAArg that prevents arginyl-tRNAArg deacylation. Moreover, the crystal structure of CtdA revealed the putative substrate-binding pocket and helped to identify key residues that modulate its amino acid specificity and activity. Together, these results significantly expand our understanding of the CtdA family, providing fundamental molecular, biochemical, evolutionary, and functional insights.

Materials and methods

Cloning

The stm2012 gene encoding CtdA (WT or mutants) from S. enterica serovar Typhimurium 14028s was synthesized by Twist Biosciences. The ctdA gene was PCR-amplified with the primer pair 632.F/R and the pEVOL with 633.F/R. For cloning into pRSF, the plasmid was amplified with 596.F/R and ctdA with 595.F/R. For cloning into pRP1b, the plasmid was amplified with 646.F/R and ctdA with 645.F/R. PrimeMax polymerase (Takara) was used for PCR reactions. The DNA fragments were cleaned up and ligated using InFusion (Takara). Escherichiacoli Stellar chemically competent cells (Takara) were used to screen plasmid clones. Sanger DNA sequencing (Quintara Biosciences) was used to confirm clones. Oligonucleotide primers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies. Primer and stm2012 gene sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Bioinformatics analysis

Targeted Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) searches of the genomes of important human bacterial pathogens were performed using the protein sequences of P. canavaninivorans, Rhizobium leguminosarum, and Clostridium perfringens CtdA as queries. Additionally, two independent HMMER searches [17] with the “Reference Proteomes” and default search options were performed using P. canavaninivorans or C. perfringens CtdA sequences as queries. The “significant query matches” for each search were combined. Redundant results were removed together with sequence hits for full-length PheRS β-subunit to avoid misrepresentation of potential genomic CtdA repetition. Additionally, only one representative strain for each species was kept.

To build a phylogeny, the HMMER search results were curated to include samples with only sequences consisting of 180–260 residues, filtering out PheRS sequences and other possible false sequences. The resulting sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.453 [18]. The sample consisted of a taxonomically representative selection of CtdA sequences obtained by randomly selecting up to ten genera per class and one species from each selected genus. These representative sequences were further supplemented with CtdA sequences from six species of interest: R. leguminosarum, P. canavaninivorans, C. perfringens, S. enterica, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterococcus faecium. This sample regime resulted in a dataset containing 226 putative CtdA sequences from across 12 bacterial, 4 eukaryotic, and 3 archaeal phyla. As an outgroup, the sample was supplemented with 19 PheRS B3/B4 domain sequences from 17 phyla across the tree of life (bacteria, archaea, and eukarya), as annotated by AARS Online [19]. These sequences are specified in the supplementary materials.

The phylogeny of the dataset was estimated using BEAST 2.7.6 [20], under an OBAMA site model [21], a gamma spike clock model [22], and a birth-death tree prior [23]. The OBAMA method compares competing substitution models using model averaging, favoring the WAG model [24] with four gamma categories (100% posterior support) in this case. The gamma spike model is a relaxed clock model that accounts for saltative branching (i.e. an accelerated rate of evolution at the time of branching). Saltative branching was strongly favored (100% posterior support), with 15% of all evolutionary changes estimated to abruptly occur at the time of bifurcation, and the remainder occurring in a clock-like fashion along the lineages. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) convergence was diagnosed using the automated stopping MCMC package [25], and the posterior distribution of trees was summarized using the CCD0 method [26]. The BEAST 2 XML file—which contains our datasets, model specifications, and prior distributions—is available as supplementary information.

Salmonella enterica growth assays

The S. enterica serovar Typhimurium 14028s ΔctdA (stm2012) strain [27] was obtained from BEI Resources (catalog number NR-29419). S. enterica serovar Typhimurium 14028s WT was a gift from Drs Michael McClelland and Weiping Chu (University of California, Irvine). Lysogeny-Miller broth (LB)-agar plates were streaked with frozen glycerol stocks of WT and ΔctdA cells and incubated overnight at 37°C. Single colonies were used to inoculate LB media. Cells were grown overnight at 37°C with constant shaking. The overnight cultures were washed twice by centrifuging for 1.5 min at 6000 rpm, removing the supernatant, and resuspending the cells with N-minimal media [5 mM KCl, 7.5 mM (NH4)2SO4, 0.5 mM K2SO4, 1 mM KH2PO4, 100 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.5% glycerol, and 0.1% casamino acids at pH 7.5]. One microliter of washed cells was used to inoculate 99 μl of fresh N-minimal media with or without l-Can (8 mg/ml). Assays with other Arg analogs, homo-Arg, homo-citrulline, and ornithine, were performed with 10 mg/ml. Growth was monitored by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm (OD600, growth) in a 96-well clear plate at 37°C with continuous shaking using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. Except for the results from Fig. 1B and C, the growth assays were performed with the “Use Lid” option selected in the Gen5.13 software as part of the plate reader protocol. The mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) of four biological replicates is displayed. The results were graphed using Prism 10 (GraphPad). l-Can was purchased from Sigma. To demonstrate that the growth phenotype is caused by the addition of Can and not by cell number differences of the overnight seed cultures, the overnight cultures were diluted to an optical density at 600 nm of 1 (measured with a Biochrom Ultrospec 10 Cell Density Meter), and 10 μl were used to inoculate 90 fresh N-minimal media with or without l-Can (10 mg/ml). Growth curves with normalized starting cultures are indicated in the corresponding figure legend.

S. enterica CtdA purification

The pRP1b plasmid harboring the CtdA gene (WT and mutants) was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells (Agilent). A colony was used to inoculate 20-ml LB media containing kanamycin (100 μg/ml), and cells were grown overnight at 37°C. The overnight seed culture was used to inoculate 1-L LB media containing kanamycin. Cells were grown at 37°C to an absorbance at 600 nm of 0.6. CtdA overexpression was induced with 1mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 5 h at 37°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4°C and 6000 × g for 20 min. Cells were resuspended with buffer containing 50 mM Tris–Cl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and Complete ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-free protease inhibitor tablets (Roche). Lysozyme (0.75 mg/ml) was added, and the resuspended cells were incubated for 30 min on ice. The solution was sonicated for 8 min (15 s on and 45 s off cycles). The lysed cells were centrifuged for 45 min at 4°C and 18 000 × g. The lysate was filtered and passed through TALON Ni-NTA resin (Takara), which was pre-washed with lysis buffer. CtdA was eluted with an imidazole gradient and stored in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–Cl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, and 40% glycerol at −20°C. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay.

CtdA expression and purification for crystallography

pRP1b-CtdA was transformed into BL21 (DE3) cells using a standard heat shock transformation protocol. Cells were grown in LB media with selective antibiotic (kanamycin) at 37°C to an OD600 of 0.6. The cultures were cooled to 18°C before protein expression was induced by the addition of 1 mM IPTG. The cells were harvested 16 h later by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 10 min. The cell pellets were stored at −80°C until purification. Cell pellets were resuspended in ice-cold lysis buffer [50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, 0.1% Triton X-100, and an EDTA-free protease inhibitor tablet (Thermo Fisher Scientific)] and lysed using high-pressure homogenization (Avestin EmulsiFlex C3). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation (45 000 × g, 45 min, and 4°C), and the supernatant was loaded onto a Histrap column (Cytivia) preequilibrated with Buffer A (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 5 mM imidazole). The column was washed with Buffer A, and the protein was eluted with a gradient of Buffer B (50 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 500 mM imidazole). Fractions with CtdA were pooled and dialyzed overnight with TEV protease (in-house) in dialysis Buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine) to cleave the His6-tag. The tag-free CtdA protein was subjected to subtraction Ni2+-NTA purification, concentrated, and purified using size exclusion chromatography (SEC; Superdex 75 26/60) using SEC buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM TCEP). CtdA fractions were pooled and concentrated to 37.5 mg/ml.

CtdA crystallization and structure determination

Crystals of CtdA were grown using the vapor diffusion sitting drop method in Intelli Plate 96–3 well (Art Robbins) plates in 0.2 M ammonium citrate dibasic, 20% polyethylene glycol (PEG) 3350, and CtdA at 35 mg/ml. X-ray diffraction data were collected using a Bruker Venture D8 system (IμS Diamond source) with a Photon III M14 detector and cold stream, and the data processed using Proteome (Bruker). The dataset of CtdA was phased using molecular replacement with the coordinates of CtdA generated by AlphaFold3 as a search model [29, 30]. The structure was completed using multiple cycles of manual building and refinement using WinCoot and Phenix Refine [30, 31], respectively. Mass photometry

Mass photometry experiments were performed using the Refeyn TwoMP instrument (Refeyn), following the manufacturer’s instructions in SEC buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM TCEP). CtdA samples were diluted to 5 nM in SEC buffer. Data were acquired for 1 min (AcquireMP software, v2024.R2.1). Molecular weight calibrations were performed using solutions of β-amylase (56, 112, and 224 kDa) and bovine serum albumin (66 kDa). Data analysis and figure generation were performed using the DiscoverMP software (v.2024.R2.1, Refeyn).

Structural modeling and analyses

Docking models were generated using the “Structure-Based Modeling Support” in ProteinsPlus (https://proteins.plus) [32]. First, potential binding pockets were identified using the DoGSite3 suite. The predicted pocket consisting of S80, L84, T104, N105, V115, G116, N170, W171, R172, and Q173 was used for docking l-Can or l-Arg using the JAMDA suite with default options selected. The ten resulting models with the best scores were individually analyzed. Only the models displaying the amino acid side chain oriented into the protein binding pocket were considered. The CtdA models with Can and Arg were superimposed using the Matchmaker tool in Chimera X (v.1.8) [33]. The Matchmaker tool in ChimeraX was also used for the structural alignment of E. coli PheRS (PDB ID: 3PCO) [34] and CtdA using all the Cα atoms.

ArgRS purification

E.coli ArgRS was purified as previously described [35]. Briefly, E. coli cells from the ASKA collection harboring the expression plasmid for the argS gene [36] were grown in LB media with Cm to an absorbance at 600 nm of 0.4, followed by induction of argS overexpression with 0.2 mM IPTG overnight at 25°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4°C and 6000 × g. Cells were resuspended with buffer containing 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8), 300 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and protease inhibitor tablets. Lysozyme (0.75 mg/ml) was added, and the resuspended cells were incubated for 30 min on ice. The solution was sonicated for 8 min (15-s on and 45-s off cycles). The lysed cells were centrifuged for 60 min at 4°C and 16 000 × g. The lysate was filtered and passed through a Ni-NTA column, pre-washed with lysis buffer. ArgRS was eluted with an imidazole gradient and stored in a buffer containing 20 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8), 100 mM NaCl, and 40% glycerol.

Aminoacylation assays

E.coli tRNAArg (argU), which is 96% identical to S. enterica tRNAArg UCU (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B), was transcribed in vitro and purified as previously described [35, 37, 38]. tRNA was radiolabeled at the 3′-end adenosine with 32P, as described before [39]. Residual Arg bound during ArgRS purification was removed as previously described [16]. Briefly, 20 μM ArgRS was incubated with 20 μM tRNAArg in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 4 mM ATP, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 0.1 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 20 min at 37°C. The reaction was quenched with 2 M urea and 1 M NaCl. ArgRS was repurified using TALON resin, as described above. Aminoacylation assays were performed in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 4 mM ATP, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mg/ml BSA, 3 μM tRNAArg with a trace amount of 32P-labeled tRNAArg, 0.5 μM ArgRS, Can (0.5 or 5 mM) or Arg (5mM), and CtdA (concentrations are indicated in the corresponding figure legend). The reactions were initiated by adding ArgRS and incubated at 37°C. At the indicated times, 2 μl of the reaction solution was mixed with 5 μl 0.1 units/μl P1 nuclease (Sigma) in 200 mM sodium acetate, pH 4.5. The quenched time points were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. One microliter of the quenched solution was spotted on cellulose thin-layer chromatography (TLC) plates (EMD Millipore). The plates were run in 0.1 M ammonium acetate and 5% acetic acid to separate aminoacyl-A76 from A76. The TLC plates were air-dried, and the radioactive products were detected by autoradiography using a Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems) and quantified using ImageJ [40]. The results were graphed using Prism 10 (GraphPad).

Cells were grown in LB media to an OD600 of 0.4, followed by induction of tufA overexpression with 0.25 mM IPTG overnight at 25°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4°C and 6000 × g. The lysate was filtered and passed through a Ni-NTA column, pre-washed with 100 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol. Deacylation assays

Arg-tRNAArg was prepared by incubating 1 mM Arg, 8 μM tRNAArg (with a trace amount of 32P-labeled tRNAArg), and 5 μM ArgRS in a solution containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 4 mM ATP, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 0.1 mg/ml BSA, and 0.2 units inorganic pyrophosphatase (New England Biolabs). The reaction was incubated at room temperature for 1.5 h and quenched with 0.3% acetic acid. Can-tRNAArg was prepared by incubating 0.5 mM Can, 10 μM tRNAArg (with a trace amount of 32P-labeled tRNAArg), and 1 μM ArgRS in a solution containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 4 mM ATP, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 0.1 mg/ml BSA, and 0.2 units inorganic pyrophosphatase. The reaction was incubated at 37°C for 10 min and quenched by adding 1 volume of phenol (pH 4.5). Aminoacylated-tRNAs were phenol-extracted, followed by ethanol precipitation, resuspended in diethylpyrocarbonate-treated water, and stored at −80°C. Deacylation assays were performed in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 0.1 mg/ml BSA with the indicated CtdA concentration. ArgRS or “activated” EF-Tu were added when indicated. The reactions were initiated by adding the aminoacyl-tRNA substrate. Can-tRNA deacylations were performed at room temperature unless noted otherwise, while Arg-tRNA deacylations were carried out at 30 or 37°C. The reaction course was monitored by mixing 2 μl of reaction solution with 5 μl 0.1 units/μl P1 nuclease (Sigma) in 200 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.5) or 1 μl of reaction solution with 2.5 μl 2 units/μl S1 nuclease in 1× S1 digestion buffer (Thermo Scientific) and 200 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.5). The plates were run in 0.1 M ammonium acetate and 5% acetic acid to separate aminoacyl-A76 from A76. The TLC plates were air-dried, and the radioactive products were detected by autoradiography using a Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems) and quantified using ImageJ [40]. The results were graphed using Prism 10 (GraphPad).

Results

Salmonella enterica is resistant to Can

We surveyed the genomes of 65 human pathogens of considerable public health interest to identify CtdA genes [43]. This survey identified genes with high sequence homology to P. canavaninivorans CtdA in the core genome of 15 of the analyzed bacteria, including S. enterica, P. aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and E. faecium (Supplementary Table S2). To test if the presence of the predicted CtdA genes in these species confers resistance to Can, we used S. enterica as a model organism. The growth of S. enterica was compared in media with or without Can. Growth curves showed that the presence of Can only minimally slowed S. enterica growth (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S2A and B). Next, we used a ctdA knockout strain of S. enterica (ΔctdA) [27] to directly test the role of CtdA in Can resistance. Notably, in contrast to WT cells, the S. enterica ΔctdA cells did not grow in Can-supplemented media (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S2B). To confirm that the lack of CtdA is responsible for the sensitivity of the ΔctdA strain to Can, we complemented the ΔctdA cells with a plasmid harboring the S. enterica ctdA gene. The expression of plasmid-borne CtdA rescued the S. enterica ΔctdA strain’s resistance to Can (Fig. 1D and E and Supplementary Fig. S2C and D). No effect was observed for overexpression of CtdA in S. enterica WT cells (Supplementary Fig. S2E and F). We also tested if other Arg analogs have similar toxic effects as Can in the ΔctdA strain. S. enterica WT and ΔctdA cells were cultured with the established Arg metabolites, homo-Arg, homo-citrulline, or ornithine (Supplementary Fig. S3A). However, only Can inhibited S. enterica ΔctdA growth (Supplementary Fig. S3B–H). Together, these results demonstrate that S. enterica encodes a functional CtdA that protects against Can toxicity.

S. enterica CtdA protects E. coli against Can toxicity

Unlike S. enterica, E. coli is sensitive to Can due to its misincorporation into proteins [44]. To test if heterologous expression of S. enterica CtdA could endow E. coli cells with resistance to Can, we transformed either an empty plasmid or a plasmid harboring the S. enterica ctdA gene into E. coli, and grew the cells with or without Can. E.coli cells carrying the empty plasmid grew robustly in the absence of Can but did not grow in the presence of Can (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S4). In contrast, cells expressing CtdA and exposed to Can grew at the same rate as those grown without Can (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. S4). These data further demonstrate that S. enterica CtdA is a Can detoxifying factor. Moreover, the ability of S. enterica CtdA to support E. coli growth in the presence of Can facilitates the functional characterization of CtdA genes from other microbial pathogens in a biosafety level 1 laboratory environment.

S. enterica CtdA exhibits robust canavanyl-tRNA deacylation activity

To test if S. enterica CtdA is a bona fide canavanyl-tRNA deacylase, we recombinantly purified CtdA and tested its enzymatic activity using Can-tRNAArg, prepared using in vitro transcribed tRNAArg and purified ArgRS. A previous study showed that the acyl linkage of Can-tRNAArg is significantly less stable than that of Arg-tRNAArg [10]. Likewise, we found that Can-tRNAArg is rapidly spontaneously hydrolyzed at room temperature (∼25°C) and 37°C relative to Arg-tRNAArg (Supplementary Fig. S5). Because the spontaneous Can-tRNAArg hydrolysis was markedly faster at 37°C, we tested the Can-tRNA deacylation activity of CtdA at room temperature (22–25°C). The deacylation assays showed that CtdA efficiently hydrolyzes Can-tRNAArg, even at low enzyme concentrations (Fig. 2A). To more accurately reflect the cellular context in which CtdA functions (i.e. CtdA must readily recognize and deacylate Can-tRNAArg as is synthesized by ArgRS), we used aminoacylation assays to test if S. enterica CtdA prevents the accumulation of Can-tRNAArg. While ArgRS efficiently synthesized Can-tRNAArg, Can-tRNAArg accumulation waned when exposed to low concentrations of CtdA (5 nM) and was entirely depleted at higher concentrations (>100 nM) (Fig. 2B). Conversely, Arg-tRNAArg synthesis by ArgRS was unaffected even in the presence of an elevated CtdA concentration (2 μM) (Fig. 2C). These results demonstrate that S. enterica CtdA is a canavanyl-tRNA deacylase.

CtdA adopts the B3/B4 fold of PheRS’s editing domain

To investigate the molecular basis of CtdA substrate recognition and catalysis, we determined the crystal structure of S. enterica CtdA. In agreement with SEC and mass photometry analyses, the oligomerization of S. enterica CtdA is dimeric with the active sites of each protomer fully accessible (Supplementary Fig. S6A–D). The S. enterica CtdA protomer is a mixed α/β protein that adopts a B3/B4 fold and is nearly identical to the CtdA structure recently determined from P. canavaninivorans (PDBID 9f7e) (RMSD, 0.6 Å; Supplementary Fig. S6E) [45]. A DALI structural similarity search identified S. enterica CtdA to exhibit the next highest structural similarity to the B3/B4 domain of E. coli and Thermus thermophilus PheRS (PDBID 6p26/3hfz; Dali Z-score, 15.0/11.3; root mean square difference 2.6/2.8 Å2; sequence identity, 14%/14%, respectively) (Fig. 3A–C) [46, 47]. The PheRS B3/B4 domain, also referred to as the PheRS editing domain, is an aa-tRNA deacylase that selectively hydrolyzes tRNAPhe incorrectly acylated with Tyr or meta-Tyr (mTyr) [28, 48]. While the secondary structural elements that define the B3 and B4 domains are conserved between CtdA and PheRS, their topologies differ (Fig. 3D) [45]. This is because CtdA and PheRS contain inserts (indels) that are specific to each protein, with the CtdA indel comprising α-helices α2–α4 (28–92, 193–200) and the PheRS indel comprising α-helices α1, α4, and α5 (220–240, 338–368, E. coli), as similarly reporter recently [45]. In PheRS, these helices mediate intra- and inter-protein interactions with other PheRS domains, including its B1/2 domain (helix α2), its B5 domain (helices α4, α5), and its A subunit (helices α4, α5) (Fig. 3E). These additional domains are not present in CtdA, likely explaining the lack of conservation in this region.

As expected based on their structural similarities, a cavity analysis shows that the putative aminoacyl binding site of S. enterica CtdA is likely equivalent to that experimentally determined for Tyr in the B3/B4 domain of PheRS, namely at the intersection of the B3, B4 and indel domains (Supplementary Fig. S7) [49–51]. Consistent with this analysis, the base of the putative binding pocket is positively charged, as observed in PheRS (Fig. 3F). Additionally, docking modeling showed that Can is readily accommodated in CtdA’s putative binding pocket and binds in a manner nearly identical to that observed for Tyr in PheRS (Fig. 3F and G). The aminoacyl binding site is defined by several residues, including N105, G116, E118, C166, Q173, and I189. These residues are highly conserved among CtdAs from different species (Supplementary Fig. S8), supporting a putative functional role in substrate recognition and catalysis.

Functional and molecular relationship of CtdA and PheRS editing domain

Beyond the annotation of CtdA genes and their structural homology, little is known about the relationship between CtdA and the B3/B4 editing domain of PheRS, especially regarding the extent to which their activities are shared. To address this, we tested if CtdA can replace the mTyr-tRNAPhe deacylation activity of PheRS, thereby preventing the erroneous translation of Phe codons with mTyr [28]. We used an E. coli strain carrying a single-residue chromosomal mutation of Gly318 to Trp (G318W) in the pheT gene (PheRS β-subunit) [28]. The G318W mutation is located in the PheRS B3/B4 domain and disables its mTyr-tRNAPhe deacylation activity, rendering the E. coli mutant strain sensitive to mTyr [28]. To determine if CtdA can rescue the E. coli G318W sensitivity to mTyr, cells were transformed with a plasmid encoding S. enterica CtdA, and growth was monitored in cultures with or without mTyr (Fig. 4A). The assays showed that CtdA expression did not protect the cells against mTyr toxicity (Fig. 4B), indicating that, despite their structural homology, CtdA and the B3/B4 domain do not share functional activities.

We also leveraged our comparative structural analyses of the S. enterica CtdA and PheRS to gain a more detailed understanding of the structural differences that led to their divergent functional evolution (Figs 3C and 4C, and Supplementary Fig. S9). We found low conservation of active site residues between the two enzymes, with stark differences at key positions. Except for a shared Asn residue (N105 in CtdA), residues implicated in the E. coli PheRS B3/B4 domain substrate specificity and catalytic activity (R244, N254, Y255, H265, F267, A312, G318, and E334) have different identities in CtdA (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. S9) [49]. G318, one of the most consequential residues for the PheRS B3/B4 domain, structurally aligns with CtdA Q173. As described above, the G318 is essential for the function of the B3/B4 domain (Fig. 4B). Substitution of G318 with Trp reduces the binding pocket size, preventing the proper accommodation of the mTyr/Tyr moiety [28, 49]. Based on our docking model, the side chain of CtdA Q173 may interact with Can’s side chain (Fig. 3G). To determine the role of Q173 in CtdA, we generated a CtdA Q173G mutant and tested its ability to rescue E. coli cells from Can toxicity. The Q173G mutation renders CtdA inactive (Fig. 4D), possibly due to the enlargement of its binding pocket. Another interesting difference between the two binding pockets is the identity of the residue on the opposite side of G318 in the B3/B4 domain and Q173 in CtdA. A His at position 265 in the B3/B4 domain aligns with G116 of CtdA, which may compensate for creating the appropriate space to accommodate Can in CtdA’s binding pocket. Consistent with this hypothesis, replacing G116 with His or Trp inactivated CtdA (Fig. 4D). We also tested the CtdA double-mutant G116H/Q173G, aiming to recreate the B3/B4 domain’s binding pocket. This CtdA mutant was also functionally defective. The only shared residue with the PheRS B3/B4 domain is CtdA’s N105. The N254 in the B3/B4 domain aids the positioning of a catalytic water molecule responsible for the nucleophilic attack on the ester bond that cleaves the amino acid from tRNA [49]. However, a B3/B4 domain N254A mutant retains almost complete enzymatic activity [49]. N105 is also predicted to be close to the carboxylic group of the amino acid substrate, as observed in our docking models (Fig. 3F and G) [45]. The lack of activity of an N105A mutant supports the essential role of N105 in the CtdA (Fig. 4D). Outside the binding pocket, we focused on A95. This residue structurally and sequentially aligns with R244 of the B3/B4 domain (Fig. 4C). PheRS B3/B4 domain R244 is predicted to stabilize the tRNA via interaction with the phosphodiester backbone of the C75 nucleotide [49, 52]. A B3/B4 domain R244A variant is almost 3-fold less active than the WT enzyme [49]. Whether A95 plays a similar function in CtdA is unknown. However, an A95-to-R mutation did not substantially alter CtdA activity (Fig. 4D).

Lastly, we explored the possibility that the mutations in CtdA could change its specificity towards mTyr, particularly because most of the mutations were made in the context of the PheRS B3/B4 domain. Additionally, although the mutations inactivated CtdA’s function against Can, the lack of activity may be caused by a change in substrate specificity rather than a loss of catalytic activity, as these residues may be involved in substrate recognition but not in catalysis. We tested if any of the CtdA mutants could rescue the sensitivity of E. coli G318W cells to mTyr. However, none of the CtdA mutants supported E. coli G318W growth in mTyr-supplemented media (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S10), suggesting that these mutations do not change the specificity towards mTyr-tRNA.

CtdA exhibits canavanyl-tRNA selectivity

The observation that CtdA does not impact Arg-tRNAArg synthesis (Fig. 2C) suggests that CtdA distinguishes between Arg- and Can-tRNA, as previously proposed [16]. Because the activity of CtdA towards Arg-tRNA has not been directly examined, we assayed CtdA’s specificity using deacylation assays with Arg-tRNAArg. In contrast to the robust deacylation of Can-tRNA (Fig. 2A), CtdA did not hydrolyze Arg-tRNAArg at low enzyme concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S11), underscoring the substrate selectivity of S. enterica CtdA. Deacylation of Arg-tRNAArg was only observed at high CtdA concentrations (>1 μM) (Fig. 5A). Even at these elevated concentrations, Arg-tRNAArg deacylation was weaker than for Can-tRNAArg. Additionally, we examined the possibility that ArgRS protects Arg-tRNAArg from CtdA-mediated deacylation by forming an ArgRS:Arg-tRNAArg complex. The reactions with or without ArgRS were initiated by adding Arg-tRNAArg to avoid preemptive interaction with either enzyme. The deacylation data revealed that CtdA does not deacylate Arg-tRNAArg in the presence of ArgRS, while the addition of 5× higher concentrations of tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase did not prevent Arg-tRNAArg hydrolysis by CtdA (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, the Arg-tRNAArg concentration in the reaction slightly increased when ArgRS was added, which we attribute to the aminoacylation of free tRNAArg and residual Arg and/or ATP and Arg-adenylate bound to the purified ArgRS or from the Arg-tRNAArg preparation. Finally, we tested if elongation factor Tu (EF-Tu) also protects Arg-tRNAArg from CtdA-catalyzed hydrolysis. EF-Tu transports aa-tRNAs to the ribosome and has been shown to protect aa-tRNA substrates from the hydrolysis of other aminoacyl-tRNA deacylases [42, 53, 54]. Deacylation assays with CtdA and varying concentrations of EF-Tu showed that Arg-tRNAArg hydrolysis by CtdA is prevented by EF-Tu (Fig. 5C). In contrast, EF-Tu did not prevent CtdA-mediated deacylation of Can-tRNAArg even at a 10 000-fold higher concentration (Fig. 5D). Together, these results show how the specificity of CtdA, in collaboration with ArgRS and EF-Tu, ensures that only Can-tRNAArg is effectively hydrolyzed, while preventing Arg-tRNAArg hydrolysis (Fig. 5E).

Molecular determinants of CtdA’s specificity

Next, we sought to understand how CtdA distinguishes between Can- and Arg-tRNAArg. We created a docking model of CtdA with Arg and superimposed it to the model with Can docked (Fig. 6A). The structural comparison showed that both amino acids can be almost identically accommodated in the CtdA’s aminoacyl binding pocket, which may be expected given their structural similarities. The fitting of Arg in the cavity may explain the deacylation of Arg-tRNAArg by CtdA, but not the stark difference in hydrolytic efficiency relative to Can-tRNAArg (Figs 2A and 5A). Thus, we examined residues that may interact with the oxa position of Can’s side chain, which may determine CtdA’s specificity. Cys166 was of particular interest because of the proximity of its sulfhydryl group to Can’s side chain and its strict evolutionary conservation (Fig. 6A and B). To investigate its role, we created three CtdA mutants (C166S, C166A, and C166G), intending to introduce distinct physicochemical and size characteristics at this position. We tested the activity of the three CtdA mutants using our plasmid-based E. coli platform (Fig. 1F and G). Expression of the CtdA mutants did not affect cell growth in the absence of Can (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S12A). In the presence of Can, the expression of CtdA C166S and C166A variants endowed E. coli cells with similar growth tolerance to Can relative to CtdA WT (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S12B). In contrast, cells harboring the CtdA C166G mutant were significantly sensitive to Can. Consistent with this, growth inhibition by Can was also observed in S. enterica ΔctdA cells expressing the plasmid-encoded CtdA C166G (Supplementary Fig. S2D). To validate their activities, we purified the CtdA mutants and tested their enzymatic activities. In agreement with our cell-based assays, CtdA C166A and C166S prevented Can-tRNAArg accumulation in aminoacylation assays with similar efficiency compared to the WT enzyme, while the C166G variant did not (Fig. 6E). Deacylation assays showed a similar pattern of Can-tRNAArg hydrolysis by the CtdA mutants relative to the WT enzyme (Fig. 6F). However, CtdA C166S exhibited weaker activity compared to the WT and C166A enzymes. Next, we probed the role of C166 in CtdA specificity using deacylation assays with Arg-tRNAArg. Notably, in contrast to its activity against Can-tRNAArg (Fig. 6E and F), the C166A mutant did not deacylate Arg-tRNAArg, while the C166S mutant effectively hydrolyzed Arg-tRNAArg (Fig. 6G). The C166G mutant also lacked activity against this substrate.

The side chain of Q173 also orients towards Can’s oxa group, albeit from a different angle (Figs 3G and 6A). To test the role of Q173 in CtdA activity, we created a CtdA Q173G mutant. E. coli cells expressing CtdA Q173G were sensitive to Can (Fig. 4D). Similarly, the CtdA mutant also showed no deacylation activity against Can- or Arg-tRNA (Fig. 6E–G), indicating that Q173 is necessary for CtdA activity. In contrast to C166, Q173 is not strictly conserved (Fig. 6B). In C. perfringens CtdA, a Glu residue occupies the corresponding position, indicating that this structural difference is catalytically tolerated [16]. Together, these results support the predicted aminoacyl binding pocket of CtdA and reveal the role of C166 in catalysis and substrate recognition. The data indicate that the residue size at position 166 is important for catalysis, while its chemistry modulates specificity (Fig. 6H).

Phylogenetic distribution of CtdA

The first CtdA genes were initially reported in P. canavaninivorans, R. legumonisarum, and C. perfringens [16], and a more recent study reported six additional bacteria with putative CtdA genes [45]. Our targeted genomic searches also identified several pathogenic bacteria with CtdA genes (Supplementary Table S2). However, the overall occurrence of CtdA in nature is unknown. To determine the phylogenetic distribution of CtdA, we performed HMMER homology searches [17] using the sequences of P. canavaninivorans and C. perfringens CtdA as queries. These two sequences were chosen due to their low sequence similarity (∼31%), which we expected to aid in identifying diverse CtdA and CtdA-like genes more broadly across genomes. Our searches uncovered a widespread distribution of putative CtdA genes in bacteria (Supplementary Table S2). Interestingly, we also identified putative CtdA genes in archaea and eukaryotes, although they were found more sparsely. In total, 1249 organisms with reference proteomes [55] encoding CtdA were identified, corresponding to 1113 (89%) bacteria, 94 (7.5%) eukaryotes, and 42 (3%) archaea (Supplementary Table S2). In bacteria, CtdA is primarily present in Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria (Supplementary Fig. S13). In eukarya, CtdA is predominantly found in fungi belonging to the Ascomycota phylum, while in archaea, it is present in species from the Euryarchaeota and Crenarchaeota phyla (Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. S13).

The phylogeny and evolution of the CtdA family

We performed a phylogenetic analysis to gain a better understanding of the relationship and evolution of the CtdA family. Representative PheRS B3/B4 domains from the three domains of life [19] were also included. The analysis revealed two distinct CtdA clades (Fig. 7A). The larger clade, which we named CtdA-1, comprises most bacterial and all the eukaryotic representative CtdA sequences, including those from S. enterica, P. canavaninivorans, C. perfringens, and R. leguminosarum. The second clade, which we named CtdA-2, consists of CtdA genes from primarily two bacterial groups (Chloroflexi and Bacillota) and all the archaeal species. The phylogenetic divergence of the CtdA-1 and CtdA-2 clades is reflected by their low sequence homology, with an ∼20% sequence identity. Notably, while some key residues we showed to be functionally important in S. enterica CtdA, such as N105 and G116, are conserved, other feature residues are not (Supplementary Fig. S14). The most noticeable difference corresponds to S. enterica CtdA’s C166 residue, which is missing in bacterial CtdA-2 and corresponds to a residue with a distinct identity in archaeal CtdA-2. Given the key role of C166 in the function and specificity of S. enterica CtdA (Fig. 6), CtdA-2 genes may have a distinct biological function from CtdA-1.

Evolution of CtdA. (A) The phylogenetic tree summarizes the distribution of CtdA in organisms from all domains of life, revealing the evolution of CtdA into two distinct clades: CtdA-1 and CtdA-2. Only members of the CtdA-1 clade have been characterized. Branch lengths are in units of amino acid substitutions per site. (B) An evolutionary model of CtdA. This model suggests that CtdA initially emerged from a duplication of a bacterial B3/B4-like domain, resulting in CtdA-1 proteins. A second duplication event involving bacterial CtdA-1 led to the evolution of CtdA-2 (although it is also possible that CtdA-2 may be ancestral to CtdA-1). After their appearance, CtdA-1 and CtdA-2 have been horizontally transferred to eukaryotic and archaeal species, respectively. Whether the CtdA emerged from a free-standing B3/B4 protein or the extant PheRS B3/B4 domain is unknown.

The identification of CtdA-2 proteins indicates that the evolution of CtdA involves different instances of horizontal gene transfer. A possible scenario is that the CtdA family evolved from a B3/B4-like domain (possibly with Tyr/mTyr-tRNA hydrolytic activity), present at the time of the last universal common ancestor (Fig. 7B). Duplication and functional diversification of the B3/B4-like domain led to the emergence of the CtdA-1 group in bacteria. It is unclear if the B3/B4 domain from which CtdA emerged was a free-standing homolog or the extant B3/B4 domain of PheRS. Members of the CtdA-1 clade were later horizontally transferred to lower eukaryotes in at least two instances. A second duplication event prompted the emergence of CtdA-2, which was later transferred to archaeal species and may have acquired a different function.

Discussion

In this work, we sought to fill important knowledge gaps about the recently discovered CtdA family. Using computational approaches, we identified putative CtdA genes in over a thousand species. While predominantly found in bacteria, CtdA genes were identified in several eukaryotic and archaeal organisms. Given its phylogenetic distribution, CtdA joins a small group of standalone aa-tRNA deacylase families (ProXp-ala, AlaX, and d-aminoacyl-tRNA deacylases) with presence in the three domains of life [56]. We also found that the CtdA family has evolved into two distinct groups, which we named CtdA-1 and CtdA-2. To date, only CtdAs from the CtdA-1 group have been studied, including S. enterica CtdA (this study). Thus, whether CtdAs belonging to the CtdA-2 group share the same functional activity or specificity warrants further investigation. Notably, our sequence comparison revealed differences in residues relative to CtdA-1 (Supplementary Fig. S14), suggesting that CtdA-2 may have aa-tRNA hydrolytic activity with a yet-to-be-determined substrate specificity. Similar cases of functional substrate diversification have occurred in other aa-tRNA deacylase families, including the prominent example of the INS superfamily, which groups nine aa-tRNA deacylase families with distinct substrate specificities that emerged from a common ancestral protein scaffold [56]. Interestingly, at least two members of the INS superfamily, ProXp-ST1 and ProXp-x, also hydrolyze tRNA mispaired with the nonproteinogenic amino acids 4-fluorothreonine and aminobutyric acid, respectively [57, 58].

Another poorly understood aspect of CtdA was its substrate specificity. It was initially suggested that CtdA discriminated between Can and Arg [16], but experimental evidence was lacking. Using deacylation assays, we showed that S. enterica CtdA has an intrinsic preference for Can-tRNAArg over Arg-tRNAArg. The availability of CtdA to discriminate against Arg-tRNA is remarkable, as it seems to be achieved via interactions with the oxa group of the Can’s side chain. With the help of a new crystal structure and functional assays, we dissected aspects of CtdA’s molecular mechanism of substrate recognition and catalysis. Docking models revealed that the putative amino acid binding pocket of CtdA can accommodate Can and Arg in a similar fashion, which may explain the deacylation of Arg-tRNAArg at elevated CtdA concentrations. The weak hydrolytic activity for Arg-tRNAArg suggests that CtdA evolved a mechanism to differentiate between Can and Arg, which is remarkable given the relatively small structural and chemical difference between the two amino acids. The evolutionary pressure acting on CtdA’s discrimination of Arg-tRNA may stem from the potential negative impacts on cellular fitness associated with undesirable ATP consumption or reduced rates of protein synthesis resulting from the hydrolysis of Arg-tRNAArg by CtdA [59]. Even low levels of Arg-tRNAArg hydrolysis by CtdA may be avoided in organisms that encode CtdA as ArgRS and EF-Tu can also protect Arg-tRNAArg from CtdA. Similar mechanisms of aa-tRNA protection by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and EF-Tu have been reported [42, 53, 54, 60]. However, in some cases, EF-Tu can protect mispaired aa-tRNAs [61, 62].

The selectivity of CtdA is not unprecedented, as other standalone aa-tRNA deacylases display exquisite specificities for the aminoacyl moiety of their substrates [56, 63]. The evolution of specificity in each aa-tRNA deacylase family is unique. For example, the specificity of ThrRS-ed, an archaea-specific Ser-tRNAThr deacylase, is determined by the differential remodeling of the catalytic center, which prevents hydrolysis of Thr-tRNAThr [64]. In contrast, YbaK, a bacterial Cys-tRNA deacylase, relies on the thiol group of the cysteinyl moiety of the substrate to reject deacylation of near-cognate substrates such as Ala-tRNA or Ser-tRNA [65].

Mutagenesis studies validated several residues in the predicted aminoacyl moiety binding pocket. Notably, a single, conserved Cys residue (C166 in S. enterica CtdA) modulated the activity and specificity of CtdA. The mechanism by which C166 modulates CtdA specificity is unclear. However, based on the activities of the CtdA C166S, C166A, and C166G variants, we speculate that the size, rather than the chemistry, of the side chain of Cys166 is a major determining factor. Accordingly, enlarging CtdA’s substrate pocket by decreasing the size of residue 166 may destabilize substrate binding. Thus, CtdA with Ser, 12 Å smaller in molecular volume than Cys [66], at position 166 retains activity and specificity. In contrast, Ala, which has a 23 Å smaller molecular volume than Cys [66], at the same position, weakens the binding of CtdA for Arg but not Can, while Gly disrupts the binding of both amino acid substrates. Interestingly, a recent report indicated that substitution of C166 with Gly in P. canavaninivorans CtdA did not impact the activity of the mutant enzyme in vitro [45]. These contrasting results may be due to differences in reaction conditions, including differences in CtdA and substrate concentrations. It is also possible that unknown structural differences between the enzymes contribute to the distinct activities in vitro. However, the role of C166 in S. enterica CtdA is also supported by cell-based assays, which show that expression of the C166G CtdA in E. coli or S. enterica renders them sensitive to Can toxicity, underscoring the functional role of C166.

Other residues potentially involved in the proper formation of the aminoacyl substrate binding pocket are G116, Q173, and I189. Decreasing or increasing the binding size pocket of CtdA by substituting these positions yielded defective CtdA mutants. This size-based mechanism of substrate selection appears to be shared with the PheRS B3/B4 domain [49]. Similar strategies of substrate recognition are also known for the aa-tRNA deacylation domain of ProRS [67] and ProXp-Ala, a free-standing aa-tRNA deacylase [66, 68]. Additional biochemical and biophysical evidence is needed to define the specificity of CtdA and the role of these residues in Can recognition. Moreover, while our docking models offered details regarding the binding of the aminoacyl moiety, how CtdA interacts and recognizes the tRNA moiety is unknown.

The discovery of C166 as a modulator of specificity and catalysis is also intriguing from an evolutionary standpoint. Clearly, a single residue substitution could redefine CtdA’s specificity for Can-tRNA, raising the question of why CtdA retains a relaxed specificity for the aminoacyl moiety. One possibility is that the extant CtdA is an evolutionary intermediate. Consequently, CtdA may evolve to be specific for Can by replacing C166 with Ala. An alternative scenario is that evolutionary pressure supports CtdA’s relaxed specificity, endowing it with additional biological roles beyond Can detoxification that remain unknown, including the potential activity against other amino acids. The latter case may help explain the presence of CtdA in organisms that may not be exposed to Can or do not use it as an energy source. Future studies may provide support for either of these hypotheses.
