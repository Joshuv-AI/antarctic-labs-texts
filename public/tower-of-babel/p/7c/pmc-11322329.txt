Mechanical property enhancement of flax fibers via supercritical fluid treatment
Authors: Amy Langhorst, Dandan Zhang, Jonah Berman, Xhulja Biraku, Julie Rieland, Mengjie Yu, Brian Love, Mihaela Banu, Alan Taub
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The desire for lightweight, carbon-negative materials has been increasing in recent years, particularly as the transportation sector reduces its global carbon footprint. Natural fibers, such as flax fiber and their composites, offer a compelling combination of properties including low density, high specific strength, and carbon negativity. However, because of the low modulus and high variability in performance, natural fibers can’t compete with glass fibers as structural reinforcements in polymer composites. In this study, flax technical fibers were treated in supercritical CO2 (scCO2), and the effects of this treatment on the morphology and properties of flax fibers are reported. Treatment in scCO2 successfully resulted in higher fiber modulus and strength by 33% and 40%, respectively. Fiber porosity was reduced by 50% and morphological changes to the fibers were observed. Specifically, fiber lumen collapsed during treatment and micro/mesoporosity was reduced by 27%. Treated flax fibers were used to create 30 vol% unidirectional flax-epoxy composites. ScCO2 treatment raised composite modulus and strength by 33% and 25%, respectively. Because of the dependence between technical fiber size and mechanical properties, the relationship between fiber modulus and fiber size were created and applied to the rule-of-mixtures. This relationship were found to be viable representations of the fiber performance within each composite. Overall, the treatment developed in this study has the potential to significantly improve natural fiber properties, enabling their consideration for use in lightweight, semi-structural composites.

Introduction

The demand for carbon-negative materials and technologies has significantly increased in recent years, as manufacturing companies seek to reduce their global carbon footprint. Transportation accounts for approximately 25% of global energy consumption, with light-duty vehicles consuming more energy than all modes of freight transportation combined1. Because of this, automakers have sought to create lightweight material alternatives to improve fuel economy. In internal combustion engine (ICE) powered automobiles, a 10% reduction in vehicle weight corresponds to a 6% improvement in fuel economy2. However, automakers are transitioning towards production of fewer ICE based powertrains and more battery electric vehicles (BEVs). BEV sales have increased over 500% since 20153. Because of the lower energy density provided by batteries compared to liquid fuels, reducing vehicle weight has a more significant effect on BEV range compared to fuel economy improvements in ICE powered vehicles. Specifically, a 10% reduction in BEV weight can improve electric range by 14%, making the value proposition for lightweight materials even more compelling4,5.

While light weighting through substitution of lower density metals has proven itself, the automotive industry has also invested heavily in creating polymeric composites containing bio-based and sustainable resins, fillers, and reinforcements, including natural fiber composites. Among examples, Ford has produced vehicle components with wheat straw and cellulose, BMW has used bamboo in interior composites, and flax fiber has reinforced components on Porsche and Polestar vehicles6. Natural fibers have the advantages of being renewable, lightweight, and low cost. Specifically, replacing glass fiber with flax reduces the environmental impact of the resulting polymer composites by 3 tons of CO2/ ton of polymer composite produced7. While the best reported values of the density normalized (specific) properties are competitive with glass fiber, the high variability makes replacing glass with natural fibers challenging (Table 1). Prior work has shown that the species, cultivation conditions, and extraction techniques can significantly affect the final performance of the extracted fibers8,9. From a design perspective, this variability makes it challenging to model the performance of desired structures and requires engineers to design to add safety factors to prevent premature failures. Because of the potential for natural fiber composites to be used in the production of lightweight, carbon-negative vehicle components, there is strong motivation to find ways to reduce fiber variability and improve the intrinsic properties of the fibers via improved cultivation/ extraction techniques or novel fiber treatments. This work seeks to develop a novel technique to improve the performance of flax fibers, resulting in fibers more suitable for the replacement of glass fiber in structural polymer composites. Flax fibers used for reinforcing composites are called technical fibers. They consist of individual plant cells (elementary fibers), bound together with a pectin-rich interface called the middle lamella. Each elementary fiber is ~ 15 µm in width and 2–5 cm long, with an internal pore called the lumen, which can be as long as 40 µm12–15. The cell wall of an elementary fiber is commonly divided into three layers which provide the structure for the plant14. The secondary cell wall makes up ~ 80% of the elementary fiber cross-section and contains highly crystalline (~ 65–70% crystallinity) cellulose microfibrils angled at ~ 10° to the cell axis in a matrix of amorphous hemicellulose and pectins9,14,16–18.

While the lumen inherently presents large internal porosity within a technical fiber, prior work has reported the presence of additional small internal cavities within flax fibers, resulting in stress concentrations during loading that lead to fiber failure13. Reductions in flax fiber porosity, achieved via chemical or thermal treatments, have improved mechanical interlocking and chemical bonding within a fiber, resulting in stiffer mechanical properties19–21. Chemical treatments have also been used to improve properties both as natural fibers and in composites, including alkali treatments, acetylation, and silane treatments22–25.

Supercritical fluid has also been explored as a treatment for natural fiber surface modification, extracting lignocellulosic components, and improving dyeability26–30. Supercritical fluids have been proven effective at swelling and plasticizing polymers. The densities of supercritical fluids are similar to many liquid, organic solvents but their diffusivities and viscosities are near those of gases, enabling significant uptake of supercritical fluids and swelling31. This swelling can interfere with intermolecular interactions, resulting in lower glass transition temperature and polymer processing temperatures.

Prior work has evaluated the use of supercritical CO2 (scCO2) as a means for solvent-less dyeing of natural fibers, and several authors have attempted to use scCO2 to extract or remove non-cellulosic components from natural fibers (Table 2)26,28,32–41. However, many of these authors also report fiber damage and reduced mechanical properties which they attribute to high temperatures, high flow rates during fluid infiltration and fluid removal, and/or the presence of moisture in the fibers during treatment. The presence of moisture in supercritical CO2 forms carbonic acid resulting in acid hydrolysis and subsequent extraction of hydrolysable material from natural fibers42. In addition, high flow rates have been shown to be effective at foaming of polymers and exfoliating nanomaterials during supercritical fluid processing43,44. Here, the rapid depressurization of the system results in rapid fluid expansion prior to removal from the polymer or nanomaterial, causing plastic deformation and has been shown to be effective in exfoliating nanoclays45–47. Additionally, high temperatures aid in the decomposition of the lignocellulosic components of natural fibers48–50. The authors hypothesize that treatment of flax fibers using low flow rates, low temperatures, and without moisture present in the system could prevent many of the damage mechanisms previously reported. The Voigt model for elastic modulus, commonly referred to as the rule-of-mixtures, is generally used to predict the upper bound for composite elastic moduli, including the moduli of natural fiber composites51–55. However, many studies have previously reported that the rule-of-mixtures does not always apply to natural fiber composites. Several authors reported that single fiber tensile properties, determined experimentally, did not match predicted fiber properties back-calculated from experimentally determined composite properties and the rule-of-mixtures18,52,55,56. Additionally, prior work has shown a very strong tensile strength and modulus dependence on fiber size, with smaller fibers containing fewer middle lamellae throughout their cross-section8,57–59. While several potential sources exist for the discrepancy between back-calculated and experimentally calculated fiber modulus values have been previously explored, we have found no work has previously been performed to take into account the effect of fiber size in a model for composite modulus.

Here, a high-pressure, supercritical fluid treatment was developed to attempt to improve flax fiber mechanical properties. The effects of treatment on fiber structure, morphology, and properties of flax fibers were evaluated. The resulting fibers were used to create unidirectional polymer composites and a relationship that considers fiber size as a non-negligible contributing factor to fiber modulus was created.

Results and discussion

Flax fibers were treated in the presence of scCO2 at 27.6 MPa for 24 h and their resulting properties were characterized via single fiber tensile testing. Treatment resulted in a 33% increase in average fiber tensile modulus and a 40% improvement in average ultimate strength (Table 3). The average values reported fall within the strength and modulus values for flax fibers, previously reported7,10,11,14. The high variability of mechanical properties within a sample of flax technical fibers is commonly reported, and has been attributed to genetic differences between plants, defects introduced during fiber extraction, and the inverse relationship between fiber cross-sectional area and fiber properties14. Smaller cross-sectional area fibers have fewer weak, pectin-rich middle lamellae between the elementary fibers that make up a technical fiber bundle, resulting in higher fiber strength and modulus than their larger counterparts10,14,60–63. To understand the mechanisms behind fiber property improvements, the dimensions and morphology of flax fibers were evaluated before and after treatment in scCO2. Fibers were fixed, microtomed, imaged and the image analyzed as in Fig. 1. Image analysis was performed on fiber cross-sections to quantify changes to technical fiber dimensions (D1 = major axis width, D2 = minor axis width), technical fiber area, elementary fiber (cellular) area, lumen area, and lumen dimensions (d1 = major axis width, d2 = minor axis width) (Table 4) Treatment resulted in a consistent trend in reducing the cross-sectional areas of both technical and elementary fibers, with average reductions of 7.9% and 7.2%, respectively. This suggests a general tendency for fiber dimensions to be reduced after treatment. However, image analysis suggested that the smaller technical fiber width, D2, was more significantly reduced than D1 during treatment (Fig. 2). Specifically, D2 was reduced by an average of 11 µm, while D1 was reduced by an average of 0.8 µm, suggesting that differences in cellular structure along the two axes contributes to the different responses to treatment. For example, the larger width of the lumen (d1) within technical fibers is commonly aligned with the larger width of the fiber (D1). Visualization and measurement of lumen dimensions shows that treatment in scCO2 visibly collapses the lumen. The smaller width of the lumen (d2) was reduced by an average of 43% while the larger lumen width (d1) was reduced by an average of 24% (Table 4). The tendency for a larger reduction in lumen d2 than d1 is consistent with the tendency for a larger reduction in technical fiber width D2 vs D1. Additionally, the lumen consisted of approximately 3.1% of cellular cross-sectional area before treatment, but only 1.2% of cellular cross-sectional area after treatment. The lumen size before treatment is consistent with previously reported values; Charlet, et al. reported that lumen consisted of approximately 2.7–4.0% of cellular area18. After treatment, the lumen size drops below the values previously reported, further confirming the observed collapse due to treatment. It is likely that most lumen behave as closed pores during treatment, as the fibers are treated at ~ 6 cm in length and the lumen, as reported in the literature, is commonly less than 40 µm long13,21,65. Prior work has shown that including the lumen in cross-sectional area measurements for fiber tensile testing can overestimate the load bearing component of the fiber66,67. When the lumen-included area is used in the calculation of fiber stress during single fiber tensile testing, the resulting moduli and ultimate strength values are lower than the inherent material property. The untreated fiber cross-sectional areas were corrected to remove 3.1% porosity and the scCO2 treated fiber cross-sectional areas were corrected to remove 1.2% porosity, representing the lumen’s contribution to cross-sectional area (Fig. 3). Treatment of fibers in scCO2 improved the fiber modulus and ultimate tensile strength by 34% and 38%, respectively, after lumen correction for cross-sectional area.Figure 3Single fiber tensile properties of untreated flax fibers before and after correction for lumen porosity as well as properties of scCO2 treated fibers.

After treatment, lumen cross-sectional area was reduced by ~ 7 µm2 while the elementary fiber (cellular) cross-sectional area was reduced by ~ 23 µm2. This suggests that the treatment is not only causing collapse of the lumens in each technical fiber, but also contributing to consolidation of micro/mesoporosity and/or free volume within the fibers. To evaluate the effects of treatment on porosity, the micro/mesoporosity and surface area of the fibers were evaluated via nitrogen physisorption. Because this technique involved evaluation of fibers cut to very small lengths (< 1 mm), it is likely that the fiber lumen was exposed during physisorption, allowing for internal micropores and mesopores to be captured with this technique, but not capturing the lumen itself, which is larger than the upper limit of the technique. The pore volume and surface area results are shown in Figs. 4, 5 and Table 5. Treatment results in a ~ 24% reduction in total micro/meso pore volume and a ~ 19% reduction in fiber surface area. Additionally, the pores most affected by treatment have widths between 40 and 100 Å, suggesting that treatment has a more significant effect on very small pores, in comparison to pores which are sized near the upper limits of the technique (~ 4000 Å) (Figs. 4, 5). Nitrogen pycnometry was used to determine the skeletal density of 65 mm flax fibers. Fiber density increased from 1.371 to 1.442 g/cm3 after treatment in scCO2, consistent with the collapse of the lumen and reductions in porosity found via nitrogen physisorption.

One possible mechanism which could be contributing to the increases in fiber mechanical properties observed is removal of non-crystalline materials from the fiber. Prior work exploring the use of scCO2 as a treatment for natural fibers has shown varying results with respect to chemical extraction of lignocellulosic components. For example, Li, et al., found that treatment in scCO2 alone did not dissolve or change the physical form or color of α-cellulose, kraft lignin, arabinogalactan or xylan (hemicellulose), xylose, glucose, or chemical components of a variety of wood species42. However, Seghini, et al. found that treating flax fibers in scCO2 removed lignin and hemicellulose29.

To evaluate if non-crystalline material was removed from the fibers, ATR-FTIR was used to evaluate whether bulk changes in fiber chemistry occurred during scCO2 exposure (Fig. 6). Key peaks which are associated with vibrations of lignocellulosic components are identified29,69. No significant changes in composition and no selective removal of major lignocellulosic components were observed after treatment in scCO2. In comparison to prior work which has observed significant chemical removal from lignocellulosic biomass, this study used much slower pressurization and depressurization rates. It is possible that the slower rates used in this work led to less or no chemical extraction. The relationship between fiber modulus and fiber cross-sectional area were generated for each fiber type, as shown in Fig. 8. Prior work has reported an inverse relationship between fiber cross-sectional area (or fiber width) and modulus8,16,20,57. Additionally, prior work has shown that the location of crack initiation in flax is within the weak middle lamellae due to the high concentration of weak pectins at this interface70,71. Because of this, as fiber cross-sectional area is reduced, the number of weak, pectin-rich middle lamellae within the cross-section are reduced, resulting in finer fibers with higher tensile strength and modulus. After calculating the data from Bourmaud’s8 work for flax fibers with small cross-sectional area, we found that the trend of the modulus-to-area relationship of these different species of flax fibers (Electra variety) is closely aligned with the trends observed by Lamy, as shown in Fig. 8. To verify this in our future work, we will improve our fiber selection setup and technique, enabling us to handle and measure single fibers with smaller cross-sectional areas.

Conclusions

A supercritical CO2 treatment has been developed for improving the properties of technical flax fibers. This study demonstrated treatment conditions which were able to increase fiber modulus by 33% and increase fiber strength by 40%. Treatment resulted in lower fiber macroporosity (lumen size) by 63%, micro/mesoporosity by 27%, and total fiber porosity by 50%. The surface area of the fiber was also reduced, suggesting that scCO2 is compacting and smoothing fibers during exposure. Fiber crystallinity and chemistry were not significantly affected by exposure. The changes in microporosity/macroporosity, crystallinity, and surface area of the fiber are not sufficient to explain the observed increase in mechanical properties. We will continue our research in this area to uncover the mechanisms in future work. Formed unidirectional flax-epoxy composites using treated fibers raised flax/epoxy composite modulus and strength by 33% and 25%, respectively. Because of the dependence between technical fiber size and mechanical properties, relationships between fiber modulus and cross-sectional area were created which accounted for fiber size as a non-negligible factor affecting fiber properties. Weighted fiber moduli from these relationships were applied to the rule-of-mixtures and were found to be an appropriate representation of the fiber performance within each composite. Future work will investigate the effects of other treatment parameters (e.g. higher pressures, temperatures, other supercritical fluids) and the potential to couple this method with modifiers (e.g. co-solvents, nanomaterials) to encourage additional improvements to crystallinity and performance.

Single fiber tensile testing

Fibers were mounted in paper frames as shown in Fig. 11a per ASTM C1557-2072. To glue the fibers to the frame and prevent slipping during testing, 1–2 drops of 3 M General Use No Run Super Glue were applied to the top and bottom edges of the window. A second frame was applied on top of the first to secure the fiber. At least 20 fibers were mounted and tested per sample. After mounting, all fibers were conditioned in a desiccator box held at 25% RH for at least 24 h prior to testing. All fibers were tested at a gauge length of 10 mm. The cross-sectional area of each fiber mounted for single fiber tensile testing was obtained via imaging of fiber widths using an Olympus inverted GX51 microscope at 5 × magnification followed by image analysis using the software ImageJ Fiji. Prior work by Feigel, et. al and Virk, et al. has shown that the approximation of a fiber cross-sectional area as an ellipse is a good model for natural fibers undergoing single fiber tensile testing73,74. Because of this, the authors measured the width of the fiber along two axes as shown in Fig. 11b (referred to as width 1, or D1, and width 2, or D2, throughout the manuscript). Fiber morphology: fiber fixation, microtoming, and image analysis

Fibers underwent fixation and mounting in polymerase chain reaction tubes (PCR) to enable ultramicrotoming and optical microscopy. Fixation was performed as previously described by Lara-Mondragón et al.76. Fixation began with vacuum infiltration of a solution containing 0.001% (v/v) Tween 20, 2.5% (w/v) Glutaraldehyde, 2% (w/v) Formaldehyde, and 0.025 M piperazine-N,N′-bis(2-ethanesulfonic acid) (PIPES) buffer (pH 7.2) for 2 h followed by solution replacement and overnight incubation at 4 °C. The following day, samples were washed for 10 min in 0.025 M phosphate buffer followed by a 20 min wash in 0.025 M PIPES solution (pH 7.2) and then dehydrated via ethanol series (25%, 35%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 3 × 100%). Infiltration with liquid resin white (LRw, EMS #14381) occurred via incubation in solutions of resin and ethanol for 24 h. The ratio of resin to ethanol was increased each day until fibers were fully infiltrated with resin over the course of 7 days and then oven cured for 24 h at 58 °C. After fixation and mounting, fibers were ultramicrotomed with a freshly broken glass knife to a thickness of 50 µm. A 1% toluidine blue solution was used to stain the sections for imaging. Transmission light microscopy was performed at 20 × magnification on a Leica DM5500 upright microscope system. At least three cross-sections were taken per sample for analysis.

Image analysis of transmission light micrographs was performed with ImageJ Fiji software. Measurements were taken to record the area and widths of each technical fiber (D1 = major axis width and D2 = minor axis width), the area of elementary fiber, as well as the area and widths of each lumen visible within the cross-section (d1 = major axis width, d2 = minor axis width). Area measurements were performed by tracing the outline of the fiber in each image. At least 10 measurements of each fiber attribute were taken per cross-section. Specimens where the lumen was obscured or folded (due to sample preparation) were excluded. The percentage of cellular area containing lumen was recorded on at least three technical fiber cross-sections per sample.

Porosity and specific surface area

Fiber microporosity and specific surface area were determined via N2 physisorption on a Micromeritics ASAP 2020. Fibers were cut into fine powders (< 1 mm fiber length) using sharp scissors; at least 0.1 g of fiber were analyzed. Fibers were degassed at 60 °C for 2 h prior to analyses by N2 physisorption at − 196 °C (77 K). BET method using 10 data multipoint with relative pressures of 0.05–1 was applied. The pore volume was calculated based on the Barret–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model.

Density

The bulk density of fibers was determined via measurement of the weight of 35 fibers cut to 65 mm in length using a microbalance. The volume of the fibers was determined by measurement of fiber widths along two axes via imaging on an Olympus inverted GX51 microscope at 5 × magnification followed by image analysis using the software ImageJ Fiji.

Fiber skeletal density was measured via gas pycnometry on an Anton Paar Ultrapyc 5000. Flax fibers were cut to 65 mm in length and 0.5–1 g of fiber was used for volume measurements via the pycnometer. Fibers were infiltrated with nitrogen at 34.5 kPa (5 psi) and held at 20 °C throughout testing. A total of 15 infiltration cycles were performed and average skeletal density was reported.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

A Rigaku SmartLab X-ray Diffractometer (XRD) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) generated at 40 kV and 44 mA was used to track fiber crystallinity. Fibers were held in a unidirectional single layer on a glass slide and conditioned in a desiccator held at 20% RH overnight prior to testing. Diffraction data was collected from 8 to 40° 2θ with a scan rate of 2°/min at 0.02° increments. 7 spots were measured on each sample. The fibers were identified as cellulose-I based on peaks for diffraction d-spacings corresponding to the (1 − 1 0) (1 1 0) and (2 0 0) planes located at approximately 14.5, 17, and 22° 2θ, respectively68,77,78. D-spacings were calculated using Matlab R2022a. The cellulose-I peaks were identified from XRD data using a built-in peak finding function and d-spacings were calculated using Bragg’s law assuming a monoclinic structure79. Attenuated total reflectance fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR)

ATR-FTIR analysis was done using a Jasco Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (FT/IR 4100). Spectra were captured using 128 scans at a resolution of 0.24 cm−1 from 4000 to 800 cm−1. Fibers were chopped into a fine powder and pressed onto the ATR crystal. A constant pressure was applied to each sample and key peaks related to lignocellulosic components were identified using OMNIC Spectra software.

Composite fabrication and tensile testing

Epon 862 resin and Epikure 3230 curing agent were used as the epoxy matrix used at a weight ratio of 100:35 and cured at 80 °C for 8 h. Buehler Release Agent (20-8186-004) was used as mold release. 30 vol% unidirectional flax composites were fabricated using a silicone mold with 8 rectangular cavities (cavity dimensions: 3.8 mm × 2.4 mm × 65 mm) (Fig. 12). Fiber tows were vacuum infiltrated with degassed resin in a vacuum oven for at least 1 h. After infiltration, the silicone mold was coated with mold release and tows were transferred to each cavity in the silicone mold. Each cavity was filled with additional epoxy and degassed for at least 2 h to ensure full infiltration of resin and removal of porosity. Fractured composite surfaces were polished using progressively finer silicon-carbide grinding paper followed by micron-sized diamond paste.
