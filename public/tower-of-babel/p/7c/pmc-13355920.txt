Singing Enhances Poetry Learning and Memorization in Preschool Children
Authors: Weijin Hu, Baishen Liang, Yi Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPreschool years are a critical period for developing advanced sociolinguistic skills, and poetry education provides unique cognitive and cultural benefits. Singing, as an alternative to rote recitation, may enhance this learning, yet empirical evidence remains limited. Across two experiments, we investigated the effects of singing versus recitation on memorizing classical Chinese poems in 118 Mandarin‐speaking preschool children. In Experiment 1a, children aged 4–5 years who learned poetry through singing recalled the material more rapidly and fluently than those who learned through rote recitation. Experiment 1b showed that these advantages persisted over a 6‐month interval. Experiment 2 demonstrated that singing continued to improve memorization speed, accuracy, and fluency even with increased semantic complexity in children aged 5–6 years. Together, these findings provide strong evidence for a “singing superiority effect,” whereby singing enhances both immediate and long‐term poem memorization in preschoolers. We discuss potential sensorimotor and motivational mechanisms by which melodic structure supports language memory and propose singing as a practical, developmentally appropriate strategy for early language acquisition.Keywords: poetry education, poetry memorization, recitation, singing

Introduction

Language is fundamental to social life and undergoes rapid development during early childhood. By the preschool age (4–6 years), children typically acquire phonemic awareness and begin to master increasingly complex word‐level communication and paralinguistic skills [1, 2, 3, 4]. Poetry, which embeds linguistic structure within culturally meaningful rhythmic and aesthetic forms, provides a valuable scaffold for supporting sophisticated language use aligned with sociocultural norms [5]. In addition, poetry education has been postulated to cultivate creative thinking and empathy [6], and is widely incorporated into early educational curricula [7]. Nevertheless, research directly examining how preschool children learn and memorize poetry remains limited.

Memory is essential in the acquisition and usage of complex language. In language communications, verbal working memory supports speech by temporally holding and manipulating linguistic information [8, 9], while long‐term memory encapsulates language knowledge [10]. Stronger memory capacities and verbal memory spans have been shown to predict better language abilities in preschoolers [11], suggesting that enhancing verbal memory may facilitate complex language development during early childhood.

Traditionally, children memorize texts through rote recitation, a mechanical repetition method widely employed in Chinese education [12]. Although effective [13], contemporary research emphasizes strategies that recruit richer contextual cues and interactive learning [14]. Singing, as one such strategy, provides melodic cues and engages the sensorimotor system, supporting both communication and memory. A growing body of research demonstrates that music training improves verbal memory [15, 16] and that encoding melodic cues alongside text materials improves recall performance [17, 18]. Singing words or phrases has been shown to improve verbal recognition compared to rote recitation [19], even for foreign‐language materials [20]. This mnemonic advantage, often referred to as the “singing superiority effect,” is believed to stem from distinctive musical features [21]. Nevertheless, some studies reported limited or context‐dependent effects of singing [22, 23].

Importantly, most prior studies have focused on word‐ or phrase‐level learning materials; leaving open the question of whether singing similarly aids memorization of longer texts with high syntactic or semantic complexity, such as poetry. Classical Chinese poetry is particularly suited to this inquiry, as it integrates aesthetic linguistic elements with melodic and rhythmic structures [24] and is generally more memorable than prose, presumably due to rhyme [25]. For centuries, Chinese educators have taught poetry through singing, based on the belief that melodic instruction supports memorization and comprehension [26]. However, empirical evidence for the singing superiority effect in learning whole poems, especially for preschoolers, is lacking.

In summary, the preschool years represent a crucial stage for higher level language development, and poetry serves as a scaffold for linguistic learning. Although previous research suggests that music and singing benefit word‐ and phrase‐level verbal memory, three key questions remain: (1) Does singing improve poetry memorization in preschool children compared to recitation? (2) If so, can these benefits be preserved over time? (3) Does linguistic complexity affect the advantages of singing?

To address these questions, we conducted two experiments. Experiment 1 compared the effects of singing versus rote recitation on poetry memorization among 4–5‐year‐old children, where Experiment 1a tested the immediate effects, and Experiment 1b examined whether these benefits persisted for 6 months. Experiment 2 tested whether linguistic complexity moderates the singing superiority effect (if present) in 5–6‐year‐old children. Results revealed that, in general, singing led to faster, more accurate, and more fluent poetry learning, and these advantages were sustained over time and unaffected by linguistic complexity.

Materials and Methods

Experiment 1a

Participants

A total of 58 healthy 4–5 years old (M = 4.879 years, SD = 0.498 years) children from a kindergarten participated in Experiment 1a. Although standardized musical ability tests were not implemented, all participants were informally screened by trained instructors or identified by teachers or parents as having normal hearing, speech, and music abilities. To further verify participants’ musical reproduction ability, audio recordings from the Sing group in Experiment 1a were evaluated for pitch accuracy (full score = 5) by a professional rater (see Text S1). Results indicated generally high pitch accuracy (M = 4.28, SD = 0.649), suggesting that children were able to reproduce melodies with reasonable fidelity.

Participants were randomly assigned to the Recite group (n = 29, 16 females, M = 4.793 years, SD = 0.491 years) or the Sing group (n = 29, 18 females, M = 4.966 years, SD = 0.499 years). The groups were balanced in age (independent t‐test, t(56) = 1.326, p = 0.190). Each group size was sufficient to detect medium to large effects, as estimated by G*Power 3.1. This research received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Science (No. H23157). Informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all participants.

Materials

The memorization material was Ode to the Vill (山村咏怀), a Song Dynasty poem written by Shao Yong (1011–1077). An English translation by Jingcheng Xu [27] reads: One glance: two, three miles/Mist Vill: four, five homes/Six, seven bowers in styles/Eight, nine, and ten blooms (一去二三里/烟村四五家/亭台六七座/八九十枝花). All materials used in the study were classical Chinese Jueju (quatrain) poems, characterized by highly regulated tonal patterns, consistent end‐rhyme schemes, and symmetrical syntactic structures.

The melody for the singing condition was originally composed by the author Weijin Hu (see Figure S1). Designed to be simple, developmentally appropriate, and pedagogically effective, the melody comprised short, repeating phrases (10–15 notes) in a strophic structure. The melodic contour aligned with the prosodic and rhythmic structure of the poetic text. To avoid familiarity confounds, all melodies were novel and not derived from existing tunes. Two audio versions were recorded by author Weijin Hu: a sung version and a spoken version without melody.

Although classical poetry is culturally ubiquitous in China, participants and their guardians confirmed that the specific poems had not been formally learned. Importantly, the melodies were novel for all participants, ensuring that melodic structure provided a new mnemonic scaffold for encoding the poetic content.

Experimental Design and Procedure

The two groups of participants were situated in separate kindergarten classrooms. The Sing group learned the poem through singing along with an audio recording, whereas the Recite group learned by listening and reciting. Audio recordings were repeated until participants could sing or recite the entire poem without musical accompaniment. Upon readiness (signaled by raising their hands), participants were individually assessed in a separate room to avoid interference. Two examiners conducted concurrent testing sessions to ensure temporal consistency across participants, while two additional teachers monitored the classroom. This arrangement ensured that children were assessed immediately upon reaching their self‐identified learning threshold, effectively minimizing variability in retention intervals. The examiner was required not to interrupt the recall. Once participants successfully recalled all four lines of the entire poem (regardless of mistakes), the recall was completed. Those who failed had to repeat the learning and recall phases. The total learning duration was recorded from the beginning of instruction to successful recall. Three outcome measures were collected: total learning duration, recall accuracy (percentage of correct words in the completed recall), and fluency (rated in the completed recall on a five‐point scale, deducting one point for each pause; minimum score of 1). Video recordings of all performances were collected and independently rated by two trained raters (see Text S1 for rater information and qualifications). Classroom conditions were consistent across groups, with environmental factors evenly balanced.

Data Analyses

Inter‐rater reliability was high for all measures (total learning duration, accuracy, and fluency; 0.851–1), with the lowest values observed for fluency (Sing: 0.906; Recite: 0.851; see Table S1). Correlation matrices for dependent variables (duration, accuracy, and fluency) are shown in Table S2. All statistical analyses across experiments were conducted using R (version 4.3.1). Group comparisons were performed using independent samples t‐tests. Data visualization was performed using Seaborn 0.13.1 in Python 3.9 throughout the study.

Experiment 1b

Participants and Materials

All participants from Experiment 1a were included in Experiment 1b, except one participant from the Recite group who transferred schools. The experimental settings, materials, and examiners remained the same. Participants were asked to recall the poem Ode to the Vill directly from memory, without audio prompts.

Design and Procedure

Experiment 1b investigated long‐term retention 6 months after Experiment 1a. Children were asked if they still remembered the poem learned earlier; affirmative responders performed individual recall in a separate room. The total recall duration, accuracy, and fluency were recorded, following the same procedure as in Experiment 1a. All performances were video‐recorded and independently rated by two raters.

Data Analyses

Inter‐rater reliability was high for all measures (0.865–1; see Table S1). Correlations among recall measures are shown in Table S3. Chi‐square tests and independent t‐tests examined differences in task completion rates, and mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) assessed the effects of Group (Sing vs. Recite, between‐subjects factor) and Timepoint (immediate in Experiment 1a vs. 6 months later in Experiment 1b, within‐subject factor) on performance.

Experiment 2

Participants

Sixty healthy children aged 5–6 years from kindergarten who had not participated in Experiment 1 were recruited. All were identified by teachers or parents as having normal hearing, speech, and music abilities, using identical screening standards as in Experiment 1. Participants were randomly assigned to the Sing group (n = 30, 13 females, M = 5.367 years, SD = 0.490 years) or the Recite group (n = 30, 15 females, M = 5.400 years, SD = 0.498 years). Group size was sufficient to detect medium to large effects, as estimated by G*Power 3.1. There were no significant age differences between groups: t(58) = −0.259, p = 0.797, Cohen's d = −0.067. Informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all participants.

Materials

The low‐difficulty poem was the same as used in Experiment 1a. The high‐difficulty poem was A Trip to Mountain West Village (游山西村) by Lu You (1125–1210). An English translation by Burton Watson [28] reads: Don't laugh because it's muddy/Year‐end wine brewed in country homes/harvests were good/To make the guest linger, fowl and pork plenty/Mountains multiply, streams double back/I doubt there's even a road/willows cluster darkly, blossoms shine/Another village ahead! (莫笑农家腊酒浑/丰年留客足鸡豚/山重水复疑无路/柳暗花明又一村). Compared to the low‐difficulty poem, this poem is longer (28 words) and more semantically abstract, making it more challenging for 5–6‐year‐old children to comprehend and memorize.

The melody for the singing condition was composed by the author Weijin Hu (see Figure S2), with properties similar to Experiment 1. Audio recordings of both poems in sung and recited versions were prepared by author Weijin Hu. Participants confirmed no prior exposure. Audio materials are provided as Supporting Information.

Experimental Design and Procedure

The Sing group learned by listening and singing along; the Recite group learned by repeated listening and recitation, as in Experiment 1a. Audio was repeated until participants could sing or recite the entire poem without music. Poems were learned on consecutive days, with the low‐difficulty poem first and the high‐difficulty poem second, reflecting scaffolded instruction typical of classroom practice. To mitigate potential order effects, the two poems featured distinct melodic and rhythmic structures, eliminating the risk of melodic transfer effects. Importantly, as the difficulty progression was identical for all participants, it did not interfere with the primary group‐level comparisons. After signaling readiness, children were individually assessed in a separate room. Duration, accuracy, and fluency were scored as in Experiment 1a. All sessions were video‐recorded for independent scoring by two raters.

Data Analyses

Inter‐rater reliability was high (low‐difficulty poem: 0.964–1; high‐difficulty poem: 0.967–1; see Table S1). Correlations among recall measures are shown in Table S4. Mixed‐design ANOVAs with Group (Sing vs. Recite) as a between‐subjects factor and Difficulty (low vs. high) as a within‐subject factor were conducted.

In addition to frequentist ANOVAs, Bayesian ANOVAs were conducted using the BayesFactor package in R to assess evidence for or against the presence of a Group × Difficulty interaction. Specifically, we compared a full model including the interaction term against a reduced model containing only the main effects based on Bayesian information criterion (BIC)‐derived Bayes factors.

To further account for differing poem lengths, recall accuracy was analyzed at the item (character) level using a generalized linear mixed‐effects model (GLMM) with a binomial logistic link function. Fixed effects included Group, Difficulty, and their interaction, with crossed random intercepts for subjects and items (Accuracy ∼ Group + Difficulty + Group × Difficulty + (1|item) + (1|subject)). BIC‐based model comparison assessed whether including the interaction improved model fit.

Results

Experiment 1a

All participants completed the learning and recall task. As shown in Figure 1, independent t‐tests revealed that the Sing group (M = 11.621, SD = 7.894) reached accurate recall significantly faster than the Recite group (M = 25.414, SD = 15.373), t(41.807) = −4.298, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = −1.129. Recall accuracy did not differ between groups: Sing (M = 0.928, SD = 0.064) versus Recite (M = 0.922, SD = 0.034), t(43.05) = 0.386, p = 0.701, Cohen's d = 0.101. However, the Sing group (M = 4.759, SD = 0.577) demonstrated significantly higher fluency than the Recite group (M = 4.345, SD = 0.484), t(56) = 2.961, p = 0.004, Cohen's d = 0.777. These results demonstrate that singing enables preschoolers to learn poetry more quickly and with higher fluency, without compromising recall accuracy. Importantly, pitch accuracy during recall was not significantly correlated with memory performance (Fluency: r(27) = −0.197, p = 0.305; Accuracy: r(27) = −0.105, p = 0.589), suggesting that individual differences in singing ability did not account for the observed memory effects.

Predictors of Long‐Term Recall in the Recite Group

To explore predictors of delayed recall in the Recite group, participants who completed the task (n = 9) were compared to noncompleters (n = 19) on their initial learning performance (Experiment 1a). As shown in Figure 2B–D, completers exhibited significantly shorter initial learning duration (M = 16.556, SD = 10.394) than noncompleters (M = 29.400, SD = 15.776), t(27) = −2.223, p = 0.035, Cohen's d = −0.962. However, there were no significant differences in initial accuracy (completer: M = 0.922, SD = 0.026; noncompleter: M = 0.923, SD = 0.038; t(27) = −0.020, p = 0.984, Cohen's d = 0.009) or fluency (completer, M = 4.333, SD = 0.500; noncompleter, M = 4.350, SD = 0.489; t(27) = −0.084, p = 0.933, Cohen's d = −0.034). These results suggest that faster initial learning predicted better long‐term (after 6 months) recall when memorizing by recitation, highlighting notable individual differences in learning efficiency for the recitation method.

For accuracy (Figure 4B), GLMM results revealed significant main effects of group (p = 0.002, OR = 0.421; β = −0.866, SE = 0.282, z = −3.077) and poem difficulty (p = 0.004, OR = 0.068; β = −2.692, SE = 0.928, z = −2.900), with higher accuracy for the Sing group and for the low‐difficulty poem. BIC‐based Bayes factor comparison indicated BF01 = 26.19, providing strong evidence that the singing advantage was consistent across poem difficulty.

Similarly, for fluency (Figure 4C), ANOVA revealed significant main effects of group, F(1, 58) = 29.959, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.341, and poem difficulty, F(1, 58) = 11.683, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.168, with the Sing group and low‐difficulty poem showing higher fluency. The interaction was not significant, F(1, 58) = 0.060, p = 0.808, partial η2 = 0.001. Bayesian analysis comparing models with and without the interaction yielded BF01 = 3.40, providing moderate evidence that the singing advantage was consistent across poem difficulty.

Overall, these findings suggest that the singing superiority effect on poetry memorization in preschool children is robust and independent of poem complexity. Analyses of potential speed‐accuracy trade‐offs revealed no significant correlations between learning duration and recall accuracy across experiments (see Tables S2–S4), although for the high‐difficulty poem, longer study duration was significantly associated with lower final accuracy (r = −0.579, p = 0.001).

Discussion

Although prior research has shown that singing words facilitates verbal memory, it remained elusive whether this advantage extends to more complex language materials like poetry, and whether preschool children developing advanced language skills can benefit similarly. This study addressed these gaps by comparing singing to rote recitation in memorizing identical classical Chinese poems among preschoolers. In Experiment 1a, children aged 4–5 who learned the poem through singing recalled materials more rapidly and fluently than those who recited. Experiment 1b further demonstrated the persistence of these benefits: 6 months later, all participants in the Sing group successfully recalled the poem, whereas most children in the Recite group (19 out of 28) could not. Experiment 1b revealed that the singing superiority effect persisted when children aged 5–6 years memorized a linguistically complex poem.

Our findings provide a significant developmental and structural extension of the singing superiority effect. While previous research has primarily demonstrated mnemonic advantages for isolated words or short phrases [19, 20], the current study provides empirical evidence that these benefits extend to long‐form and complex linguistic structures such as classical poetry. Moreover, we demonstrate that for preschool children at a critical stage of sociolinguistic development, these benefits are robust across varying levels of linguistic complexity and persist over a 6‐month interval, suggesting that melodic scaffolding provides a stable and enduring template for verbal encoding.

Mechanisms Underlying the Superiority of Singing

Across experiments, singing consistently led to faster learning, higher recall accuracy, and greater fluency. Several cognitive and motivational mechanisms likely contribute to this advantage. Musical and rhythmic structures provide robust temporal scaffolding and mnemonic cues during both encoding and retrieval, affording children greater flexibility in selecting memory strategies that leverage either linguistic or musical (melodic or rhythmic) components of the poem [29, 30]. Singing may also enhance attentional engagement compared to recitation [31] and tap into participants’ existing knowledge of both music and language [32].

Moreover, singing evokes greater pleasure and reward than recitation [33]. Musical reward, mediated by dopaminergic activity, can enhance memory consolidation [34], and may encourage spontaneous rehearsal after the initial learning. Engaging with music and rhythm also recruits motor systems, further bolstering motivation and engagement in the learning process [35, 36].

Classical Chinese poetry is inherently rhythmic and structurally regular, properties known to enhance processing fluency and aesthetic experience, thereby facilitating memory encoding regardless of the presence of melodic structures [37]. However, because identical texts were used in both the Sing and Recite groups, these intrinsic features were systematically balanced. The observed advantage of singing, therefore, reflects the specific, additional contribution of melodic structure beyond baseline linguistic fluency.

Although the current study provides empirical evidence for singing advantages in poetry learning and memorization, it was not designed to dissect the precise mechanisms underlying this effect. Further research is warranted to disentangle the specific contributions of rhythmic, melodic, attentional, motivational, and sensorimotor processes to memory and language acquisition.

Long‐Term Retention Benefits From Singing

Experiment 1b highlighted that singing supports not only immediate recall but also long‐term retention. Children in the Sing group maintained accurate recall 6 months after learning, whereas most in the Recite group did not. Within the Recite group, faster initial learning predicted better delayed recall, highlighting the importance of early learning efficiency when using recitation alone.

Moreover, among children who completed the delayed recall, the Sing group exhibited greater fluency without sacrificing accuracy. These findings suggest that singing improves poetry memory through two primary ways: (1) accelerating learning and improving recall quality; and (2) reducing the risk of completely forgetting over time, making it an inclusive strategy for diverse learners.

One plausible explanation is that singing promotes repeated engagement. After the initial exposure, children may spontaneously sing the learned materials [38], deriving pleasure and motivation [14], which encourages rehearsal and further memory consolidation.

Singing Benefits Extend to Semantically Complex Poetry

Experiment 1b examined whether the singing advantage persists with more linguistically complex poems. As expected, learning more complex poems required longer memorization time and resulted in lower accuracy and fluency, validating the difficulty manipulation. However, singing continued to provide faster, more accurate, and more fluent recall, with no interaction between poem difficulty and modality.

Although longer poems increased memory load [39], item‐level GLMM analyses demonstrated that the singing superiority effect remained robust even under this high‐demand condition. These results indicate that singing facilitates verbal memory encoding in a domain‐specific way, presumably mediated by reciprocal auditory‐motor neural pathways for speech [40, 41], different from pathways supporting lexical access [42] or higher level thematic processing [43]. Sensorimotor mechanisms shared by singing and listening may enhance oscillatory neural entrainment to auditory streams [44] and promote the sensorimotor integration in speech processing [45], particularly synchronization of neural oscillations between auditory and motor cortices [46], thereby facilitating memory encoding [47]. Notably, the singing advantage occurred independently of comprehension, indicating that melodic and emotional features of music alone can scaffold memory [48].

The effects on recall accuracy were observed primarily in older children (5–6 years; Experiment 2) but not in younger children (4–5 years; Experiment 1a,b). This may reflect the developmental maturation of dorsal articulatory and ventral lexical pathways for speech processing [49], enabling better integration of melodic articulatory commands and linguistic information in memory. Future research is expected to compare different age groups of preschoolers to clarify how sensorimotor pathways mediate the singing effects.

Implications and Limitations

Our findings support integrating singing into poetry instruction, especially for preschool learners. Given that most classical Chinese literary forms (e.g., Ci, Qu, and prose) are strictly rhythmic, singing could generalize to learning these materials [50]. Moreover, since rhythm and melody are universal features of poetry, the singing superiority effect may generalize across languages and cultures.

The present study has several limitations. First, in the Sing group, children listened to audio recordings with melody while producing a sung output, whereas the Recite group was exposed to spoken‐only recordings while producing spoken output. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as demonstrating an integrated song‐based learning advantage, rather than isolating the independent contributions of melodic input and vocal production. While this design maximizes ecological validity by reflecting authentic classroom practice that combines auditory input and motor output, it does not permit a complete dissociation between the effects of input format (melodic vs. spoken) and output modality (singing vs. reciting). Future research could employ designs that independently manipulate presentation format and production modality to disentangle their respective contributions to memory performance. Second, by conducting the study in an authentic classroom setting, we optimized ecological validity. However, this approach introduced environmental variables such as ambient noise and interpersonal interactions. While these factors were balanced across groups, future studies in more strictly controlled laboratory settings are warranted to further validate these findings. Third, the tonal nature of Mandarin Chinese [51] may introduce cultural or linguistic specificity to the results. Previous studies suggest that tonal language experience can enhance pitch perception and melodic processing [52, 53, 54], which may have conferred an advantage to the children in our study when learning melodic materials. Conversely, lexical tone processing could also interfere with music processing under certain conditions due to shared acoustic properties. The extent to which these findings generalize to nontonal language populations remains an open empirical question requiring cross‐linguistic investigation. Finally, future research should further explore the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying singing's effects on memory. Existing theories [55] and empirical studies [56, 57] highlight the beneficial impact of musical training on language capacity. Further exploration is needed to clarify how musical and linguistic processes interact across multiple phases of language acquisition. Extending these practices to diverse cultural and linguistic contexts will also provide valuable insights for educational practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that singing robustly facilitates poetry memorization over rote recitation in preschool children. Specifically, singing (1) accelerates initial learning and recall, improves recall accuracy and fluency, with effects on fluency lasting over 6 months; (2) reduces the risk of long‐term forgetting, providing a more inclusive learning environment for diverse learners, and (3) remains effective even for poems with high linguistic complexity. Together, these findings demonstrate that singing provides preschoolers with a more inclusive and temporally effective scaffold for acquiring sophisticated language materials. Further research should further investigate the cognitive neuroscience mechanisms underlying the singing superiority effect and extend these practices to broader educational contexts.
