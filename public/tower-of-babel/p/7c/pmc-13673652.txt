Clinical association between myelodysplastic syndrome and de novo bone metastasis in urothelial carcinoma
Authors: Zhilong Zestel Shen, Gan Xu, Hao Zhang, Pengru Wang, Xin Zhang, Bo Li, Jianru Xiao, Wei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo assess the association between myelodysplastic syndrome and de novo bone metastasis of urothelial carcinoma.MethodA cohort of 145,719 patients with MDS and/or UTCA included from the surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER) database was conducted to assess the risk of de novo bone metastasis of UTCA (DNBM-UTCA) in patients with previously diagnosed myelodysplastic syndrome (PD-MDS). The odds ratio for developing DNBM-UTCA between MDS and non-MDS patients was estimated. Weibull accelerated failure time model was applied to evaluate the survival outcomes of patients with UTCA.ResultsOur findings suggest that PD-MDS is a powerful risk factor for DNBM-UTCA (adjusted odds ratio, 6.428; 95% CI, 1.866–16.497; p < 0.001) and a poor prognostic factor on survival (adjusted survival time ratio, 0.485; CI, 0.338–0.695; p < 0.001).ConclusionOur findings suggest that PD-MDS was associated with higher odds of DNBM-UTCA and poorer survival outcomes, which may warrant additional clinical attention during treatment decision-making. However, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Further prospective studies and mechanistic investigations are needed to better clarify the biological basis underlying the observed association between PD-MDS and bone metastasis in UTCA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12957-026-04468-3.Keywords: Urothelial carcinoma, Myelodysplastic syndrome, Bone metastasis, Pre-metastatic niche

Introduction

Urothelial carcinoma (UTCA), also known as transitional cell carcinoma, arises from urothelial cells lining the urinary tract, including the bladder, ureters, and renal pelvis [1]. The prognosis of UTCA varies significantly with disease stage, with a 5-year survival rate of 95.8% for in situ carcinoma, 69.5% for localized disease, 36.3% for regional disease, and only 4.6% for metastatic cases [2]. Bone metastases occur in approximately 4% of UTCA patients and are associated with a median overall survival of merely 10 months [3–5]. However, treatment options for bone metastases in UTCA remain limited, as current recommendations are largely extrapolated from studies on mixed solid tumor populations rather than UTCA-specific cohorts [4]. This highlights the need for dedicated research and tailored management strategies for these patients.

Bone metastasis is a complex process involving tumor cell homing, adaptation, and niche formation within the bone microenvironment [6]. Although preclinical studies have proposed the concept of a “pre-metastatic niche“ [7, 8], clinical evidence remains limited to case reports [9–11]. While hematologic abnormalities, including myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), have been implicated in altering bone homeostasis [12, 13], their role in facilitating UTCA bone metastases has not been explored.

In this study, we identified association between PD-MDS and DNBM-UTCA and poor prognosis of PD-MDS in UTCA patients. Furthermore, our findings provide the first cohort-based clinical evidence generating a hypothesis of a pre-metastatic bone niche in UTCA, paving the way for future investigations into its clinical implications and potential therapeutic targets.

Methods

Database and participants

Patients diagnosed with MDS (from January 2000 to December 2019) or UTCA (from January 2010 to December 2019) were identified from 17 registries of Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database in SEER*Stat software version 8.4.3(https://seer.cancer.gov/)(Details in Table S1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients who were histologically diagnosed with MDS and UTCA between 2000 and 2019; (2) Availability of demographic variables, including age, sex, race. The exclusion criteria for patients with UTCA were: (1) Age < = 18 years; (2) Radiotherapy methods other than beam radiation; (3) UTCA not being the first or second tumor; (4) Metastatic status of bone, liver, brain and lung is not available; (5) Non-urothelial carcinoma tumors. The exclusion criteria for patients with MDS were: (1) Age < = 18 years; (2) Radiotherapy methods other than beam radiation; (3) MDS not being the first tumor;

Definition of latency

To enhance the reliability of the chronological order of tumor occurrence and ensure that MDS has adequate time to influence the bone microenvironment, we introduce a novel parameter called “latency” to delineate the diagnostic interval between MDS and subsequent UTCA. In the primary analysis, patients with a latency period of ≤ 6 months were excluded based on an empirical and clinically pragmatic consideration. Sensitivity analyses using multiple alternative latency thresholds (3, 12, 18, and 24 months) yielded highly consistent results (Table S2).

Grouping details of patients

The process of data screening, integration, and analysis was illustrated in Fig. 1. Eventually, 118,654 patients with UTCA as their primary or only cancer (Part 1), 79 patients with MDS followed by UTCA (Part 2), and 26,986 patients with MDS as their only cancer (Part 3) were identified. Following filtration of latency, 72 patients remained in the Part 2 category (P2b). To eliminate the potential influence of MDS chemotherapy, we further filtered out patients who received chemotherapy for MDS, resulting in 65 patients in the Part 2a category.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software, version 4.3.1. Categorical data were compared using Fisher’s exact tests, while continuous variables were analyzed using Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value less than 0.05. Additionally, a multivariable logistic regression analysis using Firth penalized logistic regression was conducted to estimate odds ratio of DNBM-UTCA in Group 1 (Part 1 and Part 2a) and Group 2 (Part 1 and Part 2b), separately. Group 3(Part 2b and Part 3) was used to identify risk factors for the occurrence of UTCA after suffering MDS. Potential confounders identified in the Fisher’s exact tests and Mann-Whitney U test (with a two-sided p-value < 0.1) were controlled for in this analysis. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each comparison. Given the relatively small number of PD-MDS cases and the imbalance between groups, adjusted risk ratios (RRs) were additionally estimated using modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the observed associations. To evaluate the influence of MDS on survival outcome, we employed the Cox proportional hazards model and accelerated failure time model. However, numerous covariates violated the proportional hazards assumption, and the survival time did not adhere to the Weibull, exponential, Gaussian, logistic, or lognormal distributions. To address this issue, we conducted propensity score matching at a ratio of 1:5(Group 1a: Group 1b) and ultimately utilized the Weibull accelerated failure time model to assess the impact of MDS on survival outcome.

Guideline

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

Association between PD-MDS and DNBM-UTCA

To eliminate the potential influence of PD-MDS chemotherapy, multivariable logistic regression was performed in Group 1 (Fig. 2; Table 2; Table S4) and Group 2 (Table 2; Table S5) after controlling for potential confounders, separately. In addition, adjusted risk ratios were estimated using modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors. The results consistently demonstrated that PD-MDS was independently associated with an increased risk of developing DNBM-UTCA (Group 1: adjusted OR, 7.118; 95% CI, 2.215–17.632; p = 0.002; adjusted RR, 5.501; 95% CI, 2.531–11.957; p < 0.001; Group 2: adjusted OR, 7.309; 95% CI, 2.260–18.173; p = 0.002; adjusted RR, 5.477; 95% CI, 2.620–11.449; p < 0.001).

In this study, PD-MDS and DNBM-UTCA exhibit a relatively clear temporal sequence by carefully managing the latency duration. Additionally, existing research provides a plausible biological explanation for the impact of MDS on the bone microenvironment [14]. Our findings further support an association between PD-MDS and the development of DNBM-UTCA. Taken together, we hypothesize that MDS may be associated with the bone metastasis of UTCA and potentially linked to alterations in the bone microenvironment.

Survival outcome of PD-MDS patients

Patients with a survival time of less than 6 months were excluded from the analysis, and their baseline characteristics are shown in Table S6. Initially, cox proportional hazards model (Table S7) and accelerated failure time model were applied to compare overall survival between patients with PD-MDS and no PD-MDS in Group 1. Nonetheless, a substantial number of covariates violated the proportional hazards assumption (Figure S1), and the survival time did not obey with the Weibull, exponential, Gaussian, logistic, or lognormal distributions (Table S8). To tackle this challenge, we resorted to propensity score matching with a ratio of 1:5 (Group 1a: Group 1b), and baseline characteristics after matching and distribution of propensity scores are shown in Table S9 and Figure S2.

Finally, the overall survival follows a Weibull distribution (Figure S3; Table S8). Subsequently, a stepwise procedure was utilized to identify significant variables using the Weibull accelerated failure time model (Table S10). Our findings reveal that the mean overall survival among UTCA patients with PD-MDS was 48.5% of patients with UTCA as the first malignant neoplasm (adjusted survival time ratio, 0.485; CI, 0.338–0.695; p < 0.001) (Table 2; Table S10).

Risk factors of developing UTCA in MDS patients

To better guide clinical practice, we further identified risk factors for the development of secondary UTCA among MDS patients in Group 3. The baseline characteristics of patients in Group 3 are presented in Table S11. The results of multivariable logistic analysis revealed that factors such as male gender (adjusted odds ratio, 3.646; 95% CI, 1.460-11.096; p = 0.011) and diagnosis between 2010 and 2015 (adjusted odds ratio, 2.805; 95% CI, 1.301–6.389; p = 0.010) were identified as risk factors for developing UTCA in patients with MDS (Table S12).

Discussion

What is firmly established is that organs destined for future metastasis are not passive recipients of circulating tumor cells, but rather undergo active and selective modifications orchestrated by the primary tumor prior to the occurrence of metastatic spread [8]. Despite widespread acceptance of the concept of a pre-metastatic microenvironment in basic research, clinical cohort-based evidence remains limited. This scarcity of clinical evidence can be attributed to the absence of symptoms at the metastatic sites, and currently, our understanding is primarily confined to case reports focusing on “tumor to tumor” metastasis [9, 15].

Our study suggests that a prior history of MDS is an independent risk factor for bone metastasis in patients with urothelial carcinoma. Nevertheless, to date, there is currently no direct basic research evidence demonstrating that MDS actively promotes the formation of a pre-metastatic niche within the bone of UTCA patients. To elucidate possible mechanisms, we compiled and summarized the extant basic research pertaining to MDS and UTCA.

Recent studies have further expanded the molecular and immunological context of urothelial carcinoma. Molecular and epigenetic profiling approaches, including urinary DNA methylation-based assays, have improved the detection and characterization of urothelial lesions, suggesting that epigenetic dysregulation is clinically relevant in this disease spectrum. In addition, PD-L1-related immune escape and tumor immune microenvironment remodeling have been increasingly recognized as important features of advanced urothelial carcinoma [16, 17]. More broadly, clonal hematopoiesis has been reported to remodel the solid tumor microenvironment toward a more inflammatory state and to be associated with adverse clinical outcomes across cancer types [18]. In hematologic neoplasms, bone marrow inflammation and stromal remodeling are also increasingly recognized as disease-associated features, as exemplified by the association between megakaryocytic activation and fibrotic evolution in myeloproliferative neoplasms [19].

Although no direct studies have explored the interaction between MDS and UTCA bone metastasis, emerging evidence suggests potential shared molecular alterations. For example, mutations in ASXL1, a chromatin regulator, have been reported in both MDS and upper tract UTCA [20, 21]. EZH2, a component of the polycomb repressive complex, exhibits distinct roles in these malignancies—its loss-of-function mutations contribute to MDS pathogenesis [22], while its overexpression in UTCA has been associated with tumor aggressiveness, making EZH2 inhibition a potential therapeutic target [23, 24]. Similarly, epigenetic alterations in TET2, a key regulator of bone homeostasis, have been implicated in both MDS-related hematopoiesis and UTCA tumor progression [25–27]. Additionally, BMP-2, a bone morphogenetic protein, has been linked to bladder cancer cell adhesion to bone tissue, while BMP signaling inhibitors, such as ACE-536, have shown promise in MDS treatment [28–30]. Collectively, these observations may provide a potential biological context for the observed association between MDS and bone metastasis in UTCA; however, the present study does not establish a causal mechanistic link, and further experimental validation is required. Specifically, shared systemic vulnerability, age-related immune senescence, chronic inflammatory status, field cancerization effects, treatment-related influences, detection bias, and unmeasured comorbidities may also contribute to the increased incidence of DNBM-UTCA observed in patients with PD-MDS.

Although there are no studies directly exploring the interaction mechanism between MDS and UTCA, the aforementioned evidence provides a broader biological background for interpreting bone metastasis in urothelial carcinoma patients following MDS. Further fundamental research is urgently needed, as it could significantly enhance our understanding of the intricate interplay between MDS and UTCA bone metastasis, potentially paving the way for innovative and groundbreaking therapeutic strategies. Our study demonstrates this possibility from a clinical point of view.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the TNM staging information in the SEER database is discontinuous across different periods, and the TNM stage used in this study represents a combination of clinical and pathological staging systems from different time frames. Second, data regarding distant lymph node metastasis and other organ metastases for UTCA were unavailable in 2016. Third, although we attempted to minimize treatment-related confounding by incorporating the available chemotherapy variable and performing additional latency-based sensitivity analyses, the SEER database only records limited initial treatment information and lacks detailed data on specific systemic therapies, chemotherapy regimens, treatment duration, treatment lines, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and treatment responses. Importantly, information regarding bone-targeted agents, including bisphosphonates and denosumab, is unavailable in SEER, and these factors may substantially influence the natural history of bone metastasis. Therefore, residual confounding caused by treatment heterogeneity cannot be completely excluded. In addition, the SEER database lacks important hematologic and biologic information related to MDS, including risk stratification, mutation profiles, blood cell counts, bone marrow microenvironment characteristics, cytogenetic abnormalities, and disease progression status, which limits further mechanistic interpretation of the observed associations. Furthermore, in the analysis identifying risk factors for MDS-related UTCA, the control group consisted of patients with MDS but without diagnosed UTCA during the observation period. Some of these patients may still develop UTCA in the future, potentially leading to patient misclassification. As an observational retrospective study based on registry-level data, the present work can only demonstrate population-level associations rather than causal relationships. Moreover, although propensity score matching and the Weibull accelerated failure time model were applied to address imbalance in observable variables and violations of the proportional hazards assumption, these statistical approaches cannot compensate for unavailable or unmeasured clinical covariates within the SEER database. Therefore, residual confounding caused by unrecorded treatment details, biologic heterogeneity, and other unmeasured factors remains unavoidable. All these limitations may introduce potential biases and should be addressed in future prospective studies and mechanistic investigations.
