Neoadjuvant Capecitabine in Operable HPV‐Negative Head and Neck Cancer: Fortuitous Findings in a Resource Constrained Setting
Authors: Marco A Mascarella, Keith Richardson, Alex Mlynarek, Michael P Hier, Derin Caglar, Livia Florianova, Marc Philippe Pusztaszeri, Khalil Sultanem, Nader Sadeghi, Nathaniel Bouganim, Khashayar Esfahani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveLimited progress has occurred in treating operable human papillomavirus (HPV)‐negative head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Accessing timely care remains challenging in public health care systems, potentially resulting in disease progression before treatment initiation.Study DesignA prospective cohort of patients receiving neoadjuvant capecitabine (NC) was compared to stage‐matched patients undergoing standard of care (SC).SettingThis study was performed at 2 academic centers in Montreal, Canada.MethodsTo ascertain the effect of 2 cycles of NC in operable HPV‐negative HNSCC patients on clinical‐to‐pathologic stage migration. Comparison to an SC group was performed to site and TNM stage matched patients. Pathologic treatment response was measured using the modified Ryan score.ResultsWe compared 16 NC patients (11 oral cavity, 3 skin, 2 larynx) with 32 SC patients. Ten NC patients exhibited a pathologic response (1 complete, 3 major, 6 minor). Clinical‐to‐pathologic stage migration differed significantly between NC and SC groups: downstage (6 vs 1), upstage (3 vs 14), no change (7 vs 17, P = .0047). There was no severe treatment toxicity related to capecitabine. All patients in the NC group underwent surgery.ConclusionNC followed by surgery demonstrates measurable pathologic response in HPV‐negative HNSCC, suggesting potential utility in resource‐limited health care settings.Keywords: health care system, HPV‐negative cancer, neoadjuvant capecitabine, pathologic response, stage migration, surgery

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) remains one of the few cancer types with increasing incidence over the last decades attributed to changes in North American demographics and risk factors. 1 , 2 Apart from patients with human papillomavirus (HPV)‐related disease, little has changed to curative‐intent regimens in patients with resectable HNSCC. The current standard of care (SC) includes surgery followed by risk‐adjusted adjuvant (chemo)radiotherapy. Often, planned microvascular reconstruction is necessary to allow for safe and wide‐margin clearance and return of function. Timely access to the operating room is necessary to avoid cancer upstaging and mortality. Delays to treatment initiation beyond 46 days from diagnosis are associated with worse outcomes in HNSCC. 3 , 4 , 5 A fact that highlights the significance of treatment delays is that most patients with resectable disease who wait beyond this timeframe have a locoregional failure. 4 , 5 In fact, roughly 35% of patients with stage III/IVA HNSCC recur in the primary site or neck within 2 years of treatment. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 The management of locoregional recurrence remains a challenge as most recurrences are unresectable and not amenable to curative doses of radiation.

The role of chemotherapy in HNSCC has been established in the adjuvant and recurrent/metastatic settings. 6 , 7 Several clinical trials utilizing neoadjuvant platinum‐based chemotherapy followed by surgery showed improved overall survival only in patients with nodal disease; there was little benefit in other patient types. 8 , 9 Newer neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens, consisting of cisplatin with docetaxel, are well tolerated in head and neck cancer patients. 8 , 9 , 10 The rise in the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors, specifically PD‐1 inhibitors, is now standard in patients with recurrent or metastatic head and neck cancer. 11 , 12 However, access to intravenous treatments for patients with HNSCC remains a challenge. Alternatives to intravenous therapy include capecitabine, an oral prodrug of fluorouracil. Capecitabine has shown response in multiple cancer types including HNSCC. 13 Often well tolerated, this drug is more commonly given in the metastatic or recurrent setting. The clinical benefit of capecitabine in HNSCC has been shown to be in the realm of 24% to 63%. 13 , 14

Within the current public health care system, delays to access the operating room and intravenous systemic therapy have significantly worsened since the COVID pandemic, increasing the risk of poorer outcomes. 15 . Delays to treatment initiation beyond 6 weeks have shown disease upstaging and poorer oncologic outcomes in this population. 2 , 16 Within the public health care system, the time‐to‐treatment initiation (TTI) in patients with operable stage III and IVa HNSCC is variable and often exceeds the proposed timeframe of 28 days from pathological diagnosis according to certain provincial regulations. Delays are often attributed to access to diagnostic imaging, the operating room, and medical optimization. The proposed benefit of short‐course neoadjuvant capecitabine (NC) prior to surgery in this population would allow for an earlier TTI, reduction in tumor volume and potential downstage migration with little associated treatment toxicity.

Given the pattern of failure in patients with HNSCC, an unmet gap in therapy exists where earlier treatment initiation is needed to address quality in HNSCC care. We propose the use of oral capecitabine followed by surgery and risk‐adjusted adjuvant therapy as decided by a multidisciplinary oncology team (MDT). Such a protocol would allow for earlier initiation of treatment within 2 weeks of pathological diagnosis with the added benefit of potentially less morbid surgery and a low toxicity profile.

Methods

Study Design

We performed a prospective case series quality improvement project, Capecitabine Prior to Tumor Resection in Ent oncology (CAPTURE), as designed through a prospective cohort study of patients ascribed to NC (2 cycles) followed by surgery in patients with treatment‐naïve American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) eighth edition stage III/IVA HNSCC as decided by the MDT to evaluate the TTI (Figure 1). For patients selected to receive capecitabine, given health care resource constraints, capecitabine treatment was targeted to be initiated within 10 days of confirmed pathological diagnosis. Adjuvant therapy would be decided following a multidisciplinary head and neck cancer tumor board discussion. Figure 1 illustrates the patient flow while on study. The objectives of the study were (1) to ascertain the pathologic treatment response in patients with stage III/IVa non‐HPV HNSCC and (2) to assess the feasibility of surgery and grade > 2 Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) toxicity related to NC. Research ethics approval was obtained was obtained for the study from the Jewish General Hospital.

CAPTURE protocol. The proposed protocol for CApecitabine Prior to TUmor Resection in Ent oncology (CAPTURE).

Patient Population

Treatment naïve patients with AJCC eighth edition stage operable III/IVA non‐HPV‐related HNSCC awaiting surgical resection where the delay would be predicted to be beyond 4 weeks were included in the study. Patients with a prior history of HNSCC or of radiation to the head and neck were excluded. SC patients were patients with the above inclusion criteria; however, were operated prior to the initiation of the CAPTURE protocol (historical cohort treated in 2021‐2023).

Capecitabine

Neoadjuvant therapy consisted of capecitabine 1500 mg bid on Days 1 to 14, followed by 1 week off, for a total of 2 cycles. Dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase testing for patients with a metabolic deficiency was performed on a case‐by‐case basis. Extirpative surgery followed within 1 to 2 weeks of completion of neoadjuvant therapy. All patients were evaluated by a medical oncologist during capecitabine therapy. The duration of 2 cycles was selected as most surgeries could be scheduled by the end of 2 cycles.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the clinical‐pathologic stage migration comparing the pretreatment clinical stage to the postoperative pathological stage. Patients with operable head and neck cancer who have clinical‐pathologic downstage migration while receiving neoadjvant therapy showed improved survival. 17 As such, downstage migration represents an early biomarker of biologically less aggressive disease. 17 The pathological response was evaluated using the modified Ryan scoring system with 4 tumor regression grades (TRGs). The TRG 0 to 3 are defined as follows: TRG 0: no viable cancer cells (complete response); TRG 1: single cells or rare small groups of cancer cells (near complete response); TRG 2: residual cancer with evident tumor regression but more than single cell or rare small groups of cancer cells (partial response); TRG 3: extensive residual cancer with no evident tumor regression (poor or no response). 16 , 18 Another clinically meaningful endpoint was the feasibility of surgery by evaluating the number of patients able to undergo surgery within 9 weeks of study initiation. Additionally, adverse events will be recorded using the CTCAE (version 5).

Covariates

Sociodemographic covariates including age, sex, smoking, and alcohol history was collected in addition, to the patients' Charlson Comorbidity Index. Tumor‐related factors, including the clinical and pathological TNM according to the AJCC eighth edition were noted. Adverse pathologic features were also be noted. Clinical staging was performed on all patients prior to and during the multidisciplinary tumor boards. Pathologic staging was performed following extirpative surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sociodemographic, clinical, and tumor‐related features among patients. Continuous and categorical variables were evaluated with nonparametric tests of association. A 1:2 comparison was performed between patients receiving NC compared to SC while matching for preoperative tumor location, T‐classification and N‐classification to evaluate for baseline versus capecitabine‐related clinical‐pathologic stage migration.

Results

Sixteen patients were prospectively followed after a multidisciplinary tumor board discussion to initiate NC while awaiting surgery (Table 1). The average age was 72 years old with 81% male patients. In total, 11 patients with oral cavity carcinoma, 3 with metastatic skin carcinoma, and 2 with larynx carcinoma were evaluated. All patients underwent surgery within 9 weeks of pathologic diagnosis. Ten patients had clinically evident tumor volume reduction with concordant pathologic response. In 1 case, there was a complete pathologic response (cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma metastasis to the parotid gland). There were 5 patients with a modified TRG 2 (partial response) and 10 with a score of TRG 3, of which 2 patients had significant evidence of fibrosis within involved lymph nodes and 1 patient had 15% necrosis within the primary tumor (Figure 2). One patient had disease progression and rapid recurrence following surgery. Downstage migration occurred in 5 patients. One patient had clinical‐pathologic upstaging. Seven patients received adjuvant therapy following surgery based on multidisciplinary tumor board recommendations. The mean follow‐up time from diagnosis was 3.5 months. The most common capecitabine‐related adverse event was a CTCAE grade 1 dermatitis. One patient required cessation of the medication after the first cycle due to a suspected coronary angiospasm. There was no grade ≥ 3 adverse event related to capecitabine.

Discussion

In this case series, NC was safe, feasible, and conferred a pathological response rate of roughly 63% with 1 patient achieving complete pathologic response. There was no severe capecitabine‐related toxicity and all patients underwent surgery. Downstage migration was much more common in patients on NC compared to patients receiving SC while upstage migration showed the reverse effect. Moreover, adjuvant therapy was thought to be safely avoided in 4 patients. When evaluating cancer subsites, patients with metastatic cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma to the parotid or neck both had a pathological reduction in tumor volume as compared to those with mucosal carcinoma primaries.

The notion of this treatment paradigm utilizing NC prior to surgery is that early initiation of capecitabine acts 2‐fold: (1) stagnation if not reduction in tumor volume and (2) potential chemoselection of patients based on clinical‐pathologic response. Disease progression while on capecitabine occurred in a single patient who also showed rapid recurrence following surgery. Growth during the preoperative capecitabine regimen showed a negative chemoselection, which illustrated the biological aggressiveness of this patient's HNSCC.

Two cycles of NC demonstrated favorable pathologic changes including tumor necrosis on histology and treatment response along involved nerves clinically. Most patients had a modified Ryan score of TRG 3, indicating little or no response to neoadjuvant therapy. However, this was the case even in patients with a clinical and/or pathologic reduction in tumor size. Given the defined modified Ryan score pathological evaluation criteria, it is possible these criteria may not be precise enough to accurately reflect tumors with stagnated growth, which may still show an overall pre‐operative capecitabine benefit in the form of non‐progression while patients await surgery. From a toxicity perspective, capecitabine was given to a range of patients: elderly, multicomorbid, and with advanced‐stage disease. Discontinuation only occurred in 1 patient due to suspicion of coronary vasospasm. That specific patient did not have any cardiac sequala following discontinuation of the medication and apart from a nuclear medicine heart scan, did not require ancillary medical evaluation.

Given the current study was planned as a pilot evaluation of capecitabine in HPV‐negative operable HNSCC, several limitations exist. First, response to neoadjuvant therapy was based on clinical to pathological stage migration which has its inherent limitations related to radiologic inaccuracies in tumor and nodal staging. As such, the modified Ryan score was used to evaluate histopathologic response to treatment. Matching was performed to reduce variables that may confound the association between treatment with capecitabine and treatment response. This is an inherent limitation to an observational study as randomization was not performed. That being said, little is currently available in the preoperative window as treatment to halt the growth of tumors. The results of this pilot study show evidence for the use of NC in patients with HPV‐negative HNSCC. A phase II clinical study, comparing NC to placebo with SC treatment could be the next step to measure the true effect of this regimen.

Conclusion

NC, given promptly during a pre‐operative therapeutic window of opportunity, and followed by extirpative surgery in patients with HPV‐negative HNSCC is safe, feasible, and has shown a measurable pathologic response. Further study is needed to evaluate the efficacy of NC in the treatment paradigm of HPV‐negative HNSCC in a resource‐constrained health care system.
