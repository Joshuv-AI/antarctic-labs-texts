Brown fat-specific RNA labelling reveals a network of inter-organ communication by secreted microRNAs
Authors: Marsel Lino, Gabriel Palermo-Ruiz, Vissarion Efthymiou, Alice Rodrigues, Shraddha Bhonsle, Allah Nawaz, Anindya Ghosh, Bruna Brasil Brandão, Yoshiyuki Watanabe, Rafael Barrera Salgueiro, Yingying Yu, C. Ronald Kahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extracellular microRNAs (miRNAs) are emerging as key regulators of organismal homeostasis. Here, using tissue-specific RNA labelling based on uracil phosphoribosyltransferase-mediated incorporation of 4-thiouracil in male mice, we develop a roadmap of miRNA transfer from brown adipose tissue (BAT) to other tissues, where they can act to regulate energy metabolism. We show that BAT secretes miRNAs in both small extracellular vesicles and is associated with plasma proteins, and that miRNAs in both compartments exhibit tissue-selective uptake in the liver, muscle and hypothalamus. Disruption of Dicer in BAT leads to a significant depletion of multiple miRNAs in both BAT and distal tissues, including 80–90% decreases in the most abundant miRNAs in muscle. Target analyses, in vitro modelling and PAR-CLIP analysis in muscle in vivo confirm that these BAT-secreted miRNAs directly interact with target mRNAs and alter mitochondrial function in recipient tissues. These findings provide a framework for understanding the role of BAT-secreted miRNAs in inter-organ communication.

Main

Circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) are secreted into plasma via highly ordered mechanisms either encapsulated in small extracellular vesicles (sEVs)/exosomes or bound to plasma proteins, such as Argonaute1,2 or apolipoproteins3. Work by us4–9 and others has shown that adipose tissue, and especially brown fat (BAT), is a major source of circulating miRNAs10–13. The presence of active BAT is metabolically beneficial and is associated with cardioprotective benefits in rodents and humans14. BAT is characterized by high mitochondrial density and expression of uncoupling protein-1 (UCP1)15–17, promoting energy expenditure in the form of heat. Indeed, the effects of BAT are due, in part, to its ability to expend energy. However, BAT also acts via secretion of a variety of bioactive molecules, which promote metabolically beneficial effects in distal organs. These have been collectively referred to as ‘BATokines’ and include metabolites, proteins, lipids and miRNAs18,19.

Recent work by our laboratory and others has shown that at least some of the miRNAs secreted by BAT can be taken up by other tissues and regulate gene expression and metabolic processes4,13. For instance, BAT-secreted miR-99b-5p and miR-378 are taken up by liver they suppress hepatic Fgf21 expression4 and regulate gluconeogenesis20, respectively. We and others have also shown that miRNA secretion in sEVs is a highly regulated process, dependent in part on nucleotide motifs within miRNAs5,8, such that the profile of secreted miRNAs represents a subset of those in the secreting cell5,8,21–23. miRNA secretion from adipose24 and other tissues25 can also be regulated by insulin8 and other stimuli26–29. The importance of circulating miRNAs from adipose tissue is further exemplified by the fact that Dicer, a key enzyme in miRNA biogenesis, becomes downregulated in adipose tissue of aged mice and restored by caloric restriction, mirroring changes in many circulating miRNAs9. Indeed, adipocyte-specific deletion of Dicer or loss of adipose tissue in mice or humans with lipodystrophy is associated with marked downregulation of multiple circulating miRNAs, which can be partly restored by transplantation of fat, particularly brown fat, from wild-type mice, and this is paralleled by major alterations in metabolic status and insulin resistance4. Together, these studies support the existence of a highly ordered and metabolically regulated mechanism of miRNA sorting and secretion in adipose tissue, which allows adipose-derived circulating miRNAs to serve as inter-tissue metabolic regulators.

At present, however, comprehensive demonstration of inter-tissue miRNA transfer in a high-throughput manner in vivo has not been possible. Here we used an approach of labelling miRNAs in BAT in vivo with 4-thiouracil (4TU) in mice with BAT-specific expression of microbial uracil phosphoribosyltransferase (UPRT)30. We find that individual miRNAs are secreted from BAT to different extents via sEVs or bound to plasma proteins. More notably, we observe that these secreted miRNAs exhibit differential uptake into liver, muscle and hypothalamus, and, in many cases, contribute significantly to the most abundant of these miRNAs in these tissues. Finally, we show that BAT-derived miRNAs enter the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) in distal tissues, where they directly interact with target genes and regulate metabolic processes in recipient tissues, including BAT-to-muscle suppression of FoxO1 by miR-1a-3p. Taken together, these findings demonstrate a robust network of cellular communication mediated by secreted miRNAs and involved in endocrine regulation.

Brown fat-specific RNA labelling in vivo using 4-thiouracil and assessment of brown fat small RNAs by 4TU-smRNA-seq

Uracil phosphoribosyltransferase (UPRT) catalyses the conversion of uracil to uridine monophosphate (Fig. 1a). UPRT homologues in mammals and other higher-eukaryotes, however, show no enzymatic activity, making transgenic expression of microbial UPRT a method for cell-specific RNA labelling with 4TU30,31. Using this principle to label and track miRNAs produced specifically in brown fat (BAT), we bred male mice carrying the gene for an HA-tagged enzymatically active UPRT from Toxoplasma gondii behind a lox-STOP-lox cassette with mice carrying Cre-recombinase driven by the UCP1 promoter resulting in BAT-specific expression of UPRT, as confirmed by HA-immunoblotting (Fig. 1b) and qPCR (Extended Data Fig. 1b). To label RNA, 4TU was administered via intraperitoneal injection (50 mg kg−1 every 48 h for 7 days), followed by RNA extraction and biotinylation of 4TU moieties for downstream detection by streptavidin–HRP (Fig. 1c,d). Consistent with UPRT expression, 4TU incorporation into RNA could only be detected in BAT from UCP1;UPRT mice and not in BAT or subcutaneous white fat (sWAT) from littermate, UPRTfl/fl control mice (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1c). To purify 4TU-labelled RNAs for downstream analysis, biotin–4TU RNA complexes were precipitated with streptavidin–Dynabeads. Efficacy of pulldown was validated using synthetic RNA oligonucleotides containing a single 4TU moiety, which confirmed that only 4TU-containing biotinylated oligonucleotides could be pulled down (Extended Data Fig. 1d). We further confirmed BAT-specific labelling by identification of 4TU RNA transcripts in BAT, but not liver, of UCP1;UPRT mice. Thus, Malat1 (nuclear lncRNA), Rab11b and Chmp2b (involved extracellular vesicle biogenesis), were labelled in BAT and regulated by stimulation with β-3-adrenergic agonist (CL 316,243) but not labelled in the liver (Extended Data Fig. 1e). Of note, miR-99b-5p, previously shown to be transferred from BAT to liver4 was labelled in BAT and increased ~fourfold by β3-agonist, resulting in a parallel increase in labelled miR-99b-5p in the livers of these mice (Extended Data Fig. 1f). Likewise, several miRNAs were upregulated in muscle following BAT stimulation, including miR-1a-3p, which is the most abundant miRNA in muscle (Extended Data Fig. 1h). Notably, there was a decrease of primary miR-1 in muscle (Extended Data Fig. 1i), which, along with the fact β3-adrenergic receptors are not found in liver and muscle32, indicates that these miRNAs may be transferred from BAT. Together, these data clearly demonstrate BAT-specific labelling of RNAs in UCP1;UPRT mice with 4TU and the transfer of one exemplary miRNA from BAT to liver.

To define the full complement of small noncoding RNAs in BAT and their labelling by 4TU, we performed small RNA (smRNA)-seq in 4TU-enriched and total RNA fractions from BAT of UCP1;UPRT mice. Among total smRNAs, miRNAs comprised 54% of reads, followed by tRNAs (33%), rRNAs (9%), snoRNAs (2%) and snRNAs (1%) (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 2a). In the 4TU-enriched fraction, tRNAs were the most abundant (52%), followed by miRNAs (34%), rRNAs (11%), snoRNAs (2%), snRNAs (1%) and piRNAs (1%) (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 2a). As we and others have shown that secreted miRNAs can mediate intercellular communication4,7,33, we focused on miRNAs. Of the 658 miRNAs identified by sequencing, we focused on the top 20% of most labelled miRNAs in BAT (Fig. 1g). For these 131 miRNAs, there was a strong correlation (R2 = 0.90) between 4TU miRNA and total miRNA abundance in BAT (Fig. 1h), indicating near steady-state labelling. While there was a small trend to increased labelling of miRNAs dependent on their uracil content, there was no correlation between miRNA uracil content and labelling efficiency (Extended Data Fig. 2b), suggesting that the main driver of labelling efficiency is RNA turnover. Notably, miR-1a-3p, miR-206-3p and miR-133a-3p (Fig. 1h), sometimes referred to as ‘myomiRs’ due to their abundance in muscle34–37, were among the top 4TU miRNAs in BAT. Other well-labelled miRNAs included the let-7 family of miRNAs, which have well-described functions in adipogenesis and are abundant in adipose tissue8,38, as well as miR-99b-5p, miR-378a-3p and miR-143-3p, which have known metabolic functions4,20.

Brown fat secretes 4TU miRNAs in small extracellular vesicle- and non-sEV-associated plasma fractions

Circulating miRNAs are associated with a variety of carriers that confer protection from RNases and facilitate transfer to distal organs1,3,39–41. These include small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), as well as non-sEV carriers such as RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), Argonaute (Ago2)1 and lipid particles (VLDL/LDL and HDL)3,6,42. To gain a better understanding of how BAT miRNAs are released into the circulation, we isolated plasma from UCP1;UPRT mice and fractionated it into sEV-enriched and non-sEV compartments by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Fig. 2a). As expected, sEVs were enriched in fractions 2–7 as determined by nanoparticle-tracking analysis (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 3a), and immunoblotting revealed sEV-marker proteins including Alix, Cav-1 and CD9 in these fractions (Fig. 2b). On the other hand, fractions 11–18 (hereafter referred to as non-sEV fractions) were relatively devoid of nanoparticles, contained no detectable level of sEV-marker proteins, but were enriched in proteins, including Ago2, ApoB100 (a component of VLDL/LDL), and Apo-AI (a component of HDL) (Fig. 2a,b and Extended Data Fig. 3a). qPCR analysis of fractionated plasma for three exemplary miRNAs showed that miR-21a-5p was almost exclusively found in sEV fractions, whereas let-7c-5p was mainly found in non-sEV fractions and miR-199a-3p was detected in both (Fig. 2c). This phenomenon is not limited to mice. Thus, compared with human white adipose tissue, we also found abundant levels of miRNA in sEVs from differentiated human brown adipocytes (hBATs) (Extended Data Fig. 3b), which for miR-1a-3p levels were ~fivefold higher in hBAT sEVs compared with differentiated human white adipocyte (hWAT) sEVs (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Treatment with RNase A alone had no effect in miR-1a-3p expression in sEV and non-sEV fractions, whereas pretreatment with Triton X-100 and RNase A resulted in an 86.5% decrease in miR-1a-3p in sEVs but not the non-sEV compartment (Extended Data Fig. 3c), confirming miRNA incorporation into sEVs. To better define miRNA secretion by BAT, we separately pooled sEV and non-sEV fractions, and sequenced 4TU-enriched and total RNA fractions by smRNA-seq to identify 4TU-labelled, BAT-derived smRNAs in plasma. Consistent with previous studies8,43, in both sEV and non-sEV fractions, the most abundant smRNAs were tRNA/tRFs. The second-most abundant were miRNAs, which represented 27% of reads in sEVs versus 17% in non-sEV compartments. The remainder of the reads in sEVs were snoRNA, snRNA and piRNA, whereas these were almost absent (~1% each) in the non-sEV fractions (Extended Data Fig. 3d). In contrast, in the 4TU-enriched fraction, miRNAs represented 48% and 35% 4TU miRNA reads in sEV and non-sEV compartments, demonstrating that BAT is a major contributor to circulating miRNAs relative to other smRNAs (Extended Data Fig. 3d). Altogether, we detected 55 4TU miRNAs abundantly in plasma, with a spectrum of abundance in sEVs versus non-sEV fractions. By calculating the relative enrichment score (defined as log2(sEV/non-sEV)), we found 18 4TU miRNAs enriched in sEVs by >1.8-fold (Fig. 2d), including let-7-f2, which was ~45-times more abundant in sEVs than non-sEVs and the exemplar miRNA miR-21a-5p (Fig. 2c), which was ~eightfold more abundant in sEVs/non-sEVs (Fig. 2d,e), whereas 18 were enriched in the non-sEV compartment by ≥1.7-fold (Fig. 2d), including the exemplar let-7c-5p and miR-339-5p, which was 32-fold more abundant in non-sEVs than sEVs (Fig. 2d,e). The remaining 19 4TU miRNAs were approximately equally abundant in both compartments (Fig. 2d). Thus, miRNAs are secreted from BAT to different degrees via sEV and non-sEV mechanisms independent of their abundance in plasma.

Not surprisingly, some of the most abundant 4TU miRNAs in plasma were also abundantly labelled in BAT, including the sEV-enriched let-7g-5p and let-7f-2 and the non-sEV-enriched let-7f-5p and let-7c-5p (Fig. 3a, asterisks). The most abundant plasma miRNA, miR-1a-3p (log2(CPM) = 15.2, with 58% of reads in sEVs, where CPM is counts per million), was also highly abundant in BAT (log2(CPM) = 11.7) (Fig. 3a, asterisk). On the other hand, for some 4TU miRNAs there were significant differences between abundance in blood relative to BAT. Thus, miR-122-5p had comparatively low abundance in BAT (log2(CPM) = 4.5) but was the second-most abundant 4TU miRNA in plasma (log2(CPM) = 14.2, with 52.6% in sEV) (Fig. 3a, asterisk). Overall, there was only a weak correlation between abundance in BAT and abundance in plasma. Hence, labelled miR-125a-5p, let-7c-5p and let-7a-5p were abundant in BAT but present at relatively low levels in plasma, whereas miR-375-3p, miR-92b-5p and miR-340-5p were more abundant in plasma compared with BAT (Fig. 3a (asterisks) and Extended Data Fig. 3e). Previous work from our laboratory5,8 and others23,44 has shown that a major determinant of miRNA sorting into sEVs is the presence of nucleotide motifs embedded within miRNAs, which can be recognized by RBPs, such as Fus, hnRNPA1 and Syncrip5,8. To determine whether this could also be observed in vivo, we performed sequence analysis among 4TU-labelled miRNAs secreted into sEV or non-sEV compartments using MEME Suite45. This revealed several 5–7 nucleotide motifs that were significantly over-represented in miRNAs enriched in sEV versus non-sEV fractions (Fig. 3b–d). Thus, a ‘UCUKGG’ motif was found in 7 of 18 miRNAs enriched in sEVs (P = 7.33 × 10−4) but was not detected in miRNAs not enriched in sEVs (Fig. 3b). Similarly, a ‘UGAGK’ motif was detected in 11 of 20 miRNAs secreted by both mechanisms (Fig. 3c), whereas a ‘CCUCCA’ motif was detected in 9 of 19 miRNAs secreted primarily by the non-sEV (protein-bound) fraction (P = 4.94 × 10−5) (Fig. 3d). Among the 80 4TU miRNAs well labelled in BAT but not found in any compartment of secreted 4TU miRNAs identified ‘URCCU’, ‘BUDCUG’ and ‘CKUUC’ motifs in 72–86% of miRNAs not detected in plasma, but only 0–6% of secreted miRNAs (Fig. 3e). Cross-referencing these data with known motif-binding preferences of RBPs, identified motifs in sEV miRNAs that matched the recognition motifs of PTBP3, PUF60, FUBP1/3 and IGF2BP1, as well as motifs in non-sEV miRNAs that match recognition motifs for SRSF2/4/5/8/10 and RBM6/23 (Extended Data Fig. 4b,c). Motifs in BAT miRNAs not found in plasma more closely matched the recognition motifs of PCBP2/4 and RBM22/23/45 proteins (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Together, these data demonstrate that secretion of different miRNAs occurs via vesicular and non-vesicular routes, likely in part dependent on motifs present in the miRNAs themselves. Further work will be required to validate this hypothesis.

Tracking BAT-derived 4TU miRNAs to the liver, muscle and hypothalamus

Tracking BAT-secreted 4TU miRNAs to distal organs, dot blot analysis of biotinylated 4TU RNA from UCP1;UPRT mice revealed abundant 4TU RNA in BAT, but also strong labelling in liver, muscle, heart and brain, but no signal in pancreas and near-background levels in spleen and kidney (Extended Data Fig. 5a), consistent with inter-tissue trafficking of 4TU-labelled miRNAs from BAT to some, but not all, of these tissues. To track BAT miRNAs to distal tissues, we performed smRNA-seq in total RNA and 4TU-enriched RNA from liver, muscle and hypothalamus of these mice. Not surprisingly, smRNA composition differed considerably from tissue to tissue. For example, in the total smRNA-seq, snoRNAs were very abundant in the hypothalamus, at significant levels in BAT and liver, and at very low levels in muscle (Extended Data Fig. 5b). In contrast, in the 4TU fraction, while snoRNAs represented approximately 12% of the 4TU smRNAs in BAT, there were no 4TU snoRNAs in the hypothalamus or other tissues, indicating that snoRNAs are not transferred from BAT to other tissues (Extended Data Fig. 5b). In comparison, the most abundant 4TU smRNAs in the liver muscle and hypothalamus, as well as plasma, were miRNAs followed by tRNAs/tRFs (Extended Data Fig. 5b), consistent with the notion that these small RNAs can be transferred from BAT to these tissues.

Roadmap of inter-tissue trafficking of BAT-derived miRNAs

To develop a roadmap of inter-tissue transfer of BAT-derived 4TU-labelled miRNAs, we integrated the 4TU miRNA data in the plasma sEV and non-sEV fractions with their accumulation in the liver, muscle and hypothalamus. The miRNAs are divided into three groups according to plasma compartment (sEV-enriched, non-sEV-enriched or both) and connected to their site of accumulation, with the thickness of the line indicating the relative abundance of the 4TU miRNA in the receiving tissue (Fig. 4a). Of the 55 4TU miRNAs detected in BAT and secreted into plasma, 17 became enriched in muscle, 13 in the liver and 25 in the hypothalamus (Fig. 4a). Of note, tissue targeting (tissue-enriched miRNA accumulation), was observed for all classes of carrier. Thus, among muscle-targeted miRNAs, three were secreted mainly in sEVs (including let-7f-2, miR-10b-5p and miR-199a-3p), whereas six were primarily in the non-sEV fraction (including let-7f-5p, miR-206-3p and miR-133a-3p), and another eight were found in both plasma compartments (including let7i-5p, miR-486b-5p, and miR-1a-3p). It is worth noting that after the 1-week labelling period, the levels of labelled miR-1a-3p, miR-206-3p and miR-133a-3p were similar in the target tissue muscle as the source tissue BAT (Fig. 4b and Extended Data Fig. 6a), indicating high level of transfer of these miRNAs to muscle. This is especially noteworthy as miR-1a-3p is the most abundant miRNA in muscle and has been referred to as a ‘myomiR’36. In a similar fashion, 13 BAT-derived and 4TU-labelled circulating miRNAs accumulated preferentially in the liver (Fig. 4a,c and Extended Data Fig. 6b). Nearly half (6 of 13) of these were transported in sEVs, whereas only 3 were found primarily in the non-sEV compartment. Among the liver-targeted 4TU miRNAs were miR-148a-3p, miR-21a-5p and miR-122-5p, all miRNAs regarded as markers of liver disease46–48. Indeed, miR-122-5p is the most abundant miRNA in the liver, representing up to 70% of miRNA reads49, and the level of 4TU-labelled miR-122-5p in the liver exceeded that in BAT by several orders of magnitude (Fig. 4c), indicating a very high rate of transfer. Consistent with this, labelled miR-122 was the second-most abundant 4TU miRNA in plasma, despite being only of intermediate abundance in BAT (Fig. 3a). Finally, nearly half of BAT-labelled miRNAs found in plasma became enriched in the hypothalamus. Among these, nine were transported mainly in sEVs, nine in the non-sEV fraction and seven miRNAs in both (Fig. 4a). The hypothalamus-enriched miRNAs included several members of the let-7 family (let-7a-e/g-5p), as well as miR-30d-5p and miR-125a/b-5p (Fig. 4a,d and Extended Data Fig. 6c). To determine whether both -3p and -5p arms of miRNAs can be transferred, we plotted total and 4TU-labelled levels of both arms for three highly transferred miRNAs, miR-1a, miR-122 and miR-125b (Fig. 4e-g). Both arms were detected in the total miRNA fraction, but only the BAT-labelled arm was transferred to the recipient tissue. Thus, we only detected 4TU miR-1a-3p, 4TU miR-122-5p and 4TU miR-125b-5p in plasma and recipient tissues, despite readily detectable levels of the opposite arm in these tissues (Fig. 4e-g). This is evidence of endogenous miRNA production, whereas the fact that only one (and often the dominant) arm is found in the 4TU fraction is evidence of BAT-specific labelling followed by strand selection and sorting for secretion. Finally, it is worth noting that an additional 80 4TU miRNAs were detected in BAT and had no detectable level in either sEV or non-sEV fractions of plasma but still appeared in other tissues. Of these, 14 accumulated primarily in liver, 15 in muscle, 39 in hypothalamus and 12 in a combination of these tissues (Extended Data Fig. 5c). It is possible that plasma levels were below the limit of detection because these miRNAs were rapidly taken up or that they were transferred via an alternative route, for example, lymphatic vessels or a cellular carrier, such as macrophages or neurons.

Brown fat secreted miRNAs have functional effects in recipient tissues

To understand what biological processes may be regulated by BAT miRNAs, we performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis using predicted gene targets of miRNAs enriched in muscle, liver and hypothalamus (Fig. 5a). Perhaps not surprisingly, the most targeted pathways by BAT miRNAs in tissues were pathways related to cancer, as miRNAs are implicated in cancer development, acting as either tumour suppressors or oncogenes50. However, the different miRNAs taken up by each tissue targeted distinct pathways. Thus, muscle-enriched miRNAs targeted genes in metabolic pathways, including FoxO and insulin signalling, as well as genes linked to diabetic complications (Fig. 5a). Liver-enriched miRNAs also targeted FoxO and TGFβ signalling but on different genes than muscle-enriched miRNAs. For example, muscle-enriched miRNAs targeted FoxO, Akt and PTEN, whereas liver-enriched miRNAs targeted Grb/Sos, Pi3K and mTOR components. Of note, BAT miRNAs enriched in the hypothalamus were predicted to target axon guidance and long-term potentiation pathways that were not targeted by the muscle or liver-enriched miRNAs (Fig. 5a). Stratification of miRNAs targeted to different tissues via sEV and non-sEV routes also revealed carrier-specific pathways targeted in each tissue (Extended Data Fig. 7a–c). For instance, among muscle-targeted miRNAs, sEV-associated miRNAs preferentially targeted ECM/cytoskeleton and cell junctions, whereas autophagy and cAMP signalling were targeted by miRNAs enriched in the non-sEV compartment (Extended Data Fig. 7a). As noted above, FoxO1 emerged as a highly targeted gene by BAT miRNAs (Fig. 5a). To test this, we transfected C2C12 muscle cells with mimics for nine of the most abundantly transferred miRNAs, and found that four of the nine, including miR-99b, miR-1a-3p, miR-203-3p and miR-21a-5p, produced significant decreases in FoxO1 protein, with miR-1a-3p having the biggest effect (~80% decrease, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 8a). We confirm direct targeting of Foxo1 by miR-1a-3p, by pulldown of Ago2–RISC using a Flag-tagged T6B peptide51 and a luciferase reporter assay. Both miR-1a-3p and Foxo1 mRNA were enriched by Ago2–RISC pulldown (Fig. 5e), and luciferase expression was decreased by miR-1a-3p in C2C12 cells expressing luciferase reporter conjugated to 3′UTR-Foxo1 (Extended Data Fig. 8b).

As FoxO1 is a major regulator of mitochondrial physiology in muscle52,53, we assessed whether BAT-derived miRNAs in plasma could affect cellular respiration using C2C12 myoblasts, widely used to model muscle physiology in vitro. Cells were treated with conditioned medium supplemented with plasma from wild-type and BAT;DicerKO mice that lack miRNAs in BAT. Indeed, supplementation with plasma from wild-type mice produced a significant increase in basal and maximal respiration relative to plasma from BAT;DicerKO mice (Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 7d). This suggested that BAT miRNAs may be a driver of cellular respiration in muscle. Focusing on some of the most abundant BAT miRNAs in plasma, we transfected C2C12 cells with miR-1a-3p, miR-122-5p and let-7a, and found that miR-1a-3p, which preferentially traffics to muscle, robustly increased cellular in C2C12 cells (Fig. 5e). By contrast, miR-122-5p, which preferentially targets liver, suppressed maximal respiration, whereas let-7a-5p, which preferentially targets hypothalamus, had no effect on cellular respiration in these cells (Fig. 5e and Extended Data Fig. 7e). Overexpressing miR-1a-3p did not alter Foxo1 mRNA, consistent with post-transcriptional regulation, whereas PGC1α, a key regulator of mitochondrial gene expression, was upregulated at the messenger RNA (Fig. 5f) and protein level along with mitochondrial complex III and IV proteins (Fig. 5f,g and Extended Data Fig. 8d,e), leading to increased ATP production in C2C12 cells (Fig. 5h). Thus, BAT-derived miR-1a-3p is a driver cellular respiration and PGC1α expression by suppressing FoxO1 in muscle cells (Extended Data Fig. 8f).

To further validate miRNA transfer in vitro, we co-cultured brown adipocytes with recipient muscle (C2C12) or liver (AML12) cells using a Transwell inserts, so that only secreted miRNAs could be transferred. In agreement with in vivo data, 24 h of co-culture resulted in 5- to 10,000-fold increases in miR-1a-3p, miR-122-5p, miR-378a-3p, miR-148a-3p and let-7a-5p, in both cell types (Extended Data Fig. 9a–d).Notably, miR-148a-3p was upregulated by nearly 10,000-fold in AML12 cells, but only by 100-fold in C2C12 cells (Extended Data Fig. 9b,d), consistent with targeting to the liver seen in vivo (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, miR-10a-5p, which was relatively less abundant in plasma, and miR-125b-5p, which was targeted to the hypothalamus, were not upregulated, providing evidence of selective uptake (Extended Data Fig. 9). However, both miR-1a-3p and miR-122-5p were significantly upregulated in both cell types and did not exhibit the tissue targeting seen in vivo. This could be due to limitations of the cell models used, or the absence of physiological stimuli that may be contributing the enhanced accumulation of these miRNAs in specific tissues.

Loss of BAT miRNAs leads to metabolic dysfunction in muscle

To assess the effect of BAT-derived miRNAs on mitochondrial function and metabolism in muscle in vivo, we generated BAT;DicerKO mice, which have BAT-specific loss of miRNA biogenesis. As expected, Dicer mRNA was decreased in BAT, but not in liver and muscle, of BAT;DicerKO mice (Fig. 6a). Plasma miRNA concentrations were also lower in BAT;DicerKO mice, especially in the sEV fractions (1.2 ng µl−1 versus 5.2 ng µl−1 in controls; P = 0.02) (Extended Data Fig. 10b). Notably, these mice had no difference in BAT mass, body weight, length and tissue weights (Extended Data Fig. 10a–d). Fasting blood glucose and plasma insulin levels were unchanged but were significantly lower upon refeeding (Fig. 6b–c), suggesting some level of metabolic dysfunction. Consistent with BAT-specific deletion of Dicer, BAT;DicerKO mice had a nearly 90% decrease in miR-1a-3p, miR-133a-3p, miR-378a-3p and let-7b-5p in BAT (Fig. 6d). Of note, levels of miR-1a-3p and miR-133a-3p were downregulated in muscle by 86% and 91%, respectively, paralleling the loss in BAT (Fig. 6d). Likewise, miR-122-5p, which we showed was preferentially transferred from BAT to liver, was reduced by ~50% in livers of BAT;DicerKO compared with wild-type. Notably, primary miRNA transcripts for miR-1a-3p and miR-133a-3p were higher in BAT;DicerKO muscle (Fig. 6e). Loss of Dicer and miRNA processing in BAT was associated with metabolic function in muscle, marked by diminished p-Akt (S473) in gastrocnemius from BAT;DicerKO mice following in vivo insulin-stimulation (1U kg−1 insulin or saline control, via the vena cava for 10 min) (Fig. 6f). Yet, we found reduced mitochondrial content (Fig. 6g), along with diminished AMPKα phosphorylation, and lower protein levels of PGC1α and mitochondrial complex subunits CI–CV in BAT;DicerKO muscle (Fig. 6h). These data are consistent with our in vitro findings, suggesting that under normal conditions, mitochondrial biogenesis in muscle is supported, at least in part, by repression of FoxO1 by BAT-produced miR-1a-3p.

Discussion

Inter-organ communication by secreted miRNAs is a notable layer of regulatory crosstalk between tissues and among cell types in a single tissue (reviewed elsewhere7,33). However, as many miRNAs are produced by multiple tissues, differentiating between endogenously produced and extracellularly derived miRNAs and determining their functions is a major challenge. In the present study, we used tissue-specific RNA labelling with 4TU to develop a roadmap of miRNA-mediated inter-organ crosstalk between BAT and other tissues in vivo. The 4TU/UPRT labelling technique55 has been used to label RNA in specific cells56,57, and a similar but not cell-specific approach has been used to track miRNAs58. Our work overcomes these limitations by adapting 4TU/UPRT labelling to track miRNAs specifically from BAT. Adipose tissue, and especially brown fat, is a major source of circulating miRNAs in both rodents and humans4,9,28. Our data demonstrate that at least one-third of BAT-produced miRNAs are released into plasma at significant levels. This release is selective and independent of the abundance of the miRNA in BAT. These secreted miRNAs are found in plasma across a continuum, with about one-third enriched in plasma sEVs, one-third enriched in the non-sEV fraction, and the remainder in both. Most notably, we demonstrate that different miRNAs are trafficked to and become enriched in different tissues, including liver, muscle and hypothalamus, where they can contribute significantly to the miRNA pool. Thus, miR-1a-3p, which is sometimes designated as a ‘myomiR’ due to its abundance in muscle, is synthesized in BAT, released into the circulation in both sEV and non-sEV fractions, and accumulates in muscle, whereas miR-122-5p, a miRNA designated as a ‘hepatomiR’ due to its abundance in liver, is also synthesized in BAT, released into the circulation, and accumulates in liver. Using a combination of bioinformatics and in vitro and in vivo models, we not only demonstrate transfer of miRNAs from BAT to muscle and other tissues, we show that miR-1a-3p, is actually largely derived by transfer from BAT, and that the transferred miRNA can be found in RISC complex with the mRNA of one of its target genes, FoxO1. Thus, this transferred miRNA contributes to control of mitochondrial biogenesis in the recipient tissue.

Since their discovery, miRNAs have been implicated in the regulation of key biological processes by targeting and promoting the degradation or suppressing the translation of specific mRNAs. Recent work by our laboratory4,7,59 and others28,60,61 has demonstrated that miRNAs can be secreted and also act at a distance, participating in inter-tissue gene regulation. Moreover, we showed that the process of miRNA secretion is highly regulated in multiple cell types5,8, in part due to unique nucleotide motifs within miRNAs, which help mediate secretion or retention62. In the present study, we find that this is also true in vivo but is even more complex than originally thought. Thus, among the most abundant 4TU-labelled miRNAs in BAT, 57 were detected in plasma, with 18 of these preferentially secreted into sEVs, 19 preferentially secreted into the non-sEV fraction of plasma, and the remaining 20 were found in both compartments. Of note, 80 additional miRNAs well labelled in BAT were not detected in either plasma compartment, indicating that miRNA secretion by both small sEV or bound to proteins is a selective process. Conversely, two of the 4TU-labelled miRNAs detected in plasma could not be detected in BAT, indicating that they are produced in BAT and almost exclusively secreted.

There are likely multiple mechanisms underlying this behaviour, including the presence of motifs within the miRNAs, which interact with different miRNA-binding proteins, thus favouring retention or secretion via a sEV or non-sEV route8,62. Notably, two of the three top-ranking motifs identified in sEV-enriched miRNAs from BAT in vivo contain a ‘G/CAUG’ sequence, similar to motifs identified in BAT sEV miRNAs in vitro5. Cross-referencing motifs enriched in sEV and non-sEV-secreted BAT-miRNAs with previously characterized recognition motifs in RBPs63, we identify several RBPs that recognize motifs in BAT-secreted miRNAs. Thus, FUBP1, IGF2BP1 and PTBP3 can recognize motifs found in sEV miRNAs, whereas non-sEV miRNAs possessed sequences that more closely matched the recognition motifs of the SRSF family of proteins. Notably, some of these RBPs are also known to also localize to endosomes, which is one site of miRNA sorting, and are also found in plasma64. Thus, to what extent each of these RBPs participates in miRNA sorting and secretion via different routes, as well as their potential role in targeting miRNAs to different tissues will require further study. Also, as SEC does not allow separation of miRNAs bound to potential non-sEV carrier proteins, such as Ago2, LDL and HDL1, determining exactly which non-sEV miRNAs are bound to which proteins will also require further study. Finally, it is worth noting that some 4TU-labelled miRNAs found in BAT could be tracked to liver, muscle or hypothalamus, with no detectable level in either the plasma-derived sEV and non-sEV fractions. It is possible that these miRNAs are very rapidly cleared from plasma and thus undetectable or that they are transferred via the lymphatic system or some cellular carrier, such as macrophages or neurons.

One of the most important findings of this study is that miRNAs secreted by BAT can be tracked to and accumulate differentially in recipient tissues, indicating the existence of a highly ordered mechanism of tissue targeting. Thus, BAT-secreted miR-122-5p, miR-148a-3p and miR-21a-5p preferentially accumulate in the liver, whereas miR-1a-3p, miR-133a-3p and miR-206-5p accumulate in the muscle, and miR-26a-5p, miR-181-5p,and multiple members of the let-7 family accumulate in the hypothalamus. More notably, our data show that for many of these miRNAs, a major fraction of what is present in the liver, muscle and hypothalamus is dependent on this transfer from BAT. For example, although miR-1a-3p, miR-133-a-3p and miR-206-3p have sometimes been referred to as ‘myomiRs’ because of their abundance in muscle65, 4TU-labelling in BAT demonstrates a robust transfer of all these miRNAs from BAT to muscle. Furthermore, BAT-specific deletion of the miRNA-processing enzyme Dicer, led to not only a >75% loss of these miRNAs in BAT, but a parallel loss of these miRNAs in muscle, despite no change in Dicer in these tissues. Consistent with this, and in contrast, studies by others have shown that muscle-specific Dicer knockout, resulted in only modest (30–50%) decreases in miR-1a-3p and other ‘myomiRs’ in muscle34,66. Taken together with our data, these findings suggest that a predominant amount of miR-1a-3p found in muscle is not synthesized in muscle but originates in BAT, is secreted and then accumulates in muscle. While more studies are needed, this is also likely true for miRNAs that become enriched in liver (for example, miR-122-5p) or hypothalamus (for example, let-7b/c/f/g, miR-26a-5p and miR-100-5p). Indeed, there is a 52% decrease in the ‘hepatomiR’ miR-122-5p in livers of BAT;DicerKO mice, despite that fact that in normal mice, miR-122-5p represents about 70% of the miRNA pool (our data and ref. 49). Although studies by others of liver-specific knockout of Dicer67 or miR-122 (refs. 68,69) may be incomplete due to apoptosis and replacement by non-KO cells, we find no evidence of apoptosis in BAT with Dicer knockout. The fact that miR-122 is decreased in liver of BAT-DicerKO mice with no change in Dicer in liver, together with our data showing that miR-122-5p was among the most abundant BAT-derived miRNA in plasma, supports the notion of significant levels of miR-122 transfer from BAT to liver.

Regardless of the mechanism, once they reach their destination, we and others have shown that secreted miRNAs can regulate mRNA/protein expression and a wide range of metabolic pathways, including growth factor signalling, cancer and neuronal function4,8,47,70. Indeed, we find that at least four miRNAs transferred from BAT to muscle (miR-99b, miR-1a-3p, miR-203-3p and miR-21a-5) can target FoxO1, a key transcription factor involved in insulin action and metabolism71,72. In addition, we find that disruption of miRNA biogenesis in BAT in vivo leads to a marked decrease in miR-1a-3p in muscle, associated with upregulation of FoxO1 and reduced mitochondrial content and function, which can be reversed by delivery of exogenous miR-1a-3p directly into the gastrocnemius muscle of BAT;DicerKO mice. More notably, we took advantage of the 4TU-labelling and using PAR-CLIP54,73, we show direct interaction between BAT-derived 4TU miR-1a-3p and its target mRNA, Foxo1, in the Ago2–RISC complex in muscle. This directly demonstrates that BAT-derived miR-1a-3p is not only transferred to muscle but also binds to its target mRNA, regulating Foxo1 expression in muscle. Of note, this mechanism is conserved in Caenorhabditis elegans, where miR-1a-3p has been shown to regulate expression of DAF-16 (the FoxO1 homologue)74,75. In addition, in humans and mice, miR-1a-3p and FoxO1 are known to regulate skeletal and cardiomyocyte physiology76–78, muscle proteostasis53 and muscle mitochondrial function52,72. In humans, miR-1 has also been shown to serve as a tumour suppressor79. A lower level of circulating miR-1a-3p has also been associated with heart failure80, has prognostic potential for acute myocardial infarction81, and is among several miRNAs implicated in human disease6,47,82. Taken together these findings, may explain the link between the presence of active BAT and improved cardiometabolic health observed in clinical studies14. As there is a loss of BAT mass and BAT-derived circulating miRNAs in ageing and obesity4,9,14,35,59,76, this process of tissue transfer is likely to decline, thus contributing to the adverse effects of ageing and obesity on metabolism, although this point will need further study. Furthermore, although very few sex-specific miRNAs have been identified in mice83, the present study was performed in male mice, so studies aimed at understanding tissue crosstalk by miRNAs in females will be important.

In summary, using a tissue-specific technique to label miRNAs produced in BAT, we have demonstrated that different populations of BAT-produced miRNAs are released into the circulation in both sEV and non-sEV carriers, and that these can be differentially transferred to the liver, muscle and hypothalamus. We have shown that these transferred miRNAs contribute significantly to the pool of miRNAs in the recipient cell and that they can regulate important cellular functions, including respiration and gene/protein expression. Of note, we provide direct evidence of BAT-derived miRNA incorporation into the Ago2–RISC complex in distal tissues, demonstrating that transferred miRNAs are functional in distal tissues. Taken together, our data demonstrate that BAT-produced miRNAs are key metabolic regulators and provide a roadmap of inter-tissue miRNA trafficking.

Methods

Animals

Animals were housed at room temperature with ad libitum access to food and water, and all experiments were performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Joslin Diabetes Center, under protocol number IACUC 2020-02. All mice were fed a chow diet (Mouse Diet 9F, PharmaServ) and experimental procedures were performed in 8–12-week-old male mice. The UPRT mice (Tg(CAG-GFP,-Uprt)985Cdoe/J, strain no. 021469) were a generous gift from O. Rando (UMass Medical School). Dicer-floxed mice (B6.Cg-Dicer1tm1Bdh/J, strain no. 006366) and Ucp1-Cre mice (Tg(Ucp1-cre)1Evdr/J, strain no. 024670) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratories. For all experiments, wild-type (floxed) littermate mice were used as controls. For brown fat stimulation, mice were injected intraperitoneally with 1 mg kg−1 of selective β3-agonist CL 316,243 (C5976, Sigma). GTTs and ITTs were performed after 16 h (for GTTs) or 6 h (for ITTs) fast, followed by baseline blood glucose measurement using an Infinity Blood Glucose Monitoring System (US Diagnostics). For GTTs, 2 g kg−1 dextrose (50-99-7, Thermo Fisher) was injected intraperitoneally. For ITTs, 0.75 U kg−1 insulin was injected intraperitoneally. Blood glucose was measured at 15, 30, 60 and 120 min post-injection. For assessment of fast–re-fed blood glucose levels, animals were fasted for 16 h, blood glucose was measured, then food was re-introduced, followed by re-fed blood glucose measurement after 4 h. Plasma insulin was measured using the Ultra Sensitive Mouse Insulin ELISA kit (90080, Crystal Chem). No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications4. Animals were assigned to groups according to genotype, and then blinded during data and sample collection.

In vivo RNA labelling with 4-thiouracil

For in vivo RNA labelling experiments, UCP1;UPRT or littermate control mice were injected with 50 mg kg−1 4TU or an equal volume of vehicle intraperitoneally four times every 48 h (performed at the same time of day) to allow for steady-state RNA labelling and accumulation of RNA in distal tissues. To prepare 4TU, stock (dissolved in dimethylsulfoxide; DMSO) was mixed (1:4) with corn oil (C8267, Sigma) by pipetting up and down. Vehicle was prepared by mixing DMSO with corn oil (1:4). Then, 6 h after the last injection, mice were anaesthetized and blood was collected by cardiac puncture into K2-EDTA-coated BD Microtainer Tubes (02-669-33, Fisher Scientific). Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2,000g for 5 min and an aliquot was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The remainder was immediately processed for isolation of plasma extracellular vesicles by SEC described below. After blood collection, mice were perfused with 10 ml saline to remove any remaining blood, and tissues were collected and snap-frozen for downstream extraction of RNA and protein.

RNA extraction from tissue, plasma and medium

To extract RNA from tissues, 10–30 mg tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and homogenized using TissueLyser II (QIAGEN), in 1 ml TRIzol (10296028, Thermo Fisher). For liquid samples, 250 µl liquid sample (culture medium or plasma) was added to 750 µl TRIzol LS Reagent (15596026, Thermo Fisher). Chloroform (200 µl) was added, samples were mixed by vortexing, and centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min to separate the organic phase from the upper aqueous phase, which was added to an equal volume of isopropanol and incubated at −20 °C overnight. Precipitated RNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000g for 10 min, washed with 80% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free water. Total RNA was quantified by Nanodrop and miRNA concentration was determined using the Qubit microRNA Assay kit (Q32880, Thermo Fisher), per manufacturer’s instructions.

Biotinylation and enrichment of 4TU miRNAs

Biotinylation of 4TU moieties was achieved by incubating RNA with MTSEA Biotin-XX (NC0903451, Fisher Scientific), 10 mM HEPES (15630080, Thermo Fisher) and 1 mM EDTA (AM9260G, Thermo Fisher) for 30 min with agitation. All reagents were obtained from Thermo Fisher unless indicated otherwise. A 10:1 ratio of RNA (µg):biotin (µg) was used, and the reaction volume was 5 µl per µg RNA being biotinylated. To stop the reaction, water was added to bring the volume up to 250 µl and an equal volume of ultrapure phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, v/v) (15593049) was added. Samples were mixed by vortexing and the aqueous phase was separated by centrifugation at 12,000g for 10 min. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube and an equal volume of isopropanol was added. Linear acrylamide was added per manufacturer’s instructions (AM9520). Samples were incubated at −20 °C overnight and RNA was precipitated by centrifugation at 20,000g for 20 min. Pellets were washed with 75% ethanol and resuspended in nuclease-free water. Biotinylation of RNA was confirmed by RNA dot blot and northern blot. Pulldown of biotinylated 4TU-RNA was achieved using streptavidin–Dynabeads (Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1, 65001), as described31. In brief, beads (10 µl per sample) were washed twice with high-salt wash buffer containing 100 mM Tris-HCl (AM9850G), 10 mM EDTA (AM9260G), 1 M NaCl (AM9760G) and 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20 (J20605.AP). Beads were blocked with 40 ng μl−1 glycogen (AM9510) for 1 h, then washed with high-salt wash buffer (HSB) and added to biotinylated RNA samples. Aliquots of RNA that was not biotinylated or biotinylated RNA from control mice (littermate wild-type mice or vehicle-treated mice) were used as controls. To each RNA sample, a 10% volume of 10× stock of HSB was added, to achieve a final concentration of 1×. Samples were incubated with streptavidin–Dynabeads for 15 min at room temperature with agitation. Beads were pelleted using magnetic racks or 96-well magnetic plates. Beads were washed five times with HSB, then eluted for 15 min in HSB supplemented with 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT; 15508013). To ensure compatibility with downstream applications, eluted RNA was re-isolated using phenol:chloroform followed by precipitation in isopropanol as described above.

RNA dot blot and northern blot

Nylon membranes (1620153, Bio-Rad) were incubated in nuclease-free water for 10 min and air-dried for 5 min. Then, 250 ng of RNA, 4TU RNA, or biotinylated 4TU RNA was blotted onto the membranes and absorbed via vacuum using the Bio-Dot 96-well Apparatus (1706545, Bio-Rad). For northern blots, RNA was resolved in 15% TBE-urea polyacrylamide gels (3450093, Bio-Rad) and transferred onto nylon membranes for 1 h at 20 V using the Genie electrophoretic transfer apparatus (4017, Idea Scientific). Transferred RNA was crosslinked onto the membranes using 120,000 J UV-radiation (254 nm). Membranes were air-dried for 5 min then washed with blocking buffer (10% SDS and 1 mM EDTA) for 30 min. Membranes were incubated with 1:10,000 streptavidin–HRP (ab7403, Abcam) in 10% SDS, and washed with 1× saline-sodium citrate buffer (150 mM NaCl and 15 mM sodium citrate, pH 7.0) three times to remove excess streptavidin–HRP. Biotinylated RNA conjugated to HRP was visualized using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (34580, Thermo Fisher) on the Chemidoc Imaging system (Bio-Rad).

qPCR of total and 4TU-enriched RNA and miRNA

Total RNA (10% dilution) and eluted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription kit (4368813, Thermo). For miRNA analysis, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the miRCURY LNA RT kit (339340, QIAGEN). Primers for miRNAs and reference genes (U6 and 5S) were predesigned and purchased from QIAGEN. Primary miRNAs were assessed by TaqMan probes for mmu-pri-miR-1 and mmu-pri-miR-133 (Mm03306163_pri and Mm03307401_pri, respectively, Thermo Fisher), using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (4444557, Thermo Fisher). Gene abundance was analysed by qPCR using the Bio-Rad CFX384 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad). Relative gene abundance was calculated using the ΔΔCt method, using the appropriate reference genes.

Isolation of plasma sEV and non-sEV fractions by size-exclusion chromatography

Plasma or medium samples were filtered with 0.22-µm syringe filters (09-720-511, Fisher Scientific), before fractionation using qEV Gen-2 70-nm columns (ICO-70, Izon Science). Columns were washed with 10 ml PBS (passed through 0.22-µm filter) and 400 µl plasma was added to the column. Plasma was fractionated per manufacturer’s instructions into 20 fractions (400 µl volume per fraction), using PBS as the mobile phase. Protein content was determined by Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (A65453, Thermo Fisher). Small extracellular vesicle content in each fraction was quantified using nanoparticle-tracking analysis using the Nanosight NS300 (Malvern Panalytical). RNA was quantified using NanoDrop 8000 Spectrophotometer (ND-8000-GL, Thermo Fisher). Columns were calibrated using known proteins standards (MWGF200-1KT, Sigma). All reagents and samples were allowed to reach room temperature and filtered to avoid introduction of particulates that would interfere with column chromatography.

RNase protection assay

Pooled sEV and non-sEV fractions from plasma or cell medium samples were subjected to RNase protection assay to quantify miRNA incorporation into sEVs. Aliquots (250 µl) of each sample were incubated with 0.2 µl RNase A (T3018L, New England Biolabs), 1% Triton X-100, or both, for 30 min at room temperature. Reactions were stopped by addition of 1 µl RNasin Ribonuclease Inhibitor (N2111, Promega). RNA was extracted by Trizol LS, and miRNA expression was assessed by qPCR as described above.

Preparation of 4TU smRNA-seq libraries

Library preparation was performed using the NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, E7560S). RNA was quantified by Agilent 2000 Bioanalyzer using smRNA chip, as well as by Qubit microRNA Assay (Q32881, Thermo). For library preparation 100 ng RNA was used as input for tissue samples, and 10 ng RNA was used as input for plasma samples. Libraries were prepared according to manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed by the Harvard Biopolymers Facility on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 using single end 100-bp reads.

Bioinformatic and statistical analyses

For bioinformatic analysis of smRNA-seq, raw data was de-multiplexed and mapped to the mm10 genome using the STAR pipeline and miRbase v.22.1 annotations. Low-expressing miRNAs (fewer than ten reads) were filtered out, and counts were normalized to spike-in RNA as follows: ‘spike-in RNA counts in the sample/mean spike-in RNA counts of all samples’, and normalized samples by spike-in RNA counts rather than library size (total read counts). We also set the library size of all samples to a constant, the mean of library sizes. We then normalized the samples by converting counts to CPM, where: CPM = 106 × count/(mean library size × normalization factor). Values were then transformed for linear modelling (Voom transformation), and counts were transformed to CPM and log2CPM. Heatmaps, unsupervised clustering, and dimension reduction for principal-component analyses were performed in R software (v.4.3.1) using the edgeR, limmaR, tidyR and ggplot2 packages. miRNA target prediction was performed using the microT-CDS, miR-Tarbase and TargetScan v.8.0 algorithms. Pathway analyses were performed using the miRPath v.4.0 software, and targeted genes were visualized using the KEGG Pathway database pathway diagrams. Statistical analyses and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism Software v.10. Nonparametric analyses were performed where assumptions of equal variance or normality of distribution were not met.

Cell culture

Cells were cultured in growth medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P–S), Thermo Fisher) and maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2. For insulin-stimulation experiments in C2C12 cells, FBS was withdrawn for 6 h, followed by stimulation with insulin (100 nM, I9278, Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min. hWATs (hTERT A41hWAT-SVF, CRL-3386, ATCC) were differentiated for 12 days with culture medium supplemented with 2% FBS, 33 μM biotin (B4639, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.5 μM insulin (I2643, Sigma-Aldrich), 17 μM Pantothenate (P5155 Sigma-Aldrich), 0.1 μM dexamethasone (D4902, Sigma-Aldrich), 2 nM T3 (T6397, Sigma-Aldrich), 500 μM IBMX (15879, Sigma-Aldrich) and 30 µM indomethacin (I7378, Sigma-Aldrich). hBATs (hTERT A41hBAT-SVF, CRL-3385, ATCC) were differentiated with the same medium for 21 days. After differentiation, FBS was replaced with exosome-free FBS (EXO-FBS-250A-1, System Biosciences) for 48 h to allow accumulation of extracellular vesicles into the medium. Cells were immediately lysed in TRIzol reagent for subsequent isolation of RNA as described above. The medium was centrifuged at 2,000g for 10 min to remove cells and debris, passed through 40-µm cell strainer, then prepared for isolation of sEVs using SEC as described above.

Transfection of miRNA mimics

MiRNA mimics, negative controls, and Cy3-transfection controls were purchased from Horizon Biosciences (Dharmacon), and 25 nmol was transfected into AML12 hepatocytes using RNAiMAX, per manufacturer instructions. After 48 h, the medium was aspirated and replaced with serum-reduced medium (DMEM supplemented with 0.3% BSA). Cells were then stimulated without or with 100 nM insulin for 15 min. Transfection was confirmed by fluorescent microscopy using a Cy3-transfection control.

Protein extraction and immunoblotting

Cells were lysed using 1× radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with cOmplete mini EDTA-Free protease inhibitors (Roche), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (SelleckChem). Protein was quantified by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher), diluted to 1 mg ml−1 with water and denatured by addition of Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad) and heating to 95 °C for 5 min. Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto 0.45-µm PVDF membranes. Protein transfer was confirmed by Ponceau S Staining Solution (A40000278, Thermo Fisher). All antibodies were from Cell Signalling and used at a dilution of 1:1,000 unless indicated otherwise. Protein abundance was assessed by immunoblotting with antibodies for HA-Tag (3724), Streptavidin–HRP (ab7403, Abcam), Alix (sc-53540, Santa Cruz), Caveolin-1 (3238), CD9 (sc-13118, Santa Cruz), Argonaute-2 (2897), Apolipoprotein B (ab20737, Abcam), Apolipoprotein A1 (ab308187, Abcam), FoxO1 (2880), phospho-Akt(S473) (9271), pan-Akt (4691), phospho-AMPKα (2535), AMPKα (5381), PGC1a Antibody (4A8) (sc-517380, Santa Cruz), OXPHOS subunits (ab110413, Abcam), firefly luciferase antibody (ab241541, Abcam), β-actin (4967) and GAPDH (2118). Blots were imaged using the Chemidoc Imaging System and quantified using Image Lab Software v.6.1 (Bio-Rad).

Cellular respiration

Oxygen consumption rate was measured in C2C12 cells using the Agilent Seahorse XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. Cells were plated and allowed to reach 70–80% confluency, then transfected using the appropriate miRNA mimics and controls using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (13778150, Thermo Fisher). After 48 h, the medium was replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM (103575-100, Agilent) supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate (103578-100, Agilent) 2 mM glutamine (103579-100, Agilent) and 10 mM glucose (103577-100, Agilent) as per manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated for 45 min at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator immediately before the start of the assay. Cells were then loaded onto the Seahorse XF96 analyzer and treated with 1.5 µM oligomycin, 0.5 M FCCP and 0.5 M rotenone/antimycin (103015-100, Agilent). Data were collected and assessed using Wave Software v.2.6 (Agilent), then exported to for analysis using GraphPad Prism Software v.10.

Pulldown of Argonaute–RISC complex

Co-precipitation of miRNAs and target genes with the RISC containing Ago2, we used Ago protein affinity purification by peptides (Ago-APP) as described previously51. In brief, 100 µg of Flag–TNRC6B(599-683) (termed T6B peptide) was conjugated to 50 µl anti-Flag M2 Magnetic Beads (M8823, Sigma). Peptide-coupled beads (5 µl or 10 µg peptide) were then added to 100 µg cell lysate and incubated at 4 °C with shaking for 3 h. RISC complexes conjugated to T6B–Dynabeads were pulled down using a magnetic rack and washed five times with 1× RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with cOmpleteTM mini EDTA-Free protease inhibitors (Roche), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (SelleckChem). Beads were resuspended in 100 µl 1× RIPA buffer and submitted for analysis by immunoblot or RNA extraction by TRIzol–chloroform as described above. Pulldown was confirmed by immunoblot using an antibody for Ago2 (2897, Cell Signalling). Gene and miRNA analysis was performed by qPCR as described above.

Photoactivatable ribonucleoside-enhanced crosslinking immunoprecipitation

To capture 4TU miRNAs from BAT in Ago2–RISC in distal tissues, we performed in vivo PAR-CLIP as described elsewhere54,73. In brief, gastrocnemius muscle was isolated from UCP1;UPRT and UPRTfl/fl mice labelled with 4TU. Tissue was ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle over dry ice, then crosslinked at UV-365 nm using the Stratalinker 2400 (Fisher Scientific). After crosslinking, tissue samples were homogenized using IP lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1 mM NaF, 0.5 % (v/v) NP40, 0.5 mM DTT and cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitory cocktail (11873580001, Roche)). Pulldown of Ago2–RISC complexes was performed using FLAG-T6B peptide as described above. In brief, 1 ml aliquots of UV-365-crosslinked homogenate from muscles of UCP1;UPRT and UPRTfl/fl mice were incubated with Dynabeads conjugated to Flag–T6B peptide (100 µg peptide per 100 µg tissue lysate) overnight. Beads were pelleted using a magnetic rack and washed with IP lysis buffer (1 ml, five times), and resuspended in 250 µl water and 750 µl of TRIzol LS was added to extract RNA. In parallel, a 10% input sample was made by diluting 25 µl of tissue lysate with 225 µl water, to which 750 µl of TRIzol LS was added for RNA extraction as described above. Analysis of miRNAs and target genes was performed by qPCR as described above. For each sample, the expression in the pulldown fraction was normalized to input as described above, and then as a fold change of level in UCP1;UPRT relative to UPRTfl/fl samples, which act as control as 4TU miRNAs are not found in UPRTfl/fl muscle and thus cannot be pulled down with Ago2–RISC following PAR-CLIP.

Transfection of miR-1 plasmids in vivo

Plasmids encoding miR-1a-3p or scramble control (MMIR-1a-1-PA-1 and MMIR-000-PA-1, respectively, System Biosciences) were transfected into HEK293 cells for amplification, followed by plasmid purification using PureLink HiPure Plasmid Maxiprep kit (K210007, Thermo Fisher). Plasmids were then transfected into gastrocnemius muscle by electroporation as described76. In brief, wild-type or BAT;DicerKO mice were anaesthetized using isoflurane, fur was removed from the lower limb and the skin was sanitized with ethanol before injection of 40 U hyaluronidase (H3506, Sigma-Aldrich) into the gastrocnemius muscle. After 30 min, 50 µg of plasmid encoding either miR-1 or scramble control was injected into the right leg. Sham injections of equal volumes of saline were performed into the left leg, which served as an additional endogenous control. Plasmids were transfected into permeabilized muscle fibres by electroporation (five 20-ms square-wave pulses (50 V cm−1) at one pulse per second). After a recovery period of 28 days, during which time no signs of injury or change in behaviour was observed, animals were killed and tissues were collected for downstreatm analyses.

Electron microscopy and histological analyses

For analyses of mitochondrial content, mice were killed and perfusion-fixed with 20 ml of EM-grade 2.5% formaldehyde/glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (pH 7.4) buffer (15949, Electron Microscopy Sciences). The gastrocnemius muscle was excised and a section from the middle of the muscle was carefully dissected and cut into small (1–2-mm) pieces. Samples were submerged in fixative overnight and submitted for processing by the Harvard Medical School Electron Microscopy Core. For analyses of cross-sectional muscle fibre area, animals were perfusion-fixed with 20 ml of 10% buffered formalin (23-427098, Fisher Scientific). Gastrocnemius muscle extracted and embedded into paraffin-blocks for haematoxylin and eosin staining by the Harvard Medical School’s Rodent Histology Core, and imaged using the Olympus VS2000 Slide Scanner at the Neurobiology Imaging Facility. Images were analysed using ImageJ Software. Mitochondrial area was calculated as a percentage of tissue area. For all analyses, three separate areas of the gastrocnemius from three individual animals were imaged.

Luciferase reporter assay

Mouse muscle genomic DNA was used to clone predicted miR-1a-3p binding site in the 3′ UTR of the Foxo1 gene (ENSMUST00000053764.6, 5′-CAGGATAATTGGTTTTACCTCTCATTCCG-3′) into the pmirGLO Dual-Luciferase miRNA Target Expression Vector (E1330, Promega), using Sac1 and Xba1 restriction enzymes (R3156 and R0145T, respectively, New England Biolabs). Cloned plasmids were transformed into DH5α competent cells (C404010, Thermo Fisher) and plasmid DNA were isolated using the QIAGEN Plasmid Midi kit (12143, QIAGEN). Plasmid was then transfected into C2C12 cells as described above, followed by transfection of miR-1a-3p or scramble control after 48 h. Cells were collected 48 h after miR-1a-3p or scramble control transfection and protein was extracted for analysis of luciferase protein by immunoblot.
