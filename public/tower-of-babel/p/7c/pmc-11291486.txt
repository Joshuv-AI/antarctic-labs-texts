Response of spontaneous plant communities to microhabitats in a riparian corridor in Beijing, China
Authors: Mengyuan Zhang, Xiaolu Li, Shuxin Fan, Kun Li, Xiaoyi Xing, Yiding Xu, Peiyao Hao, Li Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the increasing urban environmental problems, spontaneous vegetation has been gradually emphasized for high ecological value, whose distribution has been reported as strongly influenced by fine-scale environmental factors. As one of the major zones for urban spontaneous vegetation distribution, urban riparian corridors are well suited for research on the response of spontaneous plants to microhabitats. Taking the Wenyu River-North Canal in Beijing as the study site, we measured six microhabitat factors, including the level of maintenance and visitor activity, canopy density, litter thickness, and distance to water and road, to investigate their effects on spontaneous plant assemblages. The results showed that spontaneous plants respond significantly to fine-scale habitat variation, and were more responsive to human disturbance than other factors. Compared with diversity indicators, the functional trait compositions were more significantly correlated with microhabitat factors. Under lower maintenance of plants, the spontaneous communities had a higher invasion risk for plants. Thick litter can impede the occurrence of invasive species while favoring the growth of native ones. Our findings are important for furthering understanding of the spontaneous plant community establishment, and can serve as a good reference for the maintenance and management of spontaneous plants in urban riparian corridors.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Urban ecology, Sustainability, Biodiversity

Introduction

It is well known that rapid urbanization and climate change have caused a series of environmental problems on a global scale. Issues such as resource exhaustion, air pollution, urban heat islands, and biodiversity loss have emerged frequently in metropolitan areas. As a way to address these issues, it has been a consensus that fewer resources should be consumed and higher ecological values should be provided in the construction of urban green spaces. However, for a long time, in order to pursue aesthetic effects, urban green spaces have generally been dominated by planted communities, such as tidy lawns and uniformly sized trees. These highly artificial plant communities require huge maintenance costs and have certain limitations in their ecological functions.

Spontaneous plants are a group of plants that occur spontaneously without intentional cultivation which have long been neglected due to the predominance of artificially planted plants in cities1. Before the concept of spontaneous plants was introduced, spontaneous plants in urban green spaces were usually labeled as weeds. In recent years, more and more scholars have begun to pay attention to the ecological value of these plants. They grow spontaneously because they are highly adaptable to the urban environment. Therefore, spontaneous plants are considered to be utilized in urban greening to improve the resilience of landscapes, and to reduce the cost of management, thus reducing carbon emissions from green space maintenance2–4. Extensive studies have shown that spontaneous plants have a significant benefit in enhancing the ecological functions of urban green spaces5–7. As an effective supplement to plant diversity, spontaneous plants can accelerate plant community succession to enhance community stability8. Compared with cultivated plants with less diversity, spontaneous plants can provide more abundant food and habitat for animals in green spaces9. In addition, spontaneous plants can quickly cover the ground in urban vacant land to prevent soil erosion and improve soil quality6. Thus, an increasing number of scholars have advocated preserving spontaneous vegetation to enhance the ecological services of urban green spaces, especially biodiversity improvement10.

At the edges of rivers and traffic routes within urban areas, there are usually contiguous patches of vegetated lands, such as country parks, shelterbelts, and urban wilderness areas, which together form urban ecological corridors11. As indispensable green spaces in urban areas, ecological corridors play an essential role in the ecological service for cities and larger regions12. Compared with most general urban parks, these usually extensively maintained urban ecological corridors, especially riparian corridors, can support a wider variety of spontaneous plants by providing diverse habitats and dispersal pathways, thereby reducing the negative impacts of habitat fragmentation and human disturbance on spontaneous plant communities in cities9. Therefore, urban riparian corridors are one of the major zones for urban spontaneous plant conservation and have important research value.

For historical reasons, no original vegetation remains in Beijing’s riparian corridors, and almost all the existing woody plants are planted by afforestation activities or, in some parts, planted as a public park. In the past decades, attempts have been made to manage these vegetations with high intensity. With the recognition of the ecological value of spontaneous plants and the need to reduce the cost of management, the Beijing Municipal Government issued a series of regulations on strengthening weed management in 2021, which advocates the appropriate retention of spontaneous plants in country parks, shelterbelts, and vacant lands to enhance biodiversity13. However, due to the long-term pursuit of the visual effect of urban plant landscape and the neglect of its ecological value, there are big gaps between understanding spontaneous plants’ distribution and composition patterns and management practice. Even though there has been some related research in recent years concerning urban parks, attention to elucidating the behavior of spontaneous plants in urban riparian corridors is still rare9,14,15.

The occurrence of spontaneous plants in urban areas seems very stochastic, and it is difficult to understand their distribution patterns, which makes it hard to fully achieve their ecological value through appropriate conservation and management measures16–21. We assume that this difficulty is partly, though not fully, caused by factors of the diverse species and bio- and ecological traits of spontaneous plants, as well as the very complex habitat conditions in urban areas. It has been shown that spontaneous plant assemblages are shaped by a combination of dispersal processes and environmental filtering processes that are influenced by a series of multi-scale factors.plant assemblagesFor example, at the regional scale, the species composition of urban spontaneous plants is mainly affected by climatic and anthropogenic factors, the urban built environment and climatic conditions act as a filter for spontaneous plant communities18,22. While at the landscape scale, habitat size and distribution have a large impact on spontaneous plant distribution by influencing plant dispersal23. These coarse-scale studies provided certain effective information for understanding and conserving urban spontaneous plants. However, it is far from sufficient for consideration of integrating design, construction, and daily maintenance management of urban green spaces with the protection and utilization of spontaneous plants. A few published studies have suggested that the composition of the spontaneous plant community is sensitive to fine-scale environmental factors, for example, canopy density and human activity7. However, most of them focused on explaining the species diversity in responding to heterogeneous distribution rather than exploring the influencing factors in depth. There are also studies mentioning the functional traits and phylogenetic characteristics, providing avenues for understanding the microhabitat filtering of spontaneous plant community assembly24.

In an attempt to explore the adaptation of spontaneous plant communities to microhabitats, most of the current published studies have been carried out in parks, restricted by factors such as the urbanization process, habitat fragmentation, high environmental heterogeneity, and low diversity of microhabitats, resulting in quite limited knowledge25. On the contrary, urban riparian corridors are well suited for this kind of study because of their large size, contiguous morphology, and abundant habitat types. The Wenyu River-North Canal riparian corridor, located in the second green belt of Beijing, China, fulfills these conditions26. Because of its special location and large scale, this corridor plays a crucial role in ensuring the ecological security and ecological quality of Beijing and is undoubtedly a very important corridor for biodiversity conservation. That is why the Wenyu River-North Canal riparian corridor was selected as the study site for our research.

A survey of spontaneous plant diversity was conducted in the Wenyu River-North Canal ecological corridor. Based on the phenomenon of spontaneous plant response to microhabitat environmental factors observed in the field survey, we selected six microhabitat factors that may be related to spontaneous plant diversity, including the level of maintenance, the level of visitor activity, canopy density, litter thickness, distance to water, and distance to road. The species, functional and systematic diversity, and a series of functional properties of spontaneous plant communities in the corridor were analyzed in relation to environmental factors in microhabitats, aiming to clarify the drivers of spontaneous plant community establishment and their influencing mechanisms at fine scales. Only plants in the herbaceous layer were analyzed, as few spontaneous woody plants were found in this corridor in our previous study9. In this paper, we proposed to explore three questions: (1) whether the composition of spontaneous plant communities differs significantly among different types of microhabitats in urban riparian corridors; (2) what are the main drivers of spontaneous plant assemblages at fine scales; and (3) how do microenvironments act as filters for spontaneous plant communities.

Methods

Study area

The Wenyu River—Northern Canal is located on the eastern plain of Beijing (between 116°15′ and 116°54′ E and latitude 39°45′ and 40°8′ N), with a length of 88.27km. Since the corridor is located within the second green belt of Beijing, patches of shelterbelts have been planted along the riverbanks over the past two decades. Also, several parks have been constructed scattered along the riverbanks. Currently, there are a large number of green spaces distributed contiguously along the river, which together form a green belt in the city. Based on the preliminary investigation and remote sensing image analysis, Based on the preliminary investigation and remote sensing image analysis, the continuous green space located in the green city corridor associated with a river was selected as the study area, which is distributed within 1–3km on both sides of the river, with a total area of 201km2. The habitats inside this corridor are diverse, including woodland, shrub, grassland, wetland, farmland, etc. Due to the city’s flood defense needs, the riverbanks were constructed as stone walls in most sections of the river. Only a few seasonal wetlands exist in some sections with open water and parks. The central part of the corridor is located between the fifth and sixth ring roads of Beijing, which is similar in distance to the urban center and, therefore, has a relatively same level of urbanization. Thus, to some extent, the variation of urbanization level and habitat fragmentation can be avoided in this study.

Collection of plant community characteristics

The field survey was carried out from July to September 2019–2020. Considering the narrow morphology of the corridor, the transect survey was used in order to represent the characteristics of the habitats and plant communities as comprehensively as possible. Transect lines were established perpendicular to the river at a spacing of 1–2 km. According to the width of the corridor, the length of the transect lines varies between 2 and 5km. In each transect line, 10m × 10m sampling plots were set at 100–200 m intervals from the riverside. Five 1m × 1m quadrats were set in the center and four corners of each sampling plot (Fig. 1)27. The coverage and average height of each species of spontaneous plants in the quadrats were recorded. The determination of spontaneous plants was based on the cultivation status of the individual plant in the community. A plant is judged to be spontaneous if it shows no signs of cultivation and there is no regularly distributed group of plants of the same species around it. A total of 2049 quadrats were recorded.

Location of the study area, sampling design, and the environmental factors in microhabitats. Maps were drawn by authors, using ArcGIS 10.4.1 (Environmental Systems Research Institute, USA. https://www.esri.com/).

The functional traits of plants were also collected. We selected five types of traits, including origin, life span, vegetative traits, flower traits, and fruit/seed traits. A total of 23 functional traits were included in this study, as shown in Table 1. These functional traits were obtained from the TRY Database, Kew Seed Information Database, Flora of China, the Flora of Beijing, and Invasive Alien Species of Chinese28–32. Data for species not available online were obtained from references or field observations9.

Collection of microhabitat characteristics

We recorded the microhabitat characteristics of each 1m × 1m quadrat. The environmental factors were classified into three categories: horizontal direction, vertical direction, and human disturbance (Fig. 1). In the horizontal direction, we measured the distances between the center of each quadrat and the edge of the nearest road and water body with a tape measure. In the vertical direction, photographs were taken vertically upward at the center of each quadrat using a full-frame single-lens reflex camera. The pixel ratio of the canopy in these photos was then extracted as the canopy density by Adobe Photoshop33. The distance from the ground to the top surface of the litter at the center and four corners of quadrats was measured with a tape measure, and the average value was taken as the litter thickness. For human disturbance, the levels of maintenance and visitor activity were artificially rated (1–5) based on government documents34,35 and with field observation correction. A rating of 5 corresponds to very intense maintenance or frequent visitor activity, and the evaluation method is shown in Fig. S1. Using Ward’s D clustering method, quadrats were clustered according to the six microhabitat factors mentioned above, and the microhabitat types were classified accordingly. The clustering was performed in R software v. 4.1.236.

Statistical analysis

We characterized the spontaneous plant communities in 1m × 1m quadrats because of a great deal of variability of microhabitat factors within the 10m × 10m sampling plots. The relative abundance of each species was first calculated in each quadrat, and the diversity of the plant community was analyzed accordingly. The Shannon - Wiener index37, Functional dispersion38, and Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity39 were calculated as the indicators of the species, functional, and phylogenetic diversity, respectively. We also calculated the community-level weighted mean (CWM) for each trait to characterize the composition of functional traits40. For categorical functional traits, each trait category was transformed into dichotomous data indicating whether or not (0/1). The species diversity was calculated in the R package “vegan”41. The indicators of functional diversity and functional composition were calculated in the R package “FD”28. Based on the mega-tree GBOTB.extend, the phylogenetic tree was constructed in the R package “V. PhyloMaker”42. The phylogenetic diversity was calculated using the R package “Picante”43.

We used a Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) to visualize the differences in spontaneous plant community characteristics among different habitats and tested them by an Analysis of Similarity (ANOSIM) test with 999 permutations and Euclidean distance. Differences in environmental factors and plant community characteristics between microhabitat types classified by Ward’s D clustering method were analyzed. The environmental factors included the level of maintenance, the level of visitor activity, canopy density, litter thickness, distance to water, and distance to road. The plant community characteristics included the diversity indicators and the CWMs of functional traits. Kruskal-Wallis test and multiple comparisons test were performed using the R package “pgirmess” because all variables were non-normally distributed and some of them were not homogeneous in variance. Variance Partitioning Analysis (VPA) was used to calculate the contributions of different categories of microhabitat factors to the variation in diversity and functional traits. By calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) for collinearity diagnostics, the results showed that the VIF <10, so it can be assumed that there is no covariance relationship between the microhabitat factors. NMDS, ANOSIM, and VPA were performed with the R package “vegan”41.

To investigate each microhabitat factor’s influence on spontaneous plant communities’ diversity and functional trait composition, Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) were constructed using the R package “lme4”. A total of 246 separate Generalized Linear Mixed Models were conducted with six microhabitat factors as fixed effects as predictive indicators, other microhabitat factors as random effects, and 41 indicators of plant community characteristics as response variables, respectively. The model was constructed considering the type of distribution of each response variable44. We found a spatial autocorrelation in spontaneous plant diversity and functional trait composition using the “spdep” package in R45. Therefore, we obtained a set of predictor variables to represent spatial variation in quadrats using the dbMEM spatial eigenfunctions with the R package “adespatial” and included them in the random effects of GLMMs to counteract the interference of spatial autocorrelation in the regression model46. To avoid excessive complexity and difficulty of interpretation, a Spearman correlation analysis was conducted for the CWMs of functional traits. If traits were closely correlated (r > 0.70, p < 0.05), the most easily interpretable trait would be selected as dependent variables. We displayed the more interpretable trait as the dependent variable. All variables were standardized and normalized. All analyses were performed in R software v. 4.1.236.

Ethics approval

Permission was obtained for this study to carry out the field survey near the study area. The survey process complied with the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction and the Convention on the Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora.

Results

Differences in spontaneous plant community composition among different microhabitat types

A total of 172 spontaneous species belonging to 122 genera in 48 families were recorded, 121 native species, 11 domestic exotic species, 12 foreign exotic species, and 27 invasive species. In terms of the number of plant species, anemochores accounted for the highest number of species in any type of origin. In terms of frequency of occurrence, wind-borne pollinators still accounted for the largest proportion of both native and foreign exotic plants. Among foreign exotic plants, mammal dispersal dominated. For invasive plants, hydrochores contributed the most frequency (Fig. S2). We classified the 2049 1m × 1m quadrats into six microhabitat types by clustering (Fig. 3). The differences among the environmental factors of all six microhabitat types were highly significant (Fig. 3B). We named the microhabitats according to representative environmental factors: roadside woodland edges with fine maintenance (RWM), roadside woodland edges with high visitor activity (RWH), under tree canopies (UT), canopy gaps (CP), waterfront grasslands with low maintenance and low visitor activity (WG), waterfront woodland edges (WW). The number of quadrats for each microhabitat type was similar, with UT the most (405) and CP the least (305). The species composition of spontaneous plant communities in riparian corridors differed significantly between microhabitat types (ANOSIM = 0.05802, p = 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Comparing the diversity of spontaneous plant communities in different microhabitat types, communities below tree canopies under moderate maintenance and visitor activity (UT) had significantly high species diversity, functional diversity and phylogenetic diversity (Fig. 3C). In addition, the CWMs for almost all functional traits showed significant differences among microhabitat types (Fig. 3C). Overall, Communities in waterfront grasslands (WG) had significantly larger plant shapes and dispersal units and had longer flowering periods. Typical plant species include Phragmites australis and Typha orientalis, etc. Communities in roadside woodland edges with fine maintenance (RWM) had more significant flowers and fruits and had longer fruiting periods. It is worth noting that communities in canopy gaps with low maintenance (CP) had both significantly higher CWMs of native species and risk level of invasion.

Contribution of different categories of microhabitat factors to the variation of spontaneous plant community composition

The results of the VPA analysis show that microhabitat factors explained 2.18% of the total variation in the composition of the spontaneous plant communities. Human disturbance contributed the highest, followed by horizontal factors (Fig. 4A). Among the characteristics of spontaneous plant community composition, the variation in vegetative traits was the most explained (11.47%), followed by flower traits (6.78%) and life spans (4.10%). Human was the dominant factor affecting the diversity, origin, and fruit/seed traits of the spontaneous plant communities (Fig. 4B,C,G). For the variation in life span traits and vegetative traits, the horizontal factors had the highest contribution (Fig. 4D,E). For flower traits, the vertical factors had the highest interpretation (Fig. 4F).

Communities with lower levels of maintenance had higher levels of invasion risk and larger plant sizes, with higher percentages of perennials, dioecious and anemophilous species, lower percentages of annuals, biennials, hermaphroditic and entomogamous species, with later flowering and fruiting start months. Communities with higher levels of visitor activity had a higher percentage of annuals, a lower percentage of biennials, perennials, dioecious and dioecious species, shorter plants, less conspicuous flowers, and later fruiting start months.

The plant communities closer to the water’s edge had a lower risk of invasion, with a larger proportion of perennials, taller plants with longer leaves, anemophilous species, hydrochores, anemochores, and bird-dispersed plants. The plant communities near the roads had a larger proportion of native species and lower plant heights.

In environments with higher canopy density, plant communities had a larger proportion of native, a lower proportion of invasive species, and a lower risk of invasion. In addition, perennials, bird-dispersed and ant-dispersed species, species with low-growing, small leaves, earlier flowering and fruiting, short flowering duration, conspicuous flowers and fruits, and juicy fruits tended to be founded in environments with higher canopy densities. In contrast, in environments with lower canopy density, anemophilous species, hydrochores, and anemochores were more predominant. It is worth noting that litter thickness was significantly positively correlated with the proportion of native and perennial species and significantly negatively correlated with the proportion of invasive and annual species.

Discussion

Heterogeneous distribution of spontaneous plant assemblages in microhabitats and their drivers

We found that spontaneous plant assemblages are highly adaptive to subtle environmental changes. For example, habitat UT and habitat CP were similar in all environmental factors except canopy density and litter thickness, but the communities in these two types of habitat differed significantly in phylogenetic diversity and trait characteristics, such as max height and anemophilous. This phenomenon has been commonly observed in field surveys. In the same woodland, the spontaneous plant assemblages were different for those located at the edge of the canopy shadow versus those located at the center of the canopy shadow at a distance of only a few meters. Moreover, the level of maintenance varies with different locations in the same green space. For example, areas near fences, tree bases, rocks, planting bed edges, and sloping areas are easily neglected during maintenance where mechanical work is difficult, thus providing opportunities for the establishment of spontaneous plants. This fine-scale heterogeneity is instructive for the investigation approach of spontaneous plants. Given that most previous studies, although field surveys were conducted using 1m × 1m quadrats, data were analyzed with 10m × 10m or even 20m × 20m samples14,47, which very likely leads to fine-scale environmental variations obscured. Therefore, we advocate that it is necessary to investigate the heterogeneity of spontaneous plants at the microhabitat scale by sampling and analyzing with smaller samples.

Our result indicated that human disturbance is a potent driver of spontaneous plant assemblages, which is consistent with the findings of a previous study21. We further revealed that human disturbance was dominant in influencing the diversity, origin, and fruit/seed traits of spontaneous plant communities. This influence can be understood in two aspects. On the one hand, it is the fact that the activities of visitors can affect the spread of plants, especially by exacerbating the spread of exotic and invasive plants. On the other hand, trampling by visitors and maintenance can cause survival stress during community establishment by damaging aboveground parts of spontaneous plants or causing the death of whole plants. The horizontal factors had the second highest overall cumulative explanation and were the dominant factors for life span and vegetative traits. We hypothesize that horizontal factors can affect the vegetative growth of plant communities by altering moisture conditions, including infiltration from water bodies to drylands or the barriers to surface runoff by roads. Vertical factors are dominant in influencing flower traits and possibly influence plant flowering characteristics via altering the light environment. It is important to note that Factors affecting the composition of spontaneous plant communities are multi-scale and complex. Since this study was a field sampling rather than a controlled experiment, the influence of other scale environmental factors on community characteristics could not be avoided, which led to the fact that the overall percentage of explanation in the analyzed results was low.

The differences in community composition among different microhabitat types support that spontaneous plant communities respond significantly to fine-scale habitat variation. It is worth noting that spontaneous plant communities in UT, a microhabitat with high canopy density, have simultaneously higher species, functional, and phylogenetic diversities. We hypothesize that this may be due to the fact that the denser canopy of upper plants has the potential to reduce plant transpiration, thus providing relatively suitable conditions for plant survival, given the high summer temperatures in Beijing. This idea is also supported by some previous studies48–50. At the same time, the environmental filtering caused by light limitation would also prevent the colonization of most light-loving plants with high competitiveness, thus providing space for more understory species to survive, and spontaneous plants would tend to coexist in a stabilized manner, leading to high diversity51. The lowest plant heights and smallest leaves confirmed that species in habitats UT were dominated by conservative leaf strategies in light-limited environments52. This conjecture was also confirmed by the fact that invasive plants with high competitiveness did not dominate in UT habitats despite having the highest invasion risk.

Response of spontaneous plant community plant composition to microhabitat factors

Building on the results of the significant influence of microhabitat factors on spontaneous plant assemblages, the correlations between microhabitat factors and plant community characteristics are instrumental in understanding environmental factors’ filtering effect on plant communities at fine scales. Our results suggest that, compared to diversity indices, the CWMs of functional traits better reflected the effects of microhabitat factors on spontaneous plant communities.

In the maintenance measures, weeding is the one most closely associated with spontaneous plants. Weeding generally cuts off the upper part of the herb, including flowers and fruits. Thus, with the exception of clonal or perennial species, only plants with easily pollinated hermaphroditic flowers and characteristics of early flowering and fruiting are more likely to reproduce under frequent weeding. The plants with larger leaf widths present under lower maintenance, which may be due to the fact that these kinds of plants are easily detected and removed during weeding. It is necessary to point out that spontaneous plant communities under low maintenance have a higher risk of plant invasion. It can be seen that appropriate maintenance measures are necessary to ensure the forward restoration processes during spontaneous plant community succession to ensure the stability of the community. In addition, we found that frequent visitor activities would increase the spread of invasive plants, which has also been reported in previous studies53. Low-growing plants are more tolerant to frequent trampling by visitors. Fewer bird-dispersed plants were found in microhabitats with high visitor activity. Most of the dispersal of bird-dispersed plants occurs through birds feeding on fruits and then flying to another location and defecating. We therefore suggest that this may be partly due to the fact that frequent visitor activity reduces the diversity of birds and their willingness to stay, as confirmed in previous studies54–57. Besides, we also found some studies to support that human disturbances such as transportation, deforestation, land development, etc. significantly reduce the removal rate of fruits by birds58–60.

As Beijing is a water-scarce temperate city in terms of climate and hydrology, it has much fewer aquatic and wet plant species than terrestrial plants61,62. Therefore, the plant communities near the water’s edge were mostly dominated by native wet plants such as Phragmites australis and Typha orientalis. In agreement with previous findings, species equipped with large leaves are better suited to mesic environments63. The probable reason for the high proportion of hydrochores and anemochores at the water’s edge is due to the fact that wind caused by the air pressure difference between land and water and runoff water bring in the seeds, which also accords with earlier studies9. We found more bird-dispersed plants closer to the water’s edge, which may be due to the fact that birds may bring seeds when drinking and foraging at the water’s edge. This hypothesis was supported by previous studies that that the land-water interface has the highest bird diversity in Beijing64. We also found a greater proportion of native plants in road-edge communities, which is consistent with a previous study suggesting that native plants are more likely to spread through roads in urban riparian corridors9. Our study further explains that this dominance may be caused mainly by air currents brought by vehicles to assist in their spread, due to the fact that native plants are primarily anemochorous.

The upper canopy may influence the survival of the lower vegetation through light, soil moisture, etc., but light is the most dominant of those pathways. In environments with higher canopy density, plants bloom in spring before the formation of the tree canopy to take advantage of the sunlight, such as Orychophragmus violaceus, Trigonotis peduncularis, and Plantago asiatica. In addition, the upper canopy affects wind and water in the microhabitat. Hydrochores and anemochores were more predominant in microhabitats with lower canopy density. This might be explained by the fact that open environments are more conducive to wind passage. Benefiting from the lack of shelter by the upper woods, precipitation will wholly directly fall and increase surface runoff to help hydrochores spread.

The most interesting finding is that the thickness of litter was positively correlated with the proportion of native plants, whereas it negatively correlated with the proportion of invasive plants. We can infer that these results are likely related to the different survival strategies of native and invasive plants. Most invasive plants are pioneer species, which prefer to germinate in the early stages of succession, i.e., in a full sun environment, to occupy survival resources as early as possible65–67. The thick litter blocks sunlight, which may cause difficulties in germination and seedling growth, thus preventing invasive plants. We also found fewer annuals and more perennials where the litter was thicker, and we assume similar reasons as above mentioned. Annual plant seedlings have difficulty growing due to the inability to photosynthesize under the shade of litter, whereas perennials can better adapt to this situation with their persistent aerial parts. In addition, the results showed that microhabitats with thicker litter had a greater proportion of anemochores and dispersed plants with higher thousand-seed mass, larger seed size, and rougher fruit surface. The following points may explain this: anemochore seeds with scraggly surfaces can be intercepted by litter easily. Plants with larger and heavier seeds may have benefited from the larger cotyledons supplying nutrients, which supported the emergence of seedlings from under a thick litter layer. Omnivorous birds foraging in the litter may bring in more seeds, as previous studies have confirmed the role of litter in enhancing the diversity of insects and soil fauna68.

Implications for design and management of the urban riparian corridor

Although governments and managers have gradually accepted the concept of spontaneous plant conservation, we still found numerous issues to be addressed in the conservation of spontaneous plants in urban riparian corridors in the course of our research. On the one hand, in the design process, the lack of empirical research makes it difficult to predict the establishment of spontaneous plant communities. The fact is that most landscape designers, although having the awareness of taking spontaneous plants into design, do not have enough knowledge to propose specific strategies and create favorable habitats for more desired spontaneous plants, resulting in the conservation of spontaneous plants always in a passive situation. On the other hand, there is a disconnect between the practice and decision-making of green space maintenance and management due to insufficient conservation awareness. The management of many green spaces still follows the traditional mindset of tidy lawns and always fully opened bedding flowers as a criterion for green space quality evaluation. At the same time, in some deserted lands ignored by city managers, invasive plants are always proliferating, and rare native plants are gradually dying out due to the lack of adequate maintenance. These are two contradictory ends. Given our concerns, we try to propose some references for spontaneous plant conservation in urban riparian corridors based on the results of this study.

Firstly, attention should be given to microhabitat design in the urban riparian corridor. Different habitats support spontaneous plant communities with certain characteristics. Each type of habitat is irreplaceable. Given this, we suggest that diverse habitat types should be created to support spontaneous plant assemblages with as high diversity as possible. For example, although the communities in WW habitats are low in diversity, they contain spontaneous species unique to the waterfront. Habitats near the water’s edge provide living space for a large number of late flowering, late fruiting, and long fruiting plants, which can provide food sources for pollinating insects and phytophagous animals for a longer period. For this reason, the area of waterside habitat can be increased by reducing hardened banks, as presented in many parts of the Wenyu River-North Canal bank, and changing them to gently natural sloping riverbanks and wetlands within the limits of the safety requirements of flood control. Considering the fact that the communities in UT habitats have the highest diversity indicators, and given the support of native, bird-dispersed plants, ant-dispersed plants, and autochores and the resistance to invasive plants by microhabitats with high canopy density, dense woodlands can be increased appropriately. A note of caution is that planting design should be oriented to establish a healthy plant community consisting of both spontaneous and cultivated plants. According to our observation, shelterbelts have been planted with a very small row spacing in many areas of the riparian corridor in recent decades to increase green volume rapidly. The overly dense planting prevents the canopy from spreading to obtain sunlight, thus hindering healthy growth and even causing extensive dieback of cultivated woods, which should be strongly discouraged.

Secondly, we emphasize that the maintenance related to spontaneous plants should be finely planned. Although landscapes consisting of spontaneous plants can provide high ecological value and natural aesthetics, patches of overgrown spontaneous vegetation may lead to monotony in the landscape and raise safety concerns for visitors69. Therefore, specific weeding strategies should be carried out for different areas in the corridor. For example, the weeding frequency can be reduced for areas far from the road and invisible to visitors. It is also possible to designate wilderness areas in the park for spontaneous plant protection, where nature education to promote the concept of biodiversity conservation can also be carried out. Some parks in Beijing have already taken such measures and have achieved positive outcomes70. In addition, weeding should be carried out as late as possible in autumn, after the seed maturation of most spontaneous plants, to ensure the successful reproduction of the plants. The plant pollination and dispersal syndromes should be considered together for management. On the one hand, many of the entomogamous spontaneous plants found in riparian ecological corridors in this study can generally provide nectar for pollinating insects. The fruits and seeds of mammal-dispersed, bird-dispersed, and ant-dispersed plants are generally available as food for animals. Therefore, for animal diversity conservation, species that can provide support for animal diversity during pollination and dispersal could be targeted for conservation. In microhabitats where such plants commonly occur, such as the roadside woodland edges with fine maintenance (RWH) microhabitats in this study, the frequency of mowing can be appropriately reduced to promote the colonization of plants. On the other hand, in the course of management, priority areas can be identified according to the spreading pattern of plants that have a strong invasive potential. For example, the invasive plants of Amaranthus genus, such as Amaranthus hybridus, Amaranthus blitum, Amaranthus spinosus, Amaranthus retroflexus, and Amaranthus palmeri, which are harmful in Beijing area, are generally hydrochores and anemochores. Based on the results of this study, plants with these dispersal syndromes tend to occur on riverbanks near the water’s edge, so prevention and cleanup should be emphasized in these areas.

Finally, management measures should be supplemented to protect and utilize spontaneous plants in urban riparian corridors. Spontaneous plants should not be left thoroughly unattended for the sake of high diversity. The monitoring and prevention of invasive plants should be emphasized in areas with frequent visitor activities and low maintenance. In native forests or wetlands with high conservation value in riparian corridors, limiting the scope of visitor activities is necessary to avoid damage to native vegetation caused by plant invasion. In light of the positive effect of litter on the conservation of native plants, we suggest that litter should be preserved as much as possible. The fact is that, due to the dry winter weather in the temperate monsoon climate, litter in green spaces is generally cleared in the fall to prevent forest fires in Beijing. Taking this into concern, we suggest that, while retaining the litter, severe regulation for prohibiting smoking should be adopted, and education and propagation of fire risk and prevention policies to the citizens should be strengthened.

Conclusion

Our study found that spontaneous communities in urban riparian corridors responded significantly to microhabitat variations, and the functional trait compositions can reflect well the filtering effect of micro environmental factors. Our results are important in furthering our understanding of the role of environmental factors on the establishment of spontaneous plant communities at fine scales and can serve as a good reference for the design, maintenance, and management of spontaneous plants in urban riparian corridors. The site conditions of spontaneous plants in fragmented urban green spaces are more complex and variable than in riparian corridors. Therefore, we suggest that future studies pay more attention to the response of the characteristics of spontaneous plant assemblages under the synthetic effects of complex urban environmental conditions at the micro-scale. In addition to the environmental factors involved in this study, other factors at the microhabitat scale, such as the type of cultivated plants, can also have an impact on spontaneous plant communities. For example, we found in our study that there are a few spontaneous plants established by the sprouting of upper cultivated plants. Therefore, we suggest that future studies could pay more attention to the effects of cultivated plants on spontaneous plant communities. In addition, we advocate that urban green space managers and designers place more emphasis on the stability and ecological functions of plant communities in addition to the diversity of spontaneous plants and maintain healthy spontaneous plant communities with high ecological benefits.
