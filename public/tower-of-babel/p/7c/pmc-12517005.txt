Qualitative study of stakeholder perspectives on dementia policy implementation in Macao: progress, challenges and future directions
Authors: Yu Zheng, Pou Kuan Tang, Yiwei Zhao, Menghuan Song, Hao Hu, Carolina Oi Lam Ung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo better inform dementia management, it is important to understand how individuals experience, interpret and respond to dementia policy in real life. This study aimed to explore key stakeholders’ perspectives on the critical determinants of effective implementation of the first dementia policy in Macao, and the impact of policy implementation on dementia care.DesignSemi-structured individual interviews were conducted in person with key stakeholders identified in earlier phases of the research. The participants were encouraged to share their opinions on the significance and objectives of the dementia policy, the changes in dementia care related to the launch of the policy, and how to better implement the policy for continuous improvement.SettingInterviews were conducted face-to-face at the participants’ office or at a location of their choice.ParticipantsIndividuals involved in the planning, establishing, leading, managing, operating, implementing, participating in, using and/or advocating for dementia policies and/or comprehensive dementia care models.ResultsAll participants perceived the Macao Dementia Policy as a landmark initiative that raised public awareness, fostered inter-professional collaboration and established a foundational service network. At the service level, they reported improvements in the accessibility of diagnostic pathways and greater attention to caregiver support. Nevertheless, implementation challenges remained, including fragmented service information, inadequate non-pharmacological treatment options and limited service capacity. Participants recommended that future actions should focus on optimising long-term policy planning and resource allocation, strengthening the direct support network for caregivers, and enhancing public education and research-informed innovation.ConclusionMacao’s experience suggests that the successful implementation of a dementia policy requires not only the establishment of formal service structures but also a concerted focus on optimising resource allocation and strengthening caregiver support. To further enhance the policy’s effectiveness, future efforts should prioritise designing and assessing targeted interventions.Keywords: Dementia, Health policy, Aging, Health Services for the Aged, PUBLIC HEALTH

Background

Dementia is a key public health priority affecting at least 20.1 million people in the Western Pacific Region,1 where national dementia plans have been implemented in countries like Japan (2000),2 Singapore (2009),3 New Zealand,4 Republic of Korea5 and Australia (2015).6 As a progressive degenerative disease characterised by deterioration of brain cells and a decline in cognitive functions, dementia imposes a grave burden on the patients, their families, the community and the healthcare system. The dementia policies are set to provide a comprehensive framework that guides subsequent strategies and actions to reduce dementia impact and improve dementia care.7

Effective implementation of dementia plans is complex as it requires multifaceted actions across various levels, including individuals, healthcare and social affairs systems, community, national and even international.8 Due to the variations in socioeconomic status, geopolitical and natural environment, and societal and population attributes, dementia plans are formulated and implemented differently to address the local needs. Nevertheless, many common challenges remain unresolved, such as stigma and discrimination, limited resources and under-concerted efforts.9 The resultant gaps in the provision of adequate dementia services may serve as common ground for experience-sharing and continuous learning.10

In Macao, dementia poses a growing challenge, driven by a rapid demographic shift towards a ‘super-aged society’ and a socio-cultural context marked by public stigma and low dementia literacy.11 12 Significant gaps in the dementia care system placed considerable strain on families and highlighted the urgent need for a coordinated, government-led response.11 12 At the international level, the WHO identified dementia as a global public health priority and called for coordinated action.10 Alzheimer’s disease international (ADI) advanced this agenda through regional strategies and by supporting local partners.13 Influenced by both local urgency and global advocacy, Macao considered dementia a public health priority and launched its first dementia policy in 2016 with the objectives of ‘Early prevention, Early detection, Early diagnosis, Early treatment and Early support’.11 The city became the 27th jurisdiction globally to have developed policies specifically targeting dementia and was later recognised as a ‘role model’ by ADI.

The Macao Dementia Policy (MDP) formed an important component of the 10-Year Action Plan for Elderly Services led by the government.14 With the successful launch of the policy, meaningful changes in dementia care configuration, including the unification of a dementia service network, the enforcement of a dementia-friendly community and the establishment of a one-stop service model for disease screening, diagnosis, treatment and support, were observed.12 Nevertheless, the challenges in dementia care remained as the burden of dementia continued to rise. A better understanding of the policy’s performance, implementation barriers and areas for improvement is needed to inform decision-making, making the MDP more effective and responsive to people’s needs.

An earlier review provided a helicopter view of the background and the implementation of the MDP.12 It comprehensively reported the local context, drivers, key aspects and strategic approach of the policy based on a thorough analysis of publicly available information. However, research on how the implementation was executed and experienced by different stakeholders remains scarce. Therefore, this study aims to examine key stakeholders’ perceptions about the factors pertaining to the successful implementation of the MDP, as well as the impact of policy implementation on dementia care. It is expected that the study findings will contribute to the sustainable development of dementia care in Macao and provide a reference for decision-makers in other similar settings.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study employed semi-structured interviews with key stakeholders to explore the social phenomena during the implementation of the MDP. Considering the subjective and socially constructed nature of the interview content, an interpretivist epistemology, informed by social constructivism, was adopted. This was to acknowledge that the knowledge gained from the interviews was co-constructed through the interaction between the researcher and participants.15 This approach enabled the collection of rich, detailed data reflecting participants’ perspectives and subjective experiences. It further facilitated an in-depth exploration of how stakeholders interpret policy implementation within their social and cultural context, and how these interpretations shape the alignment between policy measures and the needs of dementia patients and their caregivers.

The Implementation Stages Framework was used as a conceptual lens through which we populated, organised and interpreted our findings within the field of implementation science.16 The four stages of Exploration, Installation, Initial and Full provided a structured model for interpreting stakeholder experiences as part of a developmental process. In addition, the reporting of findings in this study follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines (online supplemental file 1).17 Ethical approval was granted by the Panel on Research Ethics of the University of Macau (reference number: SSHRE21-APP063-ICMS).

Study context

Being one of the most crowded places in the world, Macao has a population of 680 000 with a steadily increasing life expectancy. The average life expectancy of Macao residents was 84.81 years in 2024 as compared with 80.24 years in 2000. The proportion of the elderly (aged 65 and above) reached 14.0% and is expected to exceed 24.8% in 2041.18 The ageing ratio rose to 106.1% in 2024, and the elderly dependency ratio was recorded at 24.8%, equivalent to about 4 adults supporting one elderly. The median population age will surge from 39.2 years in 2021 to 47.9 years in 2041, an increase of 8.7 years.19

Dementia is a major and rapidly growing challenge associated with the ageing population. In Macao, the number of dementia patients is expected to increase from 5800 in 2021 to 10 000 by 2036, representing a growth of 72.4%. It is estimated that at least 1 in four elderly people over the age of 80 will be influenced by dementia by 2036.20 The care needed for older dementia patients, typically having more comorbidities, increased frailty and more severe cognitive decline, will become more demanding. As dementia patients age, their family caregivers also grow older. This is especially worrying when at least 75% of dementia patients in Macao are currently living with family.21 The demand for medical, social and financial resources will be mounting, adding to the pressure on healthcare and social care systems.

Early in 2012, the government brought together joint forces to establish the ‘Macao Pension Security Mechanism’ which aimed to encourage ‘family care for ageing at home; active participation and vitality of the elderly’ and build an inclusive society where ‘the elderly are provided for, have a place to belong to, and have something worthwhile to do.’22 This was followed by the ‘Ten-Year Action Plan for Elderly Services 2016–2025’ to promote ‘Active Ageing’ and improve the medical care, housing and retirement security for the elderly.23 The launch of the MDP in 2016 was therefore a natural extension of the elderly initiatives to integrate resources for people living with dementia.

Participants

The main categories of key stakeholders included: (1) Policy makers/executors of the MDP or coordinators of dementia care, (2) Frontline healthcare and social work professionals related to dementia care, (3) Representatives of dementia-friendly alliance organisations and legislators and (4) Caregivers of dementia patients. The inclusion criteria for interviewees were individuals (1) Aged 18 years or above and (2) Involved in planning, establishing, leading, managing, operating, implementing, participating in, using and/or advocating for dementia policies and/or comprehensive dementia care models in Macao. Individuals who lacked the capacity to provide consent to participate in the research or did not have cognitive functioning for effective communication were excluded.

Purposive sampling was employed to recruit participants in this study. Invitations which included the participant information statement, the consent form and the interview guide were sent via official emails or professional contacts to: (1) The person-in-charge of the governmental agencies responsible for healthcare and social welfare to assign representatives eligible to take part in the study, (2) Members of the legislative council in Macao who had a track record of seeking information or raising questions about the MDP and (3) Healthcare providers and social workers at the private healthcare institutions and non-governmental organisations (including the dementia-friendly alliance organisations) who had been involved in the provision of dementia care. A snowballing strategy was used to try to get in touch with additional key stakeholders, especially the caregivers of people with dementia.

Data collection

In-depth semi-structured interviews with 25 key stakeholders were conducted between December 2021 and April 2023. The data collection period was prolonged with the interruptions due to the COVID-19 pandemic in Macao, which posed challenges to conducting face-to-face interviews. The interviews were conducted by two members of the research team (YZ and PKT), both of whom have backgrounds in public health and are trained in qualitative interview techniques. To ensure objectivity, the interviewers were independent of the policy process and had no prior relationships with participants. The semi-structured interviews took place in person at a location of their choice.

Prior to each interview, participants were provided with the study objectives and an informed consent form giving them the option to ask any questions about the study and participate voluntarily. Participants were explicitly informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time, without any penalty or consequences. They were also assured that all personal data would be kept confidential and their identities would be anonymised in research findings. No compensation was provided for their participation. After the participants signed the consent form, the interviews started and were audio-recorded. Throughout the study, the research team held regular debriefing sessions to critically reflect on emerging themes and potential researcher bias.

The interviews were guided by a semi-structured interview guide developed in consultation with earlier research work12 14 24 (online supplemental file 2). Open-ended questions were used, which centred around the participants’ perspectives on their experiences about the changes in dementia care after the launch of the MDP, the key factors influencing policy implementation and outcomes, and areas of improvement. No more interviews were deemed necessary when data saturation was achieved for key emergent themes. All participants agreed to use Chinese in the interviews. Field notes were taken during each interview. The average duration of the interviews was 49 min (range: 45–70 min). No repeat interviews were conducted.

Data analysis

The interviews’ audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and de-identified to protect participants’ confidentiality. Chinese interview transcripts were translated into English by two bilingual investigators (YZ and PKT) and subsequently verified for accuracy by another team member with expertise in medical English (YZ) through cross-checking with the original recordings. During the transcription process, 2 of the investigators (YZ and PKT) listened to the interviews, read the transcripts line by line and reviewed observation notes separately.

A phenomenological approach was used to guide the interpretation of the participant’s life experiences, focusing on capturing their viewpoint and how they perceive their experiences.25 An inductive approach was also used to, based on the participants’ input, develop broader explanations and generalisations of their experiences.26 Initial codes were generated to capture the essential elements of the data. To ensure the transparency and traceability of the research process, we maintained a detailed audit trail, including verbatim transcripts, coding notes, decision logs and meeting minutes. Specifically, the coding results for each transcript were entered into the Excel file, compared and discussed among YZ and PKT, and confirmed by the senior investigator COLU. Key ideas and recurring themes were identified. Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection to allow for iterative refinement of the inquiry.

At the conclusion of each interview, participants were informed that they could request a review of their transcript for accuracy. No such requests were made by any participant. The analytical process involved both investigator and data source triangulation. Two researchers (YZ and PKT) first coded the transcripts independently, then reviewed and discussed their findings together to refine the analysis. These interpretations were further cross-validated against findings from our prior documentary analysis of the MDP. This iterative process of data collection and analysis continued until data saturation was reached. Data saturation was determined to have been reached when no new significant concepts or themes emerged from successive interviews. This point of thematic exhaustion was noted after the 23rd interviews and confirmed in the final two interviews.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Challenges at the dementia patients and caregivers interface

It was common to see patients who lacked awareness about dementia, resulting in delayed diagnoses. Participants reported that ‘Patients did not often recognise the disease early enough, therefore delaying their diagnosis and treatment’ (Participant 17, Social care professional). Some dementia patients, fearing stigma, deliberately concealed their symptoms, leading to delayed treatment, social withdrawal and refusal to seek necessary social support. Typical responses included ‘Some patients do not accept that having dementia is equivalent to being disabled and strongly resist being assessed as ‘disabled’ to qualify for assistance. The term ‘disability’ deters them from applying for and using social resources’ (Participant 13, Healthcare professional). As some participants shared, patients often exhibited mood changes such as depression, agitation, delusions of victimisation and lack of trust.

The responsibility of caring for dementia patients often fell on family members or other unpaid caregivers. They often mistakenly believed that dementia is a natural part of ageing, rather than a disease that requires treatment. Common observations were that ‘There are many cases where family members do not take dementia seriously at all. Often, they think it is just part of getting old and feel indifferent about it, as if having dementia doesn’t really matter’ (Participant 6, Healthcare professional). Also, caregivers may not be familiar with dementia and the support system, and as participants reported, ‘Most people may not even know there is a dementia clinic. In fact, many caregivers have said that they really don’t know’ (Participant 7, Healthcare professional). Many participants were also concerned that ‘When a family member suffers from dementia, it puts tremendous pressure on the entire family. In addition to financial pressure, the mental or psychological pressure on the caregiver cannot be ignored’ (Participant 15, Policy stakeholder).

The positioning of a dementia policy

Healthcare and social care services for dementia management had always existed for people aged 65 or above, including primary and secondary medical services in the public sector free of charge, annual medical vouchers and a community service network for the elderly, etc. However, initiatives specific to dementia were lacking, contributing to the fragmentation of services and confusion for people in need. As mentioned by participants, ‘The integration, cooperation and communication between healthcare services and social services are crucial. The social service system needs to understand how the healthcare system operates, and vice versa’. (Participant 17, Social care professional). A participant described the MDP as a milestone initiative, ‘The Ten-Year Action Plan for Elderly Services 2016–2025, launched in 2012, was an innovative policy that targeted people of a particular age group. Through more than a dozen departments, we worked together to formulate a specialised people-centred policy, based on which the MDP was formulated. It is just an epoch-making action’ (Participant 21, Policy stakeholder).

Perceived outcomes of the policy

Many participants noted that positive outcomes of the policy existed at different levels of the community, system and individual. One participant mentioned a higher level of awareness and acceptance in the community, saying, ‘The actual result so far is that there is a friendly community’. ‘Regardless of the community or citizens, everyone is actually more concerned about dementia, and the situation of labelling patients has been reduced, and everyone can be more accepting’ (Participant 5, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional). Another participant also noticed that the policy had helped shift the focus towards patients’ needs, adding, ‘Even our colleagues on the frontline have better ideas and take the initiative to refer the patients to social workers. The elderly themselves are more willing to accept it and are more conscious of seeking treatment’ (Participant 17, Social care professional).

A participant mentioned that the MDP achieved the goals of ‘Early detection and Early diagnosis’ to a large extent, explaining ‘At first, we saw many new cases at the clinic, and now we are seeing regular patients most of the time’. ‘It feels like a lot of patients have been discovered, although there are still undiagnosed patients out there’. (Participant 3, Healthcare professional). The key messages about early prevention and early treatment were fostered through public education. One participant noted, ‘The MDP also mentions prevention, giving the society the data to adopt preventative measures against high-risk factors such as smoking, drinking, hypertension, diabetes, letting the public know which factors carry higher risk’ (Participant 1, Healthcare professional).

The service network for dementia patients had been structured, as another participant noted, ‘A dementia medical service system has been built. ‘Led by medical units, there is a level of professionalism for which patients or family members have high recognition, and high recognition means they will have more trust’ (Participant 1, Healthcare professional). Most of the participants also observed positive changes among their patients, explaining, ‘In the past, when there was no MDP, patients would go down many wrong paths. Now we have dementia diagnosis and treatment centres, and clear definitions, patients can be referred properly for further evaluation and follow-up’ (Participant 25, Social care professional). Clear instructions for patients and their caregivers were considered beneficial as ‘The patients intuitively feel much better and were happier, and most of the reactions we observed at the clinics are positive’ (Participant 5, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional).

These findings highlight varied perspectives on the policy’s impact on dementia care. Policymakers and coordinators emphasised systemic advancements, such as enhanced community awareness and stabilised diagnostic pathways, while frontline professionals like pharmacists and occupational therapists focused on individual-level improvements, including proactive referrals and enhanced patient engagement. This contrast reflects the policy’s multifaceted influence across both structural and experiential dimensions of care.

Improvements in communication and collaboration

The importance of cross-sectional collaboration was repeatedly mentioned. ‘I think that medical services and social services have actually improved a lot in this era. When I first entered the industry, they were more passive, and everyone just did their own thing. But now there is an interactive model’ (Participant 17, Social care professional). Another participant added, ‘We have cooperated with the Social Welfare Bureau, which makes it easier for patients or family members to find and get a lot of help from social workers’ (Participant 5, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional). Non-governmental agencies also experienced a closer collaborative relationship with the government, as one participant explained, ‘The government has made plans from early days and established a dementia working group and cooperation mechanisms for the social service agencies like us to respond and cooperate’ (Participant 19, Social care professional).

Within the health system, improvement in vertical and horizontal communication about dementia care has been reported. One participant shared, ‘The frontline is in the best position to understand the needs of people in the community. The policy provides the mechanisms for us to communicate with each other and share our experiences bottom-up’ (Participant 9, Healthcare professional). Furthermore, healthcare providers from the primary care and the hospitals were able to work more closely together, as explained by another participant: ‘There is a person in charge related to the primary care health centres and hospitals. Doctors in charge and nurses in each district will meet regularly, reflect on the challenges and discuss solutions together’ (Participant 5, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional). Fostered interprofessional collaboration was also mentioned by a participant: ‘I think the policy is successful because at the clinic we work closely with doctors, nurses, social workers and pharmacists’ (Participant 3, Healthcare professional).

Key changes in dementia care delivery

The convenience of accessing dementia-related services was enhanced as one participant explained, ‘Since the establishment of the Dementia Diagnosis Centre and the Dementia Support Centre, it has become much more convenient, offering a one-stop, streamlined service to the patients and their family’ (Participant 9, Healthcare professional). Another participant also shared that ‘Now the patients can go to any community health centre and get immediate or easy assessment and be referred to a dementia clinic as soon as possible if necessary’ (Participant 4, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional).

The dementia clinic provided counselling to the patients to explain the overall treatment plan. ‘If we find any problems, we can re-communicate and educate the patients. The patients can come over immediately. ‘It will be more convenient for the patients, and they will have a deeper understanding’ (Participant 3, Healthcare professional). To this end, nursing staff played a major role in following up the patients’ needs as another participant explained, ‘The nurses at the dementia centre are very proactive and thorough and they will call regularly to remind the patients of follow-up visits or occupational therapy training sessions’ (Participant 11, Healthcare professional).

Multiple participants saw the importance of non-pharmacotherapy for dementia patients ‘For dementia, non-drug treatments are especially helpful for patients in the early and middle stages’ (Participant 5, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional). Training courses provided by occupational therapists, physiotherapists, social workers, nurses and doctors were in high demand as ‘These helped caregivers take care of patients at home while waiting for admission to hospitals or day care centres’ (Participant 24, Social care professional). Another participant added, ‘The feedback for cognitive stimulation therapy from family members is very good. They enjoy doing activities together with the patients and are very happy to see improvement in the patient’s overall mood, expression and language skills’ (Participant 1, Healthcare professional).

Many participants emphasised the extension of care towards caregivers. As a participant mentioned, ‘Most patients with dementia are accompanied by family members, so we take the chance to talk to the family members. If a family member has an emotional problem and needs someone to talk to, or needs help from a social worker, we will refer them immediately for follow-up or the one-on-one support meeting’ (Participant 5, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional). Another participant also shared that ‘Many patients and caregivers do not talk to their family or friends because they do not want to trouble others. Instead, they are more willing to talk to medical staff. ‘Doctor/nurse-patient relationship provides a channel for them to express their feelings’ (Participant 7, Healthcare professional).

The support mechanisms for dementia patients and their families were repeatedly discussed. One participant noted, ‘The government has actually done a good job in providing social worker support. If no one is home to take care of the patient, the social worker can come to deliver meals, change dressings and help patients use medicine for as long as the patient needs it’ (Participant 22, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional). The social care support was also made available at the dementia centre as another participant mentioned, ‘Now there are social workers in the dementia diagnosis and treatment centre to provide consultation on the spot to inform patients and their families what to do next’ (Participant 2, Healthcare professional).

Challenges in policy implementation

The majority of the participants highlighted three key areas of challenges: (1) Information about dementia care services, (2) Provision of non-pharmacological treatment and (3) Service capacities and waiting times. Many participants reckoned the information about dementia-related services was scattered and not easily accessible, making it difficult for the patients and their families to find help and plan ahead. As a participant mentioned, ‘Currently, the information on what kind of support is available for different levels of dementia is not very clear. It is often when patients have serious problems that they think of queuing up for admission’ (Participant 24, Social care professional). Another participant added, ‘If I am looking for information related to dementia, I may not find comprehensive information online. I may have to search different websites or web pages, go to the web pages of different societies to find support information’ (Participant 19, Social care professional).

Despite non-pharmacological treatments being effective in improving mental and behavioural symptoms of patients with mild to moderate dementia, it could be difficult to get the patients and their families engaged, as ‘Many family members are not clear about the benefits to them of patients’ (Participant 1, Healthcare professional). Sometimes, it was also difficult to demonstrate the full benefits of such treatment in the absence of a screening mechanism. As one participant explained, ‘Without the screening mechanism, having patients of different severities or too many patients in the same treatment programme just doesn’t work for the purpose’ (Participant 10, Social care professional). Manpower was another big challenge as ‘Only about 10% of occupational therapists work in day centres or residential homes. The turnover rate is high, and the manpower allocation is uneven. The same shortage also goes with physiotherapists and even nurses’ (Participant 2, Healthcare professional).

Given the shortages of manpower and other resources, the demands of dementia-related care far exceeded the supply, whether it be a non-pharmacological training programme, day care centre, nursing home, temporary care or support during emergencies. In particular, palliative care was a key area of concern in light of the ageing population, as a participant explained, ‘End-of-life services are always in short supply. When a patient’s condition deteriorates or reaches a very advanced stage, there are currently no end-of-life services specifically for patients with dementia’ (Participant 11, Healthcare professional). Some participants also voiced their concerns for the healthcare providers, ‘I feel that I am under a lot of pressure. I really want the patients to be well, but the overall condition of dementia patients will only get worse. If family members don’t understand, get angry with us or challenge your professionalism, it’s actually very stressful’ (Participant 8, Policy stakeholder and Healthcare professional).

Resource utility and distribution were other causes for concern, as ‘I think there are many overlapping resources. Different service agencies may provide similar services, but the information is not interoperable. When patients wait at different service agencies at the same time, unnecessary capacities will be occupied (Participant 12, Healthcare professional). Another participant added, ‘There are relatively fewer support resources in certain areas, and it is challenging for the family caregivers to commute the patients back and forth to the service centres’ (Participant 18, Social care professional).

Priority action areas

In terms of how to broaden and enhance dementia care efforts, the participants provided a range of suggestions which were categorised into seven priority action areas, including: (1) Make informed decisions about long-term policy planning, (2) Optimise the use of existing supporting resources, (3) Be more strategic about public education and awareness promotion, (4) Foster the implementation of the ‘Early’ goals, (5) Increase the capacities of dementia services, (6) Strengthen the support network for caregivers; and (7) Promote dementia research and information technology applications. Priority of actions

Discussion

This study gained an in-depth understanding of the implementation of the MDP, the changes in the dementia care model, and the priority areas for action in Macao (figure 1). Participants generally agreed that the policy had been effectively implemented in raising dementia awareness, improving dementia care, promoting collaboration and integrating resources across different sectors and levels. A comparison with experienced countries and the key action areas in the WHO’s ‘Global Action Plan for Public Health Response to Dementia 2017–2025’ indicates that the performance of the MDP aligns well with international standards. Nevertheless, major challenges, including the shortage of dementia care capacities in terms of professionals and infrastructure, as well as the lack of clear and centralised guidance from service authorities to patients and caregivers on how to navigate the support system, are yet to be resolved. Accurate and timely data about population-wide dementia trajectory is crucial to informing the leadership and the strategies for better preparedness for better dementia care and reduced disparities, especially for the growing population of more severe patients and their caregivers.

Logic model of the Macao dementia policy linking stakeholders, perceived outcomes and future action areas.

The launch of the MDP is undoubtedly a milestone in the optimisation of the dementia care model. The MDP launched in 2016 clearly identified the goals of the ‘5 Earlies’ which was followed by a series of actions accordingly (figure 2). Among them, ‘Early prevention and Early detection’ targeted at raising awareness among the general public and identifying the hidden cases in the community had been prioritised as the first steps. Actions for the goals of ‘Early Diagnosis, Early Treatment and Early Support’ also paced up to ensure appropriate services and support to patients with dementia and their caregivers as the disease progresses. Soon after the launch of the policy, a dementia-friendly community promoted by publicity and education, and a comprehensive dementia service network comprising healthcare and social care systems had emerged.

An overview of the dementia care model after the launch of the dementia policy in Macao.

The concept of dementia-friendly community originates from the WHO’s ‘Age-Friendly Cities’ initiative.27 The ‘Age-Friendly Cities’ framework primarily targets the physical and social barriers confronting older adults, whereas a dementia-friendly community expands on this by specifically addressing challenges associated with cognitive impairment. An inclusive dementia-friendly community refers to a community where people with dementia can be understood, respected, supported and able to live in an environment that can maintain confidence and values in one’s own life.28 While Macao’s efforts to build a dementia-friendly community are in line with the principles advocated by the International Dementia Society,29 our findings reveal that there were patients and caregivers who still encountered stigma and discrimination. This finding highlights the central challenge in transitioning from an ‘age-friendly’ to a ‘dementia-friendly’ paradigm, as overcoming entrenched social stigma is substantially more complex than addressing physical barriers. To this end, it is necessary to better understand the caregivers’ perspectives, create conditions that allow patients and caregivers to maintain interaction with society, and at the same time, continue to improve the understanding of dementia from all walks of life. Connectivity between people with dementia or caregivers and society can help create a more inclusive dementia-friendly community.30

There is a large body of research showing that non-communicable diseases (NCDs) (such as diabetes and hypertension) and lifestyle-related risk factors (such as physical inactivity, obesity, unbalanced diet, smoking, harmful use of alcohol) increase the risk of dementia.31 32 Other potential factors associated with dementia include social isolation and midlife depression.33 34 As the Lancet Commission stated in 2020, once the above-mentioned modifiable risk factors are effectively controlled, up to 40% of dementia cases can be prevented or delayed.35 In order to control dementia well, it is necessary to control the modifiable dementia risk factors with actions targeting the ‘root causes’ of the dementia risks. The goal of ‘Early prevention’ in the MDP should be further integrated into the overall public health strategies for NCDs, helping the public to make healthier choices and follow healthier lifestyles.36

Dementia progressively impacts the daily lives of individuals and their caregivers. Health and social care services, including community support and long-term care planning, must adapt to their evolving needs.37 According to the WHO, people with dementia should have the right to live in the community and be cared for according to their needs and preferences in order to maintain a level of functioning consistent with their fundamental rights, fundamental freedoms and human dignity. Providing comprehensive, person-centred, accessible and affordable health and social care services at the community level is essential for creating dementia-friendly communities. As patients reach advanced stages of dementia, requiring higher levels of dependence and experiencing greater morbidity, a more complex mix of care is necessary. Preparing long-term care capabilities, particularly in hospice care, is crucial for ensuring smooth transitions in patient care and offering physical, psychological and spiritual support needed by the caregivers.38

While participants acknowledged the policy’s achievements in establishing a formal service network and fostering inter-professional collaboration, they also pointed to barriers that constrain its day-to-day operation. Challenges such as resource misalignment, workforce shortages and navigational difficulties for caregivers indicate that advances in structural inputs have not been fully translated into functional efficiency. Notably, the priority action recommendations proposed by stakeholders (table 2) correspond directly to these identified barriers, providing evidence-based practical directions for future policy optimisation. Specifically, fragmented information hampers access to services, which can be addressed by establishing a centralised registration system, strengthening public-private partnerships and promoting electronic service platforms. In response to workforce shortages, the comprehensive human resource planning and dedicated interprofessional team development mentioned by stakeholders can effectively tackle this challenge. Limited service capacity highlights the need to expand specialised institutions and implement proactive demand forecasting. Additionally, optimising existing support resources and strengthening public–private collaborations, suggested by participants, can resolve resource overlap and inefficiencies.

Providing sustainable care from diagnosis to the end of life requires multidisciplinary collaboration of various care providers and coordination across different system levels. This is crucial for everyone involved with dementia. Medical professionals, social service professionals and front-line staff need adequate manpower, knowledge and skills. Training should be tailored to the service scope of the trainees and the needs of dementia patients at different stages. To address these challenges, it is essential to design and offer targeted training and education to support service capacity arrangements.39 However, training alone is not sufficient to meet the long-term challenges of dementia care. It also depends on systematic support and long-term planning, which highlights the need for strong health workforce governance. This requires an intersectoral human resources for health strategy that brings together the health, social welfare and education sectors to establish a sustainable system for workforce development in dementia care. At the same time, development needs to be closely integrated with the strengthening of service systems. Careful planning and optimisation of service configurations to meet growing demand also requires ongoing collaboration between government and non-governmental organisations.40 Effective patient and service data sharing and communication mechanisms are fundamental to ensuring the continuity of care services for patients with dementia in different settings.41 For example, a centralised and secure digital platform accessible to hospital specialists, primary care physicians and community social workers would help reduce the service fragmentation identified by our participants and allow for better integration of clinical and social care information.42

To cope with the complex challenges of dementia prevention, care and support, it is necessary to strengthen strategic research aimed at policy formulation and implementation, as well as service model construction and provision. The WHO has also formulated a blueprint for promoting dementia-related research, advocating the use of comprehensive strategic research methods to address the challenges of dementia from different aspects.43 However, the current dementia research in Macao is scarce and inclines towards basic and preclinical research.12 The availability of research funding and opportunities, and patient data will be crucial to fully expand the research towards the epidemiology and risk factors of dementia, the disease burden of dementia, policy, practice and service effectiveness, etc. Such research findings will be highly useful to inform the optimisation and execution of the MDP.44

Macao’s experiences with implementing dementia policy have important implications for other countries facing similar challenges. First, effective resource integration can provide better support for people with dementia and their caregivers and improve their quality of life. Second, the community-based comprehensive dementia care model effectively promotes social inclusion. Thirdly, a model of joint participation and collaboration among key stakeholders such as the government, non-governmental organisations/groups/institutions and academic units is needed to ensure that dementia policies are as comprehensive and effective as possible. Community organisations/groups are well positioned to directly contact different strata of people with dementia, raise awareness of dementia and dementia services from all walks of life, and play a key role in increasing acceptance. Coordination between different specialities can enhance patient-centred care through enhanced understanding and communication.

This study has several limitations. Due to the nature of qualitative research, the findings are highly context-specific to Macao and are not statistically generalisable. Therefore, while the detailed perspectives gathered may offer transferable insights for other regions, careful interpretation of the results is necessary. Additionally, despite the team’s efforts to ensure the comprehensiveness of the collected information, the inclusion of individuals with dementia was ultimately deemed unfeasible due to ethical and practical constraints. Consequently, our findings reflect only the perspectives of policy-makers, service providers and caregivers, lacking the critical first-hand insights of how dementia policies are experienced by their ultimate beneficiaries. Future research should prioritise the inclusion of individuals with dementia to provide a more holistic and person-centred understanding of the policy’s impact. A further limitation is that the credibility of our interpretations was not enhanced through member checking, as no participants requested the return of transcripts for review or correction.

Conclusion

Macao’s experiences in implementing the dementia policy through resource integration, community-based care and stakeholder collaboration may offer valuable lessons for other countries. Based on the perspectives of key stakeholders, the implementation of dementia policies in Macao has enhanced public awareness and supported the establishment of a basic service network. Participants reported improvements in dementia care processes, particularly regarding convenience, follow-up efficiency and caregiver support. However, challenges remain, including fragmented service information, limited resource allocation and insufficient promotion of non-pharmacological treatments. Future actions on improving service capacity, strengthening caregiver support and investing in health services research and innovative digital solutions should be prioritised. Building on these findings, future research should shift from broad policy evaluation towards designing and assessing targeted interventions.
