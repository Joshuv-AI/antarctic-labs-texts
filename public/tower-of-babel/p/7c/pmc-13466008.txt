ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIron is essential for oxygen transport, energy metabolism, and immune regulation. Yet iron deficiency is the most common micronutrient disorder across all age groups, affecting nearly one quarter of the global population. Iron deficiency triggers nutritional immunity, a host defense mechanism that withholds and redistributes iron, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality. This review outlines normal iron physiology, distribution and absorption pathways and on the consequences of deficiency across body compartments, with particular attention to type 2–driven diseases. Beyond anemia, insufficient iron availability disrupts immune homeostasis by promoting type 2 inflammation, elevating IgE, and activating mast cells and eosinophils. Regulatory macrophages, the central hub of iron cycling, adopt an inflammatory, iron‐sequestering state that reinforces malabsorption and redistribution. Epidemiology studies show higher iron‐deficiency risk in allergic individuals; low maternal iron or early‐life iron predisposes to eczema, wheeze, and asthma, while food‐allergen elimination (notably cow's milk) further worsens anemia risk. Clinical evidence indicates that restoring iron status through diet, supplementation, or fortification lowers IgE levels, improves lung function, and alleviates symptoms of rhinitis, urticaria, and asthma. Iron may therefore represent a modifiable determinant of allergic disease development and severity. Integrating iron assessment and nutritional care into allergy management may reduce disease burden and slow the progression of allergic march.

Introduction

Iron underpins oxygen transport, cellular respiration, and immune regulation. Iron deficiency is one of the most common micronutrient deficiencies, affecting nearly one quarter of the global population [1, 2, 3, 4] favoring type 2 inflammation and IgE when scarce—this review synthesizes current understanding of iron physiology and absorption, explains inflammation‐driven functional iron deficiency, and links these processes to the risk and severity of allergic disease.

Iron metabolism is tightly regulated to balance competing needs for oxygen transport, energy generation, and host defense. Absorption occurs primarily in the duodenum via divalent metal transporter‐1 (DMT1) and ferroportin, under the systemic control of the hepcidin–ferroportin axis [5]. Inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) induce hepcidin expression, suppressing intestinal iron absorption and sequestering iron within macrophages and enterocytes, an adaptive response termed nutritional immunity [6] (host‐driven iron withholding in response to inflammation). However, when inflammation is chronic or dysregulated, this mechanism contributes to functional iron deficiency, impairing erythropoiesis and cellular bioenergetics even when total body iron stores are sufficient.

Recent evidence suggests that iron deficiency itself alters immune cell differentiation and effector function [7, 8]. Iron restriction primes macrophages toward a more pro‐inflammatory, M1‐like state. Concurrently, low intracellular iron promotes expansion of Th2 lymphocytes and augments mast‐cell and eosinophil activation through altered redox signaling and mitochondrial metabolism [9]. These immunometabolic changes provide a mechanistic explanation for epidemiological observations linking iron deficiency with increased prevalence and severity of allergic diseases. Large cohort studies have shown that low maternal ferritin or transferrin saturation during pregnancy is associated with higher risk of childhood eczema, wheeze, and asthma [10], while restrictive diets in children with food allergy—particularly cow's‐milk elimination—further exacerbate anemia and micronutrient imbalance [11].

Available evidence highlights the bidirectional relationship between iron metabolism and allergic inflammation. Iron deficiency not only results from chronic immune activation but also amplify type 2–driven pathology [7]. Understanding this interplay is essential for identifying iron as a potentially modifiable factor in atopic disease. This review therefore integrates mechanistic, clinical, and epidemiological evidence to clarify how disturbed iron homeostasis contributes to allergic sensitisation, disease expression, and progression along the atopic march.

Iron Biology

General Features/Aspects of Iron

In the Earth's continental crust, iron is the fourth most abundant element by mass (≈5.63 wt%) [12]. It occurs (i) as structural Fe2+ and Fe3+ in primary silicate and oxide minerals (e.g., olivine, pyroxene, amphibole, biotite) [13], (ii) as secondary iron oxides/oxyhydroxides such as hematite (Fe2O3), goethite (FeO(OH)), and ferrihydrite [14] that form during weathering and color soils red/yellow/brown [15], and (iii) as complexes with soil organic matter, especially humic [16, 17] and fulvic [18] substances, which stabilize Fe3+ and influence solubility and mobility.

Organisms—including humans‐ predominantly use ferrous iron (Fe2+, electron donor), and ferric iron (Fe3+, electron acceptor). Under anaerobic conditions, Fe2+ (softer Lewis acid) predominates and coordinates N/S ligands, while in aerobic environments, Fe3+(hard Lewis acid) is more stable and binds O‐donor groups, often in octahedral complexes; although intrinsically insoluble at neutral pH, Fe3+ remains in solution when stabilized by organic O‐donor ligands [7].

Iron's high affinity for oxygen is both essential and hazardous: unbound “free iron” catalyzes the reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation via Haber‐Weiss and Fenton chemistry, causing oxidative damage [8]. Accordingly, virtually all iron in the body is protein‐bound (transferrin, lactoferrin, ferritin), or incorporated into heme (Hemoglobin, myoglobin) [7], plants likewise use heme proteins (e.g., leghemoglobins [19, 20] in soy [20] and peas [21]). Indeed, no organism lacks active, specific transport system for iron.

Different Forms of Iron‐Deficiency—Absolute and Functional

Iron deficiency affects roughly a quarter of the global population [4], especially children and women of reproductive age. Iron overload is rare and mainly genetic [22] (0.1%–0.5% in those of European ancestry) or transfusion‐related—nearly universal in chronically transfused thalassemia [23, 24] and common in other transfusion‐dependent anemias [25]. In many inflammatory conditions, redistribution—not shortage dominates: iron is sequestered in ferritin within liver and macrophages, rendering it metabolically unavailable despite adequate or elevated total iron. This functional iron deficiency (FID) impairs erythropoiesis and immunity [7, 26]. “Classical” iron deficiency without inflammation is uncommon in real‐world settings, as low‐grade inflammation is frequent in the elderly [27], obese [28], athletes [29] (elevated high‐sensitivity c‐reactive protein hs‐CRP or α1‐acid Glycoprotein AGP) and apparently healthy adults (higher IL‐6/CRP) [30].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines anemia, the most severe manifestation of iron deficiency, as hemoglobin (Hb) < 11 g/dL in children, < 12 g/dL in non‐pregnant women, and < 13 g/dL in men [31] with adjustments for smoking/altitude [3].

Defining iron deficiency is more complex [32] as reduced mobilization causes functional impairments across tissues [33]. Low circulating iron is associated with poor outcomes in autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis [34], lupus [35, 36] and thyroid disorders [37]); in cancer [38] and chronic conditions including inflammatory bowel disease [39], obesity [40] and atopic diseases [41, 42]. Distinguishing absolute and functional iron deficiency is often difficult, as many intermediate states exist; if uncorrected, functional iron deficiency may progress to absolute anemia.

Iron plays a crucial role in both erythropoiesis and immunity, but its biological impact depends on availability rather than total stores. In functional iron deficiency, cytokine‐induced hepcidin (notably via IL‐6) internalizes and degrades ferroportin on enterocytes, macrophages, and hepatocytes, sequestering iron as ferritin and limiting its circulation. Thus, metabolically available iron can be insufficient even when total body stores are adequate [7].

Iron markers must therefore be interpreted against stores and inflammation (Table 1). Hemoglobin (Hb), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), classical red cell indices, fall in iron deficiency (microcytic, hypochromic anemia) but may remain normal in early FID [43, 44]. Red cell distribution width (RDW), which captures variation in RBC size, rises early but is nonspecific; in FID/inflammation it may be normal or mildly increased [45].

Ferritin reflects stores and is specific in the absence of inflammation, but—as an acute‐phase protein—may be normal or elevated despite deficiency [46, 47, 48]. Serum iron decreases in both deficiency and during inflammation. Transferrin and total iron‐binding capacity (TIBC) increase in absolute deficiency but fall during inflammation, while transferrin saturation (TSAT) is consistently reduced across restricted states.

Soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) rises with erythroid iron demand and is less affected by inflammation [49]. The sTfR–ferritin index is high in absolute deficiency but less reliable in FID due to inflammation‐driven ferritin elevation [50].

Hepcidin is suppressed in deficiency, but rises in inflammation; when both coexist, hepcidin is usually low, as erythropoietic drive overrides inflammatory signals [51].

Zinc protoporphyrin (ZPP) increases when iron is unavailable for heme [52, 53]. Reticulocyte hemoglobin (CHr/Ret‐He) and the proportion of hypochromic red cells reflect a near real‐time measure of marrow iron supply and remain reliable during inflammation [54, 55]. Bone marrow iron staining remains the gold standard: absent in absolute deficiency, retained in macrophages with reduced sideroblast iron in anemia of inflammation [56].

Non‐Transferrin‐Bound Iron (NTBI) and Labile Iron Pool (LIP)

NTBI is the circulating fraction not carried by transferrin. Physiologically it is a heterogeneous fraction comprising heme‐bound forms (hemopexin–heme, haptoglobin–hemoglobin, α1‐microglobulin–heme, THAP4 and the lipocalins LCN1 and LCN2/NGAL)—and non‐heme ferric species (notably ferric citrate, also ferric phosphate, amino acid complexes such as glycine, asparagine, catecholamines, flavonoids, and fractions bound to proteins like lactoferrin) [7]. While usually small in the blood, NTBI is substantial in normal brain interstitial fluid, suggesting importance for cells of the central nervous system [57]. Tissues incorporate NTBI via transferrin‐independent pathways: ZIP14 in hepatocytes/endocrine cells [58], L‐ and T‐type Ca2+ channels in cardiomyocytes [59]; additional contributors include ZIP8 [60], DMT1, and TRPC6.

The labile iron pool (LIP) is the intracellular, exchangeable iron fraction: Fe2+ [61] weakly buffered by glutathione, and a smaller Fe3+ fraction bound to ATP [62], phosphate [63], and citrate [64]. Intracellular Fe3+–citrate complexes maintain ferric iron solubility distinct from ferritin storage. The LIP provides iron for heme and Fe–S cluster synthesis and helps regulate cellular metabolism.

Cellular Iron Regulators

Iron Regulatory Proteins 1 and 2 (IRP1 and IRP2) [65] maintain intracellular iron balance by sensing both iron and oxygen availability and adjusting gene expression accordingly [66]. NCOA4 regulates intracellular iron storage by mediating ferritin delivery to lysosomes for degradation (ferritinophagy) (Figure 1A). Under iron‐balanced conditions, NCOA4 exhibits basal ferritin turnover. During iron deficiency, NCOA4 stabilizes to promote ferritin degradation and iron release. In contrast, during inflammation, NCOA4 becomes iron‐bound, ubiquinated, and degraded, suppressing ferritinophagy.

When iron and oxygen are sufficient, IRP1 assembles a [4Fe–4S] cluster, switching to an aconitase form (supporting NADPH and biosynthetic metabolism) that cannot bind RNA. IRP2, which lacks this cluster function, is regulated primarily through oxygen‐ and iron‐dependent degradation: the E3 ubiquitin ligase FBXL5 becomes stable only when both iron and oxygen levels are high, marking IRP2 for proteasomal destruction [67]. Under hypoxia or iron depletion, FBXL5 is destabilized, allowing IRP2 to accumulate and remain active.

Thus, IRP1 and IRP2 integrate signals from both iron and oxygen status to maintain iron homeostasis, ensuring adequate iron supply during hypoxia while preventing oxidative damage under iron‐replete conditions.

Systemic Regulation

Iron bioavailability is governed by hepcidin, the master hormone of iron homeostasis, secreted mainly by the liver [68, 69]. Hepcidin binds to ferroportin [70, 71], leading to its internalization and degradation, thereby reducing iron absorption in the gut and iron release from macrophages [43, 51] (Figure 1B–D). Major sites of ferroportin expression are the duodenal enterocytes (dietary absorption), macrophages (erythrocyte recycling), hepatocytes (iron storage), erythroid precursors and placental trophoblast [72, 73, 74]. Ferroportin exports Fe2+ in a calcium‐dependent process [75], requiring oxidation by hephaestin (HEPH) or ceruloplasmin (CP) [76] to Fe3+ for transferrin loading. Circulating iron is delivered to most tissues via transferrin, while heme export depends on hemopexin [77, 78]; ceruloplasmin facilitates cellular iron mobilization and systemic release [79]. Other iron‐binding proteins, such as lipocalin 2 [80], ferritin, lactoferrin [81, 82, 83], hemopexin [84, 85, 86, 87] or haptoglobulin [88], contribute to NTBI handling [76].

Systemic iron homeostasis is maintained by a balance between dietary absorption, macrophage recycling of senescent erythrocytes, and hepatic storage release, all coordinated by the hepcidin–ferroportin axis [69]. Under iron‐adequate conditions, hepcidin levels are finely tuned to stabilize ferroportin in enterocytes, macrophages, and hepatocytes, ensuring ~1–2 mg/day of absorbed iron complements [89, 90] ~20–25 mg/day from macrophage recycling to sustain plasma transferrin saturation (30%–35%) and normal erythropoiesis [5] (Figure 1B). Hepcidin is positively regulated by plasma and hepatic iron via the transferrin receptor 2–HFE–BMP/SMAD pathway and by inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐6 (via STAT3). Conversely, it is suppressed by signals of iron demand, including hypoxia (via renal HIF‐2α and EPO induction), erythropoietic drive through erythroferrone (ERFE), and vitamin D [91] but not cobalamin [92, 93].

In dietary iron deficiency (Figure 1C), low circulating and hepatic iron suppress hepcidin, stabilizing ferroportin and promoting intestinal absorption (3–8 mg/day) [89, 90] and hepatic release (3–5 mg/day). The adaptive response begins locally in duodenal enterocytes, where falling intracellular Fe2+ stabilizes HIF‐2α, increasing DMT1 and ferroportin expression before systemic cues emerge. As deficiency progresses, serum transferrin saturation declines, sensed by hepatocytes via the TFR2–HFE–BMP/SMAD pathway [94] and by erythroid progenitors via iron regulatory proteins (IRPs) [95]. The kidney senses hypoxia and iron restriction via HIF‐2α‐mediated erythropoietin (EPO) production, which drives erythroid precursors to release erythroferrone (ERFE). ERFE further suppresses hepatic hepcidin (HAMP), ensuring that mobilized iron is directed toward erythropoiesis [96].

In contrast, inflammation disrupts this regulatory feedback (Figure 1D). Cytokines, particularly IL‐6, induce hepcidin expression via STAT3 and BMP/SMAD signaling, resulting in ferroportin degradation and functional iron deficiency [97]. Intestinal absorption and hepatic release collapse, while splenic and hepatic macrophages continue erythrocyte phagocytosis but retain iron intracellularly, elevating ferritin and lowering plasma iron. Although inflammation usually drives hepcidin up, clinical studies show that in the setting of severe anemia with high erythropoietic demand, hepcidin can remain low despite ongoing inflammation. At the same time, EPO production is blunted by cytokines, reducing ERFE and further reinforcing iron restriction. The net result is reduced plasma transferrin saturation, impaired erythropoiesis, and the iron‐restricted phenotype characteristic of anemia of inflammation [98].

Copper in Systemic Iron Regulation

Copper is an essential cofactor for the ferroxidases ceruloplasmin (in plasma and macrophages) [99] and hephaestin (in enterocytes) [100], which oxidize Fe2+ exported via ferroportin to Fe3+ for transferrin loading. In the absence of adequate copper, iron becomes “stuck” within enterocytes and macrophages: it cannot be efficiently oxidized or released into plasma, so despite adequate body stores, circulating iron falls and a state resembling functional iron deficiency develops [101, 102, 103]. Clinically, this manifests as anemia that is unresponsive to iron therapy but correctable with copper repletion [104, 105]. In contrast, excess copper has the opposite effect: ferroxidase activity is heightened, driving increased iron efflux from tissue stores into plasma. This enhanced mobilization can indirectly exacerbate iron deficiency by promoting iron loss through routes such as menstrual bleeding (e.g., in users of copper intrauterine devices) [106, 107]. Moreover, excess zinc supplementation may induce copper deficiency (via inhibition of copper absorption) thereby provoking the “functional iron‐deficiency” phenotype of trapped cellular iron [108, 109, 110]. Thus, systemic iron availability is finely balanced by copper status: too little copper locks iron inside cells, too much copper accelerates its exit and loss, and zinc intake must also be considered because of its ability to induce secondary copper deficiency [111].

Most iron resides in erythrocytes (~3 g, ~42% of total) [112], reflecting iron's dominant role in oxygen transport, followed by skeletal muscle [113, 114, 115], liver [116, 117, 118] (~1 g each), and bone marrow [119, 120, 121, 122] that support storage and erythropoiesis. Substantial pools exist in the brain (~0.4 g) and skin (~0.4 g, basal layer of the epidermis [127, 128, 129, 130, 138]), with smaller amounts in spleen [131, 132], lungs [133, 134], heart [114, 135], and negligible amounts in kidney [136] and pancreas [137].

Consequently, iron deficiency most visibly impairs hematopoiesis, but with prolonged shortage, neural, muscular, cutaneous, and immune functions are also compromised. The high iron content in specific organs reflects functional demands and links distribution to metabolic activity and disease susceptibility.

Iron's Central Role in Oxygen Metabolism and Energy Production

Iron is the core of heme in hemoglobin (in red blood cells) and myoglobin (in muscle). Hemoglobin delivers O2 from the lungs to tissues, while myoglobin provides a muscular oxygen reserve. Because more than 80% of human cells are red blood cells [139], iron is indispensable for systemic oxygen distribution.

Iron is equally critical for cellular respiration (Figure 2B,C and Table 3). Within mitochondria, iron functions in iron–sulfur (Fe‐S) clusters in Complexes I (NADH dehydrogenase), II (succinate dehydrogenase), and III (cytochrome bc1), and heme in Complex III (cytochrome b/c1) and Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), cycling between Fe2+/Fe3+ to shuttle electrons; electron flow from NADH and FADH2 to O2 (Table 3) is mediated by these iron‐centers driving proton gradients for ATP synthesis.

Disruption/iron deficiency impairs oxidative phosphorylation and redox signaling, especially in energy demanding tissues such as brain, heart, liver, and skeletal muscle. Without iron, mitochondrial respiration falters and metabolism shifts toward less efficient hypoxic pathways. Notably, the biosynthesis of both heme and Fe–S clusters occurs within mitochondria, underscoring the need to preserve mitochondrial health to maintain systemic energy metabolism [148].

Iron and Oxygen, Deficiency Induced Metabolic Shift Toward Inflammation

Iron deficiency reduces hemoglobin synthesis and tissue oxygen delivery, promoting hypoxia. Hypoxia‐inducible factor 1‐alpha HIF‐1α stabilizes under iron deficiency due to iron‐dependent prolyl hydroxylases PHD inactivity, even with normal oxygen (HIF‐1α) [149, 150, 151, 152]. HIF‐1α accumulation promotes glycolysis and inflammatory pathways.

Iron deficiency shifts metabolism to glycolysis with increased lactate [153]—hallmarks of inflammatory immune phenotypes [154]; iron repletion increases NADH production, OXPHOS, and ATP, reducing glucose use [153, 155, 156, 157, 158] (Figure 2D).

While HIF‐1α predominates in immune cells, promoting glycolytic metabolism and pro‐inflammatory gene expression under hypoxic or inflammatory conditions, HIF‐2α plays the principal role in systemic iron homeostasis, as it is selectively stabilized in enterocytes, hepatocytes, and renal interstitial fibroblasts, where it governs intestinal iron transport and erythropoietin synthesis [159].

Dietary iron regulates mTORC1: prolonged deficiency inactivates mTOR and promotes anemia [160] while AMPK pathways are activated [161, 162, 163]; leucine can restore mTORC1 under deficiency [164], whereas zinc/copper do not [165]. Increased mTOR activity can still appear in mild deficiency without anemia [160, 166] and in neural cells [161]. Overall, iron status drives metabolic reprogramming and inflammation.

DNA Synthesis and Cell Proliferation

Iron regulates the cell cycle [167] by supporting numerous iron‐dependent proteins (reviewed previously [168, 169]).

But in brief, all DNA polymerases, helicases (XPD, FANCJ, RTEL1, DDX11 helicase), glycosylases (MutY, Endonuclease III family) contain Fe‐S cluster [169]. Heme proteins regulate checkpoints (heme‐regulated inhibitor HRI via eIF2a phosphorylation); cytochrome c release induces apoptosis; cytochrome P450 regulates steroid and hormone synthesis and thereby proliferation; soluble guanylate cyclase is a heme protein activated by NO.

Non‐heme iron proteins with mono−/di‐iron‐centers include ribonucleotide reductase regulatory subunit M2 (RRM2, dNTP synthesis) [167], prolyl hydroxylases (PHD1‐3, HIF regulation) [170] and iron‐dependent histone demethylases (epigenetic regulators) and TET DNA demethylases [171].

Together, these govern replication/repair, epigenetics, hypoxia sensing, metabolism, and apoptosis.

Neurodevelopment and Brain Function

Brain iron supports energy production, neurotransmitter synthesis, and myelination, coordinated among neurons, astrocytes, and microglia [172]. Beyond locally produced transferrin [173], brain NTBI comprises ferric iron chelated by citrate and catecholamines like dopamine [174, 175]; neurons/glia take up iron via DMT1, ZIP14, and Ca2+ channels [58, 59, 60].

The highest iron concentrations are found deep inside the brain, in regions that control movement, motivation, and learning—specifically the basal ganglia (which include the caudate, putamen, and globus pallidus), substantia nigra and the red nucleus of the midbrain, thalamus and the cerebellar nuclei [173]. Within the basal ganglia, the substantia nigra—a small area important for producing dopamine—contains pigmented dopaminergic neurons that store iron in neuromelanin, helping buffer excess iron. When iron supply is insufficient, humans show impaired dopamine and norepinephrine metabolism [176], reduced activity of iron‐dependent enzymes, altered neurotransmitter balance, and deficits in myelination, leading to cognitive and increased fatigue symptoms.

Immune System Regulation

Iron supports proliferation/function of lymphocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils, but is tightly regulated during infection via “nutritional immunity” [177]. In this host defense strategy, iron is withheld from invading microbes by sequestration within storage proteins and immune cells, thereby limiting pathogen access. When these mechanisms are insufficient, or locally imbalanced, iron can also be expelled from tissues—such as during microvascular leakage or bleeding—and this may likewise occur during mast cell degranulation, which transiently increases vascular permeability and alters local iron distribution [178].

Bleeding

Although less often emphasized, microvascular bleeding and iron loss from damaged tissue may occur as part of defensive responses, thereby limiting iron availability and contributing to host protection. When tissue injury or infection occur, fibrin‐rich clots form that physically trap pathogens and prevent their dissemination [179, 180], an established process termed immunothrombosis. At the same time, iron/heme released from lysed cells is rapidly bound by e.g., hemopexin to further limit microbial access. Similarly, stress‐induced anti‐oxidative barriers such as melanin or melanized films [181, 182], incorporate iron to stabilize cross‐linked matrices [183] and restrict pathogen spread. Together, these processes illustrate that iron withholding and controlled extracellular sequestration are integral components of innate defense.

Macrophages

Macrophages are central to clearance, wound healing, and iron distribution. While 1–2 mg of dietary iron is absorbed daily, 20–30 mg is recycled by splenic macrophages [8, 184, 185].

M2 macrophages, marked by CD163 (the hemoglobin/haptoglobin receptor), maintain homeostasis with a large LIP, low ferritin levels, and high expression of iron export/import proteins [186, 187]. In contrast, M1 macrophages sequester iron in ferritin, limiting pathogen access. Under chronic inflammation, this retention contributes to anemia of chronic disease [188, 189, 190].

Iron deficiency or immune activation reduces turnover/LIP, impairs mitochondrial metabolism (citrate cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, Figure 3B), and shifts macrophages toward hypoxic glycolysis [7, 153]. This promotes pro‐inflammatory phenotypes in infants [191], children [192] and women [193] while a large LIP supports regulatory states [194, 195].

Macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils, and NK cells require iron for ROS generation and pathogen killing [196, 197, 198]. Iron deficiency hampers ROS production [199], yielding ineffective yet heightened inflammation, a hallmark of senescent cells [154].

Overall, iron deficiency shifts macrophages toward pro‐inflammatory activity and is associated with elevated C‐reactive protein, CRP, and low‐grade inflammation [200, 201, 202, 203].

T Cells

Iron deficiency leads to defective T cell maturation and reduced activation capacity. Activated T cells upregulate transferrin receptor (TFRC/CD71), solute carrier family members SLC11A2 and SLC39A14 for iron acquisition [204, 205]. TfRC mediates diferric transferrin uptake [206] and T cells also utilize NTBI (e.g., Fe3Cit3), thereby linking their function to systemic iron homeostasis [207, 208]. Lysosomal iron is required for proliferation; alkalization impairs TfR1 uptake, reducing viability. NTBI supplementation can rescue proliferation [209]. Iron depletion/chelation suppresses proliferation and effector functions (granzyme B, GM‐CSF, and IFN‐γ) [210, 211, 212, 213].

Iron deficiency affects T‐cells at multiple levels: In the thymus, it alters maturation, reducing CD8+ thymocytes in mice while causing accumulation of immature CD1a+ thymocytes in children [214, 215], reflecting a developmental block at an earlier stage. In the periphery, T‐cell activation rapidly increases iron demand for DNA synthesis and epigenetic remodeling.

CD8+ T cells require a ~3‐fold rise in iron within 24 h, compared with a ~2‐fold rise in CD4+ T cells [216], yet several lines of evidence indicate that CD4+ cells are more vulnerable to iron deprivation [212]. Mechanistically, CD4+ T cells contain a smaller basal labile iron pool, rendering them more sensitive to perturbations in iron flux during activation [212].

Clinically, reduced CD4+ counts and CD4/CD8 ratios have been reported in children [217, 218, 219, 220] and pre‐menopausal women [221] with iron deficiency, supporting greater CD4 sensitivity in vivo.

Among CD4 subsets, Th1 cells are particularly iron‐sensitive due to regulation via the IFN‐γ/STAT1 pathway [211, 222], higher dependence on TfR1, and smaller LIP [223], whereas Th2 cells are less sensitive to TfR1 blockade or chelation [223]. Elevated serum IL‐4 is observed in iron‐deficient children [201] and women [193], consistent with a Th2 skewing.

Under severe deprivation, proliferative responses are further curtailed and non‐proliferating “altruistic” T cells emerge, stimulating other immune cells via IL‐2, while unconventional non‐B, non‐T populations secrete IL‐4 and IL‐6 in response to IL‐3 or FcγR crosslinking [202, 224].

Thus, iron deficiency favors Th2 survival and polarization at the expense of Th1 responses, predisposing to a Th2‐skewed immune environment. Iron handling also influences Th17 cells: uptake of iron via siderophore‐bound lipocalin‐2 (LCN2) suppresses Th17 polarization in experimental vasculitis [225].

These findings together indicate that altered iron homeostasis reshapes the T‐cell landscape, driving reduced Th1/Th17 activity and enhanced Th2 responses. Epidemiological data support this pattern, with children and adolescents with anemia showing increased odds of atopic diseases, particularly microcytic anemia associated with eczema and asthma [226].

B Cells

Iron shapes B‐cell function and antibody production. Activation‐induced cytidine deaminase (AID), the enzyme required for class switching and somatic hypermutation, is inhibited by Fe2+; with iron deficiency enhancing AID activity and thereby promoting class switching and affinity maturation [227]. Activated B cells require mitochondrial mass and mROS for class switching; mROS limit Fe2+ incorporation into protoporphyrin PPIX, restraining heme and preserving Bach2 function, a transcription factor crucial for class switching and lymphocyte maturation [228]. Accordingly, iron deficiency potentiates Bach2 activity by attenuating heme synthesis, thereby favoring class switching [229].

Iron transport systems also link iron homeostasis to B‐cell function. The mitochondrial transporter ABCB7, which exports iron–sulfur (Fe–S) clusters to the cytoplasm for maturation, is essential for B‐cell development, proliferation, and class switching. Deficiency of iron impairs B‐cell development/proliferation/class switching [230]. Hence, iron deficiency can influence antibody production by modulating AID activity, altering Bach2‐mediated regulation of class switching, and impairing mitochondrial Fe–S cluster export via ABCB7. In terms of antibody class, it is well acknowledged that the IgG/IgM response is blunted under iron‐deficiency [231], while clinically IgE is the antibody class most often elevated under iron deficient conditions [7, 226, 232, 233, 234, 235, 236, 237].

Clinically, iron fortification in Vietnamese children (6–8 years) improved hematologic indices and anemia and was associated with a decrease in total IgE‐levels [234]. Elevated IgE with anemia is reported in malaria [236, 238], autoimmune hemolytic anemia [239], and in infants of Schistosoma‐infected mothers [240], linking iron status to IgE‐skewed humoral responses.

Mast Cells, Granulocytes (Basophils, Neutrophils, Eosinophils)

Mast cells play a central role in immediate allergic responses and are sensitive to iron availability.

Situated directly beneath blood vessels in nearly all tissues, their granules–rich in histamine, heparin [241] and proteases‐ enable them to fine‐tune local vascular permeability and clotting, thus influencing iron flux between blood and tissues.

Under iron‐deficient conditions, mast cells release histamine, a response that can be prevented by ferritin [242] and by iron‐loaded transferrin, lactoferrin, or β‐lactoglobulin [7, 194, 243, 244]. In addition, mast cells have been reported to scavenge damaged erythrocytes [245]. Their granules also contain iron [246], likely contributing to ROS generation, which is chelatable by the iron‐binding agent desferrioxamine that accumulates in lysosomes [247], suggesting that mast cells favor iron export upon stimulation rather than long‐term storage [248, 249, 250, 251]. Because their granules also contain heparin [241], degranulation exerts anticoagulant and permeability‐enhancing effects that can increase the likelihood of local microbleeding [252]. Together, these features position mast cells as rapid “iron sentinels”: when iron levels fall, they degranulate to transiently open the microvasculature. In this process, mast cells act as vascular modulators providing short‐term access to plasma iron, whereas resident macrophages—already embedded within the tissue—serve as the metabolic hubs that capture, recycle, and redistribute iron to restore local homeostasis.

Clinically, iron deficiency is associated with chronic generalized pruritus [253] and contributes to uremic pruritus in patients with chronic kidney disease. Conversely, supplementation can provide therapeutic benefit: in a clinical study, two months of oral iron therapy ameliorated chronic idiopathic urticaria in all 81 patients with mild hyposideremia [254]. Rare but documented cases of anemia‐associated rashes in individuals with iron‐deficient or aplastic anemia further suggest that mast cells may be primed under conditions of iron deficiency.

Eosinophils need iron to produce eosinophil peroxidase (EPX), a heme‐containing enzyme, during their maturation and activation, transferrin levels increase to bring in more iron, which supports the proper formation of EPX [255]. Poor fetal iron status associates with infant eosinophilia [256]. In mice, parenteral iron‐dextran reduces lung eosinophilia, whereas an iron‐deprived diet increases eosinophils after OVA challenge [257]. HIF‐1α supports IgE‐mediated basophil responses (IL‐4 regulation) [258]. In asthma, lung granulocytes show increased cellular iron with decreased BAL iron [259]. DMT1 and TfR1 are upregulated and correlate with ILC2/Th2 cytokines, while hepcidin is decreased, suggesting increased cellular iron uptake in asthmatics.

Neutrophils express several iron metabolism‐related proteins, including transferrin receptor 1 (TFRC), ferroportin (FPN), lipocalin‐2 (LCN2), lactoferrin, enabling them to take up or release iron upon stimulation [260]. Maternal low‐iron diet increases offspring airway inflammation [261]; iron‐deficient children show reduced oxidative burst/phagocytosis [262, 263]. Hypoferremia (low plasma iron induced by hepcidin) in mice suppresses neutrophil phagocytosis, bacterial killing, ROS and cytokine production as well as neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation [264]. Deficiency of IRP 1 and 2 skews hematopoiesis and impairs granulopoiesis [265]. Neutrophils rely on iron‐dependent systems to generate antimicrobial ROS [196, 197, 198, 199, 266]. These include NADPH oxidase complex (NOX2), which produces superoxide (O2 −) [267] and myeloperoxidase (MPO), a heme enzyme that generates hypochlorous acid (HOCl) [267]. When iron is deficient, these pathways are impaired [199, 268]. As a result, neutrophils show a weaker oxidative burst despite preserved—or even increased—activation, leading to inflammation that is ineffective at microbial clearance and ultimately compromises host defense.

Iron Uptake

General Aspect of Iron Uptake

Humans obtain iron from animal and plant foods; whereas plants and microbes rely on diverse soil (and/or host‐derived) strategies. Non‐grass plants acidify the rhizosphere and use ferric‐chelate reductases (e.g., FRO2) to reduce Fe3+ for uptake, whereas grasses secrete phytosiderophores for high‐affinity chelation. Microbes also secrete siderophores to chelate iron and in both, plants and microbes, the pH [269] plays a decisive role in determing the efficiency of iron‐chelation: carboxylates (pKa ~3.5–5) dominate under acidic conditions, catecholates above pH 6 [7] and hydroxamates across a broad range (often from fungi) [270].

These mechanisms not only ensure iron availability but also shape microbial communities through cross‐feeding of siderophores and iron complexes, which can affect pathogenicity—for example, in Salmonella, where siderophore interception limits virulence [271].

Dietary Forms

Dietary iron occurs as: (1) heme iron (hemoglobin, myoglobin); (2) leghemoglobin in legumes; (3) amino‐acid and peptide chelates–iron is bound to small molecules such as nicotianamine [272, 273, 274], cysteine and histidine, glycine [275] or short peptides rich in aspartic acid (Asp) and glutamic acid (Glu) [276, 277]. These peptide/amino acid chelates derive primarily from Fe‐S cluster proteins, which are unstable and break down in the acidic environment of the stomach [278, 279, 280]; (4) low‐molecular weight non‐heme complexes, notably iron–citrate (plus malate/other organic acids) [281]; (5) iron‐coumarins [282, 283, 284] and iron‐phenolics [285, 286, 287] (e.g., polyphenols like flavonoids [288], tannins [289] and anthocyanidins [290, 291, 292, 293]); (6) carbohydrate/polysaccharide complexes [294] (pectins [295, 296], alginates [297, 298]); (7) protein‐bound iron [299, 300, 301, 302] egg transferrins [303], lactoferrin [304], plant albumins [305, 306] and lipocalins such as β‐lactoglobulin carrying iron complexes [194, 300] or heme [307]; (8) ferritin‐bound iron; (9) phosphate ligands such as phytate [308].

Factors Affecting Absorption and Dietary Bioavailability

Dietary and individual‐dependent factors strongly influence bioavailability [68, 309, 310]. Small soluble iron complexes are generally well absorbed, while phytates form large, stable complexes that strongly reduce absorption [311, 312, 313, 314]. Commensal bacteria [315] and microbes present in fermented foods (e.g., Bifidobacterium [316], Lactobacillus [317], Escherichia coli [318]) can partially overcome this by degrading phytates with phytase or inositol phosphatases [319], thereby increasing mineral availability in the colon, which itself have a small capacity to absorb iron [320, 321, 322].

Enhancers of iron absorption are vitamin C, which facilitates iron uptake [323, 324, 325, 326, 327], vitamin A [328], amino acids such as glycine or asparagine [275, 329] and bile salts [330]. Fat improves non‐heme as well as heme iron absorption [331].

Dietary polyphenols may inhibit iron absorption [332, 333, 334, 335]; however, uptake of polyphenol‐iron complexes – already containing iron, such as quercetin‐iron complexes, promotes absorption and contributes to an improved iron status [41, 194, 288, 336, 337, 338] in animal models. Similarly, anthocyanidin‐iron complexes degrade spontaneously [339, 340] or microbially [341] into smaller phenolics [275, 342, 343, 344], which are more bioavailable and may underlie reported anti‐anemic effects of anthocyanidin‐rich foods in vivo [345, 346, 347].

Supplementation strategies increasingly use encapsulated forms such as liposomal iron (ferrous iron in a phospholipid bilayer) [348] or sucrosomes (starch‐like vesicles) [349, 350]. A key distinction is that these encapsulated forms are exported via the lymphatic system rather than the portal circulation, unlike classical salts such as ferrous sulfate [351, 352].

Microbe‐to‐Host Iron Transfer

Several commensals and symbionts can transfer iron to host [353]. Escherichia coli and related Enterobacteriaceae siderophores (enterobactin, salmochelin) are internalized by mammalian cells [354]; Lactobacillus plantarum 299v enhances absorption [355, 356]; cyanobacterial siderophore‐iron complexes improve iron status in anemic rats [357]. Fermented foods containing siderophore‐producing probiotics (Bacillus, Pediococcus [358]) and siderophore‐iron enriched yeasts [359] have been shown to improve anemia, supporting the concept of microbe‐to‐host iron cross‐feeding.

Human Absorption

Average daily dietary iron intake is 10–20 mg, of which ~10% is absorbed [360]. Diet remains the main iron source for humans, offsetting daily losses via skin and gut cell shedding and minor bleeding [7, 8]. Biliary excretion of excess hepatic ferritin has also been reported [361].

Heme iron (meat, fish) is often considered to be better absorbed [362, 363, 364] than non‐heme iron, which requires reduction to Fe2+ before uptake [363, 365], though radio‐labelled meal studies also showed a similar bioavailability (~12% overcooked meat; 27% fried meat; ~9% vegetables) [362, 363]. Importantly, each form enhances absorption of the other: heme iron facilitates non‐heme uptake (the “meat factor”), while non‐heme iron and accompanying dietary factors improve heme utilization (matrix effects), meaning that both forms are physiologically interdependent. Phytate‐rich lentils [366] reduce uptake [367], while oily fish, vitamin C [368, 369] and meat [370, 371, 372, 373, 374, 375] improve it; calcium inhibits absorption only at very high levels [376, 377, 378, 379, 380]. Leghemoglobins [19, 20, 21] from soy and other legumes provide additional heme and may confound assays if not considered [381]. Balanced diets can outperform high‐meat diets in post‐menopausal women [382]. Fat enhances iron absorption via lymphatic transport; low‐fat diets can reduce mineral uptake [383], and milk may also improve iron bioavailability [384].

Mechanism of Uptake

Under steady‐state conditions, iron is mostly absorbed in the duodenum and proximal jejunum [385] (Table 4 and Figure 4). It is important to recognize the unique characteristics of the duodenum, which maintains a more acidic environment (pH 4–5) and serves as the entry site for bile and pancreatic secretions [8]. In contrast, the rest of the small intestine has a more alkaline pH of 7–9. This acidic environment is essential for optimal iron absorption [419, 420]. Consequently, any sustained elevation of duodenal pH—such as that caused by excessive or chronic use of antacids—can significantly impair iron uptake [421, 422, 423, 424].

Dietary iron uptake.

Iron‐bioavailability: dietary forms and modulators. Small soluble complexes (iron–citrate, amino acid chelates) are absorbed mainly in the duodenum, whereas larger protein‐bound forms (ferritin, lactoferrin, proteins) are preferentially taken up in the jejunum. In the colon, microbial enzymes can release iron from phytate, polyphenol, and polysaccharide complexes, though host uptake is limited. Bioavailability is reduced by phytate (forming insoluble complexes), polyphenols (precipitating iron), antacids (raising gastric pH and limiting solubilization), and inflammation (hepcidin‐mediated ferroportin block). Enhancers include vitamins C and A, bile salts, fat, amino acids, proteins, microbial activity, and soluble small complexes.

Heme iron

Heme iron remains incompletely understood as there is no single, universally accepted apical heme transporter in human enterocytes. Earlier studies proposed the proton‐coupled folate transporter HCP1 (SLC46A1) [388, 425, 426, 427, 428], with some debating its role, while several studies support its heme transport [388, 426, 429, 430] (Table 4 and Figure 4). Alternatively, MRP5 (ABCC5) may participate in heme transport [431, 432, 433]. By contrast, HRG1 (SLC48A1) localizes mainly to endosomes/lysosomes (and basolateral membrane of polarized cells) [434] and mobilizes intracellular heme prior to heme oxygenase‐1 HO1 degradation; liberated Fe2+ exits via ferroportin and is transferred to transferrin after oxidation by hephaestin/ceruloplasmin; intact heme may also be exported across the basolateral membrane by FLVCR1.

Non‐heme iron

Non‐heme iron (Fe3+) must first be reduced to Fe2+ by ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and/or duodenal ferric reductases such as duodenal cytochrome b (Dcytb/CYBRD1) [389, 435, 436, 437, 438], STEAP3 [439, 440], or FRRS1 [388, 441, 442]. Fe2+ enters enterocytes via divalent metal ion transporter 1 (DMT1/SLC11A2) [386, 443], and in some cases via ZIP14 (SLC39A14) [444, 445]. Basolateral export requires ferroportin. Amino‐acid‐iron chelates (e.g., glycine, cysteine, asparagine, histidine) are absorbed through PEPT1 (SLC15A1), mainly in the jejunum, or via duodenal DMT1 (Table 4 and Figure 4).

Protein‐bound iron

Protein‐bound iron is absorbed in the jejunum/ileum via receptor‐mediated, clathrin‐dependent endocytosis: ferritin uptake occurs via SCARA5 [446, 447]; lactoferrin uptake can occur via ITLN1 [398, 399, 411, 448], cubilin‐amnionless (CUBN‐AMN) [411, 412, 413], the mannose receptor [400], or LRP1 [449] with packaging into lipoproteins/chylomicrons for lymphatic transport [409, 450, 451, 452, 453]; transferrin‐bound iron may be internalized via CUBN‐AMN [411, 414] and the debated apical TfR1 [454], and is exported via the lymph [455], hemoglobin‐derived iron can be partially digested to heme (absorbed via HCP1) or internalized intact via CUBN‐AMN [411], exporting intact protein via the lymph.

Endogenous (e.g., LCN2) or exogenous lipocalins (e.g., beta‐lactoglobulin [194, 300]), egg proteins [303] and plant legumins such as those in peanut [417] bind iron–chelates from bacterial/plant origin, including flavonol–iron (e.g., quercetin–Fe [194, 300] catechin‐Fe [338]), facilitating uptake and systemic transport. SLC22A17 [456] mediates endocytosis of lipocalin–iron complexes at the apical surface, with export via lymphatic lipoprotein pathways. Similarly, calcium‐dependent CUBN [411]‐AMN, alone or with LRP2 [457]/DAB2 [458] supports uptake/export. This places lipocalins and other dietary proteins [458] alongside ferritin, transferrin, and lactoferrin as contributors to NTBI in circulation, highlighting their dual role in nutrition and immunity (Table 4 and Figure 4).

Allergens Binding Iron

A defining feature of many major allergens is their ability to bind, transport, and enhance absorption of iron (Table 5). This has been observed for several allergen families, including lipocalins, seed storage proteins (7S, 11S), PR10 proteins, Alt a 1 and 2S albumins [194, 195, 299, 300, 302, 337, 338, 417, 478, 479, 480].

Iron availability itself critically regulates allergen biology. In plants and fungi, iron deficiency induces stress responses that enhance the synthesis of phenolic iron‐chelating compounds [284, 481, 482].

Many plant proteins achieve iron binding via anti‐oxidative flavonoids, with iron restriction promoting the synthesis of phenolics in plants [284, 481, 482] and simultaneously upregulating many allergens themselves, which are stress‐response proteins—thereby directly linking environmental iron scarcity to increased allergen expression and iron scavenging capacity [470, 472]. Many plant allergens bind iron indirectly via antioxidant flavonoids, whose production increases during iron restriction [284, 481, 482]. For example, ligands of PR10 and 2S/7S proteins in birch and hazelnut share quercetin as a common core structure [459, 465]. Strawberry proteins bind catechins [460], while peanut allergens Ara h 2 and Ara h 6 can associate with epigallocatechin‐3‐gallate [473], quercetin [461], epicatechin [462] or proanthocyanidins [463]. Notably, such flavonoid binding can decrease the allergenicity of these proteins [464]. Consistently, Alternaria increases allergen production and pathogenicity under iron‐restricted conditions [470], whereas peanuts grown in iron‐rich soils produce fewer allergens [472].

Thus, iron deficiency shapes both allergen abundance and ligand composition, favoring the environmental release of iron‐scavenging apo‐forms.

Iron binding also modulates immunogenicity. Reduced allergenic activity of iron‐loaded (holo) form has been demonstrated for milk allergens [41, 194, 300, 468], egg proteins such as Gal d 3 [303], peanut allergens (Ara h 1–7S, Ara h 3–11S protein) [417], as well as the birch allergen Bet v 1 [195, 299] and the fungal allergen Alt a 1 [302, 470].

From an immunological perspective, the iron‐scavenging capacity of allergens has direct consequences at mucosal interfaces. In their apo‐form (iron‐depleted), allergens compete for local iron and, in an iron‐deficient host, amplify nutritional immunity, deepening iron restriction and lowering the threshold for Th2 polarization.

Conversely, iron‐loaded (holo) allergens are less immunogenic and can function as iron shuttles to antigen‐presenting cells (APCs). Because the intracellular labile iron pool determines the metabolic programming and activation status of APCs, iron delivery directly modulates antigen presentation and cytokine polarization. This has been demonstrated for major allergens across different families including Bet v 1 [195, 299], Bos d 5 [194, 300] and Alt a 1 [302, 470], where apo‐forms promote Th2 responses, whereas holo‐forms increase intracellular iron and favor tolerogenic signaling.

Importantly, this reflects antigen‐modifying immune regulation rather than global immunosuppression. The outcome likely depends on antigen load: rare, intermittent aeroallergen exposure favors a more antigen‐specific modulatory effect of iron occupancy, whereas the high and repeated exposure to dietary proteins promotes a broader tolerogenic environment.

Together, these findings identify iron occupancy as a critical immunometabolic switch controlling allergenicity.

Dietary Uptake Under Inflamed Conditions

Inflammation markedly impairs intestinal iron absorption, particularly the pathway to the portal circulation [483]. As a result, dietary iron intake can be similar between groups, yet absorption and status differ. For example, obese children show reduced bioavailability due to higher circulating hepcidin [349, 484]; similar patterns are observed across chronic inflammatory conditions in children [485], women [486], and men [487].

In IBD, mucosal inflammation and hepcidin upregulation further inhibit intestinal uptake, leaving oral iron poorly effective and increasing the risk of oxidative mucosal injury from unabsorbed luminal iron [488]. In such contexts, intravenous iron is preferred for efficient repletion [488, 489], although in long‐term observations oral iron can still provide some benefit over nearly a year in selected IBD patients [487].

The host's hepcidin‐driven restriction of iron is not only a pathological barrier to absorption but also a defense strategy. Mild iron deficiency and inflammation‐induced sequestration can transiently protect against pathogens by limiting their access to iron. Such restriction reduces susceptibility to parasites such as Plasmodium falciparum [490, 491, 492], to bacteria like Mycobacterium tuberculosis [491, 493, 494, 495, 496], and even to certain viral infections [494, 495]. This protective mechanism, however, becomes maladaptive when chronic, leading to anemia of chronic inflammation [497, 498, 499].

To improve absorption when inflammation is present, dietary modulation may help. Vitamin A supports iron mobilization and uptake [10]; vitamin C reduces non‐heme iron to facilitate absorption [500]; major whey proteins of cow's and sheep's milk, as well as certain amino acids [275, 329], also enhance absorption [275, 329, 501, 502, 503, 504]. Conversely, coffee or tea consumed with non‐heme iron–rich foods should be avoided to limit inhibition. During inflammation, when portal absorption is impaired, the lymphatic pathway may serve as an alternative and potentially more effective route of uptake [177, 505]. Thus, combining anti‐inflammatory strategies (to modulate hepcidin) with supportive dietary interventions offers a rational approach to improve iron bioavailability and mobilization (Box 1).

Iron Supplementation: Balancing Efficacy, Safety, and Context

While supplementation can correct deficiency, it must be approached with caution (Box 1). Excess unabsorbed iron may accumulate as “free” (labile) iron, generate ROS and foster pathogen growth. Clinical history underscores this risk: in children with kwashiorkor (protein‐energy malnutrition), iron supplementation during acute protein deficiency was sometimes fatal, likely due to extremely low transferrin leaving iron unbound and fueling infection [506, 507]. Decades later, a large RCT in Zanzibar showed that routine iron–folate supplementation in preschool children increased death and hospitalization, largely from infection, prompting the WHO to recommend targeted supplementation only alongside malaria control and ideally after deficiency screening [507].

Other settings illustrate the same principle. In obesity, women and children often consume similar amounts of iron as their non‐obese peers, but subclinical inflammation reduces bioavailability and produces functional iron deficiency despite adequate intake [508, 509, 510]. By contrast, iron‐fortified milks/formulas in infants have not been associated with higher risks of sepsis [511] or infection [512]. This safety likely reflects their mode of delivery: iron is incorporated in a bioavailable form absorbed via enterocytes and lymphatic transport, reducing unbound luminal iron and placing iron trafficking under immune surveillance. This controlled absorption distinguishes fortified milks from poorly absorbed salts and supports their use to safely correct iron deficiency in infancy [511, 512].

Finally, iron's impact on the gut microbiome is highly context dependent. In infants and children living in high‐infection‐burden settings, unabsorbed iron reaching the colon can reduce beneficial taxa, favor expansions of enteropathogens, alter microbial metabolites, and increase diarrhoeal morbidity. In contrast, many studies in adults report little or no microbiome change, highlighting that age, infection burden, iron status, diet, form, and dose all shape the outcome [513, 514].

Pathogenic Bacteria Adapted to Iron‐Restricted Mucosal Environment

At mucosal surfaces, microbial access to iron is limited because most extracellular iron is protein‐bound and further restricted during inflammation by nutritional immunity. As a result, many microbes are suppressed, while pathogens adapted to iron scarcity are selectively favored and typically deploy multiple, partially redundant iron‐acquisition strategies depending on the niche and inflammatory context.

Neisseria gonorrhoeae acquires iron under iron‐limited conditions by extracting Fe from human transferrin and lactoferrin [515].

A closely related strategy is heme and hemoprotein utilization, which becomes particularly important in inflamed or damaged mucosa. Both Haemophilus influenzae and Staphylococcus aureus grow in iron‐restricted environments by exploiting host heme‐containing proteins [516, 517]. In iron‐deficient states, increased epithelial fragility, vascular permeability, and minor mucosal bleeding may release hemoglobin and other heme sources locally, creating transient niches that favor heme‐adapted pathogens.

A further adaptation involves evasion of host siderophore sequestration. In the inflamed intestine, pathogenic Escherichia coli and Salmonella enterica gain a competitive advantage by producing the C‐glucosylated enterochelin derivative salmochelin [518], which is not bound by lipocalin‐2. In extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli, the iroA locus confers resistance to lipocalin‐2 and enhances virulence, while salmochelin‐deficient mutants show reduced pathogenicity [519].

Finally, some pathogens adapt by reducing iron dependence or altering metabolic state. Streptococcus species can shift toward manganese‐dependent antioxidant systems under metal limitation [520], while Chlamydia trachomatis enters a persistent, non‐replicative state under iron restriction [521]. In addition, Listeria monocytogenes, which initiates infection at the intestinal mucosa, lacks classical siderophores but can acquire iron under low‐iron conditions by using host catecholamines such as dopamine or norepinephrine as siderophore‐like iron donors [522]. Together, these mechanisms illustrate that iron restriction reshapes mucosal microbial ecology rather than uniformly suppressing infection.

Implications for Allergic Diseases/Asthma

Evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between anemia and allergy, with anemia occurring more frequently in allergic individuals and atopy predisposing to anemia development. Across U.S., Korean, and Japanese cohorts [226, 523, 524, 525], reports consistently show that individuals with atopic diseases are more likely to be iron deficient [226, 523, 526, 527] and anemic than non‐allergic individuals [226, 523, 524, 527] and that children with atopic dermatitis show higher rates of iron deficiency, with low serum iron linked with lower lung function [528]. Among food allergies, cow milk allergy is a particularly strong risk factor for iron‐deficient anemia [529, 530, 531, 532]. In adults, higher iron stores are inversely associated with asthma prevalence, while lower tissue iron has been implicated as causal for lower lung function in general [533, 534, 535].

Maternal iron status during pregnancy further influences allergic outcomes in offspring. Low maternal iron is associated with childhood wheeze, reduced lung function, increased risk of allergic sensitization [10, 536, 537, 538] and atopic eczema in early childhood [539]. Similarly, low cord blood iron at birth correlates with atopic urticaria, infantile eosinophilia, and wheeze by age four [10, 256]. In contrast, good maternal iron status reduces the risk of childhood atopic dermatitis [256] and asthma [10, 256, 536, 538, 540, 541]. In asthmatic adults, low serum iron levels are inversely related to blood eosinophil counts [542] and adequate iron stores are linked to lower odds of lifetime asthma, current asthma, and asthma exacerbations [533].

Nutritional avoidance also contributes to anemia risk. Because many allergenic foods are iron‐rich, strict elimination diets may reduce iron intake, worsening anemia risk in allergic patients. This has been reported particularly in atopic dermatitis, where dietary iron deficiency is associated with disease severity [543, 544, 545, 546], with reduced iron intake being documented in multiple studies [547, 548].

Allergic diseases are also more prevalent in patients with chronic anemic conditions. For example, individuals with beta‐thalassemia major (Cooley's anemia), who suffer from impaired hemoglobin synthesis and organomegaly, are more likely to develop allergic diseases [549, 550] and asthma [549, 551, 552, 553]. Similarly, atopic dermatitis patients also carry higher risks for anemia and cardiometabolic comorbidities, including coronary heart disease, angina, and peripheral artery disease [554].

The relationship between anemia and allergy appears bidirectional. Allergic individuals are more likely to be anemic, and the incidence of anemia is higher among those with atopy. Asthmatics without anemia have a five‐fold increased risk of developing anemia within five years [555], and allergic children as young as two nearly double their risk of anemia within a year [525]. Thus iron deficiency is both common in allergic individuals and a risk factor for worsening disease [556].

Importantly, an improved iron status has consistently been associated with reduced allergic symptoms [254, 540, 557, 558]. Prenatal iron supplementation, especially when combined with folic acid, significantly lowers the risk of atopic dermatitis in children by age six [540]. In Finland, prenatal iron supplementation reduced the risk of asthma in children of asthmatic mothers by nearly 70% [558]. In adults, dietary iron supplementation improved iron and reduced allergic rhinitis symptoms in pollen‐allergic women [41] and two months of oral iron therapy alleviated chronic idiopathic urticaria in patients with mild hyposideremia [254]. Clinical trials further suggest that iron supplementation, rather than deworming strategies, decreases IgE levels [234].

These findings align with broader evidence that nutrient/iron‐rich conditions help protect against allergy development [41, 559, 560, 561, 562, 563, 564, 565].

Conclusions

Allergic individuals face a significantly higher risk of iron deficiency than the general population. Iron deficiency is not merely a consequence of allergic disease but a biological modifier that lowers the threshold for immune activation. By imposing metabolic iron restriction and skewing immune regulation toward Th2 dominance, iron deficiency increases sensitivity to otherwise innocuous environmental proteins.

Allergenic proteins play a dual role in this process. Many major allergens possess intrinsic iron‐binding capacity. In their apo‐form, they compete for iron at the mucosal interface and within tissue compartments, thereby activating nutritional immunity. In an iron‐deficient host—where immune activation thresholds are already reduced—this iron withdrawal deepens local and systemic iron restriction and converts a physiological iron‐withholding response into Th2‐biased inflammation and IgE sensitization. Thus, iron deficiency does not directly cause allergy; rather, it creates the permissive immunometabolic environment in which iron‐scavenging proteins can emerge as allergens. Even small amounts of such proteins may suffice to trigger this self‐reinforcing cycle of iron stress and type 2 immune activation.

In iron‐sufficient conditions, or when allergens are encountered in their iron‐loaded (holo) form, these same proteins function as nutrient carriers and may support immune balance rather than inflammation.

Despite the strong evidence linking iron deficiency to allergic disease severity, iron status remains underrecognized in routine clinical care. Many clinicians and nurses fail to connect poor iron status with worsening allergic symptoms, meaning patients miss out on a simple but potentially effective treatment option. At the same time, mismanagement of food allergies frequently aggravates the problem: families are advised, or choose, to avoid entire food groups unnecessarily. Children with atopic dermatitis are especially vulnerable, as restrictive diets have been directly linked to more severe disease [545, 546, 566]. Widespread avoidance of fruits, vegetables, or animal products due to cross‐reactive symptoms often reflects caution rather than true allergy—and instead of protecting patients, it sets them up for nutritional deficiencies, including iron deficiency, that worsen the very condition they are trying to manage. Misunderstandings about allergen stability only add to the problem: for instance, people allergic to bovine serum albumin can safely eat cooked meat, since this protein is destroyed by heat [567, 568, 569]. Similarly, general dietary habits such as vegetarian or vegan diets, if not carefully balanced, can limit intake of iron, protein, and other minerals, further compounding allergic disease severity [570]. A further challenge lies in the limited awareness of iron physiology among healthcare providers: the unconscious use of antacids, which raise gastric pH and thereby blunt duodenal iron absorption, can inadvertently worsen deficiency [571, 572]. In short, poor recognition and unnecessary restriction create a vicious cycle where iron deficiency both results from, and fuels, allergic disease.

Breaking this cycle requires better awareness and proactive management by healthcare professionals [573]. Nurses and clinicians should routinely screen for iron deficiency in patients with chronic allergic disease, ask about dietary habits, and correct misconceptions about food avoidance. Only foods with proven reactions should be restricted, while safe reintroduction of tolerated foods should be encouraged. When dietary limitations are unavoidable, referral to a dietitian for structured nutritional planning is essential to prevent iron deficiency and other micronutrient gaps. Education on allergen stability—for example, how cooking reduces or eliminates some allergens—can broaden safe food options. Alongside counseling, promoting iron‐rich foods, food fortification, or targeted supplementation when needed can substantially improve both nutritional status and allergic outcomes. Ultimately, iron deficiency in allergy is preventable, but only if recognized and actively managed as part of routine care.

Because the allergenicity of many major proteins is mechanistically linked to their iron‐scavenging capacity, correcting iron deficiency addresses a root biological driver of allergic sensitization rather than merely treating its symptoms.

Literature Search Strategy

This narrative review was based on a structured, non‐systematic literature search. Each author covered specific thematic areas and conducted independent searches in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science up to January 2026. Preference was given to original research articles—particularly primary human data on iron distribution and mechanistic immune studies—over secondary reviews. Whenever possible, mechanistic findings were supported by human clinical or epidemiological evidence; where unavailable, robust in vitro as well as in vivo studies were considered. Artificial intelligence–assisted tools were used to support literature identification, while final study selection and interpretation were performed by the authors.
