Diagnostic accuracy of drawing tests and their combinations for post-stroke cognitive impairment in patients with lower education
Authors: Yanqin Wang, Yongqi Wang, Yayun Xiang, Fang Fang, Ning Yan, Yaoyue Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) is highly prevalent among ischemic stroke patients in China, a population who received less education compared to Western counterparts. Commonly used cognitive assessments often affected by educational levels. Drawing tests may mitigate educational bias, but the diagnostic accuracy of combined drawing tests remains understudied and unclear.MethodsThe diagnostic accuracy of the Cube Copying Test (CCT), Clock Drawing Test (CDT), and Pentagon Copying Test (PCT), and their various combinations was evaluated against the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) as the reference standard. Ischemic stroke patients at a hospital in Chongqing, China, were enrolled between January 2022 and June 2025. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess diagnostic accuracy.ResultsAmong 413 participants, 260 were in the acute phase (mean age was 64.3 years, and 47.7% had primary education) and 153 in the convalescent phase (mean age was 64.7 years, and 33.3% had primary education). PSCI was identified in 61.2% and 60.8% of acute and convalescent patients, respectively. Among individual tests, the CDT showed the highest AUC in both phases (acute: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.78, 0.88; convalescent: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.72, 0.86). For test combinations, the full battery (CCT + CDT+PCT) achieved the highest AUC in the acute phase (0.85, 95% CI: 0.80, 0.90), with a sensitivity of 80.5% (95% CI: 73.3%, 86.2%) and a specificity of 83.2% (95% CI: 74.1%, 90.0%); while the CCT + CDT combination performed the best in the convalescent phase (0.82, 95% CI: 0.75, 0.88), with a sensitivity of 78.5% (95% CI: 68.5%, 86.1%) and a specificity of 71.7% (95% CI: 58.4%, 82.2%). These two combinations exhibited AUC > 0.8 across sex and age subgroups. In acute patients, the full battery showed better AUC in those with primary (0.88, 95% CI: 0.80, 0.97) and secondary education (0.84, 95% CI: 0.76, 0.92) compared to higher education (0.74, 95% CI: 0.57, 0.90). This educational gradient was less evident in convalescent patients (primary school: 0.72, 95% CI: 0.52, 0.93; secondary school: 0.83, 95% CI: 0.75, 0.92; ≥university: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.42, 0.88).ConclusionCombined drawing tests demonstrate good diagnostic accuracy for PSCI, with their de-linguistic design reducing educational bias, particularly in acute patients. The CCT + CDT+PCT combination is recommended for acute-phase PSCI screening, while CCT + CDT may suffice during convalescence. These findings suggest that combined drawing tests may offer practical tools for rapid PSCI screening in stroke patients with lower education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05008-y.Keywords: Post-stroke cognitive impairment, ischemic stroke, cognitive screening, drawing tests, diagnostic accuracy

Introduction

Post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) is characterized as cognitive dysfunction resulting from a stroke, whether ischemic or hemorrhagic, and encompasses a spectrum of impairments, ranging from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia [1]. The prevalence of PSCI varies widely, with estimates ranging from 18% to 83% [2]. Up to 60% of stroke survivors experience cognitive impairment within the first year after a stroke [2, 3]. PSCI could lead to difficulties in activities of daily living, worsened mental health, reduced quality of life, and increased caregiving demands, all of which significantly impact stroke patients and their families, thereby escalating the healthcare burden [4]. Therefore, timely detection of PSCI through early and regular neuropsychological evaluations is crucial for effective management and intervention, which could improve the prognosis for stroke patients in turn [1, 5].

Cognitive impairment is often assessed using performance-based, standardized measures of cognitive function. Although no gold standard exists for PSCI screening, several brief cognitive tests have been widely utilized [1], including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [6] and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [7]. However, the MMSE has repeatedly demonstrated a ceiling effect, limiting its effectiveness in identifying MCI [8]. In contrast, the MoCA provides a wider scoring range, smaller ceiling effect, and higher sensitivity in detecting cognitive heterogeneity and MCI [9]. As a result, the MoCA is often recommended over the MMSE, particularly for detecting MCI [1]. This recommendation also applies to the screening of PSCI [1]. Munthe-Kaas, et al. [10] However, both the MMSE and MoCA heavily rely on the respondent’s language ability, and do not adequately take into account stroke-related impairments, such as aphasia and apraxia, which can adversely affect test performance and lead to an overestimation of PSCI [11]. Furthermore, two systematic reviews have demonstrated the educational bias in both the MMSE and MoCA [11, 12]. That is, highly educated individuals may perform well on cognitive assessment tasks despite experiencing cognitive decline, potentially masking their cognitive impairment [11, 12]. In contrast, individuals with lower educational levels may perform poorly due to reduced comprehension of instructions or difficulty with literary-demanding items/questions, leading to over-identification of cognitive impairment [11, 12].

The ideal cognitive impairment screening test should be quick to administer, well-accepted by patients, easy to score, as well as relatively independent of cultural background, language, and educational level [13]. Although the MMSE and MoCA were originally designed as a 5- to 10-minute screening tool, neurologists often find that their administration takes longer in practice. Drawing tests, such as the Pentagon Copying Test (PCT), Cube Copying Test (CCT), and Clock Drawing Test (CDT), offer quick and user-friendly cognitive screening by requiring visual construction and assessing multiple cognitive abilities, including visuospatial processing, executive function, planning, and fine motor skills [14, 15]. The PCT is included in the MMSE as a visuospatial test item, while the CCT and CDT are part of the MoCA, serving as assessments of visuospatial and executive function [6, 7]. These tests have been employed to screen for MCI and dementia [16], as well as PSCI [17, 18], and can be administered alone or in combination with other multidimensional cognitive assessments [18, 19]. In addition, because drawing tests are visually based, they could reduce reliance on language and require only simple instructions, making them less affected by patients’ language proficiency and educational background [20, 21]. However, the performance of combined drawing tests in detecting PSCI remains unclear.

Older adults in China have historically received considerably less education compared to their counterparts in Western countries [22]. The rapid aging of the population, coupled with increased life expectancy and shifts in lifestyles, has led to a substantial rise in the number of stroke patients in China [23]. A large multicenter cross-sectional study in China involving 24,055 first-time ischemic stroke patients reported a prevalence of PSCI as high as 78.7% [24]. The continuous increase in stroke patients, along with the high prevalence of PSCI, poses considerable challenges for both the healthcare and social care systems in China. Therefore, it is imperative to have a quick, valid, and reliable cognitive screening tool for PSCI with a small education bias. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of single and combined drawing tests for post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) in ischemic stroke patients with different educational levels, and to identify the optimal test combinations for the acute and convalescent phases.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study of ischemic stroke patients recruited from the Department of Neurology and the Department of Rehabilitation at University-Town Hospital of Chongqing Medical University between January 2022 and June 2025. Patients aged 18 years and older who met the diagnostic criteria for acute ischemic stroke, as outlined in the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke 2023 [25], and confirmed by CT or MRI, were included. We excluded patients who had: (1) audiovisual dysfunction, severe aphasia, severe motor impairment affecting upper-limb function, or a mental disorder that impeded their ability to complete the drawing tests or understand task instructions; or (2) cognitive impairment due to Alzheimer’s disease, Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal dementia, or other causes, which was confirmed by review of medical records and relevant treatment histories. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of University-Town Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (LL-202225). All patients provided written informed consent. Since cognitive function in most stroke patients significantly declines within 14 days after the stroke and at three months or more post-stroke [24, 26], participants were categorized according to stroke phase. The acute phase was defined as within 14 days after stroke onset, and the convalescent phase was defined as 3 months or more after stroke onset. A total of 413 patients were included, comprising 260 patients in the acute phase and 153 patients in the convalescent phase.

Reference standard

The cognitive assessment was conducted by a qualified neurologist in a quiet room using the Chinese version of MoCA (Beijing version) [27]. The MoCA encompasses eight cognitive domains: attention and concentration, executive function, memory, language, visual-spatial skills, abstract thinking, calculation, and orientation. Each domain is scored on a scale ranging from 1 to 6 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 30 points. For patients with less than 12 years of education, one extra point was added. Using the recommended cut-off point from a recent meta-analysis, we defined PSCI as having a MoCA score < 22 [28].

Drawing tests

Drawing tests included the CCT, CDT, and PCT. The CCT and CDT are part of the MoCA, while the PCT is a component of the MMSE [6, 7]. In the CCT, participants were asked to copy a three-dimensional cube drawing. A score of one was given if the drawing met the following criteria: (1) maintained three-dimensionality, (2) all lines were drawn and connected with little or no space, (3) lines were relatively parallel and of similar length, (4) no extra line was added, and (5) the cube’s orientation in space was preserved. In the CDT, participants were instructed to draw a clock, put in all the numbers, and set the time to 10 past 11. Scoring was based on three components: the completeness of the clock face as a closed circle, the presence of all 12 numbers, and the correct positioning of the two hands – each component contributed one point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 3 [29]. In the PCT, participants were tasked with copying a drawing of two intersecting pentagons. A score of one was assigned if the drawing met the following criteria: (1) preserved all sides and angles, (2) the two pentagons intersected, and (3) the whole figure was recognizable as a pentagonal structure [6]. For different combinations of drawing tests, the total score calculated was the sum of the scores from the specific drawing tests included in the combination. Accordingly, the score ranges for the test combinations were: 0–2 points (PCT + CCT), 0–4 points (CCT + CDT and PCT + CDT), and 0–5 points (CCT + PCT+CDT).

Covariates

Participants’ age, sex, educational attainment, and the onset of ischemic stroke were included as covariates. Educational attainment was categorized as primary school (0–6 years), secondary school (7–12 years), and university and higher (≥ 13 years).

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with percentages (n [%]), while continuous variables were expressed either as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) based on their distribution. The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to evaluate whether the continuous variables followed a normal distribution. When normality was not satisfied, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for between-group comparisons. Diagnostic performance of drawing tests for PSCI was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, optimal cut-off values, and Youden’s index. AUC values were interpreted as follows: 0.5–0.7 (poor discrimination), 0.7–0.8 (acceptable), 0.8–0.9 (excellent), and > 0.9 (outstanding). Statistical comparisons between AUC values were performed using the DeLong’s test. All subgroup analyses and pairwise comparisons were pre-specified as exploratory and hypothesis-generating; therefore, no formal correction for multiple testing was applied. Results were interpreted primarily on the basis of effect estimates, confidence intervals, and the consistency of diagnostic patterns. All analyses were performed in R version 4.2.2, ROC curve analysis was performed using the “pROC” package, and a two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of ischemic stroke patients in the acute and convalescent phase, respectively. PSCI was identified in 61.2% (159/260) of acute patients (median assessment time post-stroke: 4 days, IQR: 2, 7) and 60.8% (93/153) of convalescent patients (median assessment time: 230 days, IQR: 145, 354). The PSCI group were older (acute phase: P < 0.001; convalescent phase: P = 0.005) and had lower educational attainment (acute phase: P < 0.001; convalescent phase: P < 0.001) compared to their non-PSCI counterparts. In the acute phase, more of the PSCI group were men. A higher proportion of acute-phase PSCI patients had zero scores across all single drawing tests than non-PSCI patients: PCT (78.0% vs. 30.7%), CCT (91.8% vs. 50.5%), and CDT (55.3% vs. 12.9%) (all P < 0.001). This pattern was also seen in convalescent patients (for PSCI and non-PSCI groups, PCT: 54.8% vs. 33.3%, P = 0.009; CCT: 82.8% vs. 45.6%, P < 0.001; CDT: 26.9% vs. 3.3%, P < 0.001). Similar differences between the PSCI and non-PSCI groups were also observed for different combinations of drawing tests.

Optimal cut-off values for individual drawing test and their combinations were first determined in both acute- and convalescent patients using the maximum Youden’s index, which maximizes the balance between sensitivity and specificity. Among the individual drawing tests, the CDT demonstrated the highest AUC (Table 2, 0.83, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.78, 0.88) and specificity (84.2%, 95% CI: 75.2%, 90.4%) but the lowest sensitivity (74.9%, 95% CI: 67.2%, 81.2%) in acute patients. In contrast, the CCT had the highest sensitivity and lowest AUC and specificity, while the PCT fell in between. For convalescent patients, only the CDT achieved an acceptable AUC (0.78, 95% CI: 0.72, 0.86), whereas the AUC values for both the PCT and CCT were poor. Furthermore, the sensitivity and specificity of all tests were poor except for CCT’s sensitivity and CDT’s specificity. The AUC improved in all pairwise combinations of the drawing tests for both acute- and convalescent patients. The full test battery outperformed the pairwise combinations in acute patients (AUC: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80, 0.90; sensitivity: 80.5%, 95% CI: 73.3%, 86.2%; specificity: 83.2%, 95% CI: 74.1%, 90.0%). However, in convalescent patients, the CCT + CDT combination showed the best performance (AUC: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.75, 0.88, sensitivity: 78.5%, 95% CI: 68.5%, 86.1%; specificity: 71.7%, 95% CI: 58.4%, 82.2%), followed by the full test battery. The low specificity of the combinations of PCT + CCT (41.7%) and PCT + CDT (38.3%) at the convalescent phase may limit their use as screening tools. The ROC curves and corresponding AUC for the optimal test combinations in acute- and convalescent patients are shown in Fig. 1. In the acute phase, the AUC of the CCT + CDT+PCT combination was higher than that of any individual tests, which confirms the clinical value of combined screening tests.

Given that the CDT’s outperformed other individual drawing tests, we further compared it with each drawing test combination using DeLong’s test (Supplementary Table 1). Results showed that in acute patients, the CCT + CDT combination had a higher AUC than the CDT, while the full test battery exhibited a marginally higher AUC (P = 0.051) and achieved the highest Youden’s index (0.637) compared to the CDT (0.591) and CCT + CDT combination (0.598). In convalescent patients, the CCT + PCT combination had a lower AUC than the CDT, whereas the CCT + CDT combination, while not differing in AUC from the CDT, had a higher Youden’s index (0.502 vs. 0.448).

When samples were stratified by sex, age, and educational attainment (Fig. 2), the full test battery demonstrated excellent AUC across most subgroups in acute patients: male (0.86, 95% CI: 0.81, 0.92), female (0.80, 95% CI: 0.69, 0.91), below 60 years old (0.89, 95% CI: 0.83, 0.96), 60 years older and above (0.82, 95% CI: 0.75, 0.90), primary school education (0.88, 95% CI: 0.80, 0.97), and secondary school education (0.84, 95% CI: 0.76, 0.92). However, the AUC was only acceptable in those with university or higher education (0.74, 95% CI: 0.57, 0.90). For convalescent patients, the CCT + CDT combination showed slightly reduced but still excellent diagnostic performance across sex and age groups, with all AUC values > 0.80. This combination had an excellent AUC in patients with secondary school education (0.83, 95% CI: 0.75, 0.92), but acceptable AUC in those with primary school education (0.72, 95% CI: 0.52, 0.93) and poor AUC in those with university or higher education (0.65, 95% CI: 0.42, 0.88). In summary, the combined screening tests showed similar diagnostic accuracy across sex subgroups, minimal performance variation with age, and lower AUC in patients with higher educational attainment, particularly during the convalescent phase. The DeLong’s test showed no statistically significant differences in AUC across sex, age, and education groups in both acute and convalescent phases (Supplementary Tables 2–3).

We further explored the diagnostic performance of drawing tests and their combinations (AUC > 0.8) across various combination of sex, age, and educational attainment (Supplementary Tables 4–5). In acute-phase male patients, irrespective of age, the AUC of the PCT + CDT, CCT + CDT, and full test battery combinations decreased with increasing educational attainment. However, this trend was not replicated in acute-phase female patients due to the small number of patients with university or higher education. In convalescent patients, this educational pattern was less clear that the CCT + CDT combination and full test battery exhibited the better AUC in male patients with primary school education and those with university and higher education, as well as in female patients with secondary school education. Nevertheless, certain subgroups also had insufficient samples to estimate AUC.

Discussion

This study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of the CCT, CDT, PCT, both individually and in combination, for PSCI screening in acute and convalescent stroke patients with low educational levels. Our results showed that the full test battery combining all three drawing tests and the CCT + CDT combination demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance with excellent accuracy in acute and convalescent patients, respectively. Their diagnostic performance tended to decrease with increasing educational attainment, particularly in acute patients.

In acute patients, the CDT exhibited the highest diagnostic accuracy, while the CCT demonstrated the highest sensitivity but lowest specificity, with the PCT falling between. However, none of the tests alone provided satisfactory diagnostic accuracy in convalescent patients. Consistent with our finding, a previous study reported a sensitivity of 80%, a specificity of 76%, and an AUC of 0.86 for the CDT in acute-phase stroke patients, using a 0–13 point scoring system with PSCI diagnosis based on neurologists’ clinical judgment [30]. Similarly, the Nor-COAST study, which assessed cognitive function three months post-stroke, found the CDT to have AUC of 0.68 and 0.73 in patients with mild and severe PSCI, respectively, indicating insufficient diagnostic accuracy for post-stroke neurocognitive disorders [31]. The best diagnostic performance of the CDT among individual tests may stem from its assessment of multiple cognitive domains, including not only visuospatial function but also memory and attention, suggesting that comprehensive assessment of visual-executive function with more complex items is its key advantages [32]. In addition, the relatively lower effectiveness of the CCT and PCT in detecting PSCI compared to the CDT may partly relate to their binary scoring system. Caffarra et al. [33] introduced a 13-point PCT scoring method with five evaluation criteria (e.g., number of angles, inter-character spacing/intersection, contour closure/openness, etc.), which showed good discrimination between Lewy body dementia and Alzheimer’s disease. Similarly, Mathew et al. [34] proposed a 28-point CCT scoring system that evaluates angles, parallel lines, and surface quality. This system was found to be sensitive in detecting subtle deficits in task execution and demonstrated strong correlations with both the composite and individual scores of the Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (ACE), thereby positioning it as a recommended rapid screening tool for dementia [34]. Shino et al. [35] also validated the efficacy of the CCT in rapidly assessing visuospatial function in patients with Alzheimer’s disease by assessing connecting points and surface drawing errors. More nuanced scoring systems, which quantify structural attributes (e.g., angle count, spatial layout) and execution details (e.g., line parallelism, surface quality) of the drawing tasks – may improve the sensitivity of detecting subtle cognitive deficits that could be overlooked by simplified scoring methods. While these scoring systems offer diagnostic advantages, we opted for the CCT and PCT scoring systems from the MMSE and MoCA due to their simplicity and lack of requirement for specialized training, which minimizes inter-rater variability among neurologists and enhances their applicability in diverse clinical settings.

Regardless of whether in the acute or the convalescent phase, we found that combinations of drawing tests outperformed single drawing tests, suggesting that no single drawing test is sufficient for PSCI screening and combining drawing tests could be a more effective evaluation strategy. A previous study evaluated the sensitivity, specificity, and time efficiency of common MCI screening tools and recommended using combinations of screening tools (e.g., the AD8 and Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly [IQCODE] combination, and the DemTect and MoCA combination) to improve accuracy in initial MCI screening [36]. Similarly, another study compared the sensitivity of the fox finger imitation test, PCT, CCT, and CDT for dementia screening and confirmed that the CCT combined with CDT showed a higher sensitivity than the CCT alone [37]. The better diagnostic performance of combined drawing tests can be attributed to the specific cognitive domains that individual drawing tests focus on. For example, the CDT primarily evaluates spatial perception and executive function, while the CCT is more focused on three-dimensional structural understanding [7, 13]. By combining these tests, a broader spectrum of cognitive function can be assessed, thereby enhancing the accuracy of identifying potential cognitive deficits. Additionally, combining tests helps mitigate the limitations of individual assessments, such as varying levels of sensitivity and specificity, ultimately enhancing overall diagnostic efficacy.

In our study, the PCT was included in the optimal test combinations in the acute phase but not in the convalescent phase. This discrepancy may be explained by the differing pathophysiological and cognitive profiles between these two phases. Ischemic stroke often leads to severe brain and neuronal damage shortly after onset, including brain tissue injury, structural damage, neuronal death and deficits [38]. These acute pathologies result in more pronounced cognitive dysfunction [39], which is more easily detected through drawing tests. During convalescence, however, improvements in cerebral blood flow, neuroplasticity, and rehabilitation treatments all mitigate visuospatial deficits [40, 41]. This transition in cognitive profile suggests that while the simplicity of the PCT is advantageous for detecting PSCI in the acute phase, it may become a limitation in the convalescent phase when cognitive deficits are more subtle and less pronounced after receiving systemic treatment and neurorehabilitation [40, 41]. Taken together, these phase-specific differences in optimal test combinations may reflect the evolving cognitive profile after stroke. In the acute phase, broader drawing-based assessment may be more sensitive to PSCI because deficits in attention, executive function, and visuospatial processing are more pronounced. During convalescence, partial neurological recovery and rehabilitation-related improvement may alter the expression of these deficits and thereby influence the relative performance of different screening combinations. These phase-related differences may also have implications for longer-term functional and cognitive prognosis after stroke, as early identification of PSCI may support timely rehabilitation planning and follow-up cognitive monitoring. Furthermore, our findings showed that the diagnostic performance of PCT (part of the MMSE) was inferior to the CCT + CDT combination (part of the MoCA) in both acute and convalescent phases. This aligns with prior research comparing the MMSE and MoCA in assessing visual-executive function among patients with stroke and transient ischemic attack [18]. This may be attributed to the three-dimensional nature of CCT which requires more visual executive effort compared to the two-dimensional PCT [42].

Education has been well-documented to affect cognitive function [27]. and performance of cognitive tests such as the MMSE and MoCA [9, 43, 44]. Drawing tests, nevertheless, provide distinct advantages through their de-linguistic design, which minimizes interference from language and cultural background, thus reducing educational bias [45, 46] and making them more suitable for patients with limited education [47]. This was supported by our findings of better test performance of the full test battery in acute patients with primary/secondary school education compared to their counterparts with university or higher education. This pattern may be attributed to the reduced cognitive reserve associated with lower education 48, 49]. In the acute phase, the direct and pronounced cognitive damage caused by stroke [26], combined with reduced cognitive reserve, likely makes cognitive impairment more obvious and detectable in patients with lower education. However, during convalescence, the diagnostic accuracy of the CCT + CDT combination was highest in those with secondary education, followed by those with primary education, and lowest in those with university or higher education. This discrepancy may be explained by the interplay between cognitive recovery and cognitive reserve compensation mechanisms. As cognitive function partially recovers during convalescence [40], patients with higher education levels have higher cognitive reserve compensation mechanisms, which may mask minor cognitive impairment, making it difficulty in detecting these impairments using drawing tests [48, 49]. This aligns with prior research. For example, Stern et al. [50] demonstrated that patients with cognitive reserve are able to tolerate more severe brain pathology and thus exhibit better cognitive functioning after stroke. Additionally, Barulli et al. [51] suggested that individuals with higher cognitive reserve may adopt more efficient cognitive processing strategies and display enhanced neural plasticity. Consequently, when stroke occurs, these individuals can mobilize intact alternative neural networks to maintain relatively preserved cognitive performance [52]. Patients with secondary school education, who possess moderate cognitive reserve, may exhibit cognitive impairment that is neither as evident as in acute-phase stroke patients with primary education nor as masked by compensatory mechanisms as in patients with higher education. This balance of factors may render cognitive impairment in convalescent-phase stroke patients with secondary education more detectable by drawing tests, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy in this subgroup.

Our subgroup analysis revealed no sex differences in the diagnostic performance of drawing tests in both acute and convalescent phases, consistent with previous studies using the CDT [46, 53] and the CCT [34]. A previous large multicenter study of patients with PSCI reported that females exhibited more impairment in attention, executive function and language, and male showed more impairment in verbal memory, with no sex differences in visual function [54]. Although prior studies have indicated that age negatively impacts the diagnostic performance of the CDT and CCT [45, 46, 55, 56], our study did not observe a similar pattern. One possible explanation is that age-related compensation mechanisms may partly offset declines in visuospatial task performance in some older adults. For example, older adults may compensate for reduced function in brain regions associated with visuospatial processing (e.g., parietal or occipital lobes) by enhancing activity in other brain regions (e.g., prefrontal cortex) [57]. In addition, compared with younger people who may rely more on unilateral brain regions when performing visuospatial tasks, older adults may activate more bilateral brain regions, which could help offset age-related neural decline through plastic recombination of neurocognitive networks [58–60]. However, this interpretation remains speculative, as our subgroup analyses were exploratory and the study was not specifically powered to examine age-related differences in diagnostic accuracy. This issue warrants further investigation in larger studies specifically designed for this purpose.

This study has several strengths and limitations. Drawing tests are brief and time-efficient (typically 2–5 min), making them ideal for rapid screening for PSCI at bedside or in outpatient clinics [61]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the CCT, CDT, PCT, and their combinations for PSCI screening. Our study included both acute- and convalescent-phase stroke patients from China, a population that has historically received less education compared to their Western counterparts. This allowed us to assess the test performance of drawing tests across different post-stroke phases, identify which combination of tests yielded best results, and determine whether their test performance increased with decreasing educational attainment. However, our study was a single-center, cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study with participants recruited from a tertiary hospital, which is subject to potential selection bias and may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings, such as primary hospitals or community-based populations. The cross-sectional nature of the study precluded assessment of temporal changes in cognitive function and did not allow determination of the predictive value of drawing-based tests for long-term cognitive outcomes or post-stroke recovery trajectories. In addition, as recruitment was conducted over multiple years, unmeasured secular changes in stroke care, rehabilitation availability, and clinical practice may have influenced the results. The use of the MoCA as the reference standard of PSCI may lead to misclassification of cognitive status, which could compromise the validity of our findings. In addition, the use of the MoCA as the reference standard may have introduced incorporation bias, because the CCT and CDT are embedded within the MoCA itself. This could have artificially inflated the observed diagnostic performance of these tests and their combinations. Future studies should therefore validate drawing-based screening tools against an independent comprehensive neuropsychological assessment battery. Additionally, post-stroke aphasia may compromise participants’ ability to comprehend task instructions, while motor deficits can impede the accurate execution of drawing-based assessments – both of which may lead to an underestimation of actual cognitive capacity and, in turn, an overestimation of cognitive impairment. However, given that our study did not include patients with severe aphasia or motor deficits, the impacts of these conditions on the validity of our test results are expected to be limited. Finally, subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution. These analyses were exploratory and were not specifically powered for all strata. Some subgroups, particularly women with university-level or higher education, had relatively small sample sizes, which may have resulted in unstable estimates and wide confidence intervals. In addition, because multiple test combinations, phase-specific analyses, and subgroup comparisons were performed, there is a risk of inflated type I error. Accordingly, these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation in larger, multicenter, and preferably longitudinal studies with pre-specified analytic strategies and more comprehensive cognitive assessments. To address these aforementioned limitations, future research is needed to validate the diagnostic accuracy of drawing tests and their optimal combinations in a larger, more diverse cohorts of stroke patients, using longitudinal study designs and comprehensive neuropsychological assessment batteries.

Conclusion

Combined drawing tests demonstrate good diagnostic accuracy in ischemic stroke patients, both in the acute phase and during convalescence. The de-linguistic design of drawing tests minimized the educational bias in PSCI detection, and this was more pronounced in acute patients. The CCT + CDT+PCT combination is recommended for bedside rapid PSCI screening in low-education patients during the acute phase of ischemic stroke, while the CCT + CDT combination suffices for convalescent patients. Our results highlight the clinical utility of combining drawing tests as practical tools for rapid PSCI screening in stroke patients with lower education.
