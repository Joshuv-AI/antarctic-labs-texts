Identification of ferroptosis hub gene cDKN1A relating to immune microenvironment in rheumatoid arthritis via bioinformatic analysis and experimental verification
Authors: Fuling Chen, Fuqiang Tan, Wen Zou, Jingkun Liu, Zhengxue Quan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease in which ferroptosis appears to be implicated. This study aimed to identify ferroptosis-related therapeutic targets for RA via a series of bioinformatic analyses and experimental verification.Materials and methodsIn the present study, the GSE12021 and GSE55235 datasets were enrolled for subsequent analysis. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) affects approximately 1% of the world's population [1]. RA is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease characterized by persistent synovial hyperplasia and the progressive destruction of articular cartilage and bone, which can lead to joint insufficiency, disability, and increased mortality. The exact pathophysiological mechanism of RA is remains incompletely understood; however, synovial injury, including synovial hyperplasia and inflammation might be involved. Furthermore, mesenchymal-derived fibroblast-like synoviocytes and resident macrophages in the synovial tissue play important roles in the pathogenesis of RA [2, 3].

Although disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs have significantly improved outcomes for patients with RA, approximately 40% of patients do not respond adequately to these therapies. Persistent symptoms include pain, and progressive loss of daily activities, not only increases the financial burden on RA patients but also exposes them to potential therapeutic side effects [4]. Therefore, it is crucial to further elucidate the pathophysiological mechanism of RA and identify novel biomarkers for targeted therapy.

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death characterized by abnormal mitochondrial morphology, and it occurs primarily through lipid peroxidation and cellular dysfunction caused by an excessive intracellular iron accumulation. The occurrence and progression of degenerative diseases and tumors are closely associated with ferroptosis [5]. Recently, multiple studies have demonstrated that ferroptosis plays a key regulatory role in autoimmune and inflammatory diseases [6]. Modulation of the inflammatory immune response represents a novel strategy to target ferroptosis in inflammatory arthritis, which has been verified to reduce glutathione peroxide levels in persistent RA-derived polymorphonuclear leukocytes. However, the potential role and underlying mechanisms of ferroptosis in RA remain to be fully elucidated.

This study aimed to investigate the role of ferroptosis in the disease course and to identify ferroptosis-related biomarkers associated with RA through bioinformatics and experimental verifications. Two RA microarray datasets from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) were utilized, and differential expression analysis was performed between RA and healthy controls. Machine learning techniques were applied to identify diagnostic biomarkers for RA. Correlation analysis between the identified biomarkers and infiltrating immune cells was conducted, followed by molecular typing. In vitro experiments, overexpression or knockdown of key biomarkers was performed to clarify their regulatory relationship with ferroptosis in synovial cells and their effect on infiltrating immune cells.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and differentially expressed gene screening

Human synovial tissue gene expression profiling data were obtained from two datasets in the GEO database: GSE12021 [7] (including 13 normal synovial membrane samples and 24 rheumatoid arthritis synovial membrane samples) and GSE55235 [8] (including 10 healthy joint synovial tissue samples and 10 rheumatoid arthritis joint synovial tissue samples) [9]. Detailed sample information is presented in Table S1. To minimize technical bias arising from different experimental batches, platforms, or processing times between the GSE12021 and GSE55235 datasets, batch effect correction was performed using the "ComBat" function implemented in the R "sva" package (version 3.42.0) [10]. Prior to correction, expression matrices were normalized and no missing values were present. The effectiveness of the batch effect removal was confirmed by observing the convergence of expression distributions and the consistency of biological signals in downstream differential expression analysis and WGCNA. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between RA and normal synovial tissues were screened using the "limma" package, with the threshold set at |logFC|> 1 and adj.P.Val < 0.05. To balance statistical stringency with biological discovery, a dual-threshold strategy for gene screening was adopted. For the initial genome-wide differential expression analysis comparing RA and control synovial tissues, we applied a stringent Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment (adj.P.Val < 0.05) to minimize false positives across the vast transcriptomic landscape. However, for the identification of ferroptosis-related DEGs, the DEG list was intersected with a curated set of 259 ferroptosis regulator genes from the FerrDb database. Given that this subset is already biologically constrained and functionally validated in existing literature, a nominal p-value threshold (p < 0.05) without multiple testing correction as applied. This approach was designed to avoid excluding potentially critical ferroptosis modulators that might exhibit moderate but biologically meaningful changes in expression. Subsequent machine learning feature selection and experimental validation served as rigorous filters to confirm the true positive signals among these candidates.

Panoramic analysis of ferroptosis genes

Ferroptosis-related genes were obtained from the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb). Disease-associated gene sets were selected from experimentally validated ferroptosis regulators in human pathologies; non-human genes and entries lacking orthogonal evidence from at least two independent studies were excluded. The expression values of 259 ferroptosis gene were obtained and analyzed using the R limma package to identify DEGs with |logFC|> 1 and p < 0.05. Correlations analysis was performed among DEGs, and gene pairs with correlation coefficients > 0.4 were visualized.

Enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation analysis encompasses three categories, including biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [11] is a widely used database that stores information regarding genomes, biological pathways, and diseases. To further investigate the role of ferroptosis-related genes in RA, GO and KEGG analyses of FEDEGs were performed using “ClusterProfiler” package in R software [12].

Identification of hub FRDEGs via machine learning algorithms

To evaluate the influence of ferroptosis-related genes on RA risk, a random forest (RF) model was constructed based on differentially expressed genes in RA [13]. The SVM recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) was utilized to identify key genes, implemented via the e1071 package [14].

WGCNA

A weighted gene association network was constructed using the Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) package in R [15]. Briefly, a sample-clustering dendrogram was generated to detect and remove outlier samples. WGCNA was subsequently performed based on the gene expression profiles of the merged dataset and ferroptosis-related gene sets, and gene clustering modules were finally determined. Correlations between module eigengenes and ferroptosis gene sets were assessed to find highly correlated modules, where higher absolute values indicating greater biological relevance. The relationship between the soft-thresholding power (β) and the scale-free fit index (R2) was systematically analyzed. Folling the standard WGCNA guidelines, β = 8 was selected for the analysis, yielding a scale-free fit index of R2 value of 0.92, which satisfied the criterion of R2 > 0.9 and guaranteed a scale-free topology. Hub genes were defined based on high Module Membership (MM) and Gene Significance (GS), with the following thresholds: MM > 0.7: ensuring the gene is highly connected within the module; and GS > 0.5: ensuring the gene is strongly correlated with the disease phenotype.

Hub gene screening, validation, and enrichment analysis

RA hub genes were identified by combining genes from the RF model, SVM features, and WGCNA modules. Their potential as RA biomarkers was assessed using AUC. A nomogram was constructed using the "rms" package, and its accuracy was evaluated via calibration and clinical impact curves. DCA was performed to assess the nomogram's clinical utility. GSEA was applied to analyze the distribution of gene sets correlated with disease phenotypes. The gene sets of "c2.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt" and "c5.go.v7.4.symbols.gmt" were downloaded from the MSigDB database (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp), and GSEA 4.0.3 software was used for hub gene enrichment analysis.

Infiltration of immune cells and construction of immune characteristic subtypes

Immune cell infiltration levels in the samples were evaluated using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm, implemented via the "GSVA" R package and based on the gene sets from Bindea et al. The “Limma” package [16] analyzed RA versus normal samples, identifying immune cell differences and correlating hub gene expression with infiltration levels [17]. Samples were clustered by differential immune cell expressions in RA using "ConsensusClusterPlus" package. They were grouped based on immune cell content, with parameter settings repeated 50 times at 80% resampling rate, based on |logFC|> 2.5 genes (FDR < 0.05). Correlations of hub genes with immune subtypes were analyzed.

Construction of RA molecular subtypes

Consensus clustering analysis was performed using the "ConsensusClusterPlus" package in R, based on genes significantly different (logFC > 2.5, p < 0.05) between RA and normal tissues. RA samples were classified into different groups based on RCD. The parameters were set to repeat 50 times (reps = 50), with a resampling ratio of 80% (pItem = 0.8). The expression levels of hub genes were verified in the constructed molecular subtypes of RA. Finally, Sankey diagrams were generated to observe the correlations among the immune signature subtype groupings, RA molecular subtypes, and disease groups.

Cell lines

The RA cell line MH7A cells (arthritis fibroblasts) were purchased from ATCC and served as a vitro model of rheumatoid arthritis. Primary human synoviocytes derived from patients without RA (Control) were obtained from Procell Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). RAW264.7 macrophages were purchased from the cell bank of the Shanghai Branch of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C in complete culture medium (DMEM, Giboco, Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibico, Invitrogen, USA). Cells were treated with the medium containing 10 μM ferrostatin-1 (fer-1, MedChemExpress, HY-100579) for 24 h to inhibit ferroptosis. All experiments were independently repeated three times as biological replicates.

Indirect synovial cells and macrophage co-culture

Following the respective treatments, cells were serum-starved overnight in serum-free medium prior to co-culture. RAW264.7 macrophages were spread onto cell culture inserts (0.4 μm pore size; Corning, Corning, NY) at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells/cm2 and co-cultured in supplemented DMEM for 24 h. Macrophages were then collected for subsequent experiments.

Small interfering transfection

For transfection experiments, 2.0 × 10^5 primary human synoviocytes cells were inoculated into the transfected six-well plate and mixed gently and repeatedly. As per the manufacturer's instructions, 3 μl cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 A (CDKN1A) siRNA and 6 μl Lipofectamine 8000 reagent (Beyotime, China, C0533) were added to each well. Small interfering (si) RNA of CDKN1A (si‐CDKN1A) was purchased from GenScript Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China) with the sequence 5′‐GUCCUGUGUUCCAACUAUAGU‐3′. After 24 h of transfection, cells were collected for subsequent experiments.

Plasmids transfection

The overexpression vector (pcDNA3.1) containing CDKN1A was obtained from Tsingke Biotech Co (Beijing, China). For CDKN1A overexpression, MH7A cells with 60%–70% confluency were transfected with the constructed vector using Lipofectamine 8000 reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h of transfection, cells were collected for subsequent experiments.

Inhibitor application and control settings

All pharmacological inhibitors were dissolved in ultra-pure dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare 10 mM stock solutions, which were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C protected from light. Prior to cell treatment, stock solutions were thawed on ice and diluted in pre-warmed serum-free culture medium to the desired working concentrations. The final DMSO concentration was rigorously controlled at ≤ 0.1% across all experimental groups, a level validated in pilot assays to induce no detectable cytotoxicity or off-target effects.

Assessment of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Cells were seeded in six-well plates and treated with different reagents. First, we stain the nucleus with Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, China) for 1 h, and then cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (Beyotime, China) fluorescence probe according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were first fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 12 h, followed by a secondary fixation step using 1% osmium tetroxide at room temperature for 2 h. The samples then subjected to dehydration, infiltration, and embedding. Ultrathin Sects. (70–90 nm) were cut using an ultramicrotome (EM UC7, LEICA) and subsequently stained with 1% uranyl acetate and 0.1% lead citrate to enhance contrast. Finally, samples were analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1200EX, Japan).

Flow cytometry

After induction, macrophages were stained with anti-CD206 and anti-F4/80 antibodies (BioLegend, USA). The results were analyzed by using FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

The cell slides were initially fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min. After washing, the slides were blocked with 2% goat serum for 30 min. Primary antibodies, CDKN1A (Proteintech, 10,355–1-AP; diluted 1:100) and GPX4 (Abcam, ab125066; 1:100 dilution), were applied to each slide and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The slides were subsequently incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope and subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

Synovial tissue specimens were obtained from patients with a confirmed diagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis undergoing total knee arthroplasty at our institution. Control specimens were obtained from patients with meniscal injury. Each group comprised three biological replicates. Tissues were embedded in paraffin, cut into 4 μm sections, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed using a primary antibody against anti-CDKN1A (Proteintech, 10,355–1-AP) or anti-GPX4 (Abcam, ab125066). The staining intensity of IHC results was quantified using ImageJ software.

Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted from cells or tissues using RIPA buffer with PMSF and quantified via BCA assay. All samples were electrophoresed on the same gel under identical processing and imaging conditions. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After membrane transfer, the membrane were blocked with 5% milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies (anti-CDKN1A, Proteintech, 10,355–1-AP, 1:2,000; GPX4; GAPDH, Abcam, ab9485, 1:1,000). Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibody (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L, Abcam, ab6721, 1:5,000) for 60 min.

Glutathione (GSH) and malondialdehyde (MDA)

Cellular and tissue MDA concentration were measured using Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit (Beyotime, China, S0131S) by detecting the absorbance at 532 nm. GSH and glutathione disulfide (GSSG) concentration were measured using GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (Beyotime, China, S0053), and GSH was assessed by detecting the absorbance at 412 nm.

Ferrous ion (Fe2+)

Intracellular Fe2⁺ levels were detected using the FerroOrange fluorescent probe (DOJINDO, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence images were acquired under a microscope, and the fluorescence intensity was analyzed and quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis in this study were primarily performed using R (version 4.2.1). All data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t-tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2, San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of ferroptosis-related differential expressed genes (FRDEGs)

To investigate the effects of gene expression values on RA patients, differential gene expression analysis was performed using the “limma” package (Supplementary Fig. S1A), identifying 695 DEGs, of which 417 were upregulated and 278 were downregulated (Supplementary Fig. S2A). To further investigate the potential roles of ferroptosis-related genes in RA, differential expression analysis was conducted, and 24 FRDEGs were screened out (Supplementary Fig. S2B). The heat map displayed the expression levels of each gene (Supplementary Fig. S1B), and the correlation among these FRDEGs is displayed in Supplementary Fig. S2C. GO analysis indicated that these FRDEGs were mainly enriched in response to oxidative stress, ferrous iron binding, and regulation of cytokine biosynthetic process (Supplementary Fig. S2D). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the enriched pathways mainly included rheumatoid arthritis, IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. S2E). Supplementary Fig. S2F illustrates the rheumatoid arthritis signaling pathways.

Screening for Hub Genes by Machine Learning Algorithm and WGCNA

A prediction model of FRDEGs was established using RF (Fig. 1A and B). The Gini coefficient was applied to select the major risk genes, and two genes with Gini coefficients > 2 were identified. A total of 13 hub FRDEGs were identified (Fig. 1C). To further identify the genes associated with RA, WGCNA was performed on the expression profiles of the combined GEO dataset. A total of 11 modules were first obtained by average hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree pruning (Fig. 1D). Except for status, “marker” exhibited the highest negative correlation with the tan module (Fig. 1E). In this module, 51 genes with GS > 0.5 and MM > 0.7 were considered as the main module genes. Hub gene verification and functional regulation

The ROC curve (Fig. 1G) demonstrated that CDKN1A possessed a favorable diagnostic value in RA (AUC = 0.880). Based on the expression levels of CDKN1A, a nomogram model was constructed (Fig. 1H), and the calibration curve (Fig. 1I) indicated that the model possessed high predictive accuracy. The clinical impact curve showed that the nomogram model had robust clinical predictive performance (Fig. 1J). DCA further validated that favorable net clinical benefit provided the nomogram model (Fig. 1K). The results collectively indicate that CDKN1A is a hub gene in RA. GSEA revealed that the CDKN1A low expression group was mainly enriched in response to endoplasmic reticulum stress and ferrous iron binding (Supplementary Fig. S1C). KEGG results indicated that the group with low CDKN1A expression was mainly enriched in the MAPK signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. S1D and Supplementary Table S2).

Immune cell infiltration

Comparison of immune infiltration between the normal and RA groups revealed that activated B cells, activated CD4 T cells, activated CD8 T cells, activated dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, monocytes, and natural killer cells were significantly overexpressed in the RA group (Supplementary Fig. S3A). We analyzed the correlation between CDKN1A and the immune microenvironment and found that CDKN1A had a significant negative correlation with the degree of infiltration of immune cells, such as macrophages (Supplementary Fig. S3B-M). A consensus clustering approach was employed to classify RA patients into different immune subtypes: when k = 2, the matrix heat maps (Supplementary Fig. S3N) were clearly separated, and the slope of the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve (Supplementary Fig. S3O) was minimal. Accordingly, RA patients were stratified into two subtypes with distinct immune signatures (Supplementary Fig. S3P and Q). CDKN1A was significantly overexpressed in the “cluster1” group (Supplementary Fig. S3R). Consensus clustering was further performed for genes significantly downregulated in RA (Supplementary Fig. S4). All samples were classified into two RA molecular subtypes. CDKN1A was significantly overexpressed in the cluster A group, and the immune signature subtypes were consistent with the RA molecular subtype groups.

WB results showed that si-RNA downregulated the protein expression of CDKN1A, and fer-1 alleviated the downregulation of GPX4 induced by low CDKN1A expression (Fig. 4A and B). Consistent results were obtained by IF (Fig. 4C). To validate ferroptosis, Fe2+, TEM, GSH, and MDA assays were assessed. CDKN1A knockdown increased intracellular Fe2⁺ levels (Fig. 4D, E), induced typical ferroptosis-associated mitochondrial morphological changes (Fig. 4F), reduced GSH content (Fig. 4G), and promoted MDA accumulation (Fig. 4H). Macrophage polarization triggered by CDKN1A-related ferroptosis

A synovial cell- RAW264.7 co-culture system was established to further validate the impact of the CDKN1A-related ferroptosis of synovial cells on macrophage infiltration by detecting the polarization of macrophages. The result showed that macrophages in the si-CDKN1A cells exhibited a high level of M1 polarization, which was partially alleviated by fer-1 (Fig. 6A-C). Discussion

Recent studies have confirmed that ferroptosis of synoviocytes plays an important role in the pathogenesis of RA. Elevated iron levels and increased expression of ferroptosis drivers, including TFR1 and NCOA4, are observed in inflammatory synoviocytes, accompanied by altered expression of SLC7A11, GPX4, and NRF2. Inflammation plays an important role in RA, and ferroptosis is implicated in the onset and progression of RA through pro-inflammatory effects [18]. However, the specific signaling pathways and mechanisms by which ferroptosis in synoviocytes contributes to RA remains to be fully elucidated. In this study, integrated bioinformatics analysis and experimental verification study were performed to identify ferroptosis-related hub genes and potential biologic therapeutic targets for RA.

Bioinformatics analysis verified the presence of FRDEGs in synovial tissues derived from RA, and GO enrichment analysis further indicated that oxidative stress is associated with ferroptosis in RA. Peng et al. identified numerous differentially expressed genes in the synovial membrane of RA [19]. It is reported that oxidative stress is a key feature of RA development and progression, and a ferroptosis-mediated TNF-α/ROS positive feedback loop is involved [20]. KEGG analysis revealed the HIF-1 and IL-17 signaling pathways are involved in RA ferroptosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that severely disordered microvascular structures in RA synovial tissues cause local oxygen deficiency and the formation of a hypoxic microenvironment, which modulates ferroptosis in an HIF-1α-dependent manner. HIF-1α, a core transcriptional factor in hypoxic responses, has been demonstrated to upregulate the expression of iron uptake-related genes (e.g., TFRC) [21]. Notably, CDKN1A can inhibit HIF-1α transcriptional activity, thereby reducing the expression of its target genes [22].

Under inflammation conditions, hypoxia can lead to abnormal synovial-associated cell metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction [23]. IL-17 (TH17), derived from type 17 helper T lymphocytes, is present in inflammatory arthritic lesions such as RA, and IL-17 blockade is considered as a potential therapeutic strategy for RA [24]. IL-17, a key inflammatory cytokine secreted by Th17 cells, promotes synovial inflammation in RA through the activation of NF-κB and STAT3 pathways [25]. Recent studies have demonstrated that IL-17 inhibits SLC7A11expression, impairs glutathione (GSH) synthesis capacity, and there by increase cellular ferroptosis susceptibility [26]. The enrichment of IL-17 pathway observed in this study indicates CDKN1A may indirectly modulate SLC7A11activity through negative regulation of IL-17 downstream effectors. A study by Spurlock et al. [27] demonstrated that CDKN1A expression was significantly lower in RA patients. Our results suggested that the ferroptosis marker gene GPX4 was significantly downregulated in synovial tissues derived from RA, confirming the presence of ferroptosis in synovial tissues.

CDKN1A has been reported to delay ferroptosis onset under cystine deprivation conditions [28]. However, the relationship between CDKN1A and the ferroptosis of synovial cells remains unclear. In this study, we found that ferroptosis was significantly enhanced after CDKN1A knockdown in normal synovial cells, whereas it was inhibited after CDKN1A overexpression in MH7A cells. These results suggest that CDKN1A is involved in the regulation of ferroptosis, although further mechanistic studies are warranted to establish a direct causal relationship. The association between CDKN1A downregulation and ferroptosis activation may involve multiple signaling pathways. Previous studies have suggested that CDKN1A can enhance antioxidant defense by regulating NRF2 [29]. In RA synovial cells, decreased CDKN1A expression may impair NRF2 nuclear translocation, thereby reducing expression of its target genes SLC7A11 and GPX4 [30]. Future studies will systematically dissect CDKN1A's core regulatory network through targeted interventions at these pathway nodes.

DC-T cell interactions promote autoimmune responses in RA [31]. In this study, we found that CDKN1A was closely associated with a variety of immune cells in RA tissues, including macrophages. Macrophages are central to the pathophysiology of RA, and RA patients exhibit increased numbers of activated M1 polarized pro-inflammatory macrophages [32]. We further found that ferroptosis in synovial cells induced by low CDKN1A expression is implicated in macrophage M1 polarization. Moreover, CDKN1A may regulate T cell metabolic fitness and function. Free nucleic acids and HMGB1 released by ferroptotic cells in synovium can activate immature DCs through TLR9/RAGE receptors, thereby enhancing antigen-presenting capacity and stimulating secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [33].

RA synovial cells secrete IL-6 to induce inflammatory infiltration of neighboring cells, and promote endothelial cells to produce MMPs, thereby stimulating cartilage degradation [34]. Studies have confirmed that CDKN1A overexpression can inhibit the invasion and migration of RA synovial cells, as well as the expression of TNF-α and IL-6 [35]. This may be an important process through which CDKN1A-related ferroptosis promotes RA progression; however, the underlying mechanism requires further investigation. While this study focused on ferroptosis-specific genes from FerrDb, the inflammatory context of RA necessitates consideration of immune-related signatures. Future studies employing cell-type-specific sequencing could further dissect direct ferroptosis drivers independent of immune infiltration.

Several limitations exist in the present research. First, the mechanisms underlying the CDKN1A downregulation in RA tissues, as well as the specific molecular mechanisms by which it regulates ferroptosis, remains to be elucidated. Second, the precise mechanisms of immune responses triggered by CDKN1A downregulation in RA tissues required further investigation. Third, this study lacks in vivo experiments to verify the expression of CDKN1A and its regulatory relationship with ferroptosis, such as the establishment of a rat model of RA. Furthermore, standard vehicle/negative controls can validate treatment-specific effects, future studies using iron chelators or ferroptosis inhibitors as positive controls could further strengthen mechanistic claims. Fourth, immune-related research is restricted to macrophages only. Novel regulatory roles of CDKN1A in RA have been demonstrated in this work, whereas multiple limitations should be addressed. Regarding the immune microenvironment analysis, single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was utilized based on well-established immune signatures. It is important to note that these gene sets were originally derived from tumor immunology studies. Although the fundamental polarization states of macrophages (M1/M2) are conserved across inflammatory diseases such as RA, the application of these signatures to bulk RNA-seq data from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) requires careful interpretation. We acknowledge that computational deconvolution algorithms, including ssGSEA, cannot precisely quantify absolute cell counts or spatial localization within tissues. Furthermore, alternative deconvolution methods (e.g., CIBERSORT) were not employed for cross-validation, nor was immunohistochemistry (IHC) on synovial tissues to validate macrophage abundance. The focus of this study remained on linking the molecular mechanism of CDKN1A-mediated ferroptosis to broad immune pathways via bioinformatics, and validated with in vitrofunctional assays. Future studies incorporating multiplex IHC and single-cell sequencing are necessary to delineate the precise spatial distribution and phenotypic heterogeneity of immune cells in the RA synovium.

Conclusion

Low CDKN1A expression in synovial cells induces ferroptosis, which in turn mediates macrophage activation, suggesting that CDKN1A represents a novel potential molecular marker for RA immunotherapy.
