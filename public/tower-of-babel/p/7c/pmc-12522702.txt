Tube-bearing experience of patients with biliary and pancreatic diseases outside the hospital: based on qualitative study
Authors: Jingli Liu, Xiaoping Ye, Shuling Chang, Ziyu Wu, Bo Yuan, Qianqian Wang, Yanrong Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to understand the experience of patients with external drainage tube out of hospital with biliary and pancreatic system diseases and provide a theoretical basis and reference for formulating nursing intervention measures based on the actual experience of patients with ducts.MethodsThis is a qualitative study, semi-structured in-depth interviews were used to gather information. Purposive Sampling method was used to select patients who treated in the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery and the Pipeline Nursing Outpatient Department of a triple A hospital in Yinchuan City, Ning Xia province, China, and had external drainage tubes in place outside the hospital for more than one month as research objects. Semi-structured interviews were conducted under the guidance of phenomenological research methods, and 17 participants were finally included. This study was reviewed by the Research Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (Ethics number: KYLL-2022–1341). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, all subjects voluntarily participated in this study.ResultsFour themes and ten sub-themes were extracted, including negative coping and reduced quality of life due to changes in life and work status (difficulty in role transformation: patient role intensification, negative coping with work and social life, gastrointestinal function is impaired, limited daily dietary choices, and reduced quality of life), physical and mental stress and negative experience (psychological coping burden, physical discomfort and negative experience), disease uncertainty and negative attitudes caused by lack of knowledge (lack of knowledge disease-related knowledge leads to a sense of uncertainty about recovery from the disease, negative attitude towards pipe care, lack of knowledge and skills); and seeking for medical support positively (get a suitable drainage device, perfect fixing tape and dressing, improve the status of the subsequent visit).ConclusionThe patients’ experience of patients with drainage tube outside of hospital for hepatobiliary and pancreatic system diseases is primarily discomfort and negative experience. There are some discomfort experiences and a lack of relevant-disease knowledge, and patients are eager to get professional help from the medical staff.

Introduction

With the continuous development of various treatment technologies and progress in minimally invasive diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography and drainage (PTCD), endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) [14, 16], and other minimally invasive endoscopic approaches have become the preferred treatment for biliary and pancreatic diseases [17]. After treatment, it is necessary to place external drainage tubes, including nasobiliary/pancreatic drainage tubes, biliary/gallbladder puncture drainage tubes, and peripancreatic puncture drainage tubes to alleviate obstruction of the biliary and pancreatic systems, reduce local exudation of organs [36], and achieve adequate drainage and disease treatment. External drainage tubes are often necessary to be placed for two weeks to three months [23]. Patients frequently face out-of-hospital medical decisions with drainage tubes. Although most patients were in stable condition after discharge, their physical function did not fully recover, and they were in a transitional state of recovery.

The incidence of complications related to the out-of-hospital catheter was as high as 17.2%–39.5%, mainly including skin rupture around the puncture point, pipeline blockage, unplanned extubation, extubation or displacement, infection, and other complications [28]. Physical and psychological effects on patients and their families have varying degrees [25], seriously affecting the quality of life. Patient experience refers to the psychological feelings of patients during the medical treatment and nursing process and the experience of the details of the entire process [6]. Relevant studies on patient experience have mainly focused on the postoperative experience of patients with breast cancer [19]and chronic disease self-management experience [1]. Currently, only a few studies have focused on the patients’ experience of patients with peripherally inserted central catheter(PICC) [11, 33] and enterostomy out-of-hospital [2, 17]. The findings revealed that patients often have a decline in quality of life, restrictions on social activities, changes in body image [9], and even adverse events such as infection and tube blockage [22]. However, there are few reports on the tube experience of patients with external drainage tubes outside the hospital.

Consequently, the research questions is understand the experiences of patients with external drainage tube outside hospital with biliary and pancreatic system diseases through qualitative research, to provide a theoretical basis and reference for formulating nursing intervention measures based on the actual experiences of patients with ducts.

Methods

Study design

This study is a qualitative study, interview method. Semi-structured in-depth interviews were used to gather information, a method widely used to understand the patients’ experience [18, 21]. The specific implementation process of qualitative research interview is shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Qualitative research interview implementation flow chart

Qualitative research interview implementation flow chart

Participant

The Purposive Sampling method [7] was used to select patients as study participants who were admitted to the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery and Pipeline Nursing Outpatient Department of a triple A hospital in Yinchuan City, Ning Xia province, China, from December 2023 to March 2024 and had external drainage tubes in place outside the hospital for more than one month.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: ①patients aged 18–65 years; ②patients with out-of-hospital catheters for > 1 month; ③patients who knew the condition were willing to participate in the interview under informed conditions and had good communication skills.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: ①Patients with malignant tumors who receive drainage tube placement based on palliative care principles; ②Participants who demonstrate significant discomfort or resistance during the interview process; ③Those who completely lack the ability to perform self-care for drainage tubes outside of the hospital; ④Individuals with clearly diagnosed mental or psychological disorders.

This study was reviewed by the Research Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (Ethics number: KYLL-2022–1341). And the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, all participants voluntarily participated in this study.

Researcher

The core research team members are all mid-level or senior nursing professionals with at least 5 years of experience in hepatobiliary and pancreatic disease care. Additionally, we have specialized wound, ostomy, and continence nurses who can provide professional support to ensure patient safety with drainage tubes. The two authors responsible for interview transcription have received internal team training. The corresponding author holds a Master's degree in Nursing (Academic), and the first author is a senior associate chief nurse. Both authors strictly control and guide the overall research implementation process to ensure the study's smooth progress and the reliability and scientific validity of the research findings.

Formulation of the interview outline

The first draft of the interview outline was formed by literature review and analysis. With "nasobiliary drainage tube", "PTCD", "percutaneous hepatobiliary puncture", "T tube", "patient experience", "out-of-hospital catheter", "home catheter" and "catheter experience" as search terms were used to search and retrieve relevant literature in PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure(CNKI), Wanfang Medical Network, and other databases, to summarize the literature, and to form the first draft of the interview outline.

In order to ensure the dependability and confirmability of the qualitative research results, the peer debriefing method was used to discuss and decide the first draft of the interview outline. We selected 3 peer experts to discuss and revise the draft of the interview, including a specialist nurse in pipeline nursing, a chief physician in hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery, and an expert in qualitative research.

And finally formed 5 interview Outlines were as follows: ①What is your first feeling when you known that you need to carry drainage tube for some time after discharged? ②What is the biggest trouble for you with the drainage tube outsied hospital? ③What are your biggest concerns during tube drainaging out of hospital? ④What impact does place drainage tube during the out-of-hospital have on your life and work? ⑤What kind of help do you want to get during drainage after discharge from medical staff?

Data collection

The data were collected through semi-structured interviews and observations. Before the interviews, the researcher explained the purpose, content, and process of the research in detail to the interviewees and ensured that the interview content was only for academic research.

Preparation before interview

Patient preparation

Interviews were conducted with patients in the Pipeline Nursing Outpatient Department of the a triple A hospital in Yinchuan after the appointment with consent.

Goods preparation

Voice recorder, one notebook (to record body language, such as expression, tone, and mood change), one pen, one general interview data sheet, and some interview record charts.

Environmental preparation

This should be carried out in a place with sufficient light and a quiet environment, and the time is generally selected after the patient's treatment finished.

Conducting interviews

The interview site of this study was a corridor next to the Pipeline Nursing Outpatient Department of a triple A hospital in Yinchuan City, Ning Xia province, China. About interview duration time, ensure sufficient immersion and prolonged engagement in the concept, we allocated the interview time to patients was among 20 ~ 40 min. According to the opening time of the pipeline care clinic, the interview time was determined to be every Tuesday morning.

The interview skills such as listening, summarizing, induction, verification were used and repetition encourage patients to express their true feelings and opinions during interview process. Concurrently, it is necessary to take interview notes and record the non-body language, such as complexion, micro-expression, intonation changes, and mood fluctuations and so on. Appropriate follow-up questions were asked based on the patient's answers to obtain more detailed and comprehensive information during the interviews. Should be avoid seductive questions and listen patiently during the interviews.

If patients occur emotional agitation or discomfort during the interview suddenly, it would be suspended or ended immediately. According to the principle of saturation in qualitative research data collection (when interview data repeatedly appears and no new themes emerge from the analysis [37], 17 patients were interviewed in this study finally.

Analysis

The quantitative data of general patient information, such as age, gender, and catheterization duration, were analyzed using SPSS 27.0. Categorical data were described using rates and percentages, while continuous data were described using means and standard deviations.

The qualitative data analysis was conducted using NVivo 12 software in strict accordance with Claizzi's seven-step analysis method. The audio recordings were transcribed into text within 24 h after each interview, and the information conveyed through the interviewees' body language during the interview process was supplemented to form the text. NVivo 12 software (issued by QSR International in Australia) was used for data management and analysis. The interview data was analyzed using Claizzi's seven-step analysis method [20], with the following specific steps: ①Carefully read all the audio recording materials,②Identify significant parts; ③Code the meaningful viewpoints that appear repeatedly;④Collect the coded viewpoints;⑤Conduct detailed and comprehensive descriptions;⑥Identify similar viewpoints and develop thematic concepts;⑦Return to the interviewees to verify the data.Cohen's Kappa coefficient and percentage agreement were used to assess inter-researcher reliability. When discrepancies arose during the coding process, a consensus-building method was employed, with two researchers and the research team discussing together to ensure that the final coding accurately reflects the data.

The interview data were processed anonymously, and the names of the interviewees were replaced with numbers from P1 to P17. The research read these records individually and repeatedly to grasp each patient’s experience and overall understanding, and to identify and extract important units. The data analysis process was completed independently and simultaneously by two researchers and the controversial contents were discussed with the members of the research group and reached a consensus. Subsequently, we reached a consensus regarding themes, subthemes and detailed descriptions through discussion. Finally, we returned the interpreted data to them for verification.

And The research team roles and responsibilities are outlined below: XP and WQ: Conduct an interview. ZY and YB: transcript of interview results. JL and YY: Analysis of qualitative interview results and quantitative data.

Quality control

Before the interview, the researcher communicated with the patient to ensure the interview participants were familiar with and trusted the researcher. It was necessary to reveal their true feelings during interviews.

And every researcher had received training related to qualitative study, and provided relevant nursing services and health guidance with Pipeline Outpatient Specialist Nurses, in order to build a friendly and trusting relationship with the patients, to ensure the accuracy and authenticity of the interview results.

The researcher did not make any judgments about the interview content of the participants during the interview. In the process of data transcription, if the results were inaccurate due to dialect or other reasons, researchers needed to manually transcribe and record. When there was doubt or uncertain information, confirm the information with the interviewee as necessary.

Theme 1: Negative coping and reduced quality of life due to changes in life and work status

Among the patients, 28.26% had negative social life experiences (26, 28.26%), primarily characterized by the absence of a family role caused by strengthening the patient's role, followed by a change in work and lifestyle.

Sub-theme 1

Difficulty in role transformation: Patient Role Active/Passive Reinforcement.

As patients carried drainage tubes outside the hospital and were in the transition period of recovery from the disease, clear role strengthening appeared at the psychological level, followed by the absence of family roles. For instance:

P6 mentions: "I'm experiencing a decline in my ability to do household chores, to the point where I've become a burden. My family treats me like a seriously ill patient, not allowing me to do anything. I feel like I'm becoming useless.";(Patient Role Active Reinforcement).

These all were Patient Role Passive Reinforcement.

Sub-theme 3

Gastrointestinal function is impaired, limited daily dietary choices, and reduced quality of life.

After illness and carrying drainage tube, patients often experience varying degrees of gastrointestinal function impairment, which manifests as poor digestive ability, poor appetite, and aversion to greasy food. Consequently, they are more inclined to choose a light diet that lacks protein and fat intake, resulting in nutritional deficiencies and losses.

P4: "Dare not eat too greasy, after eating, I feel indigestion, accumulation of food";

P5: "When I smell the lampblack smell, will feel nauseous involuntarily."

P5: "When I smell the lampblack smell, will feel nauseous involuntarily."

Simultaneously, extracorporeal drainage leads to the loss of most potassium and sodium electrolytes and digestive fluid, resulting in a decrease in the digestive function of patients and complications of electrolyte disorders. Therefore, it is recommended that patients with drainage tubes undergo routine biochemical examination every 2 ~ 3 days after discharge. The daily diet should include additional electrolytes. For patients undergoing extracorporeal drainage of pancreatic fluid with significant dyspepsia, additional oral administration of compound pancreatic enzyme tablets is required..

Theme 3: Disease uncertainty and negative attitudes caused by lack of knowledge

There were 18.48% of patients with a lack of relevant knowledge (17, 18.48%), which mainly manifested as a lack of disease-related knowledge, followed by a lack of nursing pipeline knowledge and weak consciousness of tube nursing.

Sub-theme 6

Lack of knowledge disease-related knowledge leads to a sense of uncertainty about recovery from the disease.

Even if doctors provide relevant disease knowledge and health guidance upon discharge, patients have little understanding owing to different educational levels, memory loss or bias over time, and other problems [33]. Furthermore, patients exhibit varying degrees of cognitive differences in disease-related knowledge, resulting in weak compliance. Patients have a significantly increased risk of drainage complications and adverse events, and even drainage fluid develops a white pus coloration accompanied by fever for several days without emergency medical attention.

Theme 4: Seeking for medical support positively

The patients (27, 29.35%) were positive regarding surgical treatment and rehabilitation, eager to recover as soon as possible, and eager to optimize specific problems existing during the canalization process to obtain assistance from medical staff, for instance, obtaining a suitable drainage device, improving the fixed tape and dressing, and improving the return visits.

Discussion

The study findings indicated that the Tube-Bearing Experience of Patients with biliary and pancreatic diseases outside the hospital is primarily dominated by negative experiences. These negative experiences mainly manifest as decreased quality of life due to patients' passive coping strategies and difficulties in transitioning from the patient role to the rehabilitation stage. Additionally, due to patients' lack of disease-related knowledge, they exhibit significant negative psychological experiences regarding disease treatment and prognosis, including anxiety, fear, and uncertainty about their illness. Of course, patients also reported some positive experiences, primarily in actively seeking help from healthcare professionals, including improvements in fixation and drainage devices and enhancements in follow-up visit methods.

Patients of biliary and pancreatic system diseases with tube at out-of-hospital have obvious negative coping and need more family care, support, and social acceptance

The results of this study show that patients with external drainage tubes mostly adopt passive coping styles when facing medical decisions related to their diseases or external drainage tubes. Passive coping experiences occurred 26 times, accounting for 28.26% of all tube-related experiences. The main manifestations include difficulties in patient role transition (both passive and active) (64.7%), passive coping with daily work and social interactions (35.2%), and decreased quality of life (52.9%). This is the primary issue of negative experiences among patients with biliary and pancreatic diseases who have external drainage tubes.

Coping style refers to the cognitive and behavioral patterns individuals adopt when facing stressful events, which are consistent with their personal habits and psychological characteristics [12]. Originating from "stress" theory, coping style is one of the core topics in stress research, also known as coping strategies. It refers to the cognitive and behavioral responses and strategies individuals use when facing setbacks, pressures, and resolving stressful events. Coping styles have regulatory and buffering effects on stressful events and can directly affect individuals' physical and mental health [8].

Negative coping refers to dealing with stress through fear, avoidance, and other emotions, without finding effective problem-solving strategies, allowing situations to develop continuously, and thus leading to a malignant trend in stress events [10]. Numerous studies have confirmed that choosing appropriate coping strategies can enhance patients' ability to seek social support and overcome negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, which contributes to the recovery of mental health [30, 34]. Chen’s study [5]. showed that patients' coping styles are significantly correlated with illness perception and self-management behaviors. Domestic scholars have also shown that when patients tend to adopt positive coping strategies to face diseases, they have stronger disease coping abilities and are more proactive in seeking disease-related knowledge, thus alleviating their negative emotions [35].

Researchers believe that social support refers to assistance provided by organizations or institutions from all sectors of society to individuals [41]. Qian Duo et al.'s research shows that patients' primary social support and care come from their families [24]. Studies have shown that family support can significantly reduce patients' psychological pressure and improve their life satisfaction [32]. By sharing daily tasks and providing financial support, family support positively impacts patients' recovery at multiple levels. Khusaifan's research shows that high levels of social support can enhance individuals' psychological endurance and stress resistance, buffer various pressures and negative impacts such as anxiety and tension brought by stressful events in life, thereby increasing individuals' hope levels and benefiting their mental health [13].

Patients with out-of-hospital ducts for biliary and pancreatic system diseases have obvious physical and mental discomfort experience, and the body coping style should be appropriately adjusted to reduce complications

Postoperative patients with biliary and pancreatic diseases who have indwelling catheters often struggle to adapt to the physical changes, leading to limited physiological functions. This finding is consistent with Zhou Shu's research [40]. Studies have shown [27] that preoperative adaptive nasal occlusion training and respiratory training can enhance patients' adaptability and tolerance. Adjusting breathing patterns, reducing stimulation to restricted areas, and changing body positions can help alleviate discomfort. Long-term catheterization outside the hospital not only limits patients' physical activities but also requires them to be constantly cautious, leading to significant psychological stress. This greatly impacts patients' daily lives, appearance, and comfort levels. Accidental extubation, tube detachment, and internal tube displacement can cause severe trauma to patients, often requiring reoperation. This not only hampers recovery but also exacerbates conditions, potentially leading to death (CAI [3]. Therefore, implementing comfort care measures to improve patients' catheterization comfort and promote physical well-being is crucial.

American nursing scholar Mishel [26] defines disease uncertainty as a psychological state of loss of control that occurs when patients lack relevant information support to assess and cope with their illness, and are unable to properly classify and accurately predict disease outcomes [4]. It falls within the cognitive category. Healthcare providers often focus solely on patient treatment while neglecting effective communication with patients. This lack of clarity regarding disease diagnosis, progression, test results, and prognosis, coupled with unmet psychological care needs, leads to high levels of disease uncertainty and negative emotions such as anxiety in patients [38, 39].

Patients with biliary and pancreatic system diseases lack knowledge about diseases and drainage tube nursing, so education and science popularization outside hospitals should be strengthened to improve their sense of catheter experience

Knowledge reserves are the internal motivations for behavior improvement. Some studies have demonstrated that the coping ability of patients receiving home nasal feeding treatment is closely associated with the quantity and quality of acquired knowledge [29]. Sufficient knowledge is a prerequisite for a smooth transition to tube feeding and everyday life, which has a synergistic effect with the poor tube experience of patients in this study due to a lack of knowledge and skills about diseases and drainage tube nursing [31]. Currently, with the continuous expansion and improvement of the health education model, the self-care experiential health education model is a novel educational model for improving the mastery of relevant nursing skills and the self-care ability of patients through situational experience [39]. Some scholars have applied this model to the health education of patients with closed thoracic drainage tubes, and the results have revealed a significant improvement in the self-care ability and self-efficacy of patients [42]. There was study [23] adopted Internet + extended care for patients with T-tubes outside the hospital, and the results demonstrated that it could significantly improve patients' self-care ability and reduce the complication rate.

For patients with the out-of-hospital duct of biliary and pancreatic system diseases, the subsequent hardware configuration of home treatment needs to be further improved

According to Maslow's hierarchy of needs [15], physiological needs are the most basic needs of the human body, including breathing, food, water, sleep, and physiology. However, this study indicated that the medical decision regarding out-of-hospital tubes has a certain impact on the most basic physiological needs of patients. Several participants mentioned that shower demand during tube wearing could not be guaranteed, and they hoped to have water-resistant fixed devices.

Currently, The 3 M tape (nose tape and I-script tape, Fig. 2) for external fixation of patients carrying drainage tubes, guaranteeing a certain fixation effect within 3 ~ 5 days. In contrast, the average time of patients with out-of-hospital tubes is (37 ± 8.9) days. Due to the high incidence of out-of-hospital catheterization, hospitals can communicate and negotiate with relevant departments, refer to the out-of-hospital management strategy for patients with ostomy, and provide out-of-hospital tube care packages for patients discharged from the hospital with tubes, including anti-reflux screw drainage bags, iodophor disinfection swabs, multiple 3 M fixed adhesive tapes, and videos guiding the replacement of drainage bags at home. Medical personnel can also innovate and improve existing devices in this direction and apply them for related patents.

Recommendations on nursing care and management of patients outside of hospital with tube

Medical staff should strengthen communication with patients and their families, helping patients rebuild correct and positive family and social roles. Starting from family empowerment, taking families as units, and strengthening social support, efforts should be made to promote the transformation of patients' passive coping state to active coping. Guide patients in using correct breathing techniques and swallowing skills for adaptive training to ensure that coughing does not occur during breathing and eating, and to alleviate discomfort in the mouth, nose, and throat. Establish outpatient consultation hotlines or patient WeChat groups, conduct regular phone follow-ups, and hold patient discussion sessions to allow patients to receive guidance at any time, providing accurate information support and healthy lifestyle guidance to reduce unplanned extubation and complications, and promote patient rehabilitation.

Meanwhile, healthcare professionals should pay more attention to providing holistic health education to patients, especially those with indwelling catheters outside the hospital. The education should focus more on disease prognosis and outcomes, followed by relevant health guidance and demonstrations of catheter care. This will improve patients' awareness of disease-related knowledge, reduce negative psychological states caused by disease uncertainty, and enhance psychological resilience and positive coping abilities.

Take responsibility for popularizing relevant knowledge, strengthen communication with patients and their families, and accurately assess patients' mastery of disease knowledge and self-management knowledge. We can adopt multi-form and multi-dimensional health education activities, develop patient and family-led new health education methods, and appropriately carry out self-care experience or real-life experience health education for patients carrying drainage tubes outside the hospital to improve patients' mastery of relevant nursing skills. Combining with the current development of the Internet and artificial intelligence, we can use WeChat public accounts, TikTok, micro-videos and other platforms to carry out new health education and popular science methods, create vivid and visual education content, and improve patients' mastery of disease-related knowledge and nursing skills.

Conclusion

Patients with postoperative biliary and pancreatic system diseases with catheters experience varying degrees of stress. Patients encounter numerous challenges during out-of-hospital with drainage tube, including physiological adaptation and disease rehabilitation. This study conducted qualitative interviews on the tube experience of 17 patients with biliary and pancreatic system diseases. The findings indicated that patients exhibited negative social life experiences and physical and mental discomfort during carrying the drainage tube after discharge from hospital. Targeted health education and continuous nursing are necessary to maximize patients' self-care abilities, reduce canal-related complications and anxiety, and improve their quality of life.

Limitation, strengths

This study has some limitations. The qualitative interviews conducted were based on patients' experiences with catheterization over a past period of time, which relied on patients' self-reported recollections. This may be subject to recall bias, thereby potentially affecting the study results. Secondly, although this study, as a qualitative research, collected general information such as patients' 'education level and occupation', these indicators were not quantitatively analyzed in relation to patients' catheterization experiences. These data are correlated to patients' socio-economic status and education level, which may influence patients' experiences with outpatient catheterization. Therefore, future studies could incorporate more quantitative data to further extend and corroborate the conclusions of this study, which represents a potential limitation of this research." The strength of this study lies in the selection of research subjects who have been managing drainage tubes at home for more than one month. Moreover, 29.42% of the patients had two drainage tubes at home, and 23.53% were patients with multiple experiences of home tube management. Therefore, the tube management experiences of these patients are more characteristic and representative, ensuring better generalizability of the study findings.
