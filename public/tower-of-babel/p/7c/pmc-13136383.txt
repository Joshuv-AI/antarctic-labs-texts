Topologically reconstructing Pancharatnam-Berry phase via encircling exceptional point for chiral spin-orbit interaction steering
Authors: Qinghong Lyu, Qiuchen Yan, Wenjuan Zhao, Saisai Chu, Xiaoyong Hu, Qihuang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotonic spin-orbit interaction (SOI) is related to Pancharatnam-Berry (PB) phase, which is usually imposed on the cross-polarized components of light incident on a metasurface in broadband manner. However, metasurfaces exhibiting exceptional points (EPs) can block the cross-polarization conversion channel, hinting at the possibility of EPs influencing SOI. Here, we demonstrate that the accumulation of PB phase is equivalent to the encircling of an EP, leading to topologically reconstructed PB phase. Through spin-Hall effect and spin-to-vortex conversion, we experimentally realize chiral SOI steering within chosen spectral ranges and polarization, showing suppression, inversion and doubling of the PB phase accumulated via rotation. Our work introduces the possibility to control SOIs via a topologically reconstructed PB phase, offering promising possibilities for applications such as information encryption.Subject terms: Nanophotonics and plasmonics, Photonic crystals

Introduction

Photonic systems exhibit spin-orbit interaction (SOI)1, which describes the coupling between the spin and orbital angular momentum of light as it propagates through anisotropic media2,3. This interaction provides a paradigm for regulating light-matter interaction and has led to various intriguing phenomena, such as transverse spin4,5, spin-momentum locking6,7, and optical force effects8. Photonic SOI fundamentally stems from geometric phases: the spin-redirection phase originating from wavevector evolution in momentum space, and the Pancharatnam-Berry (PB) phase arising from polarization-state evolution on the Poincaré sphere9,10. PB phase are widely used in metasurfaces, a platform composed of periodically arranged sub-wavelength nanostructures11–13, to exhibit SOI phenomena, such as the photonic spin Hall effect14,15, spin-orbit conversion16–18, spin-orbit mapping19, and support applications including optical holography20–22 and lenses23. PB phase in metasurfaces are caused by the rotation of nanostructures, and is imposed on the orthogonal polarization states10,24. The integration of PB phase with nonlinear optics25,26, disorder27, and phase-change materials (PCMs)28–31 provides a means to steer photonic spin-orbit interactions and achieve diverse functionalities. Among these methods, additional tunability is introduced by PCMs, yet with increased fabrication or preservation demands. Thus, discovering paradigms that use stable materials with simple fabrication to steer photonic SOI is imperative. Metasurfaces, in addition to being platforms for SOI, are inherently open systems where each nanostructure continuously exchanges energy with the incident light32. Such non-Hermitian systems naturally contain exceptional points (EPs)32–35. EPs, non-Hermitian degeneracies where both eigenvalues and eigenvectors degenerate, introduce various phenomena, including enhanced sensitivity to perturbations, asymmetric mode switching, etc.36–39. The significance of EPs varies across different systems, such as micro-ring resonators and acoustic systems40–43. In metasurface systems, non-Hermitian Jones matrices serve as effective Hamiltonians33,34,44, leading to degenerate eigenstates that correspond to either left-circularly polarized (LCP) or right-circularly polarized (RCP) states, reflecting the chirality of the EP34. EPs in this regime indicate that the circular polarization conversion channel can be completely blocked while leaving the opposite conversion channel unaffected32. Thus, EPs provide a unique physical mechanism that governs polarization conversion processes with chirality, and have inspired a broad range of studies45–48. As PB phase-mediated SOI processes operate via polarization conversion, understanding and harnessing the influence of EPs on these processes becomes a promising task.

Here, we demonstrated EP’s role in PB phase-mediated photonic SOI, and hence revealed three chiral-sensitive mechanisms that can steer photonic SOI across visible to near-infrared spectra. In detail, PB phase accumulation via rotation is topologically equivalent to encircling a polarization conversion singularity, such as an EP, which reveals that PB phase-based SOI processes are topologically protected. By introducing static elliptical perturbations, a rotational anisotropy is induced, as their coupling to the main structure changes with rotation. This ensures that the encircling trajectory avoids full EP collapse, encountering EPs only at discrete points. Such encounters disrupt topological protection, change the winding number, and trigger three novel steering mechanisms: chiral SOI suppression, inversion, and doubling (Fig. 1). These correspond to suppressed, inverted, or doubled PB phase, respectively, at target wavelength range and polarization. The chirality is governed by the main structure of the meta-atom. To show the winding number changing mechanisms can steer photonic SOI on the geometric phase level, photonic spin-Hall effect and spin-to-vortex conversion are both examined across visible and near-infrared spectra. These effects are experimentally manifested as wavelength-sensitive spin-dependent splitting in positional space and orbital-angular-momentum (OAM) generation. Moreover, photonic SOI steering with different chirality and wavelength range is also verified through experiments. The observed results match well with the simulations and showcase novel mechanisms to steer SOI via PB phase with different winding numbers, which we term topologically reconstructed PB phase (TR-PB phase). Different from nonlinear geometric phase or active metasurfaces, our work fundamentally enriches the PB phase accumulation rule with fabrication simplicity, and adopts stable metal to steer SOI on the fundamental wave via topological control with controlled chirality. Results

Topological reconstruction controlled by a single EP under fixed wavelength

When there is only one EP induced during rotation at a fixed wavelength, chiral SOI suppression occurs, decoupling the photonic SOI from structural rotation. Here, we designed two metasurfaces to demonstrate chiral SOI suppression under different chirality and response wavelength using finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) parameter sweep simulations.

For these simulations, metasurfaces are set to the size of 10 μm * 10 μm, while perfectly matched layers are applied at the x, y and z directions. TR-PB phase used for information encryption

In this work, six possible combinations of meta-atoms are designed, as shown in Fig. 6a. Their PB phase responses are cataloged by incident polarization and wavelength in a TR-PB phase library, shown in Fig. 6b, from which the PB phase acquisition rule of any structure under specific polarization and input wavelength can be directly extracted.

Information encryption scheme.

a Six possible combinations of meta-atoms. b Library of topological reconstructed PB (TR-PB) phase responses, sorted by polarization (left and right circular polarization—LCP and RCP, respectively). c Design example of the encryption scheme. Using a modified Gerchberg-Saxton (GS) algorithm, the near-field phase distributions for the decoy image and the hidden image are generated. By comparing the two phase distributions and consulting the TR-PB phase library, the final arrangement adopting the six available meta-atoms (including the rotation angle of each main structure) is obtained. d Decryption demonstration. The encryption design workflow is presented in Fig. 6c. A modified GS algorithm first computes the near-field phase distributions of the decoy image and the hidden image. The two phase-profiles are linked through the rules of reversed, doubled, and conventional PB phase accumulation. By comparing the two distributions, a ternary mask consisting only of values −1, +1, and +2 is obtained. By consulting the TR-PB library, every specific meta-atom type of the metasurface and the rotation angle of each main structure are determined, together with the correct decryption key K4(σ, λ) that encodes the required polarization and wavelength. As shown in Fig. 6d, the hidden image is retrieved only with the correct key. For instance, under LCP illumination, the PB phase remains conventional (+2θ) across the entire spectrum, and the reconstructed hologram consistently displays the cat image (Fig. 6d, i-ii). In contrast, when the illumination switches to the polarization corresponding to the REP, the PB phase exhibits inversion and doubling at 730 nm in our design. As the wavelength changes from 850 nm to 730 nm, the reconstructed hologram accordingly switches from an inverted cat image (Fig. 6d, iii) to the dog image (Fig. 6d, iv). This spin-decoupling effect, arising from the chiral nature of the proposed TR-PB phase, enables the observed wavelength and polarization dependent image switching, thereby demonstrating the information encryption capability.

Discussion

We have proven PB phase-mediated SOI process can be interpreted as encircling an EP located at the origin of the cross-polarization conversion complex plane. By introducing EPs during the process of rotation, we have discovered topological reconstruction mechanisms to steer SOI. Both theoretical and experimental investigations reveal chiral SOI inversion, suppression, and doubling mechanisms that operate at designed spectral range and chirality, which can be utilized for information encryption, as shown in Fig. 6. In addition, while researches have focused on incorporating materials such as PCMs to introduce additional degrees of freedom for modulation, our method leverages topological control with widely used PB phase to introduce TR-PB phase, thereby introducing a new degree of freedom for metasurface design. Furthermore, the overall PCR response and modulation depth can be further enhanced through material selection, structural optimization, and the EP excitation method. For instance, this can be achieved by employing low-loss dielectric materials, refining geometric parameters, and engineering the EP introduction mechanisms. Our work suggests that there are still rich physics to be discovered for SOI process: When the topological protection of PB phase-based SOI is disrupted, the winding number exists the possibility for further evolution, as discussed in Supplementary Note 5. Our work opens up promising possibilities for the topological control in systems with singularities and advanced applications such as information encryption.

Methods

Device fabrication

The substrate used here is 160 nm ITO-coated silica glass. The substrates were cleaned by sequential ultrasonication for 5 min in acetone, methanol, and ultrapure water. The fabrication involved electron beam lithography, sequential deposition of a 6 nm titanium (Ti) layer and a 40 nm gold (Au) film, and liftoff in butanone.

Optical setup

In order to fully characterize the OAM beams, we used a Mach-Zehnder interferometer as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a. In this configuration, a Ti: Sapphire ultrafast femtosecond laser (pulse length: ≈115 fs, repetition rate: 76 MHz) centered at 800 nm is used as a light source. A horizontally-polarized beam is produced by using a linear polarizer (LP1). Then it is split in two beams by means of a 50/50 beam splitter (BS1). The upper arm of the interferometer passes through a quarter waveplate (QWP1) to produce a circularly polarized beam. A weakly focusing lens (L1, f = 150 mm) is used to reduce the beam size to be comparable with the diameter of the patterned metasurface (≈500 μm). A 20× objective lens, a lens (L2, f = 150 mm) and a camera behind the metasurface is used for recording the OAM beam created by the metasurface. The OAM beam passes through a polarization filter made of a quarter waveplate (QWP2), which has its fast axis oriented at 90° with respect to QWP1 and a linear polarizer (LP2) in cross-polarization with respect to LP1. This polarization filter is used to eliminate non-converted light passing through the metasurface. In the reference arm, the beam passes through a (HWP) that rotates the polarization of the laser beam from horizontal to vertical and it then is sent to a 5x beam expander (BE). The reference beam and the OAM beam are then recombined via a second 50:50 beam-splitter (BS2) and imaged on the camera. The interference of the two beams results in the measured characteristic spiral interference pattern (as seen in Figs. 3 and 5). In order to ensure the time overlap between the two beams, the optical path in one arm of the interferometer was adjusted using a delay line.

In Supplementary Fig. 1b, the optical setup used to examine the photonic spin Hall effect is demonstrated. Same femto-second laser is adopted as light source. A ND filter is also used to attenuate the background. A horizontally-polarized beam is subsequently produced by a linear polarizer (LP1) and it passes through a quarter waveplate (QWP1) to produce a circularly polarized beam. After passes through a 20× objective lens, light is effectively focused on the metasurface, and the transmitted beam is collected by another 20× objective lens. Then, the beam passes through a quarter waveplate (QWP2), which has its fast axis oriented at 90° with respect to QWP1, and a linear polarizer (LP2) to eliminate the undesired polarization. Eventually, spin dependent splitting is captured by the camera after passing through a focusing lens (L1, f = 150 mm). It is worth mentioning that the generally low polarization conversion efficiency of our sample results in a relatively strong co-polarized transmission component, which remains detectable after passing through the linear polarizer, and form the 0th order diffraction captured in photonic spin-Hall effect.
