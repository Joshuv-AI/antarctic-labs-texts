Elemental Analysis of Maha Kumbh‐2025 Water Along With Intercomparison With Other Water Resources Using Synchrotron‐Assisted Total‐Reflection X‐Ray Fluorescence Analysis: A Case Study
Authors: M K Tiwari, Ajay Khooha, Ayushi Trivedi, G G Potey, V K Mahadik, Ajit Kumar Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTotal reflection X‐ray fluorescence (TXRF) is a well‐established method to detect ultra‐trace elements present in water as well as in other liquids that can be diluted into the water medium. In the present case study, we have applied the TXRF technique to analyze various elements present in the Maha Kumbh‐2025 water sample, which was collected from the nose point of Triveni Sangam, at Prayagraj, India. In addition, we have also performed intercomparative quantitative analysis of other water samples collected from different water resources. Our results show that the concentrations of different elements present in the Maha Kumbh‐2025 water samples are well within the acceptable limits defined by the WHO guidelines (for drinking water). Furthermore, we did not observe the presence of any heavy toxic elements (e.g., chromium (Cr), arsenic (As), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb)) in the Maha Kumbh‐2025 water sample within the range of detection limits (DL) of our technique. This case‐study‐based investigation provides a site‐specific insight into the elemental quality of water at a location where a large mass‐gathering festival is organized. It clearly demonstrates the practical applicability of TXRF as a rapid and reliable monitoring tool for assessing the quality of water from environmental pollution aspects.Keywords: elemental analysis, Maha Kumbh‐2025, synchrotron radiation, total reflection X‐ray fluorescence, water pollution, X‐ray fluorescence

Introduction

The Maha Kumbh‐2025 Mela is a special pilgrimage festival in the Hindu religion which is characterized as the world's largest human peaceful mass gathering on the earth. It is organized after every 12 years at a particular location in the Indian sub‐continent. Recently, this event was organized at Prayagraj city (old name Allahabad) of India during the period of January–February 2025. This rare event is especially celebrated by the Hindu community and their devotees with a holy bath. The number of devotees who took part in the holy bath in the Maha Kumbh‐2025 festival during the period of approximately 45 days was expected to be more than 60 crore (~600 million). Apart from Indians, citizens of other countries also participate in a Kumbh mela festival. Considering the wide popularity of this festival, it is important to monitor the quality of water when such an event is organized.

Water pollution is a worldwide problem in many countries, posing serious health risks to all organisms including humans. Water pollution can be classified mainly into two categories: chemical contamination (organic and inorganic pollutants) and biocontamination. In the first case, the presence of various heavy toxic metals in drinking water is a primary health concern, which arises from contaminated soil with elements such as chromium (Cr), arsenic (As), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb), along with naturally occurring biofriendly elements like calcium (Ca), iron (Fe), and zinc (Zn). This situation occurs if the soil of a particular geographic terrain is inherently contaminated (El Khoury et al. 2025; Helaluddin et al. 2016). In the second route, elemental contamination of water occurs when industrial wastewater is disposed of directly into a river without proper treatment. Because of its nonbiodegradable nature, such contamination in river water does not change or decrease with time. In such a condition, various sensitive techniques such as Total Reflection X‐Ray Fluorescence (TXRF) and inductively coupled plasma‐mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) are used to estimate absolute mass concentration of different elements present in the water (Elzain et al. 2016; Al Maliki et al. 2017; Borgese et al. 2014).

Bio‐contamination of water includes the presence of harmful bacteria and microorganisms produced by various living organisms. It is primarily characterized by the presence of biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) and dissolved oxygen (DO). This type of pollution is usually dynamic in nature, because the numbers as well as types of microbes present in the water keep on changing over time. It is well known that stagnant water is an ideal breeding place for the harmful bacteria and microorganisms, whereas moving water does not allow harmful bacteria to grow more rapidly because the persistent motion of water streams keeps the BOD level low in amount thereby helping to control growth of harmful anaerobic microorganisms in running water. Most commonly used water purifying methods in such cases are electrocoagulation, UV disinfection, ultrafiltration, boiling of water, and the use of potash alum to coagulate dissolved macromolecular impurities present in water. In the case of Maha Kumbh‐2025 water, the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) of India had already provided a report on the parameters that primarily focus on the biocontamination, such as fecal coliform (FC), BOD, and DO values (Report by CPCB in OA. No 310 of 2022 (Kamlesh Singh Vs. State of UP and Ors.), n.d.).

However, above‐mentioned methods are not practical for any country to apply for the disinfection of large water bodies like rivers. Below, we present an idea that can be used in the case of flowing water (e.g., river) to reduce or remove bacteria by adopting specific water flow hydrodynamics and surface‐related phenomena by using gravitational energy (shown in Figure 1). In this case, relative frictional forces between different layers of rapidly flowing water can exert stress on the cell of a bacterium, thereby resulting in a puncturing or tearing effect on its cell wall (Lu et al. 2023). This effect can be further enhanced if water passes through a nanopatterned surface of sand particles comprising complex, rough surface topography with feature size ranging from micrometers to nanometers (coated with silicon and iron oxide nanoparticles). Such a civil structure (depicted in Figure 1) introduces curly water‐flow channels thereby producing highly complex water‐flow dynamics such as irregular velocity fluctuations, eddies, and swirling regions that transfer a large amount of kinetic energy and momentum between different layers of water.

In the present work, we have carried out a site‐specific elemental analysis of Maha Kumbh‐2025 water samples, collected from the location of Triveni Sangam, Prayagraj, India using TXRF analysis technique which provides superior detection sensitivities especially for heavy toxic elements (Tiwari et al. 2005; Garg et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2016). Furthermore, using an integrated analysis approach of a case‐based‐study, we have performed an intercomparison with water samples collected from different water resources to assess the elemental variations of the “Triveni Sangam” water quality in a broader environmental context.

Experimental Details

In order to investigate the elemental composition of various water samples collected from different locations (Google, n.d.), including the Maha Kumbh‐2025 site, TXRF measurements were performed using synchrotron X‐rays. The primary sample of interest (i.e., Maha Kumbh‐2025 water sample), was collected on February 22, 2025 from the nose point of Triveni Sangam at Prayagraj, India. This site is a confluence of three rivers (Ganga, Yamuna, and the mythical Saraswati rivers) and it holds a significant religious and cultural importance, particularly during the Maha Kumbh mela festival. Furthermore, the water sample was collected at the end of Maha Kumbh‐2025 event in order to ensure maximum probability of presence of elemental pollution in the water sample. To provide a comparative analysis, other water samples, collected within a 1‐month time period from different rivers e.g., Ganga, Sarayu, Narmada, and groundwater from Indore city (Figure 2), were analyzed along with Maha Kumbh‐2025 water. In addition, laboratory grade ultra‐pure Milli‐Q water (Merck, Millipore, Germany) as well as a few reference standards; NIST‐1640 (Trace elements in natural water) and ICP‐IV (Merck, Germany) multi‐elements sample were also analyzed using TXRF technique for the presence of different elements and determination of their detection limits (DLs).

Image showing various locations from where water samples were taken for TXRF analysis. All images were obtained from the Google Maps (Google, n.d.).

For accurate quantification, all water samples were mixed with a gallium (Ga) internal standard at a concentration of ~200 ppb. The samples were homogeneously mixed using a mechanical stirrer to ensure uniform distribution of the Ga internal standard. ~10 μL aliquot of each sample was then pipetted onto a precleaned quartz glass substrate. The samples were then dried under normal conditions, resulting in a thin, uniform residue on top of a quartz glass substrate for TXRF analysis (Tiwari et al. 2002). We have prepared and analyzed four replicates of each water sample to estimate r.m.s. variations in determined concentration values. The geometry of incident and reflected X‐rays on top of the quartz glass substrate along with the dried residues of the water sample is depicted in Figure 3. All TXRF measurements were carried out atBL‐16 beamline of the Indus‐2 synchrotron radiation facility, located at Raja Ramanna Centre for Advanced Technology (RRCAT), Indore. The details of the BL‐16 beamline such as X‐ray energy range, available photon flux are given elsewhere (Tiwari et al. 2013). All TXRF measurements were carried out using incident X‐ray excitation energy of 18 keV to ensure excitation of a broad range of elements. For this, the incident X‐ray beam of dimensions ~100 μm (V) × 10 mm (H) was allowed to fall on the quartz glass surface at grazing incident angle ~0.07° (in our case). The Maha Kumbh‐2025 water was also analyzed at incident X‐ray energy of 12 keV for efficient detection of low Z elements present in it. The characteristic fluorescence X‐rays emitted from different elements in each sample were detected using the silicon drift detector (SDD) (Amptek, USA) (Krawczyk et al. 2016), which was placed at a ~40 mm distance away from the sample in the direction (X‐axis) perpendicular to the incident plane of the X‐ray beam (Y‐Z plane) in s‐polarization geometry. Although the s‐polarization geometry offers higher signal‐to‐background ratio but it does not allow placing the SDD detector very close to the sample surface (Das et al. 2014). Each spectrum was recorded for an acquisition time of 100 s.

A schematic layout of the TXRF geometry which was used for elemental analysis of water samples, showing the incident X‐ray beam, reflected X‐ray beam, and dried residues of sample on top of a quartz glass substrate.

Results and Discussion

To determine the concentrations of different elements present in a water sample, first we investigated the relative sensitivities of different elements with respect to Ga. Furthermore, we have also determined the minimum detection limits (DLs) of our TXRF system for different elements. For this, we have measured X‐ray fluorescence spectra of different internationally accepted standard reference materials such as NIST‐1640 (Trace elements in natural water) and a multielement ICP‐IV standard sample containing ~1 ppm (1 mg/L) concentrations of each element. Using ICP‐IV standard sample, we determined both minimum detection limits as well as relative sensitivities of different elements at incident excitation X‐ray energy of 18 keV because most of the water samples were analyzed at this energy. On the other hand, the NIST‐1640 standard reference sample was purposefully analyzed at 12 keV X‐ray energy to know the detection limit values of different low‐atomic‐number (low‐Z) elements at 12 keV excitation energy because we have also analyzed the Maha Kumbh‐2025 water sample at this energy for a better understanding of the presence of low‐Z elements in it.

Figure 8a,b show the measured TXRF spectra of the Maha Kumbh‐2025 water sample at 18 and 12 keV excitation X‐ray energies, respectively. Both figures clearly show the presence of Cl, K, Ca, Fe, Zn, Br, and Sr elements in the water sample. However, we could not detect the presence of any toxic heavy metals like chromium (Cr), arsenic (As), mercury (Hg), lead (Pb) etc. in it. Absence of these hazardous elements clearly demonstrates the relative purity of the Maha Kumbh‐2025 water. By knowing the relative sensitivity of an element, the absolute mass concentrations in units of μg/ml (ppm) or ng/ml (ppb) were calculated for different water samples. The determined concentration values of various elements present in the water collected from the Maha‐Kumbh site, along with those from other water resources, are summarized in Table 1. By comparing the results presented in Table 1, it can be seen that the concentrations of all major elements in different river waters and Maha Kumbh‐2025 water are more or less the same except for iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn). The elevated level of these elements may be due to increased human activities during the Maha Kumbh‐2025 event, such as mass bathing, thereby contributing to an elevated level of sand particles mixing in the water.

On the other hand, different river waters show slightly lower concentrations of these elements compared to Maha Kumbh‐2025 water, suggesting a relatively stable background and limited mixing of sand particles in these water samples. It may be important to mention here that during the sample preparation for TXRF analysis, all water samples were homogenized properly before pipetting them onto the quartz glass substrate. A similar procedure was also followed in the case of the Maha Kumbh‐2025 water sample. The primary purpose of this homogenizing process was to recreate or mimic the actual condition of the water as expected to be during the Maha Kumbh‐2025 festival. To strengthen our statement, we have also provided photographs of the sample container (containing Maha Kumbh‐2025 water). These photographs clearly show the sedimentation of sand particles at the bottom of the container in the rest condition. There are fair chances that mixing of water introduced an aggregate of iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn) rich sand particles that had no effect on the concentration of other elements. From Table 1, it can also be seen that in the case of groundwater (bore well water) of Indore city, an elevated level of different elements was observed, which may be due to the leaching of different elements into the groundwater. This effect usually increases if one goes to higher depths. Our results clearly show the effectiveness of the TXRF method for monitoring elemental pollution and assessing the water quality of diverse water resources.

Conclusions

In the present work, which was carried out on a case‐study‐based framework using TXRF technique, we have determined absolute mass concentration values of different elements present in the water samples collected from various locations. Our results show that concentration values of various elements present in the Maha Kumbh‐2025 water, collected from the location of the Triveni Sangam site of Prayagraj city of India were found to be well within the limits as defined by the WHO guidelines (World Health Organization 2022). Furthermore, we could not observe the presence of any heavy toxic elements in this water within the range of detection limits of our technique. In addition, other commonly present elements in the Maha Kumbh‐2025water such as Calcium (Ca) and Potassium (K) were also found to be in a similar concentration range as observed in different river water samples, even after prolonged usage of water over a period of ~2 months by 600 million people. In terms of practical consequences, such a case study provides an essential message to society about the cleanliness of a water body particularly related to the presence of heavy toxic elements (if any) presence in it, when such an event with a significant chance of massive human gathering is intended. This study also suggests that time‐dependent TXRF measurements may provide useful insights if such a gatherings are held at a site having still water conditions in order to examine the influence of mass gathering on elemental composition changes in the water body. The outcomes of such a study provide important directions to the government and organizers of a nation on how to take appropriate measures, such as maintaining running water conditions and strictly avoiding the use of specific colors particularly containing heavy toxic elements, as part of our collective social responsibility in order to maintain the cleanliness of our natural water resources.
