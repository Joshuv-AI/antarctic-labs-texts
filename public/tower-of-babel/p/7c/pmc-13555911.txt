Characterization and analysis of the full-length transcriptome of Frankliniella occidentalis (Thysanoptera: Thripidae)
Authors: Li Liu, Tao Zhang, Wenbo Yue, Dingyin Li, Junrui Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Frankliniella occidentalis, an insect belonging to the order Thysanoptera, causes severe damage to agricultural and horticultural crops, resulting in significant economic losses worldwide. The development of molecular and sequencing technologies has helped elucidate the molecular mechanisms regulating its growth and development as well as its damaging activity. However, much remains to be explored. To further investigate the molecular complexity of this species, we sequenced the full-length transcriptome of mixed samples obtained from specimens at all developmental stages.ResultsOf all transcripts, 89.04% matched with the reference genome; additionally, 29,750 alternative splicing events, 2,342 genes with poly(A) sites, and 153 candidate fusion transcript events were identified, and 4,235 long noncoding RNAs were discovered.ConclusionsThis is the first full-length transcriptome of F. occidentalis reported to date. This study greatly contributes to the understanding of the molecular complexity and diversity of this insect, providing a basis to develop specific molecular targets as well as resources for gene function studies in other insects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13102-1.Keywords: Thripidae, PacBio sequencing, Long-read sequencing, Alternative splicing, Fusion gene

Introduction

Western flower thrips, Frankliniella occidentalis Pergande (Thysanoptera: Thripidae), are one of the most difficult-to-manage pests affecting agricultural and horticultural crops worldwide [1]. These insects damage plants not only through direct feeding and oviposition but also by transmitting several plant viruses, such as tomato spotted wilt orthotospovirus (TSWV), parietaria mottle virus, and maize chlorotic mottle virus [2, 3]. Currently, chemical pesticides are the primary method for controlling F. occidentalis. However, long-term and extensive use of these pesticides has led to resistance in F. occidentalis against many pesticides, including pyriproxyfen [4], spinosad [5, 6], and spinetoram [7, 8], as well as the development of cross-resistance [9]. Therefore, a better understanding of its development and adaptation is necessary to improve control measures.

Advances in omics technology and computing systems are crucial for the future control of F. occidentalis [10]. A recent study [11] sequenced its mitochondrial genome and analyzed its control region. Another study used de novo transcriptome sequencing to investigate genes associated with plant virus transmission and insecticide resistance in this species [12]. Similarly, differences in the salivary gland transcriptome between TSWV-infected and noninfected specimens were analyzed in another study focusing on F. occidentalis–virus interactions [13]. Furthermore, the whole F. occidentalis genome was sequenced at the Baylor College of Medicine Human Genome Sequencing Center using an enhanced Illumina ALLPATHS-LG sequencing and assembly strategy [14, 15]. More recently, studies using these techniques have focused on interactions between F. occidentalis and plant viruses [16–18], insecticide resistance [19], host plants and environmental adaptation [20], and development [21, 22]. Recently, researchers at the Beijing Academy of Agricultural and Forestry Sciences generated the chromosome-level genome assembly of F. occidentalis using advanced Hi-C technologies [23]. Despite the relevance of the data produced using these technologies, they were all based on second-generation sequencing technology.

In this study, third-generation sequencing technology, i.e., Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing, was used for the first time to generate the full-length transcriptome of F. occidentalis using pooled RNA samples derived from eggs, 1st - and 2nd -instar larvae, pre-pupae, pupae, and adults. The resulting transcriptome data were analyzed for transcript functional annotation, alternative splicing (AS), alternative polyadenylation (APA), fusion gene, and long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) prediction.

Materials and methods

F. occidentalis rearing and sampling

F. occidentalis were reared following previously established laboratory protocols, under a 14 h:10 h (light: dark) photoperiod, temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, and humidity of 70% ± 7%, with minor modifications and optimizations [24]. Kidney bean pods were provided daily and replaced regularly. For other development stages, pods were replaced according to the specific feeding requirements.

The device shown in Fig. 1 was used for sampling thrips’ eggs. Several plastic culture dishes with 9 cm diameters, with their bottoms removed, were covered with parafilm and placed upside down. Subsequently, a suitable volume of sterile water was introduced between the parafilm and the base, ensuring the parafilm adhered to the bottom and formed a water film. The parafilm surface was subsequently coated with an appropriate quantity of honey water. The outer circle of the whole device was covered with two circles of parafilm for fixation. After removing the bean pods, the whole device was placed in a fresh-keeping box for raising adult thrips, which were expected to lay eggs in the water film. The device was removed after 24 h, and approximately 55,000 eggs were collected into a 2.0 mL screw-capped test tube using a pipette gun. The eggs were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C.

Sampling device used to collect eggs of Frankliniella occidentalis

First- and second-instar larvae, pre-pupae, pupae, and adults were sampled from the fresh-keeping box used for rearing. Approximately 5000 1-day-old 1st instars, 1500 4-day-old 2nd instars, 1000 1-day-old pre-pupae, 1000 2-day-old pupae, and 1000 3-day-old adults were collected separately in 2.0-mL screw-capped test tubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C.

Total RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample was ground into a powder in liquid nitrogen, transferred to a 2-mL tube, homogenized for 2 min, and placed in a horizontal position for 5 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to a new EP tube containing 0.3 mL of chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (24:1). The mix was vigorously shaken for 15 s and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The upper aqueous phase containing RNA was then transferred to a new tube and mixed with an equal volume of isopropyl alcohol, followed by centrifugation at 13,600 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the RNA pellet was washed twice with 1 mL of 75% ethanol. The mix was then centrifuged at 13,600 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C to collect residual ethanol, and the pellet was air-dried for 5–10 min in a biosafety cabinet. Finally, 25–100 µL of DEPC (diethylpyrocarbonate)-treated water was added to dissolve the RNA. Subsequently, total RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop and Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Equal amounts of high-quality RNA from six samples were pooled together to obtain the final RNA sample for library preparation.

PacBio SMRTbell library preparation and sequencing

Multiple samples were pooled for full-length cDNA library construction. The Iso-Seq template was prepared following the Iso-Seq Template Preparation protocol for the Sequel Systems. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 800 to 1000 ng of total RNA using the Clontech SMARTer PCR cDNA Synthesis Kit. The CDS Primer IIA was first annealed to the poly(A)+ tail of transcripts, followed by first-strand synthesis using SMARTScribe™ Reverse Transcriptase. Then, a sufficient amount of double-stranded cDNA was produced via large-scale PCR using Clontech PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase and 5′ PCR Primer IIA (5′-AAGCAGTGGTATCAACGCAGAGTAC-3′).

Double-stranded cDNA was size-selected into 0–5 and 4.5–10 kb fractions using the BluePippin system (Sage Science, MA, USA). Another large-scale PCR was performed to enrich for > 4.5 kb cDNA for SMRTbell library construction. The DNA was repaired using the DNA Damage Repair Mix (PacBio) and End Repair Mix (PacBio). The blunt-end adapter was ligated to the cDNA using PacBio ligase. Exonucleases Exo III and Exo VII were added to the ligated cDNA to remove unrepaired DNA and linear DNA lacking the blunt-end adapter.

The BluePippin system was used again for size selection to prepare the 4.5–10 kb library. Finally, cDNAs were quantified using Qubit HS (Life Technologies, USA) and analyzed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent DNA 12000 Reagents, Agilent, USA). SMRTbell libraries were bound to polymerases using Sequel Binding Kit 2.1 and Primers V3. Polymerase–template complexes were loaded via MagBeads (PacBio MagBead Binding Kit) or by diffusion. Sequencing was performed on a PacBio Sequel sequencer (BGI-Shenzhen, China) using Sequel Sequencing Kit 2.1 and Sequel SMRT Cell 1 M v2 Tray.

Identification and annotation of full-length transcripts

The SMRT-Analysis software package smrtlink8.0 (PacBio) was used to analyze Iso-Seq data and generate consistent full-length transcript sequences, including read-of-insert recognition (smrtlink ccs), full-length transcripts identification (BGI’s house software Irs: https://github.com/zhuqianhua/lrs), clustering (isoseq3 cluster), and correction (isoseq3 polish).

Considering that polished transcripts may still present some redundancy, they were aligned to the reference genome of F. occidentalis (Genbank accession: GCA_000697945.4) using GMAP [25]. Alignment results for each sample were then combined using the official tofu_collapse program (https://github.com/Magdoll/cDNA_Cupcake), which removes redundancy according to transcript position on the genome. Finally, de-redundant transcripts were compared with the reference genome using the Cuffcompare [26] program to generate a detailed transcript classification.

High-quality transcripts were annotated in seven functional databases: the Pfam library (v14.6) [27] using hmmscan (v3.1b2) [28], the NT database (download time 20211117) using Blastn (v2.2.28) [29], the NR (download time 20211117), KOG (v20090331) [30], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (v101) [31], and SwissProt (release-2020_02) [32] databases using Blastx (v2.2.28) [29] or Diamond (v0.8.31) [33], and the gene ontology (GO) [34] database using Blast2GO (v2.5.0) [35] (for the NR annotation results).

Prediction of coding ability of novel transcripts

After obtaining new transcripts, three prediction softwares—CPC (v0.9-r2) [36], txCdsPredict, and CNCI [37]—and the Pfam database [27] were used to assess the coding ability of transcripts to distinguish mRNA and lncRNA. The parameter settings for all three prediction software packages were set to default values. Transcript identity was confirmed only when at least three of the four methods produced consistent results. Detailed rules were as follows: (1) CPC_threshold = 0: if the CPC_threshold > 0, the transcript was mRNA; otherwise, it was lncRNA. (2) CNCI_threshold = 0: if the CNCI_threshold > 0, the transcript was mRNA; otherwise, it was lncRNA. (3) txCdsPredict_threshold = 500: if txCdsPredict_threshold > 500, the transcript was mRNA; otherwise, it was lncRNA. (4) If the transcript could be mapped onto the Pfam database, it was considered as mRNA; otherwise, it was lncRNA.

Analysis of AS events

Cuffcompare and ASprofile [38] were used to detect splicing events in samples. Cuffcompare was used to compare full-length transcripts with the reference genes, and then the extract-as function in ASprofile was used to identify AS events.

Identification of APA events

APA is a common gene regulation mechanism in eukaryotes, allowing a gene to generate multiple transcripts and increasing transcript complexity. Polished isoforms were aligned to the reference genome using GMAP, and APA site identification and statistics for each gene were performed using TAPIS (v1.2.1) based on the genes’ GTF file. In transcriptomic studies, “transcript” refers to any RNA molecule transcribed from a gene, including unspliced precursors and fully spliced mature RNAs, while “isoform” specifically denotes mature mRNA molecules that arise from alternative splicing, alternative promoter usage, or alternative polyadenylation, resulting in distinct exon compositions and/or coding potentials.

Detection of fusion genes

Polished isoforms were aligned to the reference genome using GMAP (v 2020-06-02, parameters -t 24 -n 0), and fusion genes were detected using the fusion_finder.py program in the official TOFU software (v 2.2.3, default parameters). A fusion gene is identified when a single transcript aligns to two distinct genomic loci separated by a sufficiently large genomic distance. Most identified fusion events occurred within scaffolds, rather than at scaffold boundaries.

Results

Pacbio Iso-Seq-derived full-length sequences

PacBio sequencing generated a total of 639,746 polymerase reads, from which 11,471,829 subreads (50.30 Gb) and 537,462 circular consensus sequences with an N50 length of 5,402 bp were obtained. Among these, 364,203 were identified as full-length nonchimeric reads, with a mean length of 1,381 bp. For further analysis, sequences were corrected using the polish program, resulting in 164,435 polished isoforms with a mean length of 1,881 bp. Comparison of these polished isoforms with the reference genome using GMAP showed that 89.04% matched the genome, with 25.35% perfectly mapped, 69.23% uniquely mapped, and 19.81% as multiple maps. The results are presented in (Table 1). Read-of-insert length, full-length length, and isoform length are shown in Fig. 3A, B, and C, respectively. After removing any redundancy by combining the isoforms from each sample based on transcript position on the genome, a total of 17,649 unique isoforms were obtained. Of these, 2,561 known transcripts, 11,887 alternative splice forms of known transcripts, and 2,459 novel transcripts were identified (Fig. 3D).

Functional annotation

Functional annotation against seven databases (NR, NT, GO, KOG, Pfam, KEGG, and SwissProt) yielded a union set of 19,518 high-quality isoforms. The highest sequence similarity was observed in the annotation in the NT database (18,266 transcripts, 93.59%), followed by those in the NCBI NR (16,168 transcripts, 82.84%), KEGG (12,976 transcripts, 66.48%), SwissProt (12,927 transcripts, 66.23%), KOG (12,345 transcripts, 63.25%), Pfam (12,257 transcripts, 62.80%), and GO (5,491 transcripts, 28.13%) databases. Overall, 18,546 (95.02%) of transcripts had annotation support in ≥ 1 database, whereas 4,102 transcripts (21.02%) were supported by all seven databases (Table 2). Among them, the annotations into NR, KOG, KEGG, Pfam, and SwissProt (9,911 transcripts in total) are shown in (Fig. 4A). In the NCBI NR annotation, 90.38% of homologous sequences matched with F. occidentalis, 6.36% with Thrips palmi, and 3.27% with other species (Fig. 4B).

AS events

Based on the presence of different splicing modes, AS enhances transcriptome and proteome diversity. Currently, AS events are divided into several broad categories: exon skipping (SKIP) and cassette exons (MSKIP), retention of single (IR) and multiple (MIR) introns, alternative exon ends (AE), alternative transcription start site (TSS), and alternative transcription termination site (TTS) (Fig. 8A). In this study, full-length transcriptome data were analyzed using ASprofile and a total of 29,750 AS events detected. Specifically, these included 1,586 AE, 980 IR_OFF, 980 IR_ON, 124 MIR_OFF, 124 MIR_ON, 188 MSKIP_OFF, 188 MSKIP_ON, 1,196 SKIP_OFF, 1,196 SKIP_ON, 11,221 TSS, 9,977 TTS, 688 XAE, 172 XIR_OFF, 172 XIR_ON, 18 XMIR_OFF, 18 XMIR_ON, 144 XMSKIP_OFF, 144 XMSKIP_ON, 317 XSKIP_OFF, and 317 XSKIP_ON (Table 3). The complete list of genes with AS events and their corresponding AS types are provided in Table S1. In addition to the types of AS events reported in previous studies, including TSS and TTS, this study also identified SKIP, MSKIP, IR, MIR, and AE (Fig. 8B).

APA analysis

After matching the obtained full-length transcriptome data to the reference genome, APA sites were identified in 2,342 genes using TAPIS software. A total of 1,021, 570, and 751 genes exhibited one single poly(A) site, two poly(A) sites, and ≥ 3 poly(A) sites, respectively (Fig. 9A and Table S4).

Prediction of coding sequences and lncRNA transcripts

A total of 5046, 8179, 8758, and 10,225 lncRNAs were identified in the transcriptome of F. occidentalis using CPC, txCdsPredict, CNCI, and Pfam, respectively (Fig. 9B and Table S6). The intersection of these four results produced 4235 lncRNA transcripts.

Discussion

In this study, we generated the first full-length transcriptome of Frankliniella occidentalis using PacBio single-molecule long-read sequencing, providing an important resource for improving transcript annotation and understanding gene structure in this economically important pest species. Long-read sequencing, which has recently benefited from significant improvements in throughput and accuracy, represents a new generation of sequencing technology [39–41]. Compared with second-generation sequencing technologies, long-read sequencing produces substantially longer reads, reduces gaps in genome assemblies, and facilitates the accurate identification of gene isoforms, the discovery of new genes, and the detection of novel isoforms of annotated genes [42–44].

This technology has been widely applied in both plant and animal studies, including maize (Zea mays) [45], sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L. Moench) [46], upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) [47], cobia (Rachycentron canadum, Rachycentridae) [48], switchgrass (Panicum virgatum L.) [49], Ginkgo biloba [50], turbot (Scophthalmus maximus) [51], and humans [52, 53], demonstrating its effectiveness in improving transcriptome completeness and gene annotation.

Long-read sequencing is also increasingly applied in entomological research. For example, it has been used to sequence and assemble the genomes of Bombyx mori [54], Triatoma rubrofasciata [55], Anopheles coluzzii [56], and Neomicropteryx cornuta [57], providing important foundations for insect genomics. Furthermore, full-length transcriptome analyses based on this technology have been reported for Rhopalosiphum padi [58], Hyphantria cunea [59], Apis mellifera [60], Cydia pomonella L [61]., Aphis aurantii [62], Bactrocera dorsalis [63], and Spodoptera frugiperda [64], generating extensive information on transcriptional diversity and regulatory mechanisms in insects.

Omics-based studies of F. occidentalis, a representative species of Thysanoptera, have attracted considerable attention in recent years. In the present study, single-molecule long-read sequencing identified 17,649 full-length transcripts, including numerous previously unreported transcripts. The number of transcripts identified was substantially higher than that reported in previous studies based on second-generation transcriptome sequencing [12, 13, 21]. This difference may reflect the advantages of third-generation sequencing technology, the inclusion of multiple developmental stages in this study, and differences in data processing approaches.

AS is a key mechanism in the regulation of gene expression that substantially enriches the diversity of transcripts [65–67]. In this study, 29,750 AS events were identified in the full-length transcriptome of F. occidentalis, a considerably larger dataset than that obtained for C. pomonella (1,142) [61], Harmonia axyridis (1,163) [68], (A) aurantia (2,029) [62], (B) mori (1,867) [69], Gomphocerus licenti (605), and Mongolotettix japonicus (594) [70]. In contrast, the social insect A. mellifera exhibits a substantially higher number of AS events (136554) [60]. In this study, alternative TSS and TTS accounted for > 71% of the identified transcript variation events, suggesting that alternative transcription initiation and termination are major contributors to transcriptome diversity. Variation in TSS can generate transcript isoforms with different 5′ untranslated regions (5′ UTRs), potentially affecting transcriptional regulation and translation efficiency through the presence of distinct regulatory elements. Similarly, alternative TTS usage may produce transcripts with variable 3′ UTRs, potentially affecting mRNA stability, localization, and post-transcriptional regulation. Although the functional implications of these events require further validation, the high proportion of TSS and TTS events observed here indicates a potential role in shaping transcript diversity and regulating gene expression.

APA is an RNA-processing mechanism that generates distinct 3′ termini on mRNAs and other RNA polymerase II transcripts [71]. It is a pervasive phenomenon across all eukaryotic species and regarded as a principal mechanism of gene regulation. In the present study, 2,342 genes with > 1 APA site were identified. This number is considerably lower than that reported for S. frugiperda [72] and (A) mellifera [60]. Gene fusion is an important mechanism for generating new genes [73, 74] extensively studied in humans [75] but not so much in insects. The present study identified 153 candidate fusion transcripts in F. occidentalis, a higher number than that in C. pomonella (81) [61] but lower than that in (B) mori (784) [69]. Using four different methods, this study identified > 4,000 lncRNAs, a significantly higher number than that reported for R. padi [58], (C) pomonella [61], G. licenti and M. japonicus [70], Camponotus floridanus, and Harpegnathos saltator [76]. However, it is lower than the number reported in A. mellifera [60] and H. axyridis [68]. This could be explained by the different regulatory mechanisms across different species.

These regulatory mechanisms participate in multiple biological processes in insects. Studies of the orders Diptera and Hymenoptera indicate that AS regulates sex determination genes [77], with similar observations in Lepidoptera [78]. In Drosophila melanogaster, male-specific courtship behavior genes are regulated by AS [79], which also drives diversity in the chitin synthase gene of Nilaparvata lugens and Laodelphax striatellus [80] and contributes to immune-related protein diversity in Lepidopteran [81]. A study of over 250 adult Drosophila cell types revealed that APA contributes to tissue-specific regulation [82]. In Apis cerana, AS and APA regulate diverse metabolic pathways and antifreeze proteins in response to cold stress [83] and control developmental and functional transitions [84]. Although these studies provide insights into the roles of AS and APA, differences in species, sequencing technologies, and sampling strategies, among other factors, mean that future research will need to conduct further investigations focused on specific scientific questions. Similarly, research on fusion genes suggests links to insect protein evolution [85], but this requires further, more specific scientific studies to confirm.

Overall, the higher transcriptional complexity in F. occidentalis than in many species may account for its high adaptability and damaging behavior. Notably, cytochrome P450 genes and glutathione S-transferase genes play a role in pesticide resistance [19, 86–87] and host plant adaptation [88–90] in F. occidentalis. Here, we detected > 30 cytochrome P450 genes and 17 glutathione S-transferase genes having AS in F. occidentalis, which provides a plausible molecular basis for its susceptibility to strong host plant adaptation and pesticide resistance. Research on transcriptional diversity in relation to pesticide resistance and host plant adaptation represents an important direction for future studies.

Although our analyses were supported by rigorous bioinformatic validation, including genome-based mapping of candidate fusion transcripts and AS events, the results presented here should be interpreted with caution. In addition, we acknowledge that using earlier versions of annotation databases may limit the accuracy of gene functional assignments; thus, future re-annotation using updated, more comprehensive databases may refine these annotations and improve their biological interpretation. Further validation and investigation are needed to reveal the specific molecular structures and functions of the detected events.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study was the first to use single-molecule long-read sequencing technology to elucidate the complexity of the developmental transcriptome in F. occidentalis. The present results provide crucial data for studying gene function in this insect. They can also serve as a valuable reference for future studies investigating spatiotemporal gene expression, tissue and organ differentiation, and environmental adaptation in this insect. Further advances in omics technologies will enable integration of spatial transcriptomics and single-cell transcriptomics, thus providing deeper insights into F. occidentalis development.
