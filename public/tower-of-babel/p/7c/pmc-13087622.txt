Clinical Discovery and Molecular Analysis of Two Novel MSH2 Gene Mutations (p.Ala771Gly and p.Val797Gly) in Saudi Colorectal Cancer Patients: Potential Implications for Tumorigenesis
Authors: Mahmood Rasool, Absarul Haque, Mohammed Alharthi, Tainus Ali, Sajjad Karim, Loubna Siraj Mira, Fahd Al‐Abbasi, Murad Aljiffry, Mohammed Ghunaim, Abdulrahman Sibiany, Peter Natesan Pushparaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsColorectal cancer (CRC) is a serious global health problem, ranking first in men and third in women among all cancers worldwide. The genetic basis for CRC remains unclear in most cases; therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the molecular genetic basis of CRC and correlate it with disease outcomes.MethodsTumor tissues were obtained from 84 Saudi CRC patients, and their disease data and tumor characteristics were recorded. The MutS homolog 2 (MSH2) gene was analyzed for molecular underlying pathogenic variants, and various bioinformatic approaches were utilized to predict the lethality of these variants and their association with cancer pathogenicity.ResultsA total of 17 variants in four CRC types were identified in 14 patients of whom two were novel missense variations, c.2312 C > G (p.Ala771Gly) and c.2390 T > G (p. Val797Gly). The p.Ala771Gly variant was detected in five individuals (5.95% allelic frequency), while the p.Val797Gly variant was found in three patients (3.57% allelic frequency). Further analysis using the Have (y)Our Protein Explained (HOPE) tool predicted that these variants may deleteriously affect MSH2 protein‐DNA and ATP binding, resulting in functional damage to MSH2, which impairs its functions and protein‐binding affinities.ConclusionThis study provides new insights into the genetic basis of CRC and highlights the importance of molecular analysis for detecting novel variants. These novel mutations can be therapeutic targets for novel drugs in precision medicine.

Introduction

Cancer has become a significant global health concern, with increasing incidence and mortality rates, and is the leading cause of death worldwide [1, 2]. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major health concern in Saudi Arabia. The incidence of CRC is on the rise in the KSA and is the most common cancer in Saudi males (ASR 26.2 per 100,000) and the third most common cancer in Saudi females (ASR 17.8 per 100,000) and it is on the rise in both genders in various regions of KSA [3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Agency for Research in Cancer (IARC), CRC is the most common type of cancer in Saudi Arabia (14.4%). Males accounted for 19.3% and females accounted for 9.2% of all cancer cases reported in 2020. It is also the leading cause of death (15%) among all cancer types [4].

CRC is mostly associated with advanced age. The Median age at the time of diagnosis in Saudi Arabia is 59 years for males and 57 years for females [3]. CRC is a heterogeneous disease [5], and various factors influence its incidence, such as the type of gene defect, sex, physical activity level, tobacco use, and body mass index [6].

Although major advancements have been made in the diagnosis and treatment of cancer, drug resistance and cancer recurrence are key unresolved issues that lead to poor survival [7]. Consequently, further research is required to identify novel strategies for cancer treatment and improving survival.

DNA mismatch repair (MMR) gene defects are a major cause of CRC [8]. The primary function of the DNA MMR pathway is to identify base pair mismatches that may occur during DNA recombination and replication and repair them to ensure genomic stability and integrity. Approximately 15% of all CRC cases are caused by MMR gene defects [9]. The four major mutated genes corresponding to genomic instability are MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 [10].

MSH2 plays a crucial role in CRC, particularly in hereditary forms such as Lynch syndrome (LS). As a key component of the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) system, MSH2 maintains genomic stability by correcting replication errors [11]. Germline variants in MSH2 are strongly associated with LS, accounting for approximately 40% of all MMR gene variants in hereditary non‐polyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC) cases [12]. Interestingly, MSH2 variants are associated with a “classical” LS phenotype and have a higher correlation with extracolonic cancers than other MMR genes [13]. Studies have shown that MSH2‐deficient tumors exhibit microsatellite instability (MSI), a hallmark of MMR deficiency [14]. Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis revealed a lack of MSH2 protein expression in tumors with MSH2 variants, supporting the causative role of these variants in disease development [14]. MSH2 is a critical gene in sporadic and hereditary CRC. Its importance is underscored by the high frequency of MSH2 variants in LS and their associated clinical features. In Saudi Arabia, MSH2 mutations constituted 50% of the pathogenic variations in high‐risk Lynch syndrome cases in the population (15) The identification of MSH2 variants through genetic testing has significant implications for the diagnosis, prognosis, and management of hereditary CRC, highlighting the need for continued research and clinical vigilance in this area. In this study, we aimed to examine MutS homolog gene 2 (MSH2) to decode its involvement in CRC by identifying novel missense variants using Sanger sequencing and determining their role in augmenting pathogenicity using an array of in silico methods.

Methods

Patient Recruitment and Materials

In this study, we examined 84 CRC tumor samples from early‐onset patients below the age of 50 years at the King Abdulaziz University Hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The samples were collected immediately during the surgery of the patients and transported immediately to the laboratory for further processing. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Center of Excellence in Genomic Medicine Research (012‐CEGMR‐ETH‐5). All the participants in the study provided their written informed consent to participate in this study. All relevant clinical information was obtained from the patients' hospital medical records. The samples were then transferred to a molecular biology research laboratory, where genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAGEN DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit.

Mutation Analysis by Sanger Sequencing

The quality and quantity of DNA in each sample were analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop ND‐2000 (Thermo Scientific Inc., USA). The samples were then checked for DNA alterations in MSH2 using an ABI 3500xl sequencer. The resultant chromatogram peaks were analyzed using the BioEdit 7.2 sequence alignment editor.

MSH2 Mutation Analysis by Missense 3D

To predict the structural impact of missense variants identified in CRC samples, we used the crystal structure of the MSH2 complex with MSH6 (PDB ID:2O8E) [15]. We then used the missense3D online program (<http://missense3d.bc.ic.ac.uk/missense3d/>) to obtain structural alterations in MSH2 based on the amino acid substitutions found in patients with CRC [16, 17].

Structural Analysis of Wild‐Type and Mutated Structures of MSH2

The SwissModel Structure Assessment Online program was used to examine the PDB coordinates of the wild‐type and mutant MSH2 structures. The PDB structures were uploaded to the structure assessment page to obtain structural details, including Ramachandran Plots [18, 19, 20], using MolProbity (version 4.4) [21]. The QMEANDisCo method was used to estimate the model quality [22]. Furthermore, we used the CLC Drug Discovery Workbench (version 4.0) (Qiagen, USA) to generate MSH2 proteins with novel variants (p.Aly771Gly and p.Val797Gly).

Re‐assessment of Pathogenicity of SAVs Based on Dynamics (Rhapsody) Missense Mutation Analysis

We utilized the latest version of the Rhapsody interface (<http://rhapsody.csb.pitt.edu>), which features visualization of the output as heat maps. This tool utilizes three types of features: sequence‐based (SEQ), structure‐based (STR), and dynamics‐based (DYN), which are calculated for each position along the sequence and specific amino acid substitutions in UniProt coordinates specified in the search criteria (P43246 V802G, P43246 V797G, P43236 A771G, and P43246 A789G), as previously described [23, 24]. Sequence‐based characteristics were computed using the PolyPhen‐2 server [25, 26], structure‐based features were based on structural data from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), and dynamic features were computed using ProDy API [27]. The most recent version of Rhapsody includes both ‘reduced’ and ‘full’ Rhapsody classifiers [25]. The supplementary and reduced versions incorporated an extra feature known as the BLOSUM62 amino acid substitution score [28] and enhanced dynamic feature computations [25]. Conversely, the complete Rhapsody classifier employs mutation site entropy and co‐evolution characteristics derived from Pfam domains as additional features [25, 29].

MSH2 Mutation Analysis Using Have (Y)Our Protein Explained (HOPE) Tool

The MSH2 variants (p.Val802Gly, p.Val797Gly, Ala771Gly, and Ala789Gly) were subjected to further investigation using the HOPE online tool [30]. Structural information for MSH2 was automatically retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) entry 3THX (<https://www.rcsb.org/structure/3THX>), and the annotations for MSH2 were derived from UniProt entry P43246 (<https://www.uniprot.org/uniprot/P43246>). The HOPE online tool (<https://www3.cmbi.umcn.nl/hope/input/>) was used for mutation analysis, with input supplied for each missense mutation.

MSH2 Mutation Analysis by Mutation Taster

An examination of MSH2 variants (p.Val802Gly, p.Val797Gly, p.Ala789Gly, and p.Ala771Gly) was conducted using the Mutation Taster online tool based on a single‐base exchange in the coding sequence c.2405 T > G, c.2390 T > G, c.2366 C > G, and c.2312 C > G using the Ensembl Transcript ID ENST00000406134.1. In this study, the Grantham Matrix, an amino acid substitution matrix [31], was utilized to evaluate the physicochemical properties of amino acids and provided a score ranging from 0.0 to 215 based on the degree of difference between the wild‐type amino acids (Val and Ala) and the new amino acid (Gly) in MSH2.

Results

Sanger Sequencing

In the present study, we analyzed MSH2 variants in CRC patient samples from Western Saudi Arabia. A total of 84 tumor samples were obtained from the King Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAUH) from patients who underwent surgery. Subsequent analysis using Sanger sequencing revealed four distinct variants in 14 patient samples. Three individuals had double variants. The sequencing results are presented in Tables 1, 2 and Figure 1 shows the chromatogram peaks associated with the novel mutation. The first mutation (c.2312 C > G) resulted in an amino acid change from Alanine to Glycine at position p.771. The second novel mutation (c.2390 T > G) involved the replacement of Valine with Glycine at amino acid position p.797. In addition to these new variants, two previously known variants were identified in this study: p.Ala789Gly, which leads to the substitution of glycine for alanine at amino acid position 789, and p.Val802Gly, which leads to a change from valine to glycine at amino acid position 802. These variants had different frequencies within the study population, providing valuable insights into their prevalence and clinical significance. Three patients with dual MSH2 variants were identified in this study. These double variants were observed in the following combinations: p.Ala771Gly and p.Val797Gly, p.Ala771Gly and p.Val802Gly, and p.Ala789Gly and Val797Gly. Each instance was associated with distinct patient characteristics. The first combination (p.Ala771Gly and p.Val797Gly) was detected in a 26‐year‐old male patient with a right‐sided stage 3 tumor. The second combination (p.Ala771Gly and p.Val802Gly) was observed in a 39‐year‐old female patient with a stage 2 left‐sided tumor. A third combination (p.Ala789Gly and p.Val797Gly) was also identified, although specific patient information was not provided. These observations underscore the occurrence of multiple MSH2 variants in individual patients, which may have implications for disease progression and therapeutic approaches.

The p.Ala789Gly variant was the most prevalent of the four, with an allelic frequency of 8.33%. This high frequency suggests that this particular variant warrants further investigation, as it may have a greater impact on the genetic landscape of the population and associated health outcomes. The p.Ala771Gly variant was common and observed in 5.95% of the study participants. This moderate frequency suggests that although the p.Ala771Gly variant is less common than p.Ala789Gly, it represents a significant genetic change in the studied cohort. The p.Val797Gly variant had an allelic frequency of 3.57%, ranking third among the four variants in terms of prevalence. Although it is less common than the aforementioned variants, its presence in the population deserves attention and may contribute to the overall genetic diversity observed in MSH2. The least common variant identified in this study was p.Val802Gly, with an allelic frequency of 2.38%. Despite its low frequency, the presence of this variant contributes to the complexity of the genetic profile observed in the study population and may have important implications for individual carriers. The cumulative data from this study showed that approximately one‐fifth (20.23%) of the participants carried at least one MSH2 variant. The identified MSH2 variants were localized to exon 14. This indicates that these variants are located in a hotspot region of the MSH2 gene, which is important for gene function and can contribute to the development of CRC.

MSH2 Missense Mutation Analysis by In Silico Methods

To investigate the impact of single nucleotide variations (SNVs) on the MSH2/MSH6 complex in patients with CRC, we utilized the X‐ray diffraction structure of the complex (PDB ID: 2O8E) (Figure 2A) and a missense 3D tool to analyze the effects of various variants, including p.Val802Gly, p.Val797Gly, p.Ala771Gly, and p.Ala789Gly. Figure 2B shows the MSH2 protein with the two novel variants, p.Ala771Gly and p.Val797Gly, near the ATP‐binding site. Missense3D analysis, a tool for predicting the structural effects of amino acid substitutions, revealed that the p.Val802Gly mutation is likely to cause significant structural damage to the MSH2 protein. This damage is primarily due to changes in the structure of the protein cavity and the exposed/buried status of specific amino acid residues. Interestingly, the other three variants (p.Val797Gly, p.Ala771Gly, and p.Ala789Gly) showed no evidence of structural damage according to this analysis.

The Swiss‐Model Structure Assessment Tool was used to compare the structures of mutant proteins with those of the wild‐type MSH2 protein. This analysis revealed slight discrepancies in the Qualitative Model Energy Analysis (QMEAN) and clash scores between the mutant and wild‐type structures. However, no significant differences were observed in other key structural metrics, such as Ramachandran plots and MolProbity scores (Figure 3). These results suggest that the variants may cause subtle structural changes but do not lead to major perturbations in the overall conformation of the protein.

SwissModel Structure Assessment. Structural assessment of wild‐type and mutant protein structures obtained from missense 3D analysis showed a slight variation in the QMEAN score (p.Val802Gly: −2.94, p.Val797Gly: −2.88, p.Ala771Gly: −2.95, and p.Ala789Gly: −2.88) and Clash Score (p.Val802Gly: 11.92, p.Val797Gly:11.99, p.Ala771Gly:11.99, and p.Ala789Gly: 11.84) compared to the wild‐type MSH2 structure (QMEAN score: −2.87 and Clash Score: 11.83).

In addition, we generated a heat map of the amino acid sequence (740–840) of MSH2 (Figure 4E). The Rhapsody analysis tool provides probability scores that indicate the likelihood of each mutation being pathogenic. The HOPE analysis tool provides detailed predictions of the physicochemical properties of mutated residues and their potential effects on protein function. Analysis revealed that all four variants incorporated a smaller, more hydrophobic glycine residue than the original amino acid. This change could potentially disrupt the rigidity of the protein structure, thereby affecting the normal cellular function of MSH2. In addition, HOPE analysis predicted that these variants could impair both DNA and ATP binding to the MSH2 protein, indicating a possible mechanism by which these variants could impair the function of the protein in DNA mismatch repair. Mutation Taster analysis tool was used to predict the functional consequences of the identified variants. This analysis revealed that the variants p.Val802Gly and p.Val797Gly are likely to have a greater impact on the MSH2 protein structure than the variants p.Ala771Gly and p.Ala789Gly. This finding is consistent with the results of the Missense3D analysis, which also identified the V802G mutation as potentially more deleterious (Table 3).

Discussion

MSH2, a key component of the DNA MMR complex, plays a crucial role in regulating the ATR/Chk2/p53 signal transduction pathway during DNA damage repair, promoting apoptosis, and preventing the accumulation of DNA damage [32, 33]. In addition, studies have found that MSH2 deficiency may result in accelerated telomere shortening in human cells and a strong association with autophagy pathways [34, 35]. Tumor cell lines with defective MSH2 are also more susceptible to developing tumors through the initiation of insertion or deletion mutations linked to double‐stranded DNA break repair, as they lose the capacity to repair double‐stranded breaks in plasmid DNA by homologous recombination [36]. Furthermore, missense mutations in MSH2 can cause splicing defects and mis regulate the incidence and development of tumors in a tissue‐specific manner [37].

MSH2 variants were detected in 14 of the 84 patient samples, consistent with previous reports of MMR gene defects in approximately 15% of all CRC cases. This concordance with the existing literature lends credibility to our results and emphasizes the significance of MSH2 variants in CRC pathogenesis. The observed frequency underscores the necessity of genetic screening in patients with CRC to identify potential hereditary factors. In this study, we identified four missense variants involved in CRC development. The first mutation was characterized by a change from alanine to glycine at position 771 (p.Aal771Gly), whereas the second mutation was characterized by a change from alanine to glycine at position 789 (p.Ala789Gly). The third mutation was a conversion from valine to glycine at amino acid position 797 (p.Val797Gly), and the fourth and final variant involved the substitution of valine with glycine at amino acid position 802 (p.Val802Gly). The first and third variants were novel and have not been previously reported.

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are single base pair changes in DNA that can occur in both germline and somatic cells. These changes can result in the creation of new codons, which may then translate into a different amino acid than the wild type, resulting in a single amino acid variation (SAV) or missense variant [24, 25]. However, synonymous SNPs do not affect the protein sequence [25]. In this study, we identified missense variants in MSH2 in patients with CRC (p.Val802Gly, p.Val797Gly, p.Ala771Gly, and p.Ala789Gly). When Valine is substituted with glycine at amino acid position 802, Missense3D predicts structural damage in MSH2 [17, 18]. Although Ramachandran Plots revealed no abnormalities in the MSH2 mutants reported in patients with CRC, QMEAN values (global and local absolute quality estimations) revealed a difference between wild‐type MSH2 and the mutants.

Rhapsody utilizes elastic network models, which distinguishes it from industry‐standard tools such as PolyPhen‐2, SIFT, and CADD [24, 25, 38]. In the analysis of SAVs detected in patients with CRC, Rhapsody revealed PolyPhen‐2 and EV mutation scores [26, 38]. PolyPhen‐2 employs a supervised naïve Bayes classifier trained on annotations, conservation scores, and structural characteristics that describe the amino acid substitutions. However, EV variants predict the fitness of mutant protein structures through coevolution analysis [38].

HOPE is a web‐based program that analyzes the structural effects of point variants in protein sequences [32]. In patients with CRC, missense variants were detected at positions 802, 797, 771, and 789 in MSH2, resulting in glycine insertion. The mutant residue was smaller and more hydrophobic than the wild‐type residue. Glycines are highly flexible and can disrupt the stiffness of proteins at these locations, potentially interfering with normal cellular functions.

HOPE also predicted that SAVs observed in patients with CRC would affect the binding of both DNA and ATP to the MSH2 protein. A Mutation Taster study further revealed that p.Val802Gly and p.Val797Gly SAVs were predicted to have a greater impact on the structure of MSH2 than p.Ala771Gly and p.Ala789Gly SAVs.

The utilization of various bioinformatics tools to predict the structural effects of these variants is consistent with current trends in mutational analysis. This structural analysis approach provides valuable insights into the potential functional consequences of these variants and may serve as a guide for future experimental studies. The prediction that these variants can impair MSH2 protein‐DNA and ATP binding is consistent with other studies and underscores the critical role of MSH2 in DNA mismatch repair and the maintenance of genomic stability. These findings contribute to our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying CRC development and may support the development of targeted therapies and diagnostic approaches. The study′s focus on patients from Western Saudi Arabia provides valuable data on the genetics of CRC in this population, which can be compared with studies from other regions to elucidate geographic variations in the mutation patterns. This regional specificity is particularly important for understanding the genetic basis of CRC in different populations and may have implications for personalized medicine.

MSH2 variants play a significant role in CRC development, particularly in LS cases, both in Saudi Arabia and globally. In Saudi Arabia, a comprehensive study of 807 CRC cases identified eight patients (0.99%) with mismatch repair (MMR) germline variants, including MSH2, who were microsatellite instability‐high (MSI‐H) and lacked BRAF variants [39]. Another study focusing on high‐risk LS cases in Saudi Arabia found that four out of eight pathogenic variants were in the MSH2 gene, suggesting its importance in LS‐related CRC in the Saudi population [40]. In the International Collaborative Group on Hereditary Nonpolyposis Colorectal Cancer (ICG‐HNPCC) database, MSH2 variants account for 39% of all DNA mismatch repair gene variants in Lynch syndrome family members [41]. In Hong Kong, a study of young Chinese CRC patients found germline variants in MSH2 or MLH1 in more than 80% of cases under 31 years of age [42]. Similarly, in Turkey, a novel MSH2 c.2210 G > A mutation was identified in five Lynch syndrome families, suggesting a potential founder mutation for the Turkish population [43]. Interestingly, some studies have revealed contradictory findings. In South Africa′s Northern Cape province, where CRC incidence is low, 21.8% of cancers were deficient in hMLH1 or hMSH2, which is higher than the rates reported in high‐incidence areas [44]. In Nigeria, despite a small sample size, 23% of the tested CRC cases showed a loss of MMR proteins [45]. These findings suggest that MSH2 variants may play a more significant role in CRC development in populations with a lower overall CRC incidence.

In Saudi Arabia high rates of consanguinity may also influence variant allele frequencies and contribute more to hereditary CRC patterns. Several studies shown the consanguineous marriages in Saudi Arabia is very common around 56‐57.7%, and particularly first cousin made up approximately 28.4% to 33.6% [46, 47]. Therefore, consequently, consanguinity together with relatively large family sizes and tribal structures, led to elevated prevalence of autosomal disorders in Saudi Arabia and a nontrivial rate of homozygosity burden in the local population [48]. This attributed to the pathogenic variants around 30‐40% found as founder variants i.e. inherited from a common ancestor and clustered in specific tribe or population [49]. The same founder effect phenomena are also found in other highly conservative populations such as Ashkenazi Jews, in addition to middle east population groups. In Ashkenazi Jews a founder mutation in MSH2 gene was discovered (1906G > C), (proline for alanine at codon 636) in multiple unrelated Lynch syndrome families [50]. Other studies also shown increased frequency of MMR and tumor suppressor genes from ~12.4% to ~20.8%, depending upon gene panels [51].

Recent studies have shown that the tumors with defective DNA repair e.g. homologous recombination deficiency, are often sensitive to Poly (ADP‐ribose) Polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. Almost 15% of CRC may harbor homologous recombination pathway defects called as “BRCAness,” resulting in candidates for PARP inhibition [52]. Earlier works also explored that MMR‐deficient tumors (d‐MMR; MSH2 gene loss or other MMR genes loss) linked to result in sensitization of PARP inhibitors, especially in case of base excision repair (BER) or other repair pathways defects [53]. Therefore, it concludes that the loss of MSH2 or other MMR prevents recognition of mismatches that results in elevating burden on DNA repair pathways and hence inhibits PARP or BER, overwhelms the cancer cell, leading to cytotoxicity [53]. In addition, MSH2 loss results in defective MMR and BER pathways resulting in high mutational burden in microsatellites, leading to change in DNA sequences and neoantigen generation, results in tumor microenvironment rich in neoantigens and tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes, making tumors often sensitive to immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti‐PD‐1 [54]. Consequently, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) e.g. anti‐PD‐1 has changed the management of dMMR patients, resulting in outcomes improvements in some patients [55]. However, a substantial number of patients (around 40‐70%) still don′t respond to these ICBs [54]. A latest study has shown that a MSI‐H CRC patient with germline MSH2 mutation didn′t respond to anti‐PD‐1 therapy [56]. The reason for this therapy resistance may be possibly due to immune escape mechanisms, tumor heterogeneity, altered neoantigen processing and/or activation of alternate signaling pathways [54]. Further, more data suggests that all MMR genes mutations are not equivalent in influence on immune related phenotypes and may differentially regulated immune related checkpoints such as PD‐L1. A latest case study reported that deletion of MSH2 (along with MLH1 and PMS2) increased PD‐L1 expression in cancer cells, while MSH6 didn′t influence the same increase [57]. In conclusion, the MSH2 loss of function due to novel mutation even results in generating MSI‐H or dMMR and may results in neoantigen load and makes tumor a good candidate for ICB, still due to the large proportions of non‐responders to therapy, functional validation is crucial. Because some MSH2 novel variants could results in complete MMR loss or may provoke immune escape pathway regardless of high mutational burden. Consequently, simply detecting a defective mutation is insufficient and may require to further assess neoantigen load, PD‐L1k expression, immune cell infiltration etc. Therefore, functional characterization using cell and animal models and prospective translational work (larger cohort studies and early phase trials) are essential to validate if these variants truly confer therapeutic importance. Ultimately, fully integrated approaches in precision‐oncology frameworks are needed with genomic, transcriptomic and immunological biomarkers approaches to develop fully effective therapy for CRC patients with MSH2 variants.

In addition, understanding the functional consequences of these variants could aid in the development of targeted therapies or in predicting responses to current treatments. For instance, if variants reduce DNA repair efficiency, patients with these variants may respond differently to chemotherapeutic agents that depend on DNA damage. Clinically, identifying novel variants highlights the necessity for continuous updates to genetic tests for CRC screening, ensuring that clinicians have access to the most current genetic information for patient risk assessment and treatment decisions. By linking specific genetic alterations to clinical outcomes, these studies enhance our ability to predict disease risk, progression, and treatment response based on the genetic profile. Our study exemplifies the significance of interdisciplinary approaches in cancer research by combining molecular biology, bioinformatics, and clinical data for comprehensive results. In precision medicine, these findings have advanced personalized treatment approaches for CRC. Hence, MSH2 variants are significant contributors to CRC risk, particularly in LS cases, both in Saudi Arabia and worldwide. The identification of these variants is crucial for implementing effective screening and prevention strategies and guiding personalized treatment approaches for patients with CRC. Hence, MSH2 variants are prevalent in patients with CRC worldwide, with variations observed across different populations. The high frequency of MSH2 variants in young patients and populations with lower CRC incidence highlights the importance of genetic testing and screening, especially in these groups of patients. Further research is needed to fully understand the global distribution and impact of MSH2 variants on CRC development.

Limitations

The study has some key study constraints such as the relatively small sample size (n = 84), the absence of germline testing to confirm somatic versus inherited variants, and the focus solely on MSH2 without screening other MMR genes, which may influence the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified 17 variants of four different types in Saudi patients with CRC, including two novel MSH2 mutations that contribute to CRC. Our study results suggest that these variants, particularly the two novel variants, may contribute to CRC development by impairing the function and interactions of MSH2 protein. Changes in protein structure and potential disruption of DNA and ATP binding ability can affect the overall structure and function, leading to defects in DNA mismatch repair. This, in turn, can lead to increased genomic instability, promoting the development and progression of CRC. Thus, these findings not only expand our knowledge of the genetic basis of CRC but also have potential implications for personalized medicine approaches in cancer treatment. Understanding the specific variants in individual tumors could facilitate the development of targeted therapies and improve patient outcomes. In addition, this study underscores the importance of further research on the genetic basis of cancer, as it shows that new variants with potential clinical significance are still being discovered. Further functional validation studies on these variants in vivo models could provide more conclusive evidence to support these findings. It is crucial to continue research in this area to better understand the underlying causes of colorectal cancer and to develop effective treatments for this disease. In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the development of CRC, particularly in tumors with MSI. The discovery of novel variants, combined with a comprehensive analysis of their potential impact, provides valuable insights that may ultimately contribute to improved diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic strategies for patients with CRC.
