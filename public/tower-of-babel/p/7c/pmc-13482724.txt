COVID-19 infections and cognitive impairment: mechanisms and perspectives
Authors: Charlotte Wikenhauser, Theis Skovsgaard Itenov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe viral respiratory infections have gained attention in recent years. One aspect is the cognitive impairment that many patients experience either during or following infections, such as COVID-19. The aim of this Journal club is to present and discuss different studies on this topic in the hope of proposing future research questions. The four studies discussed all investigate COVID-19 patients in observational cohorts and the possible association between severe viral respiratory infection and cognitive impairment. Needham et al. and Born et al. investigate the immunological signature of COVID-19 patients to assess dysregulated immune and inflammatory responses and the association with specific biomarkers and delirium diagnosis, respectively. To supplement biochemical associations, Maamar et al. investigated agitation in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, while Aarskog et al. designed a study to investigate whether acute hypoxia and hypocapnia could increase the risk of delirium.The studies differ substantially regarding outcomes and methods, and are therefore not entirely comparable. They do, however, exemplify the need for a broader perspective in studies investigating complex problems. Future studies might therefore benefit from combining approaches to better elucidate the full physiological pathway, and not just simplify it or isolate potential causality.

Context

Patients with severe acute respiratory infections such as COVID-19 often experience neurological impairment and delirium [1]. This adversely impacts patient outcomes both during hospitalisation and following discharge [2, 3]. The neuropsychiatric syndrome, delirium, is characterised by an acute change in cognition and arousal and is known to be associated with several factors, such as disease severity, sedation, immobilisation and mechanical ventilation [4, 5]. This complicates the identification of distinct pathophysiological mechanisms that could serve as therapeutic targets. Therefore, little is known about the specific pathophysiological connection between severe viral respiratory infections and delirium, which likely contributes to the paucity of treatments available.

Respiratory failure and a pronounced systemic immune response are hallmarks of COVID-19. Both mechanisms are strongly suspected to contribute to the risk of delirium and cognitive impairment [6]. Respiratory failure, through hypoxia, treatment with mechanical ventilation and the systemic immune response, may fuel neuroinflammation. The homogenous presentation and microbial agent (i.e. COVID-19) among patients in these studies opens the opportunity to elucidate how each pathway contributes to the development of neuroinflammation and delirium in a previously unprecedented way.

The aim of this journal club is to examine four different approaches to the possible association between respiratory failure and cognitive impairment in patients with a systemic immune response due to COVID-19. Each study provides insight that exemplifies how further research could tackle the interplay between respiratory illness and neurological deficit in patients with COVID-19.

Needham et al. [7]

This prospective cohort study by Needham et al. [7] investigated 122 patients with COVID-19 at Cambridge University Hospital, UK, 53 COVID-19 patients from Sahlgrenska University Hospital, Sweden as well as 59 age-matched healthy controls and 45 patients with influenza, both from Cambridge University Hospital, UK, to assess specific biomarkers of brain injury. The primary outcome was biomarkers of inflammatory brain injury. They observed levels of neurofilament light (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and total tau, as well as markers of dysregulated host response, autoantibody production and cytokine profiles such as lung surfactant proteins. Serum was sampled at up to three time points from admission: acute (0–14 days), subacute (15–70 days) and convalescent (outpatient follow-up >80 days) to assess the association between illness severity and immunological profile.

Born et al. [8]

This retrospective cohort study by Born et al. [8] used high-dimensional immunophenotyping of cytokines, chemokines and growth factors along with immune cell profiling via mass cytometry (CyTOF) to investigate delirium in COVID-19 patients at Lausanne University Hospital, Switzerland. They compared 62 patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) due to COVID-19 in the intensive care unit (ICU) with 55 non-ICU patients with COVID-19 and 450 healthy individuals to investigate the association between delirium and potential delineating peripheral markers. The primary outcome was delirium assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method of the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) and Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS).

Maamar et al. [9]

This prospective cohort study by Maamar et al. [9] investigated 241 patients admitted to the ICU at Rennes University Hospital, France with ARDS and either laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 or influenza. Clinical and biological data were collected to assess agitation, delirium or pain during ICU stay using RASS, Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS) and/or numeric rating scale (NRS). Patient characteristics that were previously known to be contributing factors for agitation, such as length of stay, mechanical ventilation, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score or sedative and opioid use, were collected. The primary outcome was prevalence of agitation in both patient groups. COVID-19 and influenza patients were compared using propensity score matching, with scores calculated via multivariable logistic regression using age, body mass index (BMI), ileus duration, mechanical ventilation, sedation duration, cumulative doses of midazolam, propofol and morphine, Glasgow Coma Scale at admission and day-28 survival. The study also assessed whether agitation was independently associated with mortality.

Aarskog et al. [10]

This prospective cohort study by Aarskog et al. [10] investigated 126 patients with COVID-19 admitted to Oslo University Hospital, Norway to assess whether arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) and arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2) could increase the risk of delirium in patients hospitalised with acute hypoxia. The primary outcome was delirium during admission retrospectively assessed using the DSM-5 criteria (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, 5th edition). PaCO2 and SaO2 was measured in arterial blood obtained and immediately analysed upon hospital admission. The study collected data on other factors contributing to both hypocapnia, hypoxia and delirium at admission and during hospitalisation.

Needham et al. [7] investigated 122 patients with COVID-19 from Cambridge (median age: 50 (interquartile range (IQR): 31–62) years, female: 62 (51%)), and 53 COVID-19 patients from Gothenburg (median age: 51 (IQR: 35–59) years, female: 20 (38%)) compared with 59 age-matched healthy patients (median age: 50 (IQR: 32–62) years, female: 38 (65%)) and 45 patients with influenza infection (median age: 44 (IQR: 30–50) years, female: 22 (49%)). The populations were comparable, although there was a smaller proportion of females in the patient group when pooling COVID-19 and influenza patients and comparing them to healthy individuals (82 (47%) versus 39 (65%), p=0.02). Evaluating disease severity in the Cambridge COVID-19 patients based on the need for either no oxygen support, oxygen support or invasive mechanical ventilation, 70 (57%) had mild disease, 31 (25%) moderate and 21 (17%) severe disease. In the COVID-19 population from Gothenburg 0 (0%) had mild disease, 41 (77%) moderate disease and 12 (23%) severe disease. When measuring makers of acute brain injury (ABI), the study found that increasing levels of both NfL and GFAP was associated with increasing severity of COVID-19 in patients at acute and subacute timepoints, but not at convalescent measurements. Both NfL and GFAP levels generally decreased over time, while total tau showed elevations over time independent of COVID-19 infection severity, as a sign of a secondary proinflammatory process. Equivalent results were found when investigating patients with influenza. When analysing several cytokines and antibodies, the study found a broad autoantibody response in both COVID-19 and influenza patients that was not related to any specific brain-targets, but instead suggesting a seemingly more dysregulated immunological response.

Born et al. [8] investigated 62 patients admitted to ICU with COVID-19-associated ARDS. Assessing patients using a CAM-ICU, 39 (63%) patients developed delirium with a median duration of 3 days (IQR: 2–5 days). Baseline patient characteristics were comparable between patients with delirium (median age: 60 (IQR: 56–65) years, female: 7 (18%)) and without delirium (median age: 66 (IQR: 60–74) years, female: 10 (43%)). When investigating factors known to be associated with delirium the results support previous studies: delirium was associated with longer invasive mechanical ventilation, higher doses of sedative and analgesic drugs (including midazolam, propofol and fentanyl), and lower arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/inspired oxygen fraction (FIO2) ratio, as well as both a longer duration of coma (defined as RASS of −5 or −4) and longer ICU stay.

The study additionally investigated immune signatures in ICU COVID-19 patients compared to non-ICU COVID-19 patients and healthy controls and identified a set of soluble serum markers associated with both ICU admission and disease severity that included elevated CXCL1, CCL11, CXCL13 and VEGF-A, as well as reduced IL-1a, IL-21 and IL-22, aligning with previous findings. The study found that immunological markers signalling disease severity were different from markers associated with development of delirium. These markers were not affected by administration of steroids during admission with exception of CXCL13 and VEGF-A. Lastly, the study developed four different logistic regression models using L2 regularisation (ridge regression) to test prognostic value of immune signatures for delirium. They found that a model using only 12 key markers had a final area under curve (AUC) of 0.95, a sensitivity of 93%, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 89% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 86%, all indicating that these specific markers provide a predictive power compared to, for example, immunophenotyping or more elaborate markers.

Maamar et al. [9] included 241 mechanically ventilated ARDS ICU patients: 146 COVID-19 patients (median age: 65 (IQR: 55–72) years, female: 147 (32%)) and 95 propensity score-matched influenza patients (median age 59 (IQR: 51–65) years, female: 36 (38%)). The two populations were similar overall in terms of baseline characteristics, although the COVID-19 population had more diabetes, hypertension and obesity. When comparing COVID-19 and influenza-matched patients after propensity score analysis, more COVID-19 patients experienced agitation (52 (54.7%) versus 32 (33.7%, p=0.006)). However, there was no difference between the two groups regarding sedation used, ICU-length of stay, duration of mechanical ventilation or Glasgow Coma Scale at admission. Propensity score-matched multivariable analysis revealed a significantly higher 28-day survival rate in the COVID-19 cohort compared to the influenza cohort (133 (91.1%) versus 77 (81.1%, p=0.038). This analysis also identified that agitation was independently associated with increased mortality (adjusted hazard ratio (HR) 1.85, 95% CI 1.37–2.49; p<0.001).

Aarskog et al. [10] included 126 COVID-19 patients in their study. At some point during hospitalisation delirium was experienced by 29 patients (23%) and of these, 12 patients (10%) had delirium at discharge. Patients with delirium (median age: 74 (IQR: 32–93) years, female: 8 (28%)) generally had higher age, Clinical Frailty Score, Charlson Comorbidity Index and higher presence of polypharmacy when compared to patients who did not develop delirium (median age 55 (IQR: 24–94) years, female: 45 (46%)). Additionally, patients with delirium had greater respiratory challenges at admission compared to patients without delirium, by having lower oxygen saturation, lower PaO2/FIO2 ratio and higher respiratory rate. The study found that a decrease in PaCO2 was significantly associated with increased risk of delirium (risk ratio (RR) 1.67, 95% CI 1.09–2.54; p=0.017 per 1 kPa decrease in PaCO2) after adjusting for SaO2. This association remained significant even when adjusting for known predisposing factors.

Commentary

Delirium and signs of neurological distress are frequently reported as some of the most severe and traumatic experiences for patients and their next of kin, and are associated with increased mortality and morbidity [11]. This warrants investigation of possible malicious pathways that potentially could be interrupted via targeted treatment. The four studies presented in this Journal club point to important implications. First, the pathways involved in the risk of neurological impairment may depend on the cause of respiratory distress (i.e. influenza versus COVID-19). Second, the respiratory insufficiency per se may aggravate neurological impairment. Third, the pronounced immunological response to COVID-19 seems to distinctly precipitate neuroinflammation.

These four studies investigate possible associations between the neurological and respiratory failure in COVID-19 patients. All four studies are observational cohort studies, but other than that methodology and focus varies. Maamar et al. [9] investigate agitation as a marker of neurological distress in ICU patients. In the absence of more direct measurements of cognition and consciousness in sedated, mechanically ventilated ICU patients, assessment of agitation using, for example, RASS acts as a proxy for neurological status and arousal [11]. In ICU patients with either COVID-19 or influenza, Maamar et al. [9] found that agitation is more common in COVID-19 patients, and associated with increased mortality. Needham et al. focuses on brain injury markers such as cytokines and immunological biomarkers. This approach considers how the inflammatory signature, and potential dysregulated immune response, caused by systemic infections, affect brain function. Needham et al. [7] found an association between serum markers of acute brain injury (NfL and GFAP), antibodies (e.g. anti-myelin associated glycoprotein (MAG), anti-surfactant protein A (SFTPA1)) and cytokines (interleukin (IL)-6, tumour necrosis factor-α and IL-10) suggesting that both the innate and the adaptive immune system are in play in the dysregulated immunological response. These results align with Born et al. [8] who focus on delirium and biomarkers. They found strong associations between specific cytokines and chemokines (e.g. decreased IL-22, IL-21, IL-1α and increased CXCL1, CCL11, CXCL13) and delirium. While Needham et al. [7] focus on biomarkers, Born et al. [8] bring additional clinical outcomes into play, with analysis of ICU admissions and disease severity. Born et al. [8] found that specific cytokines, growth factors and chemokines are correlated with clinical end-points including 30-day mortality, illness severity at admission and coma duration, as well as biomarkers such as CXCL10, IL-22, IL-21 and IL-1α showing a negative association with increased need for mechanical ventilation. This indicates that some factors might not contribute directly to development of delirium, but instead act as mediators of disease.

Besides the immunological reaction to systemic infections such as COVID-19, these patients also experience significant respiratory challenges such as hypoxia and hypocapnia. Aarskog et al. [10] investigated how PaCO2 affects risk of delirium, thereby providing a more clinical approach. They found that patients with low PaCO2 during admission have increased risk of delirium throughout hospitalisation, even after adjusting for factors known to increase the risk of delirium. This provides interesting information, since PaCO2 levels and cognitive impairment have previously been heavily discussed. On one hand, hypercapnia has been associated with cerebral vasodilation and increased intracranial pressure. Hypocapnia, on the other hand, imposes cerebral vasoconstriction and potential ischaemia both potentially leading to neurological dysfunction through different pathways [12].

In summary, these studies find that patients with COVID-19 experience more neurological impairment than other respiratory patients, with a more pronounced immunological response and neuroinflammation. However, the observed brain injury also occurs in influenza patients, suggesting that the acute brain injury is not unique to COVID-19, but rather to severe viral infections with pronounced immunological responses.

All four studies focus on ICU patients, many of whom receive respiratory support, such as invasive mechanical ventilation. Mechanical ventilation is a known risk factor for delirium, although the mechanisms behind this have not been definitively demonstrated [13]. Given that delirium has become a core outcome after ICU admission, a deeper understanding is critical for improving long-term prognosis [14]. Very little is known regarding the pathophysiology of delirium in general, and in the ICU the causes are potentially numerous. Understanding these complex pathways is essential for developing potential therapeutic regimens to mitigate the adverse outcomes [15].

COVID-19 patients are the focus of all four studies. This gives great insight into a specific group of patients, and although some studies compare them to, for example, influenza patients, it is unclear how these outcomes compare to other diseases. Pneumonia caused by bacterial infection can also cause ARDS and delirium, and recent meta-analysis supports that delirium in pneumonia patients might be driven by immune and inflammatory responses, and not the infectious agent [16]. In fact, Needham et al. [7] also suggest that the biomarker NfL might be even more associated with pneumonia than with COVID-19 and causing increased inflammation and delirium in both groups. Despite this, the methodology varies when evaluating immunological signatures, complicating comparisons between studies.

Current research imposes some limitations. COVID-19 patients were widely studied, especially in the first year after the condition emerged in 2020. Treatment of these patients was therefore of variable quality, and results might be different today as treatments have improved. This includes the use of immunosuppressives. Future research might therefore benefit from using new cohorts, or even comparing different multicentre cohorts, to ensure greater validity of results. The four studies also have different methods of assessing neurological dysfunction, some utilising retrospective delirium diagnosis and others measuring markers of brain injury or inflammation.

All four studies also have several individual limitations. Needham et al. [7] included patients from a hospital in the UK as well as a convenience sample from a Swedish hospital, which limits representation and introduces selection bias. The COVID-19 patient cohort is also not very large, and some phenotypes may therefore be overrepresented. Additionally, the study is missing some autoantibody and cytokine data reducing power. Born et al. [8] retrospectively investigated a single centre cohort, which limits power and external validity. The cohort used by Maamar et al. [9] is also an observational, single centre cohort. In addition, while the COVID-19 patients were recorded from 2020 to 2021, influenza patients were recorded from 2006 to 2020, meaning there were possibly large changes in delirium prevention practices over the large inclusion period. Aarskog et al. [10] included patients and measured their arterial blood gas levels but report notable amounts of missing data regarding, for example, PaCO2. In some patients where arterial sampling was not possible, the venous sample was registered instead. This introduces a risk of bias and limits representation.

Implications for practice

Although the need for better treatment and prevention of delirium is evident, the pathway towards this is not entirely clear. Proposing different possible pathophysiology, the current research implies that inflammation, respiratory failure and hypocapnia play a role. This becomes important when looking for future innovative treatment options. While all efforts are already focused on providing sufficient oxygen, targeting hypocapnia in the early phase of disease has not been evaluated. Additionally, knowledge of biomarkers and the evident immunological dysregulation provides important perspectives on future research and treatment options. Inflammation has been the focus of intense research over the years of the COVID-19 pandemic, and indeed steroid treatment seems to reduce mortality. Nevertheless, the effect on delirium has not been reported which provides interesting new perspectives.

It is necessary to consider the question of potential causality, and whether both inflammation and hypocapnia are not direct causes of the neurological deterioration but might in fact be part of a complex physiology as yet unknown. This can, however, only be investigated in properly sized randomized trials. Through the research provided by these four projects, it becomes evident that there is not one clear approach to this problem. Instead of a narrow silver bullet perspective, on a very complex problem, future research could benefit from approaching the problem of delirium more broadly and not aiming to simplify complex problems. Delirium is a syndrome that can be caused by a multitude of pathophysiology, even in patients with the same disease. It is likely that any future successful treatment for delirium is a multifactorial intervention aimed at the most prevalent cause of delirium in the individual patient. For some this might be inflammation, others hypocapnia or possibly a third factor that is still unknown. Therefore, future research could benefit from a more broad approach with focus on comparability and translational potential instead of the current more narrow-minded focus.
