Comparison of the Efficacy of Ciprofol and Propofol for Rapid Sequence Induction and Intubation in Elective Non-Cardiac Surgery: A Prospective, Randomized, Non-Inferiority Trial
Authors: Yimei Lin, Junmei Wu, Chengyu Wang, Jingyi Wu, Juanlan Liu, Manman Liu, Changhong Miao, Chao Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid sequence induction and intubation (RSII) requires an agent that provides excellent intubating conditions with minimal hemodynamic disturbance. Propofol is standard but often causes hypotension. We compared the novel agent ciprofol with propofol for RSII in adult patients scheduled for elective non-cardiac surgery.MethodsIn this prospective, randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial, 170 adult patients scheduled for elective non-cardiac surgery were allocated to receive intravenous ciprofol (0.4 mg/kg, n = 85) or propofol (2.0 mg/kg, n = 85) for induction, with remifentanil and rocuronium. The non-inferiority margin was set at –10% for the primary outcome. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients with excellent intubation conditions (Viby-Mogensen scale). Secondary outcomes included incidence of hypotension, induction success rate, time to loss of consciousness (LOC), bispectral index (BIS) values, and pain on injection.ResultsExcellent intubation conditions were achieved in 96.5% (82/85) of the ciprofol group and 95.3% (81/85) of the propofol group (difference 1.2%, 95% CI: –4.4% to 6.7%). The lower limit of the 95% CI exceeded the prespecified non-inferiority margin of –10%. The per-protocol analysis confirmed non-inferiority (difference 1.2%, 95% CI –4.2% to 6.7%). The incidence of post-induction hypotension was significantly lower in the ciprofol group (15.3% vs 43.5%; RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.20 to 0.61; P < 0.001). No patient reported injection pain with ciprofol, compared to 68.2% with propofol (P < 0.001). Time to LOC and induction success rates were similar between groups.ConclusionIn adult patients undergoing elective non-cardiac surgery under a standardized RSI protocol, ciprofol 0.4 mg/kg provided intubating conditions non-inferior to propofol 2.0 mg/kg and was associated with less post-induction hypotension and injection pain. Further studies are needed in emergency, critically ill, and high-risk RSI populations.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500095742).Keywords: ciprofol, propofol, rapid sequence induction and intubation, hemodynamics, hypotension, non-inferiority trial

Introduction

Rapid sequence induction and intubation (RSII) is a technique designed to rapidly induce loss of consciousness (LOC) and achieve optimal tracheal intubation conditions, thereby minimising the duration of an unprotected airway.1 Conventional RSII employs a rapid-onset intravenous induction agent in combination with a neuromuscular blocking agent. While essential for patients at risk of aspiration or requiring emergency intubation, RSII poses inherent risks.2 The abrupt transition to unconsciousness and paralysis imposes substantial hemodynamic stress, often affecting blood pressure before and after intubation.3 Even brief reductions in mean arterial pressure (MAP) are associated with increased risks of myocardial injury, acute kidney injury, postoperative cognitive dysfunction, and mortality.4 Post-induction hypotension (PIH) is particularly concerning, as it is associated with high mortality rates and prolonged ICU and hospital stays.5–7 Therefore, selecting an appropriate sedative is critical to optimise intubation conditions while minimising adverse events.8

Propofol, a widely used phenolic γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor agonist with high lipid solubility, is commonly used for rapid induction during RSII. However, it is associated with adverse effects, including respiratory depression, hemodynamic instability (hypotension and bradycardia), pain on injection, and a narrow therapeutic index.9 These effects are partly attributed to its β-adrenergic and calcium channel-blocking properties. Previous studies indicate that 20–40% of patients receiving propofol for intubation may experience significant hypotension during surgery,10–12 which may exacerbate hemodynamic compromise, particularly in critically ill patients. Consequently, there is a need for alternative sedatives with improved cardiovascular stability during RSII.

Ciprofol is a novel intravenous anaesthetic developed in China and approved in 2020.13 It is a highly potent GABAA receptor agonist, with approximately 4–5 times greater potency than propofol, allowing for lower dosing and potentially fewer adverse effects.14 Clinical studies have demonstrated that ciprofol provides induction efficiency comparable to propofol, with advantages such as improved hemodynamic stability and minimal injection pain.15,16 However, its efficacy and safety profile specifically in the context of RSII—where hemodynamic stability is paramount—have not been extensively evaluated.

Given the limited evidence regarding ciprofol use in RSII, we conducted this non-inferiority study to investigate its effectiveness and safety compared with propofol during RSII. Because propofol is the current standard with established high rates of excellent intubation conditions, a non-inferiority design was chosen to test whether ciprofol could achieve comparable intubation efficacy while potentially offering superior hemodynamic stability and less injection pain. Importantly, this study was conducted in a controlled elective surgical setting, not in emergency or critically ill patients, as a necessary first step before extending to higher-risk populations.

Methods

Ethical approval for this study (approval number: B2024-114R) was provided by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Xiamen Branch, Fudan University (Chairperson: Boheng Zhang) on January 6, 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 2013. This randomized, double-blind, non-inferiority trial enrolled patients scheduled for elective non-cardiac surgery requiring tracheal intubation at Zhongshan Hospital, Xiamen Branch, Fudan University, Shanghai, China, between October 2025 and January 2026. The trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn; ChiCTR2500095742). This study adhered to the CONSORT guidelines for reporting randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria were adult patients aged 18–65 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III, and Mallampati class I–II. Exclusion criteria included respiratory infection or use of steroids or bronchodilators within 2 weeks before enrolment; predicted difficult airway (eg, based on history or physical examination); body mass index ≥35 kg/m2; psychiatric illness precluding cooperation; and known allergy to opioids, propofol, or ciprofol excipients (soybean oil, glycerol, triglycerides, egg lecithin, sodium oleate, and sodium hydroxide).

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number table, with patients allocated in a 1:1 ratio to the propofol group (Group P) or the ciprofol group (Group C). Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared by an independent statistician. The study was double-blinded, with participants and outcome assessors unaware of group assignment. Drug preparation was performed by an anesthesiologist not involved in induction or outcome assessment, using identical syringes covered with opaque tape to mask the appearance of the drugs. Both study drugs were diluted to identical total volume (20 mL) using 0.9% saline. Intubation procedures and evaluations were conducted by a researcher blinded to group allocation.

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring—including 5-lead electrocardiography, pulse oximetry (SpO2), and non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP)—was initiated. Baseline vital signs (mean of two NIBP measurements obtained 2 min apart, heart rate [HR]) were recorded, and bispectral index (BIS) sensors were applied. Both groups underwent RSII according to the classic model,17 consisting of preoxygenation, rapid administration of predetermined induction agent and neuromuscular blocking agent, avoidance of bag-mask ventilation, and tracheal intubation under laryngoscopy. The specific procedural steps were as follows: patients were preoxygenated with 100% oxygen (8 L/min) via a facemask until the end-tidal oxygen concentration reached ≥90%. The induction agent (propofol 2.0 mg/kg or ciprofol 0.4 mg/kg) was administered until LOC, defined as a Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation Scale (MOAA/S)18 score ≤1. If LOC was not attained within 60s, a supplementary bolus of 50% of the initial dose was given. Upon confirmation of LOC, rocuronium 1 mg/kg (a dose chosen for its onset time, which is comparable to succinylcholine at approximately 60s)8 and remifentanil 1 µg/kg were administered in rapid succession. The choice of propofol 2.0 mg/kg was based on its established efficacy for RSII in our prior work.19 Tracheal intubation was performed 60s after rocuronium administration using a video laryngoscope. Jaw thrust and positive-pressure ventilation were strictly avoided during the apnoeic period. Correct endotracheal tube placement was verified by bilateral auscultation and capnography. Subsequently, mechanical ventilation was commenced with initial settings of FiO2 50%, tidal volume 8 mL/kg, respiratory rate 10 breaths/min, and PETCO2 maintained between 35–45 mmHg. Anaesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (0.7–0.8 MAC).

Data were collected by attending anesthesiologists and trained research personnel. On the day of surgery, baseline vital signs (NIBP, HR, and BIS) were recorded upon entering the operating room. During induction, patients were assessed for pain at the injection site, and vital signs were continuously monitored and recorded. The MOAA/S was used to evaluate sedation depth every minute until successful induction (ie, a MOAA/S score ≤1). Tracheal intubation conditions were then assessed, and tracheal intubation was completed. Vital signs were continuously recorded for 5 min after intubation.

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients achieving excellent intubation conditions, assessed using the Viby-Mogensen scale (Table 1),20 which categorises conditions as excellent, good, or poor.

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes included: (1) post-induction hypotension (PIH; ≥20% decrease in MAP from baseline or MAP ≤65 mmHg)21 at pre-intubation (60s post-rocuronium; Tpre), and 1–5 min post-intubation (T1-T5); (2) anesthesia induction success rate (achievement of LOC [MOAA/S ≤1] within 60s without the use of alternative sedatives); (3) time to successful induction (from the start of induction drug administration to LOC); (4) incidence of pain at the injection site; (5) requirement for additional doses of the study drug or alternative medications; (6) mean BIS value during induction (from drug administration to LOC); (7) minimum MAP within 5 min post-intubation, MAP nadir timing, and vasopressor use (defined as MAP <60 mmHg or >30% decrease from baseline requiring ephedrine 6 mg or phenylephrine 50 μg).

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size calculation was based on the LR group reported by Suzuki et al22 the excellent rate on the Viby-Mogensen scale for tracheal intubation with 2.0 mg/kg propofol combined with 1.0 mg/kg rocuronium was 99%. That study reported 100% excellent intubation conditions and used a non-inferiority margin of –10%,19 with a one-sided α of 0.025, 80% power, and a 1:1 allocation ratio. The calculation yielded a total of 170 patients (85 per group). The –10% margin was chosen based on previous literature showing that even a 10% absolute reduction in excellent intubation conditions would not be considered clinically important in elective RSI, as the remaining excellent rate (>85%) remains acceptable, and the potential benefits of fewer hypotensive episodes justify this margin. To account for an estimated 5% dropout rate, we planned to enroll 180 patients (90 per group) to ensure adequate power for the final analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All primary analyses were performed on an intention-to-treat (ITT) basis. A per-protocol (PP) analysis was also conducted for the primary outcome as a sensitivity analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (interquartile range [IQR]), or number (%). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plots. Normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables were compared using independent-samples t-tests and Mann–Whitney U-tests, respectively. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Non-inferiority of the primary outcome (excellent intubation conditions) was confirmed if the lower limit of the 95% CI for the proportion difference (Ciprofol – Propofol) exceeded –10%. A mixed-effects model for repeated measures was used to evaluate MAP/HR trends over time. Logistic regression (univariate and multivariate) analyses were performed to identify predictors of hypotension. Variables included in the multivariate model (age, sex, BMI, Mallampati class, hypertension, Hb, Cr, GFR) were prespecified based on clinical plausibility and previous literature. All tests were two-tailed (α = 0.05), except for the non-inferiority analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.223 and MSTATA software (https://www.mstata.com/).

Discussion

In this prospective, randomized, non-inferiority trial, ciprofol (0.4 mg/kg) provided intubation conditions non-inferior to propofol (2.0 mg/kg) during RSII in patients undergoing elective non-cardiac surgery. Importantly, ciprofol induction demonstrated superior hemodynamic stability, evidenced by a markedly reduced incidence of PIH (15.3% vs 43.5%) and vasopressor requirements. In addition, the absence of pain on injection in the ciprofol group suggests an improved anesthesia experience.

Hypotension during RSII, particularly when propofol is used, poses significant cardiovascular risks due to profound suppression of sympathetic tone and baroreflex activity.24 Other commonly used RSII induction agents include etomidate and ketamine, but each has limitations. For example, while etomidate preserves hemodynamics, it causes adrenal suppression via 11β-hydroxylase inhibition, raising concerns in septic or critically ill patients.25 Furthermore, even a single dose may prolong hospital/ICU stays and ventilation duration in patients with trauma.26 Ketamine may preserve hemodynamics through endogenous catecholamine release.27–29 However, this benefit may be lost in patients with depleted catecholamine stores (eg, in septic shock), thus increasing PIH risk. Although ciprofol has demonstrated efficacy for general anesthesia induction,30,31 its role in RSII has not been well defined. Previous studies have shown that ciprofol is both effective and safe for anesthesia in older patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery32 and provides better hemodynamic stability than propofol in patients with severe aortic stenosis during induction.33 Our findings align with these observations, as induction time was under 1 min in both groups (36.3 ± 9.8s vs 35.4 ± 8.7s; P = 0.514) and no rescue induction medications were required, supporting 0.4 mg/kg ciprofol as an effective RSII agent.

The absence of pain on injection with ciprofol is likely attributable to its oil-in-water emulsion formulation, which reduces the free drug concentration in the aqueous phase,34 and avoids the discomfort commonly associated with propofol. This is clinically relevant, as pain on injection has been shown to negatively impact patient satisfaction and may contribute to unpleasant postoperative memories.35

This observation aligns with findings of Deng et al36 who compared ciprofol and propofol across three dose levels and reported distinct sedative properties between the two agents. Although mean BIS values during induction were higher in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group (42.7 ± 6.4 vs 34.7 ± 6.7, P = 0.012), this difference did not compromise intubation conditions or LOC timing. The higher BIS values may reflect pharmacodynamic differences between the two agents, but the clinical significance is unclear. Therefore, BIS should be interpreted together with clinical signs (eg, MOAA/S scores and hemodynamic responses) during ciprofol induction.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center trial with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study population consisted of relatively healthy patients (ASA I–III) undergoing elective surgery; thus, the results cannot be directly extrapolated to critically ill patients or emergency settings. Importantly, our decision to first study elective patients was deliberate: directly testing a novel agent in high-risk emergency or full-stomach populations could expose patients to unknown risks. This proof-of-concept study in a controlled setting provides the necessary foundation for future research in emergency and high-risk RSI populations. Third, we did not measure plasma concentrations of ciprofol or propofol, which could have provided additional pharmacokinetic insights. Fourth, although we assessed intraoperative awareness through postoperative interviews, we did not use a validated structured questionnaire. Fifth, the near-perfect intubation rates in both groups created a ceiling effect, limiting the ability to detect potential differences. Sixth, the high incidence of injection pain with propofol may have partially unblinded the assessors, although every effort was made to maintain blinding.

Despite these limitations, our study provides robust evidence that ciprofol is a viable alternative to propofol for RSII in elective surgical patients, with improved hemodynamic stability and patient comfort.

Conclusions

In elective non-cardiac surgical patients undergoing a standardized RSI protocol, ciprofol (0.4 mg/kg) provided intubating conditions non-inferior to propofol (2.0 mg/kg) during RSII, with a significantly lower incidence of post-induction hypotension and elimination of injection pain. These findings support the use of ciprofol as a safe and effective alternative to propofol for induction and tracheal intubation under a controlled RSII protocol in elective surgical patients. Further studies are needed before extrapolating to emergency or high-risk RSII settings.
