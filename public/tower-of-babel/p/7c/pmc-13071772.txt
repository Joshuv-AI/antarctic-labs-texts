Intravenous non-specific immunoglobulins as adjunctive therapy for invasive Streptococcus pyogenes infection: an eleven-year retrospective study
Authors: Laura Caeiro-Martínez, David Brandariz-Núñez, María Sandra Albiñana-Pérez, Enrique Alemparte-Pardavila, Mónica Mourelo-Fariña, José María Gutiérrez-Urbón, Luis Margusino-Framiñán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdjunctive intravenous non-specific immunoglobulin (IVIG) is used in the management of streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, although supporting evidence remains limited. The primary objective of this study was to describe the use of IVIG in patients with invasive Streptococcus pyogenes infection.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study over 11 years, including patients diagnosed with invasive S. pyogenes infection who received at least one dose of IVIG as adjunctive therapy.ResultsTwenty-three patients were included, with a mean age of 63.2 ± 16.3 years; 47.8% were male. Most patients (73.9%) developed septic shock and STSS, requiring admission to intensive care units. The mean Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score was 9.1 ± 4.9. High-dose IVIG (2.0 ± 0.2 g/kg) was administered promptly, with a mean time to first infusion of 0.26 ± 0.47 days from admission. In-hospital mortality was 21.7% overall and 29.4% among STSS patients. Non-survivors had significantly higher disease severity and were more likely to develop hepatic dysfunction and require continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Two patients (8.7%) experienced IVIG-related adverse events, both of which were mild.ConclusionEarly and standardized high-dose IVIG administration, in combination with prompt targeted antibiotic therapy and intensive supportive care, was associated with mortality rates similar to, or lower than, those previously reported. Initial disease severity, hepatic dysfunction, and need for CRRT were identified as predictors of mortality. IVIG demonstrated a favorable safety profile.Keywords: Intravenous immunoglobulins, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome, Toxic shock syndrome

Introduction

Streptococcus pyogenes is a Gram-positive bacterium capable of causing a broad spectrum of infections in humans, ranging from mild to moderate respiratory tract infections to severe and potentially fatal invasive diseases, such as necrotizing fasciitis and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (STSS) [1]. These conditions are driven by the release of exotoxins that act as superantigens, leading to massive immune system activation [2]. The resulting hyperinflammatory state may lead to systemic toxicity and multiorgan failure, carrying a high risk of fatal outcomes in the most severe cases [3,4].

The adjunctive use of intravenous non-specific immunoglobulin (IVIG) in severe invasive S. pyogenes infection is supported by its capacity to neutralize or inhibit superantigens, thereby attenuating the systemic inflammatory response [5]. Current international guidelines for the management of adult sepsis do not support the use of IVIG in patients with sepsis and septic shock [6]. Nevertheless, the therapeutic role of IVIG in STSS remains an area of ongoing debate. The efficacy of IVIG in this context has been investigated in different studies, with some suggesting a potential survival advantage [7–11]. However, contemporary studies have not demonstrated a consistent mortality benefit attributable to IVIG in STSS [12–14].

The primary aim of this study was to describe the use of adjunctive IVIG in patients with invasive S. pyogenes infection. Secondary aims were to determine in-hospital and 180-day mortality; identify factors associated with mortality; analyze the temporal evolution of the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score; and describe IVIG-related adverse events (AEs).

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A retrospective observational study was conducted on patients with invasive S. pyogenes disease who received IVIG as an adjunctive therapy. The study was carried out in a tertiary hospital with a capacity of 1,423 beds and a reference population of 551,703 inhabitants. The recruitment period spanned from January 2013 to December 2023. Patients aged 18 years or older who received at least one dose of IVIG were included. The follow-up period lasted six months. The clinical management of patients, including the use of IVIG, was based on the clinical judgment of the hospital’s medical staff.

Study definitions

Invasive S. pyogenes disease was defined as an infection with clinical presentation and isolation of the microorganism in blood [1]. STSS was defined in accordance with the criteria established by the Working Group on Severe Streptococcal Infections [15]. Septic shock was defined following the criteria outlined in the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock [16]. The resolution time for infection severity was defined as the duration of vasopressor use (norepinephrine and/or vasopressin) secondary to crystalloid-refractory hypotension. Severity at diagnosis was assessed using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE-II) score [17,18]. The temporal evolution of the SOFA score was assessed by calculating the mean value at baseline and at 48 h, 72 h, day 5, day 7 and day 14 after diagnosis. Time to IVIG administration was defined as the number of days from diagnosis to the initiation of IVIG therapy. The post–COVID-19 pandemic period was defined as beginning in 2022.

Data collection

The data were obtained by cross-referencing microbiological records of patients with positive cultures for S. pyogenes with the IVIG preparation database from the Pharmacy Department. Data were collected retrospectively from medical records, including: age, sex and weight; clinical data at the time of admission; medical history; source of infection, presence of STSS (and organ involvement) and septic shock; levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin, and leukocytes (prior to IVIG); need for surgical intervention; targeted antibiotic treatment and antitoxin, including duration; IVIG dosing regimen and time of administration; length of hospital stay, time to resolution of severe infection, in-hospital mortality, 180-day mortality rates and AEs related to the administration of IVIG. Only in patients admitted to the critical care units (CCU) were the following data collected: baseline APACHE-II and SOFA scores, temporal evolution of the SOFA score, and need for invasive mechanical ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and vasoactive drugs.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. It was reviewed and approved by the Galician Drug Ethics Committee (CEIm-G) on November 29, 2023, with registration code 2023/513. Informed consent was not required due to the retrospective observational design of the study and the use of pseudonymized data.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted for the collected variables. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were described using absolute frequencies and percentages. A univariable analysis comparing survivors and non-survivors was carried out for all collected variables. Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using parametric (Student’s t-test for independent samples) or non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test), as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 23 patients were included, with a mean age of 63.2 ± 16.3 years; 47.8% were male. The majority of patients (60.9%) were diagnosed in the post–COVID-19 pandemic period (see Figure 1). The most frequent comorbidity was solid malignancy (30.4%), followed by chronic pulmonary disease (21.7%).

Infections primarily involved the skin and soft tissues in 56.5% of cases, of which 53.8% presented as necrotizing fasciitis. All patients received a β-lactam antibiotic with activity against S. pyogenes, predominantly (91%) at the time of diagnosis, with penicillin G being the predominant targeted agent (73.9%). The median duration of targeted antibiotic therapy was 13.5 days (IQR: 16.8). Concurrently, all patients underwent antitoxin therapy, initially administered in the majority of cases (87%) at the onset. Linezolid was administered in 69.6% of cases, and clindamycin in 60.9%. Antitoxin therapy was switched between these agents in 30.4% of cases. The median duration of antitoxin therapy was 5.5 days (IQR: 7.5)

The mean time to IVIG administration was 0.26 ± 0.47 days (approximately 6 hours) from diagnosis. In 78.3% of cases, IVIG infusion was initiated on the same day that the diagnosis was established. The mean IVIG dose was 2 ± 0.2 g/kg, with a mean total dose of 142.2 ± 21.9 g. In 17.4% of cases, dosing was calculated based on adjusted ideal body weight. IVIG was administered over two days in 47.8% of patients, over one day in 39.2%, and over three days in 13%.

The median hospital length of stay was 23 days (IQR: 5–43.5), and the median CCU stay was 6 days (IQR: 4–18.8). The median time to resolution of severe infection was 3 days (IQR: 2–5). Overall in-hospital mortality was 21.7% and 29.4% among STSS patients (see Table 2). The median time from diagnosis to fatal outcome was 2 days (IQR = 3), and 60% of the patients died within the first 48 hours.

Two IVIG-related AEs were documented (8.7%), both of which were moderate and transient. The first was an infusion-related reaction in a patient who received the full IVIG dose over a single day; the patient developed a headache and dizziness, leading to the discontinuation of the infusion 30 minutes before completion. The second case involved interstitial alveolar oedema in a patient who received IVIG over two consecutive days. In this case, an acute transfusion-related lung injury could not be ruled out (Table 2).

Discussion

In recent years, a notable increase in the incidence of severe invasive S. pyogenes infections has been reported, a trend that appears to have intensified in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic [19,20]. This rise has been observed across all age groups, with a particular impact on children up to 10 years old and older adults [21,22]. The surge in S. pyogenes infections following the pandemic suggests that the protective measures implemented during COVID-19 may have reduced the spread of airborne respiratory infections, and that the relaxation of these measures led to a rebound in these infections [19]. Our cohort aligns temporally with this observation, with more than 60% of cases diagnosed during the post-pandemic period (from 2022 onwards).

The predominant infection type was skin and soft tissue infection, with necrotizing fasciitis representing the majority of cases, which is comparable to previous studies [7,9,10,12,13]. With respect to disease severity, our cohort exhibited a mean SOFA score of 9.1, reflecting a critical clinical status. This finding is consistent with previously published series of patients with invasive S. pyogenes infection, in which reported median SOFA scores ranged from 8 to 13 [8,12,13].

Targeted antibiotic therapy and IVIG administration were initiated immediately upon suspicion of S. pyogenes infection. IVIG was administered as early adjunctive therapy in this cohort, using high, standardized doses (2 g/kg, adjusted to ideal body weight when necessary), with the majority receiving the total dose over 1 or 2 days. In contrast, lower doses were generally employed in other studies [8,10,13]. The administration schedule also varied among studies, with the total dose given over 1 to 6 days [8,10,13]. In 47.8% of our patients, the dose was administered over two days, a strategy adopted due to the large infusion volume associated with the 2 g/kg dose, which may pose a risk of fluid overload in critically ill patients or those with comorbidities such as renal insufficiency or cardiovascular disease [23]. Mortality outcomes were comparable regardless of the duration of IVIG therapy.

The observed in-hospital and 6-month mortality rate was 21.7%, whereas among patients with STSS, it reached 29.4%. These data may be considered low when contextualized within previously published literature, where historical cohorts of patients with STSS have reported mortality rates ranging from 30–40% to as high as 70% [3,7–8,11–13,24]. Supporting our findings, the recent ISTRE study—a large, multicenter French retrospective analysis of critically ill patients with invasive S. pyogenes infection—reported an in-hospital mortality of 32% among patients with STSS and 19% in the overall cohort, despite a notably low rate of IVIG administration (8%) [25]. On the other hand, we observed early fatal outcomes (within the first 48 hours from diagnosis), indicative of aggressive infection with fulminant progression and a very narrow window for intervention.

Non-survivors in our cohort showed more pronounced organ dysfunction—including renal impairment, coagulopathy, hepatic injury, and acute respiratory distress syndrome—with hepatic involvement demonstrating the strongest and statistically significant association with mortality. In accordance with our results, Mehta et al. found that hepatic dysfunction was an independent predictor of mortality in patients with invasive Group A streptococcal infection [26]. Furthermore, the need for CRRT in patients with SSTS reflects severe acute kidney injury, typically occurring within the context of multi-organ dysfunction and increased clinical severity. This requirement is associated with a significantly higher mortality rate compared with patients who do not require such support [12,24,26].

Previous studies involving critically ill patients with septic shock have demonstrated that prognostic scoring systems, such as the SOFA score, serve as independent predictors of adverse clinical outcomes [27,28]. Consistent with these findings, in our cohort, non-survivors exhibited significantly higher SOFA and APACHE-II scores than survivors, thereby reinforcing their role as indispensable tools for the early assessment and risk stratification of critically ill patients. Regarding SOFA score evolution, we observed an early and sustained divergence between survivors and non-survivors. In the initial days following diagnosis, the SOFA scores of non-survivors remained elevated, whereas those of survivors showed a progressive decline. These findings reinforce previous evidence indicating that dynamic changes in SOFA scores are superior to baseline values alone in predicting mortality among critically ill patients, with variations within the first 48–72 hours proving particularly critical [28–29].

As previously discussed, the efficacy of IVIG as adjunctive therapy in STSS has been questioned in recent literature [12–14]. However, these studies often suffer from methodological limitations, including lack of dose standardization, use of subtherapeutic doses (<2 g/kg), and absence of detailed information regarding timing of administration. Given the mechanistic rationale underlying IVIG’s immunomodulatory role, its clinical benefit may be highly time- and dose-dependent.

The administration of IVIG in our cohort demonstrated a favorable safety profile, with a low incidence of adverse events (8.7%), all of which were mild to moderate and transient in nature. Similarly, the observational study by Anna Linnér et al. reported a low incidence of IVIG-related adverse events, with a generalized rash being the only event described [10]. However, evidence from other immunological conditions, where similar dosing regimens (2 g/kg over 1–3 days) are used, supports its general safety, although it is not devoid of risks [30–31].

This study has several limitations. Most notably, its retrospective and uncontrolled design, along with the small sample size from a single-centre cohort, limit both causal inference and statistical power. Furthermore, S. pyogenes strain genotyping was not conducted, precluding assessment of potential clonal heterogeneity in relation to virulence and mortality [19,32–33]. Despite these constraints, the study offers important strengths: the inclusion of consecutive cases over an eleven-year period minimizes selection bias, and the cohort primarily consisted of critically ill patients with STSS—a clinical presentation that remains poorly represented in the literature—in whom early and standardized IVIG administration was consistently achieved.

Conclusion

In conclusion, despite the high severity of illness and substantial organ dysfunction observed in the cohort, early and standardized administration of IVIG, in combination with prompt targeted antibiotic therapy and intensive supportive care, was associated with mortality rates similar to or lower than those reported in previous literature. Moreover, initial disease severity, hepatic dysfunction, the need for CRRT, and the temporal evolution of SOFA score emerged as strong predictors of mortality. Importantly, IVIG administration demonstrated a favorable safety profile. Therefore, prospective, multicenter, and controlled studies are needed to definitively establish the therapeutic role of adjunctive IVIG in the management of severe invasive S. pyogenes infections.
