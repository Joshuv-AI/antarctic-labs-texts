Clinical efficacy of different biomaterials for the regenerative treatment of periodontal intrabony defects: a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis
Authors: Yikun Zhou, Gengming Zhang, Mei Wang, Hong He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically compare the clinical efficacy of various biomaterial-based regenerative strategies for periodontal intrabony defects and to provide clinically meaningful evidence for optimizing treatment selection.MethodsThis study followed the PRISMA-NMA guidelines. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP databases were systematically searched from January 2010 to February 2026. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving regenerative treatment of periodontal intrabony defects were included. Interventions consisted of different biomaterial-based regenerative therapies. The primary outcomes were changes from baseline in probing depth (PD), clinical attachment level (CAL), bone gain (mm), and gingival recession (REC). Two reviewers independently performed study selection and data extraction, and the risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool. Bayesian network meta-analysis was conducted using STATA 16, Review Manager 5.4, and R 4.4.2 with the BUGSnet and gemtc packages. Effect sizes were expressed as weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% credible intervals (CrI). Treatment rankings were estimated using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA).ResultsA total of 32 RCTs and 14 treatment strategies were included. According to SUCRA rankings, bone graft combined with platelet-rich fibrin (BoneGraft + PRF) showed the highest probability of achieving PD reduction (93.6%) and bone gain (94.9%). FGF-2 combined with bone graft (FGF2 + BoneGraft) ranked first for CAL gain (91.9%), followed by BoneGraft + PRF (82.6%) and FGF-2 alone (82.4%). For bone gain, FGF2 + BoneGraft (91.2%) and stem cell therapy (82.8%) also showed favorable rankings. For gingival recession reduction (REC), where lower values indicate better outcomes, PRF combined with ascorbic acid (PRF_AA) demonstrated the best ranking (4.3%), followed by FGF2 + BoneGraft (9.6%) and PRF (19.6%). Selected pairwise estimates showed that, compared with OFD, BoneGraft + PRF produced greater PD reduction (MD = 2.09 mm, 95% CrI: 0.45 to 3.75) and bone gain (MD = 2.76 mm, 95% CrI: 0.94 to 4.59), whereas FGF2 + BoneGraft produced greater CAL gain (MD = 1.88 mm, 95% CrI: 0.32 to 3.45). For REC, PRF_AA showed a lower recession estimate than OFD (MD = -2.57 mm, 95% CrI: -6.62 to 1.47), although the CrI crossed zero.ConclusionThis study provides a comprehensive comparative evaluation of multiple biomaterial-based regenerative strategies using a network meta-analytic approach. The findings suggest that combined regenerative approaches integrating structural scaffolds and bioactive factors may provide greater clinical benefit, although the certainty of evidence should be interpreted cautiously in periodontal regeneration compared with single-material therapies. These results highlight the importance of synergistic treatment strategies and may assist clinicians in selecting more effective, evidence-based regenerative protocols tailored to specific clinical outcomes. However, given the limited evidence for certain interventions, further high-quality multicenter RCTs are required to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08671-2.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic infectious condition primarily associated with disturbances in the microbial balance of dental plaque. As the principal etiological factor, plaque initiates local inflammatory reactions within periodontal tissues, gradually leading to the separation of the gingiva from the tooth surface and subsequent destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone [1, 2]. Early clinical manifestations are often mild and easily overlooked; however, with the progression of inflammation, the lesions may extend to involve deeper periodontal structures, particularly the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, ultimately resulting in varying degrees of alveolar bone loss. In advanced stages, patients may develop vertical bone defects or, in rare cases, bone necrosis [3]. Epidemiological studies have reported that the overall prevalence of periodontitis is approximately 63%, with severe periodontitis accounting for about 24.2% of cases. Furthermore, the global burden of periodontitis has shown a noticeable increase in recent years [4]. In managing bone defects secondary to periodontitis, clinical treatment primarily focuses on controlling inflammation and facilitating the regeneration of periodontal supporting tissues, with the ultimate aim of restoring both the structural integrity and functional stability of the periodontium [5].

The current management of periodontitis typically commences with basic periodontal therapy, which includes supragingival scaling, subgingival debridement, and root planing. While these procedures effectively reduce microbial load and control local inflammation, they often fail to sustain long-term suppression of periodontal pathogens, rendering patients vulnerable to disease recurrence. Consequently, systemic antibiotics are frequently prescribed as adjunctive therapy to improve infection control. However, the delivery of orally administered drugs to periodontal tissues is limited, making it challenging to achieve therapeutic concentrations at the target site. As a result, treatment outcomes may be suboptimal and may be accompanied by various adverse reactions [3]. For patients presenting with alveolar bone defects, achieving predictable regenerative outcomes remains challenging, necessitating additional periodontal regenerative procedures [5]. In the clinical management of chronic periodontitis, the regeneration of the periodontal supporting apparatus—particularly cementum and alveolar bone—is considered a key therapeutic objective. Nevertheless, conventional regenerative approaches, such as bone grafting combined with barrier membranes, do not always yield satisfactory outcomes for certain periodontal bone defects, and complete bone regeneration may be unattainable in some cases [6]. Moreover, traditional periodontal regenerative surgery typically involves extensive flap elevation, which can cause significant trauma to the gingival tissues and may damage intact periodontal structures. These surgical procedures may lead to postoperative complications, such as gingival recession, bleeding, and prolonged healing times, thereby negatively impacting both patient experience and treatment prognosis [7]. With the evolving concepts of periodontal regeneration and advancements in regenerative medicine, Increasing attention has been directed toward the development of biomaterials designed to enhance periodontal tissue regeneration and address the limitations of traditional therapeutic strategies [8]. Biomaterials can facilitate periodontal repair by serving as scaffolds for tissue regeneration, promoting cellular migration and proliferation, and modulating the local microenvironment, thereby becoming an essential component of contemporary periodontal regenerative therapy [8, 9].

Currently, a variety of biomaterials have been utilized in periodontal regenerative treatment, encompassing natural biomaterials, growth factor–based preparations, and platelet concentrates. Among the natural biomaterials, hyaluronic acid (HA) has garnered significant attention for its potential role in periodontal tissue regeneration [10]. In the realm of growth factor–based therapies, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) and its recombinant form, rhFGF-2, have exhibited promising therapeutic effects in the management of periodontal intrabony defects. A multicenter cohort study indicated that the local administration of rhFGF-2 significantly enhanced bone fill in intrabony defects at 36 weeks post-treatment [11]. Furthermore, several studies have explored combined therapeutic strategies employing rhFGF-2 alongside bone substitute materials, such as demineralized bovine bone mineral (DBBM) and carbonate apatite (CO₃Ap). Platelet concentrates have also been extensively utilized in periodontal regenerative procedures, including platelet-rich plasma (PRP), leukocyte- and platelet-rich fibrin (L-PRF), and the more recently introduced titanium-prepared platelet-rich fibrin (T-PRF) [12].

Therefore, the present study aimed to fill an important evidence gap by conducting a Bayesian network meta-analysis to simultaneously compare the relative efficacy of multiple biomaterial categories and their combination strategies in the regenerative treatment of periodontal intrabony defects. Unlike previous studies focusing on individual materials or limited comparisons, this study provides a comprehensive framework to evaluate both single and combined biomaterial approaches across multiple clinically relevant outcomes.

Network meta-analysis allows simultaneous comparison of multiple regenerative interventions by integrating direct and indirect evidence within a connected evidence network. The Bayesian framework further enables probabilistic estimation of treatment effects and ranking probabilities, thereby providing clinically interpretable comparisons among competing biomaterial-based regenerative strategies.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension statement for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines. This study was previously registered with the International Registry for Prospective Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (registration number: PROSPERO 2026 CRD420261389070).

Research question (PICO): Population: adults with periodontitis and periodontal intrabony defects; Interventions: biomaterial-based regenerative therapies, including bone grafts, platelet concentrates, enamel matrix derivative, FGF-2, barrier membranes, stem cells, and their combinations; Comparators: OFD or other regenerative biomaterial strategies; Outcomes: changes in PD, CAL, bone gain (mm), and REC.

Search strategy

Two researchers independently searched the PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang, and VIP databases. The search covered titles, abstracts, and keywords, and the retrieval period ranged from January 2010 to February 2026. Only studies published in English or Chinese were considered. The search strategy focused on four main themes: periodontitis, intrabony defects, regenerative biomaterials, and randomized controlled trials. Keywords related to the study population included periodontitis, periodontal intrabony defect, intrabony defect, and periodontal bone defect. Intervention-related terms included bone graft, platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), platelet-rich plasma (PRP), enamel matrix derivative (EMD), barrier membrane, fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), stem cells, regenerative biomaterials, and tissue engineering. Methodological terms included randomized controlled trial, randomized, and clinical trial. Relevant Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms were combined using Boolean operators to improve the sensitivity of the search. In addition, the reference lists of the included studies were manually screened to identify potentially relevant articles (The search formula can be found in the supplementary Table 1).

Literature screening and data extraction

Two researchers independently screened the retrieved studies according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. When the agreement between the two reviewers reached Kappa value > 0.90, the final list of eligible studies was determined. Data extraction was conducted using a standardized data extraction form. One reviewer completed the data extraction, and another reviewer checked the extracted information for accuracy. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

The extracted information included the following aspects:

① Study characteristics: first author, year of publication, country, and study design.② Baseline characteristics of participants: intervention and control treatments, sample size of experimental and control groups, mean age (and standard deviation or range), and gender distribution.③ Defect characteristics and eligibility criteria: diagnostic criteria for periodontitis and characteristics of periodontal intrabony defects reported in each study.④ Outcome measures: the primary outcomes of this study included ΔPD, ΔCAL, bone gain (mm), and REC. PD and CAL were measured using a periodontal probe and expressed in millimeters. Bone gain was assessed radiographically to evaluate the regeneration of the intrabony defect, and REC represented the change in gingival recession before and after treatment.

Risk of bias assessments

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers. The Cochrane RoB 2.0 was used to evaluate the risk of bias of the included randomized controlled trials. RoB 2.0 assesses five domains:Bias arising from the randomization process;Bias due to deviations from intended interventions;Bias due to missing outcome data;Bias in measurement of the outcome;Bias in selection of the reported result.

Each domain was judged as “Low risk of bias,” “Some concerns,” or “High risk of bias.” Based on the assessments across all domains, an overall risk of bias judgment was assigned for each study. The assessment results were summarized and presented in graphical form to illustrate the distribution of bias risk across the included studies and domains. The certainty of evidence was further assessed using the GRADE framework, which evaluates risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

Bayesian network meta-analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.4.2) with the BUGSnet and gemtc packages. Continuous outcomes were analyzed as mean differences (MDs) or weighted mean differences (WMDs) in millimeters with corresponding 95% credible intervals (95% CrI). For all continuous outcomes, including CAL gain, PD reduction, bone gain, and REC, a normal likelihood with an identity link was applied within the Bayesian framework. Both fixed-effect and random-effects models were fitted for each outcome. Model fit was compared using the deviance information criterion (DIC), with lower DIC values indicating better model fit. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics for direct and network estimates. The magnitude of heterogeneity was interpreted according to the I² values, and the corresponding heterogeneity results are presented in the supplementary material. Because the random-effects model showed lower DIC values across the main outcomes, the random-effects model was selected for the primary network meta-analysis. Statistical inference was performed using the Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. Four Markov chains were run simultaneously, with 50,000 iterations for each chain, of which the first 10,000 iterations were discarded as burn-in. Model convergence was assessed by visual inspection of trace plots, density plots, and autocorrelation plots. The convergence diagnostic plots for all four outcomes are provided in the supplementary material. For studies with multiple intervention arms, all eligible arms were retained in the network meta-analysis to preserve the available randomized comparisons. Split-mouth trials were included using the reported mean changes and corresponding standard deviations for each treatment group. Because within-person correlation coefficients were not consistently reported across split-mouth studies, paired-effect estimates could not be uniformly reconstructed; therefore, These studies were analyzed using the available group-level continuous outcome data. When standard deviations were not directly reported, they were calculated from available information such as standard errors, confidence intervals, P values, or other extractable numerical data whenever possible. If the required variance information could not be obtained or calculated, the corresponding outcome data were not included in the quantitative synthesis for that specific outcome. When closed loops were present in the evidence network, the node-splitting method was applied to assess the consistency between direct and indirect evidence. A P value > 0.05 was considered to indicate no statistically significant inconsistency. Consistency was further evaluated by comparing the DIC values of the consistency and inconsistency models. The SUCRA was calculated to estimate the ranking probability of each intervention for each outcome. For the interpretation of SUCRA rankings, higher SUCRA values indicated a higher probability of better treatment performance for CAL gain, PD reduction, and bone gain. However, REC was interpreted as a lower-is-better outcome because lower REC values indicate less gingival recession. Therefore, lower SUCRA values were considered more favorable for REC. This directionality was applied consistently when interpreting the REC results.

Results

Literature search results

A total of 3,461 records were identified from PubMed (n = 667), Web of Science (n = 1231), Embase (n = 532), Cochrane Library (n = 433), CNKI (n = 265), Wanfang (n = 201), and VIP (n = 132). After removing 2,205 records before screening, including duplicates and ineligible publications, 1,256 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 1,085 records were excluded because the study population, interventions, or study design did not meet the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, 171 articles were assessed for full-text eligibility. After full-text review, 125 studies were excluded due to unavailable full text, insufficient outcome data, or low methodological quality. Finally, 32 RCTs met the eligibility criteria and were included in the network meta-analysis. The study selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Risk of bias assessment of the included studies

A total of 32 RCTs were included, and their methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool. Overall, most studies were judged as low risk of bias, indicating generally acceptable methodological quality. Several trials clearly reported appropriate randomization procedures and allocation concealment. For example, Ferrarrotti et al. [30] and Górski et al. [34] used computer-generated randomization with allocation concealment and double-blind assessment. However, several studies were rated as “some concerns” due to insufficient methodological details. For instance, Sener et al. [14] and Mubarak [20] provided limited information regarding deviations from intended interventions, while Lee et al. [28] showed some concerns related to outcome measurement due to long-term attrition and radiographic assessment performed by a single examiner. In addition, Samarth [25] lacked a publicly available protocol and did not clearly specify prespecified outcomes. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was considered acceptable, and the risk of bias was unlikely to substantially affect the reliability of the network meta-analysis (Fig. 2).

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for the main clinical periodontal outcomes was assessed using the GRADE approach. The evaluated outcomes included CAL gain, PD reduction, bone gain, and REC. Because all outcomes were continuous variables, treatment effects were summarized as mean differences (MDs) or weighted mean differences (WMDs) in millimeters with corresponding 95% credible intervals (95% CrI) derived from the Bayesian network meta-analysis. The certainty of evidence was judged by considering the following domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The overall certainty of evidence was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low. For CAL gain, PD reduction, and bone gain, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate, mainly because the included studies were randomized controlled trials but some clinical and methodological heterogeneity was present across studies. For REC, the certainty of evidence was rated as low because the number of contributing studies was limited and the credible interval was relatively wide. The GRADE summary of findings for the four outcomes is presented in Table 2. For REC, lower values indicate better clinical outcomes, and this directionality was considered when interpreting the treatment effect and certainty of evidence (Table 2).

Intervention classification and node merging

To ensure the connectivity and interpretability of the network meta-analysis, interventions with similar biological mechanisms and clinical applications were grouped into broader categories. The merging strategy was as follows:

Model fit was assessed by comparing the fixed-effect model and random-effect model using the deviance information criterion (DIC). Consistency test

Consistency between direct and indirect evidence was further evaluated by comparing the consistency and inconsistency models. As shown in Fig. 9, the DIC values of the consistency and inconsistency models were comparable across all outcomes. For CAL, PD, and bone gain, the DIC values of the consistency model were slightly lower than those of the inconsistency model (CAL: 153.91 vs. 156.82; PD: 141.65 vs. 143.80; bone gain: 125.15 vs. 126.71). For REC, the DIC value of the inconsistency model was slightly lower than that of the consistency model, but the difference was minimal (52.17 vs. 51.83). In addition, the posterior mean residual deviance values were very similar between the two models across all outcomes. These findings indicate that the inconsistency model did not provide a meaningfully better fit than the consistency model, suggesting no substantial inconsistency between direct and indirect evidence. Therefore, the consistency assumption of the network meta-analysis was considered acceptable.

Node-splitting analysis

Node-splitting analysis was performed to assess the agreement between direct and indirect evidence within the network. The results are presented in Supplementary Figs. 5–8. The estimated mean differences (MD) and corresponding 95% credible intervals (95% CrI) for direct, indirect, and network comparisons showed substantial overlap. In addition, the corresponding P values were all greater than 0.05, indicating no statistically significant differences between direct and indirect estimates. These findings suggest that the consistency assumption of the Bayesian network meta-analysis was satisfied, supporting the reliability and robustness of the overall results.

Model convergence and heterogeneity assessment

Model convergence was assessed for all four outcomes using convergence diagnostic plots. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 13–16, visual inspection of the trace plots showed that the Markov chains generally mixed well and fluctuated around stable posterior distributions for CAL gain, PD reduction, bone gain, and REC. The density plots of different chains showed substantial overlap, suggesting acceptable convergence of the Bayesian models. However, several parameters in the REC network showed relatively higher autocorrelation, which may be explained by the limited number of studies and sparse direct comparisons for some treatment nodes (Supplementary Fig. 16). Overall, the convergence diagnostics supported the reliability of the fitted Bayesian network meta-analysis models.

Heterogeneity was further evaluated using I² statistics for both direct and network estimates. As presented in Supplementary Figs. 9–12, heterogeneity varied across outcomes and treatment comparisons. For CAL gain, most comparisons showed acceptable heterogeneity, although substantial heterogeneity was observed for the PRF versus BoneGraft comparison (Supplementary Fig. 9). For PD reduction, high heterogeneity was detected in several comparisons, including OFD versus BarrierMembrane, OFD versus BoneGraft, PRF versus BoneGraft, PRF versus DrugAdjunct, and PRF versus OFD (Supplementary Fig. 10). For bone gain, substantial heterogeneity was observed, particularly in the PRF versus DrugAdjunct and PRF versus OFD comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 11). For REC, high heterogeneity was also detected, especially in the PRF versus OFD and FGF2_BoneGraft versus FGF2 comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 12). These findings indicate the presence of clinical and methodological variability among the included studies, which may be related to differences in biomaterial subtypes, surgical techniques, defect morphology, baseline defect severity, outcome measurement methods, and follow-up duration. Therefore, the random-effects model was considered appropriate for the primary network meta-analysis, and the treatment rankings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for outcomes or treatment nodes supported by limited evidence.

Discussion

This Bayesian network meta-analysis of 32 RCTs comparing 14 regenerative strategies found that combined approaches generally ranked more favorably than single-material therapies. BoneGraft + PRF ranked highest for PD reduction and bone gain, whereas FGF2_BoneGraft ranked highest for CAL gain. PRF_AA appeared favorable for reducing gingival recession when REC was interpreted as a lower-is-better outcome. However, these rankings should be interpreted cautiously because several treatment nodes were supported by few trials, and the certainty of evidence may vary across comparisons. Therefore, the findings suggest, rather than definitively prove, that combined scaffold- and bioactive-factor-based strategies may provide greater clinical benefit in periodontal intrabony defect regeneration.

Periodontal bone defects represent a major manifestation of periodontal tissue destruction, and treatment strategies primarily aim to control inflammation and promote regeneration of periodontal supporting structures. In recent years, a variety of biomaterials have been introduced into periodontal regenerative therapy, including bone grafting materials, growth factors, platelet concentrates, and stem cells. Although these materials have shown potential in facilitating periodontal tissue repair, their clinical effectiveness varies considerably. Previous studies have mainly focused on individual materials or limited direct comparisons, and systematic evidence evaluating the relative efficacy of different biomaterials remains insufficient. Therefore, this study conducted a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis to comprehensively compare the clinical outcomes of various biomaterials in the treatment of intrabony defects caused by periodontitis, aiming to provide more robust evidence-based guidance for clinical treatment strategies.

This study included 32 randomized controlled trials involving 14 intervention strategies. The network meta-analysis revealed significant differences in the efficacy of various biomaterials for periodontal regeneration. In terms of probing depth (PD) reduction and bone gain, the combination of bone grafting and PRF (BoneGraft + PRF) achieved the highest SUCRA ranking, suggesting a potential advantage of this combined treatment strategy in promoting periodontal tissue regeneration. PRF, a second-generation platelet concentrate first prepared and approved for clinical use in 2000, contains high concentrations of platelets and fibrin, along with a small number of leukocytes. It suppresses excessive expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines while enhancing the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, thereby regulating the inflammatory response and reducing the risk of postoperative infection [45]. Furthermore, PRF may release a series of antimicrobial peptides, including β-defensins, platelet microbicidal proteins (PMPs), and thrombin-induced PMPs. It can also generate reactive oxygen metabolites (such as superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals), participate in antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, and facilitate the phagocytosis of leukocytes trapped within the PRF fibrin network, among other antimicrobial mechanisms [46]. When PRF is combined with bone graft materials, the graft material provides an osteoconductive scaffold, while PRF promotes local tissue repair, potentially producing synergistic regenerative effects. Regarding clinical attachment level (CAL) improvement, FGF-2 combined with bone graft (FGF2 + BoneGraft) ranked highest, followed by BoneGraft + PRF and FGF-2 monotherapy. FGF-2 is a growth factor with strong angiogenic and proliferative properties that promotes the differentiation of mesenchymal cells into periodontal-associated cells during periodontal regeneration [47, 48]. Studies have shown that FGF-2 significantly enhances the activity and proliferation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) while improving cell adhesion and migration, thereby providing an adequate cellular source for tissue repair [49]. During the early stages of regeneration, FGF-2 downregulates the expression of osteogenic markers such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and osteocalcin, maintaining cells in a highly proliferative state. Subsequently, as the healing process progresses, it gradually promotes cell differentiation toward osteoblasts and contributes to new bone formation [50]. Meanwhile, FGF-2 also promotes angiogenesis, improving blood supply and the microenvironment within defect areas. This process facilitates the formation of new bone tissue and reconstruction of trabecular bone structure, manifested by increased bone volume fraction, trabecular number, and thickness, together with reduced trabecular spacing [51]. Studies further indicate that low concentrations of FGF-2 promote osteogenic differentiation of periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) by upregulating osteogenesis-related genes such as RUNX2, ALPL, and COL1A1, enhancing ALP activity, and accelerating early bone formation. Conversely, higher concentrations of FGF-2 may inhibit osteogenic differentiation and promote a fibroblast-like phenotype. In alveolar bone-derived mesenchymal stem cells (aBMSCs), FGF-2 enhances ALP activity and mineralized matrix deposition during early stages, thereby further promoting bone tissue formation [52]. The findings of this study further suggest that regenerative outcomes may be enhanced when FGF-2 is combined with bone graft materials. This synergistic effect likely results from the complementary roles of growth factors stimulating cellular proliferation and bone graft materials providing a structural scaffold for tissue regeneration. Regarding the improvement of gingival recession (REC), this study found that PRF combined with ascorbic acid (PRF_AA) achieved the highest SUCRA ranking. Ascorbic acid plays an essential role in collagen synthesis and tissue repair. It promotes soft tissue healing by enhancing fibroblast proliferation, stimulating collagen synthesis, and improving extracellular matrix formation [53]. When combined with PRF, it may contribute to improved soft tissue stability, thereby showing potentially favorable efficacy in reducing gingival recession. Overall, the results of this study indicate that combined treatment strategies may yield better clinical outcomes than monotherapy using a single material. This observation is consistent with the biological characteristics of periodontal regeneration, which involves coordinated interactions among multiple cell types and signaling pathways, making it difficult for a single intervention to achieve optimal regenerative outcomes. Although numerous studies have investigated biomaterials for the treatment of periodontal bone defects, most have employed traditional meta-analyses directly comparing two treatments or individual clinical trials, which limits the ability to simultaneously evaluate multiple interventions [54]. Compared with conventional meta-analysis, network meta-analysis integrates both direct and indirect evidence, enabling comparisons among multiple interventions within a single analytical framework. In this study, network meta-analysis was applied to compare 14 treatment strategies, providing more systematic, although still certainty-dependent, evidence regarding the relative efficacy of different biomaterials in periodontal regeneration. In addition to biological activity, the clinical handling properties of PRF may also contribute to its favorable performance when combined with bone graft materials. The fibrin network of PRF can act as a natural scaffold, improving the cohesion and stability of particulate graft materials (e.g., deproteinized bovine bone mineral), thereby reducing material dispersion and facilitating space maintenance within the defect site. This mechanical stabilization may enhance the regenerative microenvironment and support more predictable tissue healing. It should also be noted that different subtypes of platelet concentrates (e.g., L-PRF, T-PRF, and PRFM) may exhibit variations in their biological properties [55]. For instance, T-PRF, prepared using titanium tubes without anticoagulants, has been reported to possess a denser fibrin structure and a potentially prolonged growth factor release profile. However, due to the limited number of studies and the need to maintain network connectivity, these subtypes were grouped together in the present analysis. Therefore, the potential differences among PRF subtypes and their impact on long-term outcomes warrant further investigation.

The findings hold practical value for clinical decision-making. In regenerative treatment for intrabony periodontal defects, combining bone graft materials with bioactive agents may produce more favorable outcomes. Combinations such as BoneGraft + PRF and FGF-2 + BoneGraft tended to show favorable performance across multiple clinical indicators, suggesting that combined treatment strategies may offer potential advantages in promoting periodontal regeneration. Differences in clinical outcomes were also observed among treatment protocols. For instance, PRF_AA may be beneficial for reducing gingival recession. Therefore, clinical decision-making should involve comprehensive evaluation of defect type, tissue condition, therapeutic objectives, evidence certainty, feasibility, and cost-effectiveness in order to achieve individualized treatment strategies. Furthermore, from a safety perspective, certain biomaterials derived from autologous blood products exhibit good biocompatibility and relatively low immunogenic risk, which may provide advantages in clinical applications [56]. However, some growth factor preparations or stem cell-related therapies require higher technical expertise and involve considerable costs, and their broader clinical application still requires further evaluation of cost-effectiveness.

However, certain limitations should also be acknowledged.(1) Some interventions were represented by a relatively small number of studies, which may affect the stability of the corresponding results and treatment rankings. In addition, variations in surgical techniques, follow-up duration, and baseline characteristics of patients among the included studies may have influenced the outcomes.(2) Furthermore, this study mainly focused on clinical indicators such as PD, CAL, and bone gain, while patient-reported outcomes and long-term treatment effects were relatively underreported. Future research should include more high-quality multicenter randomized controlled trials to further validate the long-term efficacy and safety of different biomaterials in periodontal regenerative therapy.(3) In addition, to ensure network connectivity and statistical feasibility, interventions with similar biological mechanisms were grouped into broader categories (e.g., PRF-based therapies or bone graft materials). Although this approach is commonly adopted in network meta-analyses, it may obscure potential differences among specific biomaterial subtypes (e.g., different PRF preparations or graft materials). Therefore, the results of this study should be interpreted at the strategy level rather than for individual materials, and further studies with more direct comparisons are needed to allow more refined analyses.(4) Periodontal regenerative outcomes are also influenced by defect morphology, including one-, two-, and three-wall intrabony defects. Structural scaffolds may be particularly important for space maintenance in non-self-supporting defects, whereas contained three-wall defects may have greater intrinsic regenerative potential.(5) Although the certainty of evidence was formally assessed using the GRADE framework, the findings should still be interpreted cautiously. Several treatment nodes were supported by a limited number of trials, and some outcomes showed clinical and methodological heterogeneity as well as imprecision. Therefore, SUCRA rankings and apparently favorable treatment effects should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of superiority, particularly for interventions supported by sparse direct evidence. Larger, well-designed multicenter RCTs are needed to strengthen the certainty of these comparisons.

Conclusion

In conclusion, various biomaterials demonstrate differing clinical effectiveness in the regenerative treatment of intrabony periodontal defects. The findings of this Bayesian network meta-analysis suggest that combination therapies, particularly BoneGraft + PRF and FGF-2 + BoneGraft, may provide greater clinical benefits for periodontal regeneration than single-material interventions. These combined strategies appear to improve key clinical indicators, including probing depth reduction, clinical attachment level gain, and bone gain, while PRF combined with ascorbic acid may help reduce gingival recession. However, because several treatment nodes were supported by limited evidence and the certainty of evidence may vary, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed by larger, high-quality, multicenter RCTs.
