Fatal brain infection in early life: primary amoebic meningoencephalitis caused by Naegleria fowleri in neonates and infants
Authors: Javad Mahdavi, Setareh Pirayeshfar, Aref Shariati, Reza Ghasemikhah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM), caused by Naegleria fowleri, is a rare but often fatal central nervous system infection that mimics bacterial meningitis. This similarity can lead to misdiagnosis and delayed treatment, especially in neonates and infants, resulting in poor outcomes. This study evaluates the clinical and paraclinical features, diagnostic challenges, treatment strategies, and outcomes of PAM in this vulnerable population, emphasizing the importance of early and accurate diagnosis and treatment.MethodsThis systematic review followed the PRISMA statement. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar to identify studies without language restrictions describing PAM in neonates and infants published up to December 2025.ResultsThirteen studies reporting 13 patients (2 neonates and 11 infants, aged 11 days to 12 months) were included. Fever was found in all patients. Other prevalent manifestations were seizure, decreased level of consciousness, and vomiting. Exposure histories were heterogeneous and often involved contaminated well water and other freshwater sources. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis consistently demonstrated neutrophilic pleocytosis, elevated protein levels, and reduced glucose concentrations. Neuroimaging findings varied, with survivors commonly exhibiting limited abnormalities such as hydrocephalus or focal lesions. Microbiologic confirmation was primarily done by CSF microscopy, followed by CSF culture and CSF polymerase chain reaction. Amphotericin B–based combination therapy was the most frequently employed treatment; however, outcomes remained variable, with a high overall mortality rate.ConclusionsPAM in neonates and infants presents substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to nonspecific clinical manifestations, often unclear or atypical exposure histories, and rapid disease progression. The clinical presentation frequently resembles acute bacterial meningitis, which may lead to empiric antibacterial therapy and delays in initiating appropriate anti-amoebic treatment. Early clinical suspicion, timely use of microbiological diagnostic methods, and prompt initiation of targeted therapy may be associated with improved survival. Preventive measures, emphasizing the use of safe household water, are important, especially preventing neonates and infants from being exposed to untreated environmental water sources such as well water. Considering the high mortality rate, enhancing early diagnostic methods and optimizing therapeutic interventions remain essential to improving outcomes in these patients.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13925-z.

Introduction

Primary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM) is a severe, fulminant, hemorrhagic, and necrotizing form of meningitis caused by the free-living amoeba Naegleria fowleri (N. fowleri), commonly known as the “brain-eating amoeba” [1]. Although its occurrence is rare, PAM represents an acute rapidly progressive with an exceptionally high mortality rate exceeding 95% [2]. PAM is relatively more common in immunocompetent children and young adults with a recent history of exposure to amoeba-contaminated water, particularly through swimming or other recreational activities [3].

N. fowleri is a thermophilic, free-living amoeba that thrives in warm freshwater environments such as lakes, rivers, and hot springs. The organism exhibits three distinct morphological stages: trophozoite, flagellate, and cyst [4]. The trophozoite is the only reproductive and infective stage that is capable of feeding on bacteria, algae, and yeast, and reproduces through binary fission. Under nutrient-limited conditions, trophozoites transform into a motile flagellate, which is non-reproductive, or encyst into a metabolically dormant cyst capable of surviving extreme temperatures and adverse environmental conditions. These adaptive strategies allow N. fowleri to persist in diverse natural and artificial habitats [5].

Human infection occurs when trophozoites enter the nasal cavity during exposure to contaminated water, subsequently migrating to the brain via the olfactory nerve [6]. Within the central nervous system (CNS), the amoeba causes extensive neuronal destruction, inflammation, and cerebral edema [7, 8]. Early clinical manifestations are nonspecific, typically including fever, headache, and nausea, often leading to misdiagnosis as bacterial or viral meningitis. As the disease progresses, patients develop severe neurological signs such as stiff neck, seizures, and altered mental status [9, 10]. The incubation period is influenced by inoculum size and strain virulence, varying from 2 to 15 days [11].

Although PAM is not a common disease, it has been documented in multiple regions worldwide, underscoring its global public health relevance [12]. Environmental surveys indicate that Naegleria spp. are widely distributed, with pooled prevalence in water sources estimated at approximately 26% globally, including a notably high prevalence of about 23% for the pathogenic N. fowleri [13]. Seasonal peaks are observed during the summer months, when water temperatures are optimal for amoebic proliferation [14].

Neonates and infants represent a particularly vulnerable population in whom the diagnosis of PAM may be especially challenging. Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and may closely resemble neonatal sepsis or acute bacterial meningitis, potentially delaying appropriate diagnosis and treatment [15, 16]. Furthermore, the limited number of reported cases in this age group has led to an incomplete understanding of their clinical and diagnostic findings.

Given its high virulence, rapid progression, and diagnostic challenges, N. fowleri infection remains a significant public health concern. This systematic review aims to consolidate current knowledge on its clinical features, diagnostic approaches, and management strategies, with exclusive focus on neonates and infants.

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic literature search, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement was conducted to identify studies reporting PAM caused by Naegleria fowleri in infants and neonates [17]. The search included the following keywords: “Naegleria fowleri,” “N. fowleri,” “Naegleria,” “primary amoebic meningoencephalitis,” “primary amebic meningoencephalitis,” “PAM,” “brain-eating amoeba,” “brain-eating ameba,” “amoeba,” “ameba,” “meningoencephalitis,” and “brain infection.” Multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and ScienceDirect were systematically searched. Google Scholar was additionally used to identify potentially relevant articles not captured in the main databases. The search was conducted for publications published up to February 2025 and updated in December 2025. Supplementary Material 1 contains the detailed search strategy.

Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts of retrieved records were screened for relevance. Full texts of potentially eligible articles were then assessed against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction was performed using a standardized form and included year and country of publication, patient demographics, exposure history, clinical presentation and progression, duration of symptoms prior to admission, neurological examination, paraclinical findings including laboratory data and neuroimaging results, therapeutic interventions, and clinical outcomes. All included articles were managed using EndNote software. Data extraction and study selection were independently performed by two authors, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third author. Extracted data were systematically summarized in tables and figures to provide a clear and comprehensive overview.

Quality appraisal

All included studies were critically appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for case reports [19]. Quality appraisal was conducted by two authors and checked for consistency, with a third reviewer consulted when needed to resolve discrepancies. Detailed results of the appraisal are provided in Supplementary Material 2. The assessment informed the interpretation of the findings but did not determine study eligibility.

Discussion

This systematic review provides a focused synthesis of the clinically important evidence on PAM in neonates and infants, a population in which the disease remains particularly underrecognized. Rather than rarity alone, the principal challenge highlighted by this study is the convergence of nonspecific early manifestations and rapid neurological progression, which together create a narrow window for accurate diagnosis and effective intervention. In neonatal and infant care settings, where bacterial meningitis is often the default diagnostic consideration, this overlap increases the likelihood of delayed recognition and contributes to poor outcomes. Framing PAM within this context allows for a clearer interpretation of disease severity, patterns of progression, and determinants of outcome in this age group.

Although PAM remains rare, it is characterized by an exceptionally aggressive clinical course, with fatal outcomes frequently occurring within days of symptom onset. Previous reviews have reported that death may occur within 72 hours in a substantial proportion of patients, underscoring the fulminant nature of the disease [33]. In broader age groups, survivors have tended to be younger and to require prolonged hospitalization, whereas non-survivors typically experienced rapid clinical deterioration and shorter hospital stays [34]. While such age-based comparisons are not directly transferable to neonatal and infant populations, these observations are concordant with our findings, in which early and severe neurological involvement was observed among patients with unfavorable outcomes.

Consistent with this pattern, in our compilation of neonatal and infant PAM cases, systemic symptoms—including fever, seizure, and altered level of consciousness—were observed in most patients regardless of outcome. Based on the descriptive data from the included studies, neurological findings appeared to vary between patients with favorable and unfavorable outcomes. Severe manifestations such as anisocoria, non-reactive pupils, signs of raised intracranial pressure, and decerebrate posturing were observed among several patients with unfavorable outcomes, whereas hypertonia, depressed neonatal reflexes, and Kernig’s sign were reported in some survivors. Therefore, these observations may highlight the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and considering prompt anti-amoebic therapy when PAM is suspected, even when systemic signs are nonspecific.

Based on broader epidemiologic observations, PAM in older children and adults typically follows exposure to inadequately treated freshwater, including lakes, rivers, and swimming pools, with a clear seasonality favoring warmer months [35]. Environmental studies have documented the presence of N. fowleri in a substantial proportion of recreational and domestic water sources, underlining the persistent risk of exposure [36]. Cases have also been reported after nasal irrigation or use of tap water for household activities, emphasizing that even non-recreational water contact can serve as a source of infection [37, 38].

According to our study, exposure histories among neonates and infants with PAM were heterogeneous. The majority of cases were associated with contaminated well water or other freshwater sources, while in a subset of patients no definitive exposure could be ascertained. Notably, inhalation of dust during the Harmattan season was identified as a rare but reported route of exposure. These findings show that PAM risk is not limited to classical recreational water activities, but may also encompass domestic water sources and environmental exposures, particularly in resource-limited or underserved settings. This underscores the need for heightened clinical vigilance even in the absence of recognized high-risk water contact.

Survival in neonatal and infant PAM appears to vary with the extent and reversibility of CNS involvement. In our study, survivors were more often described as having limited neuroimaging abnormalities, such as communicating or non-communicating hydrocephalus or small focal parenchymal lesions, without evidence of diffuse cerebral edema, extensive leptomeningeal enhancement, or brainstem involvement. In contrast, fatal cases were more frequently reported to have widespread radiologic abnormalities, reflecting advanced intracranial inflammation and severe parenchymal injury. These observations are consistent with the known pathophysiology of PAM, in which N. fowleri gains direct access to the brain via the olfactory nerves, leading to rapid cerebral edema, tissue necrosis, and fatal herniation [39].

Notably, survival was also documented in several cases in which neuroimaging was not available. In these patients, favorable outcomes were observed in the absence of radiologic confirmation. Given the fulminant progression of PAM and its potential to lead to death within just a few days, delays in diagnosis or treatment while awaiting imaging confirmation may adversely affect patient outcomes. This issue is particularly relevant in resource-limited settings, where access to advanced neuroimaging and molecular diagnostics may be restricted, which may also contribute to delayed recognition and possible underreporting of the disease [40, 41].

Laboratory findings further emphasize the diagnostic complexity of PAM in neonates and infants. Peripheral hematologic parameters demonstrated marked variability, ranging from leukopenia to significant leukocytosis, with substantial overlap between survivors and non-survivors, limiting their prognostic utility. This observation aligns with prior reports indicating that hematological parameters have a wide range in PAM and are insufficient to reliably distinguish this infection from other causes of CNS inflammation [16, 42].

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis consistently revealed findings that closely mimic acute bacterial meningitis, including neutrophil-predominant pleocytosis, elevated protein levels, and markedly reduced glucose concentrations. Low CSF glucose in combination with elevated protein levels has been reported as a notable feature of PAM, although these findings remain indistinguishable from pyogenic meningitis [43]. The presence of red blood cells (RBCs) in the CSF, observed in several cases, may provide a supportive diagnostic clue. Although elevated RBCs can result from a traumatic lumbar puncture, they more likely reflect hemorrhagic necrosis secondary to amoebic invasion of brain tissue, with higher erythrocyte counts potentially correlating with the severity of neural necrosis and inflammation [16, 44].

Microbiologic confirmation was most commonly achieved through direct CSF microscopy, underscoring its continued diagnostic relevance, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, direct visualization of N. fowleri trophozoites is highly operator-dependent and may be prone to false-positive results, as host leukocytes and macrophages can mimic amoebic forms [45, 46]. Although molecular techniques such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and sequencing are widely regarded as efficient tools for detecting free-living amoebae, including N. fowleri, in suspected CNS infections, their routine implementation remains limited in many clinical settings because of the requirement for organism-specific assays and the associated time and resource demands [47, 48].

The diagnostic approaches used across the included patients varied, ranging from microscopy-based identification of amoebic trophozoites to molecular confirmation by PCR and sequencing. In addition, one case was diagnosed based on post-mortem brain autopsy findings. Such differences in diagnostic methods may influence the level of diagnostic certainty and should be considered when comparing findings across cases. Furthermore, the diagnostic certainty of cases confirmed solely by microscopy may be lower than that of molecularly confirmed cases, given the potential for misidentification of amoebic trophozoites.

Collectively, these findings suggest that neither routine laboratory parameters nor neuroimaging alone are sufficient for early diagnosis of PAM, underscoring the importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion and considering prompt initiation of anti-amoebic therapy when PAM is suspected.

Current therapeutic approaches for PAM rely on combination regimens rather than monotherapy, reflecting both the aggressiveness of N. fowleri infection and the limited efficacy of individual agents. Amphotericin B remains the backbone of treatment and exerts its amoebicidal effect through disruption of membrane integrity via ergosterol binding; however, its use is constrained by significant nephrotoxicity [49, 50]. Liposomal formulations have been developed to reduce renal adverse effects, although higher cost and limited availability restrict their widespread use, particularly in resource-limited settings [51].

Miltefosine, initially developed as an anti-leishmanial agent, has demonstrated activity against free-living amoebae and has been reported in the literature as part of PAM treatment regimens associated with improved survival in some cases. However, none of the patients included in this study received miltefosine therapy. Azole antifungals, including fluconazole and voriconazole, are frequently added due to their inhibitory effects on sterol biosynthesis, potentially enhancing membrane destabilization in N. fowleri [52–54]. Rifampin has frequently been used as an adjunctive agent, largely due to its favorable penetration into the CNS, while its direct anti-amoebic activity appears limited when administered alone. Collectively, these agents are administered in combination with the aim of achieving synergistic effects against the pathogen [55, 56].

Documented survivors of PAM have received early and multimodal therapy, typically involving intravenous amphotericin B, miltefosine, azoles, and rifampin, alongside intensive supportive measures. Adjunctive interventions such as corticosteroids to control cerebral edema and, in select cases, induced hypothermia to reduce intracranial pressure have been employed. Azithromycin has also been reported to exhibit synergistic activity in combination with amphotericin B, based on in vivo and in vitro studies [57–59].

Across the compiled neonatal and infant PAM cases, a wide range of therapeutic regimens was employed, most commonly centered on amphotericin B in combination with various antibacterial, antifungal, or anti-inflammatory agents. Although amphotericin B was administered in the majority of cases, outcomes varied, with death occurring in several patients despite multidrug therapy. Adjunctive agents used alongside amphotericin B included azole antifungals (such as fluconazole), rifampin, macrolides (including azithromycin), corticosteroids (most commonly dexamethasone), and a range of broad-spectrum antibacterial or antiviral agents, including ceftriaxone, vancomycin, meropenem, chloramphenicol, sulfadiazine, streptomycin, ceftazidime, metronidazole, acyclovir, and linezolid. These agents were largely initiated as part of empirical therapy to address diagnostic uncertainty and to provide coverage for alternative infectious etiologies during early clinical evaluation.

Additional supportive interventions were also used to manage complications of severe CNS involvement. Antiepileptic agents, including phenytoin, midazolam, phenobarbital, levetiracetam, and thiopental, were used to control refractory seizures, while measures such as mannitol and furosemide were administered to manage raised intracranial pressure and cerebral edema. In cases complicated by hydrocephalus, neurosurgical interventions—including external ventricular drainage or ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement—were performed, contributing to improved intracranial pressure control and clinical stabilization [21, 23, 24, 26, 28, 30, 32].

Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and neurocritical care, outcomes remain poor in neonatal and infant PAM. Among the 12 patients with available outcome data, 8 died and 4 survived, corresponding to a mortality rate of 67%. These findings emphasize the need for novel, more effective, and accessible treatment strategies, as well as innovative clinical trial designs suited to the rarity and rapid progression of this infection.

Strengths and limitations

This study represents a comprehensive, systematic synthesis of all available literature on PAM in neonates and infants. A rigorous search strategy was employed across multiple academic databases, following PRISMA guidelines, with independent dual screening and data extraction conducted to minimize bias. By integrating clinical, laboratory, imaging, and therapeutic data, the study provides a broad overview of diagnostic challenges, treatment strategies, and outcomes in this highly vulnerable population. As the first systematic review focused on neonates and infants with PAM, it provides clinically actionable insights to support early recognition, timely management, and preventive measures.

However, some limitations should be considered. The rarity of PAM in this age group may limit the generalizability of the findings. Misdiagnosis at the time of initial clinical presentation, particularly as bacterial meningitis, may have resulted in some cases not being identified or reported in the early literature. Despite the comprehensive search strategy without language restrictions, unpublished cases, non-indexed reports, or studies in less accessible languages may have been missed.

In addition, some variables were incompletely reported across the included studies, resulting in missing data for some patients. Analyses were therefore based on the available reported information.

Nonetheless, synthesizing these cases underscores important diagnostic and therapeutic considerations and suggests priorities for future research, early diagnosis, and preventive strategies in this high-risk population.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings of this review underscore the high mortality and diagnostic challenges of PAM in neonates and infants, highlighting the need for prompt clinical recognition and timely initiation of anti-amoebic therapy. In healthcare settings where bacterial meningitis is often the initial diagnostic consideration, increased awareness and vigilance among clinicians are essential to reduce delays in appropriate management.

Public health interventions aimed at minimizing exposure to contaminated water sources are critical, particularly in regions relying on untreated well water or other freshwater sources.

Educational programs for caregivers, guidance on safe water usage, and community awareness campaigns may contribute to reducing infection risk in this highly vulnerable population.

Conclusions

PAM in neonates and infants remains a devastating but underrecognized cause of rapidly fatal meningoencephalitis. This study highlights that the nonspecific clinical presentation and close resemblance to acute bacterial meningitis continue to pose major diagnostic challenges, frequently resulting in delayed initiation of appropriate anti-amoebic therapy. The available evidence suggests that early clinical suspicion, prompt microbiological confirmation—particularly through direct CSF examination and molecular methods—and early initiation of combination therapy may be associated with improved survival, although outcomes remain poor overall.

Importantly, our findings underscore the need for heightened awareness of PAM among clinicians involved in neonatal and infant care, especially in cases of meningitis that do not respond to standard antibacterial treatment. In addition to clinical vigilance, preventive strategies, including ensuring access to safe household water and avoiding exposure of neonates and infants to untreated environmental water sources, represent critical and potentially life-saving public health measures. Given the persistently high mortality rate and the limited evidence base, future efforts should focus on improving rapid diagnostic approaches, optimizing therapeutic regimens, and strengthening preventive interventions to reduce the burden of this often fatal infection in this highly vulnerable population.
