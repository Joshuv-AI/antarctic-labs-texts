Skeletal and dentoalveolar effects of different hyrax maxillary expansion protocols compared with novel magnetic expansion: a CBCT-based study
Authors: Ahmed Lotf Algahefi, Maged S. Alhammadi, Zhihua Li, Fei Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare the skeletal, dentoalveolar, and dental effects of rapid (RME), slow (SME) maxillary expansion, and magnetic maxillary expansion (MME) in adolescents with transverse maxillary deficiency using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). A retrospective analysis was conducted on 39 patients (24 males, 15 females; mean age 12.8 ± 0.9 years), equally divided into three groups (N = 13 each) based on the expansion protocol: RME, SME using hyrax expander appliance, or MME. All patients underwent CBCT imaging at baseline (T1), post-expansion (T2), and after a three-month retention period (T3) were extracted. Skeletal, dentoalveolar, and dental parameters, including maxillary basal widths, alveolar inclination, posterior tooth inclination, vertical dental height, and maxillary molar volume, were measured. Statistical analysis was performed using paired t-tests and ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (p < 0.05). All three protocols produced significant skeletal and dental expansion from T1 to T2 (p < 0.001). RME achieved the greatest skeletal widening, particularly at the nasal floor (p = 0.013), but was associated with more buccal tipping, molar volume reduction, and significantly greater relapse at the nasal floor during retention (p = 0.006). SME provided moderate but stable outcomes with fewer side effects. MME demonstrated skeletal expansion comparable to SME and, in some parameters, to RME, while showing less relapse and smaller reductions in molar volume. Vertical dental height changes were significant across groups. All expansion methods successfully addressed maxillary constriction. RME delivers quick skeletal corrections but presents a higher risk of relapse and dentoalveolar complications. SME yields stable, gradual results, whereas MME shows promise as a viable alternative, offering effective skeletal expansion with fewer negative effects and potential biological benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38665-7.

Introduction

Maxillary transverse deficiency is one of the most prevalent skeletal problems in orthodontic practice. It is frequently associated with posterior crossbite, crowding, and constricted maxillary arch form, with functional implications like impaired mastication and airway obstruction1,2. Epidemiological studies estimate that posterior crossbite occurs in approximately 8–20% of the general population, making transverse deficiency a clinically significant condition requiring early recognition and intervention3. The correction of maxillary constriction depends on rapid maxillary expansion (RME), a technique first introduced in the mid-nineteenth century and refined over the decades. RME involves the mechanical separation of the mid-palatal suture, leading to skeletal widening of the maxilla, accompanied by secondary dental and alveolar adaptations4,5.

Among the various expansion appliances, the Hyrax expander remains the most widely used due to its straightforward design, ease of fabrication, and ability to achieve rapid orthopedic effects5,6. The Hyrax expander is a tooth-borne device that transmits expansion forces via the posterior anchor teeth to the mid-palatal suture. Several clinical and radiographic investigations have documented its effectiveness in achieving skeletal separation in children and adolescents, with significant increases in intermolar and intercanine widths7,8. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) studies repeatedly demonstrate that the orthopedic effect is frequently accompanied by undesired dentoalveolar changes. These include buccal tipping of anchor teeth, alveolar bending, fenestrations of cortical bone, and reduced periodontal support9,10.

Research into the use of magnetic forces for maxillary expansion dates to the early 1980s. Experimental studies on animal models demonstrated that magnets could successfully produce separation of the mid-palatal suture, thereby establishing a foundation for subsequent clinical use11,12. Unlike conventional screw-activated expanders, magnetic devices apply light, continuous forces that are considered more biologically compatible. This steady mode of force delivery helps to minimize excessive stress on the anchoring teeth and reduces the potential for trauma to the palatal suture and adjacent tissues13. Evidence from experimental studies indicates that magnetic forces stimulate greater osteoclastic activity compared with conventional mechanical loading. This enhanced cellular response suggests that periodontal tissue remodeling may occur more efficiently under magnetic influence, thereby facilitating tooth movement14. Moreover, the increased presence of osteoclasts creates a biologically favorable environment for controlled orthodontic displacement. Complementary investigations using animal models and tissue cultures have further demonstrated that exposure to magnetic fields promotes osteoblastic activity and new bone formation within the palatal suture, reinforcing the potential of magnetic forces as a biologically advantageous alternative for maxillary expansion14,15. A major limitation of magnetic maxillary expansion (MME) is the rapid decline in force as the dental arch widens and the distance between magnetic poles increases. To counteract this attenuation, some investigators have attempted to incorporate resin spacers or additional magnetic blocks to reduce the inter-magnet gap and sustain a consistent orthodontic force11,13. While such modifications can stabilize the applied force, they are often cumbersome in clinical use and may pose hygiene challenges. The previously developed magnetic maxillary expander with a reactivation system simplified the need for repeated force reloading; however, its bulky design led to a rapid reduction in expansion force (from 10.38 N down to 1.63 N), offered only a limited activation range of about 6 mm, and still required a relatively complex reactivation protocol16. Fei Tong et al. introduced a novel magnetic palatal expansion appliance (MPEA) with a more compact design and a simplified reactivation protocol. Preclinical experiments in Beagle dogs demonstrated that this device effectively separated the mid-palatal suture and produced favorable orthodontic outcomes17. Notably, the new MPEA delivered a more consistent expansion force ranging from 10.7 to 5.5 N and offered an extended working range of 7.5 mm. Its efficacy was comprehensively validated through a combination of CBCT assessments and tetracycline fluorescence labeling, confirming both skeletal and tissue-level remodeling effects17.

Despite promising experimental results, the clinical use of magnetic maxillary expanders has remained relatively limited, largely due to concerns regarding their cost, accessibility, and the scarcity of rigorous comparative studies. This gap in the evidence base highlights the need for well-structured clinical investigations that directly contrast magnetic expanders with conventional or standard appliances such as the hyrax expander.

Current evidence indicates that both rapid and slow maxillary expansion protocols are effective in achieving clinically meaningful dental and skeletal maxillary expansion, while rapid maxillary expansion tends to produce a slightly greater increase in posterior transverse skeletal width. Additionally, slow maxillary expansion is associated with more favorable dentoalveolar responses, particularly reduced molar inclination and buccal tipping effects18.

From a stability perspective, long-term follow-up studies suggest that transverse corrections achieved with either rapid or slow expansion are generally stable when appropriate retention protocols are applied and patients are observed for extended periods after active treatment19.

In terms of patient-reported outcomes, slow maxillary expansion has been shown to result in significantly lower pain intensity during the initial week of activation compared with rapid expansion, although these differences diminish over time, with no significant pain differences observed between protocols in subsequent weeks20.

In the present study, the magnetic maxillary expander (MME), derived from the original MPEA design described by Fei Tong et al.17, was fabricated and clinically applied with minor modifications for human use. Accordingly, the present study seeks to provide a comparative clinical evaluation of a newly developed magnetic maxillary expander and traditional hyrax expansion devices (both rapid and slow expansion protocols). The primary focus is to assess their effects on basal bone dimensions, maxillary arch width, dentoalveolar structures, and tooth volume, thereby offering a comprehensive understanding of both skeletal and dental adaptations in a clinical setting.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study was carried out in the outpatient clinic of the Department of Orthodontics, School of Stomatology, Nanchang University, China. Ethical clearance was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Nanchang University (NO. 2023-009), China. All study procedures followed the applicable laws, regulations, and institutional guidelines to ensure compliance with ethical standards.

Sample size and data selection

The required sample size was estimated using G*Power software (Version 3.1.3; Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany). Calculations were based on a two-tailed test with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 85%. Data from a pilot study were used to inform the calculation, which showed a mean difference in expansion between T1 and T2 of 2.96 ± 1.46 mm at the alveolo-palatal junction for the RME and 1.68 ± 0.51 mm for the MME. This analysis indicated that a minimum of 12 patients per group would be required. This number was increased to 13 participants in each group to strengthen statistical reliability.

Patient records were retrospectively collected from the Department of Radiology, Affiliated Stomatology Hospital of Nanchang University, China, for individuals diagnosed and treated between January 2020 and July 2024. Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) age between 10 and 15 years, corresponding to the permanent or late mixed dentition stage; (2) clinical diagnosis of maxillary constriction; and (3) radiographic confirmation showing negligible root or alveolar bone resorption. Written informed consent obtained before the start of treatment by parent or legal guardian.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) incomplete or poor-quality CBCT records; (2) unerupted maxillary first molars; (3) craniofacial congenital anomalies including cleft palate, whether treated or untreated; (4) history of facial trauma; (5) history of facial surgery; (6) systemic diseases or long-term medication; (7) presence of a cardiac pacemaker; (8) maxillary cysts or tumors; (9) compromised periodontal health, active oral infections, or poor oral hygiene.

Magnetic maxillary expansion device

The magnetic maxillary expansion (MME) appliance used in this study was custom-fabricated and derived from the original magnetic palatal expansion appliance (MPEA) described by Fei Tong et al.17 that was designed for dogs, with minor modifications for human application. The MME, designed for adolescent patients, adopts a rotary locking mechanism after expansion separates the magnets by ~ 1.5–2 mm, one side is manually pushed back into contact, and then a semicircular rotating limit bar is turned 180° inward to secure the box. This design improves precision and clinical operability in human patients, allowing easier reactivation and stable repositioning.

‏The appliance is a tooth-borne expander consisting of paired permanent magnets enclosed within track-type units, with identical magnetic poles oriented face-to-face to generate a controlled repulsive force. A spiral limiting/locking rod was incorporated to regulate activation and improve appliance stability throughout treatment. For infection control, the magnetic components were disinfected using a low-temperature chemical sterilization protocol to prevent demagnetization. The magnets were enclosed within sealed housing to minimize direct exposure to the oral environment. Throughout the treatment period, no device-related adverse events were recorded. Contraindications for MME therapy included the presence of implanted electronic devices, known metal allergies, ferromagnetic craniofacial implants, and anticipated magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) during treatment.

Maxillary expansion protocol

Casted maxillary expanders were cemented to the upper dental arch using glass ionomer cement (Fig. 2), and appliance activation was initiated immediately after placement. The activation regimen differed according to the treatment group.Fig. 2Types of maxillary expanders: (A) Magnetic maxillary expander; (B) Hyrax maxillary expander.

Types of maxillary expanders: (A) Magnetic maxillary expander; (B) Hyrax maxillary expander.

Magnetic maxillary expansion (MME) clinical operation: To standardize the reactivation protocol, the appliance was reactivated every two weeks by disengaging the internal spiral limiter rod with a 180° inward rotation, approximating the paired magnetic units, and subsequently re-locking the mechanism with a 180° outward rotation. Twice-monthly reactivation was selected based on pilot clinical observations demonstrating that, after approximately two weeks of wear, magnet separation typically reached 1.5–2.0 mm, resulting in a reduction of magnetic force below the level required for effective expansion. Mechanical testing confirmed this force decay, supporting the scheduled two-week reactivation to restore optimal force delivery. This protocol ensured the maintenance of light, continuous forces conducive to controlled mid-palatal sutural remodeling while minimizing dentoalveolar side effects and preserving patient comfort as shown in the supplementary video (Supplementary Material 1).

Rapid maxillary expansion (RME): The appliance was activated twice daily, once in the morning and once in the evening, with each activation consisting of a quarter-turn of the screw10.

Slow maxillary expansion (SME): The screw was activated with a quarter-turn rotation every 48 h21.

The endpoint of expansion was defined a priori as the moment when the palatal cusps of the maxillary first permanent molars contacted the buccal cusps of the mandibular first permanent molars. This assessment was performed in centric occlusion and bilaterally using the same clinical endpoint criteria across all three groups (RME, SME, and MME) to ensure protocol consistency and comparability.

Following expansion, the screw or magnetic components were sealed with flowable resin, and the appliances were retained in situ for three months.

In the RME and SME groups, activations were performed by the patients’ parents at home after standardized verbal and written instructions were provided by the treating orthodontist. In the MME group, all activations were performed exclusively by a trained clinician in the clinic during scheduled visits, due to the technical nature of the magnetic reactivation procedure.

Compliance was monitored using scheduled follow-up visits, during which appliance status and activation progress were verified. For RME and SME, adherence was assessed by confirming the expected screw separation and activation count at each visit. For MME, compliance was ensured by direct in-clinic activation under clinician supervision. Noncompliance was defined as a discrepancy between the expected and observed activation progress or missed scheduled visits; however, no cases met the criteria for noncompliance or were excluded for this reason.

CBCT imaging

All CBCT examinations were performed with a three-dimensional (3D) eXam scanner (KaVo Dental, Biberach, Germany). The scans were acquired under standardized conditions: 120 kVp, 20.27 mAs, a rotation time of 14.7 s, a voxel resolution of 0.25 mm, and a field of view (FOV) of 16 × 16 × 10 cm. To reduce operator-related variability, all imaging procedures were carried out by a single trained technician. Patients were seated upright and instructed to bite in centric occlusion with their eyes looking straight ahead, the orbital–auricular plane was carefully aligned parallel to the floor, and the cranial axis was maintained perpendicular to the ground22,23. The image data were exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format. Skeletal and dentoalveolar parameters were analyzed using Dolphin Imaging software (version 11.95; Dolphin Imaging and Management Solutions, Chatsworth, CA), while volumetric evaluation of the maxillary first molars was performed using Mimics Medical Software (Materialise NV, version 21.0, Leuven, Belgium).

Skeletal and dentoalveolar measurement analysis

Proper head orientation in the three orthogonal planes was carried out to minimize discrepancies caused by variations in patient posture during image acquisition and to enhance the reproducibility of subsequent measurements. Once the appropriate coronal reference slice was confirmed, a comprehensive evaluation of skeletal and dentoalveolar parameters was performed using Dolphin Imaging software.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical package for social studies software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented as mean and standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of data distribution. Within-group comparisons between time points (T1 vs. T2 and T2 vs. T3) were analyzed using paired t-tests, while between-group differences were evaluated with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When significant differences were detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess measurement reliability, both intra-operator and inter-operator consistency were evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Result

The study cohort consisted of 24 males (61.5%) and 15 females (38.5%), with a mean age of 12.8 ± 0.9 years (range: 10–15 years). Measurement reliability was confirmed to be high, with intra-operator ICC ranging from 0.943 to 0.967 and inter-operator ICC values from 0.925 to 0.943, ensuring both accuracy and reproducibility of the recorded measurements.

At baseline characteristics, no statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups (RME, SME, and MME) across skeletal, dentoalveolar, and dental parameters (p > 0.05) as shown in Supplementary Table 2.

All three expansion protocols, RME, SME, MME produced significant transverse skeletal and dental increases from T1 to T2 (p < 0.001) as shown in Tables 1,2 and 3. The IMW and IPW expanded similarly across groups, with RME showing the largest average gain (IMW: 6.41 ± 3.47 mm; IPW: 7.41 ± 5.40 mm). However, these differences were not statistically significant between groups (p = 0.68 and p = 0.63, respectively). Changes in alveolar inclination were non-significant except on the right side in the MME group. Posterior teeth demonstrated buccal tipping following expansion, which was most evident in the RME group. Vertical dental height decreased, indicating molar intrusion, and MAX1.MV decreased in all groups during expansion. The greatest reduction was observed in RME (left: − 44.6 ± 60.2 mm3; right: − 33.1 ± 17.5 mm3), with MME showed the least reduction (left: − 23.7 ± 16.6 mm3; right: − 19.1 ± 21.3 mm3). Although these trends suggest that RME may be associated with more pronounced volumetric loss, the intergroup comparisons did not reach statistical significance (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated three different approaches of maxillary expansion protocols, RME, SME, and MME in growing patients with transverse maxillary deficiency. Using CBCT imaging, we assessed changes at both the skeletal, dentoalveolar, and dental levels. All three techniques were effective in producing significant transverse expansion, but the extent of the changes, their stability over time, and the associated side effects varied between protocols.

At the skeletal level, RME consistently shows the largest skeletal transverse expansion. NFW and MW.NF increased significantly more in the RME group compared with SME and MME (p = 0.013 and p = 0.017, respectively). These findings support that from a biological standpoint, RME generates heavy forces at the sutural interface in a brief period, causing immediate separation of the mid-palatal suture by disrupting its connective tissue24,25. Several previous investigations support our results, showing that RME produces greater skeletal widening than SME26–29. The MW.HP, along with other skeletal parameters, showed a similar trend, with RME producing the greatest changes, although these differences were not statistically significant. Consistent with our findings, Martina et al. also compared RME and SME and reported greater skeletal expansion with RME; however, the differences were modest and did not reach statistical significance30.

Darendeliler et al. reported a 26.2% skeletal expansion effect with magnetic expansion in adolescents, mainly in the posterior region. In contrast, our study found greater and statistically significant skeletal expansion with MME, particularly at the nasal floor and maxillary base13. These differences may reflect variations in appliance design, force application, imaging methods, and patient characteristics, as well as the small sample sizes used in such studies. In contrast, SME and MME showed similar moderate skeletal gains. This observation is consistent with the preliminary animal experiments reported by Fei Tong et al.17, who noted a similar pattern of skeletal expansion between MPEA and SME. They emphasized that this resemblance is noteworthy, as it suggests that MPEA, although it relies on a distinct mechanism of delivering continuous, gentle forces, can achieve skeletal effects comparable to those produced by SME protocols.

At the alveolar bone level, all groups showed significant increases in IMW and IPW. Although RME recorded the highest mean values, the differences among groups were not statistically significant. Several studies consistent with our findings have shown that RME produces greater skeletal expansion than SME26,29. For ECW, only slight increases were observed in the RME, SME, and MME groups compared with EPMW and EMW, again without significant intergroup differences. Our results align with previous studies showing that expansion tends to be greater in the molar region than in the canine area29,31–34. These outcomes are in line with the preliminary animal experiments reported by Fei Tong et al.17, which demonstrated that the mid-palatal suture may open either in parallel or in a wedged form, with narrower anterior and wider posterior separation. Comparable findings were also reported by Habersack et al.35 and Christie et al.36, who attributed the linear expansion pattern of the suture to factors such as the rigidity of the appliance, its position, and the patient’s age. In contrast to our results, Liu et al.21 and Gautam et al.37 observed that skeletal expansion was more pronounced anteriorly and diminished posteriorly. This discrepancy may be explained by the influence of posterior maxillary structures, such as the zygomatic and sphenoid bones, which can restrict the degree of opening in the posterior portion of the mid-palatal suture.

There was a slight decrease in alveolar bone level observed across all groups, with the RME group showing the most reduction, although the differences did not reach statistical significance. This pattern may be related to localized bone loss caused by the higher forces generated during rapid expansion, whereas SME and MME appeared to exert a milder effect. These findings are consistent with previous studies7,38,39. Furthermore, more recent investigations and systematic reviews have highlighted the periodontal and endodontic risks associated with RME, particularly when the first permanent molars are used as anchorage teeth40–42.

Concerning dental effects, posterior tooth inclination showed a consistent tendency toward buccal tipping across all groups, with the greatest mean changes observed in the RME group for both M.T.I. and PM.T.I. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.5). This outcome agrees with previous studies reporting that buccal tipping is a common side effect of both SME and RME protocols7,43,44. Moreover, other investigations have similarly found that RME tends to produce greater buccal tipping than SME18,45. Pereira et al.26 reported that the RME group exhibited nearly twice the amount of buccal tipping compared with the SME group, with significant differences between the protocols. In contrast, Martina et al.30 found no evidence of buccal tipping associated with slow expansion. These results differ from our findings. The smaller inclination changes observed in SME and MME may reflect the effect of lighter and more continuous forces, which distribute stress more evenly and reduce the risk of dental side effects46,47.

Vertical dental height showed slight reductions in all groups, with changes remaining small and statistically non-significant (p > 0.5). This indicates a minor degree of molars intrusion during expansion, a finding consistent with previous CBCT studies48,49. Lione et al.40 observed that bonded expanders and occlusal coverage can transmit intrusive forces to posterior teeth, producing modest vertical changes without major clinical consequences. Also reported a slight relative intrusion of the first molars following expansion. In contrast, Chung et al.50 and Silva Filho et al.51 reported that supporting teeth may undergo extrusion immediately after expansion. This suggests that vertical changes can vary depending on appliance design and the force system.

Molar volume analysis revealed that RME was associated with the greatest volumetric reduction (left: − 44.6 ± 60.2 mm3; right: − 33.1 ± 17.5 mm3), whereas MME showed the smallest decrease (left: − 23.7 ± 16.6 mm3; right: − 19.1 ± 21.3 mm3). Although intergroup differences were not statistically significant, these findings raise the possibility that the higher intermittent forces of RME may contribute to greater dentoalveolar remodeling or root surface loss, while SME and MME exert gentler effects on dental structures. Previous studies consistent with our findings have shown that RME using tooth-borne expanders can contribute to root resorption, as the heavy forces generated during activation are transferred to the maxilla through the anchoring teeth52. Leonardi et al.53 further demonstrated that RME was associated with a reduction in root volume of the maxillary first molars as well as the first and second premolars, with the extent of volumetric loss being particularly pronounced.

During the 3-month retention period, some relapse was noted in all groups, with the RME group showing significantly greater skeletal relapse at the MW.NF compared with SME and MME. Other skeletal and dental parameters exhibited only minor, non-significant changes, particularly in the SME and MME groups. These results agree with Fei Tong et al.17 who found that both SME and MPEA could effectively maintain expansion without further activation. Notably, magnetic expansion delivers a continuous, gentle force that supports bone remodeling, and previous studies suggest its magnetic field may also enhance bone metabolism, providing a potential biological advantage over conventional methods16,54.

This study guides clinicians when selecting maxillary expansion protocols in growing patients. Rapid maxillary expansion remains effective for rapid skeletal correction but carries higher risks of relapse and dentoalveolar side effects, underscoring the need for careful case selection and extended retention. SME offers slower but more stable results, making it suitable for patients who benefit from gradual, biologically favorable expansion. Magnetic maxillary expansion combines effectiveness with greater stability and fewer adverse effects, while also offering advantages such as continuous gentle forces and reduced reliance on patient compliance. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring treatment to individual patients’ needs and encourage further research into the long-term clinical potential of magnetic expansion.

Limitation

The limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 13 per group) may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences between expansion protocols. In addition, the retention period of three months restricts conclusions regarding long-term post-expansion stability. Although repeated-measures or mixed-effects models could formally assess group over time interactions across all time points, the present analysis was intentionally limited to predefined, clinically relevant intervals (T1–T2 and T2–T3). Consequently, global longitudinal interaction effects were not evaluated, which should be considered a methodological limitation. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and longitudinal analytical models ideally incorporating CBCT-based assessments are warranted to more comprehensively characterize the skeletal and dentoalveolar effects of MME, SME, and RME.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that all three maxillary expansion protocols, rapid, slow, and magnetic maxillary expansion, were effective in achieving significant transverse skeletal and dental changes in adolescents with maxillary constriction. Rapid expansion produced the greatest immediate skeletal widening but was associated with higher relapse and more pronounced dentoalveolar side effects. Slow expansion provided more moderate yet stable outcomes, reflecting its suitability for gradual and biologically favorable correction. Magnetic expansion achieved expansion comparable to slow expansion while demonstrating greater stability and fewer adverse dental effects, suggesting that it may represent a promising alternative in clinical practice.
