Prevalence of canine herpesvirus 1 and associated risk factors among bitches in Iranian breeding kennels and farms
Authors: Mahdieh Rezaei, Maziar Jajarmi, Saba Kamani, Mohammad Khalili, Homayoon Babaei
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanine herpesvirus 1 (CHV‐1) is an important cause of death in newborn puppies and of fertility problems in adult dogs. Identification of risk factors may help to reduce infection rates and alleviate concerns for dog owners and breeders. This study was designed to screen for CHV‐1 infection in bitches of breeding kennels and farms in Iran and relate this to possible risk factors.MethodsA total of 63 vaginal samples were collected from dogs in 5 breeding kennels (n = 47) and from 7 farms (n = 16). Real‐time polymerase chain reaction was used to detect the CHV‐1 specific glycoprotein B (gB) gene. Prevalence rates were evaluated in relation to various risk factors, including region, housing, vaccination, deworming, pregnancy, reproductive problems, number of dogs living together and hygiene conditions.ResultsIn total, 21 (33.3%) of 63 vaginal samples were positive for CHV‐1 DNA. The prevalence rate in farms (7/16; 43.7%) was higher than in kennels (14/47; 29.7%). No association was found between CHV‐1 prevalence and potential risk factors.ConclusionsCHV‐1 is highly prevalent in dogs in Iranian farms and kennels. Since the CHV1 vaccine is unlicensed in Iran, effective management strategies are essential to reduce the consequences of this pathogen.Keywords: canine herpesvirus‐1 (CHV‐1), dog, farm, kennel, real‐time PCR, vagina

BACKGROUND

Canine herpesvirus 1 (CHV‐1) is an enveloped and double‐stranded DNA α‐herpesvirus which is common in dogs, worldwide. Infection results in reproductive disorders and neonatal mortalities in dogs (Greene et al., 2012; Nöthling et al., 2008; Ström Holst et al., 2012). The host range of CHV‐1 is restricted to domestic and wild canids (Decaro et al., 2010; Greene et al., 2012; Ledbetter et al., 2009). Transmission occurs through the respiratory or genital routes (Evermann et al., 2011; Ronsse et al., 2004); it is transmitted to newborn puppies whilst in the birth canal or by direct contact with mucosal secretions (Evermann et al., 2011; Gadsden et al., 2012). Clinical signs vary depending on the age and immunity of the infected animal (Decaro et al., 2010).

Neonatal puppies are particularly susceptible to the CHV‐1 showing a fatal multisystemic disease characterized by focal necro‐hemorrhagic disorders in parenchymatous organs (Cargnelutti et al., 2015; Carmichael et al., 1965). In puppies older than 3–4 weeks and adults, asymptomatic or mild and subclinical self‐limiting infections are most common. Infections in pregnant bitches are responsible for foetal death, abortion, mummification and stillbirths (Ronsse et al., 2005). Respiratory disease, ocular signs and genital lesions are also reported (Ledbetter et al., 2009; Lefebvre et al., 2015; Losurdo et al., 2018; Piewbang et al., 2017). Lifelong latent infection is associated with localization of CHV‐1 to nerve ganglia following recovery (Greene et al., 2012). Reactivation may occur subsequent to stress or immunosuppression resulting in CHV‐1 replication, and viral shedding (Bottinelli et al., 2016; Ledbetter et al., 2009; Zubair et al., 2014).

Various diagnostic methods have been established to detect CHV‐1 infection. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is the most sensitive and most specific technique, especially for the detection of latent infection (Decaro et al., 2010; Greene et al., 2012; Ledbetter et al., 2009). Serologic studies have suggested prevalence rates of zero to 100% among domestic dogs in kennels of various parts of the world (Babaei et al., 2010; Greene et al., 2012). Recently, we screened CHV‐1 in vaginal specimens and uterine biopsies of dogs referred to the veterinary hospital of Shahid Bahonar University of Kerman (Rezaei et al., 2020). We have shown a significant outbreak of the virus that highlights the need for screening of agent in dog kennels (Rezaei et al., 2020). Previous studies have shown factors, such as kennel size, hygiene, breeding management, age, mating experience, history of abortion in bitches and kennel cough infection to be primary contributing risk factors for CHV‐1 infection and disease (Evermann et al., 2011; Ronsse et al., 2004). This study was designed to determine the incidence of CHV‐1 in dogs in breeding kennels and animal farms (horse and ruminant) in the southeast of Iran, Kerman, using real‐time PCR, and to investigate possible predisposing factors.

METHODS

Sample collection and DNA extraction

A total of 63 bitches, including 47 dogs from five breeding kennels and 16 dogs from seven animal farms, were sampled during 2017–2018. All study procedures were approved by Animal Care Committee of Veterinary College of Shahid Bahonar University of Kerman (No: 970102). These participating kennels and farms were located in various parts of Kerman city, in the southeast of Iran (Figure 1). All samples were obtained following permission from the dogs’ owners. A detailed questionnaire was used to record time of sampling, region, age, breed, housing, vaccination, deworming, pregnancy, whelping and the status of puppies, reproductive problems, number of dogs and hygiene condition of enclosures. Vaginal examination was performed to evaluate for signs of CHV‐1 infection including abnormal discharge, masses and altered appearance of the vaginal mucosa.

Geographical location of sampling sites: map of Iran showing the location of Kerman (left) and map of Kerman city showing the location of sampling sites (right). A–E are the kennels and A′–G′ are the farms.

After disinfection of the vulva, vaginal swabs were collected from each animal using sterile rayon‐tipped applicators (Puritan) and were reserved in sterile 1.5 mL microtubes containing phosphate buffer saline. All specimens were transferred to the laboratory and stored at −80°C. Two weeks after storage, DNA extraction was done using a Viral Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sterile distilled water was included as a negative control for every three samples.

Molecular detection of CHV‐1 (real‐time PCR assay)

The CHV‐1 glycoprotein B (gB) gene was detected using a real‐time PCR assay. A total volume of 20 μL containing 10 μL of RealQ Plus 2x Master Mix Green (Ampliqon), 0.3 μL of each primer (work stock solution was 10 pmol/μL), 2.5 μL of DNA template and 6.9 μL of distilled water was used for each PCR reaction. Forward primer (5′‐ACAGAGTTGATTGATAGAAGAGGTATG‐3′) and reverse primer (5′‐CTGGTGTATTAAACTTTGAAGGCTTTA‐3′) were used as previously described, producing amplicons of 136 bp (Piewbang et al., 2016).

The PCR thermal cycling consisted of one cycle at 95°C for 15 min for activation of hot start Taq DNA polymerase followed by 40 cycles, including denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing at 65°C for 20 s and elongation at 72°C for 20 s, followed by a melting step at 95°C for 10 s, 65°C for 10 s and 97°C for 1 s. Threshold cycle number (C t) and melting temperature (T m) of each amplicon were automatically analysed using software on LightCycler 96 System (Roche) (Figure 2A,B). Samples were considered positive, if their C t values were 38 or lower.

Performance evaluation of the real‐time polymerase chain reaction (real‐time PCR) to detect glycoprotein B (gB) sequence belonging to canine herpesvirus 1 (CHV‐1): (A) C t curves of 10−1 (C t = 29.02), 10−2 (C t = 32.37) and 10−3 (C t = 35.58) copies of genomic DNA per reaction, (B) T m peaks of 10, 10−2 and 10−3 copies of genomic DNA per reaction (T m = 76.8°C), (C) standard curve showing C t values of 10‐fold serial diluted genomic DNA of CHV‐1 including 1/1000 (10 copies), 1/100 (102 copies) and 1/100 (103 copies of genomic DNA); slope = −3.28, E = 2.017 and % efficiency = 101.7%.

Negative controls comprised the sterile distilled water samples from the DNA extraction step. DNA extracted from an indirect immunofluorescence antibody (IFA) detection kit containing a slide coated with CHV‐1 (Mega Screen FLUO CHV; MegaCor) was used as a positive control. Efficiency (E) of the real‐time PCR was calculated according to the test on three dilutions of positive control including 10−1, 10−2 and 10−3 (Figure 2A). C t values of dilutions were entered into Excel software and the reduction slope was calculated. Efficiency was obtained from the formula ‘E = 10(−1/slope)’ and was determined as a percentage via ‘% efficiency = (E − 1) × 100’ (Figure 2C).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS software (version 21; SPSS Inc.) with regression analysis (generalized linear model) and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Canine herpesvirus 1 (CHV‐1) prevalence categorized by age, breed, kennel, farm, pregnancy and reproductive disorders.

Twenty (31.7%) were <2 years old and 43 (68.2%) were 2–7‐year old at the time of sampling (Table 1). Ten dogs from the <2‐year age group were positive for CHV‐1 and 11 CHV‐1‐positive dogs belonged to the 2–7‐year age group. There was no association (p = 0.29) between CHV‐1 infection and age (Table 1).

The dogs comprised 14 different breeds with a predominance of German shepherd dogs (n = 42; 66.7%) (Table 1). There was no association (p = 0.77) between breed and CHV‐1 status (Table 1). Six (6/63; 9.5%) dogs were pregnant at the time of sampling, three of whom (3/6; 50%) were CHV‐1‐positive. Reproductive disorders were identified in 39.7% (25/63) of dogs, of which 7/25 (28%) were CHV‐1 positive. No significant differences (p = 0.90) were observed between CHV‐1 status and pregnancy (p = 0.90), or regarding the histories and parameters of reproductive disorders (p = 0.71) (Table 1).

Of the 63 vaginal swab samples, 47 (74.6%) belonged to dogs from breeding kennels. Among these, 14/47 samples (29.7%) were positive for CHV‐1. The highest prevalence of CHV‐1 was found in kennel A (Table 1). Sixteen (16/63; 25.4%) samples were collected from dogs kept on seven farms, all of whom were in contact with other animal species but not with other dogs. Of these, seven (7/16; 43.7%) were positive for CHV‐1 (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

CHV‐1 was first recognized by Carmichael et al. (1965) and, since then, has been found to be endemic in dog populations in several countries (Cargnelutti et al., 2015; Losurdo et al., 2018). The present survey is one of the few molecular studies to detect CHV‐1 in vaginal samples obtained from bitches in kennels and farms in Iran.

Serological studies have suggested a wide range of geographical exposure rates ranging from 22% to 85.5% in dogs (Dahlbom et al., 2009; Krogenæs et al., 2014; Yeşilbağ et al., 2012). A study performed in the southeast of Iran reported seroprevalence rates of 22.9% and 19.1% for kennelled and owned dogs respectively using an indirect IFA assay (Babaei et al., 2010). Reported exposure rates are likely to vary according to methodology and previous studies have variously used ELISA, IFA, virus neutralization and immunoperoxidase monolayer assays (Babaei et al., 2010; Carmichael et al., 1965; Dahlbom et al., 2009; Krogenæs et al., 2014; Nöthling et al., 2008; Yeşilbağ et al., 2012).

Serological techniques alone are not an ideal choice to detect CHV‐1 infections because the virus has poor immunogenic properties and neutralizing antibodies are found for only a short time following exposure (Greene et al., 2012). PCR testing is therefore advocated as a complementary test to confirm active infections (Decaro et al., 2010; Greene et al., 2012; Ledbetter et al., 2009). Conversely, some studies have failed to detect CHV‐1 DNA in dogs despite seroconversion which may be explained by latent infections, sampling methods and assay sensitivity (Bottinelli et al., 2016; Pratelli et al., 2014; Ronsse et al., 2005; Valdivia Lara et al., 2016).

Decaro et al. (2010) described a CHV‐1 real‐time PCR as a useful tool to confirm a clinical diagnosis, to study viral pathogenesis and to evaluate the efficacy of vaccines and antiviral drugs. Kapil et al. (2015) also optimized a CHV‐1‐specific PCR targeting gB gene to detect DNA in formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded tissues and offered the gB gene as an appropriate target. Accordingly, a real‐time PCR targeting this glycoprotein B (gB) gene was used in this study for the detection of CHV‐1 DNA in vaginal secretions. In the present study, CHV‐1 was detected in 21 (33.3%) of 63 vaginal samples using a real‐time PCR assay. This is higher than in our previous study on reproductive specimens of adult dogs (15%) (Rezaei et al., 2020), and some other studies in the Brazil (6%) (Monteiro et al., 2016) and the United States (12.8%) (Erles et al., 2004). In our study, the positive specimens belonged to dogs from all sampled kennels and animal farms, with no premises being virus‐free.

In the current study, the detection of CHV‐1 was not associated with age, which is in agreement with several previous studies (Bottinelli et al., 2016; Krogenæs et al., 2012; Nöthling et al., 2008). On the other hand, others have reported lower rates of CHV‐1 infection in younger dogs (Babaei et al., 2010; Evermann et al., 2011; Ronsse et al., 2004). No association was found between breed and CHV‐1 positivity but small and unbalanced numbers within the 14 breed categories made comparisons unreliable. Previously, Bottinelli et al. (2016) found no breed association and, whereas Yeşilbağ et al. (2012) found the highest CHV‐1 prevalence in Golden Retrievers (56.2%), followed by Terriers (50.0%), there was again no significant association.

It is well known that stressful conditions such as pregnancy result in viral reactivation and shedding of the virus (Greene et al., 2012). In this study, approximately one tenth of dogs were pregnant at the time of sampling and half of these samples were positive for CHV‐1. Still, no statistical association between pregnancy status and CHV‐1 infection was found. Similarly, Ström Holst et al. (2012) did not detect any difference in antibody titres between pregnant and non‐pregnant bitches. They also stated that ‘pregnancy alone does not seem to cause reactivation of the infection when management is good and other stress factors are absent’ (Ström Holst et al., 2012). Krogenæs et al. (2012) observed no association between CHV1 antibody titres and reproductive parameters like pregnancy.

In the current study, reproductive disorders were established in 25/63 (39.7%) dogs, of which 7 were infected with CHV‐1. No significant association was identified between infection and reproductive disorders and only one dog had vaginal papules and CHV‐1 infection simultaneously. Similarly, other researchers could not determine any significant differences between CHV‐1 infection and bitches with reproductive disorders compared with healthy fertile dogs (Pratelli et al., 2014; Ronsse et al., 2004; Ronsse et al., 2005). However, kennel A had a history of stillbirth, abortion and neonatal mortality and showed the highest CHV‐1 prevalence. Ronsse et al. (2004) also demonstrated an association between serological results and a history of abortion in bitches. According to Krogenæs et al. (2012), no significant associations were observed between antibody titre and breeding status, history of mattings, whelping, infertility and conditions of puppies, whereas, Dahlbom et al. (2009) observed higher CHV‐1 titres in dogs from kennels with reproductive problems compared with those that had no history of reproductive disorders, significantly. Previous studies show some relationship between seroprevalence and reproductive disorders, but no significant relationship has been observed between CHV‐1 DNA detection and the disease risk factors. It has been shown, that population size, management and hygienic conditions can influence the incidence of CHV‐1 infection via the reactivation of latent infections (Dahlbom et al., 2009). Indeed, the percentage of positive specimens in the current study was higher in the farm dogs compared with the kennel dogs (albeit not significantly; p = 0.3), suggesting a possible role of environment and/or management with regards to infection rates. On the other hand, Babaei et al. (2010) and Pratelli et al. (2014) found no association between disease status and housing type. These discrepancies in the apparent significance of environmental and managemental factors in relation to CHV‐1 infection are likely to be explained by the lack of consistency and multiple confounding factors that are inherent to field studies. For example, the kennelled dogs in our study were housed individually but had free contact with other dogs at times during the day. In contrast, the farm dogs were free roaming with other animal species but not with other dogs. Similarly, although the farm dogs tended to have poorer sanitary conditions and poorer nutrition, the kennelled dogs were sometimes also exposed to suboptimal temperatures, diets and management conditions, making direct comparisons difficult.

Despite the significant importance and potential economic losses, the mortality of puppies during or after parturition is poorly documented in Iran, with most breeders failing to submit dead puppies for necropsy (Kapil et al., 2015; Pratelli et al., 2014). Thus, although the frequency of stillborn and dead neonatal puppies suggests that CHV‐1 infection is likely to represent a significant pathogen, its exact contribution to neonatal death is unclear.

Nevertheless, the finding of such high infection rates should prompt veterinarians to advocate better management methods. Screening and eradication programmes are costly and time‐consuming and are invariably not a practical option in breeding kennels. If given during pregnancy, vaccination against CHV‐1 can significantly increase fertility and reduce neonatal mortality (Rota et al., 2020); however, it does not irradiate infection and is not available in Iran. Therefore, efforts to manage environmental stressors, maintain a warm temperature for newborn puppies and improve the sanitary and nutritional conditions are essential (Lefebvre et al., 2015; Monteiro et al., 2016). Direct contact with infected animals and/or fomites is considered to be the most important modes of transmission and therefore, the separation of infected animals including pregnant bitches and newborn puppies can reduce CHV transmission in kennels (Dahlbom et al., 2009; Greene et al., 2012). Administration of immune serum or globulin in newborn puppies can also be helpful for the prevention of disease. Newly introduced dogs may act as reservoirs of infection in kennels and should be separated and screened (Greene et al., 2012; Kapil et al., 2015; Ledbetter et al., 2009; Lefebvre et al., 2015; Losurdo et al., 2018; Monteiro et al., 2016).

CONCLUSIONS

CHV‐1 is an endemic pathogen in the southeast of Iran. Further studies are required to ascertain its distribution pattern across Iran and to identify effective control measures. Vaccination against CHV‐1 could be justifiable, particularly in valuable breeding stocks.
