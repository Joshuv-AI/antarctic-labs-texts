Technical assessment of two swab types for human and microbial DNA extraction from latent fingerprints
Authors: Josep De Alcaraz-Fossoul, Francisco Medina-Paz, Sara C Zapico
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLatent fingerprints are a valuable yet challenging source of both human and microbial DNA, especially when ridge patterns are of poor visual quality with little to no identification potential. The efficiency of DNA collection and extraction depends on the choice of the sampling technique and the specimen conditions. This study compared two swab types for collecting and extracting human and microbial DNA from the fingers and fingerprints of two participants: 4N6FLOQSwabs® Genetics (Copan Italia) and standard cotton swabs (Dealmed Cotton-Tipped Sterile Wood Applicators). Results revealed higher DNA recovery for one participant regardless of swab type, indicating variability likely due to individual differences in natural cell/DNA shedding. Microbial DNA recovery ranged from 100% (from fingers) to 30% (from fingerprints), implying a loss of DNA during deposition, with 4N6FLOQSwabs® generally outperforming cotton swabs. Total DNA recovery from fingerprints was ~57% of that from fingers, comparable between both swab types, though 4N6FLOQSwabs® yields were higher overall. STR profiling via direct PCR with MicroFLOQ® showed that 4N6FLOQSwabs® performance was also superior, successfully identifying STR loci from fingers in 74% of cases, compared to 54% with cotton swabs. This figure was similar when extracting DNA from fingerprints (~40%). This comparative study underscores the importance of selecting the appropriate swab type for optimal DNA extraction from fingers and latent fingerprints in forensic investigations. Introduction

In addition to their traditional role in human identification through ridge patterns and minutiae [1], fingerprints also constitute a source of genetic material, including human and microbial DNA, which can be valuable in criminal investigations [2]. However, STR profiling from partial, faint, or contaminated fingerprints is challenging, primarily due to insufficient or degraded genetic material [3], as well as the presence of PCR inhibitors and mixed DNA sources, which can result in low signals or allele dropouts [4]. Extreme environmental conditions (e.g., UV light, high temperatures, etc.), substrate type, and the donor’s shedding status further complicate the analysis [5]. Thus, the optimisation of DNA collection methods to preserve sample integrity and maximize DNA yield becomes a necessity.

An array of swab types, such as cotton, polyester, nylon, and foam, vary in their effectiveness for DNA collection [6–9]. Additionally, methods such as dry swabbing, wet swabbing, and pre-moistening may impact DNA yield and quality [10]. While cotton and nylon swabs are effective for human DNA [11], polyester and foam swabs are better for microbial DNA due to their hydrophobic properties [12, 13]. While cotton swabs may retain fibers that interfere with DNA extraction [14], flocked swabs are designed to enhance sample collection efficiency [15]. Then, the choice of a swab will significantly affect DNA extraction efficiency from latent fingerprints. Swabs that produce higher DNA recovery and purity are more suitable for downstream analyses such as DNA profiling and microbial identification. By identifying optimal swab types and collection methods, forensic scientists can enhance the versatility of fingerprints and improve the reliability of forensic DNA analysis [16, 17].

This study evaluates and compares the effectiveness of two swab types, 4N6FLOQSwabs® and conventional cotton swabs, for DNA collection from fingers and fingerprints. To optimize STR profiling, a direct PCR workflow using MicroFLOQ® swabs is tested. The study highlights the critical role of selecting the appropriate swab type based on the DNA target—whether human, microbial, or both—and sample type.

Materials and methods

Finger and latent fingerprint swabbing

Prior to the start of the experiment, two adult individuals (one male, “D1” and one female, “D2”) were asked to refrain from using certain products such as moisturizers, antibiotics, and caffeinated beverages for at least 12 h. Approximately 90 min before fingerprint deposition, participants washed their hands with tap water and commercial liquid hand soap and tapped them dry with a paper towel. They were instructed not to touch any objects or exit the premises. Conventional glass microscope slides were washed with 70% (v/v) bleach followed by 70% (v/v) ethanol, rinsed with ddH2O, dried with a sterile paper wipe, and exposed to UV light for 10 min before fingerprint deposition.

Before fingerprinting, participants touched their faces (nose and forehead) and gently tapped their fingers against one another. The thumb and index fingers of the right and left hands were dry-swabbed for 10 s with 4N6FLOQSwabs® (Copan Italia, Brescia, Italy) and conventional cotton swabs (Dealmed Cotton-Tipped Sterile Wood Applicators, Brooklyn, NY, USA). These two swab types were then tapped 10 times with a MicroFLOQ® (Copan Italia) pre-damped with 1 μL ddH2O for DNA profiling. In total, 16 specimens from fingers (one per finger, hand, donor, and swab) and an additional cheek DNA specimen were collected as positive controls for DNA analyses. A negative control was prepared by swabbing an unused area of a glass slide with each respective swab. Fingers were “recharged” as described earlier before depositing latent fingerprints of the thumb and index fingers of the right and left hands in duplicate. A total of 32 impressions were prepared, with each donor generating eight impressions per swab type (via duplicate imprints from four fingers) (Figure 1). The imprinting process was assisted with an electronic scale placed under the slides to monitor that the pressure of deposition remained within the range of 900 g to 1 000 g in a straight up-and-down motion for 10 s. All fingerprints were deposited in an access-controlled laboratory and swabbed following the same protocol as for the fingers with cotton and 4N6FLOQSwabs® followed by MicroFLOQ®. This approach would assess intra-donor variability across different fingers and hands as well as inter-donor variability. Both sexes were included in the study to collect data from two differing types of donors, rather than to perform a comprehensive analysis of sex-related differences in DNA yields. These controlled conditions established a “minimum efficiency threshold”, where the inability to extract and produce viable STR profiles under these optimal scenarios would imply even lower success rates in more challenging real-world situations.

DNA extraction and quantification

4N6FLOQSwabs® were introduced in a NAO®Basket (Copan Italia) containing ATL lysis buffer and proteinase K from the DNeasy® Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Then, the swabs were incubated at 56 °C for 10 min. The NAO®Basket was centrifuged at 10 000 g for 1 min and DNA was extracted from the supernatant according to manufacturer’s instructions, eluting DNA in 50 μL AE buffer. DNA from cotton swabs was extracted following the same procedure.

Total DNA was quantified with Qubit™ dsDNA HS (High Sensitivity) Assay Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in a Qubit™ Fluorometer 1.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity of DNA was assessed with NanoDrop™ One/OneC UV–Vis (Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on the ratio 260/280. After total DNA quantification, microbial DNA-specific quantification was carried out using the Femto™ Bacterial Quantification Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), based on quantitative PCR, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Direct PCR from MicroFLOQ®swabs

Direct PCR was performed from MicroFLOQ® swabs to generate STR profiles by using the Promega PowerPlex® Fusion 6C System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). The bottom of the swab was cut and introduced in a thin-wall, clear, 0.5 mL PCR tube (Genessee Scientific, Morrisville, NC, USA), along with 15 μL of nuclease-free water, 5 μL of PowerPlex® Fusion 6C 5X Master Mix, and 5 μL of PowerPlex® Fusion 6C 5X Primer Pair Mix. The PCR was conducted under the following conditions: one cycle at 96 °C for 1 min; 29 cycles at 96 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 1 min; one cycle at 60 °C for 10 min; and hold at 4 °C in a ProFlex® PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

STR fragment analysis

Fragment analysis was conducted on the SeqStudio™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under the following parameters: 7 s injection time, 1 200 V injection voltage; 1 440 s run time, 9 000 V run volt. The microsatellite analysis software on Thermo Fisher Cloud analyzed the human DNA profiles with an RFU 150 threshold for peak detection/analysis. For the D2 donor, 23 autosomal and one amelogenin STRs were analysed; for D1, 23 autosomal and one amelogenin STRs plus three Y-STRs. As controls, DNA from cheeks was also collected with both swab types to ensure the STR analysis was performed correctly and for comparison.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted with SPSS version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 MSO software (Version 2403 Build 16.0.17425.20176; Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated for each donor and comparisons across donors were conducted using one-way ANOVA and univariate analyses at α = 0.05.

Results and discussion

Relative efficiency of DNA collection from fingers and latent fingerprints

The main goal of this technical study was to evaluate the relative performance of two different swab types (i.e., 4N6FLOQSwabs® vs. Cotton) in collecting human and microbial DNA from fingers and their resultant latent fingerprints.

Total DNA concentration, based on fluorometric detection, revealed no significant differences in DNA collection between duplicates (D1: P = 0.133; D2: P = 0.225), between fingerprints (D1: P = 0.147; D2: P = 0.131), or between hands (D1: P = 0.513; D2: P = 0.809) of the same donor. These findings underlined the consistency of the methodology. However, significant differences were observed between donors D1 and D2 (P < 0.001) and between swab types per donor (D1: P = 0.004; D2: P < 0.001), indicating that both swab and donor characteristics, such as swab absorptivity and variations in DNA/cell shedding, may play critical roles in the efficiency of DNA recovery [18]. In contrast to previous reports suggesting that the dominant hand would have more frequent contact with objects and thus release more DNA [19–21], the current study detected no significant differences in DNA yields between the dominant and non-dominant hands. This may be attributed to the fact that participants washed their hands before the experiment [20].

To assess the relative efficiency of total DNA recovery between swab types, data from both donors were combined. The results revealed a statistically equivalent yield between the two swabs for fingers (P = 0.349) and fingerprints (P = 0.917). This trend persisted when examining the results separately by participant. When comparing total DNA recovery between donors (Table 1, Figure 2), 4N6FLOQSwabs® swabs were significantly more effective in recovering DNA from D2 fingers (0.18 ± 0.05 vs. 0.51 ± 0.22, P = 0.025), indicating higher efficiency when larger quantities of DNA were present. This suggested that the D1 donor may have been a poorer DNA/cell shedder, resulting in lower DNA availability for collection. In contrast, as for Cotton swabs, DNA recovery directly from D2 and D1 fingers was not statistically different (0.23 ± 0.05 vs. 0.31 ± 0.08, P = 0.111). For fingerprints, 4N6FLOQSwabs® again recovered more DNA from the D2 donor (0.12 ± 0.05 vs. 0.22 ± 0.11, P = 0.036); however, this difference was not noticed on Cotton (0.14 ± 0.02 vs. 0.16 ± 0.04, P = 0.135), implying a difference in swab efficiency and signs that the shedding status and/or DNA deposition to the surface influenced DNA collection. While Cotton swabs collected slightly more total DNA from the D1 donor’s fingers and fingerprints than 4N6FLOQSwabs® swabs did, the differences were not statistically significant (fingers: 0.23 ± 0.05 vs. 0.18 ± 0.05, P = 0.111; fingerprints: 0.14 ± 0.02 vs. 0.12 ± 0.05, P = 0.135) (Table 1). However, the non-significant results for Cotton swabs between participants may be attributed to the combined effects of lower DNA present in D1 and the slightly lower efficiency of Cotton in recovering DNA from D2, as shown by the percentages in Table 1. Overall, 4N6FLOQSwabs® demonstrated a certain superiority in recovering total DNA from fingers (~60%) and fingerprints (~70%) to Cotton, particularly in the D2 donor, though these results were not statistically significant (fingers: 0.51 ± 0.22 vs. 0.31 ± 0.08, P = 0.119; fingerprints: 0.22 ± 0.11 vs. 0.16 ± 0.04, P = 0.744, respectively). For the D1 donor, no significant differences were detected (fingers: 0.18 ± 0.05 vs. 0.23 ± 0.05, P = 0.367; fingerprints: 0.12 ± 0.05 vs. 0.14 ± 0.02, P = 0.797), despite 4N6FLOQSwabs® being slightly less efficient in absolute DNA yields.

Regarding the relative loss of DNA from fingers to fingerprints (Table 1, Figure 2), 4N6FLOQSwabs® swabs were able to collect ~70% of the total DNA from the D1 donor (0.18 ± 0.05 vs 0.12 ± 0.05, P = 0.208) and ~44% from the D2 donor (0.51 ± 0.22 vs 0.22 ± 0.11, P = 0.006), indicating higher swab efficiencies at lower DNA quantities. Cotton swabs recovered ~63% of the DNA from the D1 donor (0.23 ± 0.05 vs 0.14 ± 0.02, P = 0.023) and ~53% from D2 (0.31 ± 0.08 vs 0.16 ± 0.04, P = 0.225). Interestingly, DNA concentrations were consistently lower in D1 (0.20 ng/μL) than in D2 (0.31 ng/μL) when combining results from both swab types. On average, both 4N6FLOQSwabs® and Cotton swabs recovered ~57% of the original skin-transferred DNA from fingerprints, with 4N6FLOQSwabs® yielding slightly higher absolute DNA quantities (0.29 ng/μL) compared to Cotton (0.24 ng/μL). Then, a substantial portion of DNA did not naturally transfer from fingers to fingerprints, as both 4N6FLOQSwabs® (P < 0.001) and Cotton (P = 0.026) swabs recovered significantly less quantity of DNA from fingerprints. This suggests that, regardless of shedding status, a considerable amount of DNA is lost during the deposition process from skin to (fingerprint) surfaces.

Relative efficiency of microbial DNA collection by swab type

This study compared the performance of 4N6FLOQSwabs® and Cotton swabs in recovering microbial DNA and assessing its transfer from fingers to fingerprints. Results showed no significant differences between the participant’s duplicate samples (D1: P = 0.083; D2: P = 0.703), fingerprints (D1: P = 0.241; D2: P = 0.137), or hands (D1: P = 0.938; D2: P = 0.394), confirming the methodology's robustness. However, differences were detected between donors in microbial DNA recovery (P < 0.015) and swab type (D1: P = 0.008; D2: P < 0.001), with D2 fingers yielding significantly more DNA (4N6FLOQSwabs® = 4×; Cotton = 2×) compared to D1, though for fingerprints the recovery was similar (4N6FLOQSwabs® = 1.2×; Cotton = 1×) (Table 1). Overall, 4N6FLOQSwabs® swabs were ~60% more effective than Cotton at collecting microbial DNA from fingers (Average D1: 71.55% and D2: 49.65%; P < 0.001) and 40% more effective from fingerprints (Average D1: 47.71% and D2: 38.37%; P = 0.03) (Table 1). Microbial DNA constituted the majority of DNA collected, except in the case of Cotton swabbing fingerprints (D1: 43.24%; D2: 35.50%; P < 0.001) (Table 1, Figure 2).

Significant differences in the efficiency of the swab types at recovering microbial DNA were detected when comparing DNA collected from fingers to fingerprints for both donors (P < 0.001), at an average ~48% recovery (D1: 76.41% and D2: 21.43%) with 4N6FLOQSwabs® (D1: 0.17 ± 0.09 vs 0.13 ± 0.05; D2: 0.70 ± 0.21 vs 0.15 ± 0.07) and average ~33% recovery (D1: 50.00% and D2: 17.14%) with Cotton (D1: 0.12 ± 0.01 vs 0.06 ± 0.02; D2: 0.35 ± 0.11 vs 0.06 ± 0.03) (Table 1). However, further analyses revealed that while no significant differences were observed for D1 (4N6FLOQSwabs®: P = 0.457; Cotton: P = 0.193), the D2 donor exhibited substantial differences with both swab types (P < 0.001), highlighting 4N6FLOQSwabs®'s superior performance likely due to the lower initial DNA quantities in D1 samples. DNA transfer from fingers to fingerprints experienced a loss of total (−43%) and microbial (−60%) DNA, which could potentially complicate STR profiling and 16S rRNA sequencing due to required DNA concentration thresholds [22–24].

Characterization of human DNA by STRs

For statistical analysis, data from replicates and fingers (thumb and index) were combined, despite being extracted and analysed separately, to simplify result interpretation. STR loci and allele identification were considered “positive” when the combined duplicates provided a “correct” signal compared to the control cheek DNA specimen. Both participants had cheek swab DNA concentrations above 25 ng/μL, with 100% STR loci and alleles correctly identified. Differences in DNA concentration and possibly purity, particularly from fingerprint specimens, appeared to affect the downstream MicroFLOQ® process, influencing STR/allele signal quality.

Statistical analysis (Table 2) revealed significant differences between donors in STR loci and allele detection (P < 0.001 for both), perhaps due to differences in DNA shedding and the relatively low DNA collection. Statistical differences were also observed between swab types (P = 0.020 and P = 0.028 for STRs and alleles, respectively), with MicroFLOQ® on 4N6FLOQSwabs® swabs performing better overall. However, no significant differences in swab efficiency were overall detected when fingers and fingerprints were analysed separately for STRs (P = 0.093 and P = 0.996, respectively) and alleles (P = 0.123 and P = 0.916, respectively), nor between swab types for each donor (D1: P = 0.734 and D2: P = 0.066). No statistical differences in DNA recovery were observed between left and right fingers for either D1 (STRs: P = 1.000 and alleles: P = 0.707) or D2 (STRs: P = 0.513 and alleles: P = 0.448), suggesting both hands are equally suitable for DNA recovery.

STR and allele dropouts were detected (Table 2) between cheek specimens and fingers, particularly in the D1 with a 78% dropout rate from Cotton swabs, compared to 55% for 4N6FLOQSwabs®. No significant dropouts were noted for 4N6FLOQSwabs® in the D2. For fingerprints, DNA quality declined further across both swab types, especially for the D1 donor showing an 80% dropout compared to cheek swabs. Overall, MicroFLOQ® with 4N6FLOQSwabs® swabs was more effective for DNA recovery from fingers but showed comparable performance to Cotton swabs for fingerprints.

Conclusion

This technical study revealed significant differences in the performance of 4N6FLOQSwabs® and Cotton swabs for DNA recovery from fingers and fingerprints [23–25]. 4N6FLOQSwabs® swabs consistently yielded higher quantities and better-quality DNA, as shown by STR loci and allele analysis using direct PCR from MicroFLOQ® swabs. They also increased the recovery of microbial DNA compared to cotton swabs. Donor shedding status may also influence DNA recovery, further emphasising the influence of swab selection in forensic DNA analysis. By integrating DNA profiling with fingerprint analysis—especially relevant when friction ridge patterns are inadequate for identifications—forensic practitioners can strengthen links between individuals and crime scenes, enhancing the evidentiary value of both genetic and physical evidence. Future research should explore the effects of environmental conditions, substrate types, and storage methods on DNA extraction from latent fingerprints, as well as assess the performance of additional swab types and donor profiles.
