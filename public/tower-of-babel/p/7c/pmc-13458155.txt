Orthoptera as emerging nutritional resources: comparative analysis of protein and mineral composition
Authors: Mustafa İlçin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Orthoptera species are increasingly recognized as promising sources of alternative nutrients due to their high protein and mineral content. In this study, the nutritional composition of five Orthoptera species, Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa, Anacridium aegyptium, Uvarovistia satunini, Gryllus bimaculatus, and Saga ephippigera was evaluated with respect to protein content and selected mineral elements, including sodium (Na), magnesium (Mg), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and iron (Fe). The results revealed considerable interspecific variation in both protein and mineral composition. Protein analysis showed that the studied species contained relatively high protein levels ranging from 26.7% to 38.9%. The highest protein content was detected in Uvarovistia satunini, followed by Saga ephippigera and Anacridium aegyptium, indicating that these species may represent nutritionally valuable insect taxa. Although lower protein values were observed in Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa and Gryllus bimaculatus, their protein levels remain within the typical range reported for edible insects. Mineral analysis demonstrated that potassium was the most abundant element across all species, with particularly high concentrations recorded in Anacridium aegyptium. Sodium levels were highest in Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa, while Gryllus bimaculatus exhibited the greatest iron concentration among the examined taxa. Calcium and magnesium levels also varied notably among species, suggesting species-specific mineral accumulation patterns. Overall, the findings indicate that Orthoptera species possess significant nutritional potential, particularly in terms of protein and essential mineral content. The observed differences among taxa highlight the importance of species-level evaluations when assessing the nutritional value of insect-derived resources. These results contribute to the growing body of research on insect-based nutrition and suggest that certain Orthoptera species may serve as promising candidates for sustainable alternative food and feed sources in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55952-5.

Introduction

Many grasshopper species found worldwide are edible and have been consumed by humans in many parts of the world throughout history. In the Middle East, in 3000 BC. It is known that until the eighth century, servants carried locusts strung on sticks to royal banquets, and in the 1730s, locusts were frequently consumed roasted and grilled by many Africans, Asians, and especially Arabs; they were also salted and stored in warehouses, and kept on ships as a dessert or served with coffee. In addition, it is known that in the 1550 s, nomads from Arabia and Libya consumed locusts by boiling them and drying them in the sun to make flour for later consumption1,2. Today, insects are considered an alternative protein source by humans. Some insect species are known to be highly nutritious and provide a good source of protein, minerals, fats, vitamins, and energy. In many parts of the world, edible insects play an important role in meeting human nutritional needs. Traditionally, more than 2000 insect species are consumed by humans at one stage or another of their life cycles. These insects are of great importance as a food source for many people. It is estimated that approximately 524 insect species are found in Africa, 349 in Asia, 679 in the Americas, 152 in Australia, and 41 in Europe. Mexico is home to the largest number of insect species considered edible3. Looking at insect consumption worldwide, the most consumed insect species are: The groups are identified as Coleoptera (beetles 31%), Lepidoptera (caterpillars 18%), Hymenoptera (bees and ants 14%), and Orthoptera (grasshoppers and crickets 13%)4,5.

Insects have been proposed as a promising component of sustainable food systems aimed at addressing global challenges related to food security, population growth, and environmental sustainability6. Beyond their environmental advantages, insects also possess remarkable nutritional properties. In addition to high-quality protein, edible insects contain valuable lipids, essential amino acids, vitamins, and important minerals that contribute to human health7,8.

In recent years, the nutritional potential of insects has attracted increasing attention in the fields of food science, nutrition, and sustainable agriculture. Edible insects are considered highly efficient converters of feed into biomass and require significantly fewer environmental resources compared with traditional livestock production systems. Studies have demonstrated that insect farming generates lower greenhouse gas emissions, requires less land and water, and produces less organic waste than conventional animal husbandry9,10. Insects are consumed as food in Thailand11,12, China13,14, Mexico15–17, Latin America18, Japan19 and Africa20. Edible insects, particularly Orthoptera species such as crickets and grasshoppers, have recently gained attention as alternative sources of high-quality protein and essential minerals21. Studies have shown that these insects can contain protein levels ranging from 35% to over 70%, depending on species and developmental stage, positioning them as potential sustainable protein sources for human and animal nutrition22. In addition to protein, Orthoptera species are rich in essential minerals, including potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, and iron, which are critical for physiological functions such as nerve conduction, bone development, and oxygen transport23. For instance, Anacridium aegyptium and Gryllus bimaculatus have been reported to exhibit high potassium and iron contents, respectively, highlighting species-specific differences in mineral accumulation24,25.

Recent studies also emphasize that both protein and mineral content vary significantly among Orthoptera species, reflecting the influence of diet, habitat, and physiological factors20,21. Therefore, species-level assessments are crucial when evaluating the nutritional potential of edible insects for integration into food systems. Such investigations support the use of Orthoptera not only as a protein source but also as a valuable contributor to micronutrient intake, potentially mitigating nutritional deficiencies in human populations26. When considering its environmental impact, the grasshopper emits far less greenhouse gas than most animals and does not require large areas of land for cultivation. Furthermore, being a cold-blooded insect, it can quickly convert nutrients into protein. To produce the same amount of protein as other animals, a grasshopper consumes an average of 7–8 times less food. This is of great importance for sustainability26. In addition to protein, these insects also provide essential minerals such as iron, calcium, potassium, magnesium, and zinc, which play critical roles in human physiological processes including oxygen transport, bone formation, and enzymatic activity27,28. Due to these nutritional characteristics, Orthoptera species have been suggested as valuable alternative sources of both macro- and micronutrients29.

These findings highlight the importance of conducting species-specific nutritional assessments to better understand the potential of insects as alternative food resources. Despite the growing interest in edible insects, nutritional data for many Orthoptera species remain limited, particularly for species occurring in specific ecological regions. Comprehensive analyses of protein and mineral composition are essential to evaluate their potential contribution to human and animal nutrition. In particular, determining the concentrations of essential minerals such as sodium, magnesium, potassium, calcium, and iron can provide valuable insights into the micronutrient potential of these species. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the protein content and mineral composition of five Orthoptera species: Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa, Anacridium aegyptium, Uvarovistia satunini, Gryllus bimaculatus, and Saga ephippigera. By assessing both macronutrient and mineral profiles, this study seeks to contribute to the growing body of knowledge on insect-based nutrition and to highlight the potential role of Orthoptera species as sustainable alternative protein and micronutrient sources. Despite these advantages, detailed nutritional studies focusing on specific Orthoptera species remain limited. Therefore, expanding the nutritional database of these species is essential to better understand their potential contribution to human and animal nutrition and to support their inclusion in future sustainable food strategies.

Material and methods

Five Orthoptera species, Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa, Anacridium aegyptium, Uvarovistia satunini, Gryllus bimaculatus, and Saga ephippigera, were collected from different locations in the study area Bingöl province in Türkiye using standard sweep nets and hand-picking methods. The following specimens were collected in Turkey: Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa (5 individuals, 3♀, 2♂) in June 2025 in Genç and Solhan districts of Bingöl province; Anacridum aegyptium (3♀, 2♂) in June 2024 at 8 km. Genç district; Uvarovistia satunini (4♀, 1♂) in September 2025 at Gülbahar Dam, Göltepesi village; Gryllus bimaculatus (7 individuals, 3♀, 4♂) in July 2025 at Çiçekdere village; and Saga ephippigera (5 individuals, 3♀, 2♂) in July 2024 and August 2025 in Kelmir, Sancaklı. Sampling was conducted during the active adult period of each species to ensure maximum biomass availability. Specimens were immediately placed in clean, labelled containers and transported to the laboratory under controlled temperature conditions to prevent degradation. Collected specimens were rinsed with distilled water to remove soil and debris and then oven-dried at 60 °C until reaching a constant weight. The dried samples were ground into a fine powder using a sterilized mortar and pestle and stored in airtight containers at room temperature until further analysis. Protein analysis

The crude protein content of each species was determined using the Kjeldahl method, following standard AOAC procedures. Nitrogen content was measured and multiplied by a factor of 6.25 to calculate total protein content, expressed as a percentage of dry weight. The Kjeldahl method involves burning a nitrogen-containing sample with H2SO4, converting all the nitrogen to (NH4)2SO4, making the solution basic, distilling the released NH3, collecting it in a standard acid solution, and then determining the amount of neutralized acid by titration. For this, the sample is first burned with concentrated sulphuric acid at high temperature. Carbonaceous (C) substances are oxidized to carbon dioxide (CO2), hydrogens to water, and nitrogen bound to hydrogen to ammonium sulphate ((NH4)2SO4). The resulting solution is distilled with a 40% by weight sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. The free ammonia (NH3) is retained in an acid solution. The amount of acid neutralized by the ammonia in the acid solution is determined by titration, thus determining the amount of nitrogen29.

Mineral analysis

For mineral quantification, powdered samples were digested with a mixture of nitric acid and perchloric acid. The concentrations of sodium (Na), magnesium (Mg), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and iron (Fe) were determined using atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS). All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility, and values were expressed in mg/L. Microwave dissolution process for AAS analysis

In the AAS method for sample preparation, 1 g of grasshopper samples were taken, transferred to Teflon microwave oven containers, and 10 ml of concentrated 65% Merck nitric acid was added to each sample. For the blank, 10 mL of 65% nitric acid was added to an empty Teflon container. The containers were left open for 10 min, then the lids of the Teflon containers were tightly closed and placed in a CEM brand MARS6 ONE TOUCH (USA) microwave oven. Within 15 min, the maximum temperature was raised to 200 °C and kept at this temperature for 15 min. In total, the dissolution process was carried out by keeping it in a closed system for 30 min.

AAS method for elemental analysis

The AAS single element standard was diluted and a calibration graph was prepared at specific concentrations. The prepared solutions were read using an Analyst 800 Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS) instrument, from dilute to concentrated. According to the resulting calibration graph, the results for specific amounts of diluted samples were obtained in mg/lt. The atomic absorption instrument ensures the reliability of the results by reading each sample three times repeatedly.

Data analysis

Results were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Interspecific differences in protein and mineral contents were statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (p < 0.05) to identify significant differences among species.

The protein contents of the investigated Orthoptera species differed significantly among taxa (p < 0.05), as demonstrated by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (Table 3). All species were assigned to different homogenous groups, indicating statistically distinct protein levels among the examined insects. Among the studied species, Uvarovistia satunini exhibited the highest protein content (38.97 ± 0.25%), followed by Saga ephippigera (37.63 ± 0.21%) and Anacridium aegyptium (36.33 ± 0.21%). In contrast, substantially lower protein values were observed in Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa (29.33 ± 0.21%) and Gryllus bimaculatus (26.73 ± 0.21%). Results

The results for five locust species were examined. The four species included in the sample are Orthoptera species that are heavily effective in agricultural pests and fall into the pest category. In particular, the mole cricket (Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa) has been observed to cause significant damage to young plant seedlings, especially vegetables. The Egyptian grasshopper (Anacridium aegyptium) has been found to be quite effective in cereals over large areas and cause production losses. Uvarovistia satunini has been identified by me as the most damaging species to plant products in both orchards and oak forests, causing production losses in both nymph and adult stages. Gryllus bimaculatus is a species that can be found both in fields and in homes, indicating its widespread presence, and it is possible for it to cause plant product losses when its population increases. Finally, the carnivorous locust (Saga ephippigera), included in the sample, differs from the others in its predatory nature.

Conversely, comparatively lower protein contents were measured in Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa (29.33%) and Gryllus bimaculatus (26.73%). Despite these lower values, the protein concentrations remain within the range commonly reported for edible insects, reinforcing the relevance of Orthoptera species as potential alternative protein sources. When both parameters were considered simultaneously, Uvarovistia satunini emerged as the most prominent species in the dataset, exhibiting both the highest relative abundance and the highest protein concentration. Likewise, Saga ephippigera and Anacridium aegyptium displayed a favorable combination of relatively high abundance and elevated protein content. These patterns suggest that certain Orthoptera taxa may contribute disproportionately to the total protein biomass within the sampled insect community. Overall, the results demonstrate that while species abundance remained relatively uniform across taxa, protein composition varied considerably, highlighting interspecific differences in nutritional value. This variability indicates that species identity plays a critical role in determining the protein yield of Orthoptera communities and underscores the importance of species-level assessments in evaluating the nutritional potential of insect-derived protein resources.

The elemental composition of the investigated Orthoptera species revealed considerable interspecific variation in macro- and microelement concentrations when expressed as mg/g dry weight. Potassium (K) was the dominant element in all examined species, followed by sodium (Na), whereas iron (Fe) generally occurred at lower concentrations. These findings indicate that potassium-based ionic regulation and osmotic balance play a major physiological role in Orthopteran insects. Among the studied taxa, Anacridium aegyptium exhibited the highest potassium concentration (13.6208 mg/g), which was substantially greater than that recorded in the other species. This elevated potassium level may be associated with its large body size, muscular activity, and herbivorous feeding behavior. The same species also demonstrated the highest calcium concentration (0.7089 mg/g) and magnesium content (0.5968 mg/g), suggesting an enhanced requirement for structural and metabolic mineral regulation. Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa showed the highest sodium concentration (3.7354 mg/g), together with relatively high potassium levels (6.5591 mg/g). As a soil-dwelling species, its elevated sodium content may reflect environmental exposure to mineral-rich substrates and specific osmoregulatory adaptations related to subterranean habitats. Additionally, this species contained comparatively high iron concentrations (0.2370 mg/g), indicating active metabolic and respiratory processes. The iron concentration reached its maximum value in Gryllus bimaculatus (0.3152 mg/g), which may be linked to higher enzymatic activity and oxygen transport requirements. This species also exhibited elevated sodium and potassium levels, suggesting intense physiological activity and neuromuscular regulation. In contrast, Saga ephippigera presented the lowest calcium concentration (0.0044 mg/g) and comparatively low iron content (0.0323 mg/g) (Table 6). The reduced mineral levels observed in this predatory Tettigoniid species may be associated with differences in trophic ecology and nutritional specialization compared with herbivorous Orthoptera. Similarly, Uvarovistia satunini demonstrated relatively low concentrations for most analyzed elements, particularly sodium (0.3063 mg/g) and magnesium (0.1900 mg/g). These results may indicate species-specific metabolic adaptations or ecological differences related to habitat preference and dietary composition. Overall, the results demonstrate that elemental accumulation patterns differ substantially among Orthoptera species. Variations in Na, Mg, K, Ca, and Fe concentrations are likely influenced by ecological niche, feeding strategy, habitat conditions, and physiological requirements. Discussion

The present study demonstrated substantial interspecific variation in protein and mineral composition among the examined Orthoptera species. The obtained findings indicate that these insects constitute valuable sources of macro- and microelements, particularly potassium (K), sodium (Na), magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), and iron (Fe), in addition to their considerable protein content. Similar nutritional diversity among Orthopteran insects has been emphasized by previous studies investigating edible insect composition27,30. Compared with traditional livestock products, Orthopteran insects may provide comparable nutritional value while requiring substantially lower environmental resources such as water, land, and feed inputs3,27.

Protein analysis revealed that Uvarovistia satunini possessed the highest protein ratio (38.97%), followed closely by Saga ephippigera (37.6%) and Anacridium aegyptium (36.3%). These values are consistent with earlier reports indicating that Orthopteran insects generally contain protein levels ranging between 30–70% depending on species, developmental stage, and dietary conditions27,31. High protein levels in Orthoptera have been associated with intense muscular development and metabolic activity30. In contrast, Gryllus bimaculatus exhibited the lowest protein content (26.7%), suggesting species-specific physiological and ecological adaptations.

Rumpold and Schlüter20 reported that edible Orthoptera species commonly contain protein levels ranging between approximately 35–77% dry matter, depending on species, developmental stage, and feeding ecology. In the present study, protein ratios varied between 26.73% and 38.97%. Although the recorded values are slightly lower than the upper ranges reported by Rumpold and Schlüter20, they still fall within the nutritional spectrum described for edible insects. Particularly, Uvarovistia satunini (38.97%) and Saga ephippigera (37.6%) exhibited protein contents close to the average levels reported for many Orthopteran taxa in the literature. The relatively lower protein value detected in Gryllus bimaculatus (26.73%) may be associated with ecological factors, sampling period, physiological condition, or methodological differences in protein determination. Regarding mineral composition, Rumpold and Schlüter20 emphasized that potassium is generally the predominant mineral in edible insects, followed by phosphorus, sodium, magnesium, calcium, and iron. The current findings strongly support this observation because potassium was the dominant mineral element in all examined species. Particularly, Anacridium aegyptium exhibited extremely high potassium levels (13.6208 mg/g), which is in agreement with the literature indicating that herbivorous Orthoptera accumulate high potassium concentrations due to potassium-rich plant diets. The sodium concentrations reported in the present study also correspond with the mineral profiles described by Rumpold and Schlüter20. Among the investigated species, Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa contained the highest sodium concentration (3.7354 mg/g). This value appears relatively elevated compared with many edible insect species reported in the literature and may reflect the ecological characteristics of this soil-dwelling species. Rumpold and Schlüter20 noted that environmental conditions and habitat type significantly influence mineral accumulation in insects, which may explain the increased sodium levels observed in subterranean taxa. Calcium and magnesium levels in the present study were generally lower than potassium concentrations but remained within the ranges previously documented for Orthopteran insects. The highest calcium concentration was recorded in Anacridium aegyptium (0.7089 mg/g), whereas S. ephippigera showed remarkably low calcium levels (0.0044 mg/g). According to Rumpold and Schlüter20, mineral variability among insect species may arise from differences in feeding habits, cuticle structure, developmental stage, and ecological adaptation. Therefore, the low calcium level observed in S. ephippigera may be related to its predatory feeding behavior and trophic specialization. Iron concentrations obtained in this study were also comparable to values reported in edible insect literature. G. bimaculatus demonstrated the highest Fe concentration (0.3152 mg/g), supporting previous observations that crickets may serve as important natural iron sources. Rumpold and Schlüter20 highlighted that insects can contain iron levels comparable to or even exceeding those found in conventional meat products, thereby increasing their nutritional importance in alternative food systems. In the study conducted by Ritvanen et al.32 the nitrogen ratio was 5.00, while in our study we measured a ratio of 4.27. The nitrogen content of house crickets was lower than that obtained by Montowska et al.33, who measured nitrogen content from cricket dust (8.82% to 9.62% in dry weight), and differed from the results of Ramos-Elorduy Blásquez et al.16, who measured 10.3% nitrogen in the dried sample. However, they also noted that the variation in the original data was large. High variation in protein content in Orthoptera (grasshoppers, crickets, and closely related insects) is also seen in the reviews by Rumbold and Schlüter20 and Kouřimská and Adámková34, lower than the value reported by Ghosh et al. (2017), who measured 58.32% protein (9.33% as nitrogen) in adult G. bimaculatus. In conclusion, it can be said that our nitrogen results show quantitative differences from previous studies on this type of basis. However, many studies have presented results that support our findings.

In the current study, protein ratios ranged from 26.73 to 38.97%, confirming the nutritional importance of Orthoptera species. According to Van Huis35, many edible insects contain protein levels comparable to beef, poultry, and fish on a dry matter basis. The relatively high protein contents detected in Uvarovistia satunini (38.97%), S. ephippigera (37.63%), and A. aegyptium (36.33%) are therefore consistent with the nutritional profiles described by Van Huis35. These values indicate that Orthopteran insects may serve as alternative protein sources for both human nutrition and animal feed industries, particularly in regions facing protein deficiency and increasing food demand. Van Huis35 also emphasized that edible insects are important sources of essential minerals such as iron, calcium, magnesium, sodium, and potassium. The present study strongly supports this observation because potassium was identified as the dominant mineral element in all examined species. Particularly, A. aegyptium exhibited remarkably high potassium concentrations (13.6208 mg/g). Similarly, the elevated sodium concentration recorded in G. gryllotalpa (3.7354 mg/g) demonstrates that certain Orthopteran species may provide important electrolyte sources. Van Huis35 noted that mineral composition in edible insects may vary according to habitat conditions, diet, developmental stage, and environmental factors. Iron content is another critical nutritional parameter emphasized by Van Huis35, particularly because iron deficiency remains one of the most widespread nutritional disorders worldwide. In the present study, G. bimaculatus showed the highest iron concentration (0.3152 mg/g), supporting previous reports that crickets may constitute valuable natural iron sources. Van Huis35 further suggested that edible insects could help reduce micronutrient deficiencies in developing regions due to their rich mineral composition and high nutrient bioavailability.

An important aspect highlighted in the work of Van Huis35 is the environmental sustainability of insect production systems. Compared with traditional livestock, insects require less land, water, and feed resources while producing lower greenhouse gas emissions. The nutritional richness identified in the present study therefore increases the ecological and economic significance of Orthopteran insects. Species exhibiting high protein and mineral contents may contribute not only to food security but also to sustainable agricultural systems and circular bioeconomy models. Furthermore, Van Huis35 emphasized that cultural acceptance and scientific characterization of edible insects are essential for expanding insect-based food systems globally. In this regard, the present study contributes valuable biochemical and nutritional data regarding regional Orthoptera fauna. The identification of nutritionally rich species may support future studies focusing on edible insect domestication, feed formulation, food supplementation, and functional food development. The present findings are highly compatible with the conclusions of Van Huis35, confirming that Orthopteran insects possess substantial nutritional and ecological potential.

Halloran et al.5 highlighted that Orthopteran insects are among the most protein-rich edible insect groups and may significantly contribute to global nutritional security. In particular, Uvarovistia satunini (38.97%), Saga ephippigera (37.63%), and Anacridium aegyptium (36.3%) exhibited relatively high protein contents, supporting the argument that Orthoptera species can serve as efficient alternative protein resources for both human diets and livestock feed applications. Halloran et al.5 also emphasized that edible insects represent important sources of essential minerals necessary for human health and physiological function. The present study revealed that potassium was the dominant mineral element in all analyzed species, with particularly high levels recorded in A. aegyptium (13.6208 mg/g). Similarly, the elevated sodium concentration observed in G. gryllotalpa (3.7354 mg/g) supports the observations of Halloran et al.5 that insect mineral composition is strongly influenced by habitat conditions and feeding ecology. Iron content is another critical nutritional parameter emphasized by Halloran et al.5, particularly because edible insects may contribute to combating global micronutrient deficiencies. In the present study, G. bimaculatus exhibited the highest iron concentration (0.3152 mg/g), indicating that this species may represent a valuable dietary iron source. Iron-rich edible insects are especially important in regions where iron deficiency anemia remains a major public health problem. The calcium and magnesium levels identified in the current study also support the conclusions of Halloran et al.5, who noted that edible insects can provide significant amounts of essential minerals involved in skeletal structure, enzymatic regulation, and metabolic processes. However, considerable interspecific variation was observed among the examined taxa. For example, S. ephippigera displayed extremely low calcium concentrations (0.0044 mg/g), suggesting that trophic ecology and feeding specialization may strongly influence mineral accumulation patterns in Orthoptera species. An important aspect emphasized by Halloran et al.5 is sustainability.

Lokeshkumar et al.23 emphasized that Coiblemmus compactus possesses high crude protein content and could serve as a sustainable alternative protein source. Similarly, the present study demonstrated elevated protein ratios among all investigated species, ranging from 26.7 to 38.97%. Particularly, U. satunini (38.97%), S. ephippigera (37.63%), and A. aegyptium (36.33%) exhibited protein values comparable to those generally reported for edible Orthopteran insects. These findings reinforce the conclusion of Lokeshkumar et al.23 that Orthoptera species represent promising protein-rich organisms with significant nutritional potential.

However, clear differences are also evident in protein composition between the two studies. Lokeshkumar et al.23 investigated laboratory-reared C. compactus under controlled nutritional and environmental conditions, which likely minimized variability in nutrient composition. In contrast, the present study analyzed wild Orthoptera species collected from natural ecosystems characterized by fluctuating environmental conditions, diverse vegetation structures, and heterogeneous nutrient availability. Consequently, the lower protein content observed in G. bimaculatus (26.73%) may reflect ecological stress, seasonal feeding differences, reproductive physiology, or habitat-dependent nutritional limitations not typically encountered under laboratory rearing systems. A particularly important similarity concerns mineral composition. Lokeshkumar et al.23 reported that edible crickets contain substantial levels of essential minerals, including potassium, sodium, magnesium, calcium, and iron, which are critical for human and animal nutrition. The present study strongly supports these observations because potassium was identified as the dominant mineral element across all examined taxa. Especially, A. aegyptium demonstrated remarkably high potassium concentrations (13.6208 mg/g), indicating intensive intracellular ionic regulation and potentially high metabolic activity. Similar potassium dominance was also emphasized by Lokeshkumar et al.23, supporting the notion that Orthopteran insects are naturally rich in essential macroelements. Despite these similarities, notable differences emerged regarding mineral accumulation intensity. The potassium concentration observed in A. aegyptium was substantially higher than values generally reported for laboratory-reared edible crickets. This difference may arise from ecological feeding behavior because herbivorous wild Orthoptera consume potassium-rich plant tissues directly from natural habitats. Sodium accumulation also demonstrated important ecological differences between the studies. In the present investigation, G. gryllotalpa exhibited exceptionally high sodium levels (3.7354 mg/g), far exceeding those observed in several other taxa. Lokeshkumar et al.23 similarly identified sodium as an important nutritional component in edible crickets; however, the elevated sodium concentration observed in G. gryllotalpa likely reflects its subterranean ecology and direct contact with mineral-rich soil substrates. Such habitat-driven mineral accumulation patterns are less pronounced in laboratory-reared insects maintained under controlled environmental conditions. Magnesium and calcium values obtained in the present study also exhibited substantial interspecific variability. A. aegyptium showed the highest Mg (0.5968 mg/g) and Ca (0.7089 mg/g) concentrations, whereas S. ephippigera possessed extremely low calcium levels (0.0044 mg/g).

Among the analyzed minerals, potassium was identified as the dominant element in all examined taxa. Particularly, A. aegyptium exhibited remarkably high potassium concentrations (13.6208 mg/g), substantially exceeding those recorded in the other species. Potassium plays a critical role in intracellular osmotic balance, nerve impulse transmission, and muscle function in insects. Similar high potassium accumulation has previously been reported in phytophagous Orthoptera due to their plant-based feeding habits, since plant tissues are naturally rich in intracellular potassium ions35. Sodium concentrations varied considerably among species, with G. gryllotalpa showing the highest Na value (3.7354 mg/g). This elevated sodium concentration may be associated with its subterranean ecological niche and continuous exposure to mineral-rich soil environments. Previous ecological studies have demonstrated that soil-dwelling insects often accumulate higher electrolyte levels because of direct contact with soil particles and moisture gradients. Sodium is also essential for maintaining ionic regulation and neuromuscular coordination in insects28,31. Calcium concentrations demonstrated notable variation among the studied taxa. The highest calcium level was detected in A. aegyptium (0.7089 mg/g), whereas S. ephippigera showed extremely low calcium values (0.0044 mg/g). Calcium is an important structural and physiological element involved in cuticle stabilization, muscle contraction, and intracellular signalling processes. The low calcium content observed in S. ephippigera may be associated with trophic specialization, as predatory Orthoptera frequently differ nutritionally from herbivorous taxa because of dietary composition and metabolic requirements. Iron concentrations ranged from 0.0323 to 0.3152 mg/g, with the highest value recorded in Gryllus bimaculatus. Iron is directly involved in oxidative metabolism, respiratory enzyme systems, and electron transport pathways26,29. Furthermore, studies on living organisms have shown that peptides, sugars, and other substances derived from insects possess anti-inflammatory activity. One study investigated the anti-inflammatory effects of glycosaminoglycans derived from crickets (G. bimaculatus) using a rat model of chronic arthritis5. Therefore, elevated Fe concentrations may indicate increased metabolic and locomotory activity in this species. Conversely, the low iron content observed in S. ephippigera may reflect lower mineral storage or ecological adaptations related to predatory feeding behavior. The mineral composition identified in the present study generally supports previous findings indicating that edible insects represent important natural sources of essential nutrients and minerals27,28. Consequently, the investigated species possess considerable potential for sustainable food systems, animal nutrition, and future biotechnological applications. Furthermore, the observed interspecific variation emphasizes the importance of ecological and environmental factors in determining mineral accumulation patterns. Habitat type, soil composition, vegetation diversity, feeding preference, developmental stage, and seasonal dynamics may significantly influence elemental profiles in insects20. Therefore, future studies should incorporate broader geographical sampling, seasonal monitoring, and ecological analyses to better understand the mechanisms underlying mineral accumulation in Orthoptera species.

Conclusion

The present study revealed that the investigated Orthoptera species possess considerable nutritional potential because of their high protein and mineral contents. Potassium was identified as the dominant mineral element across all species, whereas significant interspecific differences were observed for sodium, magnesium, calcium, and iron concentrations. Anacridium aegyptium exhibited particularly high potassium and calcium levels, while Gryllotalpa gryllotalpa showed elevated sodium concentrations and Gryllus bimaculatus possessed the highest iron content. The results indicate that ecological niche, feeding strategy, and physiological adaptations strongly influence mineral accumulation patterns in Orthoptera species. In addition, the relatively high protein ratios observed in several taxa support the growing evidence that insects may serve as sustainable alternative protein sources for both human consumption and animal feed applications27. Uvarovistia satunini, an easily identifiable species, is a harmful species that can cause significant damage. It forms swarms and can be a pest in agricultural areas during both its nymph and adult stages. It is a species that mostly lives in arid and hot climates, and almost every year it attacks agricultural lands in rural areas of the region in swarms, causing serious economic damage36.

Overall, the present findings provide important baseline data concerning the biochemical and nutritional properties of Orthopteran insects from the region. These data may contribute to future studies focusing on insect-based nutrition, ecological biomonitoring, sustainable agriculture, and the utilization of Orthoptera species in food and feed industries. Further multidisciplinary investigations involving amino acid composition, fatty acid profiles, seasonal mineral variation, and larger sampling efforts are recommended to comprehensively evaluate the ecological and nutritional significance of these species.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that the investigated Orthoptera species possess significant concentrations of essential minerals, including sodium, magnesium, potassium, calcium, and iron. Among the studied taxa, A. aegyptium exhibited particularly high levels of potassium, magnesium, and calcium, highlighting its nutritional importance. Similarly, G. bimaculatus showed the highest iron concentration, while G. gryllotalpa contained the highest sodium level. These findings indicate that Orthoptera species may represent valuable sources of essential minerals and could contribute to the development of sustainable alternative protein and nutrient resources. However, the considerable interspecific variability observed in mineral composition emphasizes the need for detailed species-level analyses when evaluating the nutritional potential of insect-based food systems. Future studies should focus on broader species sampling, seasonal and ecological variations, and additional nutritional parameters such as amino acid profiles, fatty acid composition, and micronutrient diversity. Such investigations would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the nutritional value of Orthoptera species and their potential role in sustainable food and feed production systems.
