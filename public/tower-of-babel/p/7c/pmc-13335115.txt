Internal cerebral vein sacrifice during pineal region epidermoid cyst resection: illustrative case

Complications related to the deep cerebral venous system during neurosurgical interventions are rarely reported in the literature. While cerebral venous sinus thrombosis is occasionally reported postoperatively, particularly in the setting of manipulation, hypotension, or thrombogenic predisposition, reports of direct sacrifice of an internal cerebral vein (ICV) remain exceedingly uncommon.1,2

The ICVs, formed by the union of the thalamostriate and choroidal veins at the foramen of Monro, are essential conduits for venous drainage of deep cerebral structures, including the thalami, basal ganglia, and deep white matter.3 Given their anatomical proximity to the pineal region, the ICVs are at particular risk during surgical approaches to pineal and posterior third ventricular cysts. However, to our knowledge, no prior reports describe the natural history after sacrifice of an ICV in a pediatric patient. We present the case of a 14-year-old male who underwent resection of a pineal region epidermoid cyst and sustained an ICV branch avulsion. We describe his postoperative course and recovery, and highlight considerations for recognition, management, and the prognosis of such an event.

Illustrative Case

A 14-year-old boy presented with recurrent headaches. He also reported intermittent episodes of emesis, dizziness, and lightheadedness associated with the headaches. The headaches were associated with tinnitus while lying flat and worsened with Valsalva maneuvers. He denied diplopia, vomiting, or focal weakness. His neurological examination was otherwise unremarkable, with no clinical evidence of Parinaud syndrome noted preoperatively. Preoperative imaging revealed a diffusion-restricting, nonenhancing lesion in the pineal region, consistent with an epidermoid cyst. The lesion was initially diagnosed in 2021 and demonstrated significant interval growth on serial imaging in 2022 and 2024 (Fig. 1). He remained neurologically intact, although with persistent headaches.

Preoperative axial T1-weighted with contrast (A), sagittal T2-weighted (B), axial diffusion-weighted (C), and apparent diffusion coefficient (D) MR images of the pineal region epidermoid cyst.

The patient underwent resection of this lesion via a supracerebellar, infratentorial approach. This approach was selected given the midline location within the pineal region and its relationship to the deep venous system. The supracerebellar approach provided a direct inferior trajectory to the pineal region while avoiding transgression of the occipital cortex that would be required with an occipital transtentorial approach. As the cyst was circumnavigated and decompressed, the right superior quadrant cyst wall was found to be adherent to the right ICV. During this part of the dissection, a poorly visualized branch of the right ICV adherent to the tumor was avulsed. The right ICV required sacrifice for hemostatic control. The patient did not require a transfusion and remained hemodynamically stable during the resection. Immediately postoperatively, the patient followed all commands but was notably weaker in his left upper and lower extremities. Furthermore, he had no verbal output, although he nodded and gestured for communication. An MRI study completed on postoperative day 1 demonstrated diffusion restriction in the bilateral (right greater than left) thalami as well as the splenium of the corpus callosum (Fig. 2). On postoperative day 1, he exhibited increasing somnolence and forced down gaze. This extreme somnolence lasted several days, during which he required maximal assistance for basic care. He also became slightly weaker in the left upper and lower extremities and withdrew from pain instead of following commands. He remained in the pediatric intensive care unit, and normotension and eunatremia were maintained. The patient was prescribed 4 mg of dexamethasone every 6 hours primarily to mitigate edema related to surgical manipulation and tumor resection in the pineal region rather than as treatment for the venous infarction itself. The dose was maintained until postoperative day 5 and then tapered over 5 days. The patient’s alertness gradually improved over the course of a week, but he had made minimal meaningful recovery of his speech at the time of discharge (speaking individual words 3 weeks postoperatively). He had a gastrostomy tube placed, primarily for impaired arousal rather than frank dysphagia, although mild oropharyngeal discoordination was noted. The patient received physical therapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy while inpatient. Over the hospital course, his left hemiparesis began to improve. By postoperative day 6, the patient began following commands consistently on the left side again, with 4/5 strength in all muscle groups on the left.

Immediate postoperative axial diffusion-weighted (A) and apparent diffusion coefficient (B) images showing acute infarctions in the bilateral thalami and splenium of the corpus callosum.

The patient was discharged to an acute rehabilitation unit on postoperative day 18, where he spent approximately 3 weeks undergoing intensive physical and occupational therapy. By his 6-week postoperative visit, he had regained near-full strength on his left side, with only mild difficulty initiating fine motor movements and a pronator drift in his left upper extremity. His speech returned to his preoperative baseline. His dysphagia had improved significantly, allowing for the removal of his gastrostomy tube. At his 3-month follow-up appointment, he had returned to school and was performing at his preoperative academic level. Neurological examination revealed only minimal left triceps weakness. At his 8-month follow-up, he was neurologically intact with resolution of his left upper extremity motor findings. A repeat MRI study revealed decreased stroke burden and chronic thrombosis of the right ICV (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The ICVs play a pivotal role in draining the bilateral thalami, corpus callosum, and deep white matter, forming part of the deep venous system that converges into the vein of Galen.3 Because of their location within the roof of the third ventricle, they are encountered in midline approaches to pineal, thalamic, and posterior lateral and third ventricular lesions. Iatrogenic sacrifice of the ICV is avoided whenever possible due to the presumed risk of bilateral thalamic venous infarction and poor neurological outcome.

Observations

In this case, despite more conservative efforts at hemostasis, the right ICV was sacrificed intraoperatively during resection of an epidermoid cyst. The patient’s imaging demonstrated restricted diffusion in the bilateral thalami and splenium of the corpus callosum—findings consistent with venous infarction of the right ICV. The bilateral thalamic involvement correlated clinically with his postoperative hypersomnolence and mutism, both characteristic of insult to the paramedian and intralaminar nuclei, which play a central role in arousal and verbal initiation. Bilateral thalamic infarction often leads to profound impairment in consciousness and motor initiation; however, in this patient, these deficits were transient.4 Remarkably, the patient demonstrated meaningful neurological recovery over the postoperative course, with full return of left-sided strength and normalization of verbal output within 8 months.

The literature on ICV thrombosis provides some context for understanding the consequences of impaired deep venous outflow. Tan described a pediatric case of postoperative unilateral ICV thrombosis resulting in right hemispheric watershed venous infarcts with spastic hemiplegia, visual field deficits, and seizures.u1 While the symptoms were initially severe, the patient was ultimately discharged with minimal residual weakness and an intention tremor.1 Another report of 2 patients with ICV thrombosis and intraoperatively preserved ICVs described sensorium alteration, but ultimately, both patients had good outcomes.2 The spectrum of symptoms from ICV thrombosis varies widely in the literature, from minor symptoms of headaches and emesis to severe symptoms with hemiparesis, minimally conscious states, or even death, although most studies focus on the adult population.5 Furthermore, these cases describe occlusion via thrombosis, rather than direct surgical sacrifice, and thus much of the discussion is focused on initiation of anticoagulation and resolution of the thrombus.

The time course of neurological recovery following deep venous injury appears to be variable. In our patient, the most profound deficits, including hypersomnolence, mutism, and hemiparesis, were most pronounced within the 1st postoperative week and gradually improved over several weeks with supportive care and rehabilitation. By 6 weeks postoperatively, the patient had regained near-full strength and meaningful speech, and by 8 months, he had returned to his neurological baseline. Similar trajectories have been described in cases of postoperative deep venous thrombosis, in which patients initially present with substantial neurological impairment that improves significantly within several days and resolves almost completely over weeks to months.2

Recovery following thalamic injury varies considerably depending on the nuclei involved and the mechanism of injury. The thalamus serves as a central relay for multiple cortico-subcortical networks, and different vascular territories produce distinct clinical syndromes. Prior work examining vascular syndromes of the thalamus suggests that lesions involving the paramedian and intralaminar nuclei frequently produce disturbances in arousal, mutism, and cognitive slowing. Although meaningful recovery may occur over time with network reorganization for hypersomnia and neurological deficits, studies show that cognitive deficits often persist.4,6 In contrast, inferolateral (thalamogeniculate) infarcts more frequently produce persistent contralateral sensory loss, dysesthesia, and central poststroke pain syndromes, which may be more disabling in the long term. Posterior territory infarcts may lead to visual field deficits and sensory disturbances due to involvement of the pulvinar and geniculate bodies.4

Additionally, venous infarction may demonstrate a more variable clinical course than arterial infarction because venous congestion may partially resolve as collateral drainage pathways develop.5,7 Experimental investigations of the deep cerebral venous system suggest that interruption of a single major venous outflow pathway may be partially compensated by collateral drainage through alternative venous channels.7 These findings support the concept that redundancy within the deep venous network can sometimes limit the clinical impact of unilateral venous injury. In the present case, the unilateral sacrifice of the right ICV may have allowed partial compensation through the contralateral deep venous system and collateral pathways, contributing to the patient’s gradual neurological recovery.

Lessons

This case provides rare insight into the natural history of iatrogenic ICV sacrifice. While previous surgical dogma has suggested that sacrifice of an ICV would be uniformly devastating, our patient’s recovery suggests that, under certain anatomical and physiological conditions, partial compensation via contralateral drainage or collateral pathways may mitigate long-term deficits. Should injury occur, early postoperative imaging, supportive care, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation are essential for optimizing functional outcomes.

This case illustrates that iatrogenic sacrifice of the ICV, while rare and traditionally considered catastrophic, may be survivable with meaningful neurological recovery in pediatric patients. Early recognition, supportive management, and rehabilitation are essential. Further study is needed to understand the factors that allow for favorable outcomes following deep venous sacrifice.
