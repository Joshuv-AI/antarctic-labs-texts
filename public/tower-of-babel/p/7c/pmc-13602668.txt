Malaria transmission dynamics, vector diversity, and insecticide resistance profiles in two health districts of southern Benin

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria transmission in Benin is heterogeneous, influenced by ecological settings and environmental changes affecting Plasmodium spp. prevalence, vector species composition, and insecticide resistance. Comprehensive local data on transmission dynamics remain limited, particularly in southern Benin. This study assessed malaria transmission, vector diversity, and insecticide resistance in two health districts to support the National Malaria Control Programme in designing targeted and effective vector control strategies.MethodsA repeated cross-sectional study was conducted in two health districts in southern Benin (Ouidah-Kpomasse-Tori Bossito (OKT) and Adjohoun-Bonou-Dangbo (ABD) districts). In OKT, blood samples were collected from August 2021 to August 2022, while in ABD sampling was conducted from July to November 2024. Human participants were screened for Plasmodium infections using microscopy and rapid diagnostic tests. Adult Anopheles mosquitoes were collected in both districts during rainy and dry seasons using human landing catches. Mosquitoes were morphologically and molecularly identified and screened for Plasmodium spp. Insecticide resistance was assessed through detection of kdr (L1014F) and ace-1 (G119S) mutations, and bioassays with pyrethroids (alpha-cypermethrin and deltamethrin) and synergists (PBO).ResultsA total of 1,962 participants were enrolled (OKT 1,577; ABD 385). Overall Plasmodium prevalence was 28.2% in OKT and 21.0% in ABD, with higher prevalence during the rainy season compared to the dry season in OKT (37.0% vs. 20.9%, p < 0.0001). Anopheles gambiae s.l. was the predominant species complex, with seasonal shifts in species composition determined molecularly; An. gambiae s.s. dominating in the rainy season (80.0% in OKT, 54% in ABD), while An. coluzzii predominated in the dry season (92.5% in OKT, 98.3% in ABD). High frequencies of kdr L1014F mutations were detected (0.62–0.93), whereas ace-1 resistance remained low (≤0.13). Plasmodium infection in mosquitoes was rare (<2%), and blood meals showed strong anthropophagy (97.8%). PBO bioassays indicated partial restoration of susceptibility to deltamethrin and alpha-cypermethrin.ConclusionsMalaria transmission in southern Benin remains sustained by high seasonal prevalence in humans, intense human-vector contact, and widespread pyrethroid resistance in vectors. Seasonal and spatial heterogeneity in vector species composition and resistance profiles highlight the need for adaptive, locally tailored control strategies, including monitoring and management of insecticide resistance.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13892-5.

Introduction

Malaria, caused by protozoan parasites of the Plasmodium genus and transmitted by mosquitoes of the Anopheles genus, remains one of the most important infectious diseases worldwide, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Of the five Plasmodium species known to infect humans (P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. malariae, P. ovale, and P. knowlesi), P. falciparum accounts for most severe cases and deaths [2, 3]. The remaining species contribute to ongoing transmission and morbidity in endemic regions [4–6]. Despite decades of intensified malaria control interventions, including the widespread distribution of long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs), indoor residual spraying (IRS), chemoprevention programs, and the use of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), malaria continues to exert a heavy toll on public health [7–9]. Globally, malaria accounts for 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths annually in 2024, with sub-Saharan Africa alone bearing approximately 94% of this burden [10].

The persistence of malaria is driven by multiple interrelated factors, including environmental factors, seasonal fluctuations in transmission, the co-circulation of multiple Plasmodium species, and the ecological diversity of Anopheles mosquito populations. These factors collectively shape the incidence, prevalence, and spatial distribution of malaria. Furthermore, the rapid emergence and spread of insecticide resistance particularly resistance to pyrethroids associated with the kdr L1014F mutation pose a major threat to the long-term effectiveness of LLINs and IRS [7, 11–13]. Moreover, resistance linked to the ace-1 gene has also been reported in certain vector populations, further reducing the efficacy of carbamates and organophosphates [14]. In addition, metabolic resistance, which can be partially inhibited by pre-exposure to pre-exposed to piperonyl butoxide (PBO), represents another important mechanism contributing to reduced susceptibility in mosquito populations [15].

In Benin, the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) has implemented WHO‑recommended strategies, including the universal distribution of LLINs, the use of ACTs as first‑line treatment, intermittent preventive treatment for pregnant women (IPTp), and targeted IRS campaigns [16]. Nevertheless, malaria transmission remains persistently high in many regions [17–19]. The country’s malaria epidemiology is shaped by a complex vector ecology involving primary vectors such as Anopheles gambiae s.l. and Anopheles funestus, alongside secondary or local vector species including Anopheles nili, Anopheles pharoensis, Anopheles ziemanni, and Anopheles coustani reported across different ecological settings [20–22]. Entomological surveys have reported heterogeneous vector densities, diverse Anopheles species composition, and insecticide resistance [23]. Moreover, epidemiological surveys have revealed a high proportion of asymptomatic infections within communities, constituting a silent reservoir for sustaining transmission [19]. Understanding malaria transmission dynamics therefore requires an integrated approach considering both parasitological and entomological parameters. Detailed data on vector species composition, abundance, biting behavior, and insecticide resistance profiles, combined with infection prevalence in humans and mosquitoes, are essential to identify local risk factors and guide targeted interventions. However, recent data on Plasmodium spp. prevalence, vector species composition and insecticide resistance profiles in several districts of southern Benin are limited, particularly in districts undergoing environmental and urban changes. Furthermore, local variations in transmission dynamics and resistance patterns across neighboring districts remain insufficiently documented.

In this context, the present study aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of malaria transmission dynamics in two neighboring health districts in southern Benin, across different seasonal sampling periods, by analyzing the prevalence of Plasmodium infections in humans and mosquitoes, the diversity of Anopheles species, and the insecticide resistance profiles of local vector populations.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study was a repeated cross-sectional investigation conducted in rural areas of two health districts located in southern Benin. Independent cross-sectional surveys were conducted during different transmission seasons, with different participants recruited at each survey round. Both districts share similar climatic conditions, characterized by comparable rainy and dry seasons, similar malaria transmission dynamics, and the presence of the same dominant Anopheles species [24]. In southern Benin, the climate is characterized by a bimodal rainfall pattern with four climatic periods: a long rainy season (April to mid-July), a short dry period (mid-July to mid-September), short rainy season (mid-September to October), and a long dry season (November to March). The study was implemented in two predefined sites and conducted in two distinct phases. Human and entomological surveys were conducted in the same villages to assess parasite circulation in both human and mosquito populations. Detailed descriptions of the study areas have been previously documented [16, 25, 26].

Human samples collection

In the Ouidah-Kpomasse-Tori Bossito (OKT) health district, human blood samples were collected weekly from August 2021 to August 2022, allowing coverage of both rainy and dry seasons. In the ABD health district, blood collections were conducted weekly from July to November 2024. Parasitological data were analyzed separately for each study site, and no inter-site comparisons were performed due to differences in sampling periods and study duration between the OKT and ABD health districts. Prior to sample collection, sensitization sessions were conducted with local authorities and communities to explain the study objectives and secure their support. All volunteers who provided informed consent to participating to the study were included. A structured questionnaire was administered to collect socio-demographic and anthropometric data, including age, gender, weight, and height (Supplementary file 1). Additionally, approximately 2 mL of venous blood was collected from each participant into EDTA tube. The blood was utilized for haemoglobin levels measurement and malaria screening by rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) and microscopic analysis of thick and thin blood smears.

Mosquito sampling

Mosquito collections were conducted in the same villages where human blood sampling took place. Adult mosquitoes were collected both indoors and outdoors using the human landing catch (HLC) method only, which was chosen because it allows capturing mosquitoes present in both indoor and outdoor environments and provides an accurate assessment of human exposure to mosquito bites. In each village, eight collection sites were randomly selected, and mosquito collection were carried out from 7:00 pm to 7:00 am indoors and outdoors over two and ten collection nights in OKT (4 villages through Ouidah and Kpomasse districts) and ABD (6 villages across Dangbo, Adjohoun and Bonou) respectively. Importantly, mosquito sampling was conducted during both rainy and dry seasons to capture seasonal variation in vector abundance and composition. In the Ouidah-Kpomasse-Tori Bossito (OKT) health district, mosquito collections were conducted during the long dry season (November 2021 to March 2022) and the short rainy period (September to October 2022), allowing coverage of the climatic periods and assessment of seasonal variation in vector abundance and species composition. In the ABD health district, mosquito collections were conducted from September to November 2023 and from January to March 2024, including both rainy and dry season sampling period. Collected mosquitoes were morphologically identified using standard taxonomic keys [27], counted, and stored individually in labeled Eppendorf tubes at − 20 °C for subsequent molecular analyses.

Malaria diagnostic procedure

Rapid diagnostic test: Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT): The Bioline® Malaria 4 species test kit containing a cassette format membrane strip was utilized for rapid malaria diagnostic, differentiating between P. falciparum and other species (P. malariae, P. vivax, and P. ovale). The membrane strip is pre-coated with monoclonal antibodies as three separate lines: a control line, a single pan line and a P. falciparum line. The test differentiates between Plasmodium spp. based on the presence of specific proteins (HRP-2 and pLDH antigens). The procedure involved using 5 μL of whole blood from the EDTA tube and transferring it to the sample well. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, assay diluent was added, and results were read after 15 minutes. Tests in which the control line failed to appear were considered invalid and were repeated.

Plasmodium spp. detection using Microscopy: Thin and thick blood smears were prepared, stained with Giemsa, and examined under a microscope for parasite identification and density determination following WHO recommendations [28].

Identification of sibling species in Anopheles gambiae group

Genomic DNA was extracted from the subsample of wild-caught mosquitoes and used for molecular species identification within the An. gambiae s.l. complex. All these samples were amplified following the protocol described by Scott et al. [29] to identify the different species within the complex. The method of Santolamazza et al. [30] was employed to differentiate the closely related species An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii, PCR products were stored at 4 °C prior to electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel.

Screening of target-site insensitivity associated mutations (kdr and Ace-1)

The same DNA extracts of wild-caught mosquitoes were used to screen for target-site insensitivity mutations associated with insecticide resistance targeting the L1014F (kdr-west) and L1014S (kdr-east) alleles, as well as the G119S mutation in the ace-1 gene. The detection of L1014F and L1014S mutations done using amplification protocol developed by Martinez-Torres et al. [31]. The PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 46 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final elongation step at 72 °C for 5 min.

The G119S mutation was detected following the protocol of Weill et al. [32]. Primers used were Moustdir1 (5′-CCGGGNGCSACYY ATG TGGAA-3′) and Moustrev1 (5′-ACGATMACG TTC TCY TCC GA-3′). The PCR program included an initial denaturation at 93 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 93 °C for 1 min, annealing at 53 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min 30 s, with a final elongation at 72 °C for 10 min.

PCR products were subsequently digested with the AluI restriction enzyme according to the manufacturer’s instructions and separated on a 2% agarose gel.

Plasmodium spp. infection rate among An. gambiae s.l. populations

Plasmodium spp. was amplified following the protocol of Padley et al. [33]. The reactions were performed in 25 μL with specific primers for P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, and P. malariae. Thermal cycling included initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, 43 cycles of 95 °C for 45 s, 60 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min, producing amplicons of 276 – 412 bp depending on species.

Blood meal origins

Blood-fed female An. gambiae s.l. mosquitoes were processed for host blood-meal identification using a direct enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) following the protocol originally described by Beier et al. [34]. Engorged abdomens were individually homogenized in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and 50 µL of each homogenate were added to 96-well ELISA plates. After incubation and washing with PBS-Tween, species-specific peroxidase-conjugated IgG antibodies against different vertebrate hosts (human, bovine, chicken, sheep, horse, pig, dog, and rat) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Color development was performed using ortho-tolidine substrate in citrate buffer (pH 4.0). Positive reactions were identified by blue coloration, then confirmed after addition of 4N sulfuric acid, producing a yellow color. Optical densities were measured at 620 and 450 nm using an ELISA plate reader.

Insecticide susceptibility and synergist tests

Larval surveys were conducted in all study areas from September to October 2024. Larvae and pupae of An. gambiae s.l. were collected from natural breeding sites using the standard dipping method [35]. Collected specimens were placed in labelled containers according to the surveyed localities and transported to the insectarium of the Centre de Recherche Entomologique de Cotonou (CREC). Larvae were reared under standard insectary conditions (26–30 °C and 60–90% relative humidity) at the CREC until adult emergence. Insecticide susceptibility tests were carried out following the World Health Organization guidelines [36]. Briefly, non-blood-fed female An. gambiae s.l., aged 2–5 days, were used for the bioassays. Mosquitoes were exposed to pyrethroid insecticides (0.05% alpha-cypermethrin and 0.75% permethrin) impregnated or control papers for 1 h before being transferred into a clean holding tube with 10% sugar solution. In addition, to assess the potential contribution of cytochrome P450s genes in the pyrethroid resistance, mosquitoes were pre-exposed to piperonyl butoxide (PBO) for one hour, after which they were transferred to tubes lined with insecticide-impregnated papers containing pyrethroids (0.05% alpha-cypermethrin or 0.75% permethrin). For each insecticide, four batches of 20–25 female mosquitoes were introduced into individual test tubes for exposure. Mortality was determined after 24 h post insecticide exposure.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using R software version 4.3.2 (http://www.r-project.org) and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2, San Diego, CA, USA).

The variables expressed as proportions for categorical data and medians (with range) for continuous data. Fever was defined as an axillary body temperature ≥ 37.5 °C. The abundance of collected mosquito species was calculated as the cumulative number of specimens obtained from both indoor and outdoor collections.

Differences in Plasmodium spp. prevalence in human participants between seasons were assessed using the Chi-square (χ2) test. Similarly, comparisons of mosquito infection rates, mosquito species composition, sibling species distribution, and genotype frequencies between groups (season, site, or species) were performed using Chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell counts were small. Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) were also assessed using Chi-square tests

The resistance status of An. gambiae s.l. was determined following exposure to the WHO discriminating insecticide dose and classified as:susceptible (mortality rate ≥ 98%),possible resistance (mortality rate between 90% and 97%),resistant (mortality rate < 90%).

Results

Socio-demographical characteristics of the study population

A total of 1,577 individuals were enrolled in the OKT health district and 385 in the ABD health district. The median age was lower in OKT (28 years; range: 2–110) than in ABD (40 years; range: 3–95), with adults aged ≥ 20 years being the most represented group in the two districts (59.5% in OKT and 76.4% in ABD). Females were more represented in the two areas (57.8% of participants in OKT and 68.3% in ABD). The median body weight and height were 49 kg and 156 cm in OKT respectively, compared with 53 kg and 158 cm in ABD. The small proportions of participants with fever (body temperature ˃37.5°C) were recorded in the two study areas (OKT: 7.3% and ABD: 10.4%). Prevalence of Plasmodium species in study participants

Overall, 28.2% (444/1,577) of participants in OKT health district were infected with Plasmodium spp., with a significantly higher prevalence during the rainy season (37.0%) compared with the dry season (20.9%) (p < 0.0001). Across both seasons, P. falciparum was the predominant species, representing 30.7% of infections in the rainy season and 19.0% in the dry season, followed by P. malariae (1.6% vs 0.3%) and P. ovale (0.2% vs 0.2%) (data not shown).

Rapid diagnostic test (RDT) showed a higher prevalence of Plasmodium infections in the rainy season (28.0%, 201/743) than in the dry season (20.5%, 171/834) (p = 0.04)

Among the 385 participants included in ABD health district, the overall prevalence of Plasmodium infection was 21.0% (81/385). P. falciparum remained the dominant species, detected in 78 participants (20.3%), while P. malariae was identified in one participant (0.3%). Mixed infections with P. falciparum and P. malariae were observed in two participants (0.5%) (data not shown).

Mosquito abundance and sibling species composition

A total of 2,562 mosquitoes were collected in the OKT health district, including 2,084 during the rainy season and 478 during the dry season. In the ABD health district, 20,469 mosquitoes were collected during the rainy season and 16,993 during the dry season. Anopheles mosquitoes accounted for 658 (32%) and 202 (42%) of collections in OKT and for 701 (3.4%) and 796 (4.7%) in ABD during the rainy and dry season, respectively.

Across districts and seasons, An. gambiae s.l. was the predominant species, accounting for 86.0 - 91.6% of Anopheles collections, followed by An. funestus s.l. (≤8.9%). Plasmodium infection rates in An. gambiae s.l.

During the rainy season, no Plasmodium infections were detected in mosquitoes from the OKT communes. Among the 235 An. gambiae s.l. specimens analyzed in the ABD communes, 3 tested positive for Plasmodium spp., with P. falciparum being the only species detected. Infections were restricted to An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii from Bonou commune, with an infection rate of 4% (3/75) (Supplementary table 1).

During the dry season, the infections were detected in both OKT and ABD districts, but only in Kpomasse and Bonou communes, respectively. In OKT, 2 out of 10 An. gambiae s.l. specimens from Kpomasse tested positive for P. falciparum (20% infection rate). In ABD, 4 out of 67 An. gambiae s.l. from Bonou were positive, resulting in an infection rate of 5.97%. No P. malariae, P. ovale, or P. vivax infections were detected during this season (Supplementary table 1).

Blood meal sources

Among 60 blood-fed An. gambiae s.l. specimens from both districts tested for blood meal origin, host source was successfully identified in 45 specimens. Of these, 44 fed on humans and 1 on pigs, resulting in a human blood index of 97.8% (data not shown).

Discussion

This study provides an integrated characterization of malaria transmission dynamics in two health districts of southern Benin. To achieve that we combined parasitological, entomological, and molecular experiment. By jointly examining human Plasmodium infections, vector species composition, insecticide resistance markers, and Plasmodium infection in mosquitoes, the findings provide operationally relevant insights to inform malaria control strategies in settings where transmission persists despite sustained intervention efforts.

A first key aspect of this integrated analysis concerns the seasonal variation in human Plasmodium spp. prevalence.

Overall, a marked seasonal variation in Plasmodium spp. prevalence was observed, with significantly higher infection rates during the rainy season. More than one-quarter (>25%) of participants were infected during the high transmission period in OKT district, highlighting the continued intensity of Plasmodium spp. transmission despite ongoing malaria control interventions. Results obtained from rapid diagnostic tests were consistent with those from microscopy, confirming the reliability of the observed seasonal trends. This seasonal pattern aligns with the well-established influence of rainfall on malaria transmission, which acts by increasing larval habitat availability, vector abundance, and the frequency of human-vector contact [37, 38]. As reported elsewhere in Benin and across West Africa, P. falciparum remained the predominant species across both transmission seasons [18, 25, 39]. The high proportion of asymptomatic infections detected in the community represents a major challenge for malaria control, as such individuals constitute a silent reservoir capable of sustaining transmission even in the presence of effective case management and vector control interventions [40–42]. These findings therefore emphasize the need to complement existing strategies with community-based approaches that specifically target asymptomatic infections [43, 44].

Although Plasmodium infection rates in An. gambiae s.l. populations were relatively low at health district level in our study area, the detection of infected mosquitoes across surveyed districts clearly indicates that malaria transmission remains active. Importantly, the exclusive detection of P. falciparum in mosquitoes mirrors the epidemiological pattern observed in the human population. This concordance between human and vector infections suggests efficient transmission of P. falciparum by local mosquito populations [23, 24]. Furthermore, blood meal analysis revealed pronounced anthropophagic behavior, indicating strong human-vector contact, confirming that malaria transmission in the study area occurs predominantly through human-vector contact. Consequently, even in the context of relatively low vector infection rates, the combination of frequent human biting, infected mosquitoes, and a substantial reservoir of asymptomatic human infections creates favorable conditions for sustained transmission.

In this context, the entomological survey revealed that across both health districts and during both the rainy and dry season, An. gambiae s.l. was the overwhelmingly predominant malaria vector and was collected both indoors and outdoors, confirming its central role in malaria transmission in southern Benin. This pattern is consistent with numerous entomological surveys conducted in the southern part of the country, where An. gambiae s.l. is recognized as the primary and most efficient vector of Plasmodium spp. transmission [23, 24, 45]. The indoor and outdoor presence of An. gambiae s.l. further highlights its behavioral plasticity, which may allow it to maintain transmission despite widespread vector control interventions.

The study showed spatial and seasonal variation in the composition of the An. gambiae complex across the two health districts. During the rainy season, An. gambiae s.s. predominated, whereas An. coluzzii became dominant during the dry season in both health districts. These patterns are consistent with previous studies conducted in West Africa and reflect ecological differences between sibling species, particularly their adaptation to distinct larval habitats [46, 47]. While An. gambiae s.s. is typically associated with temporary, rain-dependent breeding sites, An. coluzzii is better adapted to more permanent or human-modified water bodies that persist during dry periods [46, 48]. Although hybrids were detected at low frequencies, suggesting limited interspecific mating, even rare hybridization events may facilitate the transfer of adaptive traits such as insecticide resistance alleles between species [49, 50]. Continuous monitoring of Anopheles species composition therefore remains essential, as temporal and spatial shifts in vector populations can significantly influence both transmission dynamics and the effectiveness of control interventions.

Against this entomological backdrop, molecular analyses revealed very high frequencies of the kdr West (L1014F) resistant genotype in both An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii, indicating intense and geographically widespread pyrethroid resistance in southern Benin. A slight increase in the frequency of resistant L1014F genotypes was observed during the dry season, consistent with previously reported seasonal fluctuations in insecticide resistance mutation frequencies in field mosquito populations [51]. This widespread resistance is likely driven by long-term exposure to pyrethroid-based interventions in public health and agriculture [52–54] and poses a robust challenge to the continued effectiveness of standard vector control tools such as long-lasting insecticidal nets and indoor residual spraying.

In contrast, resistance mediated by the Ace-1 gene remained low across all sites and seasons, with susceptible genotypes predominating. This finding is consistent with previous reports from southern Benin, where ace-1R allele frequencies have generally remained low [14, 45, 55], likely reflecting the historically limited use of carbamates and organophosphates in this region. The low prevalence of Ace-1-mediated resistance suggests that reduced susceptibility to these insecticide classes is not currently driven by target-site insensitivity in the study area [14]

Taken together, these findings have important implications for malaria control in southern Benin. The consistent predominance of An. gambiae s.l. across sampling periods, together with observed seasonal shifts between An. gambiae s.s. and An. coluzzii indicate that malaria transmission is sustained by a highly adaptable vector system. The pyrethroid resistance further threaten the continued effectiveness of standard interventions such as long-lasting insecticidal nets and indoor residual spraying. Continuous entomological and resistance surveillance is therefore essential to detect changes in species composition and resistance patterns and to guide the implementation of alternative or complementary vector control tools such as non-pyrethroid insecticides, next-generation nets, and integrated vector management strategies.

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed resistance patterns, bioassays conducted in the presence of PBO provided additional insights. Exposure to PBO prior to alpha-cypermethrin or deltamethrin led to a partial restoration of susceptibility in all communes, though with notable spatial heterogeneity among districts indicating that metabolic resistance mechanisms, likely mediated by monooxygenases, contribute to the observed pyrethroid resistance. However, since mortality did not reach 98% in any district, target-site mutations and potentially other metabolic pathways likely also play a role [15, 56, 57]. These findings suggest that PBO-based nets or combination formulations may improve control of resistant mosquito populations in some settings, while also underscoring the need for continuous, site-specific resistance monitoring to guide insecticide choice and relevant resistance management strategies.

This study presents some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, there was a marked imbalance in the number of human samples collected between the two health districts, with 1,577 participants enrolled in the OKT health district compared with 385 in the ABD health district. In addition, human parasitological sampling in the ABD district was conducted only from July to November 2024 because of logistical and financial constraints related to the project implementation timeline, whereas sampling in the OKT district covered a full annual cycle from August 2021 to August 2022. The absence of complete rainy- and dry-season data in the ABD district therefore limits direct comparison of parasitological data between the two districts and underscores the need for future longitudinal surveys covering both seasons in all study areas. Furthermore, although PBO synergist bioassays provided evidence that metabolic resistance mechanisms contribute to the observed pyrethroid resistance, the inference that this resistance is likely mediated by monooxygenases remains indirect and is based on phenotypic restoration of susceptibility. Additional molecular and biochemical investigations are therefore required to directly identify the detoxification enzymes involved and to quantify the relative contribution of monooxygenases and other metabolic pathways to pyrethroid resistance in local Anopheles populations

In conclusion, malaria transmission in southern Benin remains sustained by a convergence of factors, including high seasonal prevalence of human infections, substantial asymptomatic reservoirs, and widespread pyrethroid resistance in vector populations. The pronounced spatial and seasonal heterogeneity observed in vector species composition and resistance profiles highlights the need for adaptive, locally tailored malaria control strategies. Strengthening integrated surveillance systems that simultaneously monitor human infections, vector dynamics, and insecticide resistance mechanisms will be essential to optimizing intervention effectiveness and advancing malaria control and elimination efforts in comparable endemic settings. Based on the present findings, we recommend that the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) in Benin reinforce community-based approaches aimed at identifying and targeting asymptomatic infections, while explicitly addressing both indoor and outdoor transmission in highly anthropophagic vector populations. In parallel, the PNLP should prioritize the deployment of next-generation long-lasting insecticidal nets incorporating synergists such as PBO and consider the use of non-pyrethroid insecticides for indoor residual spraying as part of an evidence-based insecticide resistance management strategy
