Quality, content, and engagement of pulmonary tuberculosis related videos on Douyin: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Liang Yan, Huishan Cen, Biqing Yi, Weiling Guo, Duohua Su, Yi Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) remains a major public health concern worldwide despite being preventable and curable. China remains one of the 30 high tuberculosis (TB) burden countries, and short-video platforms such as Douyin (the Chinese mainland version of TikTok) have become important sources of health information, yet the quality of PTB-related content remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the content completeness, reliability, quality, and engagement of PTB-related videos on Douyin.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional content analysis on October 23, 2025. Using the hashtag “#pulmonary tuberculosis” under the default “comprehensive ranking”, we initially retrieved 220 videos. Videos ranked 201–220 were reserved as a priori for pilot reviewer calibration, and the remaining top 200 videos underwent formal screening, of which 191 met the inclusion criteria. We extracted video characteristics, uploader type, and engagement metrics. A PTB-specific checklist covering six dimensions (definition, symptoms, risk factors, evaluation, management, outcomes) was scored, and reliability/quality were assessed using Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmarks, Global Quality Score (GQS), modified DISCERN instrument (mDISCERN), and Medical Quality Video Evaluation Tool (MQ-VET). Spearman correlation and multivariable linear regression were performed.ResultsOf 191 videos, 150 (78.5%) were posted by health professionals. Most were uploaded in 2024–2025 (86.4%); median duration was 74 s [interquartile range (IQR) 48–110]. Content completeness was limited (median 3.0, IQR 2.0–4.5), with risk factors and outcomes rarely addressed; social-context coverage was also limited. Health professionals and science communicators achieved higher content scores than general users and news agencies (P<0.001). Overall quality was modest (median MQ-VET 46, IQR 42–48), lowest among general users and highest among science communicators (P<0.001). Engagement indicators were strongly intercorrelated but weakly or negatively associated with quality, and high-quality videos were not concentrated in top-ranking positions. In regression models, higher comment counts were associated with lower quality, whereas collections were associated with higher quality.ConclusionsPTB-related videos on Douyin showed substantial heterogeneity in content completeness and overall quality. Higher-quality videos were not consistently prioritized by platform ranking or user engagement, highlighting the need to promote trustworthy and comprehensive PTB information.Keywords: Tuberculosis (TB), social media, health communication, cross-sectional studies, Douyin

Highlight box

Key findings

• Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB)-related videos on Douyin varied substantially in content completeness, reliability, and quality.

• Health professionals generally provided higher-quality videos, but higher quality did not consistently translate into greater ranking or engagement.

• Risk factors, outcomes, financial burden, and stigma/discrimination were insufficiently addressed.

What is known and what is new?

• Short-video platforms are increasingly used for health information sharing, but popularity does not necessarily indicate reliability. Evidence on PTB-related videos on Douyin remains limited.

• This study assessed PTB-related Douyin videos using disease-specific content scoring, quality/reliability instruments, engagement metrics, and social-context coding.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PTB communication should include not only biomedical information but also treatment adherence, stigma reduction, and social support.

• Health professionals, public health institutions, and platforms should improve the visibility of accurate, comprehensive, and socially sensitive PTB content.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health concern worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, an estimated 10.7 million people developed TB in 2024 and 1.23 million died from the disease (1). Beyond its direct health impact, TB imposes substantial social and economic burdens on patients, families, and health systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (2,3). China remains one of the 30 high TB-burden countries. In the current WHO reporting cycle, China was estimated to have had 696,000 incident TB cases and 25,000 TB-related deaths in 2024, underscoring its continuing contribution to the global TB burden. Regional heterogeneity also persists within China, with the western region bearing a disproportionately higher burden than eastern, central, and northeastern regions, alongside ongoing urban-rural disparities in access to timely diagnosis and quality care (4,5). Improving public awareness of TB symptoms, routes of transmission, and the importance of early medical evaluation is therefore an important component of TB control. Against this epidemiologic background, Douyin represents a particularly relevant platform for health communication research.

Over the past decade, social media has fundamentally reshaped how people seek and share health information. Platforms such as TikTok and its Chinese mainland counterpart, Douyin, reach very large user populations worldwide (6,7). Douyin is now one of the most widely used short-video platforms in China, with approximately 780 million daily active users (8). Users view an average of over 200 videos daily, with an average viewing duration reaching 52 minutes (9). While this creates opportunities for large-scale health education, it also amplifies misinformation, sensationalized narratives, and unbalanced risk communication (10,11). A growing body of work has assessed the quality of TikTok health content across conditions such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), hypertension and aortic dissection (12-14). Across studies, content produced by health professionals or reputable organizations tends to be more accurate and comprehensive, whereas highly engaging videos from lay users may be incomplete or misleading. Systematic and scoping reviews further highlight methodological heterogeneity and the need for standardized evaluation frameworks (15).

In contrast, pulmonary TB (PTB) related content on Douyin has not been systematically evaluated. PTB also poses several communication challenges that differentiate it from many other infectious diseases assessed on social media. It is transmitted through airborne particles, often in crowded or poorly ventilated settings, and may remain latent and asymptomatic for years before progressing to active disease. The prolonged treatment course, the growing threat of drug-resistant PTB, and links with poverty, migration, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection contribute to stigma and social vulnerability (16). These features create a complex infodemiological landscape: audiences may underestimate latent infection, overestimate casual transmission, or doubt curability, which can delay care-seeking and reduce adherence. PTB is also often perceived as an “old” disease and may receive less sustained media attention than emerging infections, widening the gap between scientific guidance and what circulates online.

At the same time, digital platforms offer opportunities to normalize discussion of PTB and to promote evidence-based prevention and care. Recent WHO advocacy documents emphasize the role of social media in awareness and patient engagement, while underscoring the need for quality control and active involvement of health authorities (17). In September 2024, WHO and TikTok announced a collaboration to expand access to science-based health information on the platform (18). Independent, disease-specific evaluations, particularly for PTB, are therefore needed to inform and assess such initiatives. Against this background, we conducted a cross-sectional study to evaluate the informational quality, reliability, and content characteristics of PTB-related videos on Douyin, and to examine whether these features varied by uploader type and were aligned with user engagement. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1289/rc) (19).

Methods

Study design and overview

We conducted a cross-sectional content analysis of PTB-related videos on Douyin. The analysis focused on Chinese-language content and followed a protocol informed by prior evaluations of health information on short-video platforms. All data were obtained from publicly available videos without user interaction or collection of personal identifiers; therefore, ethics approval and informed consent were not required under local regulations.

Search strategy and video selection

On October 23, 2025, we searched Douyin using the hashtag “#pulmonary tuberculosis” under the default “comprehensive ranking” mode. To reduce personalization effects, we used a newly created account with no posted content, followers, or browsing history and cleared device caches before data collection. The search was conducted using a single device and Internet Protocol (IP) address in the Chinese mainland to maintain a consistent algorithmic context. A total of 220 videos were initially retrieved under the default comprehensive ranking. Videos ranked 201–220 were reserved a priori for pilot reviewer calibration and were not included in the final analytic sample. The remaining top 200 videos underwent formal eligibility screening, of which 191 met the inclusion criteria. Videos were eligible if they (I) provided information related to PTB (e.g., symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, or patient experiences); (II) were in Mandarin Chinese or included Chinese subtitles; and (III) conveyed health education or descriptive content rather than purely entertainment. We excluded videos that were irrelevant to PTB, exact duplicates, or ultra-short clips (<10 seconds), which typically contain minimal informational content. The complete dataset and classification criteria of video sources are available in Tables S1,S2.

Data extraction and classification of video characteristics

For each included video, we recorded upload year, uploader region (as displayed on the profile), video duration (seconds), and the number of days between the upload date and the search date. Engagement metrics were extracted at the time of data collection, including likes, comments, collections, and shares. Videos were categorized into four source types based on uploader publicly visible profile characteristics: (I) health professionals (e.g., physicians, nurses, and other credentialed medical staff affiliated with hospitals or public health institutions); (II) general users (e.g., patients or individuals without formal health credentials); (III) news agencies (e.g., television stations, newspapers, or official media accounts); and (IV) science communicators (e.g., popular science bloggers or institutional science communication accounts). Detailed classification criteria are provided in Table S2. We also recorded whether a video mentioned or demonstrated purified protein derivative (PPD) skin testing, given its widespread use for PTB screening in China.

Evaluation of PTB-specific content

To evaluate completeness of PTB-related information, we adapted a disease-specific content framework used in prior studies (20). The framework included six core dimensions: definition, symptoms, risk factors, evaluation, management, and outcomes. Each dimension was scored from 0 to 2 (0= not mentioned; 2= comprehensively and accurately covered). Partial coverage was scored as 0.5 (brief/tangential), 1.0 (basic but incomplete), or 1.5 (relatively detailed but not comprehensive), consistent with current PTB guidance. Dimension scores were summed to obtain a total content score ranging from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating more complete PTB-related content (Table S3). This framework was intended to assess whether key PTB-related topics were covered, rather than to verify the factual accuracy of statements within each domain. In response to the social dimensions of PTB, we further conducted an exploratory social-context coding among videos that remained accessible at the time of revision. Five binary variables were coded: stigma or discrimination, treatment adherence, financial burden, socioeconomic vulnerability, and social/family/work impact.

Video quality and reliability assessment

We assessed reliability and overall quality using four instruments commonly applied in online health information research. Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria assess transparency across four domains (authorship, attribution, disclosure, and currency; total 0–4) (21,22). Global Quality Score (GQS) is a 5-point Likert scale reflecting overall quality, flow, and usefulness for lay audiences (23). The modified DISCERN instrument (mDISCERN) includes five items assessing reliability and balance of treatment information (total 0–5) (24). Medical Quality Video Evaluation Tool (MQ-VET) is a validated 15-item scale designed for online medical videos; each item is scored from 1 to 5 for a total of 15–75, with higher scores indicating better quality (25). Details are provided in Tables S4-S7. To evaluate the relationship between platform visibility and information quality, we recorded each video’s position in the comprehensive ranking list and grouped videos as R1–50, R51–100, R101–150, R151–200.

Statistical analysis

All videos were independently rated by two reviewers (L.Y. and H.C.), both infectious disease physicians with ≥5 years of clinical experience and training in infection control. Before formal scoring, the reviewers calibrated the PTB content framework and the four quality instruments using a pilot set of 20 videos ranked 201–220 in the same initial Douyin retrieval (Table S8). During formal rating, each video was scored independently. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion; if consensus could not be reached, a third senior reviewer (Y.F.) adjudicated. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and were compared across source types using Kruskal-Wallis tests; categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate. When overall tests were significant, Dunn post hoc comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment were applied. Spearman rank correlation coefficients were used to assess associations between engagement indicators and quality metrics, and results were visualized using a correlation heatmap. Separate multivariable linear regression models were fitted for each quality outcome (JAMA, GQS, mDISCERN, and MQ-VET). Predictors included log10-transformed counts of likes, comments, collections, and shares, and the presence of PPD-related content. For variables containing zeros, we applied a log transformation as log10(x + 1). A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corp) and GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Study selection and general characteristics

A total of 191 videos were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Table 1 and Figure 2 summarize the baseline characteristics of the videos by source type. Health professionals accounted for the majority of uploads (150/191, 78.5%), followed by general users (21/191, 11.0%), news agencies (13/191, 6.8%), and science communicators (7/191, 3.7%). The distribution of upload years differed significantly by source type (P=0.01). Overall, 105 of 191 videos (55.0%) were uploaded in 2025 and 60 (31.4%) in 2024. Geographically, videos originated from multiple regions, with Liaoning (31/191, 16.23%), Henan (28/191, 14.66%), Sichuan (27/191, 14.14%), Shanghai (15/191, 7.85%), and Beijing (14/191, 7.33%) contributing the largest shares. Only a minority of videos (19/191, 9.95%) explicitly mentioned or demonstrated PPD testing, and the proportion did not differ significantly by source type (P>0.99). The median number of days videos had been online was 232 (IQR 147–481). Science communicators’ videos tended to be older (median 568 days, IQR 457–750), whereas health professionals’ videos were relatively newer (median 200 days, IQR 139–399). The median video duration was 74 seconds (IQR 48–110) across all sources. Science communicators produced the longest videos (median 110 seconds, IQR 105–184), general users had slightly longer videos (median 77 seconds, IQR 48–146), and news agencies tended to post shorter clips (median 34 seconds, IQR 17–80), although these differences did not reach statistical significance (P=0.09).

User engagement by source type

Engagement metrics varied markedly by source type. Overall, the median number of likes was 690 (IQR 241–1,680), comments was 129 (IQR 40–372), collections was 189 (IQR 72–550), and shares was 226 (IQR 67–911). Videos from science communicators were disproportionately popular, with median likes of 38,000 (IQR 37,000–44,000), comments of 1,077 (IQR 799–2,909), collections of 4,205 (IQR 2,553–5,940), and shares of 4,362 (IQR 2,052–6,060). News agencies’ videos also performed well, with median likes of 2,630 (IQR 1,388–7,502) and shares of 966 (IQR 315–4,889). By contrast, videos from health professionals attracted relatively modest engagement despite their numerical dominance, with median likes of 565 (IQR 214–1,292) and median shares of 224 (IQR 70–814). General users’ videos achieved intermediate popularity, with median likes of 862 (IQR 228–2,391) and median shares of 83 (IQR 41–199). Between-group differences in likes, comments, collections, and shares were all statistically significant (P≤0.01).

PTB-specific content coverage

Across all 191 videos (Figure 3), the median total content score was 3.0 (IQR 2.0–4.5) out of a maximum of 12. The median score for the definition was 0 (IQR 0–0.5). Symptoms were mentioned in a subset of videos (median score 0, IQR 0–1.0). Risk factors were seldom addressed (median score 0, IQR 0–0.5). In contrast, the evaluation dimension showed relatively higher coverage, with a median score of 1 (IQR 0–1). Management content also reached a median score of 0.5 (IQR 0–1). Information on outcomes was infrequently discussed (median 0.5, IQR 0–1). Content scores differed significantly by source type. Health professionals and science communicators outperformed general users and news agencies on most dimensions. The median score was 3.0 (IQR 2.0–5.0) for health professionals and 4.0 (IQR 1.0–5.5) for science communicators, compared with 1.0 (IQR 0.5–2.5) for general users and 2.0 (IQR 1.5–3.5) for news agencies (P<0.001). In the exploratory social-context analysis (Table S9), treatment adherence was the most commonly mentioned social dimension (48.0%), followed by social/family/work impact (15.6%), socioeconomic vulnerability (15.0%), and financial burden (6.9%). Stigma or discrimination was rarely addressed (1.2%).

Video quality and reliability metrics

Figure 4 displays the distributions of JAMA, GQS, mDISCERN, and MQ-VET scores by source type. Across all videos, the median JAMA score was 2 (IQR 2–2). JAMA scores varied by source: health professionals, news agencies, and science communicators achieved scores of 2, whereas general users had a median score of 0 (IQR 0–1; P<0.001). The median GQS for all videos was 2 (IQR 2–3). Health professionals and science communicators achieved higher GQS scores than general users (median 2–3 vs. 1–2, P<0.001). The median mDISCERN score across all videos was 3 (IQR 3–3). General users’ videos had the lowest mDISCERN scores (median 2, IQR 1–2), while videos from health professionals, news agencies, and science communicators clustered at the upper end of the scale (median 3, IQR 3–3; P<0.001). MQ-VET provided the most granular differentiation of video quality. Overall, the median MQ-VET score was 46 (IQR 42–48). General users’ videos scored poorly (median 28, IQR 27–29). Health professionals’ videos achieved a median MQ-VET score of 47 (IQR 44–48), and news agencies’ videos scored 45 (IQR 39–49). Science communicators achieved the highest MQ-VET scores (median 48, IQR 43–48; P<0.001).

Correlation and regression analyses

Spearman correlation analysis revealed positive correlations among engagement metrics (likes, comments, collections, shares) (Figure 6). Content scores and quality metrics (JAMA, GQS, mDISCERN, MQ-VET) were also positively correlated with one another. In multivariable linear regression models with each quality metric (JAMA, GQS, mDISCERN, MQ-VET) as the dependent variable and log10-transformed engagement indicators as predictors, comments and collections showed consistent effects (Figure 7). A higher number of comments was associated with lower scores across all four metrics (β −0.70 for JAMA and −8.17 for MQ-VET), whereas more collections were associated with higher quality scores (β 0.48 for JAMA and 6.04 for MQ-VET). Shares were positively associated with JAMA and MQ-VET only, and likes were not significantly related to any quality measure. The presence of PPD-related content was positively associated with GQS, with a similar but non-significant trend for the other metrics.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional analysis of 191 PTB-related Douyin videos, we identified several key findings. First, the overall completeness of PTB-related content was limited, and key clinical topics were frequently underrepresented. Specifically, information on risk factors, long-term outcomes, and treatment prognosis was rarely addressed, despite their importance for timely care-seeking and sustained adherence. Second, videos posted by health professionals and science communicators demonstrated significantly higher content completeness and overall quality than those posted by general users and news agencies. Third, higher-quality videos were not consistently prioritized in the platform’s default ranking, and quality indicators were weakly or negatively associated with engagement metrics, suggesting a misalignment between informational value and visibility (26).

These patterns suggest an “infodemiological gap”, in which users may be more likely to encounter popular but incomplete PTB content than evidence-based, comprehensive information (27). Similar discrepancies between content quality and engagement have been reported for multiple conditions on short-video platforms, where emotionally salient narratives or simplified messages can outperform educational content in reach and interaction (11,12). Our application of JAMA benchmarks, GQS, mDISCERN, and MQ-VET provides a structured view of both transparency and educational value and enables comparison with prior work on TikTok, YouTube, and Bilibili (28,29). The marked gradient in MQ-VET across uploader types suggests that uploader type was associated with differences in the accuracy, organization, and usability of PTB information. Notably, science communicators in our sample achieved both high quality and high engagement, suggesting that entertainment-oriented formats and scientific rigor may coexist in some cases. However, because this subgroup was small, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory (30).

From a PTB-control perspective, the observed content gaps are concerning but also highlight actionable opportunities (3). Under-discussion of risk factors may weaken public understanding of who is most vulnerable, potentially delaying evaluation among high-risk groups (31). Limited coverage of outcomes and prognosis may also reinforce misconceptions, such as the belief that PTB is uniformly catastrophic or, conversely, that it is trivial once symptoms improve. Because PTB treatment requires prolonged adherence and follow-up, incomplete messaging about treatment course and outcomes may contribute to poorer understanding of treatment continuity and prognosis (32). The exploratory social-context analysis showed that treatment adherence was the most frequently mentioned social dimension, whereas social/family/work impact, socioeconomic vulnerability, financial burden, and stigma or discrimination were much less visible. This imbalance suggests that PTB was still framed mainly through a biomedical lens, despite its substantial effects on employment, schooling, family relationships, social participation, and economic security (33-35). Short videos that overlook these social dimensions may miss opportunities to reduce fear, normalize care-seeking, and support patients through the long treatment process (36). Therefore, health authorities and clinical institutions should consider producing short, structured, and culturally tailored PTB content that covers core topics consistently, including transmission routes, key symptoms, risk factors, screening/diagnosis, treatment duration, and outcomes. Platform-level strategies such as verified labeling, prompts linking to authoritative resources, and ranking adjustments that reward transparency and informational completeness could help improve the visibility of high-quality PTB education (30). The strong engagement observed for some non-professional accounts also suggests that collaboration with experienced science communicators may be an efficient pathway to increase reach without compromising accuracy (37).

This study has several strengths. Our study systematically evaluated PTB-related content on Douyin using a comprehensive set of validated instruments. By combining a PTB-specific content framework with multiple quality and reliability tools, we were able to capture both disease-specific completeness and general indicators of transparency and educational value. In addition, videos were independently evaluated by trained reviewers with infectious disease expertise, and disagreements were resolved using a standardized procedure, which strengthens internal consistency. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample was derived from a single hashtag search under default ranking on one date, which may not capture the full range of TB-related content available on Douyin. Videos using alternative PTB-related keywords, different presentation styles, or lower-visibility posting strategies may have been missed. In addition, because platform algorithms and content visibility can vary over time, this approach may have overrepresented videos that were temporarily more active, more engaging, or more strongly promoted at the time of data collection. This may have biased our estimates of source composition, content completeness, and the observed relationship between informational quality and engagement. Second, engagement metrics were captured at one time point and may change over time, which limits inference about long-term visibility or diffusion. In addition, we did not collect view-count data and publicly accessible video information did not provide a stable viewer-count metric that could be systematically extracted for this study. As a result, we could not assess engagement relative to exposure (e.g., likes or comments per view) or estimate the actual audience reach of individual videos. Although official Douyin documentation indicates that play-count data may be available through authorized platform interfaces, these data require user or creator authorization and were not available within our public-data collection framework. Third, although JAMA, GQS, mDISCERN, and MQ-VET are useful for evaluating general information quality, reliability, and usefulness, they do not fully assess TB-specific clinical accuracy. No dedicated PTB-specific misinformation or accuracy checklist was applied in this study. Accordingly, potential inaccuracies related to key PTB topics, such as transmission, latent versus active TB, treatment, drug resistance, and adherence, may not have been fully captured. Fourth, some uploader subgroups, particularly science communicators, were small in size (Table S10); therefore, the corresponding subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Fifth, although stigma and social vulnerability are important dimensions of PTB communication, we did not include a dedicated stigma- or framing-related coding framework in this study. Finally, our assessment focused on Chinese-language videos and therefore may not generalize to other languages or cultural contexts.

Future work should use longitudinal designs to track how PTB-related content and engagement evolve and how they respond to major events, campaigns, or policy changes (38). Experimental or quasi-experimental studies could test whether platform prompts, verification labels, or ranking interventions increase exposure to high-quality content and improve PTB-related knowledge (39). Mixed-methods research integrating content analysis with user interviews could clarify why certain narratives attract attention and how audiences interpret PTB messages (40). In addition, analyses of comments and repost networks may help identify misinformation trajectories and stigma-related audience responses.

Conclusions

PTB-related videos on Douyin showed substantial heterogeneity in content completeness, reliability, and overall quality. Videos posted by health professionals and science communicators tended to be more accurate and comprehensive, yet they are not consistently prioritized by platform ranking or reflected in higher engagement. In contrast, highly visible videos from general users and news agencies are often incomplete, particularly with respect to risk factors, outcomes, and long-term management.
