Outcome of spine surgery in the context of spinal metastatic disease: The National Surgical Quality Improvement Program
Authors: Salim M. Yakdan, Maya Herrera, Nour Wehbe, Monifa Al Akoum, Muhammad Irfan Kaleem, Miguel A. Ruiz-Cardozo, Karan Joseph, Nada Assaf, Hani Dimassi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Spinal metastases are the third most common site of metastasis, with around 10% of cancer patients experiencing symptomatic spine involvement. Despite poor overall survival, recent advances in targeted therapies and minimally invasive surgical techniques have made surgery a more viable option for managing metastatic spine disease.Objective:Our study aims to identify perioperative risk factors associated with poor outcomes following spine surgery in the setting of spinal cord metastasis.Methods:We used the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program data. Patients with metastatic spine disease undergoing spine surgery were identified. Our primary outcome measure was 30-day morbidity and mortality after surgery. Our secondary outcome was prolonged hospital stay. Logistic regression model for each outcome based on individual characteristics was developed.Results:A total of 2109 patients were included. The 30-day morbidity and mortality rates were 19.1%. Significant predictors included smoking, more than 10% body weight loss, surgical urgency, dependent status, and preoperative albumin levels. In addition, 28.6% of patients experienced a prolonged hospital stay, with significant predictors including chemotherapy, surgical urgency, dependent status, preoperative hematocrit, neurological deficits, preoperative albumin levels, and surgical complexity.Conclusion:Our study provides valuable insights into the risk factors associated with mortality, morbidity, and prolonged hospital stay in patients with spinal cord metastasis undergoing spine surgery. These identified factors can be instrumental in assisting clinicians with risk stratification, preoperative optimization, and postoperative care planning, ultimately improving patient outcomes. Further research and validation of these predictive models are warranted to ensure their generalizability and applicability in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

The spinal column is the third most common site of metastasis, following the lungs and liver, with 10% of patients with cancer suffering from symptomatic spine metastases.[1] Symptomatic spinal metastasis is particularly associated with a poor prognosis characterized by a mean overall survival of around 1 year.[2] Due to their unfavorable outcome, patients with metastatic spine disease received limited clinical and investigational attention. With the advent of new treatment modalities such as: Targeted genetic and immunotherapy, in addition to the development of minimally invasive surgical techniques and using radiotherapy as an adjuvant therapy, surgery for spine metastatic disease has been gaining more consideration over the past decade as a treatment option for spine metastasis patients.[3] The aim is to improve the patient’s quality of life and increase survival. In this high-risk setting, it is crucial to determine the appropriate candidates for surgery to get optimal results and decrease adverse surgical events.

Numerous predictive models were developed for postoperative complications following spine surgeries.[456] However, the clinical status of spinal metastatic cancer patients and the heterogeneity of their risk factors reduce the applicability of these tools to this patient population. Few single-center studies investigated relevant risk factors of adverse postsurgical outcomes in spine surgery for metastatic disease. The modified Bauer score, nutritional score, ambulatory status, and preoperative hypoalbuminemia were found to be associated with an increased risk of mortality.[789]

Although few risk factors were addressed in the literature, no study has taken a multicentric integrative approach to determine the association between multiple preoperative parameters, surgical approach, and adverse outcomes in spine surgery for metastatic disease. The assessment of preoperative risk factors in this patient’s subset can determine a personalized risk–benefit ratio of undergoing spine surgery and subsequently provide healthcare practitioners with a guide for risk-reduction strategies. Our study aims to identify perioperative risk factors associated with poor outcomes following spine surgery in the setting of metastatic spine disease.

METHODS

National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database

The American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS-NSQIP) database is a prospectively collected data that collects 200 variables on patients’ characteristics, comorbidities, surgical details, and 30-day postoperative outcomes in adult patients from multiple institutions.[10] We performed a retrospective cohort study that included patients with metastatic spine disease who had undergone spine surgery between 2005 and 2018. Patients with secondary metastasis to the spinal cord were identified using the International Classification of Disease 9 and 10 codes. We used the Current Procedural Terminology codes to identify patients who underwent spinal procedures.

Outcomes and covariates

Our primary outcome was morbidity and mortality. Our secondary outcome was the length of stay. The composite morbidity and mortality outcome was defined as experiencing death, any thrombotic event, sepsis or septic shock, or surgical site infection within 30 days postsurgery. A prolonged hospital stay was defined as nine or more days from operation to discharge. This cutoff was selected as it represents the 75th percentile of the interquartile range (IQR) for our cohort. Our predictors were categorized into five main groups: (1) Patient General Characteristics, including gender, race, age, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status; (2) Comorbidities, such as diabetes, history of severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension requiring medication, and bleeding disorders; (3) Malignancy-Related Variables, which included >10% body weight loss, preoperative white blood cell (WBC) count, preoperative albumin level, preoperative hematocrit level, preoperative radiotherapy within 90 days, and preoperative chemotherapy within 30 days; (4) Neurological Status Variables, encompassing functional health status before surgery (dependent vs. independent) and preoperative neurological deficits; and (5) Surgical-Related Variables, including surgical complexity and surgical urgency. Preoperative neurological deficit was defined as the presence of one of the following conditions: hemiplegia, paraplegia, quadriplegia, or cerebrovascular accident with neuro-deficit. Surgery was classified as “simple” if it did not involve corpectomy, anterior approach, or fusion; as “complex” if it involved one of these procedures; and as “more complex” if it involved two or more of these procedures. Preoperative WBC counts were categorized as normal (WBC ≥4 × 109/L) or leukopenia (WBC <4 × 109/L). Preoperative albumin levels were classified as normal (≥3.6 g/dL), mild hypoalbuminemia (2.5–3.6 g/dL), and severe hypoalbuminemia (≤2.5 g/dL). In addition, preoperative hematocrit levels were grouped as either ≤32% or >32%.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages. To examine the relationships between variables and outcomes, continuous variables were analyzed using independent t-tests, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression was conducted to adjust for confounding predictors and to calculate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). P <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The performance of the logistic regression models was evaluated through both discrimination and calibration analyses. Discrimination was assessed by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Furthermore, the goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression models was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, which compares observed and expected outcomes across various predicted probability groups.[11] Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

DISCUSSION

Predicting spine surgery outcomes is an active area in spine research.[1314151617] Our study evaluated the risk factors for mortality and morbidity and prolonged hospital stay for patients with metastatic spine disease undergoing spine surgery. With the continuous improvement of primary cancer treatment and the increase in cancer patients’ survival, the number of patients with spinal metastasis is increasing.[18] This trend has shifted the treatment options in these populations more toward surgical management. A randomized controlled trial conducted by Patchell et al. have shown an added benefit from decompression surgery compared to radiotherapy alone in the treatment of patients with metastasis to the spine.[19] Surgery is usually indicated in patients with either significant metastatic spinal epidural compression or spinal instability; yet, not all patients with spinal metastases are ideal candidates for surgical intervention.[20] Predictive models can help identify those who will benefit the most, ensuring that resources are used effectively, and patients are not exposed to unnecessary risks. Furthermore, identifying risk factors, specifically modifiable ones, might give us insight into better preoperative care and optimization strategies that can improve surgical outcomes.

Our findings revealed several significant risk factors associated with mortality and morbidity in this patient population. Consistent with previous studies, male gender was found to increase mortality and morbidity risk. In a study done by Bekelis et al. to predict complications after spine surgery, male gender was associated with an increased risk of death and myocardial infarction.[4] Our study showed that smoking was also associated with increased mortality and morbidity risk. This aligns with other studies that investigated the negative impact of smoking on spine surgery outcomes[212223] and emphasizes the importance of preoperative smoking cessation interventions to mitigate this risk. On the other hand, diabetes, COPD, and hypertension were also found to increase the risk of postoperative mortality and morbidity. These findings are consistent with other studies that aimed to identify the complications and risk factors in patients undergoing spine surgery.[46] Many studies have linked diabetes to an increased risk of postoperative complications, especially surgical site infection.[2425] In a study conducted by Tao et al., it has been shown that a preoperative HbA1C of more than 8 is associated with an increased risk of all-cause complications and worse patient-reported outcome measures in spine surgery.[26] These results emphasize on the importance of preoperative optimization and control of patient’s comorbidities for better surgical outcomes.

Another noteworthy finding was the association between bleeding disorders and mortality and morbidity. Patients with preexisting bleeding disorders may be at higher risk of surgical complications and adverse outcomes due to impaired hemostasis. Adequate preoperative evaluation and optimization of hemostatic parameters are crucial in this patient population. Our results are consistent with other studies’ findings that showed a significant association between coagulopathy and risk of death, stroke, and return to the operation room in patients undergoing spine surgery.[4] Another study conducted by Puvanesarajah et al. showed that coagulopathy was associated with an increased risk of infection and transfusion rates in patients undergoing lumbar fusion.[27] This association between bleeding disorder and complications should be highlighted in our patient population, where bleeding disorders are more prevalent as a result of the malignancy on homeostatic mechanism or due to the bone marrow suppression caused by disease treatment.[2829]

Interestingly, our study showed a significant association between albumin levels and mortality, morbidity, and prolonged hospital stay in our patient population, with a size effect relationship as more severe hypoalbuminemia was associated with a higher risk of adverse events. Literature has shown that hypoalbuminemia is an independent predictor of metastatic cancer patients’ survival in general[303132] and in spinal metastasis in particular.[833] For instance, a prior study demonstrates that in cardiac surgery, low levels of albumin were associated with higher mortality and a prolonged hospital stay.[34] Hypoalbuminemia was also associated with an increased risk of infection, readmission, and wound dehiscence in elective cervical and lumbar spine surgery.[35363738] Many scoring and prediction models were created to predict mortality and morbidity in metastatic disease patients.[39404142] Most of them did not investigate the predictive ability of albumin level. A scoring system by Ghori et al.[8] added the albumin level as a predictor to the modified Bauer score, and subsequent validation of this score showed a better performance than the Bauer score alone, underlining the predictive value of albumin level.[33] Although albumin level has been linked to the patient’s nutritional status,[434445] it has been shown that albumin is also affected by a myriad of conditions. Relevant to our patient population, low albumin levels have been found to be associated with the extent of tumor size and overall tumor burden.[4647] The exact mechanism by which a tumor can affect albumin levels is not clear yet, but it was hypothesized that it can be due to the induced inflammatory state.[4448]

Surgical complexity was another significant factor associated with prolonged hospital stays. More complex surgical procedures may entail longer operative times, increased blood loss, and greater postoperative care requirements. Careful preoperative assessment and planning should be considered for patients undergoing high-complexity spine oncologic surgeries. Basques et al. in their study for predicting postoperative morbidity and readmission in lumbar spine surgery patients found that more complex surgeries and surgeries involving more levels are associated with a higher rate of readmission and adverse events.[6] Another study done by Gelfand et al. compared anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF) and posterior cervical laminectomy and fusion (PCLF) in cervical metastatic disease and found that ACCF was associated with a greater risk of overall complications compared to PCLF.[49]

Limitation

The use of NSQIP data introduces potential inherent limitations, including the risk of selection bias, missing data, and variations in data quality across different institutions. In addition, being a retrospective study, information bias, confounding variables, and potential inaccuracies in data recording may be present. While many identified risk factors are common among cancer patients, unmeasured confounder, such as primary malignancy type and specific tumor characteristics, were not captured in the NSQIP database, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Finally, further validation of our prediction model in specific cancer populations is necessary to ensure its applicability in clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

Our study provides valuable insights into the risk factors associated with mortality, morbidity, and prolonged hospital stay in spine surgery patients with spine metastasis. These identified factors can be instrumental in assisting clinicians with risk stratification, preoperative optimization, and postoperative care planning, ultimately aiming to enhance patient outcomes.
