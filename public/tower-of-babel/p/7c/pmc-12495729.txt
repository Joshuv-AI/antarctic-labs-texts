Significance of the use of a laryngotracheal flap to reconstruct defects and manage pharyngoesophageal stenosis after the resection of hypopharyngeal carcinoma with cervical oesophagus involvement: a retrospective study
Authors: Qian Qiyong, Wang Qinying, Zhou Shuihong, Zhou Minli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe main objective of this study was to investigate cervical oesophageal reconstruction by a laryngotracheal flap (LTF) and the management of postoperative pharyngoesophageal stenosis (PES) after the resection of hypopharyngeal carcinoma with cervical oesophageal involvement.MethodsThe retrospective cohort study of fourteen patients with hypopharyngeal carcinoma exhibiting cervical oesophageal involvement were included in the study from January 2012 to January 2021. Introduction

Squamous cell carcinoma of the hypopharynx (HPSCC) usually presents at an advanced stage and generally has a poor prognosis. The reasons for this poor prognosis include late presentations, submucosal spreading and early lymphatic/distant metastasis [1]. Surgery with postoperative radiation or chemoradiation therapy has been the standard treatment for HPSCC, although an organ preservation protocol with chemoradiation therapy has become more popular in recent years [2, 3]. According to a review of the literature, only 15–34% of HPSCC patients are suitable for conservation surgery, which is able to preserve laryngeal function. Most patients require radical surgery, which includes total laryngectomy, partial or total pharyngectomy and/or oesophagectomy. Radical surgery may result in large surgical defects in the hypopharyngeal and cervical oesophageal regions. Therefore, the reconstruction of surgical defects is important for restoring the continuity of the gastrointestinal tract and allowing for oral alimentation. Many methods exist for addressing hypopharyngeal defects, including the use of a pectoralis major flap, clavicular epithelial flap, free flap repair (including the use of a forearm free flap), and gastric pull-up, among others; these methods can be used to reconstruct the cervical esophagus [4–7]. However, the larynx and oesophagus can be considered when attempting to resolve hypopharyngeal defects when the laryngeal cavity stent and mucosa are not invaded by the tumour, as well as when the patient requires total laryngectomy. In this study, we investigated cervical oesophageal reconstruction via a laryngotracheal flap (LTF) and the management of postoperative pharyngoesophageal stenosis (PES) after the resection of hypopharyngeal carcinoma with cervical oesophageal involvement. This methodology can address hypopharyngeal defects in patients with hypopharyngeal cancer involving the cervical oesophagus combined with laryngeal invasion.

Materials and methods

A single-center, retrospective cohort study was performed. The participants in this report signed informed consent; moreover, they voluntarily participated in the study and had the right to withdraw at any stage. The study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the School of Medicine at Zhe Jiang University (Zhejiang, China) (Approval No. 157). All of the procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical samples and data collection

The inclusion criteria for the patients were as follows: patients with advanced hypopharyngeal cancer involving the cervical oesophagus with an invasion length of no more than 3 cm and with laryngeal function that could not be preserved; elderly or frail patients with compromised cardiopulmonary function or those who were unable to tolerate prolonged surgical procedures exceeding 3 h; or patients at high risk of aspiration following the preservation of laryngeal function.

The exclusion criteria included patients with advanced hypopharyngeal cancer involving the cervical oesophagus with an invasion length exceeding 3 cm or those for whom laryngeal function could be preserved using alternative methods.

The clinical data of 14 patients with postoperative defects for whom laryngotracheal flaps were used to reconstruct the cervical oesophagus from January 2012 to January 2021, as well as the management strategies for two patients with complicated laryngopharyngeal stenosis, were analysed. After resection of the laryngeal, pharyngeal and oesophageal tumours and bilateral neck dissection, direct anastomosis of the larynx and trachea with the oesophagus of the cervicothoracic segment was performed when the circular structure of the larynx and trachea could be preserved. Postoperative radiochemotherapy was utilised, and the follow-up occurred for 3–5 years.

Surgery

The trachea was incised at the fourth tracheal ring. After the resection of the piriform fossa and the affected side, the retained cartilage stent of the healthy side was removed as much as possible, and a flexible broad laryngeal mucosal flap or laryngeal tracheal mucosal flap was produced, which could be directly repaired with the residual hypopharyngeal and oesophageal mucosa. One side of the laryngeal structure was preserved, whereas the cricoid cartilage was excised, and the posterior portion was sutured to the prevertebral fascia. The tracheal stump was subsequently anastomosed with the oesophagus to reconstruct the degree of oesophageal continuity. A permanent tracheostomy was performed at the upper end of the trachea (Figs. 1, 2). One or both superior laryngeal arteries were retained. Two patients with pharyngoesophageal stenoses causing feeding difficulties received nasogastric tube nasal feeding and urinary catheter dilation (with water cystic fluid being used to expand the stenosis 3 times/day, 5 min/wash) after the scar tissue was removed via coblator plasma surgery at the stenosis. Moreover, these patients received nasogastric tube feeding for 1–3 months.

Statistical analysis

Overall survival was calculated by the Kaplan–Meier method based on the time interval between the last day of treatment and the date of death or the most recent follow-up.

Results

The clinical data of 14 patients with postoperative defects resulting from the use of laryngotracheal flaps to reconstruct the cervical oesophagus from 2012–2021, as well as the management strategies for two patients with complicated laryngopharyngeal stenosis, were analysed (Table 1). All 14 patients were male and aged 51–80 years, with a median age of 69.5 years. Ten of the 14 patients had a history of smoking, and 9 patients had a history of alcohol consumption. The main manifestations included sore throat, a pharyngeal foreign body sensation or a neck mass with hoarseness. Fourteen cases of hypopharyngeal carcinoma with cervical oesophageal involvement were detected. The unilateral piriform sinus and cervical oesophagus were involved in 9 patients (9/14), whereas the bilateral piriform sinus, posterior pharyngeal wall and cervical oesophagus were involved in 5 patients. After resection of the laryngeal, pharyngeal and oesophageal tumours and bilateral neck dissection, direct anastomosis of the larynx and trachea with the oesophagus of the cervicothoracic segment was performed when the circular structure of the larynx and trachea could be preserved. Postoperative radiochemotherapy was utilised, and the follow-up occurred for 3–5 years.

All of the patients exhibited healing without events, except for one patient who experienced a pharyngocutaneous fistula, which also healed after approximately 2 weeks of dressing changes. All of the patients demonstrated a good swallowing reflex (based on a video of X-ray inspection and oesophageal barium meal radiography); moreover, they were fed a normal diet free of feeding tubes and demonstrated no gastric reflux. The median radiation dose for postoperative radiotherapy was 60 Gy. Postoperative chemotherapy (involving cisplatin (80 mg/m2, day 1) and 5-fluorouracil (800 mg/m2) administered over 24 h on days 1–5) was utilised, and the follow-up occurred for 3–5 years.

Survival

The median duration of follow-up was 49 months (range = 36–60 months). Eight patients died during follow-up. Moreover, three patients had metastases in the neck, 1 patient had metastases in the lung, and 1 patient discontinued further treatment because of thoracic oesophageal carcinoma with hepatic metastasis. The 5-year overall survival was 42.9% (Fig. 3). At 14 months after surgery, two patients with pharyngoesophageal stenosis (Fig. 4) causing feeding difficulties received nasogastric tube nasal feeding and urinary catheter dilation (Fig. 5) after the scar tissue was removed by coblator plasma surgery at the stenosis; moreover, they received nasogastric tube feeding for 1–3 months (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Many reconstructive methods, such as the use of a pectoralis major flap, a clavicular epithelial flap, free flap repair (e.g., a forearm free flap), and gastric pull-up, have been used in a variety of different situations when performing reconstruction of the hypopharynx to reconstruct the cervical oesophagus [4–7]. All of these methods have various advantages and limitations.

Laryngotracheal flaps (LTFs) have been previously used to prevent defects of the cervical oesophagus in 1991[8]. Chu et al. reported that more than 80% of defects could be reconstructed via primary closure [9], and the 5-year survival rates demonstrated no significant differences compared to other methods after LTF was used [10]. Majeed FA et al. reported that total laryngopharyngeal oesophagectomy (TLPO) and gastric pull-up with stomach reconstruction (for which the operative time was less than 3 h in 17 patients by using the two-team technique) occurred between 3–4 h in 8 patients and for more than 4 h in 3 patients. Moreover, atrial fibrillation occurred in 5.71% (n = 2) of the patients. Furthermore, chest complications occurred in patients who had anastomotic leakage, including pneumothorax in 11.43% of patients (n = 4), basal atelectasis in 22.86% of patients (n = 8), pulmonary embolism in 2.85% of patients (n = 1), aspiration in 8.57% of patients (n = 3) and tracheal stenosis in 5.71% of patients (n = 2). Moreover, the postoperative 28-day mortality was 8.57% (n = 3) [11]. In this study, we used a laryngotracheal flap to reconstruct the cervical oesophagus after the resection of hypopharyngeal carcinoma with cervical oesophageal involvement, which is a recommended method that is simple and convenient to implement; moreover, it exhibits a high success rate and can be effectively used for the repair of postoperative defects in patients with hypopharyngeal cancer.

Pharyngocutaneous fistula and pharyngoesophageal stenosis represent the main postoperative complications of this procedure. Chu et al. reported that postoperative complications are rarely observed, with only 2% of patients experiencing pharyngoesophageal stenosis and 5% exhibiting pharyngocutaneous fistula [10]. The oncological results are also satisfactory. In our case series, 14 patients underwent reconstruction with primary closure, and all of the patients demonstrated good swallowing reflexes (based on a video of X-ray inspection and oesophageal barium meal radiography); moreover, the patients were fed a normal diet without the need for feeding tubes and exhibited no gastric reflux. With a follow-up of 3–5 years, the median duration of follow-up was 49 months (range = 36–60 months). Eight patients died during follow-up. Furthermore, three patients had metastases in the neck, 1 patient had metastases in the lung, and 1 patient discontinued further treatment because of thoracic oesophageal carcinoma with hepatic metastasis. In this study, among the 14 included patients, one patient developed pharyngocutaneous fistula (7%), and two patients developed pharyngoesophageal stenosis (14%). The incidence of postoperative complications in our study was significantly greater compared to that reported by other authors, which may be attributable to the relatively limited number of patients who were included in our study. Moreover, the key factor for decreasing complications is to preserve adequate blood supply to the LTF; thus, at least one superior laryngeal artery should be retained during the operation.

Pharyngoesophageal stenosis (PES) is among the most common complications following cervical oesophageal reconstruction by a laryngotracheal flap; additionally, it exhibits adverse effects on postoperative rehabilitation, adjuvant therapy, and overall survival. The postoperative management of pharyngoesophageal stenosis is one of the most challenging and controversial problems being experienced by head and neck surgeons. A number of different PES repair techniques have been described [12–14]. In this study, two patients with pharyngoesophageal stenosis causing feeding difficulties received nasogastric tube nasal feeding and urinary catheter expansion after the scar tissue was removed via coblator plasma surgery at the stenosis and were fed via nasogastric tube for 1–3 months. From a functional perspective, two patients demonstrated good swallowing reflexes and were fed a normal diet without the need for feeding tubes; moreover, these patients exhibited no gastric reflux. The performance of the urinary catheter dilation procedure is simple, and patient tolerance has been observed to be good; furthermore, the catheter is easy to fix and does not easily fall off. By a reasonable surgical design and postoperative management, the success rate of laryngotracheal flap repair can be further improved, and the occurrence of postoperative stenosis and other complications can be reduced, thereby increasing the quality of life and survival rate of patients.

This was a single-center retrospective analysis with a small sample size, which might have introduced bias in case selection. Therefore, it is essential to conduct large prospective studies in the future to validate the findings of this study.

Conclusion

As an effective repair method, the laryngotracheal flap has broad application prospects in the repair of the cervical oesophagus after the resection of hypopharyngeal carcinoma with cervical oesophageal involvement. Coblator plasma surgery and catheter dilation may effectively address the problem of eating difficulties in patients with postoperative pharyngoesophageal stenosis. However, given the limited number of evaluated cases, additional clinical validation is warranted.
