The application of fractional calculus in economic growth modelling: An approach based on regression analysis
Authors: Yi Herng Cheow, Kok Haur Ng, Chang Phang, Kooi Huat Ng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper establishes a fractional-order economic growth model to model the gross domestic product (GDP). The fractional-order model consists of a differential equation of integer and fractional orders, where the GDP is a function of several exploratory variables. An empirical application is adopted using Malaysia's GDP data from 1956 to 2018, incorporating exploratory variables such as total population, crude death rate, production of logs, gross fixed capital formation, exports of goods and services, general government final consumption expenditure, private final consumption expenditure, and the impact of investment. Extensive comparisons were carried out to evaluate the modelling performance of the full and reduced fractional-order multiple linear regression models with the benchmark models, namely full and reduced integer-order multiple linear regression models. Results indicate that the reduced fractional-order model with six exploratory variables, excluding the crude death rate and production of logs, predominates other models for the in-sample model fitting based on the Akaike information criterion, coefficient of determination and other criteria. Furthermore, the fractional-order model offers the best-of-sample forecasts evaluated based on the root mean square forecast error and mean absolute forecast error. The application of the Diebold–Mariano test also serves to confirm the superior performance of the suggested fractional-order model, revealing a significant difference in forecasting ability between the fractional-order and integer-order models.

Introduction

Macroeconomics studies a business cycle where economic activities and prices are observed and measured over time [1]. The definition can be further simplified as the economy acts as a whole system. At the same time, the demand and supply of products, services and resources are the significant factors that will affect the system. In macroeconomic analysis, there are a few issues that concerned by the economist, which are the living standards and determinants of economic growth in a country, labour productivity, causes of decline and growth of an economy, unemployment factors and causes of inflation rate [2].

Gross domestic product (GDP) is defined as an aggregate measure of production equal to the sum of gross values added by all resident institutional units engaged in production [3]. It serves as a fundamental metric to track the performance of a nation's economy and is a crucial measure for economists and investors as it includes all private and public consumptions, government expenditure, investment, changes to private inventories, paid-in construction costs and foreign balance of trade. Aside from tracking the nation's present state of the economy, GDP enables policy-makers to judge and diagnose the problem in the economy and facilitate the formulation of appropriate strategies for addressing them [4].

The calculation of GDP can be a complicated task, so a good model can effectively provide an accurate forecast. In the classical economic growth model (EGM), derivatives and integrals are often used to show the characteristic of economic development [5], [6]. Several modelling approaches can be used to model GDP, and the regression approach is the most common and widely applied approach to estimate the parameters of the classical EGM. Referring to [7] and [8], the authors used classical EGMs based on regression approach, which consisted of exploratory variables that described the GDP of their respective targeted countries. However, it should be noted that the classical EGM may lead to a variety of problems while modelling real data. For instance, for an integer-order EGM that undergoes regression with the ordinary least squares (OLS) method, when there are many exploratory variables, it will generate significant effects and lead to the prediction of the dependent variable near the boundaries. This situation could violate the assumption of OLS regression and affect the prediction accuracy, particularly if the study aims to include the endpoints as part of the analysis of interest.

Due to the shortcomings of the classical EGM in modelling real data, fractional calculus has emerged as an alternative to overcome the situation. The origins of fractional calculus can be traced back to a correspondence between Guillaume Francois Antoine Marquis de L'Hôpital, a French mathematician, and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, a German mathematician, in 1695. L'Hôpital questioned the possibility of the order of derivative being a real number instead of an integer in 1695 [9], [10], [11]. In the early stage, fractional calculus was primarily explored from a theoretical perspective. Since the 1960s, it has gained significant attention in science and engineering fields and is widely applied to various research areas. Several definitions of the fractional-order derivative have been proposed, including Grünwald-Letnikov, Riemann-Liouville, Weyl, Riesz and the Caputo representation. As for the economy field, the application of fractional calculus in economic models has only recently gained significant research interest.

Fractional calculus is good at modelling real data and is applied in many studies to investigate new phenomena and areas [12], [13], [14]. It extends the notions of integer-order differentiation and integration to the realm of functional-order [15], [16]. An EGM with fractional-order derivative properties will improve the regression's prediction values as these values will fall in a continuous bounded range, which includes the endpoints. Also, a general model can be established with fractional generalisation, and then by adjusting the fractional orders, researchers are able to gain different insights using the same model, and this will help to save resources [17].

A recent noteworthy study conducted by Tejado et al. [18] focused on building a fractional-order regression model with Caputo derivative on GDP growth of the European Union countries and compared the fractional-order regression model performance with the standard linear regression model. Additionally, Tejado et al. [19] used the same models from the European Union study in a separate analysis, comparing the fractional-order regression model with the linear regression model in the context of GDP performance across a group of seven countries. In both studies, the exploratory variables involved are the standard economic indicators such as population, arable land, gross fixed capital formation (GFCF), money and quasi money (M2), etc. The findings of their studies showed that fractional properties in the fractional-order regression model may help to improve the accuracy of the prediction of GDP data compared to the classical linear regression models.

On top of that, Tejado et al. [20] carried out a study using fraction calculus to build an EGM that compared the shared telecommunication revenue in the GDP percentage of five countries with polynomial EGM. The fractional calculus EGM is built with the Caputo derivative, while the polynomial EGM's estimated parameters use the least square method. The result showed that the fractional-order regression model performed better than the polynomial regression model. Moreover, [21] focused on the Romanian case, examining the impact of final consumption and gross investment on GDP using the Caputo fractional derivative. They evaluated both fractional-order and classical regression models with a few performance indices, which are coefficient of determination (R2), Akaike information criterion (AIC), mean square error (MSE), and mean absolute deviation (MAD). Based on the performance indices, the fractional-order model can fit well with the GDP data in contrast to the classical regression model.

Malaysia, a multiracial nation, has achieved significant development since gaining independence in 1957. It has been considered as one of the fastest-growing countries, transforming rapidly from a major agriculture sector to a country that focuses more on the production sector, especially the industrial and manufacturing sectors [22]. Also, Malaysia is one of the open economy countries in the world, with Malaysia's trading ratio maintaining an average of over 130% since 2010 after successfully overcoming the Asian financial crisis from 1997 to 1998. Economists anticipate that over the longer term, as Malaysia converges with high-income economies, the incremental growth for the country will be less dependent on the factor's accumulation. In the meantime, it will increase productivity to sustain higher potential growth. Moreover, according to the World Bank's Human Capital Index, Malaysia ranked 55th among 157 countries. Therefore, Malaysia needs to fully utilise its potential human resources to assist Malaysia in achieving its goal as a high-income and developed country. To accomplish this, Malaysia must put more effort into the country's education, health and nutrition segments to achieve the goal [23].

This study aims to establish a fractional-order EGM for predicting gross domestic product (GDP). The model integrates differential equations of both integer and fractional orders, considering GDP as a function of various exploratory variables. The empirical application focuses on Malaysia's GDP from 1956 to 2018, utilising exploratory variables such as total population, crude death rate, production of logs, gross fixed capital formation, exports of goods and services, general government final consumption expenditure, private final consumption expenditure, and the impact of investment. In spite of some other models of economic growth that are extended from the fractional calculus approach, such as those in [6], [24], this paper offers different ways of the investigation. First, to evaluate the performance of fractional order in modelling, the fractional-order EGMs are compared with benchmark models, namely full and reduced integer-order multiple regression models. The in-sample model fitting performance is assessed based on several criteria such as R2, AIC, root MSE (RMSE) and MAD. Furthermore, the GDP forecasting performance of the fractional-order EGMs is contrasted with other models using several loss functions and tested via the Diebold and Mariano test [25].

The rationale for this study lies in the need to develop a robust model for GDP prediction, considering the complexities of economic dynamics. By incorporating fractional-order modelling, which includes both integer and fractional orders in the differential equation, the study provides a more accurate representation of the relationships between GDP and various influencing factors. The specific exploratory variables chosen for the empirical application reflect key aspects of Malaysia's economic landscape, capturing demographic, economic, and consumption-related factors. The comprehensive comparisons conducted, particularly between full and reduced fractional-order multiple linear regression models and benchmark integer-order models, aim to assess the modelling performance comprehensively.

The remainder of the paper is as follows. Section 2 describes Malaysia's GDP data and eight exploratory variables employed in the analysis. Section 3 discusses the Caputo fractional calculus, integer-order and fractional-order multiple regression models, and performance measures. Then, empirical results are provided in Section 4. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper.

The data spans from 1956 to 2018 with a total of T=63 points. Methodology

Fractional calculus

In the study of fractional calculus, several types of fractional derivatives are frequently used. In this paper, the Caputo fractional derivative is chosen because it allows the inclusion of traditional initial conditions in the problem formulation. This is advantageous because, according to Caputo's definition, it does not require fractional-order initial conditions.

Definition 1: The Caputo's fractional derivative is given by [15], [16], [27], [28](2)DtαaCf(t)={1Γ(n−α)∫atfn(τ)(t−τ)α+1−ndτ,n−1<α<n,dndtnf(t),n=α, where C is meant for the Caputo fractional derivative, α is fractional-order, Dtα is defined as the derivative taken with respect to t in the order of α, fn(τ) is the nth derivation of f(t) with respect to its independent variable τ and Γ(⋅) is a gamma function [20].

Besides the Caputo fractional derivative, the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral is also frequently used [29], [30].

Results and discussions

In-sample model fits

Eight carefully selected exploratory variables were utilised to capture the intricate behaviour of Malaysia's GDP from 1956 to 2018.

To sum up our findings, the Caputo RFMRM, featuring six significant variables (total population, GFCF, EGS, GGFCE, PFCE and impact of investment), demonstrated superior performance compared to other models, encompassing both integer-order and full fractional-order models. Evaluation metrics, including AIC, R2, RMSE, and MAD, consistently favoured the Caputo RFMRM.

In terms of forecasting ability, the study employed loss functions, specifically RMSFE and MAFE, to assess the Caputo RFMRM. Utilising the one-step-ahead rolling window technique for GDP forecasts from 2006 to 2018, the results indicated that the Caputo RFMRM, with its six exploratory variables, outperformed alternative models by yielding the smallest RMSFE and MAFE. The precision of forecasts produced by the Caputo RFMRM with six significant variables was further substantiated through the DM test.

In comparison with existing literature, the study aligns with a growing body of research emphasising the efficacy of fractional calculus in economic modelling. By integrating specific exploratory variables, the Caputo RFMRM surpassed traditional models in capturing the intricacies of Malaysia's GDP dynamics. The findings suggest that employing the Caputo fractional derivative in multiple regression models provides advantages in accurately modelling Malaysia's GDP growth and enhancing predictive capabilities.

Despite the encouraging outcomes, it is essential to acknowledge certain limitations. Factors such as the choice of different fractional derivatives, variations in macroeconomic variables, and the model's adaptability to unforeseen events, such as the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and economic crises, may influence its performance.

Conclusion

This paper investigated the modelling of GDP using the economic growth model from a fractional calculus point of view. Eight exploratory variables were chosen as proposed from the literature review and used to account for the behaviour of this financial process. The analysis covers the period from 1956 to 2018, specifically to examine Malaysia's GDP. The model fitting results showed that the Caputo RFMRM with six significant variables, namely the total population of Malaysia, GFCF, EGS, GGFCE, PFCE and impact of investment, performed better than other integer order and full fractional-order models evaluated using AIC, R2, RMSE and MAD. To further investigate the forecasting ability of the Caputo RFMRM, the forecasting performance of these models was evaluated via loss functions, namely RMSFE and MAFE and tested using the DM. The one-step-ahead rolling window technique was used to forecast GDP from 2006 to 2018. Results seem to warrant the conclusion that the Caputo RFMRM provides the smallest RMSFE and MAFE, and the DM also confirms the predictive ability of the Caputo RFMRM with six exploratory variables in producing precise forecasts. In summary, employing the Caputo fractional derivative on the multiple regression models exhibits advantages in fitting Malaysia's GDP growth and enhancing its predictive capabilities. Despite the encouraging results of this study, different fractional derivatives components, the macroeconomic variables, and its ability to capture unforeseen events might also affect the model's performance. Hence, as part of future work, we intend to expand the model by incorporating various fractional derivatives. Moreover, we find that it might be beneficial to introduce a jumps component into the model to realistically capture sudden changes, particularly those induced by events such as the COVID-19 pandemic and economic crises.
