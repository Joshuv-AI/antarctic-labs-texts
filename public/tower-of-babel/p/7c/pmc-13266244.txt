Misinformation on cardiovascular disease spreads through social networks: a scoping review
Authors: Buna Bhandari, Jessica Hanae Zafra-Tanaka, Pranab Mahapatra, Marina Njelekela, Sudha Ramalingam, Iuliia Pavlovska, Juan Pablo Gonzalez-Rivas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular health remains susceptible to inaccurate information, which can harm health outcomes. This scoping review addresses a gap in comprehensive analyses by synthesising evidence on the prevalence, spread, impact and correction strategies related to cardiovascular health misinformation across diverse populations and settings.MethodsFollowing Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines, we searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, Global Index Medicus, Web of Science. A dual-reviewer process, using Covidence, screened articles in two phases (title/abstract, full-text) based on predefined inclusion criteria. Data extraction was performed by two reviewers, and results were synthesised narratively, presented by tables and figures.ResultsFrom 6348 screened articles, 22 were included. The review reveals widespread misinformation concerning smoking, nutrition, blood pressure, weight management, alcohol consumption and diabetes care. Specific examples include misleading claims about e-cigarette safety and the downplaying of alcohol’s harms. A substantial proportion of social media posts falsely discourage salt reduction (reaching 1.5 million followers) and a significant number of diet/exercise vlogs (36.7%) and YouTube videos on myocardial infarction (one-third) contain inaccuracies, often originating from unreliable sources. Instagram, Facebook, YouTube and X were identified as key platforms for dissemination.ConclusionThis review demonstrates that cardiovascular health misinformation is widespread and shaped by both platform dynamics and user-level factors. Addressing this challenge requires coordinated strategies that strengthen digital literacy, enhance visibility of evidence-based content and reduce the reach of misinformation. Collaborative efforts between public health agencies, clinical institutions and technology platforms are essential to ensure that trustworthy cardiovascular disease-related information reaches the public.

Introduction

The widespread dissemination of misinformation online presents a significant barrier to patients adopting evidence-based health practices.1 2 Health misinformation is defined as ‘a health-related claim of fact that is currently false due to its contradiction to expert consensus and/or best available evidence at the time, inaccurate due to its use of incomplete evidence, or unsubstantiated due to lack of evidence’3 and can be spread rapidly via social media.4 Indeed, research suggests that social media use increases the likelihood of individuals being susceptible to health misinformation.5 This effect is likely amplified by the increasing diversity of social media users, the platforms’ pervasive reach, reliance on social media for information and the ability of algorithms to personalise content delivery.4

A recent systematic review of 69 studies examined the main health misinformation themes and their prevalence on diverse social media. This review found the highest prevalence of misinformation related to smoking products, opioids, marijuana, vaccines, nutrition and non-communicable diseases.6 Within the non-communicable diseases category, studies evaluating YouTube videos on type 2 diabetes (T2D) and hypertension revealed that only 40.2% and 63% of videos, respectively, contained information classified as ‘useful’.7 8 Worryingly, ‘useful’ videos often received fewer views and ‘likes’ compared with misinformation videos.7 Similar trends were observed in South Asian YouTube videos related to T2D.8

Misinformation surrounding cardiovascular health has the potential to negatively impact public health outcomes. While online misinformation across various health domains is increasingly recognised, the specific extent, nature and impact of misinformation related to cardiovascular health remains poorly understood. To address this gap, this study aims to describe and synthesise existing evidence on misinformation related to cardiovascular health through a scoping review. Scoping reviews are particularly suited for emerging and heterogeneous fields where concepts, definitions and methodologies vary widely.9 This approach will identify research gaps and inform future strategies to mitigate the detrimental effects of misinformation in this critical area.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted following the guidelines set forth by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews,10 which consists of five stages: (1) developing research questions, (2) identification of relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) charting the data and (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results. The study reporting followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist.11 The methodology is outlined in detail below:

Stage 1: developing research questions

The research questions guiding this scoping review, which address the extent, sources, methodologies, impacts and potential interventions related to cardiovascular health misinformation, were as follows and detailed in our published protocol.12

Stage 2: identification of relevant studies

The identification of relevant studies was guided by the Population-Concept-Context framework, which ensured the systematic inclusion of studies addressing the following: Population: the review included studies that involved individuals of any age, gender or ethnicity who were exposed to or affected by misinformation related to cardiovascular health. Studies were considered if they examined the impact of misinformation on the prevention, management or understanding of cardiovascular disease (CVD) or its risk factors. Concept: this review focused on misinformation about cardiovascular health disseminated primarily via social networks. This concept includes various false or misleading information such as rumours, fake news, conspiracy theories and disinformation. To guide this scoping review, we define misinformation as false or misleading information shared without intent to deceive, often resulting from misunderstanding or lack of verification as per American Psychological Association, 2024 definition.13 In addition, disinformation refers to deliberately false information created and disseminated with the explicit intention to mislead or manipulate audiences.14 Fake news is a fabricated news content that mimics legitimate news formats but lacks factual accuracy, often created for political or financial gain.15

Context

The context of the review is broad, encompassing multiple settings where cardiovascular health misinformation may spread, including online platforms, healthcare environments, public health campaigns and community settings. The review is global in scope, with no geographical restrictions, though studies were limited to those published in English.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Studies focusing on misinformation regarding cardiovascular health were included, encompassing various study designs (experimental, observational, qualitative) and sources (eg, media outlets, social networks and public figures). Editorials, conference abstracts, books/book chapters and protocols were excluded. Systematic reviews were excluded but screened for relevant primary studies.

Search strategy

MEDLINE (PuBmed), EMBASE, Global Index Medicus, Web of Science were searched using a strategy developed through an iterative process until January 2025. Search terms included “misinformation,” “disinformation,” “cardiovascular health,” “fake news,” and “infodemic.” Grey literature was explored using Google and Google Scholar (online supplemental file 1).

Stage 3: study selection

The study selection process involved two stages: title and abstract screening, followed by a full-text review. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts and then full text against the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Reasons for exclusion were documented for the full-text stage. The study selection process was documented and reported following the PRISMA-SCRA guidelines.11

Stage 4: data charting

Data extraction was performed using a structured form developed and tested by two reviewers. Extracted information included general information (authors, year, country, funding), study characteristics (objectives, design, methods, setting), participant characteristics (recruitment, sample size, inclusion/exclusion), misinformation types (rumours, fake news, conspiracy theories), sources of misinformation (media outlets, social networks, public figures), interventions (if applicable), outcome measures (impacts on public health, behaviour and policy) and study limitations. Two independent reviewers extracted data for each study to ensure consistency.

Stage 5: data synthesis

Data analysis and presentation were structured to address the research questions based on JBI Manual for evidence synthesis.10 This method emphasises the use of text, complemented by tables, to describe, summarise and interpret study findings. Key details from included studies were tabulated to facilitate comparison. The narrative synthesis was conducted by organising the extracted information thematically around CVD-related topics/risk factors and prevalent social media and presenting a cohesive narrative summary of the prevalence, types and sources of misinformation as well as the methodological approaches used in the included studies. This approach allowed us to capture and synthesise the extent and nature of cardiovascular health misinformation across the literature.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this scoping review.

General characteristics of selected studies

The characteristics of the 22 included studies are detailed in online supplemental file 2. The distribution across publication years includes one study in 2018,16 four in 2020,1720 six in 2021,2126 five in 2022,2731 four in 20233235 and two in 2024.36 37 Geographically, the USA was the most represented, with 12 studies.1618 20 21 24 25 2729 31 34 35 Turkey follows with three studies,19 30 33 and the UK with two studies.17 23 Brazil,22 Japan,37 Australia,36 Canada32 and Portugal26 each contributed one study. Cross-sectional studies were the most prevalent, comprising 18 of the 22 studies.161921 Additionally, there were two randomised controlled trials (RCTs)20 24 25 with one study with two publications and one non-randomised experimental study.16

Platforms included

Among the platforms evaluated, X (formerly Twitter) was the most frequently explored, appearing in eight studies.17 21 24 25 31 32 34 37 YouTube was evaluated in six,16 20 26 30 33 35 followed by Instagram and Google17 19 22 28 29 36 in three each. Reddit27 and Pinterest18 were each analysed in one study. Additionally, Facebook was included in one study,17 combined with data from Instagram and X. One study assessed information published on websites.23

Topics of misinformation evaluated

The studies systematically explored various misinformation topics, with tobacco use, encompassing e-cigarettes and vaping, as the most frequent area of focus with seven publications.16 20 21 24 25 31 32 Nutrition was the second predominant subject in four articles,223436 followed by hypertension in three.28 33 37 Several risk factors, including alcohol consumption,23 obesity,27 belly fat,18 cholesterol management19 and weight management,17 in addition to cardiometabolic complications like diabetic foot,30 atrial fibrillation29 and myocardial infarction,26 were assessed separately in single publications. One study provided an integrated exploration covering multiple themes: nutrition, health and wellness, medicine and physical fitness.22

Tools of assessment

The DISCERN tool, a standardised questionnaire for evaluating the quality and reliability of written health information,38 was the most frequently used assessment tool (four publications).26 28 30 33 DISCERN assesses publications based on criteria like clarity of aims, reliability of sources, balance and transparency. The Global Quality Scale (GQS) was the second most common tool (two studies).30 33 The GQS uses a 5-point Likert scale to rate the overall quality of online health information, assessing factors such as educational value, usefulness and presentation.39 Other assessment tools included Johns Hopkins University’s (JHU)-Sodium Assessment Learning Tool (JHU-SALT), which evaluates sodium reduction information.28 The Principles for Health-Related Information on Social Media, which assesses the quality of health information on social media, focusing on scientific rigour, objectivity, transparency and inclusivity36; natural language processing (NLP) models that extract features to characterise content27; The Quality and Quality-Ratio scores (QRS) assess video quality based on criteria22 like appropriate daily foot care information25; Likert scales16; Social Cognitive Theory18 to analyse content based on the principle that behaviour is learnt through observation23; and expert assessments.19

Misinformation on social media platforms by topic

Nutrition

Instagram: a study in Australia aimed to evaluate the quality and accuracy of nutrition-related information posted by popular Instagram accounts.36 Accuracy was defined as the factual correctness of information compared with authoritative sources. 44.7% of the 676 posts analysed contained inaccuracies. Posts authored by nutritionists or dietitians had significantly higher quality and accuracy scores compared with those from brands. The study concluded that nutrition-related information from influential Australian Instagram accounts is often inaccurate and suboptimal.

Another study conducted in Brazil aimed to analyse the technical-scientific accuracy of exercise and health information posted by popular Instagram influencers.22 Accuracy was defined using a compounded score using the Quality criteria and QRS. The study evaluated 495 posts across topics, including nutrition, health, wellness, medicine and physical fitness. Only 2.7% of posts cite credible references. The QRS was not correlated with the influencers’ educational qualifications. The study concluded that prominent Brazilian Instagram influencers frequently disseminate low-quality information about exercise and health.

YouTube: a study conducted in the USA aimed to explore the nature of nutrition-related health information shared by English-speaking Latine individuals.35 The accuracy and reliability, defined using the Recommendations based on the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans, of 68 videos were evaluated. Results indicated that 36.7% of the vlogs contained inaccurate information concerning diet and exercise. The study concluded that further research is necessary to understand better how social media influences health behaviour.

X: a study conducted in the USA aimed to characterise the landscape of precision nutrition content. This cross-sectional study reviewed 504 posts.34 Misleading or inaccurate information was determined by two experts on genetics and nutrition. The results indicated that 29% of the tweets were potentially misleading, while 14.9% contained outright falsehoods. The study concluded that tweet content often aligns with indicators of medical hype, underscoring the need for continuous evaluation of the information environment.

Tobacco and E-cigarettes

X: a cross-sectional study aimed to analyse how social media users discuss misinformation related to electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS), particularly vaping products.32 The study reviewed 2653 tweets, finding that 98.9% expressed pro-vaping positions, with a vast majority of audience engagement focused on such content. The research concluded that terms like ‘fact check’, ‘misinformation’ and ‘fake news’ were being co-opted by providing groups to undermine critiques and create confusion about vaping’s health risks.

Another study sought to identify and describe nicotine-related discussions authored by both the public and social bots.21 It analysed 300 360 posts, revealing popular topics such as vaping, smoking, addiction, withdrawal and nicotine health risks, with bots disproportionately driving new product mentions. The study concluded that X users frequently discuss challenges like quitting smoking, nicotine withdrawal and cravings, highlighting the platform’s role in these public health conversations.

A separate study aimed to systematically evaluate ENDS-related tweets for potential misinformation regarding nicotine and addiction.31 This study analysed 904 tweets. Potential misinformation was determined by two researchers using specific criteria to assess characteristics such as selectivity, emotional bias, groupthink and false logic. A 41.7% of tweets contained potential misinformation, marked by exaggerated anti- and pro-vaping narratives. The study highlighted that antivaping exaggeration tweets distorted claims while pro-vaping tweets often misinterpreted scientific studies.

An RCT in the USA investigated the role of emotional and cognitive responses in mediating the effects of exposure to misinformation about e-cigarettes.24 The aim was to determine how discrete emotional responses and perceived relative harm influence smokers’ intentions to purchase e-cigarettes. The study included 2400 participants aged 18–84. Tweets highlighting the harms of e-cigarettes significantly affected users’ perceptions, primarily through emotional and cognitive channels. This intervention emphasised that crafting effective public health messages can substantially alter consumer behaviour, leveraging emotional dynamics to impact purchasing decisions.

Another publication from this intervention aimed to understand the conditions under which users were more likely to interact with such content, particularly regarding engagement metrics like replying, retweeting, liking and sharing.25 The results revealed a higher likelihood of engagement with tweets suggesting that e-cigarettes pose the same or greater harm than smoking regular cigarettes compared with those depicting e-cigarettes as harmless.

YouTube: in the USA, a non-randomised experimental study was conducted to evaluate the impact of YouTube videos about e-cigarettes and hookahs on viewers’ attitudes.16 This research aimed to explore how such content could influence public perception of tobacco products, specifically within a youthful demographic. Utilising a Likert scale to assess viewer responses, the study involved five carefully selected videos. Three hundred fifty participants aged 18–24 were recruited through Amazon Mechanical Turk. The exposure to these videos led to more positive attitudes towards e-cigarettes and hookahs; however, the shift in attitude did not significantly extend to combustive cigarette smoking. The study concluded that while YouTube content has the potential to enhance positivity towards certain tobacco products, this influence is nuanced and limited to specific contexts.

A US-based RCT explored corrective interventions for misleading protobacco videos.20 The study evaluated the effectiveness of obtaining evidence about the potential effects of YouTube postings about tobacco products on viewers’ attitudes towards these products. The study included 716 current and past smokers aged 15–19. Participants who received corrective messages exhibited fewer favourable attitudes towards smoking than those exposed only to the original protobacco content. This trial concluded that implementing corrective interventions could significantly mitigate the risks posed by exposure to misleading information, particularly among adolescents.

Blood pressure

X: a cross-sectional study conducted in Japan aimed to assess the dissemination of misinformation themes regarding sodium reduction and blood pressure.37 The study analysed 531 posts. Fourteen sodium-related misinformation themes were identified, with ‘Natural Salt’ being the most prevalent, accounting for 37.7% of posts and reaching 1.6 million followers. Another significant theme, ‘Reducing salt could be bad for my health’, comprised 28.6% of the posts and reached 1.5 million followers. The study found a statistical correlation between these misinformation themes and advertisements of products offered by the account, suggesting potential commercial interests involved.

YouTube: a study aimed to evaluate the quality of videos related to blood pressure reduction and hypertension treatment.33 This cross-sectional study analysed 104 videos using the GQS and modified DISCERN tool. The results indicated that 51% of the videos were useful, while 49% were misleading. A significant portion of the videos emphasised lifestyle changes (62.5%) rather than pharmacological treatments, which were only present in 39.4% of the content. Videos about alternative treatments received higher views compared with those featuring pharmacological options. The study concluded that YouTube is primarily utilised to disseminate information about lifestyle changes and alternative treatments rather than conventional hypertension treatments, with a notable prevalence of misinformation.

Google: a study conducted in the USA aimed to evaluate the quality of information and guidance available on consumer-facing websites concerning dietary sodium.28 This cross-sectional analysis assessed 69 articles using the DISCERN and JHU-SALT. The results revealed that 74% of the websites provided information on the adverse health effects of a high-sodium diet, and 80% offered lifestyle strategies to reduce blood pressure. However, comprehensive guidance on implementing these strategies was often missing. The study concluded that although consumers can access accurate information online, the guidance is often limited.

Abnormal adiposity

Pinterest: a study conducted in the USA aimed to examine the portrayal of exercises to reduce belly fat.18 This cross-sectional analysis evaluated 234 images and infographics, applying Social Cognitive Theoretical constructs to assess content. The results revealed that most pins (73.1%) emphasised being thinner rather than promoting overall health, with 52.9% depicting women in sports bras and 48.7% showing model-like figures. The study concluded that while Pinterest is commonly used for exercise ideas and inspiration, the information about belly fat loss exercises is often incomplete and misleading.

Reddit: a study conducted in the USA analysed obesity-related content focusing on misinformation, facts, stigma and positivity.27 This cross-sectional study evaluated 689 447 top-level comments (defined as comments that were made directly on a post and not another user’s comment) using NLP models. The study employed a mixed-methods approach: first, misinformation was defined based on peer-reviewed guidelines identified by the American Board of Obesity Medicine. The analysis found that misinformation was present in 0.47% of the sentences, whereas 1.88% contained stigma, 1.94% reflected positivity (affirmative language to persons with obesity) and 8.93% were factual. The study identified that misinformation had a higher average percentage of negations and fewer long words compared with factual content. Stigma was characterised by a higher proportion of swear words and fewer first-person singular pronouns. The study concluded that while misinformation about obesity was relatively scarce on Reddit, understanding its psycholinguistic markers can help provide insights into content dissemination on social media platforms.

Social media influencers (Instagram, X, Facebook): a study conducted in the UK aimed to evaluate the credibility of weight management blogs authored by social media influencers across platforms like Instagram, X and Facebook.17 This cross-sectional analysis reviewed 180 posts and articles to assess the accuracy of the information using a standardised credibility checklist. The findings indicated that many weight management blogs were not credible, with only two influencers deemed qualified to provide advice. It was also noted that the registered nutritionist scored the highest at 85%, suggesting that appropriate qualifications could enhance credibility. The study concluded that UK social media influencers may not be reliable weight management information sources.

Cholesterol

Texts: a study conducted in Turkey aimed to assess the validity of the most-read Turkish-language texts related to cholesterol over a specific period.19 This cross-sectional study evaluated 604 online texts using assessments by two expert cardiologists. The quality of cholesterol information on websites was evaluated using a checklist developed by the authors based on recent cholesterol guidelines. The sources included universities (1.3%), hospitals (0.9%), personal blogs (33.1%), health websites (30.2%) and medical journals (34.2%). The analysis revealed that 38.9% of texts were classified as sufficient and favourable, while 5.7% were categorised as misleading. Notably, 62.5% of texts named a medical practitioner, and those from universities and hospitals consistently ranked as favourable. The study highlighted a significant variation in content quality, with a statistical difference observed between misleading and favourable texts.

Atrial fibrillation ablation

Websites: a study conducted in the USA aimed to assess the accuracy of information provided to the public on hospital websites regarding atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation.29 This cross-sectional study evaluated 487 web pages from 4805 hospitals using Google. Thirty-three websites discussed success rates, with 12% of those reporting exaggerated success rates. Reported success rates higher than 86% were classified as exaggerated. Additionally, less than 10% addressed the possibility of requiring a second ablation, and only 16% mentioned the associated risks. The study highlighted that hospital websites often lack comprehensive discussions on AF ablation, with many offerings potentially misinformation about mortality, stroke prevention and the necessity of medical therapy.

Alcohol

Websites: a study conducted in the UK aimed to analyse the completeness and accuracy of cardiovascular health information disseminated by the Alcohol industry (AI) Social Aspects/Public Relations Organizations (SAPROs) compared with non-AI-funded sources.23 This cross-sectional study reviewed 36 websites using content, thematic and context analyses. The results showed that non-industry-funded health organisations were more likely to identify excess alcohol as a risk factor for major CVD and other complications. In contrast, AI/SAPRO websites often portrayed alcohol as beneficial to heart health, citing the J-shaped curve to argue for the benefits of moderate consumption. This study concluded that there are significant and concerning differences in how AI/SAPRO and non-AI-funded organisations communicate cardiovascular risks and benefits to the public.

Diabetic foot

YouTube: a study conducted in Turkey aimed to examine Turkish videos as a source of information on diabetic foot care training and assess the presence of nurses in these videos.30 This cross-sectional study analysed 87 videos using the DISCERN and GQS tools to evaluate their usefulness. The results indicated that 8% of the videos were very useful, 33.3% were moderately useful, 51.7% were somewhat useful and 6.9% were not useful. Additionally, 4.6% of the videos contained misleading information. The study concluded that there is a considerable volume of content available on diabetic foot care on YouTube.

Acute myocardial infarction

YouTube: a study conducted in Portugal sought to evaluate the relevance and quality of clinical and scientific information regarding acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in Portuguese-language videos.26 This cross-sectional study analysed 242 videos using the Health on the Net Code (honchoed) and DISCERN scores. The findings revealed that 32.6% of the videos contained clinical/scientific inaccuracies, predominantly from trainers without medical affiliation. Inaccuracies were most frequently related to signs and symptoms, appearing in 58.2% of the inaccurate videos. Notably, content from scientific societies and health or government institutions was found to be accurate. The study concluded that there is a high prevalence of misinformation about AMI on YouTube, with one-third of the videos being irrelevant or containing inaccurate information.

Discussion

This review underscores the breadth of misinformation across various domains of CVD, including nutrition, exercise, blood pressure, smoking, abnormal adiposity, cholesterol, alcohol and specific cardiovascular conditions. All the included studies presented the influence of commonly used social media platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, YouTube and X for spreading such fake and misleading information related to CVD often unknowingly. Such prevalent misinformation has a great impact on prevention and control of CVDs, which requires urgent attention to address this issue from all concerned stakeholders.

Misinformation on social media regarding exercise, nutrition and cardiovascular health presents a major public health concern. Many influential sources on platforms like Instagram and YouTube often disseminate inaccurate or suboptimal content that influences millions of followers to adopt unhealthy behaviours such as irregular exercise, excessive consumption of harmful fats22 and trust in unproven dietary supplements.28 35 In the management of hypertension, misleading claims such as the promotion of ‘natural salt’ as a healthier alternative have reached millions of users, shifting focus away from standard pharmacological treatments.28 This widespread prevalence of such misinformation undermines medical guidelines and delays necessary treatments, increasing the risk of hypertension-related complications.

Beyond hypertension, misinformation affects various cardiovascular health risk factors, including smoking, cholesterol management, weight control and diabetic care. False claims regarding ‘safer’ smoking alternatives like electronic cigarettes often dominate the evidence-based recommendations, creating confusion for the public. Similarly, disbelief about statins in cholesterol management leads to poor adherence to standard treatments,40 increasing cardiovascular risks. Social media platforms further promote misleading narratives about alcohol consumption, only highlighting the moderate drinking as beneficial while ignoring its associated health risks.23 Additionally, misinformation regarding diabetic foot care and weight management, especially on platforms like YouTube and Facebook, highlights the urgent need for improving the quality and reliability of online health information.30 The impact of misinformation is particularly concerning for acute cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction26 and AF.29 Studies reveal that a significant proportion of YouTube videos on these conditions contain misleading or incomplete information,26 with even hospital websites failing to provide accurate details on treatment risks and benefits for myocardial infarction.29

Misinformation related to CVD creates a substantial barrier to effective prevention and treatment, as inaccurate claims about risk factors, screening and therapeutic options can prompt harmful behaviours or delay proper care. Evidence indicates that misleading narratives, including unproven ‘natural remedies’, misconceptions about antihypertensive therapy41 and false concerns regarding statin safety, can reduce adherence to evidence-based interventions, increasing the risk of uncontrolled blood pressure and preventable complications.42 Such misinformation also disrupts public understanding of lifestyle guidance and weakens trust in clinicians, thereby hindering long-term disease management and efforts to reduce population-level cardiovascular risk.40 43 Beyond clinical impacts, misinformation can harm society by creating division, lowering trust in institutions and economic instability through distorted public decision-making.44 Addressing this challenge requires stronger regulatory mechanisms, improved content verification and collaboration with credible influencers to promote evidence-based health communication.

The persistence and spread of cardiovascular misinformation through social media can be better understood through theoretical lenses. The networked nature of social media and algorithmic amplification creates echo chambers that reinforce pre-existing beliefs, making corrective information less likely to penetrate.45 Social psychological mechanisms such as confirmation bias and the illusory truth effect further sustain misinformation; individuals tend to favour information that aligns with their prior attitudes and perceive repeated claims as more credible, even when false.46 47 Additionally, theories on trust in science highlight that while scientific authority is crucial for public health, pseudoscientific misinformation often mimics the language and aesthetics of legitimate science, exploiting trust to gain credibility.48 These intertwined cognitive and social dynamics suggest that cardiovascular misinformation is not merely a product of information deficits but is embedded in identity-driven and trust-based processes. Addressing these challenges requires interventions that combine prebunking and debunking strategies, critical evaluation training and culturally tailored communication approaches.49

In addition, the effectiveness of any intervention is profoundly moderated by an individual’s health literacy, which influences their ability to access, understand and critically evaluate both accurate and misleading CVD-related information.50 Individuals with limited health literacy may face difficulties in discerning credible sources from misleading ones, making them more vulnerable to misinformation related to CVDs.51 In contrast, those with higher health literacy are more likely to evaluate information critically, seek evidence-based sources and make informed decisions regarding cardiovascular health.52 These disparities in health literacy underscore the need for targeted interventions that enhance individuals’ ability to navigate complex health information environments, particularly in the digital era where misinformation is widespread.

This review highlights promising interventions to mitigate the risks of widespread misinformation on social media, particularly regarding tobacco products and e-cigarrates.16 20 24 25 While corrective strategies such as text-based and narrative interventions can counteract misinformation, especially for adolescents, a multifaceted approach is necessary.3 Age-appropriate digital interventions, partnerships with credible influencers, public health campaigns and digital literacy programmes are crucial in combating such misinformation.53 Given the high volume of misinformation, depending only on authority-led scrutiny is not a feasible approach; thus, empowering individuals with fact-checking skills is essential. The review also notes a research gap, as most studies are from high-income, English-speaking countries, focusing on Western platforms. However, findings from high-income, English-speaking contexts may not fully reflect the dynamics in low and middle-income countries,54 where factors such as limited digital literacy, weaker regulatory frameworks and resource constraints can amplify the effects of misinformation.55 This emphasises the importance of future research that should examine resource-constrained settings using diverse languages, and the role of AI-driven digital literacy interventions,56 57 implementing cyber-wellness educational programmes to enhance public awareness and resilience against m/disinformation,58 particularly related to CVDs.

Strengths and limitations of the study

To our knowledge, this is the first review specifically focusing on misinformation related to the prevention and control of CVD. A key strength is the use of the JBI guideline for scoping reviews, which ensures a rigorous and transparent approach. This methodology, combined with the absence of restrictions on study design, methodology or publication date, broadens the scope of the review and provides a comprehensive overview of the topic. Furthermore, the inclusion of grey literature enhances the review by incorporating valuable information from non-peer-reviewed sources, such as policy documents, reports and guidelines. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. Despite a comprehensive search strategy, variations in terminology and definitions across different contexts may have introduced inconsistencies in comparing findings across studies. Additionally, as a scoping review, we did not conduct a formal risk of bias assessment of the included studies, which limits our ability to fully evaluate the quality and robustness of the individual studies informing our conclusions. Nevertheless, we have highlighted several common methodological limitations that impact the strength of the evidence. Specifically, we observed a predominance of cross-sectional study designs (18 studies), which preclude establishing a causal link between misinformation exposure and behavioural or health outcomes. Additionally, the studies often rely on non-representative convenience samples (eg, specific social media content or followers), and there is high heterogeneity in assessment tools, ranging from non-validated content analysis (eg, Dedrick 2020) and subjective expert opinion (eg, Hayıroğlu 2020) to standardised instruments (eg, DISCERN, GQS), indicating that findings from these studies should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that cardiovascular health misinformation is widespread and shaped by both platform dynamics and user-level factors. Addressing this challenge requires coordinated strategies that strengthen digital literacy, enhance visibility of evidence-based content and reduce the reach of misinformation. Digital platform regulation should be prioritised to control algorithmic increase of false content, while public health policies must integrate digital literacy modules to equip individuals with skills to critically evaluate online information. Partnerships between health authorities, medical institutions and technology companies are essential for systematic verification of online health content, ensuring that accurate, evidence-based information is accessible and visible to mitigate misinformation’s deleterious effects and strengthen global cardiovascular health outcomes.
