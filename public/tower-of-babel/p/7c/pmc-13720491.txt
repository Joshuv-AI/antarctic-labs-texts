Reduced Serum Apelin-13 Levels in Medication-free Adults with Major Depressive Disorder
Authors: Fatma Taşkın Koca, Mahmut Selçuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe apelinergic system has been implicated in neuroinflammatory and neuroplastic mechanisms related to depressive disorders. Apelin-13, the most biologically active isoform of apelin, has demonstrated antidepressant-like effects in preclinical studies; however, clinical evidence remains limited. This study aimed to investigate serum Apelin-13 levels in medication-free adults with major depressive disorder (MDD) and assess their clinical relevance.MethodsThis cross-sectional, case–control study included 80 medication-free adults diagnosed with MDD and 81 healthy control subjects matched for age, sex, and body mass index. Psychiatric diagnoses were established using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5. Depression severity was assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale. Serum Apelin-13 concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Between-group comparisons, correlation analyses, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed.ResultsSerum Apelin-13 levels were significantly lower in patients with MDD than in healthy controls (p < 0.001). ROC curve analysis demonstrated a moderate discriminative ability of serum Apelin-13 levels, with an area under the curve of 0.719. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that lower serum Apelin-13 levels were independently associated with MDD after adjustment for relevant confounding variables (p < 0.001). No significant association was observed between serum Apelin-13 levels and depression severity.ConclusionThese findings indicate that serum Apelin-13 levels are reduced in medication-free adults with MDD and may reflect an underlying biological vulnerability rather than current symptom severity. Apelin-13 may represent a trait-related biomarker contributing to the biological characterization of depressive disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent and disabling psychiatric condition characterized by prolonged low mood, loss of interest or pleasure, cognitive disturbances, and substantial functional impairment [1]. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 4.7% of the global population is affected at any given time [2], and the worldwide incidence of MDD has increased markedly over recent decades. In 2019, more than 275 million new cases were reported, representing a 59% increase compared to 1990 [3]. This significant global burden underscores the need for a deeper understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying MDD and for the identification of novel biomarkers that may facilitate improved diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment strategies.

The etiology of MDD is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of biological, genetic, environmental, and psychological factors. Dysregulations in monoaminergic neurotransmission [4], impairments in neuroplasticity and neurotrophic signaling [5], chronic low-grade inflammation and immune activation [6], and disturbances in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis [4] have all been implicated in its development. Furthermore, neuroimaging studies consistently demonstrate structural and functional alterations in brain regions essential for emotional regulation, particularly the limbic system and the prefrontal cortex [7]. Despite the availability of multiple treatment modalities, remission rates remain modest, and cumulative response across treatment steps does not exceed 67% [8]. These limitations highlight the ongoing need to identify biomarkers associated with MDD pathophysiology and to delineate biological pathways that could serve as therapeutic targets.

Apelin is an endogenous peptide ligand of the G protein–coupled Apelin receptor (APJ), first isolated in the late 1990s [9]. The Apelin/APJ system plays a critical role in cardiovascular homeostasis, energy metabolism, fluid balance, and neuroprotection [10]. Within the central nervous system (CNS), Apelin signaling modulates neuroinflammation, neuroendocrine stress responses, and neuronal survival through intracellular pathways such as PI3K/Akt, MAPK, and β-arrestin signaling cascades [11,12]. Among its isoforms, Apelin-13 is the most potent bioactive fragment, exhibiting high affinity for APJ and substantial expression in CNS regions relevant to mood regulation, including the hypothalamus and hippocampus [10,13,14]. These characteristics have generated growing scientific interest in the potential role of Apelin-13 in the neurobiology of depressive disorders.

Preclinical studies provide compelling evidence that Apelin-13 may exert antidepressant and neuroprotective effects. Experimental models demonstrate that Apelin-13 administration attenuates neuroinflammation, suppresses apoptosis, and modulates autophagy in pathways implicated in depression and neurodegeneration [15-17]. Moreover, Apelin-13 has been shown to improve depressive-like behavior, anxiety, and cognitive deficits in animal models of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), Alzheimer’s disease, and Huntington’s disease [18-20]. While one study reported that intracerebroventricular infusion of Apelin-13 increased depression-like behavior in mice [21], the majority of preclinical findings support a protective role for Apelin-13 in mood regulation.

Clinical research investigating Apelin and its isoforms in psychiatric disorders remains limited and yields inconsistent findings. For example, plasma Apelin levels are elevated in first-episode psychosis but not in chronic schizophrenia [22]. In adolescent patients with MDD, serum Apelin-13 levels have been reported to be significantly lower than in healthy controls [23]. However, a more recent clinical study in medicated adults found reduced plasma Apelin levels in MDD patients but did not detect differences in serum Apelin concentrations [24]. These discrepancies may reflect methodological heterogeneity across studies—particularly differences between serum versus plasma measurements, peptide stability, assay characteristics, and preanalytical factors—which are known to significantly influence circulating peptide biomarker results in psychiatric research [25]. Additionally, antidepressants possess anti-inflammatory properties [26] that may alter Apelin signaling, potentially masking disease-specific biomarker profiles.

Given these inconsistencies and the scarcity of isoform-specific research in unmedicated adult populations, there remains a critical need to investigate Apelin-13 levels under methodologically controlled conditions. To our knowledge, no prior study has examined serum Apelin-13 concentrations in medication-free adults with MDD.

Therefore, the present study was designed to compare serum Apelin-13 levels between medication-free adult patients with MDD and matched healthy controls. We hypothesized that Apelin-13 levels would be significantly lower in individuals with MDD, reflecting potential involvement of the Apelin/APJ system in the pathophysiology of depression.

METHODS

Study Design and Ethical Approval

This study was designed as a cross-sectional, case–control investigation conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of İzmir Bakırçay University (Approval No: 1694; Date: 10/07/2024). Institutional permission was also obtained from Muğla Training and Research Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Participants

The study was conducted between August 2024 and February 2025 at the Psychiatry Outpatient Clinics of Muğla Training and Research Hospital. The sample consisted of 80 adult patients diagnosed with MDD and 81 healthy control subjects, matched for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI).

Psychiatric diagnoses were established according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria and were confirmed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders (SCID-5). All SCID-5 interviews were administered by experienced psychiatrists trained in structured diagnostic assessment.

Healthy control participants were recruited from hospital staff and the local community. The SCID-5 was also administered to all control participants to exclude any current or lifetime psychiatric disorders.

All participants were between 18 and 65 years of age. Sex was recorded based on self-report.

Exclusion Criteria

Participants were excluded if they had:

• Any comorbid psychiatric disorder other than MDD

• Current or lifetime substance or alcohol use disorder

• Autoimmune, cardiovascular, metabolic (e.g., diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome), or malignant diseases

• Acute or chronic inflammatory or infectious conditions

• History of trauma or surgery within the previous three months

• Use of antidepressant, antipsychotic, mood stabilizer, or anti-inflammatory medication within the last three months

• Pregnancy or breastfeeding

• Cognitive impairment interfering with informed consent or assessment

The same exclusion criteria were applied to the control group.

Clinical and Demographic Assessment

Sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, education level, employment status, smoking status, residential area, and family history of psychiatric disorders, were recorded using standardized data collection forms.

Depression severity was assessed in the MDD group using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D). The Turkish version of the HAM-D has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity [27].

Body weight and height were measured under standardized conditions, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared.

Blood Sampling and Laboratory Analysis

Venous blood samples were collected after 12 hours of overnight fasting, between 08:00 and 10:00 AM, to minimize circadian variation. Blood was drawn into serum separator tubes. Blood samples could not be obtained from one participant in the control group due to technical reasons; therefore, serum Apelin-13 analyses were performed on samples from 80 patients with MDD and 80 healthy controls. Following collection, samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 minutes, and serum aliquots were stored at −80°C until analysis.

Serum Apelin-13 concentrations were measured using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (BT LAB), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay had a detection range of 0.5−200 ng/L and a sensitivity of 0.024 ng/L. All reagents and samples were equilibrated to room temperature before analysis. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Thermo ScientificTM Multiskan FC microplate reader.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 (IBM Co.). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum−maximum), depending on data distribution.

Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent samples ttests for normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney Utests for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Correlations between serum Apelin-13 levels and HAM-D scores were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis.

To evaluate the discriminative ability of Apelin-13 levels between MDD patients and healthy controls, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cut-off value. Variables showing statistically significant differences between groups were further examined using multivariate logistic regression analysis, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, education level, and smoking status.

Effect sizes for between-group comparisons were calculated using Cohen’s d, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively.

A pvalue < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 161 participants were included in the study, comprising 80 patients diagnosed with MDD and 81 healthy control subjects. The median age was 30.0 years (range: 18−64) in the MDD group and 30.0 years (range: 22−63) in the control group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.063). In the MDD group, 54 participants (67.5%) were female, and 26 (32.5%) were male, whereas the control group consisted of 48 females (59.3%) and 33 males (40.7%). Sex distribution did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.278).

No statistically significant differences were observed between patients and controls for BMI, marital status, type of residence (urban vs. rural), or living arrangements (all p > 0.05). In contrast, educational attainment differed significantly between groups, with 33.8% of patients having less than a high school education, compared to 11.1% of healthy controls (p = 0.001). Unemployment was significantly more prevalent among patients with MDD (55.0%) than among controls (13.6%) (p < 0.001). Additionally, a family history of psychiatric disorders was reported more frequently in the patient group (33.8% vs. 12.3%, p = 0.001), and the prevalence of previous suicide attempts was significantly higher among patients (16.3%) compared with controls (1.2%) (p = 0.001). Smoking rates were also significantly higher in the MDD group (48.8%) than in the control group (22.2%) (p < 0.001). Detailed sociodemographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Among patients with MDD, 38.7% were classified as having mild depression, while 61.3% had moderate to severe symptoms based on HAM-D scores.

Serum Apelin-13 concentrations were available for 80 patients with MDD and 80 healthy controls, as blood sampling could not be completed for one control participant, who was therefore included only in the sociodemographic analyses. Median serum Apelin-13 levels were 38.62 ng/L (range: 17.92−149.60) in the MDD group and 77.94 ng/L (range: 10.17−158.40) in the control group. Serum Apelin-13 levels were significantly lower in patients with MDD compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001), with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = −0.808). These findings are summarized in Table 2.

ROC curve analysis demonstrated that serum Apelin-13 levels had a moderate ability to discriminate between patients with MDD and healthy controls. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.719 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.639−0.799; p < 0.001). Using a cut-off value of 49.02 ng/L, Apelin-13 levels showed a sensitivity of 71.3% and a specificity of 70.0% for distinguishing patients from controls. The ROC curve and related parameters are presented in Figure 1 and Table 3, respectively.

A multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the independent association between serum Apelin-13 levels and MDD. After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, education level, and smoking status, lower serum Apelin-13 levels remained significantly associated with MDD (B = 0.978, 95% CI: 0.967−0.989, p < 0.001). The results of the regression analysis are presented in Table 4.

Correlation analysis revealed no significant association between serum Apelin-13 levels and HAM-D scores in the MDD group (p > 0.05). Furthermore, within the patient group, serum Apelin-13 concentrations were not significantly associated with sex (p = 0.199), family history of psychiatric disorders (p = 0.883), smoking status (p = 0.965), or age. To assess the potential influence of prior antidepressant exposure, serum Apelin-13 levels were compared between treatment-naïve patients (n = 50) and previously medicated patients (n = 30). No statistically significant difference was observed between these subgroups (p > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, serum Apelin-13 levels were significantly lower in medication-free adults with MDD than in healthy controls. To our knowledge, this is the first clinical investigation to specifically evaluate serum Apelin-13 concentrations in an unmedicated adult MDD population using isoform-specific measurement. These findings support the hypothesis that dysregulation of the Apelin/APJ system may play a role in the pathophysiology of MDD [18,23,28].

Although several sociodemographic and clinical variables differed between patients with MDD and healthy controls, these factors were included as covariates in multivariate analyses. They did not account for the observed group difference in serum Apelin-13 levels. Accordingly, the primary focus of the present discussion is on the potential biological significance of reduced Apelin-13 concentrations in MDD and their implications for the underlying neurobiological mechanisms of depressive disorders.

It should also be acknowledged that certain sociodemographic and lifestyle-related variables, including education level, employment status, smoking behavior, and family history of psychiatric disorders, differed significantly between groups. Although these factors were adjusted for in multivariate analyses, residual confounding effects cannot be entirely excluded. Previous evidence suggests that sociodemographic and lifestyle factors may influence peripheral peptide levels through mechanisms related to chronic stress exposure, metabolic regulation, and inflammatory processes, which should be considered when interpreting the present findings [25,26].

The observed reduction in serum Apelin-13 levels is consistent with previous clinical findings in adolescents diagnosed with MDD, where significantly lower Apelin-13 concentrations were reported compared to healthy controls [23]. Our results extend this evidence to adult patients, suggesting that alterations in Apelin-13 are not restricted to developmental stages but may represent a persistent biological feature of depressive disorders. In contrast, Chibaatar et al. [24] reported no significant differences in serum Apelin levels between medicated MDD patients and healthy controls, although plasma Apelin levels were reduced in the patient group. This discrepancy is likely attributable to methodological differences. Notably, while previous studies measured total Apelin, the present study focused specifically on Apelin-13, the most biologically active isoform with high receptor affinity [10,15]. Isoform-specific alterations may be diluted or overlooked when total Apelin is assessed. Furthermore, the inclusion of medicated patients in earlier studies may have confounded findings, as antidepressant treatments exert anti-inflammatory and neurotrophic effects that can influence circulating peptide levels [26].

Preclinical evidence provides strong biological plausibility for the association between reduced Apelin-13 levels and depressive pathology. Experimental studies have demonstrated that Apelin-13 modulates neuroinflammation, suppresses apoptosis, and regulates autophagy—processes that are centrally implicated in the neurobiology of depression [15-17]. Additionally, Apelin-13 administration has been shown to reverse depressive-like behaviors and cognitive deficits in animal models of chronic stress, PTSD, and neurodegenerative diseases [17,19,20], through pathways involving brain-derived neurotrophic factor, AMPK/PGC-1α signaling, and synaptic plasticity [29-31]. Given the established role of impaired neuroplasticity and reduced neurotrophic support in MDD [5], decreased Apelin-13 levels may reflect broader disruptions in neuroprotective mechanisms underlying depressive disorders.

Some experimental findings have reported opposing effects, with centrally administered Apelin-13 inducing depression-like behaviors in specific animal paradigms [21]. Differences in experimental design, administration routes, dosage, and region-specific effects of Apelin signaling within the brain may explain these seemingly contradictory results. Such context-dependent actions are characteristic of neuropeptides and underscore the complexity of the Apelin/APJ system in the CNS.

In the present study, serum Apelin-13 levels demonstrated a moderate ability to discriminate between patients with MDD and healthy controls, as indicated by ROC analysis (AUC = 0.719). This level of diagnostic accuracy is insufficient for standalone clinical use. Instead, these findings suggest that Apelin-13 may be better conceptualized as a component of a multimodal biomarker panel, in combination with other biological, inflammatory, or neurotrophic markers, rather than serving as a single diagnostic indicator. Importantly, multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that lower Apelin-13 levels were independently associated with MDD after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, education level, and smoking status, reinforcing the robustness of this association.

No significant correlation was observed between serum Apelin-13 levels and depression severity as measured by HAM-D scores. This lack of association suggests that Apelin-13 may not function as a state-dependent biomarker reflecting acute symptom intensity. Instead, Apelin-13 may represent a trait-related marker indicative of underlying biological vulnerability or chronic dysregulation of neuroprotective and neuroplastic pathways in MDD. However, it should be noted that the cross-sectional design of the present study precludes definitive conclusions regarding whether reduced Apelin-13 levels represent a stable trait marker or a state-dependent phenomenon. Longitudinal studies assessing Apelin-13 levels across different phases of illness, including remission and relapse, are required to confirm its temporal stability and to determine whether Apelin-13 truly reflects a trait-related biomarker. In this context, the present interpretation is consistent with findings from other psychiatric disorders, where apelin alterations appear to be more closely related to disease presence or stage rather than symptom severity [22]. Similar alterations in apelin levels have also been reported in patients with MDD, including adolescent samples, supporting the notion that apelinergic dysregulation may not be strictly state-dependent [23,24]. The absence of significant associations with sex, smoking status, family history of psychiatric disorders, or prior antidepressant exposure further supports the notion that reduced Apelin-13 levels reflect disease-related biological alterations rather than secondary effects of demographic or treatment-related factors.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that serum Apelin-13 may reflect underlying neurobiological vulnerability in MDD rather than current symptom severity. Although its diagnostic accuracy alone is limited, Apelin-13 may be valuable as part of a multimodal biomarker panel incorporating inflammatory markers, neurotrophic factors, or neuroendocrine measures, an approach increasingly emphasized in precision psychiatry [6,28,32]. Moreover, accumulating preclinical evidence indicates that modulation of the Apelin/APJ system influences neuroinflammation, synaptic plasticity, and stress-related behaviors [16,17,30], highlighting Apelin-13 as a potential therapeutic target. Interventions aimed at enhancing Apelin/APJ signaling could represent a novel adjunctive strategy for patients with inadequate response to conventional antidepressant treatments. The present findings also emphasize the importance of conducting biomarker research in medication-free populations, as pharmacological treatments may obscure disease-related biological alterations [26].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationship between Apelin-13 levels and the development or progression of MDD. Second, serum measurements may not fully reflect CNS Apelin-13 activity, and peripheral–central dissociations cannot be excluded. Given that apelin is a neuropeptide with region-specific expression and complex central signaling properties, circulating serum levels may be influenced by peripheral production, metabolic clearance, and blood–brain barrier dynamics. Therefore, serum Apelin-13 concentrations may not directly correspond to central apelinergic activity. Future studies incorporating cerebrospinal fluid measurements, neuroimaging approaches, or experimental models assessing central Apelin/APJ signaling would be valuable to clarify the relationship between peripheral and central apelin activity. Third, although significant confounding variables were controlled for, unmeasured factors such as diet, physical activity, and stress exposure may have influenced Apelin-13 levels. Finally, while the sample size was sufficient to detect group differences, larger and longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the generalizability and temporal stability of these findings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that serum Apelin-13 levels are significantly lower in medication-free adults with MDD than in healthy controls. These findings provide novel clinical evidence supporting the involvement of the Apelin/APJ system in the pathophysiology of MDD. Although Apelin-13 does not appear to reflect acute symptom severity, its independent association with MDD suggests potential utility as a trait-related biomarker or as part of a broader biological profile of depression. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted further to elucidate the role of Apelin-13 in depressive disorders and to explore its potential as a therapeutic target.
