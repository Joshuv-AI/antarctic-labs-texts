Clinical outcomes of intramedullary nailing and dual-plate fixation for proximal humeral fractures: an indication-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jianjun Wang, Haiyan Liang, Xianqing Shi, Xuan Wei, Yaqiang Zhang, Jie Shi, Xiaoyan Ma, Yun Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical outcomes of intramedullary nailing and dual-plate fixation for the treatment of proximal humeral fractures in an indication-based retrospective cohort.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 69 patients with proximal humeral fractures admitted to our hospital from October 2022 to August 2024. Among them, 32 patients underwent fixation using Proximal humerus internal locking system (Philos) combined with a medial support plate (dual-plate group), while 37 patients underwent intramedullary nailing (intramedullary nailing group). All patients received early systematic rehabilitation therapy. Surgical parameters, hospital stay, fracture healing time, shoulder function scores, and postoperative complications were compared between the two groups.ResultsThe intramedullary nailing group had shorter operative time, hospital stay, and fracture healing time compared to the dual-plate group (P < 0.05). The intraoperative blood loss was also lower in the intramedullary nailing group (P < 0.05). Postoperatively, the shoulder function scores in both groups improved significantly compared to preoperative values (P < 0.05), with significantly higher scores in the intramedullary nailing group than in the dual-plate group (P < 0.05).ConclusionIn this indication-based retrospective cohort, intramedullary nailing was associated with shorter operative time, less intraoperative blood loss, faster fracture healing and better shoulder function scores than double plating in proximal humeral fractures with relatively preserved medial support. Double-plate fixation remains an important option for complex fractures with severe medial column deficiency and poor bone quality, despite greater surgical trauma. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that intramedullary nailing is superior for all proximal humeral fractures, and prospective randomized studies are needed to confirm the results.

Background

Proximal humeral fractures are common in clinical practice, accounting for 4–10% of all fractures, with a high incidence among the elderly [1]. These fractures often affect shoulder function and significantly impair quality of life. Clinically, non‑displaced fractures are typically managed conservatively, whereas unstable displaced fractures generally require surgical fixation. Currently, commonly used internal fixation devices include locking plates, support plates, and intramedullary nails. Double‑plate fixation provides excellent biomechanical stability and clinical efficacy for complex fractures [2]. However, due to its central fixation and minimally invasive nature, intramedullary nailing has increasingly become an important choice for fracture treatment [3]. In daily clinical practice, the choice of fixation method depends on fracture pattern, bone quality, patient factors and surgeon experience, and intramedullary nailing in particular has a recognized learning curve with respect to closed reduction and entry portal placement. There is still no consensus on the optimal fixation method for proximal humeral fractures. This study retrospectively analyzes and compares the clinical outcomes of intramedullary nailing and double plating to provide evidence for clinical decision‑making.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A total of 69 patients with proximal humeral fractures admitted to the Department of Orthopedics at Lanzhou First People’s Hospital from October 2022 to August 2024 were consecutively enrolled and categorized based on surgical methods into the dual-plate group (32 cases) and the intramedullary nailing group (37 cases).

Inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of acute Neer two‑ to four‑part proximal humeral fractures amenable to joint‑preserving internal fixation; and (3) treatment with either double plating or intramedullary nailing in accordance with the indications described below.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Pathological fractures; (2) Concomitant nerve injuries; (3) Incomplete clinical data.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Lanzhou First People’s Hospital, and all patients provided informed consent.

Surgical indications and allocation to treatment groups: In our institution, the choice between double plating and intramedullary nailing follows an indication-based strategy. Double plating is primarily reserved for fractures with severe medial column deficiency, including marked medial calcar comminution, metaphyseal extension, and very poor bone quality that require enhanced medial column support. In particular, double plating is mainly used in elderly patients in whom joint preservation is still pursued but prosthetic replacement is not clearly indicated on the basis of age, comorbidities or functional demands, or is declined by the patient for economic or cultural reasons. By contrast, intramedullary nailing is preferred for fractures with relatively preserved medial support and suitable metaphyseal canal morphology. For relatively simple Neer two-part fractures, double plating was selected only when the medial hinge was disrupted and bone quality was severely osteoporotic. In elderly patients with three- or four-part fractures and severe medial calcar destruction, joint replacement is generally considered; however, in the present series, some patients refused arthroplasty and were therefore treated with joint-preserving double plating after detailed informed consent.

Surgical methods

Dual-plate group (Fig. 1A)

Open reduction and internal fixation were performed using a lateral locking plate combined with a medial support plate. Under general anesthesia, patients were placed in a beach‑chair position and a standard deltopectoral approach was used, taking care to preserve the cephalic vein and surrounding soft tissues. After hematoma evacuation and exposure of the fracture site, reduction was achieved using the intertubercular groove and the articular surface as references and temporarily maintained with Kirschner wires. A proximal humerus locking plate (PHILOS‑type) was placed lateral to the bicipital groove, and a contoured medial support plate was added when medial calcar comminution or poor bone quality was present to enhance medial column stability. Plate position and screw lengths were adjusted under fluoroscopy to avoid subacromial impingement and intra‑articular screw penetration. The same design and brand of locking plate and medial support plate were used in all patients in the double‑plating group to minimize implant‑related variability. After fixation, shoulder motion was checked intraoperatively to confirm stability. During the study period, prophylactic intravenous antibiotics were routinely administered for up to three days according to our institutional protocol for elderly patients with osteoporotic fractures.

Intramedullary nailing group (Fig. 1B)

Limited open reduction and intramedullary nailing were performed using a standardized technique. Patients were placed in a beach‑chair position with the head turned to the contralateral side. A short anterolateral incision was made starting at the anterolateral edge of the acromion and extended distally within the safe zone above the axillary nerve; the deltoid muscle was split in line with its fibers and the split was extended no more than 5 cm distal to the acromion to remain proximal to the course of the axillary nerve. In cases requiring open reduction, the interval was slightly extended distally while keeping the deltoid split within 5 cm of the acromion. The rotator cuff was incised longitudinally at the muscle–tendon junction of the supraspinatus approximately 1–1.5 cm medial to the greater tuberosity and subsequently repaired with non‑absorbable sutures at the end of the procedure. The biceps tendon was protected throughout. A guidewire was inserted at the medial edge of the greater tuberosity footprint of the humeral head under fluoroscopic control. If residual humeral head varus deformity persisted, the entry point was adjusted slightly medially to restore alignment. After reaming, an appropriately sized proximal humeral intramedullary nail from a single manufacturer was inserted, with its proximal end seated 5–7 mm below the articular cartilage surface. Proximal locking screws were placed to capture the tuberosities, and traction on the tuberosities was applied in a standardized manner by an assistant to maintain reduction during screw insertion. Distal locking screws were then inserted. Tuberosity sutures were passed through the dedicated eyelets or proximal screw holes of the nail and tied to the proximal screws to prevent secondary proximal migration of the tuberosities. Final anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic views confirmed nail depth, screw length, and fracture reduction. During the study period, prophylactic intravenous antibiotics were routinely administered for up to three days according to our institutional protocol for elderly osteoporotic fractures.

Postoperative management

The same standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol was applied to both groups. After awakening from anesthesia on the day of surgery, patients were encouraged to actively exercise forearm and hand muscles. On the first postoperative day, assisted passive shoulder abduction, adduction, flexion, and extension were initiated as tolerated. Active range‑of‑motion exercises were generally encouraged after one week, depending on pain and radiographic findings. Follow‑up radiographs were obtained at 1, 2, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively to assess fracture healing and to guide progression to active strengthening and weight‑bearing exercises.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and compared using the chi-square (χ²) test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the two groups

In the double plating group, patients’ ages ranged from 55 to 83 years (mean 72.25 ± 8.65 years); there were 17 males and 15 females. According to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, 14 patients were in Class II and 18 in Class III. Based on the Neer classification, 10 cases were two-part fractures, 16 were three-part fractures, and 6 were four-part fractures.

In the intramedullary nailing group, patients’ ages ranged from 56 to 82 years (mean 72.56 ± 8.73 years); there were 20 males and 17 females. ASA classification included 16 patients in Class II and 21 in Class III. Neer classification identified 12 cases as two-part fractures, 17 as three-part fractures, and 8 as four-part fractures. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in age, sex, ASA classification, or fracture type (P > 0.05), confirming their comparability (Table 1).

Comparison of intraoperative parameters

The intramedullary nailing group showed significantly shorter surgical duration, lower intraoperative blood loss, smaller incision length, and reduced hospital stay compared to the double plating group (P < 0.05). However, no significant difference was found in fluoroscopy frequency between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 2). No severe perioperative complications were observed in either group. All incisions healed well, with no cases of delayed healing, fat liquefaction, or wound infection.

Comparison of clinical outcomes

Fracture healing time was 115.29 ± 20.86 days in the double plating group and 96.17 ± 28.21 days in the intramedullary nailing group, with the latter showing significantly faster healing (t = 2.692, P = 0.038). At the final follow-up, the cervicodiaphyseal angle was 143.26 ± 2.86° in the double plating group and 135.26 ± 3.57° in the intramedullary nailing group, indicating a significant difference (t = 2.551, P = 0.041). The Constant-Murley and UCLA scores were significantly higher in the intramedullary nailing group at all postoperative time points compared to the double plating group (P < 0.05) (Table 3).

Comparison of surgical safety

All patients completed follow-up, with a duration of 12 to 18 months (mean 13.50 months). In the double plating group, six patients developed complications: three cases of humeral head varus, one case of humeral head necrosis, one case of plate loosening, and one case of subacromial impingement. No cases of screw loosening, screw penetration through the humeral head, bone resorption of the greater or lesser tuberosity, or humeral head necrosis were observed. The intramedullary nailing group had no reported cases of humeral head varus, plate or screw loosening, subacromial impingement, screw penetration, tuberosity bone resorption, or humeral head necrosis.

Discussion

Double plating internal fixation for proximal humeral fractures is a commonly used surgical approach, offering direct fracture reduction and improved shoulder function. However, it involves extensive soft tissue dissection, reducing tissue continuity, compromising blood supply at the fracture site, and leading to a higher complication rate that may impact postoperative recovery. In the present study, no subacromial impingement was observed in the intramedullary nailing group, which we attribute to careful selection of a medial entry point at the humeral head footprint and strict control of nail depth 5–7 mm below the articular cartilage surface.

In the present series, double plating was mainly used for fractures with medial column deficiency or markedly poor bone quality, including some Neer two-part fractures with disruption of the medial hinge and elderly patients with severely comminuted three- or four-part fractures. Although arthroplasty is recommended by many authors for elderly patients with severely comminuted fractures, a substantial proportion of our patients declined prosthetic replacement for economic, cultural or functional reasons. In such circumstances, we attempted joint-preserving fixation using double plating after detailed informed consent. Consequently, the double-plating group inherently contained a higher proportion of more complex fractures with severe medial comminution, whereas the intramedullary nailing group tended to include relatively less complex patterns with preserved medial support. This indication-driven allocation introduces unavoidable selection bias and limits the strict comparability of the two cohorts. This context should be kept in mind when interpreting the higher surgical trauma and complication rates observed in the double-plating group.

Intramedullary nailing offers several advantages [4–6]: (1) minimally invasive implantation with smaller incisions, reduced blood loss, and less joint adhesion while preserving humeral head blood supply; (2) no requirement for extensive soft tissue stripping, shorter lever arm, reduced impact on proximal humeral structures, and lower risk of rotator cuff injury or humeral head varus; and (3) central fixation through straight nails, enhancing medial bone stability. A meta‑analysis based on 20 clinical studies involving 1,384 patients with proximal humeral fractures demonstrated that intramedullary nailing resulted in smaller incisions, shorter perioperative bleeding, shorter surgical duration, and faster fracture healing compared to locked plating [7]. However, intramedullary nailing has a recognized learning curve, and inadequate entry point selection or nail depth may increase the risk of complications; therefore, this technique should be performed by surgeons familiar with the procedure.

Our study suggests that, within our indication spectrum, intramedullary nailing was associated with shorter surgical duration, reduced intraoperative blood loss, better shoulder function scores and fewer complications compared with double plating, thereby facilitating postoperative recovery. Nevertheless, because the choice of fixation was not randomized and surgeons tended to select double plating for more complex fractures, these findings may in part reflect selection bias and should be interpreted with caution.

Double plating provides enhanced stability in complex fractures, particularly in comminuted medial column fractures, but is associated with greater soft tissue dissection and higher complication rates, necessitating careful patient selection. In addition, although the distribution of Neer fracture types was similar between our two groups, it is possible that the double‑plating group included a higher proportion of fractures with unrecorded factors such as severe metaphyseal bone loss or head‑split components, which could also have contributed to the higher complication rate. Intramedullary nailing, with its advantages of central fixation, minimally invasive exposure, shorter surgical duration, and reduced blood loss, may be better suited for elderly and osteoporotic patients when the medial column remains relatively preserved [8].

In our series, the mean cervicodiaphyseal angle at final follow-up was more valgus in the double-plating group (143.26° ± 2.86°) than in the intramedullary nailing group (135.26° ± 3.57°). A neck-shaft angle of approximately 135° is generally considered close to the physiological alignment of the proximal humerus, whereas a larger angle in the double-plating group likely reflects an intentional valgus reduction strategy to counteract secondary varus collapse in osteoporotic bone. However, this overcorrection did not translate into superior functional outcomes and may even predispose some patients to subacromial impingement when the greater tuberosity is elevated excessively. In our cohort, three patients in the double-plating group still developed humeral head varus despite initial valgus reduction, underscoring the technical challenges of achieving and maintaining a physiological cervicodiaphyseal angle in complex osteoporotic fractures.

Another point raised by the reviewers concerns the duration of antibiotic prophylaxis. During the study period, our institutional practice for elderly patients with osteoporotic proximal humeral fractures was to administer intravenous antibiotics for up to three days because of concerns about postoperative infection in this frail population. We have clarified this protocol in the Methods section. We also acknowledge that many current guidelines recommend limiting prophylaxis to 24 h for clean orthopedic procedures, and this discrepancy between our practice and guideline recommendations represents a limitation of the present study [9].

In conclusion, within the limits of this retrospective single-center study and its specific indication spectrum, intramedullary nailing for the treatment of proximal humeral fractures was associated with shorter surgical time, reduced intraoperative blood loss, faster fracture healing and better functional scores compared with double plating in our real-world cohort. However, because double plating in our series was primarily reserved for the most complex fractures with severe medial comminution or very poor bone quality, these findings should not be extrapolated to all fracture patterns. Double plating remains indispensable for those complex cases in which joint-preserving fixation is pursued and prosthetic replacement is not clearly chosen. Future studies with larger sample sizes, rigorous control of fracture patterns and surgeon-related factors, and multi-center prospective designs are required to further clarify the long-term comparative efficacy of these two fixation techniques.

Limitation

A major limitation of this study is the non-randomized, indication-based allocation of patients to double plating or intramedullary nailing. As emphasised above, double plating was reserved for fractures with severe medial column deficiency and very poor bone quality in patients in whom arthroplasty was not clearly chosen, whereas intramedullary nailing was typically used for fractures with relatively preserved medial support. As a result, the two groups do not represent strictly equivalent fracture populations, and the observed superiority of intramedullary nailing across several perioperative and functional parameters may partly reflect underlying differences in fracture complexity rather than the implants themselves. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as a pragmatic, real-world comparison of two indication-based strategies in a single institution, and not as definitive evidence that intramedullary nailing is universally preferable to double plating in all proximal humeral fractures. Furthermore, no a priori sample size calculation or power analysis was performed; instead, we included all eligible patients treated during the study period. As a result, the study may be underpowered to detect differences in relatively infrequent events such as specific complications, and the findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Conclusion

For proximal humeral fractures in a real-world, indication-based setting, intramedullary nailing was associated with less surgical trauma and better short- to mid-term functional outcomes than double plating in appropriately selected fractures with relatively preserved medial support. Double plating, on the other hand, provides higher stability in complex fractures with severe medial column deficiency but is associated with greater surgical trauma and a higher risk of complications. Therefore, our results do not indicate that intramedullary nailing is universally superior to double plating for all proximal humeral fractures; rather, they suggest that intramedullary nailing is an effective option within its specific indication spectrum, while double plating remains necessary for complex fractures requiring enhanced medial support. Clinical decision-making should remain individualized, and our findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, pending confirmation in larger prospective and randomized studies.
