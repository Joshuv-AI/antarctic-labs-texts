A single dose of an ALVAC vector-based RABV virus-like particle candidate vaccine induces a potent immune response in mice, cats and dogs
Authors: Xianyong Meng, Feihu Yan, Weiqi Wang, Shen Wang, Haiyang Cong, Jiaqi Li, Yongkun Zhao, Tiecheng Wang, Nan Li, Yuwei Gao, Jianzhong Wang, Na Feng, Xianzhu Xia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRabies, caused by the Rabies virus (RABV), is a highly fatal zoonotic disease. Existing rabies vaccines have demonstrated good immune efficacy, but the complexity of immunization procedures and high cost has impeded the elimination of RABV, particularly in the post-COVID-19 era. There is a pressing need for safer and more effective rabies vaccines that streamline vaccination protocols and reduce expense. To meet this need, we have developed a potential rabies vaccine candidate called ALVAC-RABV-VLP, utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology. This vaccine employs a canarypox virus vector (ALVAC) to generate RABV virus-like particles (VLPs). In mice, a single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP effectively activated dendritic cells (DCs), follicular helper T cells (Tfh), and the germinal centre (GC)/plasma cell axis, resulting in durable and effective humoral immune responses. The survival rate of mice challenged with lethal RABV was 100%. Similarly, in dogs and cats, a single immunization with ALVAC-RABV-VLP elicited a stronger and longer-lasting antibody response. ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced superior cellular and humoral immunity in both mice and beagles compared to the commercial inactivated rabies vaccine. In conclusion, ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced robust protective immune responses in mice, dogs and cats, offering a novel, cost-effective, efficient, and promising approach for herd prevention of rabies.

Introduction

Rabies, caused by RABV, is a fatal zoonotic disease that poses a threat to public health. Once symptomatic, the mortality rate approaches 100% even with advanced supportive care [1,2]. Globally, the economic cost of canine-mediated rabies is estimated to be $8.6 billion, including productivity losses resulting from mortality, costs associated with post-exposure prevention, and direct expenses for the healthcare sector and bite victims [3,4]. Despite these substantial costs, RABV still causes approximately 60,000 deaths worldwide each year. Moreover, more than 150 countries and a population of 17 million people are at risk of exposure to RABV [2,5].

In 2015, a global strategic plan was launched by a coalition of international organizations led by the World Health Organization (WHO), with the aim of eliminating dog-mediated human rabies deaths worldwide by 2030 [6]. Currently, most commercially available vaccines are inactivated vaccines that require 2–5 doses administered via injections. Although inactivated vaccines offer sufficient immune protection, their use is limited due to the uncertainty and high cost associated with adjuvants. The lengthy immunization procedures and expenses associated with these vaccines hinder their widespread use in both domestic and wild animals, particularly in developing countries [7,8]. On the other hand, attenuated live vaccines have the potential to confer long-lasting immunity with a single dose. However, safety concerns still exist. An example of a vaccine that addresses these issues is the V-RG vaccine, which utilizes cowpox as a vector to express the RABV G protein. This vaccine has been successfully employed for rabies control [9]. However, some patients still exhibit symptoms characteristic of poxvirus infection after receiving this vaccine, highlighting the need to improve the safety of using cowpox virus as a vaccine carrier [10]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a new low-dose, safe, and effective rabies vaccine.

The ALVAC vaccine is derived from a canarypox virus clone that has underwent four rounds of plaque purification. The recombinant ALVAC is non-replicative in mammals due to its abortive infection in mammalian cells. However, it can express antigenic products that stimulate the immune system, leading to both antibody-mediated and cell-mediated immune responses, even without productive viral replication [11]. Several ALVAC-based vaccines have been developed and licensed for veterinary use against a variety of viruses, including canine distemper virus, feline leukemia virus, West Nile virus, and equine influenza virus. These vaccines have shown to be safe for both hosts and the environment [12–14]. Traditionally, constructing recombinant DNA viruses relied on homologous recombination, a time-consuming and labour-intensive process. To improve efficiency, we have developed a highly efficient strategy for constructing recombinant ALVAC using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology [15].

Virus-like particles (VLPs) are highly structured protein particles assembled from viral proteins that mimic original virus structure. As a subunit vaccine, VLPs can effectively stimulate both T-and B-cell-mediated immune responses [16]. VLPs containing RABV G and M protein, promotes the production of virus neutralizing antibodies (VNAs), thereby enhancing immunogenicity [17]. The production of VLPs using a poxvirus vector is a promising strategy for vaccine construction. Studies on Ebola virus (EBOV) and other virus-like particles have demonstrated their positive immune effects [18], indicating that packaging VLPs with poxvirus expression can significantly improve immunogenicity.

In this study, we constructed a recombinant canarypox virus named ALVAC-RABV-VLP, which co-expressing the G and M proteins of RABV using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. When cells were infected with ALVAC-RABV-VLP, RABV VLPs self-assembled in situ. In mice, a single dose of the ALVAC-RABV-VLP vaccine effectively induced a robust immune response and provided long-lasting protection against a lethal RABV challenge. Additionally, we compared the immunogenicity of a single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP with that of a commercially available inactivated vaccine in both dogs and cats. Our results demonstrated that ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced significantly higher levels of VNAs with longer-lasting effects compared to the inactivated vaccine in dogs and cats. These findings suggest that ALVAC-RABV-VLP holds promise as an effective rabies vaccine.

Methods and materials

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Changchun Institute of Veterinary Medicine, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (approval number: IACUC AMMS-11-2022-041).

Cells, viruses and genes

CEF cells were obtained from 9–11-day-old SPF chicken embryos and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Sigma, Germany) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Gibco, USA). The cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. DF-1 cells (ATCC® CRL-12203™) were also maintained in the laboratory. Notably, all cells were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. ALVAC-CDV-F-H, derived from Purevax® Ferret Distemper (Merial, USA), was previously generated [15]. HuNPB3, a RABV street strain, was isolated from a pig that died of rabies in the Hunan Province of China in 2006 and has been stored in our laboratory [17]. RABV G and M were amplified from plasmid pCI-rERA, which contains the full-length of 1, 1932 nucleotides cDNA of the ERA strain [19], for construction of the canarypox-based recombinant virus ALVAC-RABV-VLP.

Construction of the gRNA-guided Cas9 system

The target RNA sequences for the C6 ORF of ALVAC were selected according to the general principle. Six pairs of gRNA oligos were synthesized, and the two most efficient gRNA oligos were chosen (data not shown). These gRNA sequences were annealed and subsequently cloned and inserted into the Cas9 working vector. The DNA template sequences of this gRNA with extra ends for cloning were as follows: C6gRNA-1-Sense: 5’-CTCTTAGTCGCCTAACCGTCTCAAGGATC-3’. C6gRNA-1-Anti: 5’-CTCTAAAA CGATCCTTGAGACGGTTAGGC-3’. And C6gRNA-2-sense: 5’-CTCTTAGTCGCCCACTTTTGAACTCC GGA-3’. C6gRNA-2-Anti: 5’-CTCTAAAACTCCGGAGTTCAAAAGTGGGC-3’.

Construction of the repair donor vector

The repair donor vector was constructed to the target the C6 ORF of ALVAC for homologous recombination (HDR), commonly achieved by deleting the C6 ORF and inserting the desired gene. In this study, a cassette was synthesized containing the C6 left arm, 42 K promoters, enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) gene, multiple cloning sites (MCSs), and the C6 right arm (Figure 1). This cassette was cloned into the pCAGGS vector to create the repair donor vector. To obtain the RABV gene with a homology arm, PCR was performed using specific primers. The PCR products were then cloned into the MCSs of the cassette, resulting in the final construct, pCA-C6-RABV-GM. The primer sequence with an extra end for cloning is as follows: RABV-G-F: 5’-AAACAGCTGGGGCCCGCGGCCGCGCCACCATGGTT CCTCAGGCTCTCCTGT-3’. Generation and plaque purification of recombinant virus

ALVAC-RABV-VLP recombinants were generated using a type II CRISPR-Cas system with an RNA-guided Cas9 endonuclease. Initially, the Cas9-C6 gRNA plasmid was transfected into DF-1 cells using Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Thermo, USA). After 24 h, the cells were infected with 5 PFU of ALVAC-CDV-F-H. Subsequently, the pCA-C6-RABV-GM plasmid was transfected into the DF-1 cells that had already been infected with the parental virus using Lipofectamine™ 3000. After 48 h, the recombinant virus ALVAC-RABV-VLP was rescued from DF-1 cells and subsequently purified through plaque assays. Following additional passages in CEF cells, the resulting recombinant virus ALVAC-RABV-VLP reached a titre of 107 TCID50/mL.

Genome verification of the recombinant virus

To confirm the presence of RABV G and M genes in the recombinant ALVAC-RABV-VLP and replacement the original CDV H and F gene, genomic DNA was extracted from purified ALVAC-RABV-VLP using a nucleic acid purification kit. PCR amplification was performed using specific primers for CDV H and F, as well as specific primers for RABV G and M. The PCR products representing the insertion site of the foreign gene in the recombinant virus were then sequenced. Recombinant strains with accurate sequencing results were retained for subsequent experiments. The CDV primer sequences used for PCR were as follows: CDV-H-F: 5’-ATGCTCCCCTACCAAGACAAG-3’. CDV-H-R: 5’-TTAACGGTTACATGAGAATCTTA-3’. CDV-F-F: 5’-ATGCAC AAGGGAATCCCCAA-3’. CDV-F-R: 5’-TCAGTGTGATCTCACATAGGATT-3’.

Western blot analysis

For western blot analysis, CEF cells were infected with recombinant viruses ALVAC-RABV-VLP at a MOI of 1 for 24 h. Afterward, the cell lysates were collected and subjected to electrophoresis on 10% SDS-PAGE polyacrylamide gels. The proteins were then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes for immunoblotting. A rabbit anti-G protein antibody (Sigma, USA) was used at a 1:2,000 dilution, while M protein antibodies (produced and stored in our laboratory) were used at a 1:1,000 dilution. HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit IgG (Bioworlde, USA) was used at a 1:25,000 ratio. The bands were visualized using SuperSignal™ West Atto (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Indirect immunofluorescence

For indirect immunofluorescence analysis, CEF cells were infected with the recombinant virus ALVAC-RABV-VLP and fixed at 24 h post-infection for staining. The primary antibody used was the same as that for western blotting, while Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated donkey anti-mouse or donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) served as the secondary antibody. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma, Germany). The stained cells were visualized using an EVOS M5000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Invitrogen, USA).

Production of RABV VLPs from cells infected with the recombinant ALVAC-RABV-VLP

CEF cells were infected with the recombinant ALVAC-RABV-VLP at a MOI of 1. The cell supernatant was collected 24 h post-infection. After removing the cell debris, the supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h. The resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS and loaded onto sucrose gradients of 20%−40% and 40%−60% w/v. After centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 h, the sample was separated from the sucrose cushions. The sucrose was then removed by another centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 h. Finally, the sample was resuspended in an appropriate volume of cold PBS. The collected Rabies VLPs were stained with 1% phosphotungstic acid and observed using a transmission electron microscopy (JEM-1200EXII; Japanese Electronics Corporation, Japan).

Immunogenicity and protective efficacy in animals

To assess the immunogenicity and protective capacity of ALVAC-RABV-VLP, 6-8-week-old female BALB/c mice (n = 22) were immunized intramuscularly with 107 TCID50 of ALVAC-RABV-VLP, an inactivated vaccine was not less than 106.3 FAID50/mL before inactivation. (Boehringer Ingelheim Vetmedica, Rabvac®3, HCP-SAD), or PBS as a negative control. The body temperature (measured rectally) and body weight of the animals were monitored for one week following the immunization. After immunization, lymph nodes, bone marrow, or spleens (n = 3) were collected from the inguinal lymph nodes, bone marrow, or spleen at 3, 7, and 14 days post immunization (dpi) for flow cytometry and ELISpot detection. Blood was collected, and sera were separated for VNAs and antibody subtype detection for 6 months (n = 8). 2 weeks after immunization, 5 the mice from each group were challenged with a 10 × 50% lethal dose (LD50) of HuNPB3 through intracranial injection. The mice were then observed for 10 days, and any mice that developed clinical signs of rabies during the observation period were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia. Additionally, brain histopathological sections were analyzed using immunohistochemistry (IHC). The rates of IHC-positive cells were quantified using Aipathwell® automated analysis software (Servicebio®). Three groups of beagles and cats aged twelve months (n = 3) were immunized intramuscularly with 107 TCID50 ALVAC-RABV-VLP, the inactivated vaccine Rabvac®3, or PBS, respectively. The blood samples were collected weekly and subjected to FAVN assay.

Serum neutralization assay

The RABV VNAs titre was measured by a fluorescent antibody virus neutralization (FAVN) assay, as previously described [20]. Briefly, 96-well plates were prepared by dispensing 100 μL of serial three-fold dilutions of inactivated serum into each well. Then, 50 μL of a suspension containing 100 focus-forming units (FFUs) of RABV was added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Next, 2 × 104 BSR-T7/5 cells were added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 72 h. After incubation, the samples were fixed with 80% ice-cold acetone for 30 min and stained with FITC-conjugated antibodies against the RABV N protein. Fluorescence was observed using an Olympus IX51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The fluorescence values were compared with those of a reference serum obtained from the National Institute for Biological Standards and Control in Hertfordshire, UK. The results were normalized and quantified in international units per millilitre (IU/mL).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

To detect RABV G-specific IgG and its subtypes, an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed. Briefly, 96-well polystyrene plates were coated with purified and inactivated RABV overnight at 4°C. After washing the plates three times with PBST, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma, Germany) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Mouse serum was then added at multiple dilutions and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. The plate was washed three times with PBST and then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Bioworld, USA), IgG1, or IgG2a antibodies (Bio-Techne, China) for 45 min at 37°C. After washing three times with PBST, 3,3’,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Sigma, Germany) was added for colour development. The reaction was stopped by adding 2 mol/L H2SO4 at the appropriate time, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a Bio-Rad reader (USA).

Immunohistochemistry

Brain tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and paraffin sections 3–5 μm in thickness were prepared. For immunohistochemistry, the paraffin-embedded tissue was quenched in a 3% hydrogen peroxide aqueous solution for 30 min. The sections were then incubated with a primary antibody, rabbit anti-Rabies G antibody (1:2000), which was prepared and stored in our laboratory, for 30 min. After washing the sections with PBS three times, they were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 20 min. Following another round of washing, a colour development solution containing diaminobenzidine (DAB) was applied. The positive area appeared brownish yellow, and the colour development was stopped by rinsing the slices with tap water. Hematoxylin was used to stain the nuclei, which appeared blue.

Flow cytometry assay

Flow cytometry was employed to assess the activation percentages of various cell types in different lymphoid tissues. Mouse lymph nodes were analyzed for DC cell activation at 3 dpi, B cell activation at 7 dpi, Tfh cell activation at 7 dpi, and GCB cell activation at 7 dpi. Additionally, plasma cells in the bone marrow were examined at 7 dpi, while B cells and T cells in the spleen were analyzed at 7 and 14 dpi. To obtain individual cells, the collected tissue was ground and dispersed through a 100-nm nylon filter. The cells were washed twice with PBS containing 0.2% bovine serum albumin (BSA; wt/vol) and then treated with a blocking solution containing CD16/32 (Thermo Scientific, USA) for 20 min, followed by two additional washes. Subsequently, a total of 106 cells were stained with fluorescently labelled antibodies for 30 min and then washed again. Finally, the stained cells were analyzed using a FACSVerse (Thermo Scientific, USA) instrument. The mouse antibodies used, including CD11c (FITC, N418), CD80 (PerCP-eFluor™ 710, 16-10A1), CD86 (PE, GL1), CD40 (APC, 1C10), GL-7 (PE, GL-7 (GL7)), PD-1 (PE, J43), CD69 (PE, H1.2F3), CD38 (PerCP-eFluor™ 710, 90), and CD62L (APC-eFluor™ 780, MEL-14), were obtained from Thermo Scientific. The antibodies against MHC II (APC/Cyanine7, M5/114.15.2), CD45R (FITC, RA3-6B2), CD95 (APC, SA367H8), CD4 (FITC, GK1.5), CXCR5 (APC, L138D7), CD8 (PE/Cyanine7, 53-6.7), CD19 (APC/Cyanine7, 1D3/CD19), CD138 (PE, 281-2), and CD3 (APC/Cyanine7, 145-2C11) were obtained from Biolegend.

Canine peripheral blood was collected using heparin anticoagulant tubes, and lymphocytes were isolated using a specialized kit (Solarbio, P8730). The staining protocol for DCs and T cells was the similar to that for mice. For Treg cells, after staining for CD4 and CD25 on the membrane surface, the cells were fixed in the dark at 4°C for 20 min using a fixation and permeabilization solution (Thermo Scientific, USA). The cells were then washed twice with a permeabilization buffer. Subsequently, the cells were stained with FOXP3 and washed again. Finally, the stained cells were analyzed using a FACSVerse instrument (Thermo Scientific, USA). The dog antibodies used included CD3 (BD, APC, LSM 8.358), CD4 (BD, PE, LSM 12.125), CD8 (BD, FITC, LSM 1.140), CD11c (APC-eFluor™ 780, BU15), CD80 (PE, 16-10A1), MHC II (APC, YKIX334.2), CD25 (PE-Cyanine7, P4A10), and FOXP3 (APC, FJK-16s), all of which were obtained from Thermo Scientific.

ELISPOT

An ELISPOT detection kit (Mabtech, Sweden) was used to measure the levels of IFN-γ and IL-4. Following the manufacturer's recommended method, 5 × 105 splenic cells were seeded into a 96-well plate and stimulated with 10 μg/mL protein for 36 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. Concanavalin (Thermo Scientific, USA) served as a positive control. The plate was then scanned using an ImmunoSpot reader, and the ImmunoSpot software was used to count specific spots. The number of specific spots/pores had to be at least twice the average value found in each negative control well after subtracting the background value.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni's multiple comparison tests. All P values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. ∗∗∗∗, P < 0.0001; ∗∗∗, P < 0.001; ∗∗, P < 0.01; ∗, P < 0.05.

Results

Generation and characterization of ALVAC-RABV-VLP

To develop a safe and efficient RABV vaccine, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology to construct the recombinant ALVAC-vectored vaccine expressing RABV VLPs (Figure 1A). The reconstruction of the ALVAC vector involved designing gRNA targeting the ALVAC C6 ORF and constructing repair donor vectors. The repair donor vector contained eGFP and the RABV G and M genes driven by the 42 K promoter, flanked by homologous sequences adjacent to the C6 domain (Figure 1B). The recombinant virus ALVAC-RABV-VLP was generated by employing CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing and conducting subsequent plaque screening. PCR analysis and sequencing validation were performed to confirm the successful replacement of the C6 site in the ALVAC vector with RABV G/M (data not shown). After infecting CEF cells with the recombinant ALVAC-RABV-VLP carrying the RABV G and M genes, the expression of the RABV G and M proteins was detected using western blot and indirect immunofluorescence techniques after 24 h (Figure 1C, D). To further examine the assembly of RABV VLPs, we purified the supernatant from ALVAC-RABV-VLP-infected CEF cells and performed thin-section transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis. TEM revealed the presence of bullet-shaped RABV VLPs with a diameter of approximately 75 nm and a length of 180 nm (Figure 1E). In conclusion, this study successfully constructed a recombinant ALVAC strain carrying the RABV G and M genes using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology. Furthermore, the co-expressed G and M proteins were successfully assembled into RABV VLPs in the infected CEF cells.

A single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP safely induced potent antibody in mice

To investigate the immune response triggered by ALVAC-RABV-VLP, female BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks were immunized with ALVAC-RABV-VLP, a commercial inactivated vaccine, or an equivalent volume of PBS. Serum samples were collected at various time points to evaluate the titres of RABV VNAs and G-specific antibodies (Figure 2A). The safety of the vaccines was assessed by monitoring the body weight and temperature of the immunized mice before and one week after vaccination. The results showed that the body weight of the mice immunized with ALVAC-RABV-VLP slightly decreased compared to the control group (PBS) at the first 2–4 dpi, but these changes were less pronounced than in the commercial vaccine group. By 5 dpi, the body weight of the ALVAC-RABV-VLP group had stabilized, aligned with that of the control group (Figure 2B). The body temperature of mice in both the ALVAC-RABV-VLP and commercial vaccine groups briefly increased on the first day post-immunization. However, in the ALVAC-RABV-VLP group, it gradually returned to normal levels consistent with the control group, while in the commercial vaccine group, the temperature remained elevated throughout the 5-day observation period (Figure 2C). The results demonstrated that mice vaccinated with ALVAC-RABV-VLP developed VNAs above the protective threshold of 0.5 IU/mL within one week, with a peak of 9.33 IU/mL at the third week. Protective antibodies were detected in the second week after vaccination with the inactivated vaccines, reaching a peak level of 2.53 IU/mL by the fourth week (Figure 2-D). In a separate experiment, mice were immunized with three different dilutions of the ALVAC-RABV-VLP vaccine. The results showed a positive correlation between the VNAs levels and the immunization dose (Supplementary Figure 1). Specifically, mice inoculated with ALVAC-RABV-VLP produced more than 0.5 IU/mL of VNAs within 1 week, with a peak of 9.33 IU/mL at the third week. In contrast, protective antibodies were not detected until the second week after vaccination with the inactivated vaccines, reaching a peak level of 2.53 IU/mL by the fourth week (Figure 2D). Moreover,, ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced significantly earlier and higher levels of specific IgG compared to the inactivated vaccine during the 1–4 week post-immunization period, consistent with the VNAs assay results (Figure 2E). Furthermore, analysis of the IgG subtypes revealed that both vaccines elicited immune responses dominated by IgG1, while ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced titres of both IgG1 and IgG2a that were more than double those induced by the inactivated vaccine (Figure 2F-H). To determine the duration of antibody induction by ALVAC-RABV-VLP, we monitored the antibody levels for up to six months. Remarkably, even at the sixth month postimmunization, the level of VNAs induced by ALVAC-RABV-VLP remained at 3.5 IU/mL, which was significantly higher than the minimum protective titre of 0.5 IU/mL (Figure 2I). ALVAC-RABV-VLP induces mice DCs activation at an early stage

To investigate the relationship between the rapid induction of antibody production by ALVAC-RABV-VLP and early activation of the innate immune system, flow cytometry analysis was conducted on the draining lymph nodes (dLNs) of mice 3 dpi to determine the proportions of CD11c+ CD80+ DCs, CD11c+ CD86+ DCs, and CD11c+ MHC II+ DCs. Compared with those in the negative control group, Both ALVAC-RABV-VLP and the inactivated vaccine significantly stimulated the expression of the DCs surface costimulatory molecules CD86 and CD80 (Figure 3A, B), and the T-cell costimulatory molecule MHC II (Figure 3C), in comparison to the negative control group. Notably, the activation of DCs by ALVAC-RABV-VLP was significantly greater than that induced by the inactivated vaccine, particularly in terms of the proportions of CD11c+ CD86+ DCs and CD11c+ MHC II+ DCs, which were twice as high as those in the inactivated vaccine group (Figure 3B, C). These findings suggested that ALVAC-RABV-VLP exhibits superior recruitment and activation efficiency for DCs during the early stages, emphasizing its ability to rapidly activate innate immune responses. ALVAC-RABV-VLP induces a robust germinal centre response

Antibody secretion is closely associated with the germinal centre response, where initially activated B cells and helper T cells migrate toward the follicular edge. The B-cell receptor recognizes antigens presented by antigen-presenting cells, leading to the formation of antigenic peptide MHC II-like molecular complexes. Subsequently, these complexes activate Tfh cells, and GCB cells differentiate into plasma cells [21]. To assess the activation of B cells, we analyzed the proportion of activated B cells in the dLNs and spleens at 7 dpi. Both vaccines induced a significantly higher proportion of activated B cells in the dLNs and spleens compared to the negative control group Compared to the negative control group, the proportions of activated B cells in the dLNs and spleens of the mice immunized with both vaccines were significantly higher (Figure 4A, B). Moreover, ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced a higher proportion of activated B cells in dLNs compared to the inactivated vaccine, while no significant difference was observed in the spleen (Figure 4A, B), suggesting that ALVAC-RABV-VLP more efficiently recruited and activated B cells in dLNs. Additionally, we performed analyses of Tfh, GCB, and plasma cell populations, which are directly involved in antibody secretion. Flow cytometry results demonstrated that the proportion of Tfh and GCB cells induced by ALVAC-RABV-VLP was more than twice as high as that in the inactivated vaccine group (Figure 4C, D). Furthermore, the proportion of plasma cells in the bone marrow after ALVAC-RABV-VLP immunization was significantly greater than that in the inactivated vaccine group (Figure 4E). These findings collectively suggested that compared with the inactivated vaccine, ALVAC-RABV-VLP elicits a robust humoral immune response. To compare the cellular immune responses induced by different vaccines, spleen lymphocytes from mice were isolated from mice at 14 dpi, and flow cytometry and ELISPOT assays were performed to evaluate the proportion of activated T cells and the expression of IFN-γ, respectively. The flow cytometry results demonstrated that ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced a greater proportion of activated T cells, including both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, compared to the inactivated vaccine group (Figure 5A, B), indicating that ALVAC-RABV-VLP has a greater ability to activate T cells. Furthermore, the ELISPOT analysis revealed that a single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced significantly greater expression of both IL-4 and IFN-γ compared to the inactivated vaccine group (Figure 5E, F). Specifically, the geometric mean value of IFN-γ induced by ALVAC-RABV-VLP was 1, 368 spot-forming units (SFUs)/2.5 × 105 lymphocytes, whereas the inactivated vaccine induced only 729 SFUs (Figure 5F). To assess the ability of ALVAC-RABV-VLP to induce long-lasting memory T cells, the percentage of memory T cells was measured at 60 dpi using flow cytometry. Single-dose immunization with ALVAC-RABV-VLP provides full protection for mice against a lethal RABV challenge

To investigate the protective effect of a single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP, mice were immunized with ALVAC-RABV-VLP, the inactivated vaccine, or PBS. At 14 dpi, the mice were intracranially challenged with the street RABV strain HuNPB3a at a 10× LD50. Throughout the 10-day observation period, all control mice and two mice in the inactivated vaccine group died of viral infection within 8 days (Figure 6A). In contrast, all the mice in the ALVAC-RABV-VLP-immunized group survived the infection (Figure 6A). In terms of body weight, the immunized mice initially experienced a decrease, which gradually recovered after virus infection. The change in body weight of the mice immunized with ALVAC-RABV-VLP was slightest (Figure 6B). Body temperature monitoring revealed that virus infection led to a temporary increase in body temperature, followed by a decrease, with the immune group showing gradual recovery after 7 days. Additionally, the ALVAC-RABV-VLP immunization group exhibited a faster recovery rate than the group that received the inactivated vaccine (Figure 6C). Immunohistochemical analysis of mouse brains at 5 days post challenge revealed that both ALVAC-RABV-VLP and inactivated vaccine immunization led to significantly reduced RABV G protein deposition. However, the percentage of G protein-positive cells after ALVAC-RABV-VLP immunization was approximately 10%, indicating a greater ability to inhibit RABV replication (Figure 6D, E). These results demonstrated that a single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP provides 100% protection against a lethal dose challenge while effectively reducing the viral load in the brain. A single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP can stimulate dendritic cells and T cells in dogs

Building upon the promising immunogenicity and protection demonstrated in mice, we further investigated the stimulating effects of ALVAC-RABV-VLP in dogs. Twelve-month-old beagles were immunized with ALVAC-RABV-VLP or an inactivated vaccine, while the control group received PBS. At 7 dpi, we analyzed the changes in DCs, T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the peripheral blood of the beagles. The results showed that ALVAC-RABV-VLP stimulated a significantly higher proportion of activated DCs compared to the inactivated vaccine (Figure 7A, B). Moreover, ALVAC-RABV-VLP had a stronger stimulatory effect on T cells, particularly with an increase in the proportion of CD8+ T cells, which was 1.6-fold higher than that observed in the inactivated vaccine group (Figure 7C, D). In contrast, the proportion of CD8+ T cells induced by the inactivated vaccine did not differ significantly from the PBS group (Figure 7D), indicating a lack of significant CD8+ T-cell activation by the inactivated vaccine. Additionally, ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced greater numbers of Tregs compared to the inactivated vaccine (Figure 7E). In conclusion, these findings suggest that ALVAC-RABV-VLP elicits robust cellular and humoral immune responses in beagles while also promoting the upregulation of Tregs to prevent immune imbalance caused by excessive immunity. A single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP elicits high levels of VNAs in dogs and cats

The level of VNAs is crucial for protection against rabies. To assess the VNAs induced by ALVAC-RABV-VLP in dogs and cats, serum samples were collected weekly over a 6-week period. The results revealed that ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced significantly higher levels of VNAs compared to the inactivated vaccine. Specifically, the ALVAC-RABV-VLP-immunized group exhibited antibody levels exceeding 0.5 IU/mL during the first week, reaching a peak in the third week (38.28 IU/mL), then experiencing a slight decrease while remaining significantly higher than 0.5 IU/mL (Figure 8A). In contrast, no antibodies were detected in any of the dogs in the control group. Similar to the study conducted in dogs, twelve-month-old cats were also immunized with the ALVAC-RABV-VLP vaccine. The results showed that the level of VNAs in the ALVAC-RABV-VLP group reached 5.6 IU/mL within the first week post-immunization. Furthermore, VNAs levels peaked at 29.1 IU/mL by the second week in this group (Figure 8B). In contrast, the commercial vaccine group took three weeks to reach the peak antibody titre of 16.8 IU/mL, which was lower than the peak observed in the ALVAC-RABV-VLP group (Figure 8B). These findings clearly demonstrated that the ALVAC-RABV-VLP vaccine is more effective in inducing VNAs in both dogs and cats compared to the commercial inactivated vaccine. Discussion

The lack of effective treatment options and the near 100% mortality rate following the onset of rabies highlight the importance of vaccination as the primary solution to this problem. The existing immunization strategy is costly, posing a challenge to achieving the goal of eliminating human rabies by 2030 [6,22,23]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a novel rabies vaccine that is cost-effective, requires only a single dose, and offers strong efficacy and immunity, enabling large-scale immunization. In this study, we successfully developed a vaccine for generating RABV VLPs based on the ALVAC vector. Our findings demonstrate that a single dose of ALVAC-RABV-VLP induces robust humoral and cellular immunity in mice and dogs and provides complete protection against a lethal RABV challenge in mice.

VLPs have been widely used as an effective vaccine construction strategy. Coexpression of rabies virus VLPs with membrane-anchored flagellin (EVLP-F) resulted in 100% protection against rabies virus, whereas VLPs alone provided only 75% protection [17]. In phase III clinical trials, rabies virus VLPs demonstrated a high seroprotection rate of 99.24%, highlighting their potency [24,25]. However, despite their superior effectiveness compared to control vaccines, achieving 100% protection remains elusive. This suggests that rabies VLPs may need to be combined with other adjuvants or stimulators to elicit even better immunity and complete protection. In this study, we propose a novel approach that utilizes the CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing method to modify the ALVAC vector, successfully producing rabies VLPs by co-expressing the G and M proteins. Previous studies using modified vaccinia virus ankara (MVA) vectors to express VLP vaccines against pathogenic viruses, such as Marburg virus [26], EBOV [18], and Lassa virus [27] have demonstrated their excellent immunogenicity. This highlights the potential of using poxvirus vectors to construct VLP vaccine strategies. VLPs constructed with ALVAC vectors offer several advantages over conventional VLPs, including enhanced posttranslational modification of proteins and elimination of the need for additional adjuvants. Furthermore, ALVAC-RABV-VLP can not only stimulate the immune system similarly to the parental vector virus but it also produces VLPs that structurally resemble authentic RABV particles. This facilitates early antigen recognition and endocytosis processes [28]. A previous report described the construction of a recombinant ALVAC expressing the glycoprotein of RABV, which induced neutralizing antibody levels of 8.15 IU/mL in dogs and 7.525 IU/mL in cats two weeks post-immunization with 107 TCID50 of the virus [29]. In contrast, in the current study, dogs and cats immunized with the recombinant ALVAC-RABV-VLP developed significantly higher neutralizing antibody titres of 38.28 and 29.1 IU/mL respectively at the same time. Although the immunization methods differed between the studies, these results suggest that the virus-like particle (VLP) formulation may offer distinct advantages in eliciting a more potent immune response compared to the expression of the RABV glycoprotein alone. In conclusion, ALVAC-RABV-VLP enhances the uptake, processing, and presentation of antigens by antigen-presenting cells through cross-presentation of MHC I and MHC II，leading to a robust immune response and 100% protection against lethal rabies challenge in mice.

ALVAC-RABV-VLP offers a unique combination of advantages that are highly desirable for countries with a high incidence of rabies. A single immunization with ALVAC-RABV-VLP provides long-term and efficient protection. In our study, we observed significantly greater levels of virus-neutralizing antibodies induced by ALVAC-RABV-VLP compared to a commercial inactivated vaccine in mice, dogs, and cats. Although the inactivated vaccine with Carbopol-941 adjuvant led to a slow release of immunogens and a gradual increase in VNAs over a period of 4 weeks after immunization, the peak antibody levels achieved were lower than those observed with the low-dose ALBAC-RABV-VLP (106 TCID50) formulation (Supplementary Figure 1). One key advantage of ALVAC-RABV-VLP is that it eliminates the need for inactivation procedures. For example, preparing an inactivated vaccine for the CVS-11 strain may require a high-level biosafety laboratory [30], whereas ALVAC-RABV-VLP production only requires the lowest level of laboratory containment since it does not infect mammals. Additionally, ALVAC-RABV-VLP does not require adjuvants, resulting in lower costs and prices. This also help avoid allergic reactions caused by adjuvants, which is particularly important for individuals with allergies. For instance, immunization studies in cats have shown that ALVAC can prevent adjuvant-associated fibrosarcoma [31,32]. The advantages of ALVAC-RABV-VLP, such as fewer immunization cycles, a faster antibody response, and greater safety, make it highly suitable for widespread use. It has a higher likelihood of success as a postexposure prophylaxis option, making it a better replacement for inactivated vaccines.

ALVAC-RABV-VLP elicits a potent and sustained humoral immune response by activating GCs. In our study, we observed a significantly greater number of mature DCs in the draining lymph nodes of mice immunized with ALVAC-RABV-VLP compared to those vaccinated with an inactivated vaccine. Additionally, ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced a robust germinal centre response, including Tfh cell differentiation, GCB cell proliferation, and increased plasma cell production. These findings suggest that ALVAC-RABV-VLP activates the DC-Tfh cell-GCB cell-plasma cell axis, leading to a strong and long-lasting humoral immune response. In the United States, V-RG is commonly used to immunize wild animals [33]. Vaccinia virus, the vector used in V-RG, possesses replication capabilities and exhibits a certain level of pathogenicity. However, as a replication-deficient virus, ALVAC-RG provides 100% protection against viral challenge at a lower viral infection dose than V-RG [29]. Nevertheless, in a study by Jacqueline Weyer et al., mice immunized with an infection dose of MVA-RG 100 times higher than that of V-RG exhibited a lower protection rate [34], suggesting that the immunogenicity of MVA-RG is inferior to V-RG. Studies have shown that both ALVAC and vaccinia virus can infect immature monocyte-derived dendritic cells (MDDCs), but only ALVAC infection induces subsequent maturation [35]. The production of rabies virus antibodies is closely associated with DCs, partially explaining the similar immunogenicity observed between ALVAC-RG and V-RG.

The excellent protective efficacy of ALVAC-RABV-VLP is likely due to its ability to induce robust cellular immunity in addition to a high level of neutralizing antibodies. Strong and long-lasting T-cell immunity plays a critical role in early virus clearance and mortality reduction [36–38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that ALVAC possesses a self-adjuvant effect, effectively enhancing the cellular immune response to vaccines [39]. Antibody subtyping results revealed that the IgG1-to-IgG2a ratio in mice immunized with ALVAC-RABV-VLP was close to 1, with significantly higher IgG2a levels compared to the mice immunized with the adjuvanted inactivated vaccine. Flow cytometry and ELISPOT assays also demonstrated that ALVAC-RABV-VLP induced a significantly greater percentage of CD8+ T cells or IFNγ+ T cells compared to the commercial inactivated vaccine in both dogs and mice. Crucially, in the challenge protection experiment, immunohistochemical analysis of mouse brain samples revealed that the percentages of RABV G protein-positive cells in the brains of the ALVAC-RABV-VLP-immunized group and the control group mice were approximately 10% and 90%, respectively. This indicates that ALVAC-RABV-VLP effectively reduced the deposition of the RABV G protein, highlighting its efficient virus clearance capability.

The development of ALVAC as a vaccine faces challenges due to variations between batches of CEF cells, unstable quality control, and the complexity of preparing large numbers of suspension cells. To address these challenges, we investigated the use of LMH cells (a chicken hepatocellular carcinoma cell line) for passaging ALVAC. Our preliminary results demonstrated that ALVAC can successfully proliferate on both adherent and suspension LMH cells. Notably, LMH suspension cells are currently used as a commercial production matrix for avian adenovirus vaccines. This suggests that ALVAC cultured from LMH suspension cells could be a viable option for future production. Furthermore, we conducted immunizations on Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) – deficient mice. Although some differences were observed compared to normal mice, both groups still exhibited the ability to induce a strong production of VNAs (data not shown). Therefore, further investigation is necessary to elucidate the mechanism underlying the immune response induced by ALVAC-RABV-VLP.

In conclusion, we have developed a promising vaccine candidate for rabies that generate RABV VLPs using an ALVAC vector gene-editing platform. ALVAC-RABV-VLP efficiently induced VNAs in mice, dogs and cats, providing 100% protection in mice against a lethal RABV challenge. This study presents valuable evidence supporting the efficacy and immunogenicity of ALVAC-vectored RABV VLPs, indicating that ALVAC-RABV-VLP holds potential as a single-dose, highly effective, and affordable vaccine candidate.
