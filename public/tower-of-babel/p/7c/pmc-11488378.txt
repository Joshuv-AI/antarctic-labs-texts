Thoracic myelopathy occurs due to the compression of the spinal cord, causing neurological impairment, and can potentially affect bowel and bladder control. Ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF), ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL), thoracic disc herniation (TDH), trauma, infection, posterior osteophytes, spinal cord tumor, and spinal cord herniation can result in thoracic myelopathy.1–4 OLF is the most common cause of myelopathy in which the ligamentum flavum undergoes progressive endochondral ossification.5–7 It is more common in the thoracic spine than in the cervical or lumbar spine, with the lower thoracic spine (T10–12) being the most affected area.7–11 On the other hand, TDH is uncommon and accounts for only 0.5%–4.5% of all disc herniations. TDH is often calcified and predominantly affects the lower thoracic spine, with approximately 75% of cases occurring between the T8 and T12 vertebrae.12, 13

Surgery is often needed for thoracic OLF and TDH, as they progress and inadequately respond to nonsurgical management.14, 15 Early surgical intervention is highly recommended for patients presenting with myelopathy with compression at multiple levels. Many articles on surgical techniques and outcomes for thoracic myelopathy have been published.16–18 The choice of surgical technique depends on the underlying pathology and whether the compression is more anterior or posterior. Conventional surgical methods include total laminectomy with or without facetectomy and instrumentation and a lateral extracavitary approach or costotransversectomy, as well as anterolateral retropleural approaches using thoracotomy.18 These conventional open techniques are associated with significant tissue trauma and blood loss, an increased risk of neural injury and neurological deficit, and increased morbidity.

Recently, endoscopic spine surgery has gained popularity due to its benefits, including minimal invasiveness, reduced tissue dissection and muscle trauma, improved visualization, and easy access to pathology. Endoscopic methods also involve minimal manipulation of cord and neural tissue, leading to decreased damage to the epidural blood supply and reduced epidural fibrosis and scarring. Additional benefits include reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stays, and earlier return to functional capacity and work. Endoscopic spine surgery can be done through either a transforaminal approach or an interlaminar approach, depending on whether compression is anterior or posterior and on the surgeons’ preference.

This article reports an unusual presentation of a 31-year-old male with thoracic myelopathy due to TDH with extrusion at the T8–9 level and OLF at the T10–11 level that was successfully managed using full-endoscopic transforaminal discectomy and full-endoscopic interlaminar decompression, respectively, in a single sitting. The presence of coexisting spinal disorders, that is, TDH and OPLL at noncontiguous spinal levels, makes surgical decision-making more complicated. The good results in this case suggest that full-endoscopic techniques can be successfully used to treat thoracic myelopathy due to various pathologies, thus avoiding fusion and significantly decreasing procedure-related morbidity.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 31-year-old male presented with weakness of his bilateral lower limbs and difficulty in walking beginning 6 months prior to presentation. His symptoms had worsened over the month prior to presentation to a status where he could not stand and had bladder and bowel incontinence on presentation. On neurological examination, motor power for both lower extremities was grade 2 with decreased sensations below the T8 level. Bilateral knee and ankle reflexes were exaggerated with upgoing plantars (extensor response), and he had severe lower-limb spasticity. Radiography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed T8–9 central and left paracentral TDH with significant cord compression with cord edema and T10–11 ligamentum flavum hypertrophy, more toward the right side, causing significant canal stenosis with cord edema (Fig. 1). After medical optimization, the patient was planned for a T8–9 full-endoscopic discectomy through a left-sided transforaminal approach and a T10–11 full-endoscopic decompression through a right-sided interlaminar approach.

Procedure Details

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned prone with horizontal bolsters beneath the chest and pelvic region on a radiolucent table.

The T8–9 Level

Under the guidance of an image intensifier, the T8–9 disc and midline were marked, and a stab incision was made 8 cm lateral to the midline and in line with the disc. An 18-gauge transforaminal needle was passed in the anteromedial direction toward the lateral margin of the inferior articular process (IAP) of T8 to access the foramen. A 7-mm incision was then made over the needle, a thin guidewire was introduced through the needle, the needle was removed, and a tubular dilator was passed over the guidewire. An 8-mm outer beveled sheath was then passed over the dilator and into the foramen (Fig. 2). An endoscope (6.9-mm outer diameter, 4.1-mm working channel, 25° view angle) was inserted through this left transforaminal approach, and bony landmarks were identified. Under endoscopic visualization, the IAP was defined and drilled with an endoscopic articulating burr to expose the lateral edge of the T9 superior articular process (SAP), which was also drilled to widen the foramen, improving access and visualizing the foramen, epidural fat, and disc. Though the rib head did not obstruct our access in this case, it typically needs to be shaved off to improve access to the disc space and foramen, especially above the T8 level. The T8–9 disc level was completely calcified and extended beyond the margins of end plates and was causing severe compression of the thecal sac. The posteroinferior border of the T8 vertebra and the posterosuperior border of the T9 vertebra were drilled to visualize the craniocaudal extension of the calcified disc. The ventral dura was separated from the disc hump, and the calcified disc was completely removed using small osteotomes, Kerrison punches, and a drill. The disc space was entered, all loose disc fragments were extracted, and the thecal sac was adequately decompressed (Fig. 3).

The T10–11 Level

Under the guidance of an image intensifier, the T10–11 space was marked. An endoscope (6.9-mm outer diameter, 4.1-mm working channel, 25° view angle) was inserted through the right interlaminar space. Bony landmarks were identified: hemilamina, laminofacetal junction, spinolaminar junction, pars, and facet capsule. Using an endoscopic articulating burr and Kerrison punch, a T10–11 right-sided hemilaminotomy was performed by drilling the lamino-facetal junction, lower half of the lamina (up to the attachment of the ligamentum flavum), and the spinolaminar junction. The OLF was identified and separated from the underlying dura. A complete ligamentum flavectomy was done, and over-the-top decompression of the contralateral side was achieved by drilling the undersurface of the opposite lamina and laminofacetal junction. The thecal sac was adequately decompressed (Fig. 4).

The dilator (A and B) and outer sleeve (C) docked via a right interlaminar approach for the T10–11 endoscopic decompression. Endoscopic view (D) of a decompressed thecal sac.

We planned to achieve adequate decompression with minimal manipulation of the cord; hence, intraoperative neuromonitoring was not used. The total surgical time was 160 minutes (95 minutes for the T8–9 transforaminal discectomy and 65 minutes for the T10–11 interlaminar decompression). In the postoperative period, scheduled physiotherapy was started. The patient’s spasticity improved significantly over 2 weeks, and his neurological status improved gradually to bilateral power grade 3 at the end of 2 months, when he was able to walk with a walker support. His neurological status improved completely at the end of 6 months, and he was walking independently with a normal gait.

Discussion

Thoracic myelopathy is relatively rare because of the limited degenerative changes caused by the restricted movement around the rib cage. When present, it can cause various symptoms, including girdle pain, back pain, leg pain, numbness or tingling, neurological deficit, difficulty in walking, gait abnormality, and dysfunction of the bowel and bladder.4 The commonest cause of thoracic myelopathy is OLF, followed by posterior disc osteophytes, OPLL, and TDH. Less common causes include calcified ligamentum flavum and degenerative spondylolisthesis.

Thoracic myelopathy responds inadequately to nonsurgical management.14, 15 Hence, surgical intervention is warranted. Numerous approaches have been described, including anterior, posterior, posterolateral, and circumferential methods, for tackling thoracic myelopathy.16–18 Posterior decompressive laminectomy or laminoplasty and circumferential decompression via a posterior approach are the favored approaches for posterior pathologies. Though anterior or lateral approaches are less preferred, they can be required for anterior pathology like TDH; these approaches are often associated with significant tissue damage, high morbidity, and an increased risk of neurological complications.

As observed in an earlier study, OLF predominantly occurs in the lower thoracic spine. This prevalence can be attributed to several factors, including increased mechanical stress in the thoracolumbar junction (interface between fixed rib cage and flexible lumbar spine). Moreover, increased spinal mobility and repetitive minor trauma are directly associated with this occurrence, along with the presence of high tensile forces in the posterior column.19 OLFs occur posterior to the cord; hence, posterior approaches are preferred. On the other hand, TDH is less common than lumbar or cervical disc herniation and presents a surgical challenge due to the substantial risks associated with each potential surgical approach. Thoracic disc pathology is usually located in front of the spinal cord (ventral), making posterior approaches risky due to substantial cord manipulation and a higher risk of neurological damage. Ventral and lateral approaches, on the other hand, necessitate navigating around pleural structures and can require fusion. Over time, these techniques have been modified to become less invasive, aiming to reduce approach-related morbidity and complications by minimizing tissue trauma and bone resection, decreasing neural handling, and improving patient outcomes while enhancing direct access and visualization of the pathology. Hence, endoscopic surgical techniques, after being widely accepted and successfully performed for the lumbar spine, are becoming increasingly popular for treating various thoracic spine pathologies.20–22 Advantages of endoscopic surgery include smaller incisions, better visualization of the pathology and hemostasis, less muscle and tissue damage, minimal bone resection, minimal blood loss, minimal cord handling, its execution through small windows without destabilizing the spine and therefore avoiding fusion, minimal postoperative pain, decreased hospital stay, faster functional recovery, and early mobilization and return to work, thus improving overall functional outcome and quality of life.

Ventral thoracic pathologies like TDH are more commonly addressed through the transforaminal approach, whereas dorsal (posterior) pathologies like OLF are more commonly addressed through the interlaminar approach. Both of these approaches are complementary to each other with their own indications and advantages. Observations

We presented an interesting and challenging case of thoracic myelopathy due to cord compression at multiple noncontiguous levels that was successfully managed using full-endoscopic spine surgery through sub-centimeter incisions that otherwise would have required extensive laminectomy decompression and fixation. While there is a steeper learning curve for full-endoscopic surgery of the thoracic spine, particularly with the transforaminal approach compared to the interlaminar approach, adequate exposure and training can make these procedures effective for addressing complex pathologies in a minimally invasive and straightforward manner.

Lessons

Uniportal full-endoscopic spine surgery is a safe and effective surgical option for thoracic myelopathy due to various thoracic spine pathologies. It is the least invasive with minimal tissue damage and bone resection, better access to and visualization of the pathology, and minimal cord handling and is performed through small windows without destabilizing the spine and therefore avoiding fusion. Ventral thoracic pathologies are better addressed through the transforaminal approach, while the interlaminar approach is preferred for dorsal (posterior) pathologies. Both of these approaches are complementary to each other with their own indications and advantages.
