Transcriptome analysis reveals key regulatory networks and genes involved in the acquisition of cold stress memory in pepper seedlings
Authors: Jian Li, Ping Yang, Hongbo Fu, Juan Li, Yanzhuang Wang, Keyan Zhu, Jihua Yu, Jie Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Temperature is an important limiting factor in the counter-seasonal cultivation of pepper. Currently, there are no studies on transcriptomic analysis of ‘cold stress memory’ in pepper. In this study, in order to understand the mechanism of ‘cold stress memory’ in pepper (Capsicum annuum L.), seedlings were subjected to the following treatments: normal temperature treatment (P0), the first cold treatment for 3 days (P3), the recovery temperature treatment for 3 days (R3), and another cold treatment for 3 days (RP3). The results showed that P3 plants wilted the most, RP3 the second and R3 the least. Leaf reactive oxygen species (ROS) and electrolyte leakage were the most in P3, the second in RP3 and the least in R3. In addition, RP3 had the highest accumulation of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin, followed by P3, and R3 had the least. These results suggest that pepper seedlings are characterized by ‘cold stress memory’. Transcriptomics was used to analyze the key genes and transcription factors involved in the biosynthesis of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin during the formation of ‘cold stress memory’. This study provides candidate genes and transcription factors for an in-depth study of the cold tolerance mechanism in pepper.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05660-x.

Background

The impact of global climate change on agricultural production is significant [1–4]. Human activities have led to increasing fluctuations in global temperatures and frequent weather extremes, such as intense heat and freezing, which will pose a great challenge to global food production [5, 6].

Plants under non-lethal adversity stresses can increase resistance in response to more severe adversity stresses, a phenomenon known as ‘adversity stress memory’ [7–9]. Although plants are organisms without a central nervous system and research on the determinants of memory formation is still in its infancy, some of the factors also come from the plant own stress response. Examples include calcium signaling, changes in phytohormone levels, changes in transcription levels of transcription factors, histone modifications and DNA methylation [7, 10–12]. Cold stress activates Ca2+ osmotic channels, leading to an increase in intracellular free Ca2+ concentration, which is sensed as a second messenger by calcium-dependent protein kinases (CPKs) and other lipid membrane proteins [13, 14] and activates the expression of the cold-regulated gene ICE-CBF-COR [15–17]. CBF-dependent cold regulatory mechanisms have been a hot topic of research. In addition, the formation and accumulation of antioxidant and osmotic regulation substances (e.g. soluble sugars and proline) enhance cold tolerance in plants [18, 19]. These substances maintain cell membrane stability and regulate cellular osmotic potential during cold stress [20].

Carotenoids are essential components of photosynthetic organisms that perform light energy capture and light damage defense functions [21]. Among the photodamage defenses are the lutein cycle and the cyclic luteinizing hormone cycle, which are formed by the interconversion of different carotenoid species [22]. The lutein cycle is a process in which the three carotenoid fractions (violaxanthin, zeaxanthin and antheraxanthin) are interconverted [23, 24]. Adversity stress leads to excess light energy in plants, the transmembrane proton gradient is increased, and the double epoxidized antheraxanthin is converted to the non-epoxidized zeaxanthin via the single epoxidized violaxanthin, so that the excess light energy is turned into heat energy and dissipated [25]. If there is no longer an excess of light energy, the epoxide-free zeaxanthin in turn passes through antheraxanthin and is finally converted to violaxanthin to ensure normal photosynthesis [26]. In addition, zeaxanthin as an antioxidant can directly purify excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) and alleviate the peroxidative damage caused by ROS to membrane lipids [27–29]. β-Cryptoxanthin is a precursor for zeaxanthin synthesis and is a major source for the formation of the lutein recycling pool [30].

Pepper (Capsicum annuum L.) is an agricultural product with high nutritional and economic value, especially in the cold season [25]. However, due to global climate change, cold conditions severely limit the production of pepper [31]. There is an urgent need to understand the resistance mechanisms of pepper to cold stress in order to enhance the ability of the crops to cope with extreme weather. In this study, we analyzed the changes of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin during the formation of ‘cold stress memory’ by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The key genes and transcriptional regulators related to the biosynthesis of these three substances were identified by transcriptomic analysis, which provided theoretical data support for the study of cold tolerance in pepper.

Results

Increased levels of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin create a ‘cold stress memory’ that enhances cold tolerance in pepper

First, the effect of ‘cold stress memory’ on cold tolerance of pepper was investigated. From the phenotypes of the pepper plants, the most severe wilting was observed in the P3 treatment, which was significantly restored to normal after the R3 treatment, and the wilting of the plants in the cold treatment of RP3 again was not as severe as that in P3. In addition, we found that the color of the apical portion of the leaves of pepper seedlings gradually changed from green to yellow from P0 to RP3 treatments (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, the O2−, H2O2, and MDA contents (Fig. 1B) and relative electrolyte leakage (Fig. 1F) were lower in RP3-treated plants compared with P3-treated plants, but these indices were higher in RP3 compared with R3. These results suggest that ‘cold stress memory’ enhances cold tolerance in pepper. Second, we investigated the effects of changes in zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin content on the formation of ‘cold stress memory’. Compared with P3-treated plants, RP3-treated plants had higher contents of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin, but R3-treated plants had the lowest contents of the three substances (Fig. 1C, D, E). The trend of changes in the contents of the three substances was consistent, suggesting that the increase in the contents of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin may be related to the formation of ‘cold stress memory’ in pepper.

Quality assessment of sequencing data and correlation and principal component analysis of samples

Total RNA was extracted from pepper P0, P3, R3 and RP3 samples for RNA-seq analysis. The number of all raw reads after quality filtering is in the range of 61,336 − 12,755,000 and the Q20 and Q30 values are greater than 99.01% and 95.98% respectively (Table. S1). Correlation analysis and principal component analysis were performed on all samples. The results showed that the correlation coefficients between all sample groups were greater than 0.83, and all group samples were clustered together except for P3_1 and R3_1 which were slightly dispersed from the samples of group P3 and group R3, respectively (Fig. 2A, B). These results indicate that the data from RNA sequencing are reliable.

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

A total of 382, 400, 467, 446, 442 and 341 DEGs were expressed in the RP3_vs_P3, RP3_vs_R3, P3_vs_P0, R3_vs_P0, R3_vs_P3, and RP3_vs_P0 comparison groups, respectively. A total of 1188 DEGs were co-expressed in the six comparison groups (Fig. 2C). The P3_vs_P0 comparison group had 5352 genes with up-regulated expression and 5926 genes with down-regulated expression; The R3_vs_P0 comparison group had 2,549 and 2,078 genes with up-regulated and down-regulated expression levels, respectively; The RP3_vs_P0 comparison group had 5388 and 5771 genes with up-regulated and down-regulated expression levels, respectively; The R3_vs_P3 comparison group had 6035 and 5533 genes with up-regulated and down-regulated expression levels, respectively; The RP3_vs_P3 comparison group had 668 and 701 genes with up-regulated and down-regulated expression levels, respectively; The RP3_vs_R3 comparison group had 5045 and 5376 genes with up-regulated and down-regulated expression levels, respectively (Fig. 2D).

GO Annotation and KEGG Enrichment

Functional annotation of DEGs in the six comparison groups was performed based on the Gene Ontology (GO) database (Fig. S1). More DEGs from all six comparison groups in the bioprocess were annotated in response to oxygen-containing compound. More DEGs in the P3_vs_P0, R3_vs_P3 and RP3_vs_R3 comparator groups of cellular components were annotated in the endoplasmic reticulum; more DEGs in the R3_vs_P0, RP3_vs_P0 and RP3_vs_P3 comparator groups were annotated in the thylakoid, plastid envelope and cell wall. More DEGs in the R3_vs_P0, RP3_vs_P0, RP3_vs_P3, RP3_vs_R3 comparator groups in molecular function were annotated in iron ion binding; more DEGs in the P3_vs_P0 and R3_vs_P3 comparator groups were annotated in sequence-specific DNA binding. In addition, the biological functions of DEGs during the formation of ‘cold stress memory’ were analyzed by enriched Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways (Fig. 3A, B, C, D, E, F). Functional analyses showed that during the formation of ‘cold stress memory’ plant hormone signal transduction, plant-pathogen interaction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, zeatin biosynthesis and MAPK signaling pathway-plant pathway were significantly enriched.

In this study, 26,009 differentially expressed genes were utilized to construct the co-expression networks associated with these three substances. Thirteen modules were identified by hierarchical clustering and labeled with colors from different tables (Fig. 4A). The MEblue module was highly negatively correlated with zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin content (r = -0.80, p = 0.0018; r = -0.84, p = 0.0006; r = -0.86, p = 0.0003), and the MEblue module included 3927 DEGs, and the overall expression pattern is shown in Fig. 4D. Zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin biosynthesis pathway

The biosynthetic pathways of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin were analyzed and mapped using transcriptome sequencing (Fig. 5A). Their synthesis begins with all-trans-lycopene, another branch of the carotenoid synthesis pathway. all-trans-lycopene formed first γ-carotene and then β-carotene under two successive catalytic reactions of lycopene β-cyclases (LCYB), and then β-cryptoxanthin and then zeaxanthin under successive catalytic reactions of β-carotene hydroxylase (BCH). This is followed by the formation of antheraxanthin and then violaxanthin under the successive catalyzation by zeaxanthin epoxidase (ZEP), which is reversible, and the formation of zeaxanthin under the successive catalyzation by violaxanthin De-epoxidase (VDE), which is the lutein cycle. Violaxanthin is eventually catabolized to form abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthetic precursors by indirect or direct catalysis by 9-cis epoxy carotenoid dioxygenase (NCED). There is another branch of this pathway, where β-carotene is catalyzed by β-carotene isomerase (D27) to form 9-cis-β-carotene which is then catalyzed by carotenoid cleavage dioxygenase (CCD) to form a precursor for stringolactone (SL) synthesis. In addition, β-cryptoxanthin and zeaxanthin were catalyzed by carotenoid cleavage dioxygenase (CCD) to form apocarotenoids (β-citraurin). There is also a branch in which antheraxanthin and violaxanthin form capsorubin catalyzed by capsanthin synthase (CCS). Combined with WGCNA and DEGs analysis, a total of four key genes related to zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin biosynthesis were screened out as candidate genes for further study, namely BCH1 (LOC107863219), CCD4 (LOC107870081), NCED1 (LOC107852027) and NCED2 (LOC107846808) (Fig. 5B).

Transcriptional regulation of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin biosynthesis during ‘cold stress memory’ formation

Transcription factors involved in the accumulation of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin, which may be involved in the formation of ‘cold stress memory’, were analyzed and mapped in a heat map (Fig. 6). A total of 26 differential transcription factors were identified, with the NAC (No Apical Meristem from Petunia hybrida, transcriptional activator 1/2 from Arabidopsis thaliana, cup-shaped cotyledon from Arabidopsis thaliana) family of transcription factors being the most numerous, four in total. Combined with the WGCNA results (Fig. 4B), the correlation of gene expression with zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin was higher in the MEyellow module. However, these transcription factors were not in the MEyellow module, suggesting that these transcription factors may not be positively regulating the biosynthesis of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin. Five transcription factors were identified in the MEblue module as C3H (LOC107872695), NF-YC (LOC107875284, LOC107877497), C2H2 (ALOC107875995) and FAR1 (LOC107863716). Gene expression of these five transcription factors was negatively correlated with the biosynthesis of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin and may negatively regulate the synthesis of these three substances.

Discussion

Crop seedlings are exercised prior to planting to enhance their adaptability to the environment after planting [32, 33]. Although it is currently believed that plants are organisms without a central nervous system, this process of exercising seedlings can be viewed as the plant’s ‘adversity stress memory’ [34]. It has been reported that treatment of broccoli at 10℃ for 7 days before transplanting increased the yield by 2.38 t ha− 1 over the control (18℃) [35]. This is actually a pre-cold treatment of broccoli to enhance its ability to adapt to the environment and ensure normal growth and development. The results of the study from seedling morphology, O2−, H2O2, MDA content and electrolyte leakage indicated that cold tolerance of pepper seedlings was enhanced by cold exercise treatment (Fig. 1A, B, F). In addition, we found that from P0 to RP3 treatments, the colour of the leaves in the terminal part of the plant changed from green to yellow (Fig. 1A). This phenomenon may be due to less chlorophyll and more carotenoids. We determined the content of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin in the carotenoid fractions and found that RP3 had the highest content, P3 had the second highest content and R3 had the lowest (Fig. 1C, D, E). This result could explain the previous phenomenon of tissue colour change, but slightly differently the zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin content of R3 did not match the tissue colour change. We speculate that this may be caused by changes in other substances in the carotenoid fraction. Zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin belong to the group of carotenoids, which are known to scavenge ROS and dissipate excess light energy [27–29, 36]. Previous studies have shown that increased levels of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin enhanced salt tolerance in sweetpotato [37]. These results reveal that pre-moderate cold treatment enhances the cold tolerance of pepper. Our results are similar to those reported by Leuendorf et al. in Arabidopsis thaliana [38], where discontinuous exposure to cold resulted in a gradual moderate increase in cold tolerance.

Stress responses are often associated with changes in the expression of many genes [39]. When the same stress is repeatedly applied, genes usually exhibit different transcriptional responses, which is called transcriptional memory [40, 41]. Studies of transcriptional memory have shown that the mechanism of transcript levels and activation in response to a single stress response is different from that of multiple stress responses [40]. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), RNA-seq, and chromatin immunoprecipitation qRT-PCR analyses of Brachypodium distachyon exposed to repeated cold have shown that transcriptional memory provides the Brachypodium distachyon cold domestication provided plasticity in the cold stress response [42]. In this study, transcriptome sequencing was used to analyze DEGs associated with zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin biosynthesis during ‘cold stress memory’ formation. Quality assessment of transcriptomic data, correlation and principal component analysis are important for the reliability of subsequent results. The results showed that the Q20 and Q30 values of all libraries were greater than 99.01% and 95.98%, respectively (Table S1), and the samples within the group were highly correlated and clustered together (Fig. 2A, B). For second-generation sequencing, Q20 bases should preferably reach more than 95%, and Q30 should be more than 85%, as they directly reflect the reliability and accuracy of the sequencing data [43]. Ideally, the samples should be spread out between groups and clustered together within groups for reliable test results [44]. The quality of the transcriptome data from this study meets the above requirements for subsequent analyses. In addition, we randomly selected 10 differential genes for qRT-PCR validation. Through its high sensitivity and specificity, qRT-PCR is able to accurately measure the expression levels of specific genes, making it an effective tool for validating transcriptomic data [45]. The results of this study showed a similar trend between RNA-Seq and qRT-PCR data (Fig. 7). It has been shown that the correlation between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR is around 0.80, which implies that there is some consistency between the two [46]. Validation by multiple methods illustrates that the RNA-Seq data from this study are reliable. GO functional enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis were used to identify major biological processes and metabolic pathways in which genes are associated with stress responses. In this study, GO functional enrichment analysis revealed that more DEGs were annotated in response to oxygen-containing compound, endoplasmic reticulum, sequence- specific DNA binding, thylakoid, iron ion binding, plastid envelope, and cell wall during the formation of ‘cold stress memory’ (Fig. S1). GO functional enrichment scores can reveal differences in biological processes, the distribution of genes by location in the cell, and the interaction of genes with other molecules to form complex biomolecular networks [47]. The DEGs genes in our RNA-Seq data were enriched for the corresponding functions for localization of candidate genes as well as functional analysis. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that more DEGs were enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, plant-pathogen interaction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, zeatin biosynthesis and MAPK signaling pathway-plant (Fig. 3). The role of KEGG is mainly to perform metabolic pathway analysis and gene expression analysis to reveal metabolic pathways and gene regulatory mechanisms in organisms [48]. Previously, in a transcriptome analysis of bell pepper under low-temperature stress, phytohormone signaling, MAKK signaling pathway, and flavonoid biosynthesis pathway were found at all cold stresses [49]. The two most prominent pathways in this study were the phytohormone signaling pathway and the MAPK signaling pathway. Since these two pathways were maintained under different cold treatment situations, these two pathway genes may be the key factors in coping with cold stress, but further studies are still needed.

The promoter regions of carotenoid synthesis-related enzyme genes contain binding sites. Transcription factors play important regulatory roles in carotenoid synthesis and co-expression. A number of transcription factors have been shown to be involved in carotenoid synthesis, and their biological functions have been validated in a variety of model and non-model plants. In tomato, phytochrome-interacting factor 1 (PIF1), which belongs to the basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor family, specifically recognizes the phytoene synthase (SlPSY) promoter G-box response element and represses carotenoid synthesis in tomato in dark culture [50]. The HYPOCOTYL 5 - phytochrome-interacting factor (HY5-PIFs) module can affect phytoene synthase (AtPSY) expression and promote photosynthetic carotenoid accumulation in Arabidopsis [51]. Papaya Ethylene-insensitive 3a (CpEIN3a) forms a protein complex with CpNAC2 (NAM、ATAF1/2、CUC1/2) that recognizes the phytoene desaturase 2 (CpPDS2), phytoene desaturase 4 (CpPDS4), lycopene ɛ-cyclase (CpLCYe), and β-carotene hydroxylase (CpCHYb) promoters, thereby positively regulating carotenoid accumulation [52]. v-myb avian myeloblastosis viral oncogene homolog 68 (MYB68) directly represses the transcript levels of beta-carotene hydroxylase 2 (CrBCH2) and 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase 5 (CrNCED5), which ultimately leads to a large accumulation of α- & β-carotene in citrus and a blockage of the transformation of downstream products [53]. In this study, we mined potential genes and transcription factors involved in the biosynthesis of zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin during ‘cold stress memory’ formation. We identified three more critical genes from the synthesis pathway, BCH1 (LOC107863219), CCD4 (LOC107870081), NCED1 (LOC107852027) and NCED2 (LOC107846808) (Fig. 5A). The combination of gene location and expression pattern suggests that BCH1 is regulating zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin biosynthesis (Fig. 5B). CCD4, NCDE1 and NCDE2 belong to the same gene family and are both metabolic genes, but they have different functions. Combined with gene location and expression patterns, CCD4 negatively regulates zeaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin biosynthesis, whereas NCDE1 and NCDE2 positively regulate violaxanthin biosynthesis. NCDE1 and NCDE2 as degradation genes should not be positively regulated. The expression patterns of the two genes, NCDE1 and NCDE2, throughout the experimental treatments were opposite to the violaxanthin accumulation characteristics. Therefore, NCDE1 and NCDE2 may be negatively regulating violaxanthin synthesis. Combining WGCNA analysis and transcriptional regulation analysis (Fig. 4), we identified five transcription factors in the MEblue module, namely C3H (LOC107872695), NF-YC (LOC107875284, LOC107877497), C2H2 (LOC107875995) and FAR1 (LOC107863716) (Fig. 6). These transcription factors may negatively regulate zeaxanthin, violaxanthin and β-cryptoxanthin biosynthesis, but their functions need to be verified in further experiments.

Conclusion

Based on physiology, transcriptomics, we analyzed the ‘cold stress memory’ mechanism in pepper seedlings. From the physiological point of view, plant phenotype, leaf ROS accumulation, electrolyte leakage, and zeaxanthin, violaxanthin, and β-cryptoxanthin contents indicated that pepper seedlings have ‘cold stress memory’. From transcriptomics, we extracted transcription factors for genes related to zeaxanthin, violaxanthin, and β-cryptoxanthin synthesis. These genes and transcription factors may regulate ‘cold stress memory’. Our results could provide candidate genes and transcription factors in future in-depth studies.

Methods

Plant materials and treatments

The cold-tolerant cultivar ‘Shenghan 740’ was used as the test material. Pepper seeds were germinated at 28℃ and then sown in a mixed substrate (peat, vermiculite, perlite, 3:1:1, v: v:v). The material was cultured under the following conditions: temperature 25/22℃ (day/night), photoperiod 12 h/12 h (day/night), relative humidity 75% and light intensity 200 µmol m− 2 s− 1. The experimental treatments were carried out according to Fig. 8 when plants were grown to 45 d. P3 represents a cold-start treatment for 3 days, R3 represents a return-to-temperature treatment for 3 days, RP3 represents another cold-start treatment for 3 days, and P0 is 9 days at normal temperature (samples were taken at the same time as RP3). Three biological replicates of the entire experiment were conducted and the sampling site was 3–6 true leaves lower from the bottom to the top. Samples were rapidly frozen with liquid nitrogen and placed in a -80 °C refrigerator.

Observations and physiological measurements

The electrolyte leakage of plant leaves was determined using a conductivity meter (Bante 531, Shanghai Kuosi Electronics Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) [54]. Leaf superoxide anion (O2−) production was detected by nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT) staining [55]. Leaf hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content was detected by 3,3´-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining [55]. Leaf malondialdehyde (MDA) production was detected by basic magenta sodium sulfite staining [56].

Determination of zeaxanthin, β-cryptoxanthin and antheraxanthin content

Pepper leaves were ground to powder in liquid nitrogen and 0.2 g of powder was weighed into 2 mL of anhydrous ethanol (containing 0.1% 2,6-di-tert-butyl-p-cresol) and mixed well. The samples were water-bathed (80℃) for 5 min and then 100 µL of potassium hydroxide solution (80%, w/v) was added and water-bathed again for 15 min. Remove the samples, add 1 mL of deionized water and 1 mL of n-hexane respectively, centrifuge for 5 min (3000 r/min), aspirate the supernatant and evaporate under a stream of nitrogen. The sample was re-dissolved with 0.2 mL of methanol solution and the solution was collected using a 0.22 μm organic filter membrane. Zeaxanthin, β-cryptoxanthin and antheraxanthin contents were detected using a DGLC dual ternary high performance liquid chromatography (Thermo Scientific) and a YMC Carotenoid S-3 μm (150*4.6 mm) liquid chromatography column (Beijing Huideyi Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) [57].

RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the TsingZol Total RNA Extraction Kit (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Kunming, China). RNA samples were checked for integrity and concentration. Successfully assayed samples were used to construct RNA sequencing libraries. The samples were sequenced using a second-generation sequencing platform BGI (MGISP-100RS, manufactured by Shenzhen Huada Gene Science and Technology Service Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China), which was performed by Wuhan Zhenyue Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Quality preprocessing of raw data generated by sequencing using Trimmomatic software [58]. Clean read map to the pepper reference genome UCD10Xv1.1 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_002878395.1/) using HISAT2. Using Cufflinks to calculate FPKM values for transcript or gene expression [59]. The DEseq 2 R package was used to identify DEGs (|log2 (fold change)|≥1 and FDR < 0.05).

qRT-PCR analysis

First-strand cDNA was synthesized using Goldenstar™ RT6 cDNA Synthesis Kit (Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Kunming, China). Master qPCR Mix (Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Kunming, China) containing SYBR green fluorescent dye was used in a qRT-PCR detection system (ABI ViiA 7, USA). Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primers for the qRT-PCR assay were designed using the primer primer 5 software (The primers are shown in Table S2).

Statistical analysis

Significance was analyzed using Tukey’s test in Origin 2022 software (p < 0.05); Heat maps were plotted using TBtools v2.095 [60]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment and Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis of DEGs were performed using clusterProfile software. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the R language (v4.2.2) and Metware Cloud [61, 62].
