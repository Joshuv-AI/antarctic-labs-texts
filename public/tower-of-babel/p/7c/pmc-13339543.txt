Letter to the editor regarding ‘clinical characteristics and factors associated with in-hospital post-surgical mortality in COVID-19 patients at a tertiary care center in Karachi, Pakistan
Authors: Nosheen Nasir
Published: 2026

COVID-19 severity classification

We agree that disease severity is an important determinant of postoperative outcomes. However, our study was conducted using a retrospective institutional registry encompassing all five COVID-19 waves between March 2020 and April 2022, during which clinical documentation practices and severity classification systems evolved considerably. Incorporating incompletely recorded variables would have introduced substantial information bias and reduced analytical validity through differential missingness.

Instead, we incorporated several clinically meaningful proxy indicators reflecting disease severity, including ICU admission, and complications such as ARDS and myocardial infarction. The observed associations between complications and mortality support the contribution of underlying illness severity to outcomes [1].

Regarding diagnostic uncertainty, institutional protocols required PCR or antigen confirmation prior to surgery whenever feasible. Both elective and emergency cases underwent standardized screening procedures, thereby minimizing misclassification of infection status relative to symptom-based diagnosis.

Timing between COVID-19 diagnosis and surgery

We agree that the interval between COVID-19 diagnosis and surgery is clinically relevant. However, accurate measurement of infection-to-surgery timing was not consistently feasible within our retrospective dataset. Importantly, our study aimed to characterize real-world outcomes among patients who underwent surgery despite confirmed infection within a low- and middle-income country (LMIC) setting. During much of the pandemic, surgical timing was determined primarily by clinical urgency and healthcare system constraints rather than standardized delay recommendations [2]. Consequently, omission of this variable reflects data limitations rather than conceptual oversight. We agree that prospective perioperative registries should incorporate infection timing variables to refine perioperative risk stratification models.

Emergency versus elective surgery

The correspondence emphasizes the importance of distinguishing emergency from elective procedures. This distinction was central to our analysis. While crude mortality was higher among emergency surgeries, Cox proportional hazards modeling demonstrated a lower adjusted hazard of mortality associated with emergency procedures after accounting for covariates.

We interpret this finding cautiously. The apparent protective effect likely reflects complex interactions including survivorship bias, differences in hospitalization duration, and pandemic-era surgical triage practices rather than a causal protective role of emergency surgery itself. Patients undergoing emergency procedures experienced longer hospital stays and closer monitoring, which may have influenced survival estimates. We agree that surgical urgency remains an essential stratification variable, particularly in LMIC contexts where postponement of procedures is often not feasible.

Obstetric and gynecologic procedures and maternal–fetal outcomes

We appreciate the suggestion to report maternal and fetal outcomes separately, given that obstetric and gynecologic procedures constituted the majority of surgeries in our cohort. Our analysis focused on postoperative in-hospital mortality among adult surgical patients, and neonatal outcomes were recorded in separate obstetric databases that were not linkable within the ethical and data governance framework of the present study. Nevertheless, subgroup analyses demonstrated no significant association between obstetric procedures, including lower-segment cesarean section, and maternal mortality risk. These findings are consistent with heterogeneous international evidence reporting variable maternal risk across healthcare settings [3–5]. We agree that dedicated studies examining maternal and neonatal outcomes among COVID-19 surgical patients are warranted and represent an important future direction for research in LMIC settings.

Long COVID and post-acute sequelae

The authors appropriately highlight the potential role of long COVID in perioperative risk. Our study evaluated in-hospital postoperative outcomes during acute infection, and standardized definitions of post-acute sequelae were not yet established during the study period. Documentation of persistent symptoms following recovery was inconsistent in retrospective records, preventing reliable classification of long COVID status.

Methodological context and study objectives

Our study was designed to address a significant evidence gap: perioperative outcomes among COVID-19 surgical patients in an LMIC tertiary care setting. Real-world datasets collected during a public health emergency inevitably involve trade-offs between methodological completeness and feasibility.
